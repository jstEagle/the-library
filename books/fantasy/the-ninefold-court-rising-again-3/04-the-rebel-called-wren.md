## 4. The Rebel Called Wren

The courier's map said the shop should have been a ruin.

Wren had memorized it in the crypt beneath the Belltower, by candlelight, while a man called Hallow traced the route with a finger gone gray at the tip from old frostbite. *Lamplight Lane, third turning past the tannery. A charm-seller. Villalobos. Fifteen years in the same stall and no one remembers her face.* He'd tapped the paper twice. "Either the best hider in Vell," he said, "or the Court already has her and doesn't know what she's carrying."

"And if it's compromised?" Wren had asked.

Hallow hadn't looked up. "Then you know what the fragment costs us if the Court gets its teeth into it. You do what has to be done."

*What has to be done.* Four words that fit neatly inside a knife.

Now Wren stood across the lane from the charm-seller's shop in the last gray hour before dawn, hood up, breath smoking, and told herself she was only assessing. That was the word they used in the cell. *Assessing.* Not casing. Not deciding whether a woman who sold luck-knots to tanners' wives deserved to die for the crime of sheltering something precious.

The shop was small and crooked, squeezed between a chandler's and a house that had burned once and never been properly rebuilt. Its sign was a wooden knot painted blue, faded to the color of a bruise. No guard posted. No wards that Wren could feel, and she'd been trained to feel them. Just a shuttered window and a door that looked like every other door on Lamplight Lane, which was, Wren admitted, its own kind of genius.

She'd been watching for two days.

Two days of nothing. A woman coming and going with bread and lamp oil. A child's silhouette at the window—small, still, wrong somehow, the way a held breath is wrong. And twice, twice, Wren had seen the charm-seller pause in the alley behind her own shop and stand perfectly motionless while a rat sat on her boot and *watched her*, and she'd watched it back, and then both of them had simply... continued about their business.

Beast-speaker. The rats confirmed what Hallow's intelligence couldn't. The fragment had gone not just to a stranger but to one of the marked—the ones the Court burned in the squares when it wanted to remind Vell who owned the air.

Which meant retrieval was off the table. Which meant—

The door opened.

Wren flattened herself into the shadow of the chandler's eave as Tamsin Villalobos stepped out into the cold with a slop pail in one hand and a shawl over her head, and for a moment the woman did something so strange that Wren forgot to breathe. She crouched. She set the pail down. She looked directly—at the drainpipe, at the dark under the chandler's step—and said, quietly, in a voice pitched for no human ear:

"Not today. Go on."

And from the shadow under the step, three rats emerged, considered her, and left without touching the slop.

Wren's heart was hammering before her mind caught up with why. Not fear of the woman. Fear of the *ease* of it. Fifteen years of hiding, and she still spoke to them like breathing.

*Compromised,* said the voice in Wren's head that sounded like Hallow. *A beast-speaker harboring the king's memory. The Court will peel this street open within the week.*

*Or,* said another voice, younger and less convenient, *she's kept a piece of Aldric's soul alive for nine days without knowing what it was, which is more than any of us managed.*

The charm-seller straightened, gathered her pail, and paused. Her head turned—not toward Wren exactly, but toward the darkness where Wren stood, the way an animal turns toward a sound below the range of hearing.

"You can come out," she said. "Or you can keep freezing over there until your knees give out. Either way I'm going back inside."

Wren should have run. Every instinct trained into her said *run, report, return with the cell.* Instead she pulled back her hood and crossed the lane, because some part of her had already decided two nights ago, watching a woman talk to rats in the dark, that this was going to end with her standing at this door.

"I'm looking for a charm," Wren said. "Against nightmares."

Something moved behind Tamsin Villalobos's eyes—recognition, maybe, or arithmetic. "Come in, then. Quickly."

* * *

Inside smelled of dried lavender and hot metal and, underneath it, something older—river mud, animal musk, the scent of a world most people had stopped listening to. Charms hung from every rafter in braided rows: knots and rings and knucklebones, harmless things, blessed things, the kind of magic even the Court tolerated because it asked nothing of anyone.

Except, Wren noted, for the knot above the doorframe. That one wasn't harmless. That one was a warding-braid, real work, old work, the kind her grandmother might have tied. Someone in this shop remembered more than she admitted.

The child sat in the corner by the cold hearth, wrapped in a blanket too big for her, watching Wren with eyes that were the worst thing Wren had seen in six years of rebellion. They were a child's eyes. They were also, unmistakably, in some way Wren couldn't name and didn't want to, *his*.

"You found her fast," Tamsin said. Not a question. She set the pail down by the door and positioned herself—casually, deliberately—between Wren and the corner. "Faster than whoever left her here expected."

"Did they leave instructions?"

"They left a child who doesn't speak." Tamsin's voice stayed level, pleasant, the voice of a woman discussing thread prices. "So I've been feeding her and keeping her warm and telling the neighbors she's my sister's girl, come from the coast after the fever took the rest of the family. Sister's dead, see. Sad story. Everyone loves a sad story; nobody asks questions after one." She smiled, and the smile was perfect, and Wren felt a chill crawl up her arms because she had watched rebels break in under questioning, and none of them had ever lied half so well as this charm-seller lying about a dead sister who didn't exist. "Who did you tell, before you came here?"

"No one living."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you're getting."

They regarded each other across the counter like duelists. Behind Tamsin, the child—Ember, Wren would learn she'd named her, Ember, of all the flammable names in Vell—pulled the blanket higher.

"You know what she is," Tamsin said finally.

"I know what she *carries*."

"Then say it. Out loud. Because I've spent nine days guessing, and I'm done guessing."

Wren glanced at the shutters. At the rafters. Old habit—checking sightlines, checking ears—but there were no ears here except theirs and whatever the rats reported, and the rats, she suspected, were loyal to Tamsin Villalobos first.

"The king lives," she said.

Tamsin didn't move. But the whole shop seemed to lean in—the fire stirring in the grate though Wren could have sworn it was out, the hanging charms swaying without wind.

"Aldric lives," Wren went on, low. "He sits his throne in the Amber Hall every seventh day and lets his people look at him. And wearing him, wound through him like wire through wax, is something old. Something that was old when Vell was a fishing camp. We call it the Ninefold Court, because that's what it calls itself—nine voices, one hunger. It speaks with his mouth. It signs with his hand. It has ruled us for fifteen years and eaten everything he was, everything he loved, everything that made him worth following." She heard her own voice fraying and forced it flat again. "It missed one piece. One splinter of him, cut free and smuggled out before the binding closed. His memory. His—" she hesitated, then committed, "—his love. For a woman he lost. It couldn't digest that part. It's the anchor. The one thing still holding the shape of who he was."

"And if it's destroyed," Tamsin said quietly, "he's gone forever."

"And if it's restored to him—if someone can carry it back past the Court's teeth—he has a chance of tearing loose. Of unbinding himself." Wren spread her hands. "That's the whole rebellion, right there. Not armies. Not uprisings. One memory, carried home."

Silence. The fire ticked. Somewhere outside, a gull cried once and stopped, as if corrected.

Tamsin looked at Ember for a long moment. Then back at Wren. "You said *restored*. Not delivered. Restored how? A memory isn't a parcel."

"We don't know yet. The fragment has to *want* to go back. It has to be spoken home, in the Old Tongue, by someone it trusts." Wren paused. "That was supposed to be our problem. Finding a speaker. Most of them are ash."

"And now your problem is me."

"Now my problem," Wren said carefully, "is that orders say if the fragment's keeper is compromised—a marked blood, a known gift, anyone the Court could trace—I don't retrieve. I erase. Nine days, a beast-speaker in a shop on Lamplight Lane, soldiers sweeping the lower quarters." She let it hang there. Let Tamsin do the arithmetic herself, because she deserved to.

Tamsin did. Wren watched her arrive at the sum, watched her understand exactly what the courier across the counter had been sent to do, and watched—this was the part Wren would remember later, in worse hours—watched her not flinch.

"So," Tamsin said. "Here's my counteroffer. You leave here and report that the trail went cold. The fragment's keeper fled the city, direction unknown, probably over the marshes. Tell them whatever makes them stop sending knives. In exchange, the girl stays with me, where she's safe and fed and learning words again, and you—" she pointed a finger at Wren's chest, "—come back tomorrow with everything you know about the Court, the palace, and the man it's wearing. All of it. Because if I'm going to be hanged for treason either way, I intend to commit some first."

For a moment Wren couldn't speak. Six years in the rebellion, and she had never once heard surrender and declaration in the same sentence.

"That's not how cells work," she managed. "You don't get to negotiate with—"

"Everyone negotiates with charm-sellers. It's the whole trade." Tamsin's smile again, that smooth, terrifying smile. "Take it or leave it, courier."

From the corner, small and cracked and clear, a voice that had never spoken in Wren's hearing said:

"She stays."

Both women turned. Ember stood wrapped in her blanket, those old-young eyes fixed on Wren with an expression no child should own—recognition, and grief, and something like hope worn down to the grain.

"She stays," Ember said again, in the Old Tongue and then, stumbling, in common. "I dreamed her. Before. On the road. I dreamed her coming." She frowned, searching for words like a woman groping for dropped coins in the dark. "You smell of the Belltower. And of... under. Stone and candles. You sleep underground." Her gaze sharpened. "And you knew my name before she said it."

Wren went very still. "No, I didn't."

"You thought it. Loud. When you saw me. *There she is.*" Ember tilted her head. "Why did you send me here? To this door? You knew. You chose her."

The lie Wren had prepared—fate, chance, the fragment's own cunning—died in her throat. Because the child was right, and because Tamsin was watching her now with new eyes, recalculating everything, and because Wren was suddenly, acutely aware that the next words out of her mouth would decide whether she walked out of this shop as an ally or didn't walk out at all.

"I chose her," Wren said slowly, "because the fragment needed a keeper with a hidden gift and an honest face and no ties the Court could pull. That's what I told myself." She exhaled. "And because I'd watched her feed rats from her own hand and thought: *there's a person who keeps things alive.*"

The fire popped. Tamsin looked at her for a long, long moment.

"Tomorrow," she said at last. "Dawn. Come through the chandler's yard, not the street." Then, softer, almost against her will: "Tell them at your crypt whatever keeps you safe. But Wren—" she said the courier's name like she was testing a blade's edge, having plucked it straight out of Wren's unguarded thoughts, and Wren felt her stomach drop, "—don't ever send another child to a door you haven't knocked on yourself."

"I won't," Wren said. And meant it, and hated how much she meant it.

Outside, dawn was breaking gray over the rooftops of occupied Vell. Wren pulled up her hood and walked toward the Belltower with Hallow's knife heavy in her belt and four words repeating in her head, over and over, replacing the older ones:

*I didn't do it.*

She would need better words than that by nightfall. Because somewhere between the shop and the crypt, she was going to have to decide what to report—and for the first time in six years, Wren the courier, Wren the reliable, Wren who had never once failed an order, had no idea which side of her own war she was on.

Behind her, in a crooked little shop that smelled of lavender and river mud, a king's stolen heart slept on—and its keeper had just begun to learn the price of keeping it.
