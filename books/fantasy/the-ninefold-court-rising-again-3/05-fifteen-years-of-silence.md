## 5. Fifteen Years of Silence

The word Ember learned first was *door*.

Not in Vellish, not in the trade-tongue the merchants shouted across the fish market, but in the Old Tongue—the language that came out of the child's mouth like water finding an old channel, worn smooth by centuries of disuse. Tamsin heard it and her hands stopped moving over the luck-knots she was braiding, because the word didn't sound strange to her at all.

It sounded like her mother's voice.

"Again," Tamsin said, and hated how gentle she made it sound.

"*Doran*," Ember said. The cat on the windowsill—Tamsin's cat, the gray one with the torn ear who had never been given a name because names were attachments and attachments were evidence—lifted its head and stared at the child with eyes like struck flint.

Every animal within three streets had gone still the first time Ember spoke aloud. Now, a week later, they only went quiet. Listening. The sparrows on the roofline turned their heads in unison when the child practiced vowels. The rats in the walls of the chandlery next door sat in the gaps between the floorboards like an audience.

Tamsin should have been terrified. She was terrified. But terror, she had learned, could be set aside like a tool you weren't using yet, and what remained underneath was something worse: the ache of hearing a dead woman's language in a living mouth.

"Say *water*," she said.

"*Sennet.*"

"And bread."

"*Malla.*" Ember's face did something complicated then—a flicker behind the old eyes, like a lamp behind a shuttered window. "There was—" The child stopped. Started again, slower, as if walking on ice. "A garden. There was a garden where someone said those words. A woman. She laughed when the bees answered her."

Tamsin's hands went still again.

"That's not your memory," she said quietly.

"No," Ember agreed, and looked at her with those ancient eyes, and asked nothing, which was somehow worse than asking everything.

* * *

Her mother had sung while she worked.

That was the thing Tamsin remembered most—not the fire, not even the screaming, though she carried both with her everywhere like stones sewn into her hem—but the singing. Maren Villalobos had sung to the gulls off the harbor wall and they'd wheeled in patterns no other flock made. She'd sung to the mules in the cart-yard and they'd stood gentler than any beasts in Vell. She'd sung in the Old Tongue, low, thinking her daughter asleep, and the moths had come to the window in spirals like written characters, like a language of wings.

Tamsin had been nine years old when the neighbors finally talked.

She remembered the tribunal more clearly than she wanted to. The magistrate with his soft voice asking questions he already knew the answers to. Her mother standing very straight, refusing to deny it, because denying it would have meant calling every kindness she'd ever done a lie. *I spoke to them. They spoke back. I will not say it was a sickness.*

They burned her in the Salt Square on a market day, so that everyone could watch between errands. Tamsin had been held at the front of the crowd by a neighbor's firm hand—not cruelly, the neighbor had wept while she held her—and told to look away, and she hadn't. Not because she was brave. Because some part of her nine-year-old mind understood, with terrible clarity, that this was the last time she would ever hear her mother's voice, and the Old Tongue coming out of a burning mouth sounded almost like birdsong.

Afterward, Tamsin had done the arithmetic of survival. It was simple. Her gift was a body buried in the yard; her job was to walk over the grave every day without looking down.

So she'd learned to sell charms that were only knots and knots that were only string. She'd learned to look bored when soldiers passed. She'd learned to keep her lips still around animals, to think at them only in shapeless grey wanting, never words, never words. Fifteen years. Long enough for the girl who'd watched the fire to become a woman who sold luck to people whose luck she privately despised.

Long enough that she'd almost believed the grave stayed closed.

Then a knock at night, and a silent child, and now this: sitting cross-legged on her own floor at midnight, teaching the Old Tongue to a fragment of a stolen king, feeling the grave open under her feet one shovelful at a time.

Every word she gave Ember, she gave twice—once aloud, once in the place inside her she'd bricked over. And the brickwork was coming loose. Last night she'd caught herself answering the gray cat's stare with an actual thought, shaped, deliberate: *not yet*. And the cat had tilted its head like a person.

Fifteen years of silence, exhuming itself one syllable at a time.

* * *

Wren came at dusk three days later, through the back way, smelling of chimney smoke and river mud, and Tamsin knew before the courier opened her mouth that the answer was going to be no.

"You can't stay here," Wren said. She said it fast, the way people say things they've rehearsed against argument. "The sweeps are getting closer. Two charm-sellers taken from Tanner's Row last week. The Court doesn't knock anymore—it just arrives, and afterward there's a smell."

"I know what there is afterward," Tamsin said. "I was nine."

Something moved through Wren's face—she was bad at hiding things, this rebel, which seemed like a professional failing—and then she crouched by Ember, who was braiding straw into shapes that might have been crowns or might have been cages, and lowered her voice anyway. "The crypt is safe. Under the belltower of Saint Verity's. The bells are consecrated lead; the Court's listeners can't hear through them. There are thirty of us. We have food, we have a plan, and we have a use for the king's memory that doesn't involve waiting for soldiers to kick in your door."

"A plan," Tamsin repeated.

"To restore the anchor. To speak him free." Wren's eyes were bright and slightly feverish, the eyes of someone who had decided hope was cheaper than despair. "You've seen what the child remembers. The garden. The woman laughing. That's not random—that's the seam. The Court took everything from Aldric except what it couldn't digest, and what it couldn't digest was love, and the fragment is that love, hidden in flesh small enough to smuggle past its teeth." She paused. "And it came here. To you. Of all the doors in Vell, it came to yours."

"Don't," Tamsin said.

"I'm not saying—"

"You're saying it. Everyone says it eventually. The face, the hair, whatever resemblance you've all decided I have to a dead queen I know nothing about." She stood, and her voice came out flat and cold, the voice she used on customers who tried to haggle. "I sell charms. They don't work. That's the whole of my significance. I'm not an anchor, I'm not a seam, I'm not a symbol. I'm a woman who intends to die of old age in a bed, quietly, having never once been interesting."

Ember looked up from the straw. Said nothing. The gray cat, on the sill, said nothing louder.

"Tamsin." Wren rose too, and for a moment the courier bravado dropped and there was just a tired young woman with a knife scar through one eyebrow. "My brother was taken in the second sweep. I never found out if there was a fire or a cell or worse. I joined the rebellion because waiting to be interesting nearly killed me too." She put a folded paper on the table—a map, a route, a door marked in charcoal. "The offer stands. It stands until it can't."

She left through the back way. Tamsin looked at the map for a long time, and then she fed it to the stove, and hated herself for how long she'd held it first.

* * *

That night she dreamed of the Salt Square.

In the dream it was raining, the way it hadn't rained that day, and the crowd held umbrellas instead of her, and her mother stood on the pyre dry and singing, and the gulls came down through the rain in their impossible patterns, and Tamsin woke with the Old Tongue rising in her own throat like floodwater, one word pushing at her teeth—

She strangled it. Sat up in the dark. Ember slept in the alcove behind the curtain, breathing small and even, and the city outside was quiet in the specific way that meant it wasn't quiet at all.

*It came to your door.*

She lay back down and stared at the ceiling beams and did the arithmetic of survival again, fifteen years older: two lives now, not one. Hers, and the small ancient one behind the curtain. The grave was open either way. The only question left was whether she stood over it or climbed in.

* * *

The soldiers came for the chandlery on the fourth morning.

No knock. No warrant read aloud. Just boots in the street at an hour when honest streets were empty, and then the smell—wax and tallow first, because it was a chandlery, and then the other smell underneath, the one Tamsin knew from a market day fifteen years ago, the one that never entirely leaves wool or memory.

She got Ember to the back room before the flames came through the party wall. Through the slats of the shutters she watched the Chandlers' Row burn—the shop where old Mistress Corva had let her warm her hands as a girl, the shop whose rats had gossiped with her mother's ghosts of gossip, gone orange and roaring while soldiers stood in a line with pikes and watched with the patient faces of men doing paperwork.

"They're not putting it out," Ember whispered. In Vellish. The child was learning which languages were for which dangers. "Why aren't they putting it out?"

"Because it isn't a punishment," Tamsin said. Her voice sounded far away to herself. "It's a message. *We know something was here. We're burning the ground to be sure.*"

The heat came through the wall and with it, faintly, screaming—not human, animal, the chandlery cats, the ones that had lived in the loft, and Tamsin felt the Old Tongue rise in her throat again, felt herself about to call to them, to tell them *out, run, the river gate*, and she stood in her burning neighbor's doorway with the words trembling on her tongue and understood that this was the moment. This was the exact moment her whole life had been aimed at. Silence or speech. Grave or exhumation.

She opened the shutter. She looked at the smoke. And somewhere in the roar she heard—or imagined she heard, it hardly mattered—her mother's voice, singing, low, the way she'd sung to moths.

Tamsin turned from the fire, knelt, and took Ember's face in both hands.

"We're leaving," she said. "Get the cloth with the sigil. Get nothing else."

The child's eyes went wide and old and, for the first time since arriving at her door, young. "Where?"

Tamsin pulled the map's ghost from memory—she'd burned it but she'd read it twice, she'd always read everything twice—and reached for her cloak.

"To the bells," she said. "Saint Verity's. There are people who'll want to meet my face."

Behind them, Chandlers' Row burned, and ahead of them the belltower stood black against the morning, and Tamsin Villalobos walked toward both with grief finally, finally refusing to stay buried.
