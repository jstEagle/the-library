## 6. The Crypt Beneath the Belltower

**Tamsin**

The belltower of Saint Vess had not rung in nine years. The Court pulled its bells the way it pulled teeth—quickly, and without asking permission—and what remained was a hollow stone finger pointing at a sky nobody owned anymore.

Wren led them down through its bones at dusk, past roosting gulls who watched Tamsin with an attention she did her best to ignore. *Not now,* she told them silently. *Not here.* But the gulls only tilted their heads, and one of them said—softly, in the way gulls say things—*she-who-listens* and Tamsin's stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

They descended a spiral stair that smelled of pigeon droppings and old rain, and then the stair stopped being a stair and became a tunnel, and the tunnel opened into a crypt where forty people were pretending not to be soldiers.

Candles everywhere. Maps on stone tables weighted with daggers. A woman sharpening something that didn't need sharpening. And along the far wall, niches cut for coffins that had been emptied long ago and filled instead with crates, blankets, children's chalk drawings.

Ember's hand found Tamsin's and gripped it hard enough to hurt.

"It's all right," Tamsin murmured, and then caught herself, because it wasn't, because nothing about this was all right, because she had spent fifteen years building a life out of gray wool and small lies and she had just walked into the middle of a rebellion carrying the single most dangerous child in Vell.

"You came," Wren said. She stood near the entrance, arms crossed, and there was something in her face Tamsin hadn't seen before—not triumph, exactly. Relief, maybe. The kind that frightens the person feeling it.

"You burned down my street," Tamsin said. "I didn't have a lot of options."

"The Court burned your street. We gave you somewhere to be when it finished." Wren stepped closer, lowered her voice. "There's someone you need to meet."

"I need to sleep. Ember needs to sleep. Whatever this is—"

"Tamsin." Just her name, but Wren said it like a hand on the shoulder. "Please."

So they followed her deeper into the crypt, past the candle racks, to a niche where an old man sat wrapped in a blanket, his eyes milky at the edges, his hands folded like a man waiting for a meal he'd been promised hours ago.

"This is Brother Anselm," Wren said. "He was chaplain at the coronation. Nine years ago."

Tamsin went cold. "The coronation."

"Tell her," Wren said to him. "Tell her what you told us."

The old man lifted his face toward her, though Tamsin suspected he couldn't truly see her. He saw something. His breath caught.

"Ah," he said softly. "Oh, my dear. Oh, you poor creature."

"I'm not poor," Tamsin said. "And I'm not anything. I sell luck-knots."

Brother Anselm smiled the way people smile at funerals. "Sit down, charm-seller. Let me tell you how a kingdom dies. It doesn't die by the sword. It dies by a vow."

* * *

**Wren**

Wren watched Tamsin listen, and watched her not believe, and watched belief arrive anyway, the way winter arrives—slowly and then all at once.

"King Aldric made nine vows at his coronation," Anselm said. "One for each blessing. Long life. Good harvests. Mercy in judgment. You know the form. Every king swears them; every priest knows they're theater." He paused. "But Aldric meant them. That was his mistake. He meant every word, and meaning is a door."

"What does that mean, a door?" Tamsin asked.

"The Old Tongue isn't a language of words," Anselm said. "It's a language of bindings. Promises made in it don't describe—they *hold*. And the thing we call the Ninefold Court—it's older than vows, older than crowns. It can't take a body that hasn't invited it. So it waits, century after century, for a king sincere enough to open himself with nine promises, spoken aloud, before gods and witnesses." His hands trembled. "Aldric was the most sincere man I ever blessed. It slid into him like water into dry ground. One vow at a time. By the ninth, there wasn't enough of him left to scream."

Tamsin's hand had gone to Ember's shoulder without her seeming to notice. The child stood very still, listening with that old, old face.

"But he hid something," Wren said. "Before the ninth vow finished, some part of him—the part that loved hardest—tore loose and ran. It's been running for nine years. Through whoever would carry it. It finally settled in—" she nodded at Ember "—that."

"A fragment," Tamsin said slowly. "Of memory."

"Not just memory. *Anchor.* The Court holds him through the vows. But a vow can be unbound—by the person it was sworn for, or by someone the swearer loved more than the vow itself." Anselm leaned forward. "That's why the fragment ran to you, girl. That's why it crossed half the city, past every ward and every hunter, and knocked on *your* door."

"I don't understand."

"No?" Wren said gently. "Then look at her, Anselm. Really look."

The old man reached up, and Tamsin—after a hesitation Wren could see cost her something—let him touch her face. His fingers traced her cheekbone, her jaw, the line of her brow.

"Maren," he whispered. "Queen Maren. Dead six years, and still the most beautiful grief in this city." He drew his hand back. "You have her face. Not a copy—a cousin's likeness. Her blood, I'd wager, three generations back."

The crypt went quiet around them. Even the candle flames seemed to lean in.

"That's nonsense," Tamsin said. "My mother was a dyer from the Tanner's Quarter. My grandmother was nobody. There's no queen in my family."

"Queens are made, not born," Anselm said. "Maren was a dyer's daughter too, before Aldric met her at a harvest fair and forgot how to be a king for three days." He smiled sadly. "You're her great-niece twice removed, or I'm a blind old fraud. And the fragment knew. Memory remembers faces the way rivers remember the sea. It ran to the last living echo of the woman Aldric loved, because that echo is the only key left in the world."

Wren watched Tamsin's face do several things very quickly—denial, fear, a flicker of something almost like anger—and settle on none of them.

"So that's why," Tamsin said quietly. "Not chance. Not kindness. Some dead woman's face." She laughed, and it was an ugly sound. "Fifteen years I've kept my head down. Fifteen years being no one. And the whole time I was walking around wearing a target I didn't know about."

"Yes," Wren said. Because lying to her now would be worse than cruelty.

"And if I refuse? If I walk out of this crypt tonight and go back to selling knots?"

Anselm folded his hands again. "Then the king stays devoured, the Court keeps Vell forever, and eventually it finds the fragment anyway and unmakes that poor child hunting for the rest of what it wants. Refusal is allowed, my dear. It's just not survivable. Not for you. Not for anyone you've let close."

Tamsin looked down at Ember. Ember looked up at her, and said—in the Old Tongue, soft as ash falling—one word.

Wren didn't know the word. But she saw what it did to Tamsin: a flinch, deep and involuntary, like a blow landing on an old scar.

"What did it say?" she asked.

Tamsin's voice came out flat. "It said *stay*."

* * *

**Tamsin**

Later—much later, after the maps were rolled up and the candles burned to stubs and the rebels pretended to sleep on their bedrolls—Tamsin sat in an empty coffin niche with her back against cold stone and tried to breathe.

Ember slept against her side, small and warm and humming faintly, the way a struck bell hums after the sound should have ended. In sleep, the child dreamed in fragments, and the fragments leaked: a garden, a woman laughing, a crown whispering. Tamsin kept her gift furled tight so she wouldn't hear the dreams properly. She'd heard enough.

*A vow can be unbound by someone the swearer loved.*

She turned it over and over, looking for the flaw, the trick, the exit. There had to be an exit. There was always an exit; that was the whole architecture of her life. Gray wool. Small lies. Doors with two ways out.

But there was no way out of a face. You couldn't stop having your mother's cheekbones. You couldn't unkiss a dead queen from your bloodline.

Footsteps. Wren, moving quietly between the sleeping bodies, and stopping when she saw Tamsin awake.

"You should sleep," Wren said.

"So should you."

"I will. Later." Wren crouched, forearms on knees, studying her. "You're angry."

"I'm *furious*." Tamsin kept her voice below a whisper. "You knew. Before you ever knocked on my door, you knew what I looked like. You sent that child to me like—like posting a letter. Did you think I'd never find out?"

"I hoped you'd never need to." Wren didn't look away. "Yes. I chose you. I'd tracked the fragment for four months, and it kept circling the Tanner's Quarter, and then I saw you at your stall and I swear to you, Tamsin, my heart stopped. Because I'd seen that face before—in the old portraits they haven't scraped off the palace walls yet. Queen Maren's face, alive, selling luck-knots in occupied Vell." She exhaled. "I told myself it was fate. It wasn't. It was a gamble. I put a child on your doorstep because you were the best odds I'd ever seen, and I'd do it again, and I'm sorry, and those are all true at once."

"That's not an apology. That's arithmetic."

"I know." Wren's voice cracked, just slightly, on the second word. "I'm not good at sorry. I'm good at odds. It's why I'm still alive and better people aren't."

Somewhere above them, wind moved through the empty belfry, a low moan like the tower remembering its own voice.

"Why do you care?" Tamsin asked. "About the king. About any of it. You're a courier. You could run cargo until the war ends or you die, either one honestly earned."

For a long moment Wren said nothing. Then: "My brother spoke the Old Tongue. Never taught, just—born with it, like you. He used to talk to the horses in our stable, and the horses talked back, and I thought it was the most beautiful thing in the world." Her jaw tightened. "The Court found out when he was eleven. They didn't burn him. Burning's for witches. For speakers they do something else. I won't tell you what. You'll see it soon enough, if we fail."

Tamsin looked at her—at the hard courier's posture, the scarred knuckles, the eyes that had done arithmetic with people's lives and hated themselves for being good at it—and felt the furious thing inside her lose altitude. Not forgive. Not yet. Maybe not ever. But land.

"If I do this," Tamsin said, "if I let you people try to use my face as a key—I want terms. Ember stays with me. Nobody uses that child as bait or a tool. And when it's over—whatever happens—you get us out of Vell. Both of us. Somewhere the Court has never heard of."

"Done," Wren said instantly.

"You agreed too fast."

"I told you. Best odds I ever saw." But she was smiling, tiredly, and it changed her whole face. "Get some sleep, Maren's-echo. Tomorrow we start teaching you the vows. All nine. You'll need to know exactly which chains we're breaking."

She moved off toward her bedroll, and Tamsin stayed in the coffin niche with the sleeping child and the cold stone and the weight of a dead woman's face laid over her own like a borrowed cloak.

Ember stirred. Murmured. And in the Old Tongue, still mostly asleep, said another word—one Tamsin's mother had sung to her over a dye-vat twenty years ago, a lullaby-word, a word for *safe*.

Tamsin closed her eyes.

Above them, faint through nine feet of stone, the wind in the belfry changed pitch. It sounded less like wind now. More like listening.

More like something with nine voices, learning where the key had been hiding all along.
