## 7. The Court Wears Many Faces

The belltower crypt smelled of candlewax and old stone, and for three days it had almost felt like safety.

Wren had run messages through occupied Vell for six years. She knew the weight of a city watching you—the shopkeeper who counted your steps, the beggar whose eyes followed too long, the way a street emptied not because of weather but because of soldiers. Safety was never a place. It was a window of time. And windows closed.

She stood at the crypt's edge now, watching Ember sleep on a pallet of sacking, Tamsin seated beside the child with a guttering lamp between them. The charm-seller was teaching her small words again—*bread, bird, door*—her voice pitched low, as if the Old Tongue might bruise if handled roughly.

"You should sleep," Wren said.

Tamsin didn't look up. "Ember dreamed about the garden again. The one with the woman laughing." A pause. "I don't like that I know what her laugh sounded like before she told me."

"You've never heard it?"

"Never." Tamsin's hands went still over the child's hair. "But I know it. The way you know a word in a language you can't speak."

Wren said nothing to that. There were things she'd stopped saying around Tamsin lately, sentences she swallowed whole, because the truth inside them had teeth. *I chose you. I put that child at your door. You were never found—you were placed.*

The fragment had come to her in the spring, smuggled out of the palace in a courier's hollowed heel, half-mad with remembering. The rebels had argued for weeks: destroy it, hide it, use it. Wren had studied its face—that old, old gaze in a child's skull—and thought of the reports from the lower quarters, of a charm-seller whose luck-knots actually worked, who'd survived fifteen years in a city that burned magic-bearers by the cartload.

Survived that long, that quietly? That wasn't luck. That was discipline. And discipline meant someone who could be trusted with the most dangerous thing in Vell.

So Wren had written the address on a scrap and paid a beggar-child to knock. Not fate. Arithmetic. She'd told herself it was kindness dressed as strategy, or strategy dressed as kindness—she still wasn't sure which lie she preferred—and every day since, watching Tamsin braid Ember's hair with hands that shook, she'd regretted it more and understood it less.

"The Court knows the fragment exists," she said instead. "It's why they sweep the quarters. They're not hunting rebels anymore. They're hunting a memory."

Tamsin finally looked up. In lamplight her face did the thing it always did—struck Wren sideways, made her think of portraits in rebel safehouses, the dead queen with her sharp cheekbones and patient mouth. "Then we move Ember tonight."

"We move at dusk. There's a route through the tanneries—"

The bell above them rang.

Not the hour. Not the alarm from the watchtower. The great cracked bell of Saint Aldous, silent for nine years, tolled once—a deep, wrong note that rolled down through the stones of the crypt like something swallowing.

Every rat in the walls went still. Wren felt it happen, felt the silence spread outward the way cold does.

"That's not possible," she whispered. "That bell's been broken since the occupation began."

Tamsin was already on her feet, pulling Ember up by the hand. "They're here."

* * *

They came through the door of the crypt wearing faces Wren trusted.

Brother Callow, first through, gray-bearded, gentle-voiced—he'd carried Wren on his back through the flooded sewers two winters past when she'd taken a crossbow bolt in the thigh. He smiled at her now, and the smile was right, and everything behind it was wrong. His eyes moved like water finding level. His shadow fell at an angle that disagreed with the lamps.

Behind him came Marta of the dockside cell, and young Perrin, and old Sef, and each of them wore their own face the way a man wears a coat belonging to someone he'd killed.

"No," Wren breathed. Because grief needed proof, and the proof was standing in the doorway smiling with Brother Callow's mouth. "No, you're dead. All of you are—"

"They died well," said the thing wearing Sef's face, and its voice was nine voices braided into one, harmonics underneath the words like roots under soil. "Which is more than can be said for what happens next."

Tamsin shoved Ember behind her. "Wren. The back wall. There's a drain—"

"I know the drain."

"The Court wants only the fragment," the enforcer said pleasantly, advancing. Its borrowed body moved stiffly, as if the joints had been relearned overnight. "The king misses this piece of himself terribly. Give it back, and the rest of you may keep your lives, such as they are. We are generous. We have had centuries to practice generosity."

"You're not the Court," Wren said, backing toward the drain, knife out, knowing it was useless, holding it anyway. "You're its fingers. Its teeth. It sends its *face*—"

"It sends what opens doors," the thing agreed. "Faces open doors. Trust is just a lock that hasn't been picked yet."

Then it looked past Wren, past Tamsin, directly at Ember—and smiled wider than Brother Callow's face had ever been built to smile.

Ember screamed.

Not a child's scream. A sound in the Old Tongue, one syllable of it, and every candle in the crypt guttered out at once, and every rat in the walls shrieked back, and in the dark there was the sound of running feet and grasping hands and Tamsin shouting—

Wren caught an arm, a wrist, struck at something soft, was thrown against stone hard enough that her shoulder burst into white fire. Hands—Callow's hands, she knew the calluses, God help her, she knew the calluses—closed on her throat and lifted.

"She is nothing," the nine voices said, close and intimate, wearing a friend's breath. "A courier. A stray. But you—little fragment—we have missed you. Come home. The king keeps your room exactly as you left it."

And then light—Tamsin's lamp, hurled, oil-flame blooming across the floor—and the grip loosened for half a heartbeat, and Wren dropped, rolled, grabbed cloth. Small cloth. Ember's sleeve.

She ran.

* * *

They came up through the tannery drains into rain, which was a mercy, because blood washes and screams drown.

Wren carried Ember on her back until her legs gave out, then dragged her, then half-carried her again, and somewhere behind them Tamsin kept pace, white-faced, silent, one hand pressed to her side where a blade—not a blade, something colder—had opened her during the dark. Wren had seen the wound. It wasn't bleeding right. It was bleeding *slow*, like the cut itself was reluctant, like the steel had been dipped in patience.

They holed up under a bridge where the canal stink covered theirs, and Wren bound Tamsin's wound with strips of her own shirt and tried not to look at how gray her lips were.

"Talk to me," she said. "Stay awake. Talk."

"He knew my mother's song," Tamsin said. Her voice was flat, distant, shock-flat. "The thing wearing Sef. It hummed it. Two bars. Nobody living knows that song but me." She turned her head, and her eyes were terrible and clear. "It's been listening to me for years, hasn't it. Since before Ember. Maybe since I was born."

Wren's hands stopped moving.

This was the moment. She knew it was the moment, the way you know a stair isn't there in the dark. Tell her the truth now, and lose her. Keep it, and lose her worse, later.

"Wren." Tamsin's voice sharpened. "How did the Court find the crypt?"

"A traitor. Someone sold us. We'll find who—"

"How did *Ember* find *me*?" The question came out quiet, which was worse than shouting. "Fifteen years I stayed invisible. Fifteen years, and then one night a child carrying the most hunted thing in Vell knocks on *my* door. Of all the doors in this city. Explain that to me like I'm stupid, Wren, because I'm starting to feel very stupid indeed."

The rain filled the silence. Under the bridge, the black water moved without wind.

"I sent her," Wren said.

She made herself hold Tamsin's stare. Made herself stand in it.

"There was no omen. No sign. I tracked you for a month—I'd heard rumors about a charm-seller whose knots worked—and I decided you were disciplined enough, isolated enough, desperate enough. I wrote your address on a scrap and paid a beggar to deliver her." Her voice didn't shake, and she hated it for that. "You weren't chosen by fate. You were chosen by me. Because I did the arithmetic and decided your death was an acceptable cost of the king's freedom. That's the whole truth. I'd tell you I'm sorry, but I'm not sure sorry covers doing arithmetic with someone's life."

Tamsin was silent for a long time. Long enough that Wren catalogued the ways this could end: Tamsin walking into the rain to die alone; Tamsin giving her up to the first patrol; Tamsin simply closing her eyes and letting the slow-cold wound finish its work.

Then Tamsin laughed—one dry, broken note, nothing like humor.

"Of course," she said. "Of course. Do you know what the worst part is? If you'd asked me—if you'd told me, and let me choose—I would have said yes. I would have taken her in anyway. That's what makes it unbearable. You didn't even need to lie to me. You just didn't want to risk my answer being *no*." She pressed her bandaged side. "Was any of it real? The lessons. The words. Sitting with her while she slept."

"All of it was real. That's what I regret." Wren's throat tightened. "I built a gamble and it turned into something I can't do arithmetic on anymore. I'd burn the plan to keep her. To keep you. I just—needed you to hate me for the right reason, not the wrong one."

"Well, I've got plenty of reasons." Tamsin closed her eyes. When she spoke again, her voice had gone somewhere beyond anger, into the flat country past it. "Sef hummed my mother's song, Wren. Which means the Court has been inside my life longer than you have. Which means whatever you gambled on—it was already losing before you rolled the dice."

"What do you mean?"

Tamsin opened her eyes, and Wren saw the wound beneath her hand had begun to darken, veins of shadow spreading out from the bandage like frost across glass.

"The enforcer called her 'the fragment,'" Tamsin said slowly. "Come home, it said. The king keeps your room. But Ember isn't a piece of his memory, is she. She's older than that. She answered in the Old Tongue before kingdoms existed." Her breath hitched. "Wren—the Court didn't take her because she's Aldric's anchor. It took her because it recognized her. Whatever that child remembers—whatever came before the crown—I think the Court is afraid of her. And I think it just carried the only thing it fears straight into its own house."

The rain drummed. Somewhere across the city, faint and wrong, the cracked bell of Saint Aldous tolled again—and again, and again, counting toward something.

Wren got to her feet, shoulder screaming, and pulled Tamsin up beside her.

"Then we go get her," she said. "Before it figures out what it's stolen."

Tamsin looked down at her blackening wound, then at Wren, and something in her face settled—grief and fury poured into one mold and hardened.

"Yes," she said. "We're going to need a lot of charms."
