## 8. Into the Whispering Crown

The palace gates of Vell had never opened for a charm-seller before. Tamsin knew that the way she knew the weight of her own name, and she let the guard's suspicion sit in the air between them like weather.

"Summoned," she said. "By the steward's office. Third seal." She held up the forged letter Wren had pressed into her hands at dawn, its wax still warm from a rebel signet they'd taken off a dead man two winters ago. "I'm expected before the noon bell."

The guard looked at her gray coat, her plain boots, the luck-knots strung at her wrist like a peddler's rosary. He looked at the letter. He looked, briefly and without meaning to, at her face—and something flickered there, the same flicker she'd been catching all week on every face between the crypt and the palace walls. Recognition without memory. Grief without cause.

*You look like her,* people didn't say anymore. They'd stopped saying it after the queen died. But their eyes said it for them.

"Wait here."

She waited. Above the gatehouse, gulls wheeled in the pale morning, and Tamsin listened to them the way other people listened to music. *Stone stairs, left of the gate, nineteen steps down to a door that sticks,* one gull reported lazily. *Two men with crossbows behind it. One is asleep.* Another: *The kennel master beats the dogs. The dogs hate him. The dogs would open any door for kindness.*

Every corridor of the palace had been drawn for her over three nights, in the voices of rats and ravens and one extremely opinionated cat who lived in the kitchens and considered herself underpaid. Ember had sat cross-legged on the crypt floor each evening, eyes closed, feeding the memories back—*the hall of pillars, the turning stair, the room where the crown sleeps when he does not wear it*—and Wren had inked them onto scraps of silk until the whole route lay spread out like a wound.

Now Tamsin folded the map away inside her head and walked through the gate as if she owned nothing but a satchel of charms and a talent for luck.

Which was, of course, almost true.

* * *

The summons itself had been the Court's idea—or so Wren believed, and Tamsin had chosen to believe it too, because the alternative was that the fragment had slipped loose somewhere in Ember's small bright mind and called attention to them both. A charm-seller summoned to court. It should have been a death sentence. Instead Wren had grinned, sharp and wild, and said, "They're inviting you in. Do you know how many rebels have prayed for exactly that?"

Prayed, yes. Praying was safe. Walking through the gate was not.

A steward's man met her inside the doors—a thin functionary with ink-stained cuffs and eyes that stayed fixed slightly left of her face, as if looking directly at her might cost him something.

"The Lady of the household requests—" he began, then stopped, visibly rearranging his sentence. "You are the charm-wright. You will wait in the antechamber."

"I know the way," Tamsin said pleasantly, and watched him flinch.

Because she did know the way. That was the problem. She knew it from the inside of a rat's memory and the outside of a raven's eye, and she walked the pillared hall with the unhurried stride of someone who had done it a thousand times, while the functionary hurried to keep up, unsettled by an instinct he couldn't name.

The beasts went with her, or their knowledge did. A sparrow rode the eaves above the first gallery. Mice tracked her progress along the wainscoting, reporting in their small urgent way: *guards here, guards there, none watching her, all watching the throne room door.* The kitchen cat had taken up a position near the great hearth and sent, once, a single image: the king, seen from floor level, sitting alone at midnight in a lit room, talking to no one, answering anyway.

Tamsin carried that image with her down the last corridor like a stone in her chest.

*He talks to them,* she thought. *Nine of them. And he answers.*

* * *

They made her wait two hours in the antechamber, which she understood as theater. Power enjoyed making people wait; it proved it could. She spent the time re-tying luck-knots with hands that did not shake, because fifteen years of practice had taught her fingers to be calm even when the rest of her was screaming, and because Ember had kissed her palm that morning—the Old Tongue word for *come home* pressed into her skin like a brand—and she intended to honor it.

When the doors finally opened, they opened on darkness.

The throne room of Vell had been built to catch the sun: high clerestory windows, gold leaf, banners that drank the light. Now the windows were shuttered, the banners gone, and a hundred candles burned in ranks like soldiers. At the far end, on a throne that had held nine generations of true kings, sat King Aldric.

He looked—Tamsin searched for the word and hated every candidate—*maintained*. Like a house kept standing by someone who no longer lived in it. His robes were immaculate. His face was handsome in the worn way of a coin passed through too many hands. His eyes were open and fixed on her, and they were the eyes of a man standing at a window, watching rain, from very far away.

And beneath his silence, she heard them.

Not with her ears. With the older listening, the gift her mother had died for, the thing she had buried like a body and dug up like a thief. Nine voices, layered under the king's own like sediment under topsoil. They were not speaking to her yet. They were speaking to each other, a low constant congress, and the sound of it was the sound Tamsin imagined a crown would make if a crown could whisper: patient, ancient, and pleased.

*There she is,* the voices said, in the tone of collectors finding the missing piece.

"Charm-wright." The king's voice came out alone, and it was a good voice, a king's voice, ruined only by the faintest echo beneath it, like a second speaker half a breath behind. "You are summoned. Approach."

She approached. Her boots sounded very loud on the stone. Halfway down the room she stopped, because stopping was a power too, and because the rats had told her about the pressure plates in the third and fourth flagstones—not traps, just old defenses, and old defenses meant the Court remembered being afraid.

"Your Majesty honors me," she said, and bowed the exact amount a free citizen bows: enough, no more.

"You resemble someone." The words came flat, uninflected. A puppet's line. But underneath, one of the nine voices stirred, tasting the air of her. "That is why you are here."

"People say I have an honest face."

"People say many things about you, Tamsin Villalobos." Her name in the king's mouth, in *their* mouths, landed like a hand on the back of her neck. "They say you sell harmless charms. They say you have never once drawn the attention of the law. Fifteen years, and not a whisper." The king's head tilted, a degree, a mechanism. "Silence like that is not natural. Silence like that is *practiced*. And practiced silence interests us."

Behind the shutters, the sparrows had gone still. In the walls, the mice had stopped breathing. Tamsin felt the whole hidden city of beasts holding its breath around her, and she stood in the middle of it, alone, the way she'd stood alone for fifteen years.

"What do you want?" she asked. "If you wanted me arrested, I'd already be in chains. If you wanted me dead, I'd already be ash. So you want something else."

For a moment—only a moment—one voice rose above the others, and it was almost human, almost tired, and it said: *Ask him what he remembers about the garden.*

Then the moment closed, and the nine spoke together, and their unity had the smoothness of oil.

"We want to make you an offer, charm-wright. We know what walks beside you. We know what came to your door in the dark, wearing a child. You have been keeping it. Feeding it. Teaching it." The king's mouth smiled without the king. "We do not object. Curiosity is a fine quality in a vessel."

Tamsin's blood went cold and stayed cold. *They don't know where Ember is.* They knew the child existed; they could feel the fragment the way a man feels a splinter. But the crypt, the rebels, Wren—all of it was still dark to them, and everything depended on keeping it that way.

"The offer," she said, because her voice would hold if she made it hold. "Say it."

"Walk away." The word came gentle, coaxing, a mother's word, a lover's word. "Take your satchel and your knots and your practiced silence, and walk out of this city, and live. No one will follow you. No one will ever look at you again. We will unmake every record of you—your mother's trial, your mother's fire, the name Villalobos struck from every ledger. You will be no one, and no one cannot be hunted."

The candles leaned in their hundreds, flames bending toward her as if the room itself were inhaling.

"And in exchange?" Her voice cracked on the second word, and she hated herself for it.

"In exchange, you leave the child where it lies. You stop teaching it words. You forget the door it knocked on, and the word it spoke, and the woman it remembers laughing in a garden." The nine voices softened, went silken. "You have spent fifteen years buying safety with silence, Tamsin Villalobos. We are offering you the finest silence ever sold. Permanent. Painless. *Yours.*"

* * *

And here was the terrible thing, the thing she would carry afterward even if she survived everything else: for three full heartbeats, she wanted it.

Three heartbeats of a life unfolding before her like a road in summer—a cottage somewhere green, no fires, no hunts, no listening at keyholes, no child with old eyes depending on her to be brave. Three heartbeats of *enough*, of *survived*, of putting the gift back in the ground and walking away whistling.

Her mother had died screaming her gift into the square. Tamsin had promised the smoke, privately, at eight years old, *never me.* Fifteen years of gray careful life, all of it built on that promise. The Court wasn't offering her a bargain. It was offering her the rest of her life, pre-approved.

*Walk away,* whispered one voice, and it sounded horribly like her own.

Then she saw it—the image the kitchen cat had sent her, rising unbidden: the king at midnight, alone, lit by one candle, talking to no one, answering anyway. Nine parasites wearing a man like a house, and the man still in there, still asking the garden questions, still waiting twenty years for someone to come.

And she heard Ember's voice, small and clear, saying the Old Tongue word for *home*, and the word meaning, somehow, her shop, her crooked door, her.

"No," Tamsin said.

The candles straightened. The nine voices went utterly silent, and in that silence the king's own voice surfaced alone, raw and startled, like a drowning man breaking water:

"—no. *No.* Did you—did you hear me? Are you *there*? Can you hear—"

And then the sediment closed over him, layer by layer, and the hollow eyes refocused, and the smile returned without him.

"A pity," said the Court, through the king's lips, in nine voices braided into one. "We had hoped to spare the city further unpleasantness. But vessels that refuse the cup must be broken, and anchors that resist must be pulled."

The king rose from the throne. Behind the shuttered windows, every bird in the palace rose at once, screaming.

"We will keep the child," the Court said pleasantly. "We find we already know where it sleeps."

Tamsin ran.
