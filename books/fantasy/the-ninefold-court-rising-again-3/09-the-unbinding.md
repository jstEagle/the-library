## 9. The Unbinding

The throne room of Vell had not always been this cold.

Tamsin knew that the way she knew her own heartbeat—not from memory but from the stones themselves. The marble under her bare feet remembered warmth: braziers lit for coronations, a thousand bodies pressed together in joy. Now the braziers were dark, and the only heat came from the nine candles arranged in a ring before the throne, each one burning with a flame the color of an old bruise.

She had walked here through every guarded corridor with a basket of luck-knots on her arm and a soldier's hand at her elbow, and no one had stopped her, because her beasts had mapped the way and told her which eyes to avoid. A rat had chewed through the signal-ropes of the east gate. Gulls had mobbed the watchtower so thickly that the lookouts saw nothing but white wings and screaming. The palace was blind tonight, and it did not know it yet.

Ember stood beside her. They had taken the child from the Court's enforcers two days ago—Wren bleeding, Tamsin bargaining with a falcon's fury—and Ember had not spoken since. Not from fear. From gathering. Tamsin could feel it now, standing in the cold, the Old Tongue coiled inside the small body like spring water under ice.

On the throne sat King Aldric.

What remained of him.

He was younger than his portraits and older than his years, hollow-eyed, hands resting on the armrests as if they had been placed there by someone else. His mouth was closed, but his lips moved faintly, continuously, like a man murmuring in sleep. Nine voices lived under his skin. She could hear them when the wind shifted—a chorus whispering through his teeth, harmonizing on some endless wordless note.

*You came.*

The voice came from everywhere. From the candles. From the walls. From Aldric's still mouth.

Tamsin set down her basket. Her hands were steady. Fifteen years of practice had taught her that much: terror lives in the hands first, and you starve it there.

"I came to sell charms," she said. "I was summoned."

*We summoned nothing.* The flames leaned toward her. *The child called to us all its life without knowing. And you—you came because you cannot help it. Speakers always come to the crown eventually. Moths and bells and little gray sellers of knots.*

"You know what I am."

*We have known since you crossed the threshold. Your gift is written on you like ink on skin. It smells of birds.* A pause, and something almost like pleasure rippled through the room. *We have eaten many speakers. Yours will be sweet.*

"Not mine," Tamsin said. "I'm not here to give you anything yet. I'm here to make a withdrawal."

For the first time, Aldric's head lifted. Just slightly. Just enough. And behind the hollow eyes, deep down, something looked out at her—something drowning, something that had been drowning for years and had learned to stop struggling, but recognized, suddenly, the shape of a rope.

*There is nothing to withdraw,* the Court said. *The king is ours. We are woven through his marrow. Every vow he spoke at his coronation is a thread, and we hold every end.*

"Every thread has a first knot." Tamsin knelt, slowly, deliberately, and took Ember's hand. "And every knot can be spoken loose. In the tongue your threads were tied in."

The candles flared.

*Do not,* the Court said, and for the first time the layered voices were not smooth. There was a crack in them, hairline-fine. *Little seller. You do not know what you hold. Speak one syllable of the Old Tongue in this room and we will unravel you nerve from nerve. We will take everything you are and wear it.*

"That's the idea," said Tamsin Villalobos, who had spent fifteen years being no one, "eventually."

*

She began with Ember's voice, not her own.

That was Wren's plan, shouted through bloodied teeth in the crypt's ruins: *It went into him through a vow, so it comes out through a voice—but not yours, Tamsin, yours it will grab, it knows speakers, let Ember carry the sound and you shape it, you're the bank of the river, don't be the river.*

So Tamsin crouched behind the child and put her hands on Ember's shoulders, and whispered the first word against the small ear—the Old Tongue word for *remember*, the word her mother used to sing to the kitchen mice, the word she had not spoken aloud in fifteen years.

Ember opened their mouth.

The word came out like a bell struck underwater. Every candle guttered. Somewhere far above, every bird left the palace roof at once, a thunderclap of wings, and the rats in the walls poured downward, fleeing, and Tamsin felt the throne room itself flinch—the stones remembering warmth, remembering music, remembering being only stone.

Aldric gasped.

It was a small sound. It was the loudest thing Tamsin had ever heard.

One thread snapped. She felt it go—a vibration through the floor, through her teeth, a single note of the nine-voice chord going silent. On the throne, the king's right hand twitched, fingers curling as if remembering they belonged to him.

*STOP.*

The Court's voice filled the room the way floodwater fills a cellar. The bruise-colored flames stood tall and began to bend, all nine, bending toward the throne like courtiers bowing—or like leeches turning.

*You will pay for that,* the Court said, and now it spoke to her alone, and the other voices dropped away until it sounded almost human, almost kind. *But we are generous. We offer terms. Walk away, speaker. Take the child. Leave the king. In exchange: silence. Ours forever and yours. We will never hunt you. We will never speak your name. You will sell your charms in your gray little shop until you die warm in your bed, and no flame will ever come for you as it came for your mother.*

Tamsin went very still.

Because the terrible thing—the thing she would never tell Wren, never tell anyone—was that for three full heartbeats, she wanted it. She could see the shop. The kettle. The cats on the sill. The long safe gray life, unburned.

Then she looked at Aldric's face. At the drowning thing behind his eyes, looking at her rope.

"No," she said.

*No?*

"My mother burned singing," Tamsin said. "They gave her the same offer, I think, or something like it. Be quiet and live. And she sang anyway, because a gift isn't a possession, it's a promise—to everything that talks and everything that listens. So no. No terms. No walking away." She tightened her hands on Ember's shoulders. "We're doing the whole unbinding. Right now. And you're going to hate every second."

*Then you will pay the price,* the Court said, and the kindness drained out of it like blood from a floor. *Every binding holds because something holds it. Our anchor in the king is his love—one woman, one garden, one laugh, buried so deep he cannot reach it. To loosen our grip, you must offer an anchor of equal weight. Love for love. Memory for memory. That is the law beneath all laws, older than kingdoms, older than us.*

"Fine," Tamsin said.

*You misunderstand.* The flames turned black at their hearts. *Not his memories. He has none left to give; we drank them years ago. We mean yours.*

*

There it was. The trap inside the bargain, dressed as a price.

She thought of Wren's face in the crypt firelight. Of Ember falling asleep against her shoulder, dead weight and trust. Of her mother's hands shaping dough and charm-knots with the same motion, humming in the Old Tongue, teaching her without teaching her that magic was just attention, just love pointed at the world.

*All of it,* the Court whispered, hungry now, greedy, the greed rolling off it in waves she could taste. *Your mother's songs. The child's weight against your chest. The courier's crooked smile. Everything you love, everything you remember loving—we take it all, and in the taking we loosen, and while we loosen, the child speaks, and the king wakes. That is the trade. That is the only trade.*

"And if I say yes," Tamsin asked, "will you keep your word? Will you let him go?"

*We will be torn from him whether we consent or not,* the Court said, and there was something almost like honesty in it, or its shadow. *The anchor pulls us out root and branch. Yes, seller. When the last memory leaves you, he will be free. We keep the old laws. They are the only things that bind us too.*

Tamsin closed her eyes.

*Don't,* said Ember.

It was the child's voice—small, cracked, real—in the Old Tongue and then, stumblingly, in the common speech, the words Wren had taught them on the road. *Don't. Please. I'll remember for both of us. I promised.*

"You will," Tamsin said. Her throat hurt. "That's exactly what you'll do. But a story needs someone to happen to, little ember, and I—" She laughed, wet and short. "I've been no one for fifteen years. Turns out I got good at it."

She kissed the top of Ember's head. She thought: *Wren, I'm sorry about the crooked-smile thing, you'll have to tell me someday whether I'd have liked you.* She thought: *Mama, sing me out.*

Then she lifted her face to the black-hearted candles and began, in the common tongue because the Court demanded clarity, to recite her own heart.

"My mother's name was Sefa. She hummed when she worked. The song was about a wren building a nest in a bell tower—"

The first pull came. It didn't hurt. That was the horror of it—it was like setting down a pack carried so long she'd forgotten its weight, except the weight was the whole sky and she was forgetting the sky.

"—she smelled of rosemary and smoke. She held my hands over hers at the hearth. She said, *the world is talking, Tammy, all you have to do is answer—*"

Gone. The hearth went dark in her. A name emptied out and became a shape without contents.

"There's a courier," she pressed on, faster, feeding the fire, giving the Court everything, watching Aldric's back straighten finger by finger, vertebra by vertebra, the nine-voice chord fraying note by note as the anchor tore free. "Crooked smile. Sent a child to my door and lied to me and saved my life. I haven't—I don't know what I feel yet, but it's big, it's a country, it's—"

Taken. A country erased from her map.

"Ember sleeps against my shoulder. Ember calls me—" What did the child call her? There had been a word. There had been—

Gone. Gone. Gone.

She was kneeling on cold stone reciting a list of shapes with nothing inside them, and she kept speaking because the king's mouth was open now, truly open, and a scream was coming out of it that was nine voices collapsing into one, and the candles were shrieking blue, and the Court was tearing loose from Aldric's marrow root and branch, gorged and staggering drunk on stolen love, too heavy to hold its grip—

—and King Aldric stood up.

He stood up like a man surfacing from deep water, and the first air he breathed was a roar, and his hand closed around the nearest black candle and drove it down into the stone, snapping it, and the Court screamed with his voice and with no voice at all.

*TAM SIN VIL LA LO BOS—*

"Wrong," Aldric said—his voice, one voice, hoarse and alive and furious. "Her name belongs to herself. And so does mine."

He broke the second candle. The third. With each one the presence in the room thinned, unraveled, went up like smoke in a draft. On the ninth, he turned to the throne itself and put his shoulder to it, and Tamsin—who was sitting on the floor now, holding Ember's hand, holding someone's hand, she couldn't remember whose, couldn't remember why her face was wet—watched the seat of the Ninefold Court crack down the middle like ice in spring.

The last of it fled upward through the broken roof, thin and shrieking and starving, out over the city of Vell, and the gulls chased it, screaming, all the way past the walls.

Silence. Warmth, returning slowly, like feeling coming back to a numb limb.

The king knelt in front of her. He looked wrecked and ancient and twenty-five years old, all at once.

"You paid," he said. "I felt every coin."

Tamsin blinked at him. She searched herself the way you pat your pockets after losing something, and found pockets full of ash, and found that she was crying anyway, though she could not have said for whom.

"I don't understand," she said pleasantly. "Why am I crying?"

Behind her, small and fierce and certain, a child's voice began to speak.

* * *

The doors of the throne room burst open before Ember reached the third sentence—Wren in the doorway, sword half-drawn, bandaged, wild-eyed, taking in the shattered throne, the kneeling king, the weeping charm-seller on the floor.

"Tamsin." Wren crossed the room in six strides and dropped to her knees, and her hands hovered, afraid to touch. "Tamsin, look at me. Do you know me?"

Tamsin smiled at the stranger with the crooked smile, and wiped her face, and said the truest thing she had left:

"No. But I think I'd like to."
