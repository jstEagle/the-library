## 10. What Remains When Memory Burns

**Tamsin**

The shop smelled of cedar and old thread, the way it always had, and Tamsin stood in the middle of it feeling like a stranger in a borrowed coat.

Someone had rebuilt it for her. That was what Wren said—that after the palace, after the fire and the shattering of nine voices in one throne room, the rebels had carried her back to Vell and put her life back together plank by plank. The luck-knots hung from their pegs. The counter bore the same scratch marks she was told she'd made over fifteen years of restless thumbs. A gray cat slept in the window box, and when she passed it, her hand moved on its own to scratch behind its ears.

"You're kind to cats," Ember said from the doorway, watching her with those old-young eyes. "You were always kind to them."

"I don't remember that."

"I know." The child crossed the room and sat at the little worktable, patient as sunrise. "But I do."

* * *

**Wren**

The first weeks, Wren had kept watch like a soldier guarding a wound that might reopen. She expected grief to break Tamsin open, or worse—expected nothing, an emptiness so complete the woman would simply stop, mid-motion, and have to be reminded how to finish pouring tea.

It wasn't like that. It was quieter and crueler than that. Tamsin worked. She sold charms. She laughed at things strangers said in the market, and then sometimes she stopped laughing halfway through, as if the laugh had run out of road, and stared at nothing until Ember touched her sleeve.

"She gave everything," Wren told the king once, in the crypt beneath the belltower where they still met. Aldric sat thinner than his portraits now, free and hollow-eyed in the way of men who'd been someone else's house for years. "Her mother's songs. Every memory of—" He didn't say it either. Neither of them said *me*. "And she doesn't even know what she paid. She just feels poor."

"That is why you stay," Aldric said.

Wren looked at the floor. "I sent her to your door. I thought I was saving a king. Turns out I was spending a woman who never agreed to be spent." She swallowed. "So yes. I stay. Until the ledger balances."

"Ledgers don't balance with love in them," the king said. "Believe me. I've read the arithmetic."

* * *

**Tamsin**

Ember came every day. Not to help—Tamsin could tell the child had decided against pity—but to *tell*.

Today: "Your mother's name was Sefa. She braided charms into horsehair and sang while she worked, off-key, on purpose, because she said true songs attracted attention."

Tamsin's hands stilled over the knot she was tying. "I don't know any songs."

"You know one. You hum it when you think no one's listening. You hummed it the night I knocked on your door, before you ever opened it. That's how I knew you were safe." Ember leaned forward. "Sing it now. Out loud. I'll be listening on purpose too."

And Tamsin opened her mouth, not knowing what would come out, and a melody did—a small, wandering thing, four notes up and three down—and halfway through the second line her eyes filled and spilled over, and she kept singing because stopping felt like drowning, and Ember listened with her whole body, memorizing her, keeping her.

"Why does it make me cry?" Tamsin asked when it was done.

"Because it's yours," Ember said. "Even if you can't hold it anymore. It holds you."

* * *

**Wren**

In the third month, the edicts changed. Not all of them—beast-speaking remained outlawed, the old law ink still legible under the new—but the burnings stopped. Aldric, ruling again in fact though he refused the word *restored*, issued a decree that read less like mercy than apology: *No one in my kingdom will die for the sound of their own voice.*

Wren delivered the proclamation herself, folded into the usual charm order, and watched Tamsin read it by the window light.

"They'll still be afraid," Tamsin said. "Fear outlives kings."

"Yes. But they won't be hunted to ash. That's something." Wren hesitated, then set a wrapped bundle on the counter. Inside was a scrap of cloth, scorched at one edge, embroidered with a sigil—the one Ember had carried through the dark to her door, a lifetime ago. "We found this in the palace rubble. Thought you should have it back."

Tamsin turned it over in her fingers. Her brow creased, the way it did when a song caught her. "This means something to me. Doesn't it?"

"It means everything to you," Wren said. "That's the trouble."

Tamsin looked up then—really looked, the first time since the throne room—and something moved behind her eyes like a fish under ice. "You stayed," she said. "Everyone tells me what happened. Nobody stays to explain it twice."

"I'm not going anywhere," Wren said. "Take as long as you need. I'm very patient. It's my worst quality."

For the first time in three months, Tamsin smiled with her whole face. It was a stranger's smile and it wasn't, and Wren's chest hurt, and she let it hurt.

* * *

**Tamsin**

Spring came to Vell the way it always had, indifferent to empires and parasites and the price of a soul. Tamsin learned to live beside the hollow place inside her the way you learn to live beside a missing tooth—you keep touching it, keep being surprised.

She learned other things. That she tied knots left-over-right, always. That she flinched at the smell of smoke. That when Wren entered a room, some part of her stood up straighter, attentive as a hound, and she had no idea why, only that it was the closest thing to warmth the hollow place allowed.

One evening Ember fell asleep at the worktable mid-story—the story of the night she'd knocked, retold for the fortieth time—and Tamsin covered her with a blanket and stood a long moment watching her breathe.

"Do I love her?" she asked Wren quietly.

"Yes."

"How do I know? If I don't remember?"

Wren considered it honestly, which Tamsin was learning to trust about her. "You don't know it the way you used to know things. But you feel it the way you feel weather. Some knowledge lives lower than memory. Yours went down there to survive. It's still down there. It's still *yours*."

Tamsin nodded slowly. "Then I suppose I'll wait for spring down there too."

* * *

**Tamsin**

On the last evening of the chapter of her life she couldn't read, the gray cat jumped onto the counter and sat facing her, tail curled around its feet, and looked at her with the particular attention cats reserve for moments of consequence.

Tamsin looked back. Something in her chest tilted, the way a scale tilts.

The cat opened its mouth and said, in a voice like dry leaves and warm milk, one word in a language Tamsin had never studied and had somehow never forgotten:

*"Sefa."*

Her mother's name. Her hands flew to her mouth. The word hadn't come *from* her—it had come *to* her, offered across the counter like a coin, like a gift, like a knot tied left-over-right by someone who loved her enough to leave it where she'd find it.

"I don't know what that means," Tamsin whispered. But her tears said otherwise; they arrived without permission, without story, without a single memory attached—just pure response, the way a bell rings when struck.

The cat blinked slowly. Patient. Waiting.

And Tamsin—hollowed, unmoored, holding nothing but a name she couldn't place—did the only thing that felt true. She answered.

"*Sefa,*" she said back, in the Old Tongue, in her mother's mother's tongue, in the language animals answered before kingdoms existed. "Yes. I hear you. I'm still here."

Outside, the bells of Vell rang the hour. In the doorway, Wren stood very still, watching, and Ember woke and said nothing at all, because there was finally nothing left to remind her of.

The cat purred. The shop held its warmth around them like cupped hands.

And Tamsin Villalobos, who had given away every reason she had to weep and found she could still weep anyway, reached out and scratched the cat behind its ears—and began, note by wandering note, to sing.
