## 2. The Hunt Proclaimed

The bells woke the city before the criers did.

Rafael heard them from the guildhall beneath the mapmaker's shop—six strokes instead of the usual five, a rhythm he had never once heard in thirty years of keeping accounts in that room. The sound came down through the floorboards like water through a cracked hull, and it set the ink trembling in its wells.

He was balancing the autumn ledgers when it began. Candlelight on vellum, columns of names and prices: a widow in Saltrow who had traded her shadow's weight for one winter without grief; a ferryman who paid two memories of his mother for safe passage through the Mirefen each solstice; a boy of nine whose stammer had been purchased by something patient in the deepwood, at the usual rate. Ordinary entries. Honest ones. Rafael ran his pen down the margins and found them all in order, and he told himself the bells were nothing—a funeral, perhaps, or a fire in the Tanner's Quarter.

Then came the voices.

They rose up through the grate above his desk, distorted by stone and distance but unmistakable: the crown's criers, working their rounds through Ashfall's morning streets.

*"By order of His Majesty, let it be known: those born with the old gifts, those marked by the Ninefold Court, are declared enemies of the realm. Let them be named. Let them be surrendered. Let none shelter them."*

Rafael set down his pen very carefully, as though it were something living that might startle.

He had recorded bargains for three decades. He knew every clause, every interest rate of memory and name and year-of-life that the Court exacted. He had believed—he had *needed* to believe—that the ledger was a mirror, not a wall. That recording the world's secret debts kept no one safe and endangered no one either. Invisibility was simply what careful men achieved through diligence. Through silence. Through never writing anything down that could be read aloud.

But the criers kept speaking, and the words kept falling through the grate onto his desk like ash.

*"...let it further be known that any guild, society, or brotherhood found harboring the gifted shall be dissolved, its members attainted, its goods forfeit to the crown..."*

He looked at the ledger open before him. Three hundred pages of names. Not abstractions—faces, voices, hands that had shaken his across a table in this same lamplit room. He thought of the widow in Saltrow. He thought of the boy.

He thought: *I sold myself an illusion, and I have been paying myself interest on it for thirty years.*

Upstairs, the shop bell rang—the front door, the customer entrance. Then it rang again, urgently, the way Sera Vane rang it when she didn't want to be announced properly.

* * *

Sera had run messages through Ashfall since she was eleven years old, and she knew the city the way other people knew their own hands. She knew which gutters froze first in winter. She knew that the fishmongers on Corben Street shouted loudest when they were worried about their own stalls. And she knew, within four blocks of leaving her lodgings, that today was different, because everyone was standing still.

Whole streets stood motionless in the cold, faces turned toward the criers. A woman held a loaf of bread against her chest like a child she was shielding. Two guardsmen walked the line of onlookers with their halberds shouldered—not hurrying, not needing to hurry. Men who moved slowly were men who owned the ground they stood on.

Sera counted doorways between herself and cover out of habit, and that was when she saw them bring out Mistress Ellery.

The apothecary's door stood open, and Ellery came through it sideways, dragged by the arm, her gray hair coming loose from its pins. She had bought a bargain from the Court eleven years ago—Sera didn't know the terms; almost nobody did—but everyone in the district knew her tinctures worked on wounds that shouldn't heal, and apparently now so did the crown. A neighbor stood on the step wringing her apron. Nobody spoke. Nobody moved except the guards, and Ellery, who twisted once in their grip and looked back at her own doorway as if memorizing it.

"Let it be recorded," one of the guards said loudly, for the street, "that the traitor Ellery harbored unlicensed arts."

Sera pressed herself into the shadow of a buttress and did the arithmetic she hated doing: if they were taking apothecaries in daylight, off Corben Street, in front of forty witnesses, then there was no list small enough to hide on. There was only *before* and *after*, and everyone in Ashfall was about to learn which side of the proclamation they stood on.

She should have gone home. She should have locked her door and burned her delivery routes and become, overnight, a woman who carried nothing but bread and letters about rents.

Instead she went to the provost's annex, because a courier's greatest talent is being invisible in plain sight, and because the proclamation board outside the annex would carry the full text before any copy reached the districts. While a clerk argued with a carter about a blocked archway, Sera lifted the fresh-writ proclamation off its nail and folded it into her coat, and her hands were steady until she was three streets away, and then they were not.

The guildhall beneath the mapmaker's shop was the last place anyone with sense would go. Which meant, today, it was the only place worth going at all.

* * *

Rafael opened the hidden door before her third knock.

Sera slipped inside smelling of cold air and lamp oil and fear-sweat, and she put the stolen proclamation on his desk without a word. Unfolded, it covered most of the surface. Official seal. Crown's hand. And there, in the fourth paragraph, in language drafted by lawyers or worse:

*...the so-called Guild of Hidden Accounts, keepers of unlawful compacts with powers beyond the realm, a nest of traitors operating within the city...*

"They know we exist," Sera said. "Not rumor. They know our *name*, Rafael. Who writes a thing like that? Who knows a thing like that?"

Rafael read the paragraph twice. The candle flame leaned and righted itself, and he watched it do so because watching anything else required a steadiness he wasn't sure he had.

"I've kept these ledgers for thirty years," he said finally. "Thirty years believing that what isn't spoken stays unheard. That care was armor." He touched the edge of the parchment. "I built my whole life inside that belief, Sera. And it turns out I built it inside a house with glass walls."

"So what do we do?"

It was the question he'd been circling since the bells, and every answer he owned was the old kind: move the ledgers deeper, change the routes, go quieter. Hide better. Be more invisible than ever.

He looked at the proclamation again—at *nest of traitors*—and understood, with the clarity that arrives only after a long illusion breaks, that invisibility had never been granted to him. He had *purchased* it, entry by entry, year by year, with his silence. And purchases could be seized.

"We stop pretending the ledger doesn't hold the world together," he said. "Because if they've come hunting the gifted by name, someone taught them the names. And everything I know about how names travel—" he tapped the oldest ledger on the shelf behind him, the one bound in gray leather that no client had ever been allowed to open, "—starts there."

Sera frowned at it. "What's in that one?"

"An entry," Rafael said, "that has been wrong since the day I recorded it. Thirteen years ago, a payment made by a man who should never have needed to bargain at all." He pulled the ledger down and blew dust from its cover, and beneath the dust the gray leather seemed almost to breathe.

"The king," he said quietly. "The entry was signed by the king."

Outside, distantly, another crier took up the refrain, and somewhere a door was being broken open, and Rafael Blackwood—who had spent a lifetime watching and writing and staying safely, carefully unseen—felt fear harden in his chest into something with edges.

Resolve, the old books called it. He called it the moment the mapmaker finally stepped onto the map.
