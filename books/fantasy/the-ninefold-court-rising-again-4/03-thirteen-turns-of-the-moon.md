## 3. Thirteen Turns of the Moon

The oldest ledger lived three floors below the guildhall, past the ink vaults and the room where they kept the maps that bit, in a case of black ironwood that had no lock because nothing locked by ironwood could be opened by anyone who wasn't meant to open it.

Rafael had opened it four times in thirty years.

Each time, the air of the vault changed first. It was not colder, exactly—it was *older*, a stillness like the inside of a sealed letter, like breath held for centuries. The lamp he carried burned with a flame that leaned away from the case, as if even fire understood what it was approaching.

*The First Ledger.* Not the oldest book in the world—there were older, he was sure of it, though he could not have said how he was sure—but the oldest account kept by the Ninefold Court's own hand. Every bargain ever struck between mortal and power passed through its pages eventually, the way all rivers pass through the sea. The Court did not write in it themselves. That was the mapkeepers' burden and their privilege: to transcribe, to balance, to keep the accounts straight so that the world stayed straight with them.

He set the lamp on the reading stand and drew on the gloves of undyed silk—undyed, because color was an opinion, and the First Ledger tolerated no opinions—and lifted the cover.

The smell rose up to meet him: vellum and myrrh and something beneath both, faint as a coin held too long in the mouth. The first page bore no title. It bore only nine names, written in nine hands, none of them human, arranged in a circle around a single word:

*Owed.*

"Good evening," Rafael said, because his mother had raised him to be polite even to furniture, and because somewhere in thirty years of practice he had come to believe the ledger listened.

It rustled, very slightly, in answer.

He turned to the index. The entries were organized not by date but by weight—the Court reckoned bargains the way jewelers reckoned stones, by carat and flaw—and Rafael had spent enough of his life among its headings to navigate it blind. *Bargains of blood. Bargains of name. Bargains of season and seed. Bargains of crown.*

His finger stopped there.

*Bargains of Crown* had two entries. It had always had two entries. He knew this the way he knew the number of stairs between floors, the way he knew the grain of his own desk: the guildhall's ledgers were copies of copies, but every copykeeper memorized the shape of the original, the way a priest memorizes scripture he will never be permitted to read whole. Two entries under *Crown*. One from the founding of Vessarin's line, when the first king had traded a century of harvest luck for the right to levy roads through faerie ground. One from a border war three hundred years dead.

There were now three entries.

Rafael stood very still in the lamplight. The flame leaned harder away from the book.

Thirteen years ago, the third entry said—or rather, the ledger said it in the way ledgers say things, in columns and sigils and small merciless arithmetic. A payment made. A payment received. And against the payer's name, in the Court's cold shorthand for royalty, the mark he had spent the last two days telling himself could not possibly mean what it meant.

*The king himself.*

Not an ancestor. Not a regent. The entry was thirteen years old, and Aldric Vessarin had worn the crown for nineteen. Which meant that nineteen years ago there had been no entry, because there had been no bargain, because—

Because the man who sat the throne now had bought it.

Rafael sat down slowly on the vault's only chair, which creaked under him like something waking. His hands, he noticed distantly, were steady. They were always steady. It was one of the things people praised about him, that Rafael Blackwood never trembled, and he had long ago stopped telling anyone that trembling was simply not something his body knew how to do, that some part of him had been traded or trained or hollowed into calm long before he ever learned what hollowness cost.

He began to read.

* * *

The Court's shorthand was not difficult. It was merely honest, and honesty is always difficult to look at.

A bargain of crown, the entry read. Price: one kingdom's fealty, rendered in perpetuity. Payment: renewal each turn of the moon, thirteen turns to the binding, then the debt comes due entire.

And below that, in the margin, in a hand that was not the Court's—not any of the nine hands—a note scrawled thin and hurried, like a man writing while someone watched over his shoulder:

*It does not eat gold. It eats what shines. Gifted things. Bright things. Bring me the shining ones or the thirteenth turn takes us all.*

Rafael read the note three times.

Then he closed the ledger—not slammed it, never slammed it; you did not slam doors in houses where something might be home—and sat in the dark with the lamp turned low, and thought about the proclamation Sera had stolen, the one nailed to the guildhall door with a knife instead of a nail.

*A hunt for the gifted. For the protection of the realm. All persons possessed of uncanny arts shall present themselves for assessment.*

Assessment. He had read the word a hundred times since, turning it over like a counterfeit coin. Now it rang true in the worst way. The thing on the throne was not hunting the gifted out of fear. Fear was a human organ; it did not grow in hollow places. It was hunting them the way a farmer hunts—no. The way a starving man hunts. Each lunar turn for thirteen years, something had reached out of the king's borrowed skin and taken a bright thing, a gifted soul, and fed it into the binding to renew the lease on a stolen life. And now the thirteenth turn approached, and the payments had grown thin, the gifted grown wary, hidden, scattered across a kingdom that had taught them for generations to be small—

So the crown had declared a hunt. Had flushed them into the light like birds from cover, gathered and counted and *assessed*, and every one of those assessments was a meal.

Rafael put his head back against the chair and laughed once, a short, terrible sound that the vault swallowed whole.

Midwinter. He worked it backward without wanting to, the arithmetic doing itself behind his eyes the way accounts always did. Thirteen turns from the founding entry. The thirteenth new moon fell on the longest night. Six weeks. Perhaps less.

In six weeks, either the binding would be fed to bursting and the thing on the throne would be permanent—crown and kingdom and all—or the debt would come due entire, and whatever happened to a hollow vessel when its lease expired would happen in front of the whole court, in front of the whole *country*, and gods only knew what would crawl out of the vacancy.

Or.

Or someone unwound it first.

He looked at the ledger. The ledger did not look back, which was somehow worse.

* * *

He did not sleep. He climbed the three flights to the guildhall, lit every lamp, and pulled the working ledgers—all forty years of his own careful hand—and laid them open across the great table like the wings of a vast, doomed bird.

Here was the truth he had been circling for two days, the crack running through the foundation of everything he believed: he had always told himself the ledgers recorded the world. That the mapkeeper's office was a window, not a wall. That when he wrote *a bargain struck, a debt incurred, a payment due,* he was the historian, the observer, the man safely outside the frame.

But the Court did not employ observers. The Court employed *accountants.* And an accountant does not watch the flow of money—an accountant *moves* it. Every entry he had ever balanced, every column he had ever squared, had been a thread in a loom he had never been permitted to see. Forty years of tidy sums. Forty years of keeping the world straight.

And threaded through all of it, renewed each month like a subscription paid in blood, the largest account of his lifetime: the crown's.

He could feel it now that he knew to look. When he laid his palm flat on the open pages of his own ledgers, he could feel the binding humming through them, a vibration too deep for ears, felt in the bones of the hand. Thirteen years of monthly renewals. Thirteen years of entries in his own handwriting—*payment rendered, account current, balance maintained*—each one a stitch holding a hollow thing inside a stolen king.

*Your ledgers hold it together,* whispered the part of him that had finally stopped lying. *You hold it together. You have been the jailer's pen for thirteen years and called it clerical work.*

"I didn't know," Rafael said aloud, to the empty hall, to the lamps, to no one who would forgive him.

Knowing, though—that was the terrible gift of the night. Because dread, he discovered, sitting alone at three in the morning with forty years of complicity spread before him, dread has a twin. Dread's twin is purpose.

All his life he had kept silent. Silent about the door in the unmapped alley, silent about the nine voices, silent about the realms no crown would ever chart, silent because silence was the fee and he had always, always paid his fees on time. He had told himself it was neutrality. He had told himself it was professionalism. He had told himself a great many tidy things in a tidy hand.

Now there was a task worth the silence. Worth breaking it, if it came to that. Worth spending it.

Six weeks. A binding woven of entries. An enemy that lived in the margins of his own books.

Rafael Blackwood rolled up his sleeves, took a fresh sheet, and did the only thing he knew how to do with a problem: he began an inventory. The binding's threads, listed and numbered. The renewal dates, mapped against the lunar tables. The hunters' routes, cross-referenced against the districts where the gifted had lived, before living became dangerous. Page after page, column after column, until the gray light of dawn found him surrounded by the skeleton of the thing on the throne, laid bare in ink.

At the top of the final page, he wrote the question that the whole night had been building toward, the question whose answer would cost more than he dared yet calculate:

*Where does one go to strike a bargain with the Ninefold Court?*

The lamps guttered, all together, all at once.

And in the corner of the guildhall, in the shadow beside the shelf of atlases, where no shadow should have stood at sunrise—

a door opened.
