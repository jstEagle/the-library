## 4. The Cartographer's Debt

The summons came the way all the Court's summons came: as a correction.

Rafael had been re-inking the coastline of Vhalen's Reach when he noticed it—a street in his own master map of Corvane that had not been there that morning. A narrow alley, unlabelled, running off Tanner's Row where no alley existed. He set down his pen and stared at it the way a man stares at a word he has written a thousand times and suddenly cannot read.

The Court did not knock. The Court simply redrew the world until you found yourself standing at its door.

He knew better than to ignore it. In forty years of keeping the Ninefold Court's ledgers—forty years of recording bargains between mortals and the old powers, debts measured in names and first breaths and last regrets—he had learned one law above all others: *the Court does not summon twice.* There was no second invitation because there was never, properly speaking, a first. There was only the moment you arrived, which had always been waiting for you.

Rafael closed the ledger of the king's impossible entry—the one with the payment made thirteen years ago, the one that had cracked his certainty like frost across a window—and locked it in the iron box beneath the guildhall floor. Then he took up his coat, told Master Aldous he was going out to survey drainage, and walked into the grey afternoon toward Tanner's Row.

The alley was exactly where the map said it would be. It was also nowhere. Rafael stood at its mouth and felt the city lean away from him, the noise of cart-wheels and fishwives thinning to a held breath. The walls on either side were older than the streets around them, older than the stones they were cut from—black basalt veined with something that glimmered faintly gold, like light trapped in tar. Above him the sky narrowed to a ribbon, and in that ribbon hung a moon that was not the moon over Corvane. It was full, and silver-white, and it was midwinter's moon though the season was autumn still.

*Thirteen turns,* he thought. *It has already begun counting.*

He went in.

* * *

The door at the alley's end was made of paper.

Not parchment—paper, ordinary rag-paper such as any scrivener might use, stretched across a frame of dark wood and covered in writing so dense and fine that from a distance it looked like grey silk. Up close, Rafael recognized his own hand. Every line was his. Forty years of entries, transcriptions, marginalia, corrections—all of it copied onto this single sheet in miniature, a life's work compressed into a door.

He had never seen it before. He understood it anyway. This was what the Court kept of him. This was the shape of his account.

Rafael pressed his palm flat against the paper, and the writing rose to meet his skin like iron filings to a lodestone, and then there was no door and no alley and no Corvane at all—only falling through ink.

* * *

He came to himself standing in a hall with no ceiling.

Above him, where a roof should have been, nine moons turned slowly at different heights and speeds, some waxing, some waning, one forever full. Their combined light fell without shadow, so that everything in the hall stood outlined and exact: pillars carved from whalebone and glacier-ice and petrified song; a floor of black water so still it held his reflection more faithfully than any mirror; and around the hall's circumference, nine thrones, each occupied.

He had mapped this place once. He remembered mapping it—three nights of feverish work when he was twenty-six, drawing the Ninefold Court from memory of a dream he could not prove he'd had. The map hung now in the vault beneath the guildhall, catalogued as *Realm 9: Unverified.*

Standing in it, Rafael felt the strange grief of a man proven right about the thing he most wished to doubt.

"Cartographer."

The voice came from everywhere. Or rather—from nine places at once, nine voices braided into one utterance like strands of a rope. He turned slowly. The figures on the thrones were not figures, quite. One was a woman whose body was made of tide charts, her face a shifting isobar. One was an old man composed entirely of ledger-lines, columns of figures flowing down his robes. One was a child sitting sideways on its throne, chewing thoughtfully on what appeared to be a compass needle. Others he had no language for: a smear of aurora, a hollow suit of armor filled with bees, a throne draped in mourning crepe upon which sat nothing visible except a pair of gloved hands folded in patience.

"You have found the king's entry," said the tide-chart woman, and her voice was the sound of water withdrawing from a shore.

"You have questions," said the ledger-man, in a voice like pages turning.

"You have already begun answering them yourself," said the child, around the compass needle, in a voice that made Rafael's teeth ache with nostalgia for a childhood he hadn't had. "That is why we called you. You are the only keeper in three hundred years who reads ahead of his accounts."

Rafael bowed, because one bowed to the Court even when the Court had no bodies worth bowing to. "Then you know what I found. A payment made by the crown. Thirteen years past. Renewed monthly." He straightened. "I want to know what I've been keeping bound."

Silence, of the particular quality that meant the Court was deciding how much truth a mortal could carry without breaking.

"The throne of Corvane is occupied," said the aurora-smear, in a voice like wind through high passes. "But not by a king."

"The true king sleeps," said the armored thing, bees droning in counterpoint. "Has slept for thirteen years, sealed inside his own flesh while another wears it. A bargain struck by his sorcerer in desperation—or ambition; the two wore the same face that winter."

"The binding is elegant," said the mourning hands, and their voice was soft as dust settling. "Thirteen threads, renewed at every turn of the moon. Each thread anchored in an account. Each account kept by—" the gloves turned, delicately, toward him, "—a keeper who believed himself a mere clerk."

Rafael's mouth had gone dry. "My ledgers hold it together."

"Your ledgers *are* it," said the child simply. "Every entry you recorded became a stitch. You thought you were writing down the world, little cartographer. You were sewing it shut."

He had suspected. Since the night of the audit, since the king's impossible payment, some part of him had known—and knowing it now spoken aloud was like having a bone he'd broken long ago finally set. The pain was terrible and clean.

"The hunt," he said. "The criers. The gifted being dragged from their homes."

"The binding requires feeding," said the tide-woman. "Hollow things eat what they cannot be. The gifted burn brightest. Midwinter brings the thirteenth turn, and the thirteenth turn must be fed, or the binding fails—and the sorcerer intends it shall not fail. He means to fill the king's body with so much stolen fire that the true king drowns in it forever."

"And if it *does* fail?" Rafael asked. "If the binding breaks?"

The nine voices spoke together then, for the first time, and the sound was like a chord struck on an instrument made of winters:

"*Then it must be unbound, not broken. And only a keeper can unbind. And the price is known.*"

* * *

They laid it out for him with the merciless clarity of an invoice.

The rite required a keeper to strike the thirteen entries from the binding ledger—one per turn, or all at once under the thirteenth moon—with his own hand, in his own blood-ink, sealing each deletion with a renunciation. Not of the Court. Of his *account* with it. Of everything the account contained.

"Your maps," said the ledger-man, and his voice was almost gentle, which was worse than cruelty. "Forty years of charting. Vhalen's Reach, where the drowned keep their parliament. The Glasswood, and the roads that walk at night. The Salt Cathedral. The Ninety-Nine Stairs of the Moon Market. Every realm you ever verified with your own feet and your own eyes—"

"—every realm you ever *dreamed*," said the tide-woman, "for your dreams are maps too—"

"—will close to you," finished the child, and it stopped chewing, and looked at him with eyes like two small eclipses. "You will stand before a door in an unmapped alley and feel nothing. You will hear the Moon Market's bells in your sleep and wake thinking them church bells. You will forget the way home, cartographer. All the ways home. Because home, for you, was never Corvane. Home was *here*, and everywhere like here, and we are taking it."

"Why?" The word tore out of him rougher than he intended. "Why take that? I'm striking the entries. I'm doing the work. Why must I lose—"

"Balance." The mourning gloves spread wide. "You wish to unmake a king's binding woven from thirteen accounts. Such unmaking requires an account of equal weight. What weighs more than a kingdom? Only a man's entire world. That is the arithmetic, keeper. We do not make the rules of it. We merely keep them, as you kept yours."

Rafael stood in the shadowless light and let the grief move through him like weather. He thought of the Glasswood in autumn, leaves like stained glass shattering sweetly underfoot. Of the Moon Market at midnight, where you could buy back a day of your childhood if you haggled well. Of the drowned parliament of Vhalen, arguing eternally in bubbles of silver speech. Forty years. He had crossed into his first other-realm at nineteen, half-starved, running from a father who called his gift witchcraft—and the realms had taken him in. Had *kept* him, the way the Court kept accounts. Faithfully. Forever.

And now he was being asked to trade them for strangers. For a hunted girl with frost in her palms he'd never met. For a king he'd never seen. For people who would never know his name or thank him, because the price included, he understood suddenly, being *forgotten by the realms themselves*—no door would open for him again, no threshold recognize him, no dream admit him.

Home, in exchange for belonging somewhere else.

"You offer me nothing," he said quietly, "except the chance to buy my way out of my own life."

"We offer you the only currency that matters," said the child. "Choice. The hollow king feeds at midwinter whether you act or not. The hunt goes on whether you act or not. You may keep your maps and watch, keeper. Keepers are very good at watching." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "Or you may spend everything, and become, at the last, simply a man among men. We are told—" the eclipsed eyes blinked, "—it has its compensations."

The nine thrones waited. The nine moons turned. Somewhere beyond the hall's edge, water that was not water lapped at shores that were not shores, and Rafael understood with a final, terrible tenderness that he was hearing them say goodbye already—that some part of him had agreed the moment the alley appeared on his map, and the rest of him was only catching up, the way a reader catches up to an ending he has always known was coming.

"I'll need the binding ledger," he said. His voice did not shake, and he was proud of that, and ashamed of the pride. "A copy of the original entries. And time. The thirteenth turn falls at midwinter?"

"At midwinter," said the nine voices together. "In the palace. Where the binding was woven, it must be unwoven. The Court can place you near, keeper. But the last steps, you walk alone."

The paper door reassembled itself behind him out of falling ink. Before it closed, the mourning gloves lifted in something that might have been salute or benediction, and the child called after him—not in the many-voiced chord but in a single small voice, almost human:

"When you strike the last entry, cartographer—write us a margin note. Something true. So the realms remember there was once a man who gave them up to save a stranger."

Rafael woke on the flagstones of Tanner's Row with ink on his fingers and midwinter's moon burning full in an autumn sky, thirteen days early.

The countdown had begun.
