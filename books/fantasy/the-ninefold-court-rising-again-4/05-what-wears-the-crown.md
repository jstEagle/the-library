## 5. What Wears the Crown

The palace kitchens received Sera Vane the way a river receives a stone—without ceremony, without pause, without ever truly noticing her.

She had been provisioner's assistant for four days now, wearing another woman's face of tired competence and a borrowed apron that smelled of lye and onions. The seal on her papers was good work; she had paid for it in coin she could not spare, from a man in the Tanner's Quarter who did not ask why a courier needed to walk into the lion's mouth. He had only looked at her hands—courier's hands, scarred at the knuckles, ink-stained along the right forefinger—and said, "Whatever you're carrying in there, carry it light."

So she carried it light. She stacked cabbages. She hauled barrels of salted cod up stairs that smelled of tallow smoke and old ambition. She learned the rhythms of the kitchens: the head cook's fury crested at midmorning and again before the evening meal; the scullery boys stole figs when they thought no one watched; and every servant, down to the girl who turned the spit, spoke of His Majesty in the lowered, careful voice people use for the dead.

*He weeps,* the spit-girl had whispered on the second day, scrubbing a pot with the fervor of someone who wanted very much not to be overheard. *The king weeps. At nothing. My cousin saw him in the orangery, just standing among the orange trees, crying like his heart was breaking, and then he stopped—all at once, like a tap shut off—and walked on as if nothing happened.*

Sera had thought: grief, perhaps. Madness, perhaps. Kings were made of strange pressures.

Now, on the fourth day, she carried a tray of sweetmeats and candied ginger up toward the royal apartments, because the under-steward had a limp and a fondness for bribes, and Sera had both the legs and the coin.

* * *

The corridor outside the king's study was long and hung with tapestries of hunts that had happened before anyone alive was born. Hounds leapt through frozen forests; stags died in beautiful postures. Sera walked slowly, letting the tray's weight make her gait humble, and counted doors out of habit—the way she counted exits in any room, the way Rafael Blackwood must count debts.

She had read his ledgers. That was the strange thing that kept circling back to her in the dark hours before dawn, lying in the provisioners' dormitory among women who snored like bellows. She had spent three years running messages for the guild, and she had never once been allowed past the outer room where the clients waited. But two months ago, a misdelivered parcel—a ledger page wrapped in oilcloth instead of the maps it should have held—had come into her hands, and she had read it before returning it, because she was Sera Vane, and she had never in her life left a sealed thing unread.

It had been an account. A bargain. *Received of [the name was struck through, burned away]: one favor, owing. Paid to [struck through]: safe passage, one child, across the water.* In the margin, in a hand so precise it looked printed: *Balance maintained.*

She had thought it quaint. Bookkeeping for witches.

Now she walked toward the King of Callowmere with candied ginger sweating under a linen cloth, and she understood that the ledger pages she'd seen since—the ones Rafael had let her glimpse when the hunt began, when he'd started striking entries and the hunters had come sniffing after them like wolves after blood—were not quaint at all. They were load-bearing walls.

The guards at the study door did not stop her. That was the first wrong thing. She had watched them turn away a chamberlain's own messenger that morning; she had watched them search a wine steward's basket to the bottom. But when Sera came with her tray, they stepped aside almost eagerly, the way men step aside from something they do not wish to look at directly.

"Leave it inside," one said quietly. "He likes the door closed behind you."

*Behind me.* The words settled cold between her shoulder blades.

* * *

The study smelled of ink and beeswax and, beneath both, something faintly sweetish and wrong—like fruit going soft in a bowl no one has emptied.

The king stood at the window with his back to her.

He was not what she expected. She had seen his likeness on proclamations, coins, the painted glass of the cathedral: a broad man with a soldier's jaw and a beard gone iron at the edges. This man was thinner. The proclamation portraits had weight to them, presence; this man seemed drawn in lighter ink, as if some artist had grown tired halfway through and left the shading unfinished. His doublet was magnificent. The man inside it was approximate.

"Your Majesty," Sera said, and knelt with the tray, eyes down, heart hammering against her ribs like a bird in a sack.

Silence. Long enough that she dared a glance upward.

He was weeping.

Tears ran silently down his face and dropped onto the window ledge, and his expression did not change at all. It was like watching rain fall from a clear sky. Then, all at once, the tears stopped—not fading, but ceasing, the way a lamp goes out when the wick is pinched—and the king turned around.

His shadow did not turn with him.

It stayed facing the window a half-second too long. Sera felt her stomach drop through the floor, through the foundations, through the cold earth beneath, and keep falling. When the shadow finally swung around to align with its owner, it moved the way cloth moves when shaken out—too fluid, too late, like a thing remembering its duty rather than performing it.

"You brought sweets," the king said. His voice was pleasant. His voice was a room where someone had just left.

"The cook's compliments, Majesty. Candied ginger."

"I don't like ginger." He crossed the room—his shadow lagged again at each turning, and Sera fixed her eyes firmly on the tray and thought about breathing—and lifted the cloth. He looked at the candied ginger for a long moment. "I liked ginger," he said, softer. "Thirteen years ago I liked ginger. Before the winter I can't remember."

Sera's mouth went dry. "Majesty?"

He looked up at her, and his eyes were the worst part. Not hollow—worse than hollow. *Crowded.* Like a house where too many people are pressed against the windows of a single room, all trying to look out through one pair of eyes.

"You're new," he said. "You have ink on your fingers. Couriers have ink on their fingers." And then, in a whisper pitched so low she felt it more than heard it, in a voice suddenly, desperately *different*—younger, rougher, *his*—he said:

"*Blackwood.*"

The word fell between them like a dropped knife.

The king's pleasant mask slid back down. He smiled, wide and empty as a painted grin. "Thank you for the sweets. You may go."

Sera backed toward the door. Every instinct she owned screamed at her to run, to bolt, to put the whole palace and its lagging shadows behind her—but the fragment had spoken, and she had heard it, and she understood with terrible clarity what she had heard: a drowning man using the last air in his lungs to call a name.

She paused at the door. "Your Majesty," she said carefully, "is there... a message? For this Blackwood?"

The king stood by the window with tears beginning again down his unchanged face. And from somewhere deep inside the crowded dark of him, that other voice—faint as sound through a wall, frayed as a rope's last strand—

"*Tell him. The thirteenth turn. Tell him the account comes due at midwinter, and tell him—*" a shudder went through the royal body, and the shadow rippled like disturbed water "*—tell him I know what my silence bought. Tell him I am sorry. Tell him—*"

The pleasant voice cut through like a blade through silk: "You may *go*, provisioner."

* * *

Sera walked out past the guards, down the long corridor of frozen hunts, and did not begin to shake until she reached the stairwell, where she sat down hard on a step among the smell of tallow and stone, and shook, and pressed her fist against her mouth.

Horror came first, in waves. The shadow. The tears from nowhere. The eyes full of pressed faces.

Pity came after, and stayed longer. Because she had heard the true king—there was a true king, half-drowned in his own body, thirteen years a prisoner behind his own face—and the first thing he had done with a stranger's ear was not to beg for rescue. It was to send word to a bookkeeper. To say *I'm sorry*. As if the debt were his.

And resolve came last, quiet and cold as well-water, filling the place where fear had been.

Because she knew the name. Of course she knew the name. Everyone in the guild knew it, though they said it rarely and always slightly bowed, the way you speak of weather that could kill you. Rafael Blackwood, keeper of the ledgers, mapmaker of places that weren't supposed to exist, the man who had spent the last weeks tearing threads out of the binding while hunters burned closer to the guildhall.

A stranger, then. She had read his margins and run his messages, and he had never once asked her name.

But the drowned king had trusted her with his last breath of speech, and Sera Vane had decided, sitting on that cold stair with the taste of candied ginger still in the air behind her, that she would spend it well.

By nightfall she had traded her apron for her old riding cloak, slipped out through the provisioners' gate with the guard paid to be blind, and taken the east road out of the city—toward the smuggler's safehouse below the cliffs, toward the man with the precise hand who kept accounts of impossible things.

Toward Rafael Blackwood, who would need to hear, before midwinter, exactly what wore the crown—and what still lived, whispering, inside it.
