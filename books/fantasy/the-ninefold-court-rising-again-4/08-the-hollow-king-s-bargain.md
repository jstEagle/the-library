## 8. The Hollow King's Bargain

The palace at night smelled of tallow and old stone and something else beneath—something like the air inside a sealed tomb, sweet with rot that had never been allowed to breathe. Rafael walked the eastern corridor with a forged seal of provision in his pocket and Sera's directions written in her quick, slanted hand memorized until the paper could be burned. Two guards at the second turning had looked through him as though he were furniture. That was the sorcerer's work, he knew now. Vessarion Ashgrave had woven not-seeing into the very stones, so that the courtiers could pass their hollow king each day without ever once wondering why his eyes did not blink in the candlelight.

The throne room doors stood open.

Of course they did. It wanted to be found. Thirteen years of borrowed breath, and the thing on the throne had grown tired of waiting for someone to come and ask it the only question that mattered: *what are you?*

Rafael crossed the threshold alone. The hall stretched before him, pillars rising into dark where the banners of nine generations hung motionless—no draft reached them, no wind stirred the embroidered hawks of House Aldermere. At the far end, beneath the great window of colored glass, the king sat upon the throne with his hands folded in his lap like a man at his own funeral.

Moonlight came through the window in long blue blades. Midwinter moon. One turn remaining.

"You are the ledger-keeper." The voice was the king's voice, warm and deep, and wrong in a way Rafael could not have named before this moment but could not unhear now. Every word arrived a half-beat after the mouth moved. "I have felt you writing. All these years. Little scratches in the dark, like mice in the walls of me."

"I keep accounts," Rafael said. His voice echoed strangely; the hall swallowed sound the way deep water swallows light. "Bargains struck. Debts owed. Names given and names taken."

"Then you know what I am."

Rafael stopped ten paces from the dais and made himself look up into the king's face. Up close, the horror was quieter than he'd expected. The face was handsome, weathered, human—the true king's face, worn by thirteen years of use. But the eyes behind it were like windows of a house from which every stick of furniture had been carried out. Light went in and nothing looked back.

"I know you are not Aldermere," Rafael said. "I know what sits on this throne is a vessel. A shape borrowed and bound. I know the binding renews each lunar turn, and I know the thirteenth turn falls tomorrow night." He drew the ledger from his coat—the oldest one, the one whose vellum cover had been stitched by hands that predated the kingdom—and held it up between them like a shield or a mirror. "And I know your name. Your true one. The one that was traded away."

Something moved in the hollow face. Not an expression—an *attempt* at one, grief reaching for features that had forgotten how to hold it.

"They did not tell me there would be so much hunger," the thing said. "When they poured me into him. They said a body is a coat. They did not say the coat would ache. They did not say I would feel his wife dying inside my borrowed chest and grieve her with his heart while wearing his face, and not know which of us was weeping." The hands unfolded, flexed, pressed flat against the armrests of the throne. "They did not tell me I would want to stop."

"Then stop," Rafael said. "That is why I've come. I carry an offer from the Ninefold Court."

The name landed in the hall like a dropped bell. Every banner trembled, once, though still no wind moved.

"The old powers do not make offers," the hollow thing said. "They make trades."

"All bargains are trades. This one is simple." Rafael climbed the three steps to the dais, slowly, the ledger open across both forearms like a deacon bearing scripture. "You were bound into this body by a bargain struck thirteen years ago. The terms were written in the old language and entered into the Court's books—which means the Court holds the debt. And the Court has authorized me to renegotiate."

He turned the ledger toward the throne. On the page, in ink that seemed to drink the moonlight rather than reflect it, the original entry glowed faintly:

*One vessel, emptied. One shape, borrowed. Renewed at each turning of the moon, thirteen turnings, until the vessel forgets itself entirely. Payment deferred to the crown in perpetuity.*

"Here is the new term," Rafael said. "Freedom. You walk out of this body whole and unnamed, returned to whatever country you were carved from—or remade, if remaking is possible; the Court will hear your preference. In exchange, the debt transfers. No longer deferred to the crown in perpetuity. Due *now*, payable by the crown itself, in the coin the crown holds most cheaply and the Court values most dearly: truth. The king restored will speak the binding aloud. He will name the architect. He will let the Ninefold Court stand openly in this kingdom as sanctuary for the hunted. That is the price. That is the trade."

For a long moment the hall was silent enough that Rafael could hear his own heart, and beneath it—a second heartbeat, slower, deeper, coming from somewhere under the floor, or under the world.

"You would give me a door out," the hollow thing said softly.

"The Court opens doors. It is what doors are *for*."

"And him?" The borrowed hand rose, gestured vaguely at its own chest. "The one who was poured out to make room for me?"

"He wakes. Screaming, likely. Grieving thirteen lost years. But himself, and free, and king."

The thing on the throne looked down at its hands for a long time. When it spoke again, the half-beat lag was gone, and that absence was somehow more terrible than its presence—as though the mask had slipped so far it had become the face.

"There was a garden," it said. "Before. I remember a garden with no sun in it, and nine voices arguing about the shape of rivers, and I was—" The hands closed into fists. "I don't remember what I was. That is the cruelest part of the hunger. You eat and eat and cannot remember why you were hungry."

"Then take the trade and go home and remember."

"Yes," it whispered. "Yes, I—"

"*No.*"

The word cracked through the hall like ice splitting on a winter pond. Rafael spun. At the base of the dais, where no door stood and no footsteps had sounded, a man had simply *arrived*—tall, silver-haired, robed in grey so pale it was nearly white, and carrying himself with the unhurried certainty of someone who had never once been surprised.

Vessarion Ashgrave. Court sorcerer. Architect.

"You should not have been able to enter this room," Vessarion said. His voice was mild, almost kind, and it made Rafael's skin crawl worse than any threat could have. "I wove the stones against intrusion myself. Which means you did not intrude. You were *admitted*." His pale eyes fixed on the ledger. "By that. By thirteen years of entries in the Court's own hand. Clever, Blackwood. The guild taught you well, before the guild taught you nothing worth keeping."

"The guild taught me arithmetic," Rafael said. "You taught me everything else. Whether you meant to or not."

Vessarion ascended the dais steps and placed himself between Rafael and the throne, and the temperature of the hall dropped so sharply that Rafael's breath bloomed white. "This does not concern you, keeper. You balance accounts. This is statecraft. The realm needed a king who would not die of the wasting sickness, and I provided one. Thirteen years of stability. Harvests collected. Borders held. Would you undo all of it for the sake of—one empty man and one hungry shadow?"

"I would undo it because the account is false," Rafael said, and heard his own voice steady in a way that surprised him. "Every entry in every ledger I have kept rests on this one entry, and this one entry is fraud. A vessel emptied without consent. A shape borrowed without term of return. A payment deferred by a dead man's signature—you signed as the king's physician the week before the king could no longer hold a pen. I checked. I have spent six weeks checking. You forged the consent, Vessarion. And the Court does not honor contracts built on forgery."

"The Court honors *power*," Vessarion said.

"The Court honors *balance*." Rafael stepped around him, deliberately, placing himself again in the moonlight before the throne. "There is a difference, and it is the entire difference, and it is why your binding is failing. Look at it." He opened the ledger wide and turned it toward the sorcerer. "Thirteen years of renewals, and every renewal costs more. First the gifted, quietly bought. Then the gifted, quietly taken. Now the gifted hunted in the streets, because the binding must feed faster each turn to hold the same weight. You built a debt that compounds, Ashgrave. You built it in the one language where compounding debts always, always come due. Tomorrow night is the thirteenth turning. The final payment. And when the vessel forgets itself entirely—" he tapped the entry, tapped the clause, "*entirely*"—"there will be nothing left to bind. No king. No shape. Just hunger with a crown on it, and no ledger in existence large enough to hold what it will owe."

Vessarion's composure flickered. Only for an instant—a tightening around the eyes—but Rafael had spent thirty years reading faces across bargaining tables, and he knew what he'd seen. The sorcerer knew. He had always known. He had simply believed, as powerful men always believe, that the due date would arrive on someone else's watch.

"You are threatening the crown," Vessarion said quietly.

"I am *auditing* it."

The silence that followed had weight. In it, Rafael became aware of the second heartbeat again, louder now, closer—rising through the floor, through the dais, through the soles of his boots. And beneath it, faint as a voice through a wall, something else: a whisper, threadbare and human, saying a name over and over. His name. *Rafael. Rafael. Rafael.* The true king, buried alive in his own body for thirteen years, calling for the one man whose ledgers touched every thread of his cage.

"He can hear us," Rafael said. Not a question.

"He has always been able to hear," said the hollow thing on the throne, and its voice was gentle now, and tired, and utterly done. "He has been listening to you write for thirteen years, keeper. He is the reason I wavered. Every night he whispers your name at the walls of me, and I could never understand why, until tonight." The borrowed head lifted, and the empty eyes found Rafael's, and for one impossible moment something looked *out* of them—something vast and old and grieving, asking without words for the door Rafael had promised.

Vessarion raised his hand, and the moonlight in the great window curdled from blue to sickly green. "Then we conclude this audit," the sorcerer said, "the way all disputes between accountants conclude. With a reckoning."

The banners snapped upright all at once, though still there was no wind. The doors at the far end of the hall boomed shut. And Rafael, holding the oldest ledger in the world against his chest, understood that the next few minutes would decide whether tomorrow's moon rose on a freed king or on a hunger wearing one—

—and that either way, he would need to start writing.

* * *

The first blow was not a blow at all. It was a word.

Vessarion spoke it in the old language, and the word took the shape of a chain, and the chain leapt from his mouth across the ten feet of frozen air between them and wrapped itself around the ledger in Rafael's arms, wrenching it downward with force enough to crack the dais stone. Rafael held on. His shoulders screamed. The vellum cover bit into his forearms, and where it bit, the pages began to glow—the whole book waking, entry by entry, thirteen years of recorded bargains stirring like a hive disturbed.

"You cannot read fast enough to save yourself," Vessarion said, walking forward, drawing a second word from the air. "No one can."

"I'm not reading," Rafael gasped. "I'm *citing*."

And he spoke the counter-term—the clause of fraudulent consent, the forged signature, the debt transferred—in the old language, syllable by syllable, each one dragging a thread of power out of the sorcerer's working and winding it back into the book. The chain around the ledger dimmed, thinned, frayed. Vessarion's stride faltered. For the first time in thirteen years, the architect of the binding felt the architecture shift beneath his feet.

"You dare *amend* my contract—"

"It was never yours. It was entered into the Court's books, which makes it the Court's property, which makes me its officer, and officer—" Rafael planted his heel against the dais step and hauled back on the ledger, tearing the dissolving chain apart link by link, "—officers may strike false entries. *I move to strike.*"

The hall shuddered. Somewhere below the floor, the second heartbeat lurched and quickened. The hollow thing on the throne made a sound that was not a word in any language—hope, perhaps, in its rawest form—and the green light in the window guttered.

But Vessarion Ashgrave had not survived four kings by losing arguments in his own throne room. He abandoned the chain entirely, spread both arms, and spoke a single word that Rafael knew, and dreaded, and had hoped never to hear spoken aloud:

"*Hunters.*"

Far away—through stone, through streets, through the frozen dark of the city—the palace gates began to open. And Rafael, still gripping the ledger, felt the last unstruck threads of the binding pull taut around him like wires, and heard, distantly, the sound of boots. Many boots. Coming fast.

Midwinter moonrise was hours away. He only had to survive until then.

He opened the ledger to a fresh page, uncapped his pen with his teeth, and began to write.
