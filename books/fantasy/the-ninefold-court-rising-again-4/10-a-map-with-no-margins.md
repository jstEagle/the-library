## 10. A Map With No Margins

Rafael woke to the smell of bread.

Not incense, not the cold mineral breath of the Court's deep places, not the ozone tang of a binding tearing loose—bread. Yeasty and warm and utterly ordinary, drifting up through the floorboards of some room he did not recognize, in a building he could not name, in a city that felt like a word spoken by a stranger.

He lay still for a long time, listening to his own heart, counting its beats the way he had once counted entries. Sixty-one. Sixty-two. Still there. He was still there. That much, at least, the ledgers had not claimed.

Then he tried to remember the Ninefold Court, and found nothing.

It was not forgetting as he had always imagined it—not a door closed, not a page torn out. It was as if an entire country had been lifted out of the world, and with it every road he had ever walked toward it. He reached for the door in the unmapped alley and his mind slid past it like water off glass. He reached for the nine voices, the pale galleries, the ledger of names owed, and there was only a smooth, featureless quiet where those memories had lived. Worse—he could feel the *shape* of what was missing, the way a tongue finds the gap of a lost tooth. He knew he had known. He simply no longer knew *what*.

The maps. His maps. Forty years of charting realms that shouldn't exist, inked in silver and grave-dust, folded into pockets of space no mortal hand had drawn before his. Gone. Not burned—burning leaves ash, and ash can be read. Simply gone, as though he had never held a pen for anything but taxes and coastlines.

He sat up too fast and the room tilted. A narrow bed, a window with frost ferns crawling the glass, midwinter light lying thin and white across the floor. On the chair beside the bed, someone had folded his clothes—his good coat, the one with the hidden pockets—and set his boots beneath them, toes facing the door, the way you arrange things for a guest you hope will stay.

A guest. The word snagged somewhere in his chest.

* * *

Sera Vane found him standing at the window, watching smoke rise from a hundred chimneys, and she knew at once—by the way he stared at the city, patient and polite, like a man studying a map of somewhere he'd never been—that the price had been paid in full.

"You're awake," she said.

"I'm awake." He turned. There were new lines around his eyes, or perhaps she had simply never had occasion to look this closely before. "You have me at a disadvantage, mistress Vane. I'm told I know you. I'm told I owe you."

"You don't owe me anything." She crossed the room and pressed a cup of broth into his hands, because his were trembling and he seemed the sort who would rather die than admit it. "You saved—" She stopped. Started again. "There are two hundred and eleven people alive this morning who were on the crown's lists. Children. A woman who lights lamps. A man who can make rain come when he whistles. They're alive because you tore a king's chains out of a book."

"Did I?" He said it without self-pity, which was somehow worse. "I remember the throne room. I remember fire, and your voice, and a man screaming in a voice that was finally his own. Everything before that is—" he made a small, helpless gesture, a cartographer's gesture, indicating blank parchment. "There was a place. I know there was a place. I spent my whole life there, I think. And now when I reach for it, my hands close on nothing."

Sera had carried messages through burning streets and sat all night in a smuggler's cellar listening to hunters' boots on the cobbles above, and none of it had hurt the way this quiet did. "Was it worth it?" she asked, and hated herself for asking.

Rafael considered the question honestly, which was the most Sera-like thing he had ever done. "Ask me again in a year," he said. "But this morning—the bread smells good. The frost on the glass is very fine. And I am apparently free." He looked up at her, and something in his face was raw and young. "Is that what freedom feels like? Like being unmoored?"

"No," Sera said. "That's what being *new* feels like. Freedom comes later. Usually with breakfast."

* * *

They came for him at noon—but they came gently, which no one in Vhalderan had ever done.

The palace sent a carriage with no crest, and four guards who left their swords at the door, and a summons written not on vellum but on plain paper, in the restored king's own hand: *Come as a friend. Come hungry. Come at once.*

The throne room had been changed. Rafael noticed it immediately, the way he would once have noticed a single entry out of balance. The throne itself stood empty, pushed back against the wall as though the room had decided, provisionally, to do without it. Where it had faced the hall there was now a long table, and at the table sat people—dozens of them, more arriving as the doors opened. The lamp-lighter woman. The rain-whistler. A boy with silver showing at the roots of his hair, sitting beside his mother, eating an orange with the concentration of the starved. The gifted of Vhalderan, gathered in the open, in daylight, in the seat of the power that had hunted them.

And at the head of the table, Aldric—no titles, no crown, just a broad-shouldered man with tired eyes and a scar on his palm shaped like a keyhole—rose to meet him.

"Thirteen years," the king said. "Thirteen years I watched from inside my own skull while something wore my face and signed my name. Thirteen years I screamed into the dark, and one voice heard me." He took Rafael's hand. "Yours. A name whispered down a thread of ink. You came for a stranger, knowing the price."

"I'm told I knew the price," Rafael said carefully. "I'm told I paid it gladly. I confess I'll have to take both on faith."

The king laughed—a real laugh, rough and startled out of him, the laugh of a man rediscovering the use of his own throat. Then he turned to the room, and the room went silent.

"Hear me," Aldric said, and his voice carried the way voices carry when they have been silent thirteen years. "By right of blood and by right of suffering, I decree: the Ninefold Court—" he paused, and glanced at Rafael, and something passed between them that Rafael could not read and did not need to— "the Ninefold Court is hereby made lawful sanctuary for all gifted persons within my realm. No hunter shall cross its threshold. No ledger of the hunt shall be honored. Let this be written into law this day, and let no king after me unpick it."

The boy with the silver hair dropped his orange. The lamp-lighter woman began to cry without any pretense of hiding it. Somewhere in the hall a man started whistling, softly, and Rafael felt—rather than saw—the air go sweet and heavy with coming rain.

Justice, written into law. An account balanced at last.

* * *

Evening found them on the palace roof, where the king kept a garden nobody used, under a sky rinsed clean by the rain-whistler's afternoon work. Sera brought two cups of mulled wine and found Rafael at the low wall, looking out over the city with an expression she was beginning to learn how to read: wonder worn over grief, like paint over old wood.

"They gave me these," he said, tapping his temple, when she handed him a cup. "Memories. All the ones from *this* side. Every bargain I ever recorded between mortals and—well. Between mortals and elsewhere. I remember the people. I remember what they paid, and what they feared, and what they loved. I remember every debt and every mercy." He drank. "Only the other country is gone. The roads, the doors, the nine voices. Whatever I was, there."

"Do you miss it?"

He was quiet a long time. Below them the city glowed, window by window, and somewhere a child was practicing scales badly, and the smell of bread had given way to the smell of woodsmoke, and Rafael Blackwood—who had spent forty years believing he belonged to places no map could hold—found himself belonging, entirely and without effort, to this one.

"I miss the shape of it," he said at last. "Like missing a language you dreamed in. But here's the thing I've learned today, sitting at that table: I spent my life keeping accounts for powers that never once asked my name. And yesterday two hundred strangers learned my name and said it like it meant something." He looked at her. "Home was never on the map, Sera. I think I always knew that. I just needed the map taken away to see it."

Sera reached into her coat and brought out a book. Plain-bound, undyed leather, unmarked. She had bought it that morning from a stationer's stall, haggling, because some things should be ordinary.

"A ledger," she said, "with nothing in it. First page blank. Last page blank. All the pages in between, blank." She put it in his hands. "The old books recorded what people owed to powers that didn't care about them. Write whatever you want in this one. Debts forgiven. Names saved. Recipes. Bad poetry. The price of bread."

Rafael held it the way he had once held the Court's oldest ledger—with both hands, reverently—but this time there was nothing weighing it down but possibility. He opened to the first page. The paper was good paper; it would take ink well.

"And what would you have me write first?" he asked.

Sera looked out at the city, at the sanctuary lit up below them, at the king walking his gardens alone and *whistling*, badly, like a man trying to remember how.

"Write down that it's midwinter," she said, "and the hunt is over, and everyone we know is alive. Then leave the rest of the pages for what comes next."

Rafael uncapped his pen. Beneath the date, in a hand steadier than it had been that morning, he wrote:

*Midwinter. The hunt is ended. Two hundred and eleven souls accounted for, and every account settled in full.*

*No margins on this map. We begin here.*

He signed it—not with the careful cipher he had once used to seal bargains with impossible things, but plainly, the way a man signs a letter to a friend:

*Rafael.*

And then, because the night was cold and the wine was warm and the city below was full of people learning, at last, that they were safe, he added one line more—an entry in no ledger that had ever existed before, the first record of a new age:

*Entry the first: we stayed.*
