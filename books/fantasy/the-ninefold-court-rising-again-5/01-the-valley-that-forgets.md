## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The fox was dying in the bracken behind the tannery, and Quentin Lockridge had perhaps a quarter of an hour to decide whether he would let it.

He had found it by accident, the way he found most things in Hollowmere—by listening. The valley taught its children early that sound carried: across the millpond, up the slate roofs of the weavers' row, into the ears of anyone with nothing better to do than listen for other people's sins. So Quentin had learned to listen *down*, beneath the human noise, to the smaller conversations—the gossip of starlings on the churchyard wall, the complaints of moles under the turnip field, the low continuous murmur of the river, which spoke a language older than any spoken by people and had never once told him a lie.

It was the river that had told him something was bleeding out among the ferns. Not in words. Rivers don't use words. It was more like a stain in the current of all that quiet, a wrongness he could feel behind his teeth, and so at first light, before his grandmother's kettle had begun its morning song, he had pulled on his boots and slipped out through the gap in the hazel hedge that only he and the hedgehogs used.

Now he crouched in the wet bracken with dew soaking through the knees of his trousers, looking down at a young vixen—no, not the old vixen from the ridge, this one was her daughter or her granddaughter, he couldn't be sure—with her hind leg caught in a snare meant for rabbits. The wire had bitten deep. The fur around it was dark and stiff, and each time she moved, the wound wept fresh, and she made a sound like a hinge in a house no one lived in anymore.

"Hush," Quentin said, before he could stop himself, before he remembered to be careful. "Hush now. I've got you."

The vixen went still. Not calm—still the way prey goes still when the hawk's shadow crosses the grass. Her amber eye rolled up at him, and he felt her thinking the thought every wild thing thought when it looked at him and understood what he was offering: *this is either salvation or the last mistake I will ever make.*

"I know," he said softly. "I know. But you can't chew through wire, and I can't carry you home, and if you stay here the tanner's dogs will find you by noon." He worked off his coat as he talked, draping it over her head—not to blind her, exactly, but to give her somewhere dark to be while the worst of it happened. "So here is what we're going to do instead."

What they were going to do instead required words.

Not the words of Hollowmere, which were plain as bread and safe as bread. Other words. Words his grandmother called *the old grammar*—though she said it the way one says *the plague*. Words that did not describe things but instructed them, gently, the way spring instructs the ice to reconsider. He had discovered them himself, years ago, half-buried in a lullaby his mother used to sing before she died, syllables that had never quite made sense in any tongue until the night he'd whispered them over a sparrow with a broken wing and felt the wing knit under his cupped hands like a secret being kept.

Magic, he understood now—and he understood it the way you understand a scar, without remembering the cut—did not survive in Hollowmere. Everyone knew that. The valley sat in a fold of the hills where the world had gone quiet a long time ago; the old stories said the Ninefold Court had died on the far side of the ridge, and whatever power it had once poured into bloodlines and beasts had drained away rather than cross that stony skyline. That was the whole reason families like his were here. Hollowmere was where you came when you wanted the past to leave you alone. Nothing enchanted could reach you. Which meant, Quentin had realized slowly, over many years, that anything enchanted already inside the valley—including a boy who could speak to foxes—was living on borrowed time, held together by whispering and luck.

He bent close to the vixen's ear. He checked the bracken, the treeline, the grey light thickening over the tannery roof. Then, barely louder than breath:

"*Softly, softly, the wire remembers being a willow. Willow, remember water. Water, remember letting go.*"

The words tasted like cold spring air and copper. They always did. And the wire—there was no dramatic shudder, no flash; there was only a small, tired relaxation in the metal, as though a hand that had been clenched for hours finally opened—and Quentin drew the snare loose from the ruined leg while the vixen trembled under his coat.

The wound was bad. Deeper than the sparrow's wing. He pressed both palms around it, feeling the heat of the flesh, the quick panicked drum of her heart, and said the second part, the part that cost him, that left him faint and tasting stars:

"*Mend, as the moss mends the stone. Slowly, but wholly. Be as you were before the iron knew your name.*"

Warmth ran out of him like water from a tipped jug. He knelt there swaying, watching under the edge of his coat as the torn flesh drew closed—not perfectly; there would be a pale line there forever, a thin silver seam in the russet fur—but closed, and clean, and no longer killing her.

"There," he whispered, and his voice cracked on it, because it always cracked a little, at this moment, the moment where the world was briefly right. "There. Go on, then. Before someone sees you limping toward mercy and asks questions."

The vixen nosed the coat off her head. For a long moment she simply looked at him—that long, level, ancient look that wild animals gave him and only him—and then she pressed her muzzle once against his wrist, deliberate as a signature, and vanished into the bracken without a single fern trembling after her.

Quentin sat back on his heels in the wet grass and let the shaking start. It always came after, the shaking, the price collected in installments. He watched the light come up over Hollowmere—the millpond going from pewter to pearl, smoke rising straight from forty chimneys, the bell in the little stone church preparing itself for morning service—and he felt the two feelings he always felt, sitting side by side in his chest like strangers forced to share a bench.

The first was this: *I love it here.* The valley was beautiful in the way of things that do not know they are being looked at. His whole life fit inside it like a hand inside a glove.

The second was this: *I am suffocating.*

* * *

His grandmother was waiting when he came in through the kitchen door, which she always was, which was one of the facts of the universe, like winter.

She didn't ask where he'd been. She looked at the mud on his knees, the burr in his sleeve, the particular grey shade of exhaustion that healing left on his face, and she set down the bread knife with great care, the way she did when she was choosing not to say something.

"Sit," she said. "Eat."

He sat. He ate. The bread was warm and the butter was scarce and the silence between them was the oldest furniture in the room.

"You're getting stronger," his grandmother said at last, to the window. "The healing. Each year it takes less out of you and gives back more."

"I'm careful."

"You're seventeen," she said. "Careful is a word old women use and young men ignore." She turned then, and looked at him, and Quentin saw again what he always saw in her face when they spoke of this: not anger, and not quite fear, but something worn smooth by handling, like a stone picked up and put down ten thousand times. Grief that had become habit. "Your grandfather could do what you do. Did I ever tell you that?"

"No," said Quentin, who had heard it eleven times.

"He could talk to anything with feathers. Birds loved him. He'd stand in the yard and the sky would come down to meet him." She smiled, briefly, at some private memory, and then the smile folded itself away. "And one day a king's man rode through the valley asking questions, and your grandfather stood in this very kitchen and swore to me that he had never once spoken a word to a crow. Swore it while a wren sat on the sill behind him, chattering at him like an old friend. The man believed him. Most men believe what they cannot imagine. But your grandfather never trusted the sky again. Never whistled. Never sang. He said—" and here her voice did the thing it did, thinned and roughened, "—he said a gift unused was a grief, but a gift discovered was a grave."

Quentin looked down at his hands. There was dried blood on them, vixen's blood, and beneath it the faint warmth that never entirely faded anymore, the way a banked stove stays warm in its iron.

"Why are you telling me this today?" he asked. "You tell me every year. Why does it feel different today?"

His grandmother was quiet for a long moment. Outside, a cart rattled past on the road, heading early toward the village square, which was odd—it was too soon for market. Odder still, there were voices with it, several, and they sounded less like neighbors greeting one another than like people hurrying toward something.

"Because last night," she said slowly, "I dreamed about the ridge. And in sixty years in this valley, I have never once dreamed about the ridge. The valley doesn't allow it. Something is coming up the road, child, or coming down out of the hills, and my bones woke me to say: whatever it is, the hiding place your family bled to build may not hold much longer."

From the direction of the square, faint but unmistakable across the morning water, came the sound of a hammer striking nails into wood—and beneath it, the crows of Hollowmere, dozens of them, rising off the churchyard wall in a single ragged black sheet, screaming one word over and over in their rust-hinged voices, a word Quentin alone in all the valley could understand:

"*Paper. Paper. Paper.*"
