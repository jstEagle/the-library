## 2. A Proclamation on the Church Door

The paper arrived on a Tuesday, nailed to the church door with four iron nails, and by Wednesday half of Hollowmere had memorized it.

Quentin heard about it the way he heard about most things—through birds. A jackdaw had landed on his grandmother's fence that morning, feathers ruffled with something more than wind, and croaked at him in its cracked, gossiping voice: *Paper. Nails. All the village walking to the church like crows to a dead lamb.*

His grandmother had gone pale in a way he'd never seen before. Not the soft gray of age but the white of milk spilled across her face, and she'd gripped his wrist hard enough to bruise and said only one word: *Don't.*

So of course he went.

Not immediately—that would have been suspicious, and suspicion was the one crop Hollowmere grew better than any other. He waited through the morning chores, fed the hens, split wood until his shoulders burned, and let the valley believe he was just Quentin Lockridge, twenty years old and unremarkable as rain. Then, when the church bell rang noon and drew most of the village down the lane, he drifted after them like a leaf caught in their current.

The crowd stood three deep before the church door, and Quentin was tall enough to read over the bowed heads of the miller's daughters.

The proclamation was written in a fine, official hand, black ink on cream paper, the crown's seal stamped in red wax at the bottom like a wound that wouldn't close. He read it twice, because the first time his eyes refused to accept what they were seeing.

*By order of His Majesty's Grace, let it be known throughout the shires: that those who hold speech with beasts—the abomination called beast-speaking—are traitors to the crown and enemies of God. Such persons corrupt the natural order ordained since the fall of the Ninefold Court. They are to be sought, seized, and put to question. Any soul who gives true information leading to the taking of such a traitor shall be rewarded with land and title, per the crown's generosity.*

Below that, smaller: *Witch-hunters are coming. Be ready to receive them as loyal subjects.*

Quentin read *land and title* three times, and each time the words seemed larger, hungrier, like mouths opening in the paper itself.

Land. In Hollowmere, land was everything and everyone had too little of it. The Tamworths had quarreled with the Brackens over a strip of meadow for two generations. Old Hob Merrick still told the story of how his father had lost the orchard slope in a card game and wept for a week. And here was the crown, holding out acres like a hand offering bread to starving men—and all it cost was a neighbor's life.

He looked around at the faces tilted up toward the door. He knew every one of them. He'd been born under the same gray sky as these people, drunk from the same well, buried kin in the same churchyard. The miller's daughters were whispering the proclamation's words back to each other, tasting them. Tamworth the younger stood with his arms crossed and his jaw working, doing arithmetic Quentin could almost see: *one informant, so many acres*. Even gentle Widow Crane, who had once bandaged Quentin's knee when he fell from the pear tree, was reading it with her lips moving slowly, and though her expression held no hunger, it held something worse—fear, the kind that makes people do terrible things simply to feel safe.

Nobody spoke. That was the strangest part. Thirty-odd people standing in silence before a death warrant written in general terms, and not one of them saying *this is monstrous*, or even *this is nothing to do with us.* Their silence had a texture to it, thick and warm, like the air before a storm. Everyone was thinking the same private thought, and no one would say it aloud because saying it aloud might summon the very thing they feared—or wanted.

*They're counting heads,* Quentin thought. *Right now, without knowing it, they're all counting heads. Wondering whose head doesn't fit.*

And then, with a sensation like cold water poured down the back of his neck, he understood: he was the head that didn't fit. He had always been. The valley hadn't been hiding him all these years. It had been *saving him up*.

A crow sat on the church roof ridge, watching the crowd with one glossy eye. When Quentin glanced at it, it dipped its head—a small, deliberate motion—and croaked, low enough that only someone who listened could catch it: *Bad paper. Bad nails. Burn it, boy, burn it while they sleep.*

*I can't,* he answered silently, the way he answered all of them, with meaning rather than sound. *Burning it tells them exactly where to look.*

*Then look away first,* said the crow, which was either wisdom or bird-brain nonsense, and flew off toward the ridge.

*

He walked home the long way, along the river path, because the long way passed the willow stand where the animals came to him.

They were already waiting. They always seemed to know. A hedgehog sat in the leaf mold like a small armored elder; two magpies paced the mud flat with their heads cocked; the old brown hare that had lost an ear to a snare years ago—he'd healed the wound with whispered words when he was nine, and the hare had never forgotten—sat upright at the edge of the grass.

*Well?* asked the hare, in the blunt way hares had. *What does the paper say?*

Quentin sat down on a root and told them. He gave it to them straight, the way they deserved: that speaking with beasts was named treason; that hunters were coming with instruments he didn't want to imagine; that any neighbor could sell his name for a field.

The silence among them lasted longer than the silence at the church.

It was the hedgehog who finally spoke, in its dry rustling voice: *We wondered when the humans would remember what they did a hundred years ago. They forgot the Court. They did not forget how to fear it.*

*The Court?* Quentin frowned. His grandmother had taught him never to ask about the Ninefold Court—had gone strange and hard whenever he'd tried, so many times that eventually he'd stopped trying. But the animals spoke of it sometimes, in fragments, the way old men speak of a war they survived. *What does the Court have to do with a piece of paper on a church door?*

*Everything,* said the magpie, and both birds said it together, which magpies found very satisfying. *Everything, everything. The paper is only the shadow. The crown fears the thing that casts it.*

*You talk in circles.*

*We talk in centuries,* said the hare. *You'll learn the difference soon enough, I think. But circles won't save you now, boy. What will you do?*

That was the question, wasn't it. Quentin looked down at his hands—chapped knuckles, dirt in the creases, hands that had healed foxes and calmed panicked cattle and talked a drowning dog out of the river. Traitor's hands, apparently. He'd hidden the gift his whole life, wrapped it in silence the way you wrap a blade in cloth, and it had worked, it had worked for twenty years—

Except it hadn't been working. It had been waiting. There was a difference, and the proclamation had drawn the line between them in red wax.

*I don't know,* he admitted. *Run, maybe. Except where? Every road out of this valley leads to somewhere the king's word reaches.*

*Roads,* said the magpie, and then stopped, and the two birds exchanged a look with such weight behind it that Quentin felt the air change. *There are roads,* the nearer magpie said carefully, *and there are roads.*

*Meaning what?*

*Meaning,* said the hedgehog, *that some of us remember things the maps were made to forget. But that is a conversation for someone older than we are, child. Someone dying, if truth be told, and glad of it. Go home. Feed your grandmother's fire. And start deciding what you can carry.*

*

That night he couldn't sleep.

He lay in his narrow bed under the eaves listening to the house settle, and beyond it the valley settling too—the river muttering, an owl asking its one eternal question, wind combing the rye. Ordinary sounds. Safe sounds. Sounds he had taken for granted every night of his life, the way you take water for granted until someone tells you the well is poisoned.

His grandmother sat downstairs. He could hear the creak of her chair, the tick of her knitting needles going too fast, and once, very quietly, the sound of her crying—not sobbing, just leaking, the way old grief leaks out of old people when it thinks no one is awake to see.

He thought about the neighbors' faces at the church door. He kept returning to Wren—her absence from the crowd, actually, she hadn't been there, and that frightened him more than anything, because Wren missed nothing, Wren was always first to any gathering, first with news, first with opinions. If she'd stayed away, it was because she already knew what the paper said. Or because she couldn't bear to look at it.

Or because she was looking at something else. Someone else. Him.

No. He pushed the thought away and it crawled back like a hedgehog into the same gap. Wren had known his secret since they were children, since the day he'd talked her out of a panic when a bull chased her into the brook and she'd turned to find him calmly telling the animal to go home, and the animal had *gone home*. She'd kept it for ten years. Ten years was a fortress.

But the proclamation offered land and title, and ten years was also a debt, and debts got heavy, and the crown paid well.

*Stop it,* he told himself. *She'd sooner cut off her own hand.*

But the witch-hunters were coming, and witch-hunters did not need informants to be wicked—only to be frightened, or tired, or greedy, or merely mistaken. And everyone was mistaken about something.

Somewhere past midnight, the moon rose above the ridge line, and Quentin noticed—for the first time in his life, or perhaps just the first time he'd thought to count—that it was not full, and would not be full for some turns yet. He found himself wondering, absurdly, how many turns it had left. How many nights remained between this ordinary darkness and whatever darkness was riding toward Hollowmere with the hunters.

In the morning, he made himself a promise in the language he used with animals, which was not words exactly, more a shape of intent pressed into the chest: *I will not be taken quietly in my own valley. If I must run, I will run toward something, not merely away.*

He just needed to learn what lay toward.

At dawn, gray light seeping through the shutters, he heard a scratching at the kitchen window—and beneath it, faint as smoke, a voice he knew, thin and old and urgent, calling his name.

The old vixen had come down off the high scree, and she had come to tell him about roads.
