## 4. What Wren Saw

Wren Ashdown had always known that Hollowmere kept its secrets the way a pond keeps stones—visible only when the light was right, and never all at once. She had grown up on the same lane as Quentin Lockridge, had waded the same cold streams, had learned to read his silences the way other girls learned to read letters. So when she saw him crouched in the alder shade behind the tannery, head tilted toward three crows perched on the fence rail, she knew at once that this was not a boy idling away an afternoon.

She knew it the way you know a wound is deep: by the quality of the stillness around it.

The crows were not afraid of him. That was the first wrong thing. Crows feared everything—dogs, children, shadows of hawks, the memory of thrown stones. These three sat within arm's reach of Quentin as though he were a fence post they had grown fond of, and their heads turned when he spoke, and their eyes—black beads stitched with starlight—did not blink.

Wren stopped with her basket of dyed wool half-lifted and did not breathe.

He was talking to them.

Not *at* them, the way farmers talk at weather or at stubborn mules, filling the air because silence is worse. He was talking *to* them, and they were answering—not in words, but in that low rattling croak that crows use among themselves, one, then another, then the third, each sound arriving like a sentence in a conversation Wren could not follow but could not deny. Quentin laughed at something the middle crow said—a real laugh, unguarded, the laugh he never let out in front of people—and the sound of it went through her like a hook through cloth.

*Say something,* she told herself. *Call his name. Make it ordinary.*

But her voice had gone somewhere far away, down some hole in her chest, and while she stood there frozen, the largest crow spread its wings, dipped its head to Quentin almost formally—the way a courtier bows, she thought absurdly, the way a herald acknowledges a lord—and lifted off into the grey morning. The other two followed. Quentin watched them go with an expression Wren had no name for: grief and joy wearing each other's clothes.

Then he turned and saw her.

For one heartbeat neither of them moved. The world narrowed to the space between them, ten paces of trampled mud and wood-shavings, and in that space Wren watched him understand what she had seen. His face did not go white. It went *old*. It went old the way her grandmother's face had gone old on the day the tax-man came, the way faces go when a thing long dreaded finally puts its hand on your shoulder.

"Wren," he said.

"Quentin." Her own voice sounded borrowed. "I—I was just passing."

"You weren't just passing." He rose slowly, brushing bark-dust from his knees, and came toward her with his hands open, palms out, the way you approach a frightened animal. The irony of it struck her even through her fear. "You've known since we were nine that I'm strange. You've seen me do things. You've never—" He stopped. Started again. "Please don't look at me like that."

Like what? Like you're a stranger? Like you're a story my mother told me to frighten me straight? Like you are exactly what the proclamation says?

Because that was the other thing, the thing sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone. Three days ago the crown's men had nailed a decree to the church door, and the whole valley had come to read it, and Wren had read it twice. *All who hold speech with beasts are traitors to the realm.* *Informants will be rewarded in land.* She had stood in the crowd and felt the hunger move through it like wind through wheat—good, honest, desperate people, people she'd known all her life, reading about land, about fields for sons and dowries for daughters, and she had thought: *we are all capable of anything.*

She had not known she was thinking of herself.

"I have to go," she said.

"Wren—"

"I have to go, Quentin." And she went, faster than walking, slower than running, her basket bumping against her hip, the wool inside it bleeding color onto her apron where her damp hands gripped it. Behind her she heard him say her name once more, softly, and then not at all, and somehow the softness was worse than shouting would have been.

* * *

That night she could not sleep, and so she went to the chest.

It sat under her bed the way such chests sit under beds in every house in Hollowmere: heavy, locked, spoken of never. Her mother had given her the key on her deathbed along with instructions that amounted to *never*, and Wren had obeyed for eleven years. But obedience is a door that fear opens from the inside, and tonight her hands shook so badly she dropped the key twice before the lock gave.

Inside lay the things she had been promised she would never see. A ring of braided silver and black horn, too large for any finger she owned. A scrap of banner, moth-eaten, embroidered with a sigil she had spent her childhood pretending not to recognize whenever it surfaced in old stories: nine seats arranged in a circle, and at the crown of the circle a tenth place that was not a seat at all but a doorway, drawn open. A letter, sealed with wax gone the color of dried blood, addressed to a name that made her breath catch:

*To the Keeper of the Ninth Seat, in the year of the Court's going.*

Her family name was Ashdown. But the letter was addressed to *Ashdún*—and beneath it, in a different, older hand, someone had written: *the last who kept the Door's account.*

Wren sat on the cold floorboards with the letter in her lap and understood, with the slow terrible clarity of midnight, that she had always known. Not the facts—she could not have recited them, could not have said what the Ninth Seat was or what a Keeper kept. But she had known the shape of it, the way you know the shape of a room you grew up in even blindfolded. Why else had her mother flinched at the word *court*? Why else had the family never sung the old songs, never danced the circle-dance at harvest, never spoken of the years before Hollowmere? Why else had her mother pressed the key into her hand and whispered, "If ever the hunts come, remember whose blood quieted itself to survive"—and died before she could say more?

She broke the seal. The letter inside was short, the ink browned with age, the handwriting cramped and urgent:

*They are burning the courts. The Ninefold has fallen, and the tenth door—the Door between—will be closed from the far side. Someone must remain to keep its account, to remember it exists, so that when the moon turns thirteen times it may be found again. We drew lots. Our line lost, which means our line lives, which is the cruelest kind of winning. Forget us. Forgetting is the price of safety, and we pay it gladly, and we pay it forever.*

*But if you are reading this, then forever has ended.*

Beneath the signature, almost as an afterthought, in ink fresher than the rest—as if added later, perhaps by her mother, perhaps by her mother's mother—ran a single line:

*The Lockridges carried what we could not.*

Wren read that line four times. The Lockridges. Quentin's family. The family that had come to the valley two generations back, quiet people, careful people, people who kept their shutters closed at night and their voices low. The family everyone politely assumed were simply shy.

*The Lockridges carried what we could not.*

Carried. Not kept, not guarded—*carried*, the way you carry a coal wrapped in ash, the way you carry a debt. And now she understood why Quentin spoke to crows and foxes and the marsh-herons no one else could approach, why the animals of Hollowmere came to him calm as lambs, why her mother had watched the Lockridge boy across the market square with an expression Wren had mistaken for disapproval and now recognized, with a lurch, as *hope*.

And she understood, too, what she had done—or nearly done—by standing in the alder shade with her mouth full of unsaid words, by walking away instead of calling his name, by letting him see her face go old and afraid. Because there were ears everywhere in a valley the week after a proclamation. There was a woman named Maren Cobb who had already asked the reeve, loudly and publicly, whether "land" meant good plowland or just pasture. There were boys who followed strangers home for the pleasure of knowing where strangers lived. And Wren Ashdown had been seen leaving the place where a Lockridge had been caught doing the very thing the crown had promised to burn.

By tomorrow, someone would ask her what she'd seen.

By tomorrow night, someone would decide for her.

* * *

She went to his house before dawn, while the mist still held the valley in its cupped hands, meaning to warn him. She had rehearsed it all night: *I saw nothing. I'll swear I saw nothing. If they ask, I'll tell them you were talking to yourself, that you do it all the time, that grief makes people strange.* She had rehearsed the lie until it sat comfortably in her mouth, and she believed in it the way you believe in a rope bridge—enough to step onto, not enough to stop trembling.

Quentin wasn't home.

His grandmother was, and the old woman opened the door before Wren could knock a second time, as if she had been standing behind it all night waiting for exactly this visit. Her eyes went to Wren's face and then, sharply, to Wren's hands—to the ink stain on her fingers where the old letter had bled against her skin.

"You've opened the chest," the grandmother said. It was not a question.

"How did you—"

"Ink remembers," the old woman said, which made no sense and perfect sense at once. She stepped aside to let Wren in, and the warmth of the kitchen swallowed her, bread-smell and peat-smoke and the particular hush of a house where important things are never said aloud. "Sit. Eat nothing; there's no time. Quentin went out before light. The vixen is dying—the old red one from the north slope, the one he's tended since it was a kit—and he would not let it die alone." Her voice caught on the last words, and Wren saw, with a jolt, that the grandmother was not merely worried. She was grieving in advance. "Listen to me, girl. Hunters crossed the ridge yesterday evening. Two of them, maybe more, dressed as surveyors. They'll be at the church by noon, and by noon they'll have names, and by dusk they'll have my grandson unless—"

"Unless what?"

The old woman looked at her for a long moment, and in that look Wren felt weighed, measured, found either sufficient or wanting—she could not tell which.

"Unless someone tells them a lie so complete it becomes the truth," the grandmother said. "Can you do that, Wren Ashdown? Keeper's blood, liar's tongue—your family was always better at the second than the first. Can you stand in front of the crown's men and swear on whatever they put in front of you that you saw a boy talking to himself in the alder shade, mad with grief for his dead father, harmless as a wet hen?"

"Yes," Wren said. "I can do that."

It was true. She meant it wholly. She would have sworn it on the altar stone, on her mother's grave, on the old broken banner hidden in her chest.

Which was why, an hour later, standing in the churchyard as the hunters' boots rang on the frost-hard ground, watching the crowd gather hungry as gulls, hearing the chief hunter call for witnesses in a voice like a file drawn across iron—she could not explain, afterward or ever, why her feet carried her forward instead of back.

Why her hand rose.

Why she heard her own voice, clear and steady and carrying across the churchyard, say: "I have something to report."

Later—much later, in the country with no name, in the shadow of the Thirteenth Door—she would insist until her dying day that she had stepped forward to lie, to bury Quentin in the safest lie she could build. And perhaps she had. Perhaps the words that left her mouth were even the ones she intended.

But the chief hunter wrote down what she said, and what she said was this:

"The Lockridge boy speaks with beasts. I saw it myself. Behind the tannery, at morning. He was giving them orders."

And the hunter smiled, and thanked her, and asked her name for the record, and paid her in the only coin informants receive before the land comes due: a nod, a mark in a ledger, and the promise that she would be remembered.

That night, Wren Ashdown did not sleep. She sat on her floor beside the open chest with the ninth-seat banner across her knees, and she listened to the dogs of Hollowmere begin, one by one, to howl.
