## 5. The Betrayal

The dogs had been howling half the night, and by the hour before dawn they had stopped all at once, and that silence was worse.

Quentin lay in his loft bed with his eyes open, listening to it. In Hollowmere, silence was a garment everyone wore; it was stitched into the valley, hemmed at the ridgeline where the old magic could not pass. But this was not that silence. This was a held breath. The dogs had not gone to sleep—they had gone to ground, the way animals do when something walks through their world that smells of iron and purpose.

He thought of Wren. He had thought of her all night too, though he could not have said why—only that after he'd left the church square yesterday he had felt watched, the way a hare feels watched before it ever sees the hawk. He kept seeing her face as she read the proclamation, her lips moving over *informants will be paid in land*, and the way her eyes had flicked up and found him across the crowd, and then just as quickly away.

She wouldn't. He told himself she wouldn't. She was Wren, who had shared stolen plums with him since they were six; who knew about the foxes and the crows and the winter he'd spent whole evenings talking to the mill-pond ice because the fish beneath it were lonely; who had never once, in eleven years of friendship, looked at him the way the neighbors had looked at the proclamation.

But she had looked at him like that yesterday.

The knock came while the sky was still the color of ash.

Not a knock—a battering, fists on wood, and then his grandmother's door groaning open without anyone answering it, and boots on the floorboards below. Quentin sat up so fast the bed frame cracked against the wall. Downstairs, a voice like oiled leather said, "Search the house. The old woman can watch or she can be made to watch."

His grandmother's voice answered, steady and cold as well-water: "There is nothing here but an old woman and her loom."

"There's a boy," the voice said. "There's always a boy."

Quentin was already moving. He had planned this escape for two days now—badly, desperately, in the snatched hours between chores—but planning meant nothing when the moment arrived, and the moment had arrived with boots on his grandmother's floor. He pulled on his coat with the bread and cheese already sewn into its lining, took up the walking stick he'd cut from alder two nights ago because the old vixen had told him, *the marsh paths answer alder*, and went to the window.

Below, in the gray light, he saw them: five men in the crown's colors, drab red and black, with long poles tipped in silver wire and a sixth figure standing apart in the lane, hooded, hands folded, waiting. Witch-hunters. Real ones, not the tax-collectors who sometimes wore the title as a threat. Their leader carried a lantern with no candle in it that nevertheless threw light, a sickly yellow glow that crawled along the ground like something alive looking for holes to pour into.

And beside the leader, giving quiet instructions in a low voice, stood a figure whose silhouette Quentin knew the way he knew his own hands—the set of the shoulders, the restless shift of weight from foot to foot.

No, he thought. No, no, no.

The figure turned its head toward the house, and even through the hood he heard the voice say, "The loft. He sleeps in the loft. There's a window facing the orchard."

Wren's voice.

It didn't sound like her. That was the terrible thing—it sounded exactly like her, the same low rasp she got when she was nervous, the same habit of swallowing before hard words—and Quentin's whole body rejected it, some animal part of him refusing to file that sound under that name. His mind scrambled for any other explanation. A trick. A sister. A stranger wearing her coat. Anyone.

But the hunters were already on the stairs, and there was no time for explanations, and perhaps that was mercy.

He went out the window.

* * *

The orchard grass was wet and cold and came up to his chest, and Quentin ran bent double through it while behind him the loft door burst open and men shouted his name—not his name, actually, but *Lockridge boy*, as if the name itself were the crime. The lantern-light swept the trees above him, hunting. He reached the hedge at the orchard's edge and put his lips close to the dark tangle of bramble and whispered, "Open, please, please open," which is nonsense to say to a hedge, except it wasn't nonsense, because the hedge had known him since he was small, since the first time he'd crept out to heal a broken-winged blackbird and the hedge had heard him speak to the bird and had, somehow, understood what he was.

The brambles drew apart like fingers uncurling.

He went through and the hedge closed behind him and he heard one of the hunters swear and strike it with a pole and the thorns hold fast.

Then he ran.

* * *

The marshes began south of the village, past the tannery with its stink, past the last crooked fence where the valley gave up trying to be farmland and surrendered to reed and black water. Quentin hit the fence line at full speed and did not slow down, because slowing down meant thinking, and thinking meant Wren's voice saying *there's a window facing the orchard*, and he could not afford to think. Grief was a luxury. Grief was a thing you bought with time, and time was being spent behind him by men with silver wire.

The marsh accepted him the way it accepted everything—without judgment, without ease. Cold water sucked at his boots. Reeds taller than his head closed overhead, slicing the gray sky into ribbons. Somewhere behind him, faintly, the hunters had reached the fence line and were arguing; he heard the word *dogs* and his stomach clenched until he remembered that every dog in Hollowmere had been howling all night and then had gone silent, and he understood, suddenly, why. They hadn't been warning the village.

They'd been telling *him*. And then they had stopped, so the hunters would have nothing to follow.

"Thank you," he gasped to the reeds, to the water, to the invisible dogs, and the word *thank* seemed to move through the marsh ahead of him like a ripple.

They came to him one by one, the ones he had helped. It was the only map he had. The old vixen's debt had sent him north in his plans, but plans dissolved in knee-deep water, and so instead he followed the creatures themselves. A heron rose out of the mist ahead and stood waiting on a hummock until he caught up, then lifted again, unhurried, a pale flag in the fog. When the heron's path ended at a channel too wide to wade, an otter surfaced beside him, whiskered and bright-eyed, and chittered, *"This way, this way, the deep path, the safe path"*—and swam alongside while Quentin floated on his back with his bundle held above the water, laughing once, wildly, because it was absurd and wonderful and he was going to die or he was going to live and either way an otter was guiding him like a boatman.

*"You healed my kit two winters back,"* the otter said. *"Fish-bone stuck in her throat. You talked it loose."*

"I remember," Quentin panted.

*"We all remember. That's your trouble, Lockridge boy. You thought nobody remembered. Everything remembers you."*

Behind him, distantly, hounds gave voice—one bay, then another, then a chorus. They had brought dogs after all, dogs from outside the valley, dogs that did not know him. The otter's ears flattened. *"They'll find the scent at the fence. You have perhaps an hour. The ridge is three hours at a man's pace."*

"Then show me how not to go at a man's pace."

The otter laughed, a barking, bubbling sound, and dove.

* * *

They moved him like a relay of ghosts. The otter took him through the flooded channels where the hunters' dogs would lose the scent in water. At the marsh's northern edge a badger was waiting—healed once, Quentin vaguely recalled, of a snare-wound, years ago, a badger he'd barely helped and had never expected to see again—who led him through a tunnel system under an abandoned peat-cutting, through the dark and the dripping earth, emerging on the far side into a stand of birches where crows dropped out of the sky around him like falling leaves.

*"We saw the riders on the east road,"* said the oldest crow, the one with the crooked wing he'd splinted the autumn before last. *"Five men, two dogs, and the hooded one. The hooded one rides apart. Even the horses don't like her."*

*Her.* Quentin's throat closed. He made himself ask: "Did you see her face?"

*"Hoods are for faces we're not meant to know,"* the crow said, and cocked its head at him with one gold eye. *"You know it already. We can smell it on you. Grief has a smell, Lockridge boy. Like green wood burning."*

"Don't," Quentin said. "Don't say it. If I don't say it, it isn't true yet, and I have to run for three more hours, and I can't carry it and run."

The crow regarded him for a long moment. Then it said, gently, for a crow, *"Carry her memory north instead. That's lighter. That's the debt you owe the vixen anyway. Two memories weigh less than one grief."*

And Quentin, standing in the birch wood with the hounds' voices threading through the trees behind him, found that his legs worked again. He shouldered his stick and ran.

* * *

The ridge rose out of the morning like the spine of a sleeping animal, dark fells climbing into cloud, and Quentin climbed it with his lungs full of knives and his heart hammering a rhythm he no longer controlled. Twice the dogs came close enough that he heard men's voices between the bays—*"he's here, he's here in the scree"*—and twice the mountain itself seemed to mislay him, a fall of stone shifting, a shadow deepening, the crows rising in noisy diversion off a false trail to the west. Every animal he had ever helped, he thought, was spending itself for him today, calling in debts he had never known he'd opened. A stoat he couldn't even remember. A lame ewe. A hive of bees whose queen had been dying, whom he'd sung back to life with words no one must hear.

At the top of the ridge, where the wind came clean and cold off the empty country beyond, Quentin stopped and turned and looked back down into Hollowmere one last time.

The valley lay in its bowl of hills, smoke rising straight from chimneys, fields stitched neat and green, the church spire thin as a needle. It looked exactly as it had looked every day of his life. It looked like nothing was wrong. It looked like a place that had practiced forgetting for so long that it had forgotten how to do anything else. Somewhere down there was his grandmother's loom and his narrow bed and the tannery wall where he'd first spoken to foxes, and somewhere down there was Wren Ashdown, and he let himself feel one second of it—one second, no more, the full weight of it, her voice saying *the loft, he sleeps in the loft*—and then he put it down the way you put down a load too heavy to carry farther, and marked the spot, and turned away.

"You can come back for it later," he said aloud, to himself, to the wind. "If there is a later."

Then he crossed the ridgeline.

Magic touched him like a wave breaking over a drowning man.

He had no word for what it was—all his life it had been the thing that *stopped* at the ridge, the absent thing, the reason Hollowmere was hollow—and now it was everywhere, in the air and the ground and the light, thick as water, and it poured into him through his skin and his breath and the soles of his feet and it *recognized* him. That was the horror of it. It didn't just touch him; it knew him, it called him by a name that wasn't Lockridge, it rushed toward some hollow place inside him that he had never known was shaped like a throne.

He fell to his knees on the bare fell-top. The world went white and ringing. He heard—no, he *felt*—voices, hundreds of them, thousands, the land itself speaking, the dead kingdom's roads and ruins and roots all turning toward him at once like faces toward a herald's trumpet, and underneath it all, vast and patient and terribly sad, something ancient stirred that had been waiting a hundred years for exactly this weight on exactly this threshold.

*The Ninth Seat knows its blood,* said everything, at once, in no language and all of them.

Quentin screamed, or laughed, or both, and clawed his way forward on hands and knees, down the northern slope, out of the crest of it, and the pressure eased—not gone, never gone now, he understood that, it would be in him forever the way a nail is in a board—but bearable. He knelt in the heather shaking, bleeding from the nose, and looked up.

Before him the land fell away into a country that was not on any map: a wide dark valley of broken towers and roads that glimmered faintly silver, winding where roads should not wind, moving. Mist pooled in it like milk in a bowl. And on the mist, here and there, shapes moved—antlered, tall, watching.

From the heather at his elbow, a fox spoke—the young dog-fox who'd followed him up the fell, panting, having refused at the last moment to stay behind.

*"Well,"* it said. *"You're through. Welcome to the country with no name, heir-blood."*

Behind them, far below on the southern slopes, the hounds had fallen silent. The witch-hunters had stopped at the ridge line. Quentin understood, with a lurch, that they would not give up—that *she* would not give up, the hooded one, whose voice he knew better than his own heartbeat.

He had crossed out of the world that forgot. Whatever followed him now would be following him into the world that remembered everything.
