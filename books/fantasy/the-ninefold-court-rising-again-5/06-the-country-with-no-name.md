## 6. The Country With No Name

Quentin had thought the ridge would be the worst of it. He'd been told—no, he'd always known, in the wordless way children in Hollowmere know things—that magic died at the ridge line, that whatever touched the valley was ground down to nothing by the time it reached the fields. What no one had told him was what it felt like from the other side: not a wall keeping magic out, but a dam holding back a sea. When he crossed the stones at the top of the pass, soaked to the ribs with marsh water and shaking from three days without sleep, the whole weight of that sea came over the top of him at once.

He fell to his knees on the northern slope and was sick into the heather. The world roared. Not with sound—with *presence*. Every blade of grass had a voice here, or the ghost of one; every stone muttered in its sleep. The foxes of Hollowmere had spoken to him plainly, simply, in short bright sentences. But this country spoke the way a cathedral speaks: everything at once, layered, ancient, indifferent to whether he could bear it.

"Too much," he gasped aloud, and heard his own voice come out cracked and small. "It's too much."

A raven dropped out of the grey sky and landed on a boulder beside him. It was not one of his crows—he knew his crows the way he knew his own hands—but it looked at him with an expression of frank appraisal, like a landlord examining a tenant.

*You're loud,* it said. *For someone so quiet.*

"I don't—" Quentin pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "I can't sort it. Everything's talking."

*Then stop listening with your ears.* The raven hopped closer, head cocked. *You crossed the border of Erasthel. This is the country with no name because the men who burned its maps gave it no name worth keeping. Here, speech is older than breath. You'll have to learn to hear the way the dead hear—or you'll go mad before nightfall, and I'll eat whatever's left.*

"You're very kind," Quentin said weakly.

*I'm honest. It's kinder.* The raven ruffled its feathers. *Follow me. There's shelter below, if the roads are willing.*

"The roads are willing?"

The raven gave him a look that said, with great patience, *you'll see*, and lifted off northward.

* * *

The roads were willing.

That was the only way Quentin could describe what happened as they descended into the first valley of the erased kingdom. The track they followed began as ordinary mud and broken paving—but twice that afternoon, when the raven led him toward a ravine, the road bent. Not visibly, not all at once, but the way a river bends: gradually, patiently, so that by the time Quentin realized the ravine was now behind them and the path ahead ran smooth and dry through open country, he couldn't say when it had changed. The third time, he felt it—a long muscular shudder under his boots, like a horse shifting its weight.

"Sorry," he said automatically, to the road.

*Don't apologize,* said a voice beneath his feet, deep and slow as groundwater. *Apologies weigh. Just walk kindly.*

He walked kindly for the rest of the day.

Toward evening the land opened, and Quentin saw the first ruins of the Ninefold Court.

He had expected rubble. He had imagined, without knowing it, something like the ruined mill outside Hollowmere—broken walls, weeds, sheep grazing among the stones. What he saw instead stopped him mid-stride with a pain in his chest like homesickness for a place he'd never been.

It had been a palace once—no, more than a palace. A court. Terraces cascaded down a hillside toward a lake gone silver-grey with reeds, and every surface still held carvings: beasts in procession, beasts in counsel, beasts crowned. Fox and heron and wolf and stag, each rendered with such dignity that they seemed less decoration than portraiture. A century of rain had softened the edges but not the intent. Whoever had built this place had loved these animals—not as servants, not as sport, but as *peers*.

"It's beautiful," he whispered.

*It was,* the raven said. *Look again. Look with your new ears.*

And looking, listening, Quentin heard what the beauty was made of. The terraces weren't silent. Every carved beast still held its note, faint as a finger's pressure on a struck bell—the foxes still speaking their quick counsel, the herons their cold judgments, the wolves their long loyalties. Not alive, exactly. But not finished either. Waiting the way a song waits inside a shut piano.

"They're still in there."

*Echoes. Courtiers who wouldn't leave when the Court fell, so they became their own monuments.* The raven settled on a fallen lintel carved with nine thrones. *Some call it haunting. In Erasthel we called it attendance.*

"What happened to them?" Quentin asked, though some part of him already dreaded the answer. "The Court. The people."

The raven was quiet a long moment. Wind moved through the reeds by the dead lake.

*Men came with fire and lists,* it said finally. *Lists of names. Anyone who spoke with beasts, anyone who sheltered them, anyone whose grandmother had been seen trading words with a wren. They took the courtiers first, then the speakers, then the ones who merely hadn't denounced their neighbors quickly enough. And the beasts of the court—they didn't fight, at the end. They walked into the wild places and forgot how to be courtiers, because remembering hurt too much. Most stayed forgotten.* A pause. *Not all.*

"Where did everyone go? The ones who survived?"

*South. Over your ridge. Into valleys where the magic pooled thin enough to hide a gift like water hiding a coal.* The raven turned its bright eye on him. *You know one of those valleys. You grew up in it. Ask yourself sometime why your family chose Hollowmere, Lockridge. Ask why the name fits.*

Quentin's mouth went dry. *Lockridge*. His grandmother's warnings rose up in him—*gifts like yours are why we came here*—and suddenly the words meant something different, something with foundations and cellars and generations buried underneath.

Before he could speak, the light changed.

* * *

It came down the overgrown processional road as the last sun cleared the hills: a stag, white where old stags go grey, with antlers like a winter orchard—and between the antlers, resting light as a circlet rather than grown there, a band of tarnished silver worked in the shape of nine small beasts.

Quentin had seen deer all his life. He had spoken to shy does in the hollows behind Hollowmere, traded gossip with a brash young buck about the best clover. This was not that. The difference was not size, though the stag stood taller than any horse. The difference was that when the stag looked at him, Quentin felt *seen*—catalogued, weighed, and found to be an open book with pages missing.

*So,* the stag said. Its voice filled the ruined terrace the way organ-fill fills a nave. *The valley sends us a boy.*

"I'm not—I'm not from the valley," Quentin managed. "I mean, I am, but I didn't come from it. I fled it."

*Everyone comes from somewhere, heir-blood.*

The words went through him like cold water. "I'm not—there's been a mistake. I'm a farmer's grandson. My father mended fences. I'm nobody's heir."

The stag descended the terrace steps unhurried, hooves striking sparks of sound off the old stone, until it stood close enough that Quentin could see himself reflected, tiny and trembling, in its dark eye.

*Ninth Seat,* the stag said. *The seat of Speakers. The seat that stands between the Court and the voiceless world and carries the words of each to the other. When the lists were read and the courtiers scattered, the Ninth Seat was empty—and a speaker slipped south with a child not yet born, into a valley that forgets.* The stag lowered its great head. *I am Vaelion, herald of the Court That Fell. Testing heirs is my office, though I have tested no one in ninety-nine years. Will you be tested?*

Every instinct Quentin owned screamed at him to refuse—to turn around, to run back south, to find some hole in the world where he could heal foxes quietly and never be more than a rumor. But he thought of the old vixen dying in the brush, pressing her memory into his hands: *carry me north.* He thought of the carved foxes still holding their note in the stone. He thought of Wren's face, and shoved the thought away before it could bleed.

"Yes," he said.

Vaelion stepped closer, so close the stag's breath stirred Quentin's hair, warm and smelling of winter woods. *Speak to me,* the herald said. *Not as a beggar speaks to a lord. As the Ninth Seat speaks—as an equal, with the old courtesy. If the words come true from you, the stone itself will know.*

Quentin closed his eyes. He was terrified. He was exhausted, grieving, hunted, lost—and beneath all of it, for the first time in his life, he was standing in a country where nothing about him needed hiding.

He reached for the old courtesy without knowing how he knew it, the way you know the shape of a door you've never opened. He bowed—not low, not servile, but the bow of one seat acknowledging another.

"Vaelion, herald of the Court That Fell," he said, and heard his own voice come out strange and formal and entirely his own. "I am Quentin, son of Aldous, grandson of Maren Lockridge—who I think was Maren of the Ninth House before she ever took a fence-mender's name. I crossed your border with a debt I owe a dead vixen, and I ask the roads' kindness and the Court's remembrance. I have nothing to offer but a gift I've spent my whole life being ashamed of. Tell me what it's for."

Silence. Then, all across the ruined terraces, the carved beasts began to sing.

It was barely sound—more a resonance rising out of the stone, the notes they had held for a century finally released, fox-quick and heron-cold and wolf-faithful, braiding together into a chord that made the lake-water tremble and brought Quentin to his knees again, though this time not from sickness. Above him, Vaelion bowed his antlered head, and the silver circlet gleamed.

*The stone knows,* the herald said. *Rise, Quentin of the Ninth House. Heir-blood of the Ninth Seat. Your inheritance has been waiting for you longer than you have been alive—and others know it too.*

The joy lasted exactly as long as it took Quentin to hear the last words. He got slowly to his feet. "Others?"

*Word travels strangely in a country with no name,* Vaelion said, and his voice had gone grim as a closing door. *But it travels. The men who burned the maps never stopped hunting what they burned them to destroy. Their chief hunter crossed the border two nights ago, carrying a relic of silence older than your grandmother's grandmother. He is coming for the Door. And now, heir-blood, he is coming for you.*

Far to the south, beyond the ridge, beyond the valley that forgot, the moon hung thin and new above the hills—one turn begun, twelve still to fall.
