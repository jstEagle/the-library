## 7. The Hunt Behind the Moon

The dead country did not sleep, but it dreamed, and Quentin had learned to walk inside its dreams.

Three nights past the ridge, he followed a road that moved when no one watched it. He knew this because the crows told him — *the road you slept beside is gone, little speaker; the road ahead is the one you left behind* — and because the stones themselves seemed to rearrange their patience, like old men shifting in pews. He had stopped asking why. The kingdom that had been erased from maps had not been erased from itself. It remembered everything. It was only waiting for someone to remember with it.

He traveled by moonlight now. The beasts had taught him that: *the crown's men hunt by day, by paper, by names written in ledgers.* So Quentin moved through the silvered ruins of what had once been a market town, its arches furred with moss, its wells breathing cold air upward like sleeping throats. A fox kit trotted at his heel — not the vixen whose memory he carried north, but her granddaughter, or so the crows insisted, though how they knew such things he could not say.

*She is heavy with your debt,* the kit said. It spoke in the way all beasts spoke to him: not sound, exactly, but meaning pressed against the inside of his skull, warm as a hand.

"I know," Quentin murmured. "I haven't forgotten."

*You forget when you look at the moon.*

He looked up despite himself. The moon hung over the broken rooftops, one turn shy of fullness, and there was something wrong with it tonight — a shadow at its edge like a thumbprint on glass. The thirteenth turn was coming. Everyone in this country, feathered and furred and otherwise, counted toward it the way drowning men count waves.

The stag-herald had named him heir-blood of the Ninth Seat, and the naming had changed the air around him. Beasts came to the edges of his fire now and watched him with eyes like banked coals. Some knelt. That frightened him more than the hunters did. He had healed a fox with whispered words in a valley that forgot, and now whole species bowed to him in the ruins of a kingdom his grandmother had fled before she could walk. The inheritance sat in his chest like a second heart, beating slightly out of time with his own.

Tonight, though, the animals were uneasy in a new way.

*Something walks the road behind you,* the crows reported, dropping out of the dark in ones and twos. *It does not speak to us. It cannot. But it carries something that makes our voices die in our throats when we come near.*

"Men?" Quentin asked.

*One man. And a thing older than men.*

Quentin stood very still among the moss-eaten arches. He thought of Wren — he tried not to, most hours, and failed in most of them — and of the voice at dawn that had given his name to the witch-hunters, cracked and trembling and unmistakable. He had run from that voice for three days. Now something else was coming down the moving roads after him, something that made crows go silent.

The kit pressed against his ankle. *Hide your gift,* she said. *Whatever comes, hide it.*

"How?"

*Be silent. Even in your head. Even to us.*

And then, from the ridge behind the ruined town, came a sound Quentin had never heard before and understood instantly: the absence of birdsong. Not quiet — silence, deliberate and total, spreading across the country like ink through water. Wherever it passed, the night went deaf.

He ran.

* * *

Wren Alcott had crossed the border three days behind the hunters and one day behind her courage.

She had told herself a hundred stories on the road south and then east and then wherever the land bent her: that she hadn't truly informed, that the words she'd spoken to the sergeant had been too soft to count, that she'd only meant to warn them off somehow, to say enough to satisfy them and not enough to be believed. She had rehearsed the story until she almost believed it, and then she had reached Hollowmere's churchyard on her way out and seen the fresh proclamation nailed beneath the old one, and the new parchment said, in the neat hand of the crown's chief hunter:

*The informant who named the beast-speaker Lockridge will receive her due at season's end.*

Her name. Her actual name, written where the whole valley could read it, promised thirty acres and the miller's fallow field. There was no version of the story anymore in which she had not done it. She had done it, and she had been paid for it in advance, and the payment was still waiting for her back home like a grave with her name half-carved.

So she followed the hunters instead of going home. It was the only penance she could think of that cost more than guilt.

They were easy to track, the witch-hunters — six men in grey livery, and at their center a tall figure in black whom the others called Master Corvane and did not look at directly. Wren kept half a mile between herself and them, sleeping in ditches, eating stolen apples and wild onions, learning to move the way animals moved. She had grown up in Hollowmere, where nothing magical survived the ridge line, and she had never imagined the world beyond it would feel like this: thick, watchful, alive in the walls and under the roots. On her first night past the border, a hare had looked at her for a long moment with an expression she could only call *assessment*, and she had burst into tears in front of it.

She was following them into a country that could hear everything she thought. Good. Let it hear. Let it know what she was and what she was trying to undo.

On the fourth night she saw Corvane use the relic.

They had caught something in the woods — Wren couldn't see what, only hear it: a high chittering, a thrash of small bodies, a mother-something defending young. Then Corvane lifted a rod of black iron, no longer than a forearm, wrapped at the grip in white leather gone grey with age, and spoke a single word Wren's ears refused to hold.

The noise in the forest didn't stop so much as get erased. One moment there were creatures screaming; the next, a hole in the world shaped exactly like their voices. Wren felt it pass over her own skin, a pressure behind the eyes, a taste like coins. The soldiers dragged three small brown shapes out of the bracken — otters, she thought, impossibly far from any river — and Corvane held the iron rod near their heads and asked questions, and the otters' mouths moved and nothing came out. Their faces, Wren thought, were worse than screams. They were the faces of people shouting underwater.

When the soldiers got what they wanted — a direction, a ruin, a name — Corvane snapped the smallest otter's neck without looking at it, and the other two he simply left, silenced, blinking in the torchlight, alive in every way except the one that mattered.

Wren lay in the ferns with her hand clamped over her own mouth and understood, finally and completely, what she had loosed on Quentin. Not six men with crossbows. Six men and a thing that made the whole world mute — and Quentin, who spoke to everything, who had spent his life in conversation with the grass and the crows and the dying vixens, would be cut off from every friend he had. Alone in a dead kingdom, deafened in the only language that loved him.

She waited until the hunters' fire burned low. Then she got up and walked east, faster than she had ever walked, toward the moving roads and the door she'd heard the soldiers mutter about.

*Thirteenth Door. Thirteenth turn. Take the heir-blood alive.*

Behind her, far off, the night went silent in one direction and then another, like a tide going out. She did not stop to wonder what it meant. She already knew: it meant he was running, and it meant the silence was gaining.

* * *

Master Corvane did not believe in the Ninefold Court. This was his strength.

He had served the crown for thirty years, through two kings and one regency, and in thirty years he had learned that belief was a door and doors opened from both sides. So he carried no faith into the erased country — only the relic, which he called the Grey Word, taken from a witch's pyre forty years ago along with her tongue, and which silenced beast-speech within a hundred paces of its iron. Around him, the dead kingdom seethed with memory and malice, and he walked through it the way a tax collector walks through a funeral: unmoved, unloved, unbothered.

What interested him was arithmetic. The boy Lockridge had crossed the ridge, which meant the boy was heir-blood, which meant the Ninth Seat was not empty but merely vacant, which meant the crown's oldest fear — the fear buried under a century of burned maps — had a face and a name and a limp from the marsh crossing. Kill the boy before the moon turned, and the vacancy stayed vacant forever. Every road in this country bent toward one place, and every animal in it bent toward the boy, and Corvane intended to arrive first.

The trouble was the animals.

They could not speak near him — the Grey Word saw to that — but they could *act*. Roads closed ahead of his column and opened behind it. Bridges of ancient stone collapsed with theatrical slowness just as his men reached them, always missing him, always taking a soldier. Crows escorted them at a distance, silent as carried lanterns, counting. On the fifth night his pickets found every horse's tack unbuckled and hung neatly in the trees, arranged in a pattern none of the men would describe aloud.

Corvane adjusted. He always adjusted. He began carrying the Grey Word unsheathed, its circle of silence rolling before the column like a plow, and where it passed, the country's watching presence flinched. He noted the flinch with professional satisfaction. Whatever this land was, it was not invulnerable. Nothing that could be wounded by silence was invulnerable.

On the sixth night, a stag stepped onto the road ahead of them.

It was enormous, grey as ash, wearing between its antlers a circlet of rusted bronze that had once, unmistakably, been a crown. The soldiers raised their crossbows. Corvane lifted one finger, and they held.

The stag looked at him for a long moment. Then, deliberately, in view of thirty men, it lowered its head and drew one hoof through the dust of the road — a single stroke, a mark, a letter in no alphabet Corvane knew — and stood waiting.

"It wants me to read it," Corvane said softly. "It cannot speak here, so it writes." He studied the mark. "Which means it believes I might be persuaded."

He raised the Grey Word, and the silence rolled forward and swallowed the stag's corner of the world, and the great animal did not flee. It stood inside the silence like a king standing in rain, and it looked at Corvane with an expression he had seen only once before in thirty years — on the face of a witch at the stake who had stopped screaming and started smiling.

Then the stag turned and walked away unhurried, and the mark it had drawn remained in the dust, glowing faintly, refusing to be scuffed away by six pairs of boots.

That night Corvane sent three riders ahead at full gallop, and he sat by his cold fire turning the Grey Word in his hands, and for the first time in thirty years he allowed himself a small, disciplined flicker of the thing he did not carry:

Belief.

Because he had finally translated the mark in the road, working it against the fragments in the old witch-burnings' records, and what it said was simple, and it was addressed to him, and it was this:

*She waits for you at the Door too, hunter. The one whose tongue you took. Ask her whose word the Grey Word really is.*

Corvane did not sleep. In the morning, the column moved faster, and behind them the moon rose one night short of its thirteenth turn, and far to the east two travelers — one hunted, one hunting herself — were converging on the same broken threshold through the same singing dark.
