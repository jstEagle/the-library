## 8. The Price of the Ninth Seat

The Thirteenth Door did not look like a door.

It looked like a wound in the evening—a seam of pale light stitched down the face of a cliff that had no right to exist, standing alone in a meadow where the grass grew silver and bent always toward it, as if even the land remembered which way home was. Quentin had walked three days through the erased kingdom to reach it, and now that he stood before it, his legs would not carry him the last hundred paces.

The vixen's memory pressed warm against his ribs, wrapped in the oilcloth of his heart: *carry me north*. He had carried her this far. But the light in the seam flickered when he breathed, and he understood, with the certainty of animals, that it was listening.

"You come at last." The voice came from everywhere—the grass, the stone, the fading sky. "We have waited nine generations for an heir to stand here and be afraid."

"I'm not afraid," Quentin said, and then, because the Court's remnant deserved better than a lie, he amended: "I am afraid. I've been afraid since the proclamation went up on the church door. I thought coming here would end that."

"Nothing here ends," said the voice. "Everything here waits. Come closer, heir, and see what waiting costs."

* * *

He crossed the silver grass, and the world changed around him the way a dream changes when you realize you're dreaming. The meadow became a hall—no walls, no roof, only columns of moonlight where pillars had been, and upon each pillar perched or stood or curled the shapes of beasts: a heron with eyes like old coins, a wolf grown gray at the muzzle, a serpent wearing a crown of shed skins, nine in all, arranged in a broken circle with one space empty.

The Ninth Seat.

It was not empty, exactly. It was a chair of white stone, worn smooth by centuries of no one sitting in it, and on its armrest lay a folded cloak of ordinary brown wool—the kind a farm boy might wear to market. Quentin knew it instantly. His grandmother had one just like it, patched at the elbows. He had buried her in it.

"That is the price," said the heron, speaking now with a voice like a hinge long unused. "Not your blood. Not your life. Those we could take, and taking is easy. No—the Court asks what cannot be taken, only given."

The wolf stepped forward, her breath fogging though there was no cold. "You must give up the valley. Hollowmere will forget you as you forgot it. Your name—Lockridge, the ridge-name, the hiding-name—you must lay it down at this threshold like a coat outgrown."

"And safety," said the serpent, and its voice was soft as a scale drawn over stone. "The valley was built on safety. Silence, secrecy, smallness. You may keep any life but that one. Walk through the Door and you will never again be invisible. Never again unremarkable. Every beast will know you; every crown will hunt you. Is that a prize, boy? We ask you honestly. We have seen heirs turn back."

Quentin looked at the brown cloak on the armrest.

He thought of Hollowmere: the smell of bread from Marigold Fenwick's oven, the creek where he'd learned to swim, his grandmother's kitchen with its crooked shelf and its kettle that whistled three notes. He thought of the fox kit he had healed with words no one must hear, and how the healing had felt more true than anything else in his whole hidden life.

"I buried my grandmother in a cloak like that," he said. "She told me our family came to the valley so the gift would die out. She said it like an apology she couldn't finish." His throat tightened. "I don't want the valley to forget me. But I think—I think it already did. That's what the valley does. It forgets. It forgot the Court, it forgot the roads, it forgot every name but its own." He lifted his chin. "If the price is giving up being forgotten, then I've been paying toward this my whole life."

The nine beasts were silent a moment, and in that silence the seam of light behind them seemed to steady, like a held breath released.

* * *

Then he smelled woodsmoke where there should have been none, and heard, faint and impossible, a human footstep in the silver grass.

Quentin turned.

Wren stood at the edge of the meadow, mud to her knees, her braid half-undone, her eyes red-rimmed and furious with exhaustion. She looked as though she had walked through every nightmare between Hollowmere and here, and perhaps she had. Behind her, far off in the darkening country, something glinted—the cold starlight gleam of the hunter's relic, and the sound of hounds that made no sound at all.

"No," Quentin said. "No, you can't be here."

"Apparently I can," Wren said, and her voice cracked in the middle. "Three days, Quentin. Three days behind you, following crows who hated me on principle and told me so. Daily." She took another step forward and stopped, swaying. "I had to know. I had to see—if you were alive, if—"

"If I was angry."

"Aren't you?"

He should have been. He had rehearsed it across the marshes and the moving roads: the words he'd say, cold and clean, the way she had said *I told them where you sleep* —except she hadn't said those words, had she. He'd heard her voice among the hunters, but he had also spent a lifetime learning the difference between what beasts said and what they meant, and Wren had never once, in fifteen years of friendship, meant him harm.

"The informant's voice was yours," he said carefully. "Or it wore your voice. Which was it?"

Wren's hands twisted together. "I went to the church door to read the proclamation again because I didn't believe it. And a hunter saw me looking too long, asked if I knew anyone, and I—I panicked. I said your name. One word, before I could stop it. Then I ran home and threw up and told myself I'd imagined it, that they wouldn't come, that saying a name wasn't the same as—" She stopped. "They came at dawn anyway. I don't know if they needed my word or already had it. I've been trying to decide ever since whether I betrayed you or just failed you, and I can't tell the difference anymore, and either way I couldn't let them be the ones who found you first."

Quentin let out a breath he felt he'd been holding since the marshes.

"She speaks truth," rumbled the wolf. "Truth tastes different from fear-lies. This one's tongue is clean."

"I know," Quentin said simply. "I knew before you said it. I refuse to believe anything else about her. That's not naivety—it's fifteen years of evidence."

Something in Wren's face broke open—not relief exactly, but the thing that comes before relief, when a person has carried a weight so long they've forgotten their shoulders once worked without it.

"They're coming," she whispered, glancing back at the dark. "The chief hunter. He crossed into the dead country after us. He has a relic that eats voices, Quentin. The crows won't go near it. Nothing living will."

"Then we are nearly out of time," said the heron, "and the price stands unpaid. Heir. Choose. Lay down the name Lockridge, the valley, the safety of silence—and take up the Ninth Seat. Or turn back and live small and hidden until the hunters find you anyway, for they will. The moon turns its thirteenth face tomorrow night."

Quentin looked at the white stone chair. At the brown cloak. At Wren, mud-streaked and stubborn, who had followed betrayal all this way to unsay it.

He thought of his grandmother's unfinished apology, and understood at last that she had sent him here without knowing it—that every warning, every *hide it, bury it, starve it*, had been a map drawn backward, pointing away from the one place he needed to go.

"My grandmother's name was Alba Lockridge," he said slowly. "But before the valley, before the hiding, our family had another name. She hummed it once, when she thought I was asleep. A court-name." He stepped forward. "I'll lay down Lockridge. I'll give you back the valley's forgetting. And I'll walk into whatever the crown sends hunting me—because a seat that costs nothing protects nothing, and I'd rather be hunted for truth than safe inside a lie."

He reached out and touched the brown wool cloak.

The meadow exhaled. The nine pillars of moonlight flared, and the seam of light behind them widened by a hand's breadth, humming like a struck bell. Somewhere far to the south, past the border, past the ridge line, he felt Hollowmere begin, gently, to forget his name—and felt, astonishingly, no grief. Only room.

"It is accepted," said the nine voices as one. "The Ninth Seat is claimed. Now, heir—there remains only the turning of the moon, and one who arrives behind you with silence in his fist."

Wren grabbed Quentin's arm, her grip fierce. Down in the dark country, the soundless hounds had begun to run.

"He's here," she breathed. "Quentin—he's here."
