## 10. The Court Rising

Dawn came to the dead country the way it had not come in a hundred years: slowly, and then all at once.

Quentin stood on the steps of the Thirteenth Door with Wren's hand still warm in his, watching light pour down through the ruined crown of the Ninefold Court like honey through a cracked jar. The stone seats—nine of them, arranged in a crescent around a floor of living moss—had been gray and silent when he first saw them, weeks ago or years ago; he could no longer measure time the way he used to. Now they were waking. Color moved through them like blood returning to a numb limb: lichen-green flushing into jade, river-slate into silver, the Ninth Seat itself kindling from within until it glowed the deep amber of fox-eyes at dusk.

The vixen's color. He had carried her memory north, and she had carried him the rest of the way.

Around the crescent of seats, the beasts were gathering. Not the way animals gathered—nervous, sideways, ready to bolt—but the way courtiers once had, Quentin realized, the way his grandmother's half-forgotten stories described. The stag-herald stood at the head of them, his antlers hung with the first new growth of the season, pale as candlewax. Behind him came wolves with frost still in their ruffs, herons who walked rather than flew because this was a day for walking, a great brown bear moving with the deliberate dignity of an old judge, and hundreds more—a river of fur and feather and scale flowing up through the broken gates of the court that humans had erased and beasts had refused to forget.

And among them, small and ragged and alive, the animals Quentin had known by name. The marsh heron whose wing he had splinted two winters past. The blind mole who had mapped the tunnels beneath Hollowmere's churchyard. The crow—*Bramble*, she had finally told him her name, laughing in that dry cawing way of hers—who had been his first friend and his fiercest defender, and who now perched on the arm of the Ninth Seat as though she had always belonged there.

"Sit," Bramble said. "You're making the moss nervous."

"I'm making *you* nervous."

"I'm never nervous. I'm strategically alert." She preened her wing, but her black eye was soft when it found his. "Sit down, speaker. This is your morning."

Quentin sat.

The Ninth Seat received him the way water receives a stone—not with resistance but with adjustment, closing gently around him, warming where he touched it. He felt the court's memory rise through the stone into his bones: nine hundred years of oaths sworn here, of borders drawn in trust instead of fear, of a covenant between the people who built walls and the people who ran beneath them. He felt too the severing—the night the last queen closed her hand and let the maps be burned, choosing erasure over slaughter, sealing the door so that the hunting would stop even if the remembering did not.

They had waited a century for someone who could hear both sides of the silence.

Wren sat down beside him on the lowest step, close enough that their shoulders touched. She was thinner than she had been in Hollowmere, harder at the edges, and there was a new scar along her jaw where the hunter's relic had struck her—but her eyes were the same eyes that had watched him across a schoolroom desk for fifteen years, guileless and stubborn and afraid of far less than she pretended.

"You don't have to do this part," Quentin said quietly. "You could go home. The valley will take you back. They'll say you were taken against your will."

"The valley," Wren said, "can take its land payments and bury them." She looked out at the assembled court—at the wolves and herons and the bear with his judge's gravity—and something in her face steadied. "I reported you. Or I tried to. I stood in front of that proclamation with my heart going like a hammer and I said your name out loud, and whether or not they'd have come for you anyway doesn't matter, because I *said it*. You don't get to forgive that by deciding I didn't mean it. And I don't get to be your witness without saying what I did."

"I know." Quentin turned his hand over, palm up, on the step between them—an old gesture, from the days when they sealed childhood promises with spit-shaken hands. "That's why it has to be you. A witness who's never lied isn't worth much. A witness who knows exactly what silence costs—" he looked out at the ruined court, at the hundred years of emptiness that silence had bought, "—that's the only kind worth swearing in."

Wren laughed, wet-eyed and unwilling to admit it. "You've gotten insufferable since you became royalty."

"Speaker," corrected the stag-herald, approaching with steps that made the moss shiver. "There is no royalty here anymore. That was the old mistake, and we do not make old mistakes twice." He lowered his great head until one dark eye regarded Quentin at close range, and in it Quentin saw reflected a boy kneeling on a hillside whispering to a wounded fox, and a man standing before a door that should not open, opening it anyway. "Kneel, if you would. What is given today is given low, so that it may lift everything else."

Quentin knelt.

The stag-herald touched his brow to Quentin's forehead, and the whole court breathed in at once—a sound like wind through ten thousand trees—and spoke:

"*Blood of the Ninth Seat, child of the hidden valley, friend of the small and voice of the voiceless: the Court asks nothing of your obedience, for obedience is what broke us. It asks only your word. Will you speak for the beasts when men will not listen?*"

"I will."

"*Will you speak for men when beasts will not forgive?*"

"I will."

"*Will you keep the door open? Knowing what comes through it? Knowing that the valley below will change—that the forgetting will end, whether it wishes to or not?*"

Quentin thought of Hollowmere then. Of the ridge line where magic died, of his grandmother's kitchen with its iron kettle and its careful silences, of neighbors who had read a proclamation with hunger in their faces and friends who had almost betrayed him out of love. He thought of the life he might have lived if he had stayed: small, safe, suffocating, a held breath lasting sixty years.

He thought of the vixen dying in the bracken, spending her last hours not on fear but on giving away the only thing she had left: a road, a memory, a debt.

"Yes," he said. "Even if it breaks my heart. Especially then."

"*Then rise,*" said the stag-herald, "*and speak.*"

Quentin rose, and the words came to him—not composed but remembered, rising through the seat and the stone and the century of waiting, in a language older than the crown's decree and younger than yesterday's forgiveness:

"*Let the Ninefold Court rise—not as conquest, but as covenant. Let no wall be built that the wild things have not consented to. Let no hunt be run that the hunted cannot answer. Let every beast keep its name and every human keep their hearing, and let the space between them be called neither wilderness nor property, but home.*"

He paused, and looked at Wren.

"And let there be witnesses," he said. "Sworn ones. Who know the price of silence, and have paid it, and speak anyway. Wren Ashcombe of Hollowmere—I ask you to be the first."

Wren knelt beside him, in front of the whole risen court, in front of wolves and herons and a bear old enough to remember the burning of the maps, and her voice did not shake at all.

"I witnessed the betrayal," she said clearly. "I witness the mending. Let both be written down."

The court roared—or sang, or simply *was*, a sound that was not sound at all but weather, a change in the pressure of the world. Above the crescent of seats, the sky itself seemed to lean closer. And beyond the broken gates, Quentin felt it begin: the slow, irresistible seep of magic running downhill, off the plateau of the dead country, toward the ridge line, toward the valley that had spent a hundred years teaching itself to forget.

It would reach Hollowmere by autumn. The foxes would start answering children who talked to them. The old stones in the church wall would wake up. His grandmother's iron kettle would sing songs she hadn't heard since she was a girl, and she would cry, and she would understand at last why the family had come to that valley, and why they could never again stay.

Some of it would be terrible. Quentin knew that. Covenants cut both ways, and a valley that had chosen blindness would not thank him for returned sight. There would be anger, and fear, and men with proclamations who discovered that proclamations no longer worked the way they used to. There would be hard mornings, and worse nights, and a long road of learning how two kinds of people shared one world without one of them owning it.

But there would also be this: a girl on a hillside at dusk, laughing, while a fox told her the names of all the stars.

* * *

They camped that night in the court's great hall, under a roof half-collapsed and wholly beautiful, stars showing through the gaps in the vaulting like candles lit for guests. The beasts had dispersed to their territories—to roads unmoving now, settled back into their proper shapes, to forests remembering how to be forests—but Bramble stayed, roosting on the lintel of the Thirteenth Door, which stood open and would stand open forever.

"You'll go down," Quentin said to Wren. They sat side by side against the Ninth Seat's base, sharing a blanket against the mountain cold. "Someone has to carry word to the valley before the magic gets there. Someone they'll listen to. Someone who—" he smiled, "—isn't a traitor to the crown. Officially."

"Unofficially?"

"Oh, completely. But the paperwork hasn't caught up."

Wren was quiet a long moment. "Come with me. Just to see it. One walk down the old road, one look at the ridge from the other side. You've earned a goodbye."

Quentin looked out through the open door, north and east, where the road ran on toward countries that had never heard of the Ninefold Court and would need, very soon, to learn. There were other valleys. Other ridgelines. Other children whispering to wounded things in the dark, praying no one heard. The covenant was young and he was its only speaker, and speakers were meant to travel.

"One look," he said. "One goodbye. And then—" he nodded at the road, at the wide waiting dark full of voices he could already feel straining toward him like seedlings toward sun, "—then the work begins."

Above them, Bramble ruffled her feathers and settled into the shape of a promise.

"Better rest, speaker," she said. "Tomorrow, the world starts talking back."

Far below, at the edge of hearing, the first fox in Hollowmere opened her eyes in the dark and—for the first time in a hundred years—answered.
