## 1. The Thief of Yesterday

The Hollow Valley kept its secrets the way a miser keeps coins: close, and counting.

Odile Eastwick knew every one of its secrets by heart—the crooked mile of the river where the water ran backward at dusk, the orchard of pear trees that fruited out of season because the valley had forgotten what season meant, the shrine on the hill with no god in it that the old-timers still left bread for, out of habit older than their memories of why. The valley was a pocket sewn shut against the world. Magic could not enter it. Magic could not follow anyone in. And so the valley had become, over four hundred years, the place where people came to stop being hunted—and Odile had become the thing they never saw coming, because in a land without magic, she was the only magic there was, and she was patient.

She sat now on the flat stone lip of the river landing, mending a net she didn't need, watching the ferryman die.

His name was Aldous Vane, though half the valley called him Old Ferry, and he had poled travelers across the Graywash for fifty-one years. She knew this the way she knew most things—not because he'd told her, but because she'd taken it from him years ago, gently, a memory of his father teaching him to read the river's moods lifted like a coin from an open palm while he dozed at the tiller. He had never missed it. That was the trick of it, the mercy of it: a stolen memory leaves no hole. It leaves only a smoothness, a place where the mind slides over and doesn't catch, the way a tongue finds the gap where a tooth used to be and learns, eventually, not to worry at it.

He was dying of the wet-lung, and everyone knew it, and today he had rowed himself out to the middle of the crossing one last time and then rowed back, and now he sat on the bench beneath the leaning willow where he'd sat every evening for fifty-one years, watching the water go gold and then copper and then that strange bruised violet the valley made of sunset, because the valley's sunsets were slightly wrong—colors a shade too deep, as if the light itself had been diluted—and he was crying.

Not sobbing. Just crying, the way old men cry, quietly, as if leaking.

Odile set down her net. This was the part she hated, and the part she was best at, and she had never once managed to make those two facts cancel each other out.

* * *

The valley had three rules for her, and she had carved them herself into the beam above her hearth when she was nineteen and foolish enough to think rules were things you wrote once and obeyed forever:

*Never steal pain.*

*Never steal from family.*

*Never leave.*

She had added them in order of difficulty, which is to say in reverse order of importance, which is to say she'd been lying to herself about all three since before she understood what any of them cost.

The first rule was mercy and discipline both. Pain-memory was easy coin—it fetched triple what joy did in the rare corners of the world where memory-trade existed at all, because there were always buyers who wanted someone else's grief to feel instead of their own emptiness—but taking it left marks. Not on the thief. On the theft. A memory of pain, once taken, could never be given back whole; it frayed at the edges like wet cloth, and whatever ragged remnant returned to its owner was worse than the ache it replaced. So: never steal pain. She had watched a man in her grandmother's time—she thought it was her grandmother's time; the memory was secondhand and soft-edged—have his grief lifted from him by a trader who promised relief, and what came back to fill the space was nothing at all, and nothing, Odile had learned early, was the one wound that never scarred over.

The second rule was harder than it sounded, because *family* was a word the valley used loosely and Odile used not at all. It meant blood, mostly. It meant the Eastwicks, what was left of them, which was her and a great-aunt in the far village who sent her honey cakes every solstice and whom she visited twice a year out of something she refused to name. It meant that no matter how hungry winter made her, no matter how tempting the rich warm memories of the miller's wedding or the cooper's daughter's first steps, she did not touch the minds of the people who would grieve her if she vanished. Family got to keep everything, even the boring parts. Even the parts they'd have traded away gladly. That was what family meant. It was the only definition of the word she trusted.

The third rule she kept the way a bird keeps the shape of its cage: perfectly, and with increasing awareness of the bars.

Magic could not follow into the valley. But memory, Odile's particular and unlicensed gift, was a border-crosser. What she took inside stayed alive inside her, warm and flickering, and if she ever walked out past the boundary stones—past the cairn on the north ridge where the air changed texture like passing through a curtain of cold water—everything she carried would wake up. The stolen lives would stir. The valley's deadening hush would peel off her gift like a bandage off a burn, and she would feel, all at once, the full weight of a thousand lifetimes that did not belong to her, screaming to be somewhere, to be *used*, to be lived.

She had stood at the cairn exactly once, when she was twenty-two. She had lasted eleven seconds. She still dreamed about it.

* * *

Aldous Vane was still crying when she crossed the landing to him, her mending basket on her hip like a housewife's prop, harmless as bread.

"Evening, Odile-girl," he said, and wiped his face with the heel of his hand, embarrassed. His voice was a bellows with a leak in it. "Don't mind me. Water gets in my eyes this time of day."

"It's the smoke from my chimney," she said. "It does that to everyone on the landing." This was a lie so worn it was practically a greeting. There was no smoke. There was rarely smoke; she burned driftwood and peat and very little else.

"You mending that net or mourning it?" He nodded at the basket. "Been working that same tear for a week."

"It's a difficult tear."

"It's a hole." He laughed, which turned into a cough, which went on long enough that she set the basket down and sat beside him on the bench, close enough that her shoulder touched his, which was the whole point. Touch was the door. It always had been. Skin to skin, or nearly—a sleeve was enough, if the sleeve was thin—but touch was the hinge on which the whole quiet larceny of her life turned, and she had spent thirty-one years making sure nobody found her touching them suspicious.

The cough subsided. He breathed. The river said what rivers say, which is nothing, endlessly.

"I rowed out midstream today," Aldous said. "Just to look at it from the middle. You know what I remembered?"

Here it was. Here was always here, the moment she both dreaded and lived for, the moment the dying offered up their best things like plates held out at a feast. "What?"

"That I don't remember anything worth remembering." He said it lightly, but his hand found her wrist where it lay on the bench between them, and his grip was cold and strong. "Fifty-one years on this water, girl, and when I go looking through myself for something to hold on to at the end, it's all—" he waved his free hand vaguely at the violet air, "—weather. Fares. The year the pears came wrong. I've got nothing. I've been saving nothing my whole life and now the pantry's bare and it's suppertime."

Odile looked at the river. The first star had come out, or the thing the valley used instead of stars, a bright smudge that didn't quite twinkle.

"There was snow," she said carefully. She knew the shape of what was in him; she always did, at the end, the way a locksmith knows the shape of a lock by the wear around the keyhole. "When you were small. Your mother woke you before dawn—"

"Don't." His voice cracked. Then: "No. Go on. How do you know that?"

"You told me. Years ago. Drunk on plum wine at the harvest fire, telling anybody who'd listen." Also a lie. Also practically a greeting. But she needed him to believe the memory was already shared, already common property, because the theft was easier when the owner thought the thing being lifted had been given freely once.

"Did I." He was quiet a while. His thumb moved on her wrist, back and forth, an old man's fidget. "I dream about it. Haven't seen snow in forty years—we get that wet grey slop, never the real thing—and I dream about it and I wake up and I can't find it anywhere in me. I know I had it. First snow. She wrapped me in the blue blanket and carried me out and the whole world was silent, that silence snow makes, like the world holding its breath, and I was three, maybe four, and I put my hand out and caught one and it didn't melt right away, or I dreamed it didn't, and I looked at it and I thought—I don't even know what I thought. Something important. And now it's gone, Odile. It's gone and I'm going tomorrow or the day after and I can't find it, and that's not dying, that's being robbed."

The word landed in her chest like a fishhook.

"I could tell you about mine," she said, which was a deflection and also true, because she had seventeen first-snows in her keeping, gathered over the years from travelers and traders and one blind fiddler who had described his so beautifully she'd had to have it. Seventeen winters that weren't hers. She owned more snow than the valley had seen in a decade.

"No," Aldous said. And then, turning his head, looking at her with eyes gone pale as the water: "You're her, aren't you. The one they whisper about. The taker."

The river said nothing. Somewhere upstream a nightjar called, twice, and stopped, as if embarrassed.

Odile did not deny it. Denial was a tool and this was not the moment for tools. "Who told you?"

"Nobody tells me anything anymore, girl, I'm furniture. But furniture hears." He tightened his grip on her wrist—not restraining her; asking. "They say you can lift a thing clean out. They say the person never misses it. They say it's like—" he searched, "—like a splinter coming out you never felt go in."

"They say a lot of things."

"Take it." His voice was suddenly fierce, sudden as weather. "Take the snow. If it's anywhere in me, take it. Because if I die with it lost—if I die not able to find my own mother's face in the blue blanket—then whoever took it took it twice, and I'll have been robbed by God and man both."

And there it was, the knot she could never untangle, the moral pretzel she had been folding and unfolding for thirty-one years: he was asking. He was begging. But the memory wasn't lost—he had told her so himself, he'd dreamed of it last week, it was in him, dimmed by age and the valley's strange forgetting haze but *there*, his, one of the last lit windows in a house going dark. To take it now would be to make true the very robbery he mourned. To take it now would be to complete, with her own hands, the crime he was grieving.

Unless.

Unless he gave it. Unless, at the threshold of the dark, a man chose to send his treasure ahead of him rather than let the dark have it. Was that robbery? Her grandmother—her actual grandmother, in the two real memories Odile had of her, both of hands, both of flour—had said once, in a voice Odile could no longer fully hear: *a gift given at death isn't stolen, girl, it's escorted.* But her grandmother had also been, according to certain whispers Odile had spent years refusing to listen to, a memory-trader of some renown before she'd buried herself in this valley, and so her maxims arrived pre-poisoned.

"Old Ferry," Odile said. "Look at me."

He looked.

"If I take it, you won't miss it. You understand? Whatever you're afraid of—that dying blind, that dying robbed—you won't feel it. You'll feel light. That's all anyone ever feels. Light, and then sleep."

"And you'll keep it safe." It wasn't a question. It was a commission. An old man hiring a courier for the one delivery that mattered.

"I keep everything safe," Odile said. "It's the only thing I'm good at."

"Furniture says otherwise," Aldous said, and smiled, and died a little more, right there between one breath and the next, the way the dying do—still talking, still warm, but with some lamp behind the eyes turned low. "Do it now, before my nerve goes. My nerve's the last thing I've got and it's leaving ahead of me."

* * *

This was the craft of it, and she loved the craft of it, and loving it was the thing she forgave herself least.

She turned on the bench to face him, took his cold hand in both of hers, and closed her eyes, and reached.

Inside a human mind there is a room—everyone has one—where the oldest things are kept. Not the biggest things, not the loudest. The oldest. It has no door in most people; it has only a habit of approach, a path worn smooth by decades of returning, the way a footpath wears into a hillside. Odile had learned to see the paths. She followed his now, down through the weather and the fares and the year the pears came wrong, past the great shadowed warehouses of his marriage and his son who'd gone to the coast and never written, down and down to where the floor of him turned to packed snow—

There.

First snow. Age four. The blue blanket, wool, smelling of cedar and milk. Pre-dawn dark going pearl at the edges. His mother's breath clouding above him, her voice pitched low like a secret: *look, lovey, look what the sky's done while we slept.* The silence—yes, the silence, the held-breath hush, the whole world muffled and listening. His mittens. One mitten off, pressed into the white, the perfect print of a four-year-old's hand, five fingers, a small star of a boy stamped into the endless blank page of the world. And the single flake on his bare palm, lingering, hexagonal, impossibly detailed, a cathedral small enough to hold, not melting, or melting so slowly it seemed like consent. And the feeling underneath all of it, the feeling the memory was actually *for*: that the world was vast and new and had come quietly, in the night, just to show him something beautiful, just for him, before anyone else was awake.

Odile took it the way you lift a coal from a banked fire: swiftly, surely, wrapping it as she lifted so it wouldn't gutter.

It settled into her the way all of them did—into the deep archive of her, the vault behind her ribs where a thousand lives were shelved in the dark, labeled and dusted and utterly inert. Somewhere in that vault were seventeen other first snows. Now eighteen. She filed his beside a fisherman's memory of his daughter's birth and a widow's memory of her husband whistling in the garden, and she did the thing she always did afterward, the ritual she had never described to anyone because describing it would require admitting it existed: she said thank you. Silently. To the shelved life. *Thank you. I'll keep you well.* As if the memory could hear. As if Aldous Vane, in whatever hour remained to him, might somehow be comforted by a gratitude he would never know was owed to him.

She opened her eyes.

Aldous was looking at the river with an expression she knew intimately and had built her whole moral architecture around avoiding: peace. Not bliss. Peace. The smoothness where the tooth had been. He would fall asleep tonight without dreaming of snow, and wake without missing it, and die Thursday with a lightness in him that was really just absence wearing a kind face.

"Better?" she asked.

"Better," he said wonderingly. "Lighter. Like I put down a pack I forgot I was carrying." He patted her hand with his free one, twice, the way you'd pat a good horse. "Knew you would. Knew it the moment you sat down. You've got a giver's hands, girl. Funny, in a taker."

"I'm not a taker," Odile said. "I'm a keeper. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"Yes," she said, and did not believe it, and he heard that she did not believe it, because the dying hear everything, and he smiled at her with terrible kindness and said:

"Then someday, girl, somebody'll keep *you*. See they do. Don't you go ending up like me—full of everybody's yesterdays and none of your own." He leaned back against the willow. The light had gone entirely. The bruised-violet dusk had thickened to a purple so deep it was nearly a sound. "Now clear off. A man wants privacy for his last sunsets."

* * *

She walked home along the towpath with the mending basket on her hip and Aldous's snow burning quietly behind her ribs, and she did the accounting she always did, the ledger she kept in her head because writing it down would make it real and making it real would make it unbearable.

One thousand and forty-two. That was the count. One thousand and forty-two lives, whole or fragmentary, shelved in the dark of her. A bride's terror-joy walking the aisle at Verance, a city she had never seen. A soldier's last morning before a battle whose name she'd had to ask about. A baker's pride in a loaf. A child's certainty that the moon followed her home. A hundred mothers' lullabies in nine languages. Snow, rain, sea-crossings, hangovers, forgiveness, one coronation, six funerals, and the entire interior of a stranger's wedding day, which she took out sometimes, at night, alone, and walked through like a woman trying on a dress in an empty shop.

She owned a thousand lives. She lived none of them. That was the trade, the bargain she had struck with the valley without ever signing anything: it hid her, and she harvested it, and the harvest filled a vault that no one would ever open, least of all her. Because opening it—living it—was impossible. The memories weren't hers. Wearing them was theft enough; *being* them would be a madness. And so they sat, the largest fortune in the Nine Realms, and she sat, the richest pauper alive, eating barley stew alone at a table set for one, in a cottage whose walls she had warded with nothing but habit and the valley's own indifference.

Her hearth was cold. She knelt to build the fire, and her hands did it without her—kindling, peat, the strike of flint—while the rest of her stood in the doorway of her own skull looking out at the dark valley and thinking about Aldous's last words.

*Somebody'll keep you. Don't you go ending up like me.*

"Too late," she said aloud, to the empty room, and the empty room, being the Hollow Valley, agreed with her completely.

She ate. She banked the fire. She climbed to her narrow bed under the eaves and pulled the quilt—made by the great-aunt, honey-cake-scented even now—up to her chin, and she lay in the dark doing the other thing she always did, the nightly penance disguised as pleasure: she opened the vault a crack and let one memory wash over her, just one, chosen at random, a borrowed yesterday to fall asleep inside.

Tonight it was a young man's memory of learning to dance, in a kitchen, badly, to his sister's fiddle, everyone laughing, flour on the floor, lamplight, belonging.

She fell asleep inside it the way you fall asleep outside a lit window in the cold: warmed, and not invited.

She did not hear, in the hours before dawn, the sound that no one in the Hollow Valley had heard in living memory—the sound of hooves on the north road, beyond the cairn, beyond the boundary, where magic could not follow and therefore nothing should have been able to arrive.

She did not hear it. But the valley did. And the valley, which had forgotten seasons and gods and the reason for bread on empty shrines, remembered, suddenly and all at once, how to be afraid.
