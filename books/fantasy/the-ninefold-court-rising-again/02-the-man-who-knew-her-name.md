## 2. The Man Who Knew Her Name

The Hollow Valley had three rules that everyone knew and one that no one spoke aloud.

The first was that the mist never lifted. It pooled in the low fields like milk gone soft, curled through the birch stands, hung over the river in grey ribbons that the wind could not unravel. Travelers said it made them feel as though they were walking through the inside of a held breath. Odile had lived beneath it for eleven years and had stopped noticing it the way a fish stops noticing water—until days like this one, when something in the air felt *watched*, and then the mist seemed less like weather and more like a curtain someone might part at any moment.

The second rule was that outsiders never found the valley. Not because it was hidden on any map—the valley appeared on maps, a smudge of green between two mountain spines with no name worth printing—but because anyone who came looking for it found themselves, an hour later, on the far side of the pass with no memory of turning around. The valley did not hide itself; it simply declined to be remembered. Pilgrims called it God's forgetting. Traders called it the tax you didn't pay. Odile called it home, which was its own kind of joke, because she had never once thought of another place as anything but scenery.

The third rule belonged to her alone: never steal pain. She could take almost anything else—a first kiss, a mother's voice singing off-key, the particular gold of a harvest sunset thirty years gone—and she did, carefully, politely, always with consent or near enough to it, trading small comforts for small coins until her cellar shelves in the crooked house by the ferry landing rattled with stoppered glass. But pain stayed where it grew. She had learned why early, and she had never told a living soul what it had cost her to learn it.

And the rule no one spoke: no one in the valley asked where the memories went when Odile took them. The valley preferred its mercies unexamined.

It was market day, the kind of morning that made even the mist seem generous. Woodsmoke and wet wool, frying onions at Marenna's stall, the ferry bell clanging its slow bronze heartbeat across the water. Odile moved through all of it in her grey coat with her hood back and her hands in her pockets, and no one looked at her twice, which was exactly how she liked it. She was the woman who bought eggs and paid too much for bread and once pulled old Tam's grandson out of the flood channel. She was furniture with a pulse. She had spent years making herself into furniture, and she had believed, right up until that morning, that she was content with the upholstery.

She was haggling over winter apples—purely for the pleasure of it, since she'd stolen the memory of a perfect orchard autumn from a dying farmer two springs past and hadn't actually craved fruit since—when the stranger walked out of the mist at the north end of the square.

Later, she would try to reconstruct that first sight and find it stubbornly incomplete, the way dreams are. She remembered his boots first, because they were wrong: road-worn leather of a cut no cobbler in the valley made, mud on them from clay that wasn't valley clay, reddish, iron-rich. She remembered that he walked like a man who knew precisely how many steps remained between himself and his destination. And she remembered that the crowd parted around him without knowing they were doing it, the way water parts around a keel.

He was not beautiful, exactly. He was *arranged* beautifully, which is a different thing. Dark hair pushed back from a face that seemed perpetually on the verge of deciding whether to be amused. A traveler's cloak, good wool, dyed the deep blue-black of deep water. And eyes the color of that same water—grey-green, patient, and fixed on her from across the entire square as though everyone else in it were scenery.

No one looked at her twice. That was the arrangement. That was the whole architecture of her life.

This man looked at her the way people look at a lighthouse.

Her first instinct was the professional one: assess, catalog, dismiss. Cutpurse? No—he carried himself like money, not need. Inquisitor? The word surfaced from somewhere cold and slid away again; there were no inquisitors here, there was nothing here to interrogate. Debt collector? She owed no debts except the ones she kept in glass.

Then he crossed the square, weaving through carts and children and Marenna's onion smoke, and stopped an arm's length away, and smiled, and said:

"Odile Eastwick."

Not a question. Not a guess. Her name, spoken in a low warm voice that put the stress in the wrong place—not *Odile* Eastwick, the way the valley said it, but O*dile*, the way her grandmother had said it, the way nobody living said it anymore.

The apple seller's voice went distant. The mist pressed close. Somewhere behind her ribs, under eleven years of careful upholstery, something sat up.

"That's a name," Odile said, "and I'm sure it belongs to somebody. But this is the Hollow Valley, friend, and names are cheap here. Everyone's got a spare."

"Your grandmother was Sable Eastwick," he said. "A trader, before the roads changed. She used to say your name that way—O*dile*—because she learned it from a song." He tilted his head, and the amusement in his face settled into something older and stranger. "You have her hands. You probably don't know that. There's no one left who would tell you."

Odile set down the apple she was holding very gently, the way one sets down a thing that might go off.

"I'm going to ask you a question," she said, "and I want you to think hard before answering, because the answer determines whether we have a pleasant conversation or whether I scream thief and watch six farmers introduce you to their fists. How do you know my grandmother?"

"Because I've been looking for her family," he said, "for longer than I'd like to admit. And because I found you, which—" he glanced up, at the mist, at the mountains that declined to be crossed by strangers, and his smile turned wry and a little rueful, "—should have been impossible. I've been walking toward this valley for nine days. I should have walked out of the pass nine times. Something let me through."

"Something's asleep, more likely," Odile said. But her heart was doing something unprofessional. Because he was right—that was the trouble. Outsiders did not find the valley. The mist did not part. And yet here stood a man with foreign mud on his boots saying her grandmother's name in her grandmother's cadence, looking at her like a lighthouse, and every instinct she owned was ringing like a struck bell.

"You're afraid of me," he observed. "That's sensible. But you're also curious, and curiosity is going to win, so let me save us both the hour. My name is Caspian. I'm staying at the ferry inn because it's the only inn. And I would like very much to buy you dinner tonight."

"Why?"

"Because," Caspian said, "I know things about you that you don't know about yourself, and I've learned that those conversations go better over stew than over market stalls." He stepped back, unhurried, and inclined his head with a formality that belonged to some other century. "Sunset. The corner table by the window. I'll order the trout; you strike me as someone who resents choosing."

And he walked away into the crowd, which closed behind him like water, and Odile stood among the apples feeling like a woman whose pocket had been picked without her feeling a single tug.

* * *

She spent the afternoon telling herself she wouldn't go.

She cleaned the cellar shelves, which needed no cleaning. She counted her vials—one hundred and forty-three, each labeled in her small precise hand: *First snow, seen from a sickbed. A wedding, mostly the dancing. Learning to swim, age seven, the terror and then the joy.* A thousand lives in glass, and hers the only life she'd never lived inside. She held the vial marked *First snow* and thought about the dying ferryman she'd taken it from, how he had smiled as it left him, grateful someone wanted it, and she thought: *this is what I am. A magpie. A well-kept attic.*

She told herself she wouldn't go because going meant wanting, and wanting meant being visible, and visible things got taken. That was the oldest law she knew, older than the valley, older than the mist.

At sunset she was standing outside the ferry inn with her hood up, hating herself with tremendous efficiency.

Inside, the common room smelled of tallow and trout and wet dog—two drovers had come in ahead of her—and Caspian had, of course, taken the corner table by the window. Two places set. He saw her in the doorway and didn't wave, didn't smile too widely, just moved the pitcher an inch to make room, as if she were expected, as if she had always been expected, and that small presumption undid her more than any grand gesture could have. When had anyone last set a place for her?

She sat. She kept her hood up out of spite for another full minute, then put it down, because spite was a luxury and the trout smelled extraordinary.

"You resent choosing," she said.

"I gambled."

"And if I hadn't come?"

"You'd have come tomorrow instead, having spent the night composing arguments against me." He poured her wine without asking—valley plum wine, rough as gravel, served in cups that had seen better centuries. "You'd have wanted to deliver them in person. You strike me as someone who hates leaving arguments unsaid more than she hates losing them."

"You've known me one afternoon."

"I've known *of* you considerably longer." He said it lightly, but something underneath the lightness creaked, a floorboard bearing weight it shouldn't. He caught it, smoothed it over, lifted his cup. "To impossible meetings."

"To strangers who shouldn't exist," Odile countered, and drank, and watched him over the rim.

Here is what she noticed, in the way thieves notice: he ate like a man remembering food rather than enjoying it, savoring each bite with a focus slightly too intense, as though trout were a memory he was handling carefully. His hands were clean but calloused in odd places, the palm-heels, the fingertips—hands that gripped things often but not tools. And twice, when the fire popped or a chair scraped, he flinched—not outwardly, not visibly, but she saw the stillness that followed the flinch, the half-second recalibration, a man listening to a room the way she listened to pockets.

He was hiding something large. The knowledge sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

But—and this was the treacherous part, the part she would turn over for many sleepless nights after—being across a table from someone who *saw* her was like sunlight after eleven years of mist. He asked questions and waited for the answers. He laughed at her deflections instead of being defeated by them, which made her want to sharpen them. When she made a joke about the innkeeper's catastrophic gravy he laughed fully, head tipped back, and the sound startled her so much she nearly spilled her wine, because she realized she couldn't remember the last time she'd made anyone laugh on purpose rather than stealing the memory of laughter from someone funnier.

"So," she said finally, when the plates were ruins and the candles had burned down a finger's width. "You know my name. You know my grandmother. You found an unffindable valley. Either you're the most persistent man alive or something wanted you here." She folded her hands. "I don't buy coincidence, Caspian. I sell memories, and coincidences are the cheapest counterfeit in the trade. So. Why me?"

For a long moment he studied her, and she watched the amusement drain out of his face and leave something rawer, something almost frightened, and she understood suddenly that he had rehearsed this evening and was now discovering the rehearsal useless.

"Because in nine days," he said quietly, "when every road and every ruse failed to bring me to this place, I stopped at the last shrine on the mountain road—the old covenant shrine, cracked and forgotten—and I swore that if the valley would have me, I would tell you everything, all at once, however mad it sounds." He reached into his coat and drew out a book.

It was old. That was the first thing. Old the way bones are old—not worn but *finished*, complete in its age, its leather cover gone the color of dried blood, its corners rubbed round. He laid it on the table between the candle stubs, and Odile felt the hair rise on her arms, because the valley was magic-dead, magic-proof, magic-*forgetting*, and yet the book seemed to sit in the candlelight like a stone sitting in a stream, the light bending around it strangely.

"What is it?" she asked, and hated how quiet her voice came out.

"A ledger of sorts. Prophecies. Recorded a hundred years ago, give or take, by an order that no longer exists." His fingers rested on the cover without opening it. "Tomorrow I'll show you the pages. Tomorrow I'll explain the Ninefold Court, and the covenant, and the war that's coming down out of the east like weather. Tonight I'll only show you one thing, because if I show you more you'll run, and if I show you nothing you'll leave anyway, and I find I can't bear either."

"Caspian."

"Tonight," he said, "one page."

He opened the book. The spine crackled softly, a sound like a knuckle. The pages were heavy vellum, yellowed, covered in a fine angular script, and he turned them with the reverence of a man handling ash, until he found the leaf he wanted and turned the book to face her.

The page was headed with a date.

Odile read it once. Then again. Then a third time, slowly, syllable by syllable, while the inn's noise receded to a hum and the candle flames leaned as though listening.

It was tomorrow's date. Day, month, year—all of it, exact, written in ink that had dried before her mother was born.

Below the date, in that same fine hand, a passage of perhaps fifteen lines. Most of it was dense with archaic phrasing she'd need daylight and patience to untangle. But three phrases leapt out at her the way names leap out of a crowd, because they were her own.

*...the thief shall come up out of the hollow...*

*...she who keeps others' yesterdays in glass...*

*...and her name is Odile.*

The inn swayed. She gripped the table edge. Somewhere a drover laughed at his own joke and the sound arrived late, muffled, underwater.

"That's not possible," she said. "Books rot. Ink fades. This is a forgery, a trick, a—" But even as she said it she was leaning closer, thief's eyes reading the page the way she read locks, checking the work, and the work was flawless. The ink had oxidized. The vellum had aged true. Whoever had written this had written it a century ago, and a century ago someone had written her name, her *name*, spelled correctly, in a valley that forgot itself, describing a girl who kept yesterday in glass.

"Who wrote this?" she whispered.

"The Order of the Ninefold Covenant," Caspian said. "They're dead. All of them. Dead a hundred years, every one." He watched her across the candlelight, and his voice dropped to nearly nothing. "But they weren't wrong. Odile—they were never once wrong."

She looked up from the page, from her own name sitting in its century-old ink like a trap already sprung, and found him watching her with an expression she couldn't entirely parse—wonder braided with guilt, tenderness braided with calculation, a man looking at a person he had sought for a very long time and was now, at last, terribly close to.

And beneath the thrill of being *seen*—truly seen, named, known, for the first time in her hidden life—something colder stirred in Odile Eastwick, the old animal caution, the pickpocket's wisdom: *no one finds a mark by accident.*

"You said tomorrow you'd explain everything," she said.

"I did."

"Then understand something first." She closed the book gently, her hand flat on the cover, over her own buried name. "I've spent my life taking things from people who trusted me. I know every shape a lie can wear. If that book says what you claim—if it truly has my name in it—then you didn't find me, Caspian. You were sent. And I will want to know, before this goes one step further, exactly *who did the sending.*"

Caspian held her gaze. For a heartbeat, something moved behind his grey-green eyes—regret, perhaps, arriving early for an appointment.

"Fair," he said softly. "Tomorrow, then. Everything."

Outside the inn's fogged windows, the mist pressed against the glass like a listener, and far to the east, beyond the mountains and the dark, the first outriders of a coming war were making camp—and none of them, yet, knew her name.

Only the book did.
