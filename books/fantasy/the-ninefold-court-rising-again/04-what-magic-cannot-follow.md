## 4. What Magic Cannot Follow

The Hollow Valley had no gates. That was the first thing a stranger needed to understand about it, and the last thing they ever did.

There was no wall, no ward-stone, no line drawn in salt or ash or old blood. The valley simply *ended*—one step you were inside it, and one step you were not—and the only people who ever found its edges were the ones who already knew where to look. For three hundred years it had been the place where magic could not follow: no scrying, no curses, no fae blood running hot in pursuit. The Nine Realms had broken their covenant a century ago, and the world outside had gone rotten with wild magic since, but the valley remained what it had always been—a hollow in the world's teeth, a pocket of quiet where the drowned and the hunted and the forgotten could set down whatever they carried and never be found by it again.

Odile Eastwick had lived there eleven years. She knew every stone of the ferry road, every bend of the grey river, every face that came down from the high passes looking thinner than when they'd left them. She knew which houses kept lamps lit past midnight for walkers coming home, and which doors were painted blue because blue was supposed to mean *safe* here, though nothing in the valley had ever needed to be safe *from*. She knew the smell of the baker's yeast on cold mornings, the sound of the loom-house clacking like patient rain, the exact weight of silence at dusk when the whole valley seemed to hold its breath and listen for nothing at all.

She had thought she would die there. Not soon—she was twenty-six, and thieves who planned well died old—but eventually, in some small house with a good view of the water, with her hoard of stolen days arranged around her like a dragon's bed of coins.

She had not planned on Caspian.

He walked ahead of her now along the ferry road, his coat catching the wind off the river, moving with the particular confidence of a man who has never once been lost. He had told her to pack light. He had told her to say her goodbyes without explanations, because explanations would only frighten people, and because—"and I'm sorry for this part"—no one in the valley could follow them past the border. Magic could not follow into the valley; by the same mercy, or the same cruelty, those touched by magic could not easily leave it.

"I still don't understand why we're walking," Odile said. "You claim to be an emissary of a court of nine immortal kings. Don't they have horses? Doors that go places?"

"The Court's roads are closed," Caspian said, not turning around. "Have been for a century. We walk the mortal way, like everything else that's been broken." He glanced back at her, and his smile did the thing it always did—the thing that made her feel simultaneously seen through and seen *to*, both of which were unbearable. "Besides. You'll want your legs under you when we cross."

"When we cross what?"

"The border."

Odile stopped walking.

The word sat between them on the road like something dropped. She looked past him, down the valley, to where the river bent north around the shoulder of Greyfell Hill and the ferry road ran out of sight behind the last stand of alders. Everyone in the valley knew the border was there, the way everyone knows where the horizon is. Nobody went there. There was nothing to go *for*.

"You said dinner," she said. "You said there was a book, and my name in it, and a date. You didn't say anything about leaving."

"I said a great many things over two dinners," Caspian said. "I find it's easier to be believed a little at a time." He turned fully now, and the wind pushed his dark hair back from his face, and for once he wasn't smiling. "Odile. Everything I told you is true. The prophecy, the date, your name written in ink before your grandmother drew breath. I know how it sounds. I know what it costs you to hear it. But the Ninefold Court is dying, and the free cities are next after it, and the person the prophecy names is standing on this road arguing with me about horses."

"And if I don't come?"

"Then I go alone, and I fail, and in ten years or twenty there won't be anywhere left that magic can't follow." He said it gently, which was worse. "I'm not dragging you, Odile. I'm asking."

She looked back the way they'd come. Far down the road, the village lay in its bowl of green—smoke rising straight from forty chimneys, the loom-house clacking, the baker's boy hauling his cart up from the mill with that familiar tuneless whistling she'd heard every morning for eleven years. Nobody there knew what she was. Nobody there knew what she could do, what she had done, the thousand small thefts of yesterday she'd committed against strangers passing through. In the valley she was simply Odile Eastwick, who kept to herself, who mended nets in winter, whose eyes were the color of a river in November. A woman of no consequence in a place of no consequence.

A cage, said a voice in her head that sounded unpleasantly like Caspian. A very comfortable cage, with the door painted shut.

"They'll wonder what happened to me," she said.

"No," Caspian said softly. "They won't. That's the mercy of this place. Grief can't follow either."

She wanted to hit him for that. She wanted to hit him because it was true.

* * *

She spent her last evening in the valley doing ordinary things, and each one felt like reading her own eulogy.

She ate supper at Mother Ilse's table, where she'd eaten every seventh-night for a decade, and let the old woman fuss over her portion and complain about her boots. She helped haul the ferry chain for the last crossing, her shoulders remembering the rhythm, the river sliding black and silver beneath the boards. She walked past her own house—the little stone cottage with the blue door, because even here she had wanted the symbol—and stood in the road looking at the lamp she'd left burning in the window, and made herself not go in and blow it out. Let it burn. Let some traveler see it and think someone was waiting up for them. It would be the warmest lie she ever told.

Her hoard she packed in oilcloth, wrapped tight against her ribs: the vials and locket-cases and wax-sealed stoppers, a thousand stolen moments sleeping in glass. First snow on a dying man's tongue. A wedding dance in a city she'd never seen. A mother's hands braiding hair. The taste of honey wine at a festival whose name she'd taken along with the memory of it. Eleven years of harvest, none of it hers, all of it hers, heavier than iron and lighter than air.

Never steal pain. Never steal from family. Never leave the valley.

Two rules out of three, she was keeping tonight. It would have to do.

At dawn she met Caspian at the north bend of the river, where the alders gave way to bare hillside and the road climbed toward the pass. Mist lay on the water like a sheet pulled over a face. She had a pack on her back and her grandmother's knife at her belt and a feeling in her chest like a door swinging on rusted hinges.

"Last chance to tell me it's all a con," she said. "That there's no prophecy, no court, no war. That you're just a very elaborate pickpocket and I'm the mark."

"I could tell you that," Caspian said. "You wouldn't believe me. You've already checked my pockets twice."

"Three times," Odile said. "You keep interesting things in your left boot."

"That's fair." And then, without any ceremony at all, without trumpets or omens or so much as a change in the wind: "The border's just past the cairn. Twenty steps, maybe thirty. When you feel it, stop walking. Let it come slowly. Fighting it makes it worse."

"Feel what?"

He didn't answer. Of course he didn't. He just started walking, coat snapping in the wind, and after a moment—after one long look back at the valley, at the smoke rising straight as plumb lines from forty chimneys, at the blue door she couldn't see anymore but knew exactly where it was—Odile followed.

* * *

The first thing she noticed was the birdsong.

It was a stupid thing to notice first, and she would think so later, but it was true: the birds on the far side of the cairn sang differently. In the valley the songbirds had always sounded slightly *wrong* to her ear, thin and mechanical, notes without soul—as though they were imitating music they'd heard described rather than heard. She had assumed that was simply what birds were. Now, thirty steps past a pile of mossy stones, the dawn chorus opened above her like a cathedral door, and every note carried weight, and color, and *intention*, and Odile stopped dead on the road with her hand pressed to her sternum because something behind her ribs had begun to sing too.

"Oh," she said. Her voice came out strange, doubled, as though someone were whispering it back to her half a heartbeat late. "Oh, that's—"

"Breathe," Caspian said. He had stopped a few paces ahead and turned to watch her, and his face held something she hadn't seen on it before—not charm, not calculation, but a kind of grim tenderness, the look of a man watching someone take a wound he'd warned them about. "Let it come slowly. Don't fight it."

Magic was returning to her.

She hadn't known she was full of it. That was the joke, the filthy cosmic joke of it: eleven years in the one place where magic could not follow, and she had been carrying a hoard of it the entire time, packed against her ribs in oilcloth, a thousand stolen moments steeping in glass like preserves in a larder. Inside the valley they had been inert. Sleeping. She had taken them out sometimes at night, turned the vials in lamplight, felt nothing but the weight of them—beautiful, useless, safe. Mementos. Curiosities. A dragon's hoard made of glass.

Now, with every step past the cairn, they were waking up.

It started as warmth. Then as sound—faint, layered, coming from everywhere and nowhere, the way a seashell holds the sea. By the time the alders fell away behind her and the road began to climb through open moorland, the sounds had become scenes. Snow bloomed against her skin that was not falling. Music swelled in her ears that no fiddle within fifty miles was playing. Someone's hands—warm, chapped, beloved—closed around hers, and Odile gasped and staggered and caught herself against a boundary stone, because her body did not know the difference between a memory and a moment. It had never had to learn. In the valley, nothing had ever asked it to.

"They're stirring," Caspian said quietly. "All of them. Every memory you carry—they know they're home territory now. Magic recognizes magic."

"It feels like—" She groped for the words, and they came up dripping. "It feels like being a house with all the doors open in a storm. Like everything I ever took wants *out*."

"Or in. Or through. It varies." He moved closer, close enough that she could grip his sleeve without meaning to, and she did mean to, actually, and hated that she did. "This is the part I should have explained better over dinner. The valley doesn't just hide you from magic, Odile. It holds you together. Outside, you're not a woman who owns memories. You're a vessel with a thousand voices in it, and they all want the wheel."

The mist was burning off the moorland, and the world beyond the valley was opening up around her for the first time in eleven years, and it was *too much*. That was the truth of it, the plain ugly truth: the horizon went on and on, further than she remembered horizons going, hills rolling away into mountains rolling away into a sky so large it made her dizzy to look at. In the valley, the sky had been a ceiling of sensible size. Here the sky was a throat. Here the wind smelled of pine and far-off rain and something else, something older and stranger underneath—wild magic, rotted and unbound, seeping up from a broken covenant like water through the floor of a ruined house. She could taste it. Gods help her, she could *taste* it, coppery and sweet, and some deep animal part of her flinched from it while another part leaned into the wind like a starving woman smelling bread.

And underneath all of it, growing louder with every step, came the grief.

She hadn't expected the grief. She had expected vertigo, yes—her feet kept trying to convince her the ground was tilting, and twice she had to stop and put her hand flat on a stone until the world agreed to hold still. She had expected the strangeness, the noise, the overwhelming *bigness* of a world she'd shrunk herself to avoid. But grief arrived unannounced, settling over her shoulders like a wet cloak, and it took her a mile of walking to understand what it was mourning.

Not the village. Not Mother Ilse's table, or the loom-house clacking, or the blue door. Those hurt, but they were simple hurts, clean as bone.

What she mourned was the version of herself she'd been for eleven years. The woman who had believed the valley was a refuge. Who had told herself, every single morning for four thousand mornings, *I am safe here, I am hidden here, I am free here*—when the truth, which rose in her now with every stir of waking magic, was that she had chosen the smallest room in the world and called it freedom. She had a thousand lives in glass at her ribs and she had lived none of them. She had watched a whole world rot beyond her borders—had *felt* it rotting, now, tasted the wrongness on the wind—and she had stayed, because staying was easy, because hiding was the one thing she had ever truly mastered. The valley had protected her from everything except herself. That was what cages did. That was what cages were *for*.

"Stop," she said, and stopped, and stood on the moor with the wind pulling her hair southward, back toward everything she'd left, and pressed both hands over the bundle at her chest as though holding a wound closed. "Give me a moment."

Caspian, to his credit, said nothing. He stood a few paces off with his back to her, giving her the courtesy of his silence, watching the road ahead where it climbed toward a pass still white with old snow. Waiting. He was good at waiting. She was beginning to suspect it was one of the few honest things about him.

Below her, tiny with distance, the Hollow Valley lay in its cradle of hills, veiled in morning gold. From up here she could see the shape of the whole thing—the river's grey ribbon, the cluster of roofs, the fields stitched neat as patchwork—and she understood, with a clarity that felt like a second theft, how small it was. How small she had made her life. A pocket. A hollow. A place named for the shape of an absence.

Somewhere down there, a lamp was still burning in her window.

"Who did you used to be?" she asked suddenly. "Before you came to find me. Before the Court sent you. Everyone comes from somewhere, Caspian. Even messengers."

He was quiet long enough that she thought he might not answer. When he did, his voice had lost its polish, and that frightened her more than the vertigo had.

"Someone who made a promise he shouldn't have," he said. "To people who trusted him. And I've spent a hundred years learning what that promise cost." He turned and looked at her then, and the morning light caught his face, and for the first time she saw the age under the youth of him—the depth of him, like looking down a well and realizing the water is much farther than you thought. "You'll hate me before this is over, Odile. I need you to know that I already know that. And I need you to come anyway."

The stolen memories stirred against her ribs, restless, murmuring, a congregation waking in a locked church. The wind carried the taste of copper and rain and distant burning. Somewhere beyond the pass, in cities she had only ever visited as other people's memories, a war was eating the world one free town at a time, and a usurper's inquisitors were hunting every soul who could do what she could do—and according to a book written before her birth, the fate of nine realms hung on a thief from a valley where magic couldn't follow.

She adjusted the straps of her pack. She squared her shoulders against the wind. She took one last look south—at the gold-veiled valley, at the blue door she couldn't see, at the life she was leaving burning in the window like an offering to no one—

—and turned north, and climbed.

Behind her, the mist closed over the Hollow Valley like a page turning.

Ahead, on the road where it crested the pass, Caspian had gone very still. He was staring at something beyond the ridge, and his hand had dropped to the knife at his belt, and when he spoke, all the gentleness had gone out of his voice.

"Odile," he said. "We have company. And I don't think it waited for us to cross."
