## 5. The Free City of Bellharbor

The road to Bellharbor smelled of salt and smoke, and Odile could not tell which of the two she should fear more.

They had walked three days since crossing the valley's border—three days of Caspian walking half a step ahead like a man who knew the way because he had dreamed it, three days of her stolen memories rustling inside her skull like moths against glass. The world beyond the Hollow Valley was louder than any memory she had ever taken. Wind had opinions here. Rivers argued with their banks. And everywhere, threading beneath it all, ran that faint wrongness she had felt the moment magic touched her again: a sourness in the air, like fruit going over, as though the land itself were quietly rotting from its edges inward.

"The borders are failing," Caspian said, when she finally named it aloud on the second evening. He said it the way other men said *it might rain*. "Where the covenant is broken, magic pools and stagnates instead of flowing. You'll see worse before you see better."

She did not ask him how long the covenant had been broken. He would only say *a century* again, in that maddening, gentle voice, as if a hundred years of unraveling were a fact one could simply carry, like a stone in a pocket.

Now, cresting the last hill at dusk of the third day, she saw Bellharbor spread below them like a spilled jewelry box—and understood that she was looking at a wound pretending to be a city.

It was beautiful. That was the horror of it. Canals ran through it in silver braids, catching the last copper light, and lanterns were already waking along the quays, hundreds of them, thousands, so that the waterways glowed like veins of molten gold through the dark body of the town. Ships rode at anchor in the outer harbor, their rigging black lace against a bruised-purple sky. Gulls wheeled and screamed. Somewhere a bell was ringing the evening hour, and the sound rolled out over the water and came back changed.

But there were too many ships.

Odile counted without meaning to—the habit of a thief's eye, always tallying what could be lifted, what could be sold, what was watched. There should have been cargo ships, fishing boats, ferries. Instead the harbor was choked with lean, shallow-draughted vessels flying no colors at all, packed gunwale to gunwale, and every one of them crowded with people. Not sailors. *Passengers.* She could see them even at this distance: small dark clusters along the rails, hanging bedding over the sides to dry, cooking over little fires on deck because there was nowhere else to cook.

"Refugees," she said.

"The eastern cities fell first," Caspian said. "Veyran doesn't burn them. He doesn't have to. He sends his inquisitors in and takes everyone's memories of resistance, of loyalty, of anything worth fighting for. What's left walks out on its own feet." He paused. "Most of them."

Odile looked at him. In the dying light his face was all planes and shadow, handsome the way a coin is handsome—struck, identical on both sides, hiding nothing except everything. "You keep saying things like that," she said. "*Most of them.* Like you're reading me a ledger."

"I'm trying to prepare you."

"For what?"

He didn't answer. He started down the hill toward the gates, and after a moment she followed, because following him was the only plan she had, and she hated how easily her feet had learned that lesson.

* * *

Bellharbor took her in the way a tide takes in a piece of driftwood—indifferently, thoroughly, tumbling her along streets that smelled of fish brine, tar, frying onions, woodsmoke, and under all of it, faintly, sweetly, something that made the back of her teeth ache.

She stopped dead in the middle of the Fishmarket Bridge.

"What is that?" she breathed.

Caspian followed her gaze down an alley mouth where a woman sat behind a table hung with hundreds of small glass vials, each stoppered with wax, each holding a curl of light no bigger than a firefly—a different color for each one. Rose-gold. Green as pond water. One, near the end of the row, a deep bruised violet that Odile felt in her chest before she consciously recognized it.

"Memory," Caspian said. "Loose memories. Bought and sold." He glanced at her sidelong. "I assumed your valley kept you innocent of the trade."

"We don't trade," Odile said stiffly. "We take, and we keep, and we give back sometimes, if the taking was—" She stopped. *If the taking was kind*, she had almost said, and heard how thin that sounded spoken into a city where memory came by the vial. "That violet one," she said instead. "What is it?"

Caspian didn't need to look. "Grief. Someone bottled their mourning and sold it rather than carry it." He shrugged. "There's a market for everything. Some people would rather be empty than hurt."

Odile stared at the vials until the woman behind the table noticed her staring and drew them closer, one hand cupping the row protectively, and something about that small instinctive gesture—that even here, in this city of buyers and sellers, a keeper still shielded her wares like a mother shielding children—made Odile walk on faster than she meant to.

The refugee markets were strung along the canals like laundry. Tarps and sailcloth stretched between lampposts; cook-fires in iron buckets; children asleep in rows on woven mats while their parents sat awake beside them, faces lit orange, speaking in low voices in four or five languages Odile half-recognized from stolen summers spent in port towns she had never actually visited. A boy no older than Wick-that-was-not-yet-Wick hawked skewers of eel. An old man had laid out his entire life for sale on a blanket: a wedding ring, a carpenter's plane, a child's carved horse, a folded letter gone soft at the creases from being read too many times. He wasn't begging. He was selling. His eyes were the worst part—not desperate, not yet, but *deciding*, weighing each object, deciding which pieces of himself he could bear to become without.

Odile bought two eel skewers with coins she'd lifted from a toll collector outside the city gates, and told herself the theft balanced out. It was the first lie she'd told herself since crossing the border that didn't sit comfortably.

"They're not from the war," she said, around a mouthful of eel, watching a family unpack bedding beneath a bridge arch. The mother had a shawl knotted over her hair and hands that moved with the automatic competence of someone who had made a home out of nothing six times already this year. "Look at them. They're not soldiers' families. They're just—" She gestured helplessly at the whole sprawling, flickering market. "People. Farmers. Shopkeepers."

"That's what I've been telling you." Caspian's voice had lost its charm entirely; underneath it was something tired and old. "Veyran isn't conquering cities, Odile. He's *emptying* them. An army marches on bread and steel, but a king rules on certainty. Take a people's memories of why they resisted, and you never have to fight them at all. They wake up loyal." He looked down the canal, at the lantern-light trembling on black water. "The free cities are what's left. Bellharbor, Verance, Saltbridge, the rest. Places where enough people remembered enough, loudly enough, to hold."

"And they'll hold?"

"Ask me again tomorrow," Caspian said, "and I may give you a different answer."

* * *

They found lodging above a chandlery in the Rope Quarter, a room with a window overlooking a canal and a bed wide enough that they lay back to back like a pair of spoons put away wrong, not touching. Odile did not sleep. Her hoard was restless tonight—all thousand lives murmuring at once, stirred up by the smell of loose memory in the streets below, by the grief-violet glow she could still see if she closed her eyes. The ferryman's first snow kept surfacing, cold and clean and impossibly far away, a small white country she used to own.

Near midnight she gave up, dressed, and went down to the canal alone. She told herself it was to walk. She told herself a great many things.

The city at night was a different animal. The markets had folded into themselves; the canals ran quiet; the lanterns burned low over water the color of strong tea. Odile walked the towpath with her hood up, letting the noise of her hoard settle, and that was when she heard the singing.

It came from a building across the water—a squat stone structure with narrow windows set high, wedged between a rope-walk and a cooperage, marked with a sign she couldn't read at this distance. The song was low and layered, many voices, men and women and children, rising and falling in a pattern that was less melody than breathing. It sounded, Odile thought with a jolt, like the way her hoard sounded on quiet nights. A thousand small voices keeping each other company.

She crossed the bridge.

Inside, past a door left open against the summer heat, was a hall lit by tallow candles, and the hall was full of people sitting on the floor in concentric rings around a central brazier. Old, young, whole families. At the center of the rings, tending the coals, stood a slight figure in dove-grey robes with silver thread at the cuffs—a memory-keeper. Odile knew the type at once, the way she'd known the vial-seller: the careful hands, the listening posture, the eyes that looked at people slightly *behind* their faces, where the memories lived. This one was young, hardly older than Odile herself, with close-cropped black hair and ink-stained fingers.

"What is this place?" Odile whispered to a woman near the door.

The woman smiled wearily. "A keeping-house, dear. Sister Vess keeps memories for folk who can't hold their own anymore—the addled, the grieving, the ones fleeing east with nothing but what's in their heads. They come and leave a memory in her care, like leaving a coat at a cloakroom, and take it back when they're settled. Or—" the woman's smile flickered, "—when their kin want to remember for them." She nodded toward the rings of singers. "We sing so the house stays warm. Kept memories go quiet and dim if nobody tends them. Hers are the loudest-kept in Bellharbor. Everyone says so."

Odile stood at the edge of the candlelight and listened to the singing and thought: *this is what I could have been, if the valley had doors instead of walls.*

She was still thinking it when the windows shattered.

Not all of them—just the high narrow ones on the street side, blown inward in a bloom of glass, and through them came shapes in grey-and-crimson livery, dropping on ropes with the smooth horrible coordination of spiders descending. The singing stopped mid-note. Candles guttered in the draft. And the temperature of the room changed—not colder, exactly, but *emptier*, the way air feels before lightning, and every hair on Odile's arms rose, and the hoard in her skull flinched as one.

Inquisitors. She knew it without being told. Three of them, boots crunching glass, crimson sashes, and each wearing at the temple a slim band of pale metal that hummed a note just below hearing—a note Odile felt plucking at her, at the seams of her, like fingers testing a purse-string.

"Sister Anwen Vess." The lead inquisitor was a broad woman with a face like a closed door and a voice pitched to carry without effort. She held a scroll she did not unroll. "By edict of King Veyran, the practice of memory-keeping is outlawed. All kept memories are forfeit to the Crown. Surrender your ledger and open your vaults, and the Crown will be merciful."

The young keeper—Vess—rose slowly from the brazier. She was trembling. Odile saw it plainly, saw her fists shake at her sides, and saw her plant her feet anyway.

"There are four hundred memories in my keeping," Vess said. Her voice cracked and steadied. "A grandmother's recipe. A drowned son's laugh. A wedding. A lullaby. None of them belong to your king. Some of their owners are *dead*, and these are all that's left of them, and I will not hand the dead to a thief in a crown."

"Then you are in violation," the inquisitor said, without heat, without hatred, which was somehow infinitely worse, "of the Edict of Memory. The penalty is consumption." She raised one hand, and the band at her temple began to sing.

Odile had stolen pain once, early on, by accident—a fisherman's crushed hand, taken in a moment of panic—and she had carried it three days before she found a way to give it back, and she had wept the whole time, and she had made her rule afterward: *never steal pain.* But she had never imagined pain like this. The inquisitor's art was theft weaponized, refined, industrialized. Odile felt the pull of it from across the room, felt her own hoard strain toward it like dogs toward a whistle, and understood in one white-hot flash what she was. What she had always been. A cottage industry of the same crime, done gently, done kindly, done for bread—but the same.

"No!" Vess threw up her hands and the brazier flared—but she was a keeper, not a fighter; her craft was tending, not taking. The inquisitor's power closed over her like a net.

What happened next, Odile's mind recorded in terrible, perfect detail, the way it recorded everything, the curse of a memory-thief: that nothing can ever be blurred or softened or forgotten.

Vess screamed, and then she didn't scream, and that was worse—her face going smooth and vacant as the inquisitors *drained* her, pulling the kept memories out through her, using her as a spigot, four hundred lives tearing loose through a single frail person. The vials of her vault burst in their cases, lights streaming out like freed birds, and the inquisitors caught them, funneled them, *bottled* them midair into black lead caskets that they stowed with the efficiency of men loading fish. And Vess herself—when they had taken everything she kept, they took what she *was*, her own memories, her name, her training, her self, pouring her out into the casket too, until the dove-grey robes crumpled around a body that was no longer anyone at all.

And then, because the body was still breathing, and because the edict demanded consumption—

One of the inquisitors struck flint to a taper and touched it to the hem of the empty robes, and the flame climbed her like ivy, fast and terribly bright, and the rings of singers broke apart screaming, and mothers grabbed children, and the woman who had spoken to Odile at the door was on her knees making a sound Odile would hear for the rest of her life.

Odile did not move.

Later, hating herself, she would inventory the reasons: too many witnesses, armed enemies, Caspian waiting at the lodging, no plan. All true. All cowardice dressed as strategy. The truth was simpler and stood in the center of the burning: she had spent her whole life believing her gift was harmless because she was gentle with it. Now she had watched gentleness's opposite, and the difference was not kind versus cruel. It was scale. It was *purpose*. The inquisitor had emptied a woman to fill a king's coffers. Odile had emptied strangers to fill her own loneliness. The flame did not care about the distinction. The flame was teaching her the arithmetic of prophecy, the thing a century-old book could never convey: that every line of it would be paid for in bodies, and some of the bodies would be burned by people who told themselves stories exactly as comfortable as hers.

The inquisitors filed out through the broken windows, caskets in hand, unhurried. The lead inquisitor paused at the sill, swept her gaze across the survivors—across Odile, hood up, heart hammering, bands humming—and Odile felt that low note *taste* her, slide across her hoard like a tongue across teeth, linger a half-second too long.

Then they were gone, dropping into the dark, and the keeping-house burned itself out against wet stone, and the singing did not start again.

* * *

She got back to the chandlery as the sky was graying. Caspian was awake, sitting fully dressed by the window, and the look on his face when she entered told her he had seen the glow, heard the screams, known exactly where she had been.

"Now you understand," he said softly. "This is what the prophecy weighs. This is what Veyran builds his throne on. Every day we walk, more houses burn."

"Don't." Her voice came out flat and strange. "Don't use it. Don't make it a *reason*. She had a name. Vess. She was twenty-five years old and she kept a drowned boy's laugh safe for his mother, and they poured her into a box and lit her like a candle, and you want to make it an argument for the righteousness of our cause." She pressed her palms against her eyes. Behind them, unbidden, bloomed the ferryman's snow, falling forever in a valley that suddenly seemed very small and very far away. "I stole a thousand memories, Caspian. A *thousand*. I told myself it was mercy, that I gave them back when I could, that I never took pain. Standing in that room tonight, watching them do it with armies and ledgers—I couldn't tell you honestly what separates me from them. Scale. Only scale."

Caspian was quiet a long moment. When he spoke, it was carefully, the way a man walks on river ice. "You gave back," he said. "That's the difference. They never give anything back."

"You don't know that I gave back all of them."

Something crossed his face then—fast, quickly buried, but Odile had spent a lifetime reading the space behind people's faces, and she caught it. A flinch. A door closing.

"Caspian," she said slowly. "What aren't you telling me?"

He smiled, and it was the charming stranger's smile from the first night in the valley, and for the first time it looked like something worn rather than something grown. "Get some sleep," he said. "Tomorrow we buy passage upriver toward Verance. There's someone there you need to meet—a priestess of the old covenant. If anyone can teach you what your gift is truly for, before you have to use it—it's her."

He turned back to the window. Below, in the gray dawn, a cart was passing along the quay, loaded with ash and charred timber from the keeping-house, and workers were already scrubbing the cobbles, and by noon, Odile understood, there would be no trace that Sister Anwen Vess had ever kept a single thing safe for anyone.

She lay down on the bed and did not sleep, and somewhere in the restless dark of her hoard, a thousand borrowed voices murmured—and beneath them, new and cold, a question she could not silence:

*What else has he already decided I don't need to know?*
