## 6. The Usurper's Edict

The proclamation arrived in Bellharbor nailed to the customs house door, and Caspian read it three times before his hands stopped shaking.

BY DECREE OF VEYRAN THE FIRST, TRUE KING OF THE NINE REALMS, it began, in ink so heavy it had bled through the paper like a bruise. Memory-magic is hereby declared anathema. The theft of memory—the keeping of memory for trade, the bearing of another's past within one's flesh—is a crime against the natural order of mortal souls. Practitioners shall be taken. Their hoards shall be burned. They themselves shall be unmade in the public square, that all may witness the cleansing.

Below the decree, in smaller script, as though an afterthought—as though the clerk's hand had not trembled writing it—came the second clause:

Let it further be known that agents of the fallen Court walk among the faithful. Any soul with knowledge of the Covenant-Bearer, she who is named in the false prophecies, shall report to the Office of Inquisition. Rewards are offered. Silence will be judged complicity.

And below *that*, folded into the parchment's crease where only someone who knew to look would ever find it, was the list.

Caspian had known there would be a list. He had helped compile parts of it, two winters ago, in a cold room in Veyran's capital, before he understood what the lists were for. He had watched names go onto paper and told himself they were only names—that the Court's intelligence was a ledger like any other, that knowledge was not yet cruelty. He had been younger then. He had believed the ends of restoration could be separated from the means of spies.

His own handwriting was on this list. He recognized the slant of it, the way he crossed his sevens.

But it was the first name that made the harbor lights swim.

*Odile Eastwick.*

Not a description. Not a title, not *the thief of the Hollow Valley*, not *a woman of unknown aspect*. Her name. Her full name, spelled correctly, which meant someone had given it to the inquisitors whole—and Caspian stood in the lamplight of the customs house porch with the wind off the canals cutting through his coat, and did the arithmetic of betrayal, and found that the sum included himself.

He had given them the valley. That much was unavoidable; the Court had needed the Covenant-Bearer found, and Caspian was the only agent whose maps reached into the magic-dead places. But he had given them the valley, not the name. He had sworn to himself, in the particular way liars swear oaths to themselves, that her name would stay his. A single coin of secrecy, kept back from an otherwise honest purse.

Someone else had paid it. Someone who knew her. Someone close enough to know what her mother had called her at night.

Or—and the thought arrived with the gentleness of a knife entering—he had given more than he remembered giving. Names leaked. Names were water; you built a wall around them and they found the mortar's cracks. Perhaps he had said it once, tired, in a report he did not reread. Perhaps some clerk with a good ear had kept it the way Odile kept other people's snowfalls: stolen, hoarded, spent without permission.

It didn't matter how. It mattered that it was there, in ink, under seal, above a reward figure that would buy a fisherman's family ten lifetimes of comfort.

*She who is named in the false prophecies.* False. Veyran called the prophecy false, and hunted its subject anyway. Caspian almost admired the efficiency of the contradiction. Fear did not require belief. A king did not need to think a prophecy true to kill everyone it mentioned; he only needed to consider the possibility unbearable.

* * *

He found Odile at the refugee market near Saltcanal Bridge, and the sight of her stopped him longer than he wanted it to.

She was bargaining over a bolt of grey wool with a woman who had three children hanging off her like burrs, and she was losing the negotiation on purpose—Caspian could see it, the studied reluctance, the theatrical sigh before she paid too much. She wrapped the wool around the youngest child's shoulders herself, crouching to tuck the edge in, saying something that made the girl giggle. Then she straightened, caught him watching, and her face did the thing it always did: closed by degrees, shutter by shutter, until what remained was the polite mask of a stranger.

"You're staring," she said.

"I'm waiting."

"For what?"

"For you to admit you just gave away half our funds because a child looked cold."

Her mouth twitched. She smothered it. "I overpaid because the wool is grey and grey is ugly and ugliness should cost extra." She fell into step beside him, and they moved together into the market's current, shoulder to shoulder among the displaced of three provinces. "Well? You've got your court face on. What did the pretty paper say?"

So she'd seen him read it. Of course she had. He was beginning to understand that very little escaped her—not out of suspicion exactly, or not only, but out of the professional habit of a woman who had survived by noticing everything, because in the valley, unnoticed things were the ones that got stolen.

"It's an edict," he said. "Veyran has outlawed memory-magic throughout the Nine Realms. Theft, trade, keeping—all of it. Punishment is—" he chose the word on the parchment rather than a softer one, because she deserved the truth's weight if not its entirety, "—unmaking. Public."

Odile was quiet for four steps. Five. He counted.

"Charming," she said at last. "A king who's afraid of remembering. That tracks. Men who steal thrones usually want everyone else's memories gone—it makes the stealing easier the second time."

"He also named the Covenant-Bearer. There's a hunt. Rewards for information."

"Mm." She picked up an apple from a stall, weighed it, set it down again. "Do I get a reward for knowing myself? I'm told I'm the one in the prophecy."

She said it lightly, but Caspian heard the wire underneath. Since the valley, since the book with her date written in century-old ink, she had worn the prophecy the way she wore the grey wool market's noise: wrapped around her, useful, suffocating. And every day since Bellharbor's burning—since the inquisitor and the memory-keeper and the smell—he could feel her deciding, hour by hour, whether she believed in any of it enough to keep walking toward it.

"The list," he said, and stopped himself.

"The list?" One eyebrow. She had a thief's gift for hearing the sentence behind the sentence.

"There's a list of persons of interest. Agents of the old Court, sympathizers, that class of thing. You're on it. So am I. It's nothing you don't already know—you've seen the inquisitors work."

All true. All beside the point. He watched her accept it with a small nod, watched her file it away in whatever ledgers she kept behind her eyes, and hated himself with a clean and thorough hatred, the kind that felt almost virtuous, the kind that reformers mistake for conscience.

Because here was the shape of it, the shape he turned over every night when the others slept: the Court had sent him to find the Covenant-Bearer, and the Court's records—Sable's records, the herald-fragments, the scattered testimony of a century—described her in ways that were becoming harder to dismiss. Bloodlines. The Eastwick mark. A grandmother who traded in memories along routes no honest trader walked. Caspian had read those files by candlelight until the words blurred, and every new detail fit her like a key fits the lock it was cut for, and he had begun to suspect that he had not found Odile Eastwick at all.

He had been aimed at her.

And now her name sat at the top of a usurper's list, and he did not tell her that either, and the tenderness growing in him—this inconvenient, undisciplined, *mortal* tenderness, the kind the Court trained out of its agents and he had apparently failed to surrender—kept whispering that a secret kept for someone's protection was still a cage, only gilded. He had watched her live in a cage already. The valley was a cage she'd chosen, soft-walled, invisible. He would not build her a second one out of his own cowardice dressed up as mercy.

Tomorrow, he told himself. Tomorrow I'll tell her about the name, the bloodline, all of it.

He had been telling himself tomorrow for eleven days.

* * *

That evening the edict came alive.

They heard it before they saw it: bells, the wrong kind, the rapid cracked ringing that Bellharbor used for fire, and then the crowd's sound changing texture—from market murmur to something tighter, wetter, a sound like a held breath breaking. Caspian pushed through the press on Tanners' Walk, Odile a step ahead of him, small and quick and impossible to lose, and came out onto the canal embankment where the inquisitors had set their portable scaffold in the fish-market square.

There were six of them in their bone-pale masks, and one prisoner: an old man, a memory-keeper by the look of him, the kind who took in other people's griefs for safekeeping when families broke, who held the last words of the dead for the living who couldn't carry them. His hands were bound with cord soaked in salt-water so the marks wouldn't heal clean. An inquisitor stood at his shoulder reading the charges aloud, and the canal carried the voice to every bridge and balcony, because that was the point, that was always the point.

"—found keeping forty-one foreign memories upon his person, contrary to the King's Edict—"

Forty-one. Caspian did the arithmetic differently than the clerk had. Forty-one griefs. Forty-one last words. Forty-one pieces of other people's lives that this man had carried so no one else had to, and for that, the natural order of mortal souls required his unmaking.

"They won't really," Odile said beside him. Very quietly. Not a question. A hope wearing a question's clothes.

"They will."

"Why?" And there it was—the crack in her voice, the real one, the one she'd never have permitted in daylight. "Why does it matter to him? One old man in one canal city. Why spend inquisitors on—"

"Because every keeper is a library," Caspian said. "And Veyran is burning libraries."

The unmaking was not a fire, exactly. Fire would have been merciful and legible. The inquisitors used a rite—a wordless, grinding rite that Caspian felt in his teeth before he understood it with his mind, a resonance like a struck bell that never stops striking—and the old man's memories were drawn out of him into a copper vessel, all forty-one, visible for a moment in the vessel's mouth as a shimmering heat-haze of other lives, other voices, a bride laughing somewhere, a mother calling a child home—and then the inquisitor upended the vessel over the canal, and forty-one lives went into the black water and were gone, and the man who had carried them sagged between his guards like a coat falling off a hook.

What remained was breathing. That was the horror of the unmaking. It left the body and even, in a manner, left the mind—but emptied, smooth, a man-shaped absence. They propped him against a post afterward, per the edict's instruction, *that all may witness*. He smiled at the crowd. He would smile at anything now. He had nothing left to do but smile.

The crowd dispersed in silence, which was worse than screaming. Silence was what fear looked like when fear had finished learning its lesson.

Odile did not disperse. She stood at the embankment rail with her knuckles white on the stone, looking down at where the shimmer had touched the water, and Caspian watched her watch the canal as though she could see each sunk life descending, settling, joining the silt. When she finally spoke, her voice was stripped down to bare timber.

"That could have been me."

"Yes."

"In the valley I told myself it was different. What I did. I take a little, I give a little, everyone leaves lighter or happier, nobody—" She stopped. Restarted. "I never unmade anyone. I never *took everything*."

"No. You didn't."

"But it's the same crime on his books." She turned to look at him, and her eyes in the lamplight were the color of the canal, dark water over dark water. "Isn't it. To Veyran, to his inquisitors—I'm the same thing as that old man. Only worse. A keeper with a hundred times his hoard."

Caspian said nothing, because everything he could say was either a lie or a confession.

She laughed—one short, airless note with no floor under it. "And I'm the person the prophecy wants to *save* everyone. The Covenant-Bearer. Me." She pressed the heel of her hand against her sternum, hard, as though pressing something back inside. "I stole a ferryman's last snowfall, Caspian. I've stolen hundreds of moments that weren't mine because I was lonely and it was easy and it felt like living. And now there's a king who burns people for it, and a book that says I'm going to fix the world, and both of them are wrong about me, and I can't prove either one wrong, and—"

"Odile."

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't comfort me. You're bad at it and we both know why." Her gaze sharpened, sudden and surgical, the look she used on locks and lies. "You know more than you're saying. You always know more than you're saying. I can hear it rattling around in you like coins in a purse."

The canal lapped at the pilings. Somewhere across the water, a fiddler began to play, badly, defiantly, the way musicians play in cities that have just remembered they can die.

"What I know," Caspian said carefully, "is that the list exists, and that we leave Bellharbor tomorrow, north road, before the inquisitors finish mapping the refugee quarters. What I know is that Sable says the next safehouse is Verance, and Verance has a council, and councils mean politics, and politics mean time, which we are running out of. What I know is that you are the bravest person I have met in two centuries of meeting people, and that bravery and terror are neighbors and share a wall."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

She searched his face for a long moment—the face he had polished for decades precisely so that searches would find nothing—and whatever she found or didn't find, she let it go, turning back to the black water where forty-one lives lay sinking into the mud of Bellharbor.

"When we get to Verance," she said, "you're going to tell me the rest. Whatever 'the rest' turns out to be. Swear it."

"I swear it."

Liar, said the part of him that kept the ledgers. Liar, liar, agent, spy. And beneath that, quieter, more dangerous, in a voice he did not recognize as his own training or his own duty: *tell her, tell her tonight, tell her about the name at the top of the list and the grandmother and the blood and the way you were aimed at her like an arrow, tell her before she reads it in someone's captured papers, tell her while she can still hate you for the reason instead of the method—*

Instead he said, "You should sleep. We ride at dawn."

And Odile Eastwick, the Covenant-Bearer, the thief of yesterday, nodded and walked back toward the safehouse through streets that smelled of smoke and brine, and Caspian stayed at the rail a while longer, alone with the edict folded in his coat like a knife he couldn't stop touching, listening to the bad fiddler play, and doing the only arithmetic left to him: counting the days remaining before his secrets came due, and wondering, with the first honest prayer he'd offered in a hundred years, whether there would be enough tomorrows to hold them all.

By morning, the north road out of Bellharbor was already whispering with rumor: that the inquisitors had a new quarry, a woman, unnamed but described; that the reward had doubled overnight; that she was traveling with companions—a priestess, a foundling, a child.

The hunt, when it came, would come faster than any of them planned for.
