## 7. A Foundling, a Priest, a Lockpick

Bellharbor smelled of salt and smoke and something sweeter underneath—fried dough from the refugee stalls along the Canal of Nine Bells, where vendors shouted prices in four languages and nobody trusted the coin of any of them.

I had been in the city three days, and I had learned three things. First: the inquisitors wore ash-grey cloaks and moved in pairs, always in pairs, because a memory-hunter alone could be ambushed by anyone whose family they'd emptied. Second: the canals ran green-black at dusk and reflected nothing honestly, which made them good places to think and better places to disappear things. Third: I could not do this alone.

That third lesson had cost me a night's sleep and most of my pride, so I spent the morning spending it—walking the markets, listening the way Caspian had taught me to listen on the road, not for words but for the shape underneath them. The shape of people who had lost something and were angry enough to want it back.

* * *

Brannic found me before I found him, which I suppose should have been my first clue that he was worth finding.

He was sitting on the steps of the Tidewalkers' Chapel with his long legs folded under him like a heron's, grey-skinned and grey-eyed, with the faint tracery of fae blood showing at his temples like frost ferns on glass. A foundling, the chapel sisters called children like him—left at some border shrine during the first years after the covenant broke, when magic curdled in the veins of half-born children and their mortal parents couldn't bear to look at what it made them.

He heard me coming before I reached the bottom step.

"You walk quiet," he said, without opening his eyes. "But doors talk louder."

"I'm sorry?"

"Doors." He opened his eyes then, and they were the pale grey of canal fog. "Every door in this city has a voice. Hinges, mostly. But doors remember being opened and closed. They know who comes and goes. They've been screaming for a week about you—the inn on Rope Street, the chandler's on Salt Lane, the archive on Candlemaker's Row." He tilted his head. "You read a lot for a thief."

"I'm not a thief," I said, out of habit rather than conviction.

"Everyone who isn't a thief says that exactly that way." He unfolded himself and stood, and up close he was taller than I'd guessed, all elbows and patience. "You're the one the heralds are whispering about. Covenant-Bearer. There's a price on your name in three cities and a prayer for you in two."

"And which are you doing? Pricing or praying?"

"Neither." Something flickered across his face—not quite a smile, but the place where one would live if it ever visited. "The doors told me you'd come. They've been telling me for three days. *She'll need someone who hears,* they said. And I have been waiting on these steps getting rained on, so I hope you're worth it."

It occurred to me, standing there in the thin drizzle with a stranger who spoke to architecture, that Caspian had never once asked whether I wanted to be recruited either. Perhaps that was simply how prophecy worked—it arrived, sat down uninvited, and waited to be fed.

"Why?" I asked. It was the only question I trusted myself with. "Why help? You don't know me."

"No," Brannic agreed. "But I know the Court. Or—" he touched the frost-fern tracery at his temple, gentle, like touching an old scar, "—I know what's left of it in my blood. When the covenant broke, everything fae in me went quiet. Like a room with the furniture covered in sheets. I've lived my whole life in that room, listening to doors because nothing else would speak." His grey eyes fixed on mine, and there was a hunger in them I recognized, because I kept the same one behind my ribs. "You're going to wake it up. Everyone says so. So yes, Odile Eastwick. I'll come. I'll carry whatever you need carried, open whatever needs opening. Just don't ask me to stop listening."

We shook hands on it, his grip cool as stone in shade, and that was the whole ceremony. Later I would learn that Brannic did everything that way—no oaths, no speeches, just a decision made once and then honored until it killed him or didn't.

* * *

Mother Sable took more convincing, which surprised no one who had ever met her.

I tracked her to the Lantern District, to a soup kitchen run out of a deconsecrated shrine, where she ladled barley broth to refugees with hands that had clearly once performed finer work. She was sixty if she was a day, built like a dock crane, with iron hair cropped short and the faded tattoo of a nine-pointed star on the back of her right hand—scratched over, deliberately, by someone who had wanted very badly to stop being what she'd been.

"Defrocked," she said, before I'd finished my first sentence. "Unfrocked. Unrobed. Whatever word the Synod uses now. I know why you're here, girl. Everyone knows why you're here. The answer is no."

"I haven't asked anything yet."

"You were going to ask me about the old covenant. About the rites of the Ninefold Court. About whether the oath can be spoken again by mortal lips." She set down the ladle with the deliberate care of a woman who wanted very much to throw it. "I spent forty years studying those rites. I sang them at novices who are dead now, burned or hollowed by Veyran's inquisitors while I hid in kitchens like this one. So spare me the hopeful eyes. The covenant is ash. Ash doesn't rise."

"Prophecy says otherwise."

"Prophecy," Mother Sable said, "is a knife somebody else holds."

We stared at each other across the soup pot, and around us the refugees ate in silence, hollow-cheeked men and women from the border villages, from the free towns already fallen. A child coughed. Somewhere outside, bells rang the hour, all nine of them, though the district only had five towers—the extra three came from memory, Sable told me later, from a time when the city had been fuller and its bells more numerous, and the sound lingered the way grief lingers.

"Who broke it?" I asked quietly. "The covenant. Do the records say?"

Something moved behind her eyes—a door closing, or trying to. "The records say a theft. That's all any of them say. A theft at the heart of the Court, a hundred years ago, and no thief ever named." She turned back to her pot. "Go away, Covenant-Bearer. Find someone younger to disappoint."

I should have gone. Instead I picked up a bowl and started ladling broth beside her, and we worked in silence for an hour, and somewhere in that silence she decided something, because when the last of the refugees filed out she said, without looking at me:

"There's a ledger. Inquisitor Maren Voss keeps it herself. Names, thefts, debts of memory. If your name is written anywhere in this world in ink that matters, it's written there." Now she looked at me, and her face was grim as a headstone. "I'll come with you, girl. Not for prophecy. For penance. I stood in a temple a hundred years too late and did nothing, and I have been doing nothing ever since. Consider this the end of it."

I didn't yet understand what she meant by *a hundred years too late*. I wouldn't, for a long time. But I wrote down the name *Maren Voss* in the back of my mind, next to the names of everyone else hunting me, and I felt the shape of my life narrowing toward it like a road toward a gate.

* * *

Wick I found stealing my purse.

I felt the lift happen—light fingers, professional, at the crowded mouth of Fishmonger's Stair—and instead of grabbing the wrist I let it happen, then followed the small figure through the market at an unhurried stroll, curious. The thief ducked under a fish stall, cut through a tannery yard, scaled a wall with handholds I hadn't known existed, and finally dropped into an alley off Rope Street, breathing hard, counting coins that weren't there.

"It's empty," I said from the alley mouth. "Has been since the bridge. I keep pebbles in it for exactly this."

The thief spun. Young—young enough that I guessed sixteen and worried I was flattering them. Sharp chin, cropped dark hair going grey at the temples far too early, and eyes that were the problem. Eyes that were *empty* was the cruel way to put it, and cruelty was the wrong tool. They were clear, rather. Clear as water with nothing living in it. I knew that look. I had seen it in mirrors in my worst years, and once, terribly, in the faces of people I'd stolen from too deeply.

"You've been thieved," I said. "Your memories."

Wick's jaw tightened. "Since I was six. Wake up in a gutter in Verance, know my own name and how to pick locks and how to make fire and not one thing else. No mother's face. No birthday. Nothing." They tossed my pebble-purse back; it landed at my feet with a sad little clatter. "Inquisitors did it, probably. Or the war. Doesn't matter. I built new ones. I'm building them still. Every day I write down what happened the day before, in a book, so the book remembers even if I can't."

"That's the bravest thing I've heard all week."

"Don't patronize me. I'm a professional."

"I'm serious." I crouched, slowly, the way you approach a cat that has been kicked. "My name is Odile. I take memories too—but I give them back, mostly, and I never take them from anyone who hasn't offered. I'm going to the Ninefold Court to reforge the covenant, and when it reforges, maybe—" I heard the hope leave my own mouth before I could catch it, and hated myself for letting it loose in front of a child, "—maybe everything magic stole starts coming home. Maybe yours do."

Wick was silent a long moment. Rain dripped off the eaves between us, counting seconds.

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then you'll still have the book. And you'll still have us. Me, and a man who talks to doors, and a priestess who makes soup like a weapon." I held out my hand. "Family's not what you remember, Wick. It's who shows up. I'm showing up."

They looked at my hand like it might be a trap, which was fair, because I had thought the same of every offered hand for twenty years. Then they took it, brief and hard as a lock clicking shut.

"One rule," Wick said. "You never take mine. Not to save my life, not to save yours, not for the covenant, not for anything. Promise."

"I promise," I said—and some old instinct, some thief's ear for the weight of words, whispered to me that this was a vow I might someday be forced to break, and that breaking it would cost more than any theft I'd ever committed. I promised anyway. What else does a found family run on, if not promises made slightly ahead of the strength to keep them?

* * *

We met that evening in the back room of the Drowned Lantern, a canal-side tavern where the owner asked no questions and watered the wine so thoroughly it was practically a civic service. Five of us around a scarred table: me, Caspian with his infuriating half-smile, Brannic listening to the tavern's doors murmur, Mother Sable sharpening a paring knife with liturgical precision, and Wick teaching themselves the room's exits by heart.

It was, I realized somewhere between the bad wine and Brannic's dry report that the front door *liked us* but the cellar hatch *had doubts*, the first table I had ever sat at that felt like mine. Not rented. Not stolen. Mine.

"To the company," Caspian said, raising his cup, "of the Covenant-Bearer."

"To the company," I echoed, and meant it, and beneath the meaning felt the prophecy turning its pages somewhere ahead of us, patient as winter.

It was Mother Sable who brought the evening down to earth, as she would bring down so many evenings after. She laid her paring knife flat on the table, point toward the door, and said:

"Enjoy it tonight. Tomorrow we move for Verance, and tomorrow, girl, you should know—inquisitor ledgers list their quarries by priority. And a fishwife I trust tells me Voss's ledger was copied and circulated among the free cities' spies last month." Her old eyes met mine, steady and sorry. "Yours isn't just in it. Yours is the first name written down."

Outside, the nine bells of Bellharbor rang the midnight hour—five real, three remembered—and the rain came down soft as pages turning.
