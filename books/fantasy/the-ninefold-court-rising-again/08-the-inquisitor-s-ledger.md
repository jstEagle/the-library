## 8. The Inquisitor's Ledger

Mother Sable had buried three names in her life, and she remembered every one of them the way a tongue remembers a cracked tooth. She had thought, when she burned her robes and fled the cloister at Vespergate, that she was done keeping accounts. But accounts kept themselves. They waited in ledgers. They waited in people.

She was thinking of this on the third night in Bellharbor, in the rented room above the Saltcellar tavern, while rain needled the shutters and the canal below exhaled its smell of brine and fish-offal and tar. The room was small enough that four people made a crowd: Odile sitting cross-legged on the one intact chair like a cat pretending it hadn't chosen to stay; Wick curled in the window seat with a stolen apple core going brown beside them; Brannic half-asleep against the doorframe because, he'd said, doors talked less when you leaned on them. Caspian was downstairs buying wine he would not drink and listening to things he would not repeat until morning.

And between them all, on the table, lay the ledger.

Sable had paid a great deal for it. Not coin—coin was the cheapest of prices—but a favor owed to a woman named Ivetta who ran the reliquary-black-market behind the tannery, and a truth told aloud that Sable had kept behind her teeth for sixty years. The ledger had cost her more than the robes had. That was how she knew it mattered.

It was an inquisitor's ledger. Bound in gray hide—she did not let herself wonder whose—with brass corners gone green, and stamped on the cover with the nine-pointed star of Veyran's new order, each point tipped with a tiny engraved eye. *The Registry of Recovered Memories, Northern District.* Recovered. As if memories were runaway horses. As if they could be brought home to their proper stable, which was, of course, the usurper's crown.

"You're certain this is real," Odile said. It wasn't quite a question. Sable had learned, these few days on the road, that Odile Eastwick phrased her fears as questions so she could deny having asked them.

"I copied entries from one just like it, forty years ago, when I still wore gray wool and believed the Church of Nine Oaths served anyone but itself." Sable drew the lamp closer. The light made the ledger's pages look like old skin. "The inquisitors keep two books. One is public—the edicts, the burnings, the lists of the condemned. This is the other one. Every memory seized by the Order, catalogued. Donor. Content. Condition. Disposition."

"Disposition," Wick repeated, tasting the word. "Like cargo."

"Exactly like cargo." Sable opened the cover.

* * *

She had meant to be clinical about it. She had been a priestess once; she knew how to read horror with a steady voice, how to stand in a charnel house and recite the rites without letting her hands shake. But the ledger did not read like a list. It read like a graveyard where every stone had been polished and alphabetized.

*Item 411. Donor: male, approx. 40 yrs., ferryman, Hollow Valley approach. Contents: first snowfall, childhood, w/ attendant emotion (joy). Condition: excellent. Disposition: Crown Reserve.*

Sable heard Odile stop breathing.

She turned the page slowly, giving the thief time to school her face, though nothing could school what moved behind it. *Item 412. Donor: female, 17 yrs., weaver's apprentice, Millbrook. Contents: wedding day, elder sister. Condition: good, some grief-bleed at edges.* *Item 415. Donor: unknown vagrant. Contents: lullaby (fragmentary), mother's voice. Condition: poor. Marked for destruction.*

"Marked for destruction," Brannic murmured, without opening his eyes. His door-sense worked on pages too, Sable suspected; he heard the hinges in everything. "They throw away the broken ones."

"They throw away what has no value to the Crown." Sable kept her voice level, the way she had once kept it over funeral biers. "A lullaby feeds no one's power. A wedding day, a first snow—they carry joy, and joy is what burns brightest when Veyran wears it. You understand what he's built, yes? A crown of other people's happiness. He cannot feel it himself—he feels only the taking—but worn together, a thousand stolen joys make him seem radiant. Beloved. The crowds in Corvane weep when he passes. They think it's love."

Wick had gone very still in the window seat. Sable saw the girl—no, Sable corrected herself, the child, whatever ageless face the memory-loss had given them—press their thumb into the soft part of their wrist, counting their own pulse, proving they still had something inside them no one had catalogued.

"How many?" Odile asked quietly.

"In the northern district alone? Four hundred entries a year since the Order was founded."

"And in all districts?"

Sable turned to the back of the book, where a summary page tallied the whole grim arithmetic in a clerk's beautiful hand. She let Odile look. She did not say the number aloud. Some numbers should only ever be seen, never spoken; spoken, they became furniture, and this must never become furniture.

Odile's finger came down on the page—not on the total, but lower, tracing backward through the years. Sable watched her go still in a new way. Not the stillness of horror. The stillness of recognition.

"These aren't all inquisitor takings." Her voice had changed. It had gone thin and careful, the voice of someone walking out onto ice they already know will crack. "Look at the acquisition marks. There are two kinds. The Order's seal—and this other one. A circle with a notch cut in it."

"A trader's mark," Sable said, though she pretended to check. "Yes."

"Memories get sold. To the inquisitors." Odile laughed, one short ugly note with no floor under it. "There's a market. Of course there's a market. There's always been a market—I've sold to collectors myself, little vials, discreet buyers—" She stopped. Sable watched her arrive somewhere she did not want to be. "How long?"

"The trade predates the Order. When Veyran rose, he didn't invent the traffic. He nationalized it." Sable closed her eyes briefly. Sixty years ago she had been a novice named Sabine, kneeling in the archive at Vespergate, copying registry pages by candlelight, telling herself it was holy work, preservation, stewardship of the mortal soul against fae predation. She had been very young, and very good at not asking what happened to the donors. "Every free city has its brokers. Bellharbor has at least three. And every broker sells to the Crown eventually, because the Crown pays in safety. Or in silence."

* * *

The rain thickened. Downstairs someone laughed too loudly, and a chair scraped, and the ordinary world went on being ordinary, which Sable had always found to be the most obscene thing about catastrophes.

Odile had taken the ledger from the table. She held it now the way she held everything—a pickpocket's hold, casual on the surface, knuckles ready beneath. She was turning pages faster now, hunting. Sable recognized the hunt. She had spent thirty years watching penitents search their own confessions for the sentence that would make the sin survivable.

"Names," Odile said. "Do they record names?"

"Donors, often. Sellers, sometimes. Buyers—" Sable hesitated. "Buyers are protected. Anonymity is part of the price."

"But sellers sometimes." Odile's finger stopped. Sable saw the color leave her face in stages, like a tide going out. "There's a mark here. A circle with a notch. It appears—Sable, it appears dozens of times. Over decades. Same hand, same mark, always selling north-country memories. Ferry-men. Farmers. Valley folk."

Ah, thought Sable. There it is.

She had known this moment would come. She had bought the ledger partly for this reason and had told herself it was for strategy—for understanding the enemy—and had known even as she told herself that she was also, in the oldest habit of priests, arranging for someone else's reckoning. It was cowardice dressed as pedagogy. She did it anyway. She had learned long ago that she was not brave, only stubborn, and that the difference showed mostly in what you made other people look at.

"Odile," she said gently. "Whose mark is that?"

"You know whose mark that is." Odile did not look up. Her thumb rested on the page like a bird on a wire. "You've seen my grandmother's seal on the trader's kit I carry. The wax stamp in the bottom of my satchel. I've used it myself, a dozen times—it opens doors, brokers trust it. It's a circle with a notch cut in it. It was hers."

The room held its breath. Even Brannic opened his eyes.

"Then either your grandmother sold to the Order," Sable said, "or the Order's founders learned the trade from her."

"That's worse."

"Yes. It usually is."

Odile stood up so fast the chair went over. Wick flinched; Brannic caught the chair without appearing to move, the way he caught everything, doors and chairs and falling people, as if the world were a house he was quietly holding together. Odile paced the length of the room—four steps, turn, four steps—clutching the ledger against her ribs like a shield or like contraband.

"I never—" She stopped. Started again. "I stole to eat. To live. I took pretty things, harmless things, first snows and festival days, and I told myself—I told myself there was no body count. Pain stays with its owner. That was the rule. I left everyone their grief, their shame, their worst nights. What's missing, a snowfall? A dance? People lose whole years to fever. They lose them to drink. Nobody dies because someone took their memory of a fair."

"No," Sable agreed. "Nobody dies of it."

"Then why does it feel like—"

"Because it isn't death. It's something the Order understood better than we did, God forgive us all." Sable rose, joints cracking, and crossed to the window. Below, the canal slid past black and patient, carrying its cargo of reflections—lamplight, gulls, a woman's face looking down from a window, which was her own. "A man can lose his leg and remain a man. Take his name, and what walks around afterward wearing his face? Memory isn't one of the things a person has, Odile. It's the thing that has the person. The Order knows this. It's why they catalogue. It's why they pay. Because a stolen joy is worth more than gold, and the person it was taken from becomes—" she searched for the word and hated each candidate— "*lighter*. Easier to rule. A town whose festival days have been skimmed doesn't riot. A family that's forgotten why it loved itself doesn't resist. Your thefts were small. But you were working in the same mine as the Empire, child, with the same tools, and the shaft goes all the way down."

"I'm not them."

"No," Sable said. "Not yet. But the ledger doesn't care about yet."

* * *

Later—after Odile had gone silent, after Wick had wordlessly fetched water and no one drank it, after Brannic had finally slept with his ear against the door—Caspian came back up with the wine and the news, and Sable watched him take in the overturned chair and the open ledger and Odile sitting motionless in the dark by the window, and she watched him understand instantly, the way clever men do, exactly what had happened and exactly what it would cost him.

"There's a notice board outside the Harbor Master's office," he said, too casually. "New postings. Inquisitor proclamations."

"Read them to us," Sable said, because she wanted to watch his face while he lied, and she was not disappointed. He was good. He was very good. Only the pause—one heartbeat too long before the second sentence—gave him away, and Sable doubted anyone born without her particular training would catch it.

"Standard edicts. Curfew after bells. Rewards for reporting unlicensed magic." He folded the paper away. "Nothing about us specifically."

He was lying. Sable had spent forty years reading confession-faces, and Caspian's was a psalter written in a language she almost spoke. Something was posted about them—about one of them—and he had decided the others shouldn't see it. She filed this away next to the other small wrongnesses: the way he watched Odile measure rooms for exits; the way he never let the prophecy book out of arm's reach; the way, when he thought himself unobserved, he looked at Odile with an expression that was equal parts tenderness and guilt, the exact face Sable had once seen on her own reflection in the vestry mirror, the day she signed the order that sent a frightened girl away to keep a secret safe.

We are all keeping something, she thought. The question is which of us will run out of room to keep it.

But that was tomorrow's work. Tonight there was the ledger, and the girl in the window, and the thing that had to be said before sleep, because shame untreated festers into either cruelty or despair, and Sable had administered last rites to enough of both.

She sat down across from Odile. For a long moment neither spoke. The rain filled the silence with static.

"When I was nineteen," Sable said, "I helped the archive masters inventory a seizure from a village called Aldermoor. Two hundred entries. Harvest songs, christenings, a man's memory of his father's hands teaching him whittling. Beautiful things. I catalogued them beautifully. I gave each one a number." She looked at her own hands, liver-spotted, steady. "Forty years later I can still recite some of those numbers. Item 88, the whittling. I don't know the man's name. I never asked. Asking would have meant admitting he was a man."

Odile looked up. Her eyes were dry, which Sable knew was worse than tears.

"What I did was legal, sanctified, and praised," Sable went on. "What you did was desperate, hungry, and small. And we both fed the same beast, and neither of us counted the bodies, because the bodies don't fall down. They walk away lighter and don't know to grieve. That's the trick of it. That's the horror of it. So no—you don't get to decide tonight whether you're guilty. Guilt is a luxury for people who are finished. You're not finished. You're armed, Odile Eastwick. You know now what your gift does at scale, and you know the enemy keeps books. Which means you can do the one thing neither your grandmother nor my Church nor Veyran's inquisitors ever bothered to do."

"Which is?"

"Count." Sable tapped the ledger. "Starting with yourself. And then, when you've finished hating what you find—we have a war to win, and I am too old, and Wick is too young, and Brannic hears too much, and Caspian—" she paused, choosing, "—Caspian knows too little that he's admitted. We need you clear-eyed, not clean. Clean can come later, if there's a later."

Odile was quiet a long time. Then she pulled the ledger toward herself again and, in the lamplight, with a stolen pencil stub, began—at the top of a fresh page, in a thief's quick slanted hand—to write.

Sable watched the first entry appear and felt something loosen in her chest that she had not realized was clenched: not absolution, nothing so kind, but perhaps its raw material.

*Item 1,* Odile wrote. *Donor: ferryman, male, approx. 40. Contents: first snow.*

Below it, after a moment: *Return pending.*

She wrote through the rest of the night. And near dawn, pale as canal-water, she stopped on a page deep in the middle years of the book and made a sound Sable had heard only twice before in her life—from women standing at open graves.

"Sable." Odile's voice was ash. "The mark. My grandmother's mark. The last entry with her seal—it's dated twenty-two years ago. And the item description says—" She swallowed. Turned the book around with a hand that shook.

Sable read it. Read it again. Felt the old fear, the one she had carried out of Vespergate burning in her sleeves, rise up and put its cold mouth against her ear.

*Item 3,907. Seller: private party (seal: circled notch). Contents: identity, complete. Donor: female child, approx. 6 yrs., provenance unknown. Condition: pristine. Disposition:* and then a word Sable had never once seen in forty years of registries, a word that appeared nowhere else in the volume—

*Unrecoverable.*

In the window seat, Wick stirred in their sleep and murmured something that might have been a name, or might have been the shape a name leaves behind when someone takes it.

Sable met Odile's eyes across the table, and neither of them said it aloud, because saying it would make it real, and it was not yet dawn, and some doors—Brannic would have told them—are better heard through than opened.

But the ledger lay open between them, and the entry lay open on the page, and a six-year-old child with no memories and no history had been sleeping ten feet away for three nights, trusting them all.

Outside, the harbor bells rang the hour, and somewhere in the gray streets of Bellharbor, boots were already moving.
