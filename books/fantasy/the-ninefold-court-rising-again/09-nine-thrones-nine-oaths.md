## 9. Nine Thrones, Nine Oaths

Caspian had rehearsed this speech a hundred times on the road from the Court—on the salt flats, in the ferry-shelters of the borderlands, in the long grey silence of the Hollow Valley's edge where no magic lived to carry his words anywhere but into his own ears. He had rehearsed it for a room of skeptical mortal councilors, for frightened villagers, perhaps even for the Covenant-Bearer herself, whoever she turned out to be.

He had not rehearsed it for a kitchen.

The safehouse Mother Sable had chosen was a smuggler's cottage wedged into Bellharbor's lowest terrace, where the canal water slapped at the foundations all night like something impatient to be let in. Its single room smelled of tallow smoke, brine, and old rope. A kettle muttered over the fire. Brannic sat cross-legged on the floor with his back against the door—he always sat against doors; Caspian suspected it soothed him to hear them breathe—and Wick was picking the lock of a breadbox purely, they had announced, "for practice." Mother Sable stirred the fire with the iron patience of a woman who had watched a hundred years of explanations and outlasted most of them.

And Odile Eastwick sat at the table with her arms folded, watching him with the flat, patient attention of someone who had stolen better secrets than his and knew exactly how much a speech could be worth.

"So," she said. "Nine thrones."

"Nine thrones," Caspian agreed, and abandoned the speech entirely.

He pulled a chair around and sat astride it, facing her, because he wanted to see her face when it landed—all of it, the whole weight of what he'd carried across a continent in a book bound in moth-grey leather. The prophecy book lay between them on the table. He did not touch it. He had been careful, these last days, not to touch it too often.

"You grew up thinking magic was small," he began. "Candles that gutter when you're angry. Charms against warts. The little residue that leaks through even into the valley your family hid in. That's not what magic is. That's what magic is *starving*."

Wick looked up from the breadbox, pick still in the keyhole.

"A century ago," Caspian said, "there were nine great houses of the fae—the Ninefold Court. Not kings the way your mortal kings are kings, not men with crowns and tax ledgers. Think of them as... load-bearing pillars. Each house held up one part of the world's workings. House Vael kept the rivers running true and the rain remembering where to fall. House Ondremere kept the borders between things—the line between waking and dream, between the living and the dead, between *inside* and *outside*. House Thistledown—" he almost smiled— "kept luck. Actual luck. The reason one field flourishes and its neighbor fails, the reason some children are born on lucky days. Nine houses, nine functions, and every one of them bound by oath to a mortal city. Not ruled it. *Bound*. The fae gave the cities their protection and their craft; the cities gave the Court memory."

"Memory," Odile repeated. Her voice was carefully neutral. He had learned that about her: the flatter her voice, the more was moving underneath.

"The fae are brilliant and nearly immortal and they forget," Caspian said simply. "That's the truth no song tells you. A fae lord can hold a grudge for three hundred years but cannot tell you his mother's face. They live in an eternal present, gorgeous and weightless, and without mortals tethering them—without human memory poured into the covenant like ballast—they drift apart. Forget themselves. Forget each other. Forget the oaths. The covenant wasn't a treaty, Odile. It was a *keepsake*. Mortals remembered for the fae, and the fae kept the world stitched together for mortals. Neither side could survive alone. That was the whole design. That was the beauty of it."

"And then?" Sable asked quietly, though Caspian suspected she already knew. She had been there. Whatever she'd told them—that she was defrocked, disgraced, a footnote—she stirred the fire like a woman listening to her own confession secondhand.

"And then someone stole from the Court," Caspian said. "A theft so deep the Court never even felt the wound—only woke one morning, a hundred years ago, unable to remember why it mattered. The memory of the true heir was taken. Cut out. And when the heir was forgotten, the succession failed, and when the succession failed, the nine houses began to argue over who should hold the regency, and while they argued, the oths went unsworn. One year. Then five. Then twenty. The covenant rotted from the center outward, and the world has been rotting with it ever since."

He stood, because sitting made it feel small, and walked to the window where the canal glimmered below, black water stitched with lamplight.

"You've seen the edges of it already, if you know how to look. Magic doesn't die cleanly—it rots. The Rotwood east of here, where the trees grow knuckle-jointed and the beasts have too many joints and none of them the right kind. The way charms fail in the lowlands but misfire in the highlands. The way the dead linger wrong near old covenant-sites, confused, because House Ondremere isn't holding the door anymore and things leak both directions. Every strange thing you've ever heard a grandmother mutter about—the fair folk going quiet, the wells turning bitter, the winters arriving out of order—that's not folklore fading. That's the stitching coming undone."

Behind him he heard the soft click of the breadbox surrendering to Wick. Nobody ate anything. Good, some petty part of him noted. They were listening.

"Veyran understands this," he went on. "That's the worst part. He isn't a fool. Twenty years ago he was just a border-lord with a decent army and a grievance, and he looked at the rot and asked the right question: if the Court is empty, why should the throne stay empty? He marched into the Court's ruin, and he found nine hollow thrones and the husks of the fae who remained—memoryless, drifting, barely more than ghosts wearing beautiful bodies—and he did something no usurper had thought to do before."

"He took their memories," Odile said.

It wasn't a guess. Caspian turned. She was looking at her own hands on the table, and he understood suddenly that she had guessed because it was what *she* would do. What she had done, a thousand times in miniature, in a valley where nobody would ever know.

"He wears them," Caspian said. "Nine lords' worth of stolen memory, woven into a crown only he can see. It's how he holds power over the fae remnants—he owns what they've lost, and he returns a scrap here, a scrap there, in exchange for obedience. It's how his inquisitors hunt so well. And it's why he fears you above everything else alive, Odile. Because a crown made of stolen memories can be *unmade* by the person with the right to take them back. And because the prophecy names a Covenant-Bearer who will reforge what he has spent twenty years making sure stays broken."

The kettle shrieked. Sable lifted it off the flame without hurrying. Steam curled up through the lamplight like something leaving.

Odile hadn't moved. "You keep saying *prophecy*," she said. "Like it's weather. Like it's just a thing that happens to people. But prophecies don't march armies. Prophecies don't burn memory-keepers in market squares. Men do. So spell it out for me, Caspian. What does the scale look like? Not in poetry. In bodies."

He respected that. He hated it, and he respected it. She had seen an inquisitor burn a man alive in a fish-market six days ago and she had not once mentioned it since, which meant she was carrying it somewhere heavy, and now she was asking him to put numbers on the weight.

"Fine," he said. "Bodies."

He counted them off, and made himself watch her face as he did.

"When the covenant broke, the first thing to fail was the wards on the mortal cities—the old pacts that kept open war between realms impossible. Within a generation, the free cities started fortifying against each other, because trust is also a thing the Court used to hold up. Veyran's rise gave the fear a shape and a flag. He holds four cities outright: Duskhaven, Greywater, Karth, and the capital at Veyran's Reach. Three more pay him tribute and hate him. Only Bellharbor and Verance are still truly free, and Verance is a council of merchants who think the war is a negotiation waiting for the right price. His army is forty thousand on paper, and paper flatters him—but even half that, fed on conscription and terror and the inquisitors' memory-tithe, is enough to swallow the free cities inside two years. Maybe less."

"Bodies," Odile said again, unmoved.

"Every city that falls gets its memory-keepers burned or bent," Caspian said. "Priests, archivists, hedge-witches, people like you. Anyone who can hold or move memory is either conscripted into his ledger-houses or killed. The ones who bend become his instruments. The ones who refuse—" He stopped. Started again, flatter. "In Duskhaven alone, after the fall, they burned two hundred and eleven. We have the lists. I have read the lists. I have some of their names memorized, because someone should."

Silence. The canal slapped at the foundations. Brannic had gone very still against the door, and Caspian realized the boy was listening to more than wood and hinges—listening to the whole city's sleep, maybe, the vast murmur of Bellharbor's dreams pressing in through the walls, and whatever he heard there made his eyes wide and wet.

"That's the scale," Caspian finished. "Not a quest. Not an adventure. A war that's already lost, being fought over the corpse of a promise, and one line in a hundred-year-old book saying it doesn't have to end that way. Nine thrones, nine oaths, and a thief from a hole in the world holding the only needle that can stitch it back."

Odile looked at him for a long moment. Then she laughed—not bitterly, which would have been easier to bear, but with a kind of stunned, exhausted disbelief, one hand pressed over her mouth.

"You're serious," she said. "You crossed the world, you found me in the one place nothing can find anyone, you dragged me past the border of my own life—you want me to walk into a war and *reforge reality*, and you're telling me this in a smuggler's kitchen while a child picks locks for practice."

"Wick is seventeen," Wick said, offended, around a mouthful of stolen bread.

"You want the alternative?" Caspian asked. "The alternative is that in two years, Veyran's inquisitors come to the Hollow Valley. They'll find it eventually. Everything gets found eventually. And they'll find a valley full of people hiding from magic, and they'll strip every memory worth having, and they won't kill you, Odile—they'll put you in a ledger-house and use you until there's nothing left to use. You've seen what they do. You saw the keeper burn."

Her hand had gone, without her seeming to notice, to her own temple—the gesture he'd begun to recognize, the unconscious touch of a woman checking that what she carried was still hers.

"I didn't ask for any of this," she said quietly.

"No," Caspian agreed. "You didn't. Nobody asks. That's rather the point of covenants. They're not wishes. They're promises, and promises are always made by people who would rather be doing something else." He hesitated, and the next words came harder than the whole speech had. "I know what it is to be drafted by a story bigger than yourself. I've carried this book since I was younger than Wick. The Court chose me as its herald when I was fourteen—a foundling with good lungs and no family to miss me. I didn't ask either. But I'll tell you what I learned, carrying it: the prophecy doesn't say *who you must be*. It says what must be done. Who does it—that's still a choice. That part's yours. It's the only part that's yours."

Something shifted behind her eyes—something he couldn't name and, he reminded himself sternly, shouldn't try to. He was her herald, not her confessor. There were things in the book he hadn't shown her. There were things in the book he had burned. He set that thought down carefully, the way you set down something hot, and turned to the practical matter before his face could betray him.

"Which brings us to roads," he said. "Bellharbor can't hold. The harbor council will negotiate within the season—I've read the letters. If the covenant is to be reforged, it happens at the Court itself, in the covenant chamber under the nine thrones, and we are four hundred leagues from it, with Veyran's army moving east along the Salt Road and his inquisitors fanned across every crossing between here and the mountains."

"There's no road through the mountains," Sable said. "Not anymore. Not since the Rotwood spread."

"Then we go through the Rotwood," Caspian said, and had the rare pleasure of seeing three faces go pale at once.

"The Rotwood's not a forest, it's a *wound*," Sable said sharply. "Broken covenant-magic, a hundred years festering. The trees remember being something else and hate what they became. Things live in there that were never born—they congealed out of all the memories the land lost when the oaths failed. Memory-wraiths. Grief given teeth."

"Good," said Wick, with the bright terrible enthusiasm of youth.

"It's also the only route the inquisitors can't easily follow," Caspian said. "Their ledger-craft needs clean lines, roads, records, tolls. The Rotwood eats records." He looked at Brannic, still silent against the door. "And we may have an advantage no company has ever carried into that place. Brannic. You hear doors?"

The boy shrugged, embarrassed. "Doors. Gates. Ways through. Everything that opens is... loud, if you listen. Even doors that aren't built yet." He frowned at the floorboards. "Even doors that got shut a long time ago and forgot how. Sometimes I can hear those. Under the other sounds."

"In a forest grown over a broken covenant," Caspian said, "where every path the old world took has been sealed and swallowed? A boy who can hear the doors the world shut a hundred years ago might be the most valuable person in this room. Present company included."

Brannic went pink to his ears and studied the doorframe as if it had said something kind about him.

Odile was quiet a long moment. When Caspian glanced over, she was looking at the map he'd unrolled earlier and abandoned—his own fault, maps never persuaded anyone—and her finger had come to rest not on the Rotwood but on the space beyond it, where his cartographer's ink ran out into blank vellum and a single handwritten word: *the Court.*

"Nine thrones," she murmured. "Nine oaths. And a thief." Her finger tapped the blank space once, twice, deciding. "All right, herald. You've made your pitch. Here's mine: we leave tomorrow, before dawn, before the harbor council sells us all a nice polite betrayal. We go through your haunted forest, and on the way, you're going to teach me everything—every name, every house, every oath, every lie this covenant was built on. Because if I'm going to be the person your book says I am, I'm not doing it blind. And Caspian?"

She looked up at him, and her eyes in the lamplight were the exact grey-green of canal water over old stone.

"If there's anything in that book about me that you haven't told me—I'll know. Stealing secrets is the one trade I'm a master of."

Caspian held her gaze, and smiled, and lied with his whole face, because it was the only mercy left to offer her:

"Then you'd have found it already."

Outside, far off toward the harbor mouth, a bell began to toll—once, twice, a third time—and Mother Sable set down her cup and said, very quietly, the words every refugee in Bellharbor knew the meaning of:

"They're burning another one."

* * *

They left before dawn, as promised, slipping down the water-stairs while the gulls were still arguing with the dark. The Rotwood waited eastward, a black seam on the horizon beneath the last stars, and Caspian walked at the front of the little company with the book against his ribs and Brannic's hand resting lightly on his shoulder, following the sound of a door that had been shut for a hundred years.

Behind them, faint and thin across the water, the bell was still ringing.
