## 10. The Road Through Rotwood

Rotwood announced itself long before we reached it.

We smelled it first—a sweetness like overripe plums and something underneath that was not fruit at all but rot dressed in perfume, the smell of a thing trying to remember what it had been before it died. Then we heard it: a low, arrhythmic groaning from the treeline, as if the forest were dreaming aloud in a language it had half forgotten.

"Charming," Wick said, hopping down from the cart's tailboard to squint at the horizon. "I've slept in worse places."

"You have not," Mother Sable said without looking up from her ledgers. She rode the cart now, her defrocked robes traded for a pilgrim's grey cloak, though she still sat like someone accustomed to a throne-shaped chair. "You told me yourself the worst place you ever slept was a grain silo in Verance, and that only because of the rats."

"The rats were polite company compared to this."

Brannic said nothing. He rarely did when we approached anything with edges. He stood at the cart's front with his head tilted at an angle I'd come to think of as *listening*—though there was nothing yet to hear, or so I assumed, until I remembered what Brannic heard was not sound. Doors, he called them. Every threshold, every boundary between one state of being and another, sang to him if he pressed his ear against the world's skin. It was fae blood that did it, thin and diluted through six generations of foundlings, but enough. Enough that he'd heard our approach to Bellharbor three streets early. Enough that he'd heard the inquisitor coming up the stairwell while the rest of us were still laughing.

He was listening now, and whatever he heard made his jaw tighten.

"It's bad in there," he said. "The doors are... broken. Not locked. Broken. Like someone kicked them all off their hinges a hundred years ago and nobody's fixed them since."

The covenant, then. The pact between the Ninefold Court and the mortal cities, shattered a century past—the wound Caspian had spent two nights by the fire explaining to us, drawing nine circles and nine lines in the ash until even Wick understood. Where the covenant held, magic flowed clean, bounded, answerable. Where it broke, magic pooled and curdled like milk left in the sun.

Rotwood had been a border forest once, planted—Sable claimed—as a living treaty-marker between the mortal city of Vell and the fae House of Thorns. Now the House of Thorns was silent and its trees had gone mad with grief, or hunger, or both.

"We could go around," Sable offered, which was generous of her, because going around meant four extra days on roads where Veyran's inquisitors were burning memory-keepers alive, and everyone knew it. She offered anyway. That was the kind of woman she'd decided to be, out here, away from her burned robes and her ruined priory. She offered things she didn't mean so that we could refuse them and feel brave about it.

"No," I said, and was surprised at how easily the word came. "Around is longer. Longer means more chances for Voss to catch our scent." Maren Voss. Even thinking the name made my palms itch. The inquisitor who had nearly taken Wick in Bellharbor, who had looked at me across a burning market square with eyes like a ledger's columns—everything recorded, nothing forgiven. "Through. Quickly, quietly, and nobody wanders."

"I never wander," Wick said.

"You wandered into the harbor master's strongroom in Bellharbor."

"That wasn't wandering. That was *research*."

Caspian laughed from the back of the cart, and I did not look at him. I had gotten very good, in the days since Salt Bridge, at not looking at him. At not looking too long, anyway. He had lied to me—I still turned the shape of that lie over in the dark, a stone worn smooth by handling—and yet here he rode among us, feeding the mule apple cores and telling Wick stories about the Ninefold Court as if he were a tour guide and not a recruiter who had known, perhaps from the first moment, exactly what I was.

Solo, I would have gone around Rotwood alone, in the dark, and been through it in a night. Solo, I wouldn't have had to explain myself to anyone.

I was beginning to suspect that solo had been another kind of cage.

* * *

The trees closed over us within the hour, and the light went strange.

It came down green and thick, filtered through leaves that were too large and hung at angles leaves shouldn't hang, as if each one had grown toward some sun that no longer existed and simply refused to accept the change. The trunks were worse. They spiraled. Every tree in Rotwood grew in a slow corkscrew, winding upward as though trying to follow the ghost of a path, and the deeper we went the tighter the spirals wound, until some of the younger trees had twisted themselves into knots no woodcutter could name.

And they moved. Not much. Not fast. But when I looked away from a trunk and back again, it had leaned a fraction closer to the road.

"Don't touch the bark," Sable said quietly. "Old treaty-trees hold oaths in their grain. These ones hold broken ones. Touch them and you'll feel it—all of it. A century of promises snapping one after another, like a neck."

"How do you know?" Wick asked.

"Because I touched one, sixty years ago, and I screamed for an hour, and they defrocked me partly for that too." She said it lightly, the way she said everything that mattered most.

I kept my hands inside my sleeves and walked close to the cart.

The beasts came at dusk. Or rather—we heard them, and heard them wrong. A sound like a fox screaming came from above us; we looked up and saw nothing. A birdcall sounded from knee height in the ferns, but the cadence was wrong, stretched, the same three notes repeated until the repetition itself became menacing. Something heavy moved through the underbrush parallel to our line of march, keeping pace, never showing itself.

"They're twisted, not turned," Caspian murmured, falling into step beside me. I let him, because there was safety in numbers and he was the best armed of us. "The covenant-break didn't make monsters out of them. It just... unmade the rules they lived by. A fox doesn't know it should be afraid of fire anymore. An owl hunts at noon. Everything's still here, but nothing's in its right place."

"Like a stolen memory put back in the wrong head," I said.

He glanced at me. "Yes," he said softly. "Exactly like that."

I hated how often we arrived at the same thought. It felt like evidence of something I hadn't agreed to.

We made camp that night in a ring of stones—an old waymarker, Sable said, one of the covenant-road's resting stations, back when mortals and fae traveled between realms as casually as neighbors borrowing salt. The stones were carved with interlaced patterns, human hands woven with too-long fingers, and half the carvings had been deliberately chiseled away. Someone had tried, a hundred years ago, to erase the friendship from the rock.

Wick took first watch. Brannic second. I lay with my back against the cart wheel and did not sleep, because I never truly slept in strange country, and because my hoard was restless.

That is the closest way I can say it. The memories I carry are not inert. They stir. In ordinary country they lie quiet, silt at the bottom of me. But Rotwood's broken air seemed to agitate them, the way a storm churns a pond, and all night I felt them turning over: a fisherman's dawn, a bride's terror, a child's first taste of honey, a dying man's last sight of snow. They pressed against the inside of my skull wanting *out*, wanting to be felt, and I gripped them the way you grip a net full of eels and stared at the spiral-trunked trees and waited for morning.

Near midnight, Wick screamed.

Not loudly—that was the terrible part. A small, bitten-off sound, quickly smothered, the scream of someone who has learned that screaming brings worse things. I was up before I'd finished hearing it, knife already in my hand, and found Wick sitting bolt upright by the dead fire with their hands clamped over their mouth and their eyes enormous.

"Wick." I knelt. Kept my voice low. "Hey. Look at me. You're here. We're here."

They shook their head. Their fingers peeled slowly from their lips. "I dreamed," they whispered. "I dreamed about—" Their face worked. "I don't know. That's the whole point, isn't it? I dreamed about something and I can't keep hold of it. There was water. And someone singing? And then it was just *gone*, like a—" Their voice cracked. "Like someone reached into me and took it out while I was sleeping."

Behind me, I heard Sable sit up. Heard Caspian go very still.

"Nothing took it," I said, and I was surprised by the ferocity in my own voice. "Nothing touches you. Not here, not anywhere. Not while I'm breathing." I realized, saying it, that I meant it as more than comfort. That somewhere between Bellharbor and Salt Bridge, Wick's empty six-year-old childhood had become mine to guard, and I had signed no contract and made no vow and done it anyway. "Dreams fall apart. Everyone's do. Especially here, where the air's rotten. It doesn't mean anyone stole anything."

Wick looked at me for a long moment. Then, slowly, they lay back down, and after a while their breathing evened, and I stayed kneeling beside them until Sable's hand settled briefly on my shoulder and moved on.

Across the embers, Brannic was staring into the dark beyond the camp with his head cocked, listening.

Later—much later, in the smallest hour—he touched my arm, and I nearly put the knife through him on reflex.

"Something's coming," he breathed. "A door just opened. Out there." He pointed north, into the black lattice of the trees. "It opened a long time ago and it's been walking ever since."

"What kind of door?"

"The bad kind." His young-old face was grey in the starlight. "Odile—it doesn't have any walls."

* * *

We broke camp in the dark, cold rations, no fire, no talk. Brannic directed us by a language of taps and hand-signs he invented on the spot, pointing left along a deer trail when the main road bent toward whatever was walking, and we followed him because none of us could hear what he heard and all of us had decided, without discussion, that his ears were the map now.

That, I thought, moving soft through the ferns with five other people's breathing around me, was new.

For eleven years my survival plan had been a single word: *away*. Away from danger, away from questions, away from anyone who might ask what I carried. I had never once in my life trusted another person's senses more than my own. And here I was placing my life on a half-fae foundling's impossible hearing, following him blind through a cursed wood because he could feel thresholds and I couldn't, and the remarkable thing—the thing I turned over as I walked—was that it didn't feel like weakness. It felt like having more self than one body could hold.

The wraith found us anyway, an hour before dawn.

It came through the trees without disturbing them—or rather, the trees disturbed themselves around it, leaning away, spirals tightening, leaves dropping out of season. It was tall and roughly person-shaped, but it moved the way smoke moves when it remembers being a curtain. And where it passed, the world went *blank*. Ferns it drifted over stood bare afterward, not eaten but emptied, green drained to a colorless ghost-green. A nightbird sat frozen on a branch as the thing passed beneath and did not fly away, and I understood with a lurch that it couldn't remember how.

"A memory-wraith," Sable whispered. Her face had gone the color of old tallow. "Gods preserve us. I thought they were stories."

"What does it want?" Wick whispered back.

"To remember. It can't, so it takes. It'll drink your past out through your eyes if it touches you, and leave you like—" Sable stopped. Like the husks, she didn't say. Like the fae lords in their empty court, though none of us knew that yet. "Like the trees. Standing, and nothing in the standing."

"It's between us and the road," Caspian murmured. He had his sword drawn, and I watched his knuckles whiten around the grip and understood that he knew, as I did, that steel meant nothing here. "And it's drifting toward the camp. Toward the cart. If it reaches the ledgers—"

Sable's inquisitor-ledgers. Pages of names and thefts, including mine. Paper remembers; paper is memory; a wraith might feed on ink the way a moth feeds on wool.

"It won't take the books," I heard myself say. "It'll take whoever's holding them."

Everyone looked at Sable. Sable looked at the wraith, and squared her shoulders with the particular stubbornness of a woman deciding which sin to commit next.

"No," Brannic said.

We all turned. The boy had gone rigid, both hands pressed flat against the ground, against the roots of the nearest spiral-tree, and his eyes were wide and wet and shining with a terror that had nothing childish in it.

"It's not looking for food," he whispered. "I can hear it. It's a *door*, Odile, it's a door that walks, and it's not open because it wants in. It's open because—" His breath hitched. "Because everything in this wood is a broken door. And it's crying. It's been crying for a hundred years and it doesn't know why, because it can't remember what it lost either."

The forest groaned around us. The wraith turned—not toward Sable, not toward the cart—but toward Brannic, toward the boy pressed to the earth with his gift pouring out of him like blood in water, the loudest remembering thing for miles.

Toward the only one of us who could hear it.

"It knows he's listening," Sable breathed. "Oh, gods. It wants to be heard."

Every instinct I owned screamed the old word. *Away.* Run, scatter, save myself; I had outrun better monsters than grief given legs. But Wick was grabbing Brannic's arm and refusing to let go, and Sable was planting herself in front of them both like a wall of grey wool and fury, and Caspian was moving to flank the wraith with a blade that couldn't help, and there was no *away* anymore. There was only *together*, or nothing.

So I did the bravest cowardly thing I have ever done. I stopped hiding what I was.

"Everyone hold still," I said, and stepped out of the ferns into the open, into the wraith's blank attention, and opened my hoard.

I have never described what that feels like and I hope never to need to again: peeling myself open along a seam no one else has, letting a thousand borrowed lives press upward toward the light. The wraith *turned*. All of its smoke-attention swung to me, to the feast standing upright in the path, a thief offering the whole purse. It drifted toward me, and the air went cold and colorless, and the nearest trees' spirals began, very slowly, to unwind—

—and Brannic shouted, "*No!*"—and flung himself between us, hands raised, and did the thing none of us expected and I will wonder about for the rest of my life.

He listened *at* it.

He pressed his gift against the wraith the way he pressed his ear to a locked door, and he *listened*—not to steal, not to fight, but the way you listen to a stranger weeping in the night. And the wraith stopped. Hung there, smoke trembling, a hand's breadth from my opened hoard, from the boy's open hands, from everything it could have taken.

"There you are," Brannic whispered, tears running freely down his face. "There you are. You're the treaty-road, aren't you? You're the crossing-point. You were a *gate*, and when the covenant broke, the gate broke, and nobody ever shut you properly or opened you properly, and you've been stuck halfway for a hundred years. Half-open. Half-empty." His voice broke. "You're not hungry. You're *homesick*."

The wraith made a sound. I will hear it until I die—a sound like wind through an empty house, like a hinge that has rusted in one position, like the word *yes* spoken by something that had forgotten words existed.

And then Brannic, still weeping, still listening, began to hum. Low, tuneless, a foundling's made-up lullaby, and he reached out and laid both palms against the wraith's smoking edge as if laying hands on a doorframe, and said, in the voice of a boy who hears doors:

"You can rest. I'll stand in the doorway. Go on. I've got the threshold."

The wraith poured itself into his hands like water finding a crack, and was gone.

The forest exhaled. I swear it—an actual sigh ran through the whole wood, a million spiraled trees relaxing a fraction toward straightness, and in the sudden stillness the birds remembered themselves and began, raggedly, to sing.

Brannic swayed. Wick caught him. I stood there with my hoard still gaping open and my heart slamming and realized my cheeks were wet, and had no memory of when that had started.

* * *

We walked out of Rotwood at midday, into honest sunlight, and none of us spoke for a long time.

At the treeline Sable stopped, turned, and bowed—actually bowed—to the forest, deep and formal, the bow of a priestess to a congregation. Whatever she said was too quiet to carry. Then she came on, and we went down together toward the river road and the free cities and the war.

Wick walked beside Brannic the whole afternoon, talking enough for both of them, describing everything they saw in case his ears needed help with his eyes. Caspian dropped back to walk beside me, and for once I didn't angle away.

"You almost fed it your whole hoard," he said quietly. "To save him."

"To buy time," I corrected.

"Odile."

"Eleven years I survived because I only counted to one," I said, watching the road ahead instead of him. "One person. One exit. One life to lose. Out here—" I gestured vaguely at all of it: the boy, the priestess, the pickpocket, the liar. "Out here the arithmetic's broken and I can't fix it. So yes. I would have fed it everything. Don't make me say it twice."

He smiled, and I pretended not to see, and we walked on.

It was near dusk when Brannic suddenly stopped dead in the middle of the road, head cocked, listening, and went white as bone.

"Another door," he whispered. "Far off. Behind us." He swallowed. "This one isn't crying. This one's *hunting*."

And far to the south, faint as a struck match, I felt it too—the prickle of a trained mind combing the world for a thief's signature. Somewhere behind us, patient and precise, Maren Voss had picked up our trail.

The hunt was on, and this time, we would run it together.
