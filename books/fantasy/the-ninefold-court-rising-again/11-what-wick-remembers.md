## 11. What Wick Remembers

The fire had burned down to its red bones by the time the camp went quiet.

Wick counted the sounds the way other people counted coins: Brannic's slow, whistling breath from under the cart, where he always slept with his back against the wheel as if doors might open in his dreams and he wanted to be near one. Mother Sable turning over twice, muttering half a prayer to a god whose name she'd burned along with her robes. Caspian on watch, standing too still at the edge of the firelight, the way he did when he thought no one was watching him carry something heavy.

And Odile.

Odile slept last and woke first, curled small near the ashes like a cat that had learned not to trust warmth. Even asleep her hands were busy—fingers twitching against the ground, sorting through whatever she carried behind her eyes. A thousand stolen lives, Sable said. Wick watched them move under her skin sometimes, like minnows in shallow water.

Wick didn't sleep much. Sleeping meant dreaming, and dreaming meant the dark place before age six, the place with no floor and no walls and nothing to stand on. Better to count sounds. Better to stay awake inside the only self they had—the thin one, the six-year-and-some one, the one that began mid-story like a book with its first chapters torn out.

So Wick lay on their bedroll and watched the stars through the black lace of Rotwood's dying branches, three days past the wraith, two days from Salt Bridge, and decided tonight was the night.

They'd been deciding it for a week.

* * *

It happened after supper the next evening, or what passed for supper—barley porridge with pine needles in it because Wick had read somewhere, in some memory of someone else's childhood that Odile had once described to them like a bedtime story, that pine needles kept away scurvy. The company sat around the fire in the wet green gloom of Rotwood, where the trees grew in spirals and the moss glowed faintly wrong, and Brannic was telling a story about a door in Bellharbor that sang when you knocked it in thirds.

"And then," Brannic said, spreading his big hands, "the whole doorframe *harmonizes*, and the harbor master comes running because he thinks the building's drowning—"

"Because you knocked in thirds," Mother Sable said, "which is forbidden in every coastal city since the Tidewrack Accords."

"Which is *why* I knocked in thirds."

Caspian laughed—a real laugh, unguarded, gone as quick as it came—and Wick felt the familiar ache underneath their ribs. Not hunger. They knew hunger. This was the other thing, the thing they'd never had a word for until they'd watched this ragged company for long enough to learn it.

Belonging. Watching it happen to other people.

Odile sat apart, as always, sharpening her knives with a whetstone worn smooth as a river pebble. *Shk. Shk. Shk.* The sound was steady as a heartbeat. Wick had noticed she did it when she was thinking, when she was frightened, when she was happy—she did it the way Wick counted sounds.

Wick took a breath, and their hands shook, and they made themselves speak anyway, because they'd promised themselves, and promises were the only possessions Wick had ever owned outright.

"Odile."

*Shk.* The whetstone paused. "Hm?"

"Can I—" Wick's voice did the thing it did sometimes, sliding up into a register that wasn't quite theirs, the voice of whoever they'd been before six. They hated that. They pushed through it. "Can I ask you something? About your gift?"

The fire cracked. Brannic's story died mid-harmonize. Everyone was looking now, and Wick wished desperately they'd waited for the watch change, but there was no unsaying it.

Odile set down knife and stone. "Ask."

"How do you know," Wick said, "if a memory is yours?"

* * *

The silence went on so long that the moss-light seemed to brighten to fill it, glowing faintly green-gold between the twisted trunks, and somewhere far off in Rotwood something called out in a voice like a hinge.

"I mean," Wick said, faster now, because stopping would be worse, "you steal them. You've told us. First snow. Weddings. Lullabies. You take them out of people and keep them. So how do you know which ones are yours? How do you know any of them are yours?"

Odile didn't answer right away. Wick watched her face do something complicated—the thief's blankness sliding on like a mask, then sliding off again because this was Wick asking, and maybe masks worked differently on found family than on strangers.

"They're heavier," she said finally. "Mine. The ones I was born with. Stolen memories sit light—you can hold a stranger's wedding day in your mind and feel almost nothing, just the shape of it. But your own..." She touched her sternum, an unconscious gesture. "Your own have weight. They pull. Like stones sewn into your coat."

"Show me."

"What?"

"Inside." Wick tapped their own temple. "Show me what a heavy one feels like. Yours. Just—one. Just for a moment."

"Wick." Mother Sable's voice, gentle and warning at once.

"It's fair," Wick said, louder than they meant to. "I'm asking. That's the difference, isn't it? Asking. Nobody asked me anything, back when—" Their voice cracked sideways again. They pressed on. "Back when whatever happened, happened."

Odile looked at them across the fire for a long moment. Then she closed her eyes, and Wick saw her do it—saw her reach inward the way she must reach into others, and her expression changed, softened, went young. When she opened her eyes they were bright and unfocused, seeing something very far away.

"Winter," she said quietly. "I'm four years old, maybe five. There's a woman lifting me above her head in falling snow. Her sleeves smell like cardamom and woodsmoke. I'm screaming with laughter. That's mine. It has weight. It pulls at me every time I touch it, like—" she hesitated— "like a hand holding my ankle. Gently. But holding."

Wick nodded slowly. Something cold was settling in their stomach, cold and clean and certain.

"Okay," they said. "Then here's my confession. And then here's my question. And I need you to hear both before you say anything, because I've practiced this for a week and if you interrupt me I'll lose my nerve and we'll all pretend it never happened and I'll hate all of you forever, politely, forever."

Brannic made a strangled sound that might have been a laugh swallowed whole. Sable reached over and gripped Wick's shoulder—steadying, warm.

"My first memory," Wick said, "is falling down stairs. I'm six years old. I fall down stairs in a house I don't recognize, and a woman I don't know picks me up and says, 'Careful, little wick, you'll gutter out.' That's the beginning. That's the *first*. Everything before is—" they groped for the shape of it— "it's not dark. Dark means something's there and the light's gone out. Before-six is *empty*. It's a room with no walls. Sometimes I dream it and I fall forever and I wake up counting sounds because sounds are real and the dream isn't."

The fire popped. No one moved.

"And here's the question." Wick's hands were shaking badly now; they clasped them together to make them stop, or try. "Inquisitors keep ledgers. Sable told us. Ledgers of stolen memories—what was taken, from whom, and who holds it now. Veyran's people catalogued a century of theft. So somebody, somewhere, wrote down every memory that got pulled out of a child in the night. And children get stolen from, don't they? Children can't fight. Children can't even *report* it, because how do you tell someone your before is missing when you can't remember having a before?"

"Oh," Brannic said softly. "*Oh*, Wick."

"So my question is this." Wick turned to Odile, and made themselves look directly into her eyes, which took everything they had. "Did you take mine? Are you the reason I'm empty?"

* * *

What Wick saw in Odile's face then was worth more than any memory.

Confusion first—genuine, total, the blankness of someone confronted with an accusation they lack the history to understand. Then horror, arriving like cold water, flooding up from wherever her certainty lived. Then something else beneath the horror, something older and sadder, and Wick realized with a strange floating clarity that they were watching Odile Eastwick remember being afraid of herself.

"No," Odile said. "Wick, no. I've never touched you. I swear it on—" she stopped, because what did she have to swear on? A thousand borrowed lives and no family to name. "I swear it on the snow. On the woman in the snow. I have never taken anything from you. I didn't know—I didn't know you were *empty*, I thought—"

"You thought I was private." Wick laughed, and it came out broken. "Everyone thinks I'm private."

"Whoever did it," Odile said, and her voice had gone low and dangerous in a way Wick had only heard once before, at the memory bazaar, when she'd seen her grandmother's mark on those vials, "took everything. All of it. Every wall of the room. That's not theft, that's—that's *amputation*. What kind of monster—"

"The kind that keeps ledgers," Mother Sable said quietly.

Everyone turned to her. Sable sat with her hands folded, her burned-out eyes fixed on the fire, and when she spoke again her voice had the careful flatness of a priestess reciting something she'd sworn never to recite.

"There were orders, during the first years after the covenant broke. Before Veyran codified it, when it was still chaos—factions stealing memories as weapons. One faction specialized in children. Heirs, mostly. Bloodlines that mattered to the old pact. Take a child's whole past and you don't just wound them—you *unmake their claim*. A child with no memory of their blood can't inherit. Can't be recognized. Can't prove who they are." She looked up. "Some of those ledgers list entries marked only by description. Age. Location. Nothing else, because nothing else survived the taking. *Child, approximately six, taken whole.*"

The green-gold moss-light flickered. Somewhere in Rotwood, the hinge-voiced thing called again, closer.

"That could be anyone," Caspian said. His voice was carefully casual in the way Wick had learned to distrust. "Half the realm's full of orphans from the border wars."

"Yes," Sable said. "It could."

But she was looking at Wick the way she looked at scripture—with recognition.

Wick felt it land in their chest like a dropped coin: the weight Odile had described. Except this wasn't a memory. It was the opposite of a memory—it was the weight of an absence, suddenly given a shape. *Heir. Bloodlines that mattered. Unmade claim.*

No. No, that was madness. Wick was a lockpick. Wick was a gutterspark, a stray, a nobody who'd taught themselves letters from tavern signs. Whatever they'd been before six, it wasn't—

It couldn't be—

"Stop," Wick said aloud, and everyone startled. "Stop looking at me like that. I didn't ask for that. I asked one question and you've all gone grave-faced and oracular and I *hate* it." Their voice was climbing toward panic. "I asked because I needed to know if *you* did it, Odile. Because if it was you, I could—hate you, or forgive you, or something, but it would be *knowable*. Instead it's a hundred-year-old ledger and a faction and I can't even hate the right person because the right person is probably *dead*—"

"Breathe," Brannic rumbled.

"Don't tell me to breathe!"

"Then scream," Brannic offered placidly. "Works better. Less dizziness."

And Wick screamed—not loud, not long, but real, a short raw tear of sound that went up into the spiral branches of Rotwood and came back changed, echoing strangely off covenant-broken magic, sounding for a moment like several voices instead of one. Like a chorus of every year they'd lost.

When it faded, the company sat very still. Then Brannic nodded, satisfied, as if Wick had passed some test, and went back to poking the fire.

* * *

Later, when the others had rolled into their blankets and the fire had gone down to its red bones again, Wick found Odile sitting awake at the camp's edge, knees drawn up, whetstone motionless in her lap. Wick sat down beside her. Neither of them spoke for a while. In Rotwood, silence was never really silent—the trees creaked in their spirals, the moss breathed its faint light, and far off, things moved that had been people once, or animals once, or neither once.

"I believe you," Wick said finally. "That you didn't take mine. For the record. If records matter anymore."

"Thank you." A pause. "For what it's worth, I checked. Tonight. All thousand-some. None of them are yours. None of them are even *shaped* like yours—an absence that big leaves a shadow on the thief, did you know that? Taking a whole life costs something. Whoever did it paid a price, or didn't care enough to notice."

"Comforting."

"I'm told comfort isn't my strongest suit." Odile turned the whetstone over in her fingers. "Wick. What you said at the fire. About wanting it to be knowable. About hating me or forgiving me." She was quiet a moment. "I need you to say the rest of it. Whatever you practiced. I think I already know, and I need to hear it anyway."

Wick looked out at the dark trees. Counted sounds: wind, creak, distant hinge-thing, Brannic's whistle-breath, Sable's prayer-mutter, Caspian's too-still silence on watch. Six sounds. Six years and some. The arithmetic of their whole self.

"When I was lying awake deciding to ask you," they said, "I wasn't really deciding whether to ask. I was deciding whether to ask *before* or *after* I told you the other thing. The real thing." They pulled their knees up to match hers. "Here it is. I'm scared of you, Odile. Not of you-you. Of your gift. Because I know now what it feels like to have everything taken, and you're the only person I've ever met who can do the taking, and I love you—I love all of them, I love this stupid barley-and-pine-needle family—and love means you *could*. That's what love is, partly. Being able to hurt someone and choosing not to, every day, forever, without anyone checking."

Odile had gone very still beside them.

"So here's what I'm asking." Wick turned to face her fully. "Never take mine. My memories. Not to save my life, not to save yours, not to save the mission, not for the prophecy, not for the Court, not for the nine realms and all their knitting-together. Never. Promise me. Promise me knowing that someday you might be desperate, and tired, and sure you're right, and that the promise will feel like a stone in your shoe on the worst day of your life. Promise me *anyway*."

The silence stretched. The moss-light pulsed, slow as breathing.

"You're asking me to promise something," Odile said at last, very quietly, "that I may be forced to break."

"I know."

"You understand that I can't promise lightly. That if I give you my word and then break it, I'll have become the thing that made you—the thing that emptied you. You'd rather I never promised at all?"

"No," Wick said. "I'd rather you promised and fought like hell to keep it. Because that's the difference between you and whoever emptied me. They took without asking. You'll take without asking *and it will cost you everything*, and I'll know, even if I don't know why, even if all I have left is six years and some—I'll know that you fought. That's worth more to me than safety. Safety's just a room with thick walls. I've lived in rooms with thick walls. They're all empty on the inside."

Odile looked at them for a long time. In the moss-light her face seemed younger and older at once, a thousand lives moving under her skin, and Wick watched her choose.

"On my mother's name," Odile said. "On the woman in the snow, whose face I still have, whose sleeves smelled like cardamom and woodsmoke. On every heavy memory I own. I will never take yours, Wick. Not for the nine realms. Not for the covenant. Not to save your life or mine or the world's. Your memories are yours—what there is of them, and everything you build on top of what's missing. I am not a door into you. I refuse to be a door into you."

Wick hadn't known they were going to cry until it was already happening—great ugly silent tears, the kind that come from somewhere below six years old, from the empty room itself. Odile put an arm around them, awkwardly, the way a person does when they've read about comforting people more than they've done it, and Wick leaned into the awkwardness and let it hold them anyway.

"Thank you," they managed.

"Don't thank me yet." Odile's voice was odd—strained, looking-at-something-far-away strained. "Promises are easy tonight. We hit Salt Bridge tomorrow. And the inquisitors there take memories as toll, Wick. As *toll*. Which means tomorrow, someone in this company is going to have to pay, and I'm the only one who can decide what the payment is."

She said it lightly. But Wick heard the weight underneath—the heavy kind, the pulling kind, the stone-sewn-in-the-coat kind.

And later, curled on their bedroll counting the night sounds of Rotwood—wind, creak, hinge-thing, whistle-breath, prayer-mutter, too-still-watch—Wick fell asleep for the first time in weeks without counting first, and dreamed of nothing at all, and woke at dawn to the smell of smoke that wasn't their fire, and the sound of Brannic saying, quietly, from the edge of camp:

"Odile. There's a road ahead. And there are boots on it. Lots of boots."
