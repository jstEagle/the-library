## 12. The Toll of Salt Bridge

The bridge announced itself long before we saw it, by smell.

Salt, first—the whole estuary breathing brine into the marshland like a sleeping thing—but under it something sharper. Ozone and hot iron. Wick smelled it too, or felt it; they had gone quiet an hour back, the way they did when the world was about to ask them a question they couldn't answer.

"That's memory-burning," Mother Sable said, without opening her eyes. She rode with her hands folded in her lap as if in prayer, though she'd told us once she no longer believed anyone was listening. "You get so you can tell it from woodsmoke. Woodsmoke wants to warm you. That smell doesn't want anything. It just takes."

The causeway rose out of the grey marsh in a long pale spine, and at its crown sat Salt Bridge itself: a humped span of white stone older than any of the free cities, its parapets crusted with salt-flowers that glittered like frost that had forgotten how to melt. Below it, the tide ran amber-brown with silt, carrying gulls and driftwood and—once, briefly—a child's shoe, which I looked away from.

And above it, on the far tower, hung a banner I knew now the way you know the sound of your own name spoken by a stranger with bad intentions: black on bone-white, nine broken circles. Veyran's mark. The usurper's edict made cloth.

"They'll have a toll," Caspian said. He had dismounted to walk beside my horse, close enough that our stirrups nearly touched, and there was something in his voice he was trying very hard to make casual. "Memory-magic is outlawed in the occupied cities, but out here the inquisitors do their own accounting. They take memories as tax."

"From everyone?" Wick asked.

"From travelers. From anyone whose face suggests a story." He glanced at me, and I caught it again—that flicker, that half-second where his eyes went somewhere else, consulting something behind them. "They can't tell a stolen memory from an owned one, Odile. But they can tell *quantity*. A person carrying hundreds of lives lights up to their instruments like a lantern in a cellar."

"Then I'm a bonfire," I said.

"You're a lighthouse," Sable said dryly. "Pray they're not sailors."

Brannic said nothing at all. He never said much when we approached anything with walls, but I'd learned to read him anyway: he tilted his head slightly, the way a dog does at a whistle only it can hear, and I knew he was listening to the bridge. Brannic heard doors—not the wood and iron of them but the *idea* of them, the intention, the hinge and the lock and whatever stood waiting on the other side. He'd saved us twice already with that ear. Once in Rotwood, when the wraith came whispering through a gap in the world wearing my mother's voice.

"What do you hear?" I asked him quietly.

His jaw worked. Words were expensive for Brannic; he spent them like a miser. "Chains," he said at last. "A door that opens both ways. And underneath—" his hand rose vaguely toward his own temple, "—a lot of people crying where nobody's home."

*People crying where nobody's home.* I turned that over as we rode down onto the causeway, and I didn't like what it meant. The inquisitors kept ledgers—I knew that much from Sable's grim briefings—and ledgers need vaults. Vaults need doors. And doors, in this new world we were walking through, needed keepers who had been emptied out enough to hold anything.

I had been emptying myself for years before any of this started. It turns out I was practicing.

* * *

The tollhouse squatted at the bridge's midpoint like a tumor on the spine of the old stone, all fresh timber and black iron bolted carelessly onto masonry that had carried pilgrims and merchants for eight hundred years without asking anyone for a single coin. Two braziers burned at either end of the checkpoint, and the smoke rising off them was not woodsmoke. I could feel it from thirty paces—a faint tug at the base of my skull, a hook dragging gently through the water of me. They fed the fires with distilled memory. Someone's anniversary, someone's funeral, someone's name said aloud by a loving mouth—all of it burning to light the inquisitors' work.

There were six of them. Five soldiers in Veyran's black-and-white, bored and cold-chapped, spears leaning against the parapet. And one inquisitor, standing at a lectern beneath a canopy against the drizzle, writing in a ledger with a pen that scratched like a beetle in a wall.

He looked up as we clattered onto the span, and I watched his face do the thing faces did around me lately: sharpen. Focus. A fisherman noticing a shadow under his boat.

"Travelers." His voice was pleasant. That was the worst part of them, I was learning. They weren't monsters; monsters would have been simpler. He was perhaps fifty, grey at the temples, with ink-stained fingers and the mild eyes of a schoolmaster. "Welcome to Salt Bridge. State your business and your origin."

"Pilgrims," Sable said smoothly, "bound for the shrines at Verance. We come from the river villages."

"The river villages." He wrote it down. The pen-scratch set my teeth on edge. "Names?"

Sable gave four names that belonged to no one. I gave mine last, and watched his pen pause over the page.

"Odile," he repeated. "Unusual, this far north." He smiled at me, and behind the mildness something moved, patient and vast, like a pike turning under still water. "You'll understand that the King's Edict requires inspection of all travelers. Memory-magic has been outlawed by decree—its practice, its trade, and its transport. The toll for crossing is small." He gestured at the braziers with his pen. "A memory apiece. Something minor. A meal. A road. Nothing you'll miss."

"And if we refuse?" Caspian asked.

The inquisitor's smile widened by a degree. "Then you swim, and the tide decides. Though—" he tilted his head, and I felt the hook sink deeper, that gentle dragging pull, "—I confess I'm curious about your companion. She carries rather more than a meal's worth. Rather more than a *year's* worth, if my instrument isn't lying to me." He tapped the lectern. Beneath his ledger sat a shallow bronze dish filled with liquid that reflected nothing—not the grey sky, not the brazier-light, nothing at all. A scrying pan. In its blank surface, I had no doubt, I blazed like a festival.

My stomach dropped through the bridge and into the amber water below.

"We're just travelers," I said, and hated how thin it sounded.

"Mm," said the inquisitor, and opened his ledger to a fresh page. "Everyone is, until they aren't."

* * *

They took us off the horses. That was the first concession, and once you've made the first one the rest arrive like guests after a feast. Spears herded us against the parapet while the inquisitor—he introduced himself as Toll-Keeper Anselm, as though it were a parish appointment—examined our papers, our packs, our faces. Wick shook. Not visibly; Wick never did anything visibly. But I stood next to them and I felt it coming off them like heat off a stove, and I remembered what Wick had told me on the road through Rotwood, in the dark, in the smallest voice I'd ever heard from them:

*I don't have anything before six years old. If they take the rest, there won't be a me left to miss it.*

Anselm reached Wick last. He held up the scrying pan and studied its nothing-colored surface, and his eyebrows rose with genuine scholarly interest.

"Well," he said softly. "Well, well. You're barely there at all, are you, little one? What happened to you?"

Wick said nothing. Their knuckles were white on the parapet stone.

"Ah," Anselm said, with terrible gentleness. "One of those. The war makes so many of you. Emptied vessels." He turned to me, and now the pike was fully surfaced, the schoolmaster gone entirely. "You know what I think, Odile-of-the-river-villages? I think you're not a pilgrim. I think you're a carrier. A smuggler of the old art. And I think that vessel beside you is yours—taken young, kept close, because a thief always needs a strongbox."

"I'm not—" My voice cracked. I steadied it. "We've done nothing."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You've done quite a lot. The question is only how much, and whether the Crown will thank you for it." He closed the ledger. Behind him, two soldiers shifted their spears off the parapet and pointed them nowhere in particular, which is how soldiers point them when the order hasn't come yet but everyone knows it's coming. "Here is what will happen. You will each pay the standard toll. Then the lady will pay a special tariff, assessed at my discretion, for unlicensed transport of mnemonic substance." His smile was almost kind. "Or you may refuse, and I will send word ahead to Verance, where the garrison has a full interrogation suite and considerably less patience than I. I'm told they begin with the empty ones. They find the screaming shorter."

The rain thickened. Somewhere below the bridge, the tide turned, and the gulls went up screaming.

I looked at my people. At Sable, whose hands had gone still in her lap, praying to no one. At Brannic, whose head was bowed as if listening to some door deep under the world that even he dreaded hearing. At Caspian, whose face was composed into careful blankness—but whose eyes had found mine with a ferocity that said *not yet, not here, we are too close*, and beneath the ferocity something rawer that I refused, right then, to name.

At Wick. Sixteen years old and holding maybe ten years of self, gripping the stone like the wind might blow them off the face of the earth entirely.

"All right," I said.

Anselm's brows lifted, pleased. "Sensible."

"But the toll comes from me." I stepped forward, off the parapet, into the open space between my family and his spears. "All of it. For all five. You said discretion was yours. Use it. One payment, one payer."

"That's not—"

"I'm a carrier, you said yourself." I let the truth slide out sideways, dressed as a confession. "Which means everything I carry is contraband anyway. Confiscate it. That's what you do with contraband, isn't it? You don't tax the smuggler's companions for the smuggler's goods." I held his gaze. Schoolmasters respect a clever student; it's their one weakness. "Unless your discretion runs smaller than your suspicion."

Something flickered across his face—vanity wrestling procedure—and vanity won, the way it usually does in men with titles. "One payer," he conceded. "But I'll choose what's taken. Standard tariff first." He gestured to the nearer brazier. "Hold your hand over the coals. The fire does the rest."

I knew how it worked, of course. Better than he did. Distilled flame ate memory from the outside in, newest and brightest first, unless you knew the trick—which was that memory wasn't really stored in the mind at all, but in the grip the mind kept on it, and a thief's grip was different from a keeper's. I could choose what burned. I could feed the fire a life I'd stolen decades ago, some stranger's afternoon I'd been hoarding like a dragon hoards candlesticks, and Anselm's instruments would read it as a rich payment and never know it cost me nothing I actually lived.

I raised my hand over the coals.

Heat licked my palm. The pulling began—that familiar tidal drag, the sensation of a page being turned somewhere inside me—and I rifled my hoard the way I hadn't let myself do since the valley. *Not the snow,* some panicked voice said absurdly, *not the ferryman's snow, not that one, not—* and then, because panic points where it shouldn't, my inner eye fell on a vial-shaped memory near the bottom of the stack, one I'd taken years ago from a dying woman in a market town whose name I never learned, and which I had never once opened because opening it hurt in a way that wasn't mine.

Her wedding day.

That was all I knew of her. Grey-haired, laughing, dancing badly with a man who must have been her husband, in a courtyard strung with paper lanterns, while someone's fiddle played and children chased each other between the tables and she thought—this was the part I'd never been able to bear, the part that had made me close the vial forever—she thought, looking at his dear foolish sweating face under the lanterns, *forty-one years and I'd marry him again tomorrow, I would, I swear it.*

I had stolen that. Off a dying woman, in a market, for no reason except that it was bright and I was hungry and hunger was the only law I kept.

I fed it to the fire.

It went out of me the way all thefts go out—smoothly, easily, the flame drinking the brightness down—and for one horrible half-second I was in the courtyard again, lanterns and fiddle and forty-one years, and then it was ash on the wind over the amber water and some dead woman's joy was warming an inquisitor's post.

"There," I said, and my voice came out steady, which felt like a second theft. "Paid."

Anselm consulted his pan. Whatever blazed there seemed to satisfy him; his eyebrows climbed with connoisseur's appreciation. "Rich," he murmured. "Warm. A celebration of some kind?" He looked up at me with real curiosity. "Yours?"

"Yes," I lied, and the lie fit so neatly into place that I understood, with a sick lurch, how easy the rest of my life had made this. How practiced the shape of my mouth was. "Mine."

He waved us through. Just like that. Spears lowered, ledger closed, toll collected, the great machine of Veyran's justice grinding onward on a diet of other people's happiness. We gathered the horses and walked them past the tollhouse, past the braziers, past the scrying pan full of nothing, and the white stones of the bridge rang under the horses' feet like the bells of a church that had forgotten its god.

I did not look back at the fire.

* * *

We camped that night in a hollow beyond the bridge, among dunes stiff with salt-grass, and built a small fire because the cold demanded it and no amount of guilt was going to warm anybody. Sable cooked. Brannic took first watch without being asked. Caspian sat beside me at the fire's edge, close, saying nothing, and after a while he said, quietly, "What did you give them?"

"A stranger's wedding day."

He was silent. The fire popped. Out on the dark flats, the tide breathed in and out.

"It was hers," I said. I hadn't meant to say anything; the words came anyway, low, aimed at the flames. "Whoever she was. Some woman in a market town, years ago. She was dying and I took it because it was beautiful and I wanted it. I told myself the rules, you know? Never steal pain. Never steal from family. Never leave the valley. I had *rules*, Caspian. Rules that were all about keeping me comfortable. And every beautiful thing I ever lifted off somebody dying alone in a market—that was fine, that was within the rules, that didn't count." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Today I paid a toll with a woman's happiest day and I lied to the man who took it and said it was *mine*, because the truth was worse. Because the truth was that I stole it and never even watched it. I hoarded her wedding like a coin."

"Odile—"

"She thought she'd marry him again tomorrow." My throat closed. "Forty-one years. She'd have married him again tomorrow. And now it's smoke over Salt Bridge, and he's probably dead too, and there's no one left in the world who remembers that day except the fire that ate it."

Caspian's hand moved—hovered near mine on the cold sand, didn't land. I was grateful and furious about that in equal measure.

"You saved Wick today," he said finally. "And Sable. And Brannic. And me."

"I know." I stared into the fire until my eyes stung. "That's what's unforgivable about it. It *worked*. That's the lesson, isn't it? That's what this whole crusade is teaching me. Every mile east, the price gets paid in other people's lives and the mission keeps moving, and I keep being the one who spends them, because I'm the only one who can. Grandmother's blood, remember. Thieves all the way down." I pulled my knees up. "How many strangers' days do I burn between here and the Ninefold Court, Caspian? Do you know? Does your book say?"

He flinched. Small—almost nothing—but I'd spent my whole life reading faces for the flicker of a hidden pocket, and I saw it. Saw the flinch, and saw what it snagged on: not the accusation. The word *book.*

"Caspian," I said slowly. "Does your book say?"

He looked at me across the fire, and the firelight moved in his eyes, and for three full seconds—one breath, two, three—Caspian, who had lied to inquisitors and princes and, I suspected, to himself, looked like a man deciding whether to tell the truth.

"No," he said at last. And then, softer, in a voice I'd never heard him use: "But it says something else. About you. And I—" his hand closed, empty, in his lap, "—I've been meaning to tell you. There's a line I haven't read you. A line I've been..."

He stopped. Behind him, at the edge of the firelight, Brannic had gone rigid on watch, head cocked toward the dark, listening to some door none of the rest of us could hear.

"Caspian," I said. "What line?"
