## 13. Caspian's Lie

The fire had burned down to its bones by the time Odile woke.

She didn't know what pulled her from sleep. Not a sound—the camp was quiet, the kind of quiet that came after Wick finally stopped talking and Brannic's breathing settled into the slow tide of true rest. Not a smell—woodsmoke and wet ash, the green rot of Rotwood still clinging to everything like a second skin. It was something older than either. A thief's instinct, honed in a valley where she'd spent years reading the small betrayals of bodies: the shift of weight, the held breath, the lie folded into stillness.

She opened her eyes without moving her head and looked across the embers at Caspian.

He was sitting with his back to a lightning-split oak, the prophecy book open on his knees. That alone wasn't strange. He read it every night, turning pages by candlelight with the reverence of a priest handling relics, tracing lines of ink as though he could feel the pulse beneath them. She'd watched him do it a dozen times on the road from Bellharbor, half-annoyed, half-fascinated. The book was why she was here. The book was the whole reason any of this existed—her name written in ink before her birth, a date, a valley, a destiny she hadn't asked for.

But tonight he wasn't reading.

Tonight he was tearing.

Page by page, quietly, with the care of a man defusing something, he was feeding them to the fire.

Odile lay very still and watched a century-old prophecy curl into orange lace and vanish. His face was lit from below, and it was the wrong face for what he was doing—not cruel, not gleeful, but *grieved*, grief worn like a coat he'd put on so long ago he'd forgotten how to take it off. His lips moved as each page went, whispering words she couldn't catch, and she understood with a cold, settling certainty that they were apologies.

She counted. Four pages. Five. Her heart was doing something strange and slow, like a bell rung underwater.

Then she rose, silently, the way she did everything, and said, "That's not yours to burn."

He flinched. For a man who claimed to be sent by the Ninefold Court, for a man who'd walked into an unfindable valley and known her name, he flinched like an ordinary guilty man caught at an ordinary crime—and somehow that was worse.

"Odile." He closed the book over his hands, as if hiding the wound would help. "You should be asleep."

"I was." She came around the fire, stepping over Brannic's outstretched legs, and stopped close enough to see the ash on his fingers. "I've been watching you for three nights, actually. You sleep last, you wake first, and whenever you think no one's looking, you check on me the way a man checks a lock he knows is failing." She held out her hand. "Give me the book."

"It's Court property."

"So am I, apparently. Give me the book."

A muscle worked in his jaw. Somewhere off in the dark, Rotwood groaned—a sound like a door too large for its frame—and neither of them moved until it faded. Then he set the book in her palm, and it was lighter than it should have been. Lighter the way a purse is lighter after a cutpurse has been at it.

She turned the pages slowly. The prophecy ran in a spidery hand across vellum gone the color of old teeth, and there were gaps now, ragged stumps of spine where pages had been razored free long ago—but fresh gaps too, five of them, their edges browned by smoke. She found the page with her date. She found the page with her name. And she found the place where the verse should have continued past *the Covenant-Bearer shall stand at the ninth gate* and simply—stopped. Cut clean.

"What was on those pages?" she asked.

"Odile."

"What. Was. On. Them."

He looked into the fire instead of at her. When he finally spoke, his voice was quiet enough that she had to lean toward him, and she hated herself for leaning.

"The ending," he said. "What happens when the covenant is reforged."

"And?"

And then he made the mistake. She watched him make it—watched the pause stretch one heartbeat too long, watched his eyes flick left, the tell of a man assembling a lie brick by brick. She'd seen ferryman's wives do it. She'd done it herself a thousand times, smiling, lifting a memory while her mouth said something kind.

"There isn't much detail," he said. "The ink's degraded. Rituals, mostly. Oaths to speak. Nothing that—"

"Caspian." She said his name gently, and that gentleness was a blade; she saw him recognize it. "I steal memories for a living. I know what a lie looks like from the inside. Try again."

The fire popped. Embers climbed like dying stars.

He closed his eyes. "You die," he said.

The word landed in the clearing and stayed there, ringing.

"The Covenant-Bearer completes the rite," he went on, low and fast now, as if he could outrun the truth by speaking quickly, "and the covenant takes everything. The prophecy names it plainly—it always named it plainly, I'm the one who made it complicated. *She who binds the realms shall be unbound from them.* I told myself it was ambiguous. I told myself it meant exile, or transformation, or—" He laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "I'm very good at telling myself things."

"How long?" Odile asked. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears, calm, almost bored, and she knew that calm—it was the same flat quiet that came over her when she lifted a memory, the thief's trance, except now she was the vault being emptied. "How long have you known?"

"Since before I came to the valley."

The world tilted slightly, righted itself.

"Since before." She repeated it, tasting it. "Before the dinner. Before the book. Before you sat down across from me in the only home I've ever had and told me I was chosen." Something hot was rising in her chest, and she welcomed it, because the alternative was the cold. "Chosen by whom, Caspian? Because it wasn't the Court. The Court doesn't send anyone anywhere—it's a ruin full of empty chairs. You chose. You looked at the prophecy, you read the ending, and you got on a boat anyway."

"I did what needed doing."

"Don't." The word cracked out of her louder than she intended. Brannic stirred; Wick murmured in sleep. Odile lowered her voice to something worse than shouting—something precise. "Don't you dare dress this up as duty. I have spent my entire life being *useful*. Useful to strangers who paid me. Useful to a gift I never wanted. Do you know what it's like to be worth something only for what you can take? And then you arrive, and for the first time someone looks at *me*—my name, my face, my terrible jokes about canal fish—and I thought—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "I thought I'd been *seen*. Not recruited. Seen."

"You were both," Caspian said. "I won't insult you by pretending otherwise."

"At least have the decency to lie well."

"No." He stood, and up close he was taller than she remembered, or she was smaller. "You deserve better than my best lie. Here is the truth, all of it: the Court sent me because I'm the last herald they have and I was expendable. The prophecy gave me your name and your death in the same line, and I read it a hundred times looking for a loophole, and there wasn't one. And I came anyway, because if you don't die, everyone else does. Veyran wins. The free cities burn. The fae go out like candles." His voice frayed. "I chose the world over you. Every day since, I've been trying to find a way to choose both, and every day I fail, and every night I burn pages because I can't stand for you to read what I already know is coming."

"That's not sacrifice," Odile said. "Burning the pages. That's just theft. You're stealing the truth from me the way I steal memories from other people—you decided what I could carry." She hugged the ruined book against her chest, feeling its missing weight like missing teeth. "Do you want to know the worst part? I have a rule. Never steal pain. I built my whole life around it. And you—" her breath hitched, and she hated that too, hated the tear that got loose and tracked down her cheek in the firelight where he could see it— "you carried my death around for months and never once thought it belonged to me."

"Odile—"

"No." She stepped back. "No, you don't get to say my name like that. Not tonight."

Behind her, the camp was waking—she heard Wick sit up, heard the soft rustle of Brannic reaching for his axe, felt Mother Sable's eyes open somewhere in the dark like two banked coals. Let them hear. Let them all hear. A family that formed around a secret deserved to watch it come apart.

"Here's what happens now," she said. "We go to the Court. I don't know if the prophecy is true—I've met prophecies, they're mostly weather, mostly guesswork dressed in poetry. But I decide what I carry from here on. Me. Not you, not the Court, not some dead scribe with good handwriting." She pressed the book back into his hands, hard enough to hurt. "Keep it. Read it to me every night if you like. Every word. Including the ones you burned—if you remember them. Because I promise you, Caspian, I will find out what they said. It's the only thing I know how to do."

She turned away from the fire, away from his face, and walked to the edge of the camp where the dark of Rotwood stood waiting, patient as a creditor.

"Odile." His voice behind her was wrecked. "Where are you going?"

"For a walk," she said, without turning. "Alone. It's the only company I trust tonight."

The trees swallowed her in three steps. Behind her the fire shrank to an orange coin, then a spark, then nothing, and she walked blind through the twisted wood with tears cooling on her face and her hands shaking in a way they hadn't shaken even when Veyran's inquisitors were burning memory-keepers alive in Bellharbor's markets.

Because fire she could understand. Fire was honest.

This was the oldest wound, opened fresh: not the danger, not the death sentence scrawled in century-old ink. Being *used*. Being the tool someone reached for when the work needed doing, valued exactly as far as her usefulness and not one inch further. She'd fled the Nine Realms to escape it, built a valley around herself like a wall, and the first person to make her feel visible had walked straight through the wall carrying her name and her death in the same book.

Somewhere ahead, water dripped from black branches. Somewhere behind her, faintly, she heard footsteps—light ones, quick ones, the unmistakable rhythm of a teenager who refused to be left alone.

"Wick," she called softly, not stopping, not turning, "if you're following me, I swear—"

"If you forget me, I'll introduce myself again," came the answer, breathless and stubborn, closer now. "You said family shows up. Well. Here I am."

And Odile stood in the dark between the crooked trees, torn clean down the middle—one half still walking toward the coast and oblivion, the other half already turning back toward the small stubborn voice, the dying fire, the liar who wept while he burned pages—

—when the forest itself went silent, every dripping branch, every groaning door-sound, all at once, the way a room goes quiet when someone dangerous has entered it.

Ahead of her, deep in Rotwood, something began to hum a lullaby in a voice that knew her grandmother's name.
