## 14. The Free Cities' Council

Verance had been built by people who believed the world could be argued into order.

Mother Sable felt it in every stone of the Council Spire as they climbed it—every stair a syllogism, every landing a rebuttal, the whole tower rising in white granite and measured proportion above the city like a sermon delivered to the clouds. No gargoyle glared from its eaves. No saint wept on its lintels. The old gods had been scraped away here, along with everything else that could not be cross-examined, and what remained was a building that looked at you with the patient, terrible confidence of a man who has never once been wrong and never once been tested.

She had walked up these stairs forty years ago, a novice in grey wool with ink still under her fingernails from copying covenant-liturgies she barely understood. She had come then to plead for the shrine-cities of the eastern marches. She had been received politely, thanked sincerely, and forgotten completely. The marches burned within the decade.

Now her knees ached on the same stairs, and Odile Eastwick climbed beside her with a thief's flat watchfulness, and Caspian moved like a man walking into a room he'd already memorized, and behind them came Brannic—too tall for the corridor, hooded against stares—and Wick, small and quick and cataloguing every guard post with a professional's eye.

"They'll have wards," Wick murmured. "Cheap ones. I could—"

"You could walk through them like a lady," Sable said, "because we are not sneaking into this council. That is the entire point of us."

Wick made a face but subsided. The child had no memories before their sixth year; Sable had learned not to ask what shaped them, only to watch what they did with the shape they had. What they did, mostly, was count exits.

At the top of the stairs, doors of pale oak stood open onto the council chamber, and Sable stopped a moment on the threshold to let the room tell her what it was.

It told her: nine seats on a raised crescent, one for each free city, occupied by men and women in merchant silks and magistrate's wool. It told her: guards along the walls with pikes, ceremonial and dull. It told her: clerks at side tables with ledgers open, quills moving, recording nothing that mattered. And it told her, in the particular stillness around three empty chairs near the western wall, that someone had expected them and chosen to make them wait.

"Chairs are over there," Odile said quietly. "The ones nobody's using."

"I see them."

"They're doing it on purpose."

"Of course they're doing it on purpose." Sable gathered her robes—the good ones, the ones she had sworn never to wear again after Verance's archivists stripped her orders from the rolls. Grey wool gone soft with age, embroidered at the hem with the nine-linked rings of the old covenant, stitched by hands two generations dead. "Everything in this room is done on purpose. That is its disease."

They sat. They waited. The council let them wait while a delegate from Saltmarsh finished a tedious report on herring tariffs, and Sable understood that too was on purpose—that the first thing these people would give the Covenant-Bearer and her companions would be fish.

She used the time as she had learned to use all time: praying. Not to any god who listened anymore. The old covenant had bound mortal prayer to fae hearing, and when the pact broke, every prayer in the Nine Realms became a letter mailed to a burned house. But the posture of prayer steadied her. Hands folded. Breath low. She prayed the way a soldier checks a blade—not because the enemy fears it, but because the hand does.

"—and therefore the assessment stands," the Saltmarsh delegate concluded. "Next: the matter of the petitioners."

A woman rose from the center seat of the crescent. Chancellor Ives, Sable remembered her name from forty years ago too—Ives had been a junior clerk then, taking notes with the same compressed mouth. Now she wore the chancellor's chain of interlocked silver squares and looked out at the little company with the expression of a woman reading a bill of lading.

"Mother Sable," Ives said. "Defrocked. Of the Order of the Nine Links." A pause precisely long enough to be heard. "You bring a petition regarding—prophecy?"

"Not a petition, Chancellor. A warning, an account, and a request for arms."

"An account." Ives glanced at a clerk, who nodded. "We received your preliminary letter. You claim the covenant between the Nine Realms can be reforged. You claim a bearer has been named by prophecy. You claim the usurper Veyran will march on the free cities within the year unless—" she consulted the page, though Sable doubted she needed to— "unless we commit militia, treasury, and treaty-rights to an expedition into fae territory. Have I summarized fairly?"

"Fairly enough."

"Then let me summarize our position." Ives folded her hands. "One hundred years ago, the covenant broke. Whatever bond once tied our cities to the Ninefold Court dissolved, and the world did not end. The borders grew strange, yes. Magic soured at the marches. We adjusted. We built walls, we chartered wardens, we taxed the odd and licensed the useful. For a century, Verance and her sisters have prospered by exactly one principle: we owe nothing to thrones we cannot audit." Her voice did not rise; it narrowed. "And now you ask us to believe that a thief, a defrocked priestess, a foundling, a cutpurse, and a stranger with no surname carry a charter older than any of our cities, written in a book none of us may examine, demanding blood and gold on faith alone."

"The book is here," Caspian said. "You may examine anything you wish."

Every head turned toward him. He had that gift—Sable had watched it work on Odile, and watched it curdle, and understood even then that it was not entirely a gift. When Caspian spoke, rooms reorganized themselves around his voice the way iron filings answer a magnet. He rose, drew the prophecy book from its oilcloth wrappings, and laid it on the clerk's table with the reverence of a man presenting a relic he personally did not believe in.

Sable had read it cover to cover twice since Rotwood. It was genuine; that was the horror of it. Century-old vellum, period bindings, ink chemically consistent with its claimed age, and on a page near the end, in a hand that predated Odile Eastwick's grandmother by decades:

*When the thief of yesterday crosses the valley's edge in the month of ash, the Covenant-Bearer rises.*

And beneath it, smaller, in the same hand: *the date shall be known.*

Tonight's date. Her valley. Her name.

Ives descended from the crescent and examined the book herself, turning pages with dry, competent fingers. The chamber held its breath with theatrical patience.

"Clever work," Ives said at last.

"Chancellor—"

"Ink and vellum can be aged, Mother. Hands can be forged. I have seen forged relics sold in this city's markets for more than your order's annual budget." She closed the book gently, almost tenderly, and Sable realized with a cold drop in her stomach that the gentleness was the insult. "But set forgery aside. Grant me the impossible: the book is true. Consider what you are actually asking." She began to walk along the crescent, and the delegates' eyes followed her like students'. "A century ago, the fae broke faith with mortals—or mortals with the fae; the histories disagree, which should itself instruct us. Since then, the fae realms have rotted at their edges, and things have crept out of that rot into ours. And your remedy is to reopen the door. To bind us again—permanently, irrevocably, by oath—to courts of creatures who live centuries, hold grudges longer, and regard our lives the way we regard livestock. You call Veyran a usurper. Perhaps he is. But he is a *human* usurper, Chancellor, and I know how to negotiate with human ambition. Tell me how one negotiates with the Ninefold Court."

"You don't," Sable said.

Ives stopped.

"You don't negotiate with the Court," Sable said, rising, because her knees had decided for her. "That is the first thing my order taught and the last thing your grandfathers unlearned. The covenant was never a treaty between equals. It was a promise between neighbors. Mortal and fae, sharing a wall, keeping one hearth between them so neither side froze. When it broke, the wall came down—and what is coming through the gap is not negotiation. It is weather."

"Rhetoric," said a heavy delegate from Graywater.

"Ledgers, then." Sable's voice cracked across the chamber like a whip on ice, and she was distantly astonished at herself—forty years of silence, and it came out of her now like water from a broken cask. "You want accounts? I kept them. Forty years in the field shrines, and I wrote down every soul who came to me hollowed. The memory-takers worked the eastern roads for a generation before anyone called it a plague. Whole villages that forgot their dead. Children who forgot their mothers' faces. An entire parish that sang at harvest and could not say why. I recorded four hundred and six names before the archivists struck our order from the rolls for 'superstition.'" She turned slowly, letting them see her face, letting them find their own towns in it if they had ears to. "Veyran did not invent the hungering. He organized it. He gave it uniforms and ledgers and a doctrine, and now his inquisitors burn memory-keepers alive in Bellharbor's fish market while honest burghers stand by and applaud, because the edict promised them safety, and safety is the one commodity your cities have never once failed to buy."

"Allegations," Ives said. "Secondhand."

"I was there," Odile said.

Her voice was quiet, and that was why it carried. Sable had heard her describe the fish market once, at night, to the fire, in five flat sentences, and never again. Now she stood in the white light of the council chamber with her thief's stillness and said, "I watched Inquisitor Voss's people chain a woman to a post in Bellharbor. She was a memory-keeper—a midwife, mostly. She carried other people's griefs so they didn't have to. They burned her with her ledger. Half the crowd cheered. The other half checked their own heads to make sure nothing was missing." She paused. "Your walls won't stop them. I steal memories for a living. Walls are nothing. Your treasuries are nothing. What Veyran wants is in your skulls, and he has an army of people very good at taking it."

Silence. A clerk's quill scratched. Somewhere high above, wind worried at the spire's stone ribs.

Then the Graywater delegate laughed—not cruelly, worse, comfortably. "A thief vouches for a priestess vouches for a prophet. Forgive us, mistress, if we decline to arm our cities on the testimony of confessed felons about monsters none of us have seen."

"You've seen them," Brannic said.

He had not spoken since the doors. His voice came out low and resonant, the voice of a large man careful with large things, and when the delegates turned, he pushed back his hood.

Sable watched them look at him. Watched them see the height, the wrongness of the proportions, the faint silver tracery at his temples where fae blood showed through skin like frost under bark. Watched the fear arrive, and the arithmetic behind the fear—*one of them, here, in our chamber, at our table*—and watched Chancellor Ives, to her credit, not reach for the guards.

"Rotwood," Brannic said. "Two weeks east of here, past the toll-roads. Your maps mark it as timberland. Go and listen. The trees there remember being something else. The beasts remember being men. That is what a broken covenant sounds like, honored delegates. It does not sound like rhetoric. It sounds like teeth." He bowed, precise and bitter. "I am what grows on your border when you stop paying attention to it."

"Enough." Ives returned to her seat, and Sable saw the moment the chamber's mind closed—it happened the way a harbor gate closes, visibly, mechanically, without malice. "Here is Verance's answer, and I give it to you plainly, because plainly is all this council knows. We will not arm for a war against the usurper on the strength of an unauditable book. We will not break the Peace of the Sisters to fund an expedition into fae territory. If Veyran's armies march west, the cities will treat—as cities always have. We survived a broken covenant. We will survive a new king." She gathered her papers. "You will be given safe conduct out of Verance and three days' grace beyond the walls. Use them well."

"That's it?" Wick's voice, sharp with adolescent contempt. "People are dying and you're giving us *safe conduct*?"

"Wick." Sable's hand found the girl's shoulder.

"No—they're just going to sit here with their herring reports until the inquisitors knock on the door—"

"*Wick.*"

The chamber resumed its business around them, the way water resumes around a withdrawn hand. Delegates murmured. Clerks advanced with the next agenda item. Someone proposed a resolution about grain storage, and it was seconded, and it passed, and the world went on deciding the price of grain while the price of souls went unrecorded.

Sable stood in the middle of it and felt forty years land on her at once.

Because they were not villains. That was the thing no one warned novices about. She had spent her life expecting wickedness and finding instead this: decent, tired, intelligent people making the most defensible available choice, each one individually reasonable, collectively fatal. The council of forty years ago had been the same. The council of eighty years ago, that declined to investigate a stolen heir's memory because the paperwork was irregular—they had been reasonable too. Reasonableness was how the world ended. Not in fire. In minutes duly approved and filed.

She thought of the shrine at Marrow Ford, where she had held a woman who had forgotten her own children and kept rocking them anyway because the body remembers what the mind loses. She thought of the archivist who defrocked her, apologetic, kind, certain. She thought of Odile in the doorway at dawn, asking nothing, offering a place at a fire.

*You let it happen,* she told the chamber silently. *All of it. Every time. You let it happen because stopping it would cost something that could be counted, and you only count.*

There was a smell of tallow and stone dust. There was the rustle of agendas. And there was, folded inside her traveling cloak where it had ridden for forty years against her heart, the robe she had sworn never to wear again and had put on this morning anyway, because some part of her had known she would need to end it properly.

Grey wool. Nine linked rings at the hem, stitched by hands two generations dead.

She took it out in full view of the council, and the nearest delegates fell quiet one by one, the way birds go quiet, and then the whole chamber, sensing theater or sacrilege, went still.

"Forty years ago," Sable said, "I stood where you sit and asked you to protect the eastern marches. You declined, reasonably. They burned. Thirty years ago I brought you the first ledgers of the memory-takers. You declined, reasonably. Those villages are still hollow. Today I have brought you the end of the world, and you have declined, reasonably, and you will survive this decision the way you survived the others—briefly." She lifted the robe over her head. Beneath it she was only a thin old woman in a road-stained shift, and she was surprised to find her hands steady. "My order believed truth was a debt owed publicly or not at all. I have been a coward about this garment for forty years. I have hidden it, apologized for it, worn it today like a costume hoping you'd see the embroidery and remember what your grandmothers knew. You did not. So I will do what I should have done at the archivists' tribunal."

From her sleeve she took the flint and steel she carried for campfires, and knelt, and laid the grey wool on the white marble floor of the Council Spire of Verance, and struck sparks into it until it caught.

The fire was small. Wool burns modestly. But the smoke rose straight and dark in the still air, and the smell of it—lanolin and old incense—went through the chamber like a bell through fog, and Sable knelt in front of forty years of political despair and watched her last belonging to the old covenant curl and blacken, and felt, underneath the fury, underneath the shame, something she had not felt since Marrow Ford: clean.

"When this is ash," she said, not loudly, "you may sweep me out with it. But hear the last words of the Order of the Nine Links, since no one else will ever speak them: *the covenant was not a chain. It was the only rope over the gorge, and you are cutting it to save the weight.*"

The flames ate the nine linked rings last. She watched each ring vanish and counted them like a rosary, like names, like the four hundred and six.

Then she rose, ash on her knees, and turned to her strange, improbable company—the thief, the liar, the foundling, the child with no yesterdays—and saw that Odile was looking at her not with pity, which she could have borne, but with recognition, which she could not.

"Well," Sable said, and her voice only shook a little. "I'm defrocked twice over now. Once by the church and once by fire. There's nothing left to take from me, so I suppose I'm finally free." She squared her shoulders toward the doors. "Come. We have three days' grace and a bazaar to rob."

Odile blinked. "How did you—"

"Child, I've watched you case every room we've entered since Bellharbor, including my chapel. You've been circling Verance's black market like a moth since we crossed the bridge." The old priestess allowed herself one dry smile, the first in days. "Besides—you'll need coin for the road east, and I suspect what you're actually hunting in that bazaar isn't coin at all. So. Shall we go and find out what your grandmother left in this city?"

Something flickered across Odile's face—there and gone, quick as a pickpocket's hand.

Behind them, as the great oak doors swung shut on the council of the free cities and its resolutions about grain, the smoke of the burnt robes still hung in the white chamber like a question no clerk would record.
