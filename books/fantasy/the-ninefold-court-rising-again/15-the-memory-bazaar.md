## 15. The Memory Bazaar

Verance wore its respectability the way a drunk wears his Sunday coat—carefully, and only where people could see.

Above ground it was all pale stone and lawful commerce: fishmongers shouting the morning's catch along the canal steps, scribes in grey robes hurrying between the council hall and the counting houses, children feeding bread to gulls that had grown fat and entitled on generations of tourists. The free cities liked to think of Verance as their spine, straight and unbending. But Odile had spent her whole life learning how cities bent, and she could feel this one bowing at the waist, down beneath the cobbles, down where the canals ran dark and the lamplighters never went.

That was where the bazaar lived.

She'd heard whispers of it for three days, since the company had slipped into Verance ahead of Veyran's advancing shadow. Whispers were Wick's currency more than hers, but even Odile had caught the shape of it: *the Undermarket, the Quiet Fair, the place where you can sell what you don't want to remember.* Every city that bordered the old fae roads had one, Sable said. Verance's was simply older than most, and better hidden, because Verance had more to hide.

"You shouldn't go alone," Caspian had said that morning, standing in the doorway of their rented rooms with his arms folded like a man trying to hold a door shut with his body.

"I shouldn't go *accompanied*," Odile corrected him, winding a drab brown scarf around her hair. "A stranger asking questions draws eyes. A woman shopping doesn't."

"And if someone recognizes what you are?"

"Then they'll recognize me as a customer." She smiled, and watched him not smile back. "Everyone down there is buying or selling something they're ashamed of, Caspian. I'll be the most honest person in the room."

He'd wanted to say more. She'd seen it gathering behind his teeth like weather. But he was still paying off the debt of the burned prophecy page, still walking carefully around her the way a man walks around a floorboard he knows will creak, and so he only said, "Sable wants to come."

"Sable wants to come everywhere." Odile settled her scarf, checked the knife at her hip out of habit rather than need—the bazaar would have its own rules about blades—and pulled on a coat that made her look like a widow of modest means. "Tell her I'll be back before the lamps are lit. If I'm not—"

"If you're not, we come after you."

"No." She met his eyes and held them until he understood. "If I'm not, you get Brannic and Wick across the border pass while it's still open. The mission doesn't die because I do."

"That's not—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "That's not how family works."

The word landed in her chest like a coin dropped down a well. She hadn't gotten used to it yet. She suspected she never would.

"We'll argue about it when I'm back," she said, and left before either of them could ruin the moment by meaning it too much.

* * *

The way down began behind a chandler's shop on Tallow Lane, through a door that smelled of tallow and old smoke and opened onto stairs that should not have fit inside the building's footprint. That was the first sign she was in covenant-country again—not true covenant, nothing so clean, but the residue of it, the way magic pooled and soured near the old fae roads. Space got soft here. Doors were suggestions. Stairs went where they pleased.

Odile counted forty-one steps, which was thirty more than the building could contain, and arrived in a low brick tunnel lit by lamps that burned without flame—a fae trick, or the corpse of one. The light was the color of weak tea. It made everyone who walked in it look like they were keeping a secret, which, she supposed, they were.

The bazaar announced itself first by sound: a murmur like a hive fed on honey, hundreds of voices pitched low, the particular hush of people conducting business that would cost them their necks above ground. Then by smell—candlewax and wet stone, yes, but underneath it something stranger, sweeter, the scent of memories themselves when they were kept fresh. Odile knew that smell intimately. Her own hoard, locked in a warded case in her room at the inn, breathed the same perfume in the dark. First snow. A wedding. A mother's lullaby. Grief, joy, boredom, love—all of it had a scent, faintly floral and faintly rotten, like flowers pressed too long in a book.

She had expected a market. What she found was a cathedral of other people's lives.

Stalls lined the tunnel walls and spilled into side chambers carved from the living rock, each one hung with vials—thousands of them, tens of thousands, glass catching the tea-colored light and throwing it back in little amber constellations. Some vials were plain as apothecary stock. Others were ornate, stoppered with silver, wrapped in wire cages, labeled in scripts Odile didn't recognize. Merchants sat among their wares like spiders in webs of glass: a fat man with rings on every finger; a woman whose eyes were different colors; a boy no older than Wick sorting vials by some system invisible to outsiders, his hands moving with the tenderness of a nurse handling infants.

And everywhere, customers. Hunched figures holding vials to their temples, closing their eyes, sampling. An old soldier paying three silver coins for something small and blue, his hands shaking until he clutched the purchase to his chest and his face went smooth as a boy's. A young wife selling—Odile watched the transaction with clinical horror—a memory of a funeral, presumably her own grief, weighed on a brass scale against a purse of coins. The merchant tasted it, nodded once, and paid her double what she'd asked.

"Genuine mourning," the merchant murmured to his assistant. "Unmixed. Rare, these days. Everyone keeps their griefs now. They've learned griefs are proof you loved somebody."

Odile moved through it all with her heart hammering and her face arranged into polite, bored interest. This was her sin laid out in a marketplace. This was what she did, dressed in stalls and sold by weight. In the Hollow Valley she'd told herself a hundred comfortable stories about it—that she took only what people wouldn't miss, that she gave comfort as often as she took it, that a dying ferryman hardly needed the memory of first snow where he was going. Standing here, watching grief sold by the dram, those stories curdled in her stomach.

*You're just like them,* whispered a voice that sounded unpleasantly like Mother Sable. *You just never had a stall.*

*I never sold,* she answered it silently. *I only kept.* And then, honestly, the way the bazaar forced honesty: *Is that better? Is hoarding better than trading? At least theirs circulate. Someone grieving gets to buy back a wedding day. My thousand lives sit in a box growing mold.*

She shook it off and got to work.

Her cover story was simple: a buyer for a private collector upriver, looking for pre-Breaking stock, willing to pay well for provenance. Provenance was the magic word in any market built on stolen things. Everyone wanted to know where a memory came from. Almost nobody wanted to answer.

The fat man with the rings went first. He showed her a tray of "court memories"—all fakes, she could tell by the flatness of the glass, the way the light moved through them like light through dyed water instead of light through lived time. Real memories had depth. They had weather in them.

"Pre-Breaking?" she asked, skeptical, letting her accent drift south toward the river towns where collectors actually lived.

"Older than old, lady. Older than the war." He leaned close, breath like pickled fish. "I've got a piece of the coronation of the last true king of Bellharbor. I've got a midwife who attended a fae birth, may the gates keep her soul. I've got—"

"You've got whatever your suppliers tell you you've got," she said, and moved on before his indignation could become attention.

It was the fourth stall, down a side chamber where the lamps burned lower and the merchandise sat under lock and chain, where she found the mark.

The stallkeeper there was an ancient woman, thin as a candle stub, wrapped in shawls despite the tunnel's stale warmth. Her wares were different. No loose trays here, no browsing. Each vial sat in its own velvet cradle, catalogued, numbered, guarded. These were the genuine articles—Odile could feel it from three paces away, the way you can feel heat from a banked fire. Century-old memories, maybe older. Pre-Breaking. The real thing.

And stamped into the lead seals of six of the oldest vials, pressed there by a stamping tool wielded by a hand that had been dust for eighty years:

A raven, standing on a key.

Odile stopped breathing.

She knew that mark the way you know your own name—not because anyone had shown it to her recently, but because it had been around her all her life without her seeing it. On the flyleaf of the storybook her grandmother had read to her. Pressed into the wax that sealed the jar of plum preserves that sat unopened on a shelf in the Hollow Valley house for as long as Odile could remember, preserved past any reason, past any season, kept the way you keep relics. Scratched into the underside of the workbench where Grandmother Eastwick had taught her to pick locks, hidden where only a child crawling under the bench would ever find it.

*A raven standing on a key.* Grandmother's trader's mark, from her years on the roads before she'd retired to the valley and turned silent as a stone. Odile had assumed—had always assumed—it meant what trader's marks meant: *goods honest, prices fair, debts honored.*

Standing in the tea-colored light, staring at six lead seals in a black-market cathedral of stolen lives, Odile felt the assumption crack down the middle.

Grandmother hadn't been a cloth trader. Grandmother hadn't been a spice trader. Grandmother Eastwick had been *here*, or somewhere like here, doing *this*—and not as a dabbler. Those seals were masterwork. That stamp had pressed thousands of vials. You didn't get a mark like that pressed into century-old lead unless you were one of the people the whole trade knew by name.

"Lady?"

The old stallkeeper was watching her. Not with suspicion—with something worse. Recognition of recognition.

"Those seals," Odile managed. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. Thieves learn early that the voice is the last thing to betray you, and she'd been a thief longer than she'd been anything else. "The raven-and-key. That's an old house. Whose?"

The woman looked at her a long moment. Her eyes, milky at the rims, were sharp as needles at the center.

"You know the mark," she said. Not a question.

"I know it," Odile admitted. There was no point lying to a woman who dealt in truth by the vial. "I'm trying to find out why."

"Then you're the first in fifty years." The stallkeeper rose, joints cracking like green firewood, and lifted down one of the six sealed vials with both hands, the reverence of a priest elevating a relic. "This mark belonged to a supplier called the Keymistress. Before my time, but my mother's mother traded with her people. They say she was the finest in nine cities. They say her goods never faded, never spoiled—she had ways of keeping, ways nobody's matched since. Ways from before the Breaking." The old woman's voice dropped. "They also say she didn't steal what she sold. She *harvested*. Whole seasons of a town's life, taken clean, taken all at once. They say that's how she got so good at keeping. Practice."

The tunnel seemed to tilt. Odile put her hand on the stall's edge to steady herself, and the wood was warm under her palm, everything in this place was warm, thousands of memories breathing heat like sleeping animals.

Whole seasons. Taken clean. Taken all at once.

A valley that magic couldn't follow. A perfect hiding place. A woman who vanished from the roads one year and was never seen again, raising a granddaughter in a hollow where no inquisitor, no fae lord, no creditor could ever reach.

*Not a refuge,* Odile thought, cold spreading through her chest. *A warehouse.*

"What happened to her?" she asked. Her mouth was dry. "The Keymistress."

The stallkeeper set the vial down and studied Odile's face properly now, the needle-sharp gaze moving over her features like fingers checking coins for clipping.

"No one knows," she said slowly. "She stopped delivering. Her routes went dark. Some said the inquisitors got her. Some said the fae. My mother's mother swore the Keymistress found something she wanted worse than the trade, and went to ground to keep it." A pause. The lamp flames—or their corpse-light—flickered. "You have her look, you know. Around the eyes."

"I get that a lot," Odile said, which was a lie, and they both knew it.

She bought nothing. She couldn't have said what she paid for passage back up the forty-one steps; her hands moved through the ritual of it, coins into palms, nods exchanged, the thief's choreography running on its own while the rest of her stood frozen in front of a raven standing on a key.

* * *

Above ground, evening had come while she was below. Lamps were being lit along the canal, exactly as she'd promised Caspian, and the gaslight made halos of itself in the damp air. Somewhere a fiddler was playing badly. Somewhere children were being called home to supper. The ordinary world, going about its ordinary business, utterly unaware that under its feet a woman had just learned her whole childhood might be inventory.

Odile stood on Tallow Lane with the crowd flowing around her and let herself feel it, all of it, because Sable had taught her that feelings dodged come back with interest.

Her grandmother had been a memory-thief. Not a hobbyist, not a survivor like Odile—an *institution*, a name whispered in markets, a master of the craft with methods from before the Breaking. Methods from when the covenant still held. Which meant Grandmother Eastwick had learned her trade when memory-magic was legal, when it was part of the great weaving between mortal and fae realms—and then the covenant broke, and the trade went underground, and Grandmother went to ground in a valley where magic couldn't follow.

Where nothing could follow. Where a stolen treasure could be kept forever.

*Found something she wanted worse than the trade, and went to ground to keep it.*

Odile thought of the valley: the quiet streets, the ferryman, the rules she'd inherited without knowing she'd inherited them. *Never steal pain. Never steal from family. Never leave.* She'd thought they were wisdom. Now she wondered if they were instructions. Containment protocols. The habits of a woman keeping something safe—or keeping something *contained*—and training her granddaughter to keep it without ever knowing what *it* was.

Because there was a house in the Hollow Valley. Grandmother's house. Sealed since her death, warded in ways Odile had always assumed were sentiment—a dead woman's curtains drawn, a dead woman's kitchen left as she left it. The family had never gone in. *Never steal from family*, and never, apparently, go through their belongings. Odile had honored it the way you honor a grave.

But graves, she understood now, could be vaults.

The lamplighter passed her on his rounds, touching flame to the next lamp, and its light fell across her face and she realized she was smiling, and the smile scared her more than anything in the tunnel below. Because it wasn't grief. It was the old thrill, the lockpick's thrill, the *there's a door and I can open it* thrill, rising in her like water finding its level.

Somewhere behind her ribs, under the fear and the grief and the vertigo, the thief in Odile Eastwick was already planning the route home.

* * *

She found the others at the inn, in the rented room that had become their war council, maps spread across the bed and Wick cross-legged on the floor restringing a crossbow. Four faces turned toward the door. Four expressions of relief poorly disguised. Caspian's relief was the least disguised of all, and she loved him a little for failing to hide it, and hated him a little too, for reasons that were getting harder to name.

"You're back before the lamps," he said.

"The lamps beat me by minutes." She closed the door. Leaned against it. Looked around at them—Brannic half-dozing in the corner, hearing doors in his dreams; Sable mending a cloak with a defrocked priestess's neat stitches; Wick looking up with those memoryless eyes that trusted her completely, which was the heaviest thing anyone had ever handed her.

"My grandmother," Odile said, "wasn't hiding in the Hollow Valley."

Sable's needle paused mid-stitch.

"She was storing something in it." Odile pushed off the door and crossed to the map table, though the route she needed to draw wasn't on any map. "There's a mark. Raven and key. It's all over the memory trade—a century old, famous, feared. And it's on the preserves jar in our kitchen." She laughed, short and bitter. "I grew up three feet from a legend's storeroom and I never once picked that door. Family rule." Her hand curled into a fist. "Well. I'm done honoring rules written by a woman who lied to me my entire childhood."

Wick tilted their head. "What's in the house?"

"That," said Odile Eastwick, heir to nothing and thief of everything, feeling the past reach up out of its grave and close its hand around her ankle, "is what we're going to find out."

Outside, far to the north, thunder rolled over mountains that separated them from the valley—and from whatever her grandmother had died protecting.
