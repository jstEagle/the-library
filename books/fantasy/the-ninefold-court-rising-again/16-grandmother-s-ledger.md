## 16. Grandmother's Ledger

The vault door had been waiting a hundred years to be disappointed.

Odile felt that the moment she laid her palm against it—a slab of blackened iron set into the cellar wall beneath the burned-out shell of Eastwick & Sons, Memory Brokers, its hinges furred with rust like orange moss. The lock was old covenant-work, the kind that answered not to keys but to names, and she had half-expected the metal to refuse her, to sit dumb and sealed until the city itself crumbled into the harbor.

But when she whispered *Eastwick*, the tumblers turned like tired joints, one after another, each with a sound like a bone being reset.

"Of course," she murmured. "It knows me."

Behind her, at the top of the cellar stairs, Wick's head appeared through the broken trapdoor, silhouetted against the gray light of Verance's merchant quarter. "Anything down there worth stealing?"

"That's what I'm trying to find out."

"Define 'worth.'"

"Wick." She didn't turn around. "Go watch the street. If anyone wearing white gloves comes near this building, you run and you don't stop running until you've found Sable."

White gloves. That was how the inquisitors dressed up their hands before they went into other people's heads—clean hands for unclean work. Odile had seen enough of them since Bellharbor to last several lifetimes, and she owned a thousand lifetimes' worth of seeing.

Wick's footsteps retreated. The trapdoor thumped shut. And Odile stood alone in the dark of her grandmother's house with a shuttered lantern, a stolen key-word, and a heartbeat that would not slow down.

She had told herself, walking here from the bazaar, that this was reconnaissance. That finding vials marked with the Eastwick trader's knot in Verance's memory market meant nothing more sinister than an old family inventory sold off by some distant cousin or seized by debt-collectors. That blood was just blood, and a trader's mark was just a mark, the way a smith's hammer or a cooper's hoop meant nothing but trade.

She hadn't believed herself. She was a thief; lying was her native language, and she knew all its dialects, including the one spoken to oneself in a quiet voice on a dark street.

* * *

The lantern came alive with a scrape of flint, and light crawled across a room that time had forgotten to finish killing.

Shelves first—ironwood shelves, still true after a century, running floor to ceiling along every wall. Upon them, rows upon rows of memory-vials in padded slots, thousands of them, glass gone cloudy with age, their contents dimmed to the color of weak tea or faded to nothing at all. Some had cracked and wept their contents long ago; those shelves wore a faint iridescent crust, like the residue of melted frost. A few still held their brightness. One, near the door, glowed a soft gold that made Odile's chest ache before she even touched it, and when she lifted it she caught an impression of sun-warmed stone, laundry snapping on a line, someone humming—

She put it down carefully. It wasn't hers. None of this was hers, and yet the whole room hummed against her skin like a struck bell, because she was made of the same substance as everything on these shelves. Stolen days. Borrowed lives. The hoard of a family that had made theft into a trade and then, apparently, into something much worse.

In the center of the room stood a desk, oak, scarred by ink-rings and knife-marks, with a high-backed chair pushed in as if the person who sat there had only stepped away to tend the kettle. On the desk: a lamp with oil long dried to varnish, a pen with its nib corroded, a small brass instrument Odile recognized from Sable's lectures as a memory-gauge—the kind brokers used to weigh a vial's depth before pricing it—and, square and heavy and bound in green leather gone black at the corners,

a ledger.

Her grandmother's name was stamped into the cover in gilt that had dulled to the color of old honey: **MAREN EASTWICK**.

Odile stared at it for a long moment. Maren. The name landed strangely—not like a stranger's, not quite like family either. Like a word in a language she'd studied but never spoken. Her mother had said it perhaps three times in Odile's whole childhood, always in the same lowered tone reserved for storms and debts: *your grandmother Maren*. Never *grandmother* plainly. Always with the weight attached.

Maren Eastwick, who had walked out of the Hollow Valley ninety-nine years ago and never come back.

Odile opened the ledger.

* * *

The early pages were almost boring. That was the horror of it, later—how ordinary it began.

Trade entries, neat as psalms. *Sold: summer of a childhood, 4 years' depth, to House Aldery, 12 crowns.* *Bought: grief of widowhood, partial, damaged, no resale.* *Traded: first kiss for two hours of a soldier's courage; favorable margin.* Maren Eastwick had written her accounts in a small, precise, upright hand, each entry ruled and numbered, the margins annotated in a shorthand Odile recognized with a jolt because she used it herself—had invented it herself, she'd thought, at eleven, scratching it into the dirt of the valley. Her own secret cipher, waiting in a dead woman's ledger a century before she was born.

She read standing up. Then her legs decided otherwise, and she read sitting in her grandmother's chair, which received her weight with a creak like a sigh of recognition.

The entries changed slowly. Page by page, year by year, the trades grew larger. Deeper. *Depth* was the broker's word for how far into a life a memory reached—one season was shallow, a decade was deep, a whole self was the thing brokers whispered about and never wrote down. Maren's ledgers started at seasons. By the fortieth page they were buying decades. And the buyers' names changed too—fewer merchants, fewer houses, and appearing again and again, in entries where the price column simply read *negotiated*: a name Odile knew from Sable's furious history lessons.

Veyran.

Not the king yet. Then, he must have been only a ambitious lord with cold eyes and a hunger the ledger recorded without comment, the way a butcher's book records pounds and cuts.

*Sold to Lord Veyran: the last twenty years of a river-priest, complete. He wept when it left him. Paid triple.*

Odile's mouth had gone dry somewhere around page thirty. Now she turned pages with fingertips that felt like someone else's.

And then, near the middle of the book, the handwriting changed.

Not much. The same upright precision, but pressed harder, the pen digging, letters slightly taller, as if the writer had begun writing standing up, or writing afraid. The date at the top of the page was one hundred years old almost to the month. The entry was longer than any before it, and it was not a sale.

*They asked me for something no one has ever asked me for,* Maren Eastwick had written. *The Court itself. Nine lords in their nine seats sent a herald to my shop with a request wrapped in courtesy, and underneath the courtesy a threat, and underneath the threat a desperation they could not hide.*

*The Court keeps its memories in trust. That is what the covenant means, whatever the priests say—that what the fae are, the mortals hold, and what the mortals are, the fae remember. Nine thrones, nine oaths. But a memory kept in trust can be moved, if your hands are good enough.*

*They want me to steal for them. From themselves.*

Odile stopped. Read it again. The lantern hissed. Somewhere overhead, muffled through floors of rotting timber, Verance went about its afternoon—cart wheels, a fishwife's cry, the far bell of the tide-clock—and none of it, none of it, seemed to belong to the same world as this cellar.

*There is an heir,* the entry continued. *A child born at the turning of the age, mortal-born and fae-sworn both, the hinge the covenant hangs on. The Court has guarded the knowledge of the heir for nine generations, passing it from throne to throne, because while the heir lives unknown, the usurper's kind cannot kill what they cannot find. The memory of the heir IS the protection. A secret kept in nine minds at once.*

*And now they want it out of nine minds and into one vial, so it cannot be tortured out of any of them. So no lord, broken on a rack or drowned in a nightmare, can give up what would end the covenant forever.*

*They say I am the only broker alive with the depth of hand to move a memory that large without tearing it. They say it will rest in the vault below my shop, warded, until the danger passes.*

*I have told them yes.*

Odile looked up from the page at the shelves, at the thousand dusty vials, and understood with a slow, sick lurch that she was sitting inside the sentence she had just read. This was the vault. These were the wards, dead now, their ironwork green with century-old decay. Somewhere in this room—or once in this room—had rested the single most protected secret in the Nine Realms.

She turned the page.

*It is done.*

Two words, and then the handwriting broke. Not the pressure this time—the *shape*. The letters leaned, slurred, ran downhill toward the margin like water finding a crack. Whatever Maren Eastwick had done in the vault below her shop, she had done it to herself as much as to the memory.

*I did not tear it. I want that written somewhere, even if no one ever reads this. I did not tear it. It sits whole in its glass, and it is the most beautiful thing I have ever held. A whole soul in a vial. Do you know what that is, reader? Whoever you are? It is a life with no edges. It is everything anyone has ever been, poured clean.*

*I held it for one hour before I warded it away. In that hour I understood something the Court does not understand, something no priest has ever preached: whoever holds the heir's memory holds the covenant. Not the Court. Not the nine. Whoever holds it. They gave me their own throat and called it safety.*

*I could have returned it. I am writing this instead.*

*Here is what I told myself, and may the Reader of All Ledgers judge whether it was lie or truth: the Court is proud and the lords are cruel in their beauty, and the covenant binds the fae to us as much as us to them, and maybe—maybe—it should be mortal hands that hold the balance. Maybe the age of kneeling to beautiful things is over. Maybe I am not a thief tonight. Maybe I am the first free woman in the Nine Realms.*

*Or maybe I am exactly what they always said brokers were. Maybe I looked at a soul in glass and could not put it down.*

*The vial is hidden where no inquisitor, no lord, no herald will think to search, because they will be searching for treasure, and I have buried it as trash. Let the record show: the memory of the true heir of the Ninefold Court rests in a vessel marked with the lowest grade in my inventory. Spoiled goods. Damaged stock. No resale.*

*If you are reading this, then either the Court has fallen and it no longer matters, or you have my blood, in which case it matters more than anything, and I am sorry. I am sorry the way a stone is sorry. I did it anyway.*

*One thing more. The heir. I saw them—I saw the shape of the life in the glass, the way a broker sees, from the outside, the whole weather of a person. Born in the valley country. Marked by the turning age. And the Court's herald said a thing I have carried every day since, the way a splinter carries poison:*

*The heir will come back for it. The covenant is patient. Blood remembers what minds forget.*

*So if you have my blood—if you are reading this with my hands—you should know that the Court will call you home someday, and it will not ask nicely, and it will not care what you were planning to be instead.*

*I hid a map. Under the gauge. Forgive me. Or don't. Keep the ledger anyway. Someone in this family ought to keep honest books.*

*M.E.*

* * *

For a while Odile did not move.

The lantern had burned low; the light lay amber and trembling across the open page, and her shadow sat huge on the shelf-wall behind her, hunched like a gargoyle among the dead vials. Her breath came out visible in the cellar's cold, little ghosts dissolving one after another, and she watched them absently, because looking at anything else required a steadiness she did not currently possess.

*Under the gauge.*

The brass memory-gauge still sat on the desk where a hundred years had left it. She picked it up with hands that obeyed her only because she ordered them to, one motion at a time, and turned it over. The base plate was screwed fast—four small screws, green with age. She took her knife and worked them loose, one, two, three, four, each squeaking loose like a small animal protesting, and pried the plate away.

Folded flat between the plates of the base, thin as a wafer, was a sheet of paper gone soft and brown as autumn.

She unfolded it on the desk with the same care she'd use for a wounded bird.

A map. Hand-drawn, in that same hard-pressed hand: the coast of the Hollow Valley country, the valley itself rendered in loving detail—she knew every line of it, the ferry crossing, the terraced fields, the shrine-less hills where magic couldn't follow. And there, at the valley's heart, a single X drawn over a place Odile knew better than anywhere on earth.

Her own house. The cottage where she had been born. The garden her mother had planted. The floorboards under which, presumably, wrapped in rags and marked as spoiled goods, sat the memory of the true heir of the Ninefold Court.

*Blood remembers what minds forget.*

"Oh," Odile said aloud, to the empty vault, to the dead woman's chair, to nobody. "Oh, no."

Because the pieces were assembling themselves now, refusing her permission, clicking together with the horrible smoothness of a lock built by a master. Her grandmother had walked out of the valley and vanished. Her mother had raised her inside the valley, inside the one place magic could not reach, inside the one place no fae herald, no prophecy, no covenant-pull could ever touch her—raised her behind walls Maren Eastwick had built out of a stolen soul. The valley wasn't hiding her from the world. The valley was hiding her from the *Court*.

And Caspian—Caspian, who had arrived impossible and uninvited, who had known her name, who had carried a book a century old with tonight's date written in it—had found her the moment she'd crossed her own threshold of years. The turning of the age. The hinge. *Born at the turning of the age.*

She was the same age, almost to the month, as the date on the ledger's breaking page.

She stood up so fast the chair went over backward, clattering, and she didn't pick it up. She stood in the amber dark among her grandmother's hoard with the map shaking in her fingers and felt the truth settle over her the way the valley's silence used to settle over everything: total, inescapable, absolute.

Her bloodline hadn't just survived the breaking of the world.

Her bloodline had *broken* it. Her grandmother's hands had pulled the keystone from the arch, and every burned memory-keeper in Bellharbor, every wraith in Rotwood, every toll of terror paid at Salt Bridge, every empty throne in a ruined court—all of it grew from this cellar, from this ledger, from a woman who had held a soul in glass for one hour and could not put it down.

And the Court's patience had ended. The herald had already come. He had brown hair that fell in his eyes and a smile like a door opening, and he had lied to her beautifully, and she had let him, because being seen had felt like sunlight after a lifetime of shadow.

Somewhere above, faint through the floors, Wick's whistle sounded—two notes, sharp, the signal. Someone in white gloves on the street.

Odile folded the map into her coat, closed the ledger, took both, and climbed toward the light with her grandmother's confession heavy as a second spine between her shoulders—and with one thought repeating in time with her footsteps, all the way up:

*The heir is me. The heir is me. The heir is me.*
