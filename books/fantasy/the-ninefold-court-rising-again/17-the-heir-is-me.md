## 17. The Heir Is Me

Caspian had lied to kings and lied to priests, had lied with a straight face to a fae lord's herald while the man's silver eyes weighed him like coin, and he had never once felt the particular sickness that came with lying to Odile Eastwick.

Until now.

He sat in the loft above Verance's tannery district with her grandmother's ledger open on his knees, and the lamplight made the old ink look wet, as though the words were still bleeding out of the page. He had not meant to read it. That was what he would tell himself later, in the long hours when lying to himself was all that was left. He had meant only to make sure the ledger was safe—Mother Sable had entrusted it to him while she went down to argue with the city's harbor-wardens—and safe things did not need reading.

But the ledger had fallen open at a page near the end, and his eyes had gone to it the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth.

*Item the last,* said the faded hand, in a trader's script so precise it might have been typeset. *Taken from the Court of Ninefold Oath, from the vault beneath the ninth throne: the memory of the true heir. Description as follows.*

And then, in ink a century old:

*A girl-child of the Ravensworth line. Dark-haired. Left eye paler than the right, the color of river ice. Born under the waning of the Harvest Moon. Marked at birth by the Court itself—a ring of frost-scar about the left wrist, given when the nine lords laid their blessing on her.*

Caspian looked at his own hands for a long time.

Then he rolled back the left sleeve of Odile's spare shirt—he kept one of hers folded in his pack, and he was not going to examine why—and pressed two fingers to the inside of his own wrist, where there was no mark at all, because the mark had never been his to wear.

The frost-scar. He had seen it a hundred times. A pale ring of skin on Odile's left wrist that she called an old burn, that she never explained, that Wick had once asked about and been told, with a thief's easy smile, *a story for another day.* She had a thousand stories for other days. It was how she kept people at arm's length—one story held just out of reach, forever promised, never delivered.

It was not a burn. It was a blessing. Nine fae lords had pressed their thumbs to a newborn's wrist a hundred years ago—not a hundred years, no; twenty-three years ago, but the Court's time ran strange, and the ledger's arithmetic bent like light through water—and they had marked her as theirs before she could even cry properly.

And he had known.

That was the thing. That was the rot at the center of it, the thing he could not carve out no matter how he turned the knife. He had known since Bellharbor. Since the first night, maybe. Since the ferry crossing, when she'd pushed her sleeves up to row against the current and the lamplight had caught the scar, and something in the oldest part of him—the part that had been raised on heralds' songs and court protocol, the part that knew every royal bloodline the way other children knew their prayers—had sat up and *recognized.*

He had looked at that pale ring on her wrist and thought: *there you are.*

And then he had said nothing, and pulled the oars, and let her believe she'd been chosen for herself.

* * *

The prophecy book lay in the bottom of his pack beneath a change of clothes and a coil of rope, wrapped in oilcloth the way one wraps a corpse. He unwrapped it now, though he had sworn he wouldn't touch it again until Verance was behind them. The leather cover was warm. It was always warm, faintly, like a sleeping animal, and he had learned not to think too hard about what fed it.

He turned to the page he had burned a copy of—the page whose original fragment he had fed to a candle flame in front of Odile's disbelieving eyes, the lie he had built out of a truth. The book had forgiven him for it. Books of prophecy always forgave their readers; it was the readers who could not forgive themselves.

The relevant passage sat in the middle third of the page, in the crabbed hand of some dead scribe who had written it down without understanding what he copied:

*When the ninth moon wanes over the valley that magic cannot find, the Covenant-Bearer comes down from hiding. Thief-born, heir-blooded. Her wrist bears the frost-ring of the Nine. She shall gather the scattered, walk the rotted road, and stand before the empty thrones, and what was stolen by her grandmother's hand she shall return with her own.*

*Thief-born, heir-blooded.*

He had read that line a hundred times on the road from the valley, and each time he had performed the same small sleight of mind. *Thief-born*, yes—that was Odile, obviously, that was the whole architecture of her. And *heir-blooded*—well, prophecy spoke in riddles, didn't it? Bloodlines were tangled things after a century. Half the free cities probably had some diluted drop of Court inheritance in them. It didn't have to mean what it plainly said. He had stood in the doorway of his own denial like Brannic standing at a door, listening to everything behind it, refusing to open it.

But the door had opened anyway, last night, in her grandmother's vault, when Odile had come up out of the dark holding that ledger with her face gray as ash and said, in a voice he would hear until he died: *Caspian. My grandmother stole it. She stole the memory of the heir, and the heir was—I think the heir was me.*

She had figured it out herself. In half a night, alone in a dead woman's vault, she had done what he had spent weeks refusing to do.

And she had looked at him—at him specifically, not at Sable, not at the others—with a question in her eyes that he had answered with a carefully calibrated half-truth: *I suspected the heir might be connected to your family. I didn't know it was you.*

A half-truth. Which is to say, a whole lie wearing half a truth as a mask. He had known. He had known in Bellharbor when he watched her haggle with a fishwife and thought, absurdly, *the lords of the Ninefold Court chose this woman, and she is spending her divine destiny arguing over the price of eels.* He had known in Rotwood when the memory-wraith screamed her name in a voice that no mortal throat should have known, and he had understood in one cold flash that the wraith recognized her—that everything old and fae in this world would recognize her, because she had been marked by them before her first breath.

He had known, and he had kissed her knuckles at the Salt Bridge toll and said *it'll be all right* as if he were merely a man comforting a woman, instead of an agent of the Court steering its stolen heir home to the altar.

* * *

Below him, through the floorboards, he heard the sounds of the household: Sable's low voice arguing doctrine with a harbor-warden who had come to the wrong door; Wick's laugh, sharp and bright as struck flint; Brannic humming tunelessly while he mended straps. His found family, or Odile's found family that he had been permitted to travel alongside. And somewhere in that noise, Odile's footsteps, coming up the outside stair.

He had perhaps ten seconds. He spent six of them deciding to tell the truth and four of them failing.

"Caspian." She came through the door the way she came through everything—sideways, quiet, already cataloguing the exits. Her eyes went to the book in his lap and narrowed. "You're reading it again. You swore you'd stop."

"I was making sure it was safe."

"Nothing wrapped in oilcloth needs checking twice a day." She crossed the room, dropped onto the bench across from him, and pushed her hair out of her face. There were shadows under her eyes. She hadn't slept since the vault; none of them had slept well since the vault, but hers was a different species of sleeplessness. He knew the difference now. He had learned to read her the way she read locks—by feel, in the dark, by the small resistances. "Sable says the council won't move. Says Veyran's army will reach the Court before the first snow."

"The council is afraid."

"The council is *smart*. Fear usually is." She leaned forward, elbows on knees, and studied him, and he felt the study like a searchlight. "You've been strange since the vault. Strang*er.* Even Wick noticed, and Wick thinks feelings are weather."

"I'm fine."

"You're doing the thing," she said. "Where you answer a question I didn't ask so you don't have to answer the one I did."

Gods, but he loved her. That was the unforgivable part, wasn't it? If he had merely used her—if he had been a cold instrument of the Court, a herald in a handsome coat—it would have been simple treachery, clean as a cut purse. But he loved her, loved the way she talked to horses and threatened to steal memories of anyone who snored, loved that she had taken in a memoryless child and a defrocked priestess and a fae foundling because she couldn't stand to see anyone go unclaimed, loved her most of all in the moments when she was hardest to love. And love did not launder the lie. Love just meant he had betrayed her *affectionately*, which was somehow worse.

"Caspian." Her voice changed. Quieter. The thief's voice, the interrogator's, the one that got people talking before they knew they'd started. "What aren't you telling me?"

There it was. The open lock, offered freely, and his hands shaking too badly to pick it honestly.

"When I recruited you," he said, "in the valley. When I asked you to dinner and showed you the book. What did you think I saw?"

She blinked. "I don't—what do you mean?"

"That night. The first night. I knew your name before you gave it to me. I knew about the valley. You asked me once how an outsider found a place magic can't follow, and I gave you an answer about maps and stubbornness." He made himself keep his eyes on hers. She deserved that much, at least—his eyes, when the rest of him failed her. "That wasn't the answer."

"No," she said slowly. "I don't imagine it was."

"I found you because I was looking for you. Not for *a* Covenant-Bearer. For you." The words came out of him like teeth being drawn, one at a time, each with its own little root of blood. "The Court sent me into the world with a description. A hundred years old, written down by heralds who remembered more than anyone realized. A girl of the Ravensworth line. Dark-haired. One eye paler than the other. Born under the waning Harvest Moon."

Her breath stopped. He watched it stop.

"And a frost-scar," he said, "on the left wrist."

Her hand moved before her mind did—he saw it happen, saw the body outrun the thought—her right hand closing around her left wrist, covering the pale ring the way a child covers a bruise. "That's a burn," she said. "From when I was—"

"It isn't a burn, Odile. It has never been a burn. You know it isn't. You've always known. It doesn't hurt, it doesn't fade, it's perfectly smooth after twenty-three years, and you have shown it to exactly no one." He gentled his voice, which felt like adding insult. "Nine fae lords touched your wrist the day you were born. They blessed you. They claimed you. And then my grandmother's generation—no. Your grandmother. Your grandmother stole their memory of ever having done it, and the memory of who you were, and the Court forgot its own heir, and the covenant broke, and the world began to rot. All of it. The borders. The armies. Veyran. Everything. It all starts with a theft, and the theft starts—" he gestured helplessly at her, at all of her— "*here.*"

The silence in the loft was total. Below them, Wick laughed at something again, and the sound fell into the silence like a coin into a well, falling and falling without landing.

"You knew," Odile said at last. Her voice was very flat, which he had learned was worse than loud. "In the valley. When you showed me the prophecy. When you said the Court sent me, when you said I was *chosen*—"

"You were chosen."

"Don't." She was on her feet. He hadn't seen her rise; thieves moved like that when they were frightened. "Don't you dare dress it up. Chosen for *what*, Caspian? Say it. Say the whole sentence."

He said it. There was nothing left in him but obedience. "Chosen for your blood. Chosen because you were the heir. Because the prophecy needed the heir, and the heir needed to be found, and I was the one sent to find her." He swallowed. "You weren't recruited because of who you are, Odile. You were recruited because of what you are. I hid it because—"

"Because I'd have said no."

"Yes."

"And you needed the heir more than you needed me to say yes." She laughed, one short, awful note. "Do you know what I thought, that first night? Do you know what it was like? I have spent my whole life invisible. A thousand stolen lives and not one of them mine. And this impossible beautiful stranger walks into a valley that cannot be found and knows my *name*, and asks me to dinner like I'm a person, and tells me there's a book that's been waiting for me since before I was born, and I thought—" her voice cracked, and she hated it, he could see her hating it— "I thought, *finally. Finally someone sees me.* And it turns out you weren't seeing me at all. You were seeing a description. You were checking me off a list. Wrist, hair, eyes, moon. Like appraising stock."

"Odile—"

"How long?" She had backed toward the window, arms crossed tight over the wrist she was still hiding. "How long have you known it was me? Truly. Not suspected. Known."

He could lie again. He was good at it. It would buy him an hour, a day, and cost him everything that remained.

"Since the ferry," he said. "Since I saw the scar in the lamplight. Before Bellharbor. Before any of it. I have known almost from the moment I met you."

Something went out of her face. Not anger—anger would have been bearable. It was the look of someone finding the false bottom in a chest they'd already emptied their heart into. "The ferry," she repeated. "So every mile since the ferry. Every campfire. Every time you—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening. "Was any of it real? Any of it, Caspian? Or was I a mission the entire way?"

And here was the cruelest question of all, because the honest answer was the one that would wound her deepest: *all of it was real, and that changes nothing, and I knew that it would change nothing, and I let myself love you precisely because I believed it would make the betrayal survivable.* Love as anesthetic. Tenderness as a tool of state.

"Some of it was duty," he said quietly. "All of it was real. I know those don't fit together. I've been trying to make them fit together since Bellharbor, and they don't, and I should have told you a hundred times, and every time I found a reason—your trust, the mission, the others, the war—and every reason was just fear wearing a uniform."

She looked at him for a long moment. Outside the window, the harbor bells of Verance rang the evening watch, and gulls wheeled screaming over the refugee rooftops, and the world went on being a world in which none of this had happened yet.

"My grandmother broke the world," she said, "and you broke me. Funny. Both thefts." She pushed off from the windowsill. "I need—I can't be in this room with you right now."

"Odile, wait. Please. Just—let me—"

"Let you what?" She turned in the doorway, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "Apologize? You're sorry. Fine. I believe you're sorry. But here's what you don't understand, Caspian, and maybe you never will: Sable lied to me too, and it hurt. Wick lies about small things and it stings. But you—" her hand rose, trembling slightly, and pointed at him, and the finger might as well have been a knife— "you took the one thing I had. The one thing. Everyone in my life wanted something *from* me—my valley, my gift, my silence—but you were supposed to want *me*. I built that. I chose to believe it. I let myself be seen. And the whole time you were reading me off a shopping list written by dead fae."

She was gone down the stairs before he could answer, and her steps made no sound at all, which was how he knew how badly he had hurt her: Odile Eastwick only walked silently when she was hunting, or fleeing, or both.

Caspian sat alone with the prophecy book warming in his lap like a living thing, like a heart that was not his own, and understood that he had finally committed the one crime his whole training had never covered: he had stolen a person's belief in herself, and there was no vault in the world he could break into to give it back.

On the page before him, indifferent as ever, the old ink waited beneath his thumb. He looked down at the next line—the line he had never dared read aloud, the one that had driven him to burn pages and swallow truths and steer a woman he loved toward a door marked with her own death—

*And the Covenant-Bearer shall give back what was stolen, and the giving shall empty her, and the Nine shall rise upon an heir who remembers nothing.*

He closed the book.

Downstairs, a door slammed. Then another. Then Wick's voice, high and worried, calling up through the floorboards:

"Caspian! CASPIAN! She's gone—her pack's gone from the hook—Caspian, *where would she go?*"
