## 18. Walking Away

She left in the hour before dawn, when even the birds had not yet decided to sing.

Odile packed the way she'd learned to pack as a girl in the valley: quickly, silently, leaving no story behind her. A change of clothes rolled tight. The flint and steel. The small clay pot of rendered fat that Mother Sable swore by for chapped hands and cold mornings. She did not take the prophecy book—Caspian could keep his book, his lies, his careful half-truths stacked like coins in a merchant's till. She did not take anything he had given her, which meant she took almost nothing at all, because he had given her most of what she carried these days: the map folded into her pocket, the habit of checking the horizon twice, the word *heir* sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone.

The camp was a scatter of sleeping shapes around a banked fire. Brannic lay on his back with his mouth open, one long fae-blooded hand curled near his ear as if he might catch a door whispering even in dreams. Wick was a knot of limbs in their blanket, close to the coals the way only someone who had slept cold for years could be. Mother Sable sat propped against a stone, awake as always before the others, her rosary of river-glass moving through her fingers click, click, click.

Sable looked up. Odile held one finger to her lips.

The old priestess studied her for a long moment—the packed bag, the boots already laced, the face that was trying very hard to be nothing at all. Sable did not raise an alarm. She did not stand. She only reached out and pressed two fingers, briefly, to Odile's wrist, where the pulse beat.

"Whatever you're walking toward," she murmured, "it will still be there when you arrive. And so will we."

"That's what I'm afraid of," Odile said, and then hated herself for saying it, because it was too honest, and honesty was a currency she'd never learned to spend well.

She walked out of the camp and did not look back. Looking back was how you got caught. Looking back was how you ended up staying.

* * *

The road east ran down through hills gone gold and rust with autumn, toward a coast she had only seen in other people's memories. That was the joke of her life, if she let herself laugh at it: Odile Eastwick, who owned a thousand sunsets, had never watched one with her own eyes over open water. She owned a fisherman's memory of the sea at Bellharbor, all grey chop and gull-scream. She owned a bride's memory of standing on white cliffs while petals stuck to her lip-glossed mouth. But those were borrowed rooms in a house that wasn't hers. She had lived her whole life in other people's houses.

*Heir.* Caspian's voice again, doing its patient, reasonable work. *You are the heir.*

No. She was a thief from a valley where magic couldn't follow, and everything since she'd crossed that border had been proof that the world outside wanted something from her. Veyran wanted her blood on a ledger. The Court wanted its memory back. Caspian wanted—he had wanted—she didn't know anymore, and that not-knowing was the worst part, worse than betrayal itself. At least a knife told you where it stood.

The company had been her first attempt at something else. Seven days ago she would have said the words without flinching: her people. Her ragged, impossible, found family. Brannic who heard doors. Wick who had nothing and gave everything anyway. Sable who burned her own robes rather than lie to a room full of frightened councilmen. And Caspian—

Caspian, who had known. Who had known about the heir, known about the death clause, known about her grandmother, and fed it to her in doses, like medicine, like poison, deciding for her what she could bear.

Her grandmother had stolen a memory and broken the world. Caspian had stolen the truth and broken something smaller, but it had been hers, the truth about her own life, and he had taken it just as cleanly.

So she walked. Through morning fog that smelled of wet leaves and distant peat-smoke. Through a village where a woman was nailing up a notice board and Odile kept her hood up and her eyes down because the notice bore the seal of the usurper and the word *COVENANT-BEARER* in letters tall enough to read from horseback. Through afternoon rain that came slantwise off the sea and turned the road to soup. She walked until her legs ached in the old familiar way, the way they used to ache back when she was only a thief and not a prophecy, and there was a bleak comfort in that. Pain was honest. Pain belonged to you.

By dusk the land flattened and she could smell it—salt, kelp, tar, the vast cold breathing of water. The coast. She crested a dune gone grey with marram grass and there it was at last with her own eyes, not a fisherman's, not a bride's: the sea, enormous and indifferent, running silver under the last light.

She stood there a long time. Waiting to feel free.

She felt instead like a woman standing at the edge of a very large empty room.

* * *

There was a fishing village huddled below the dunes, maybe thirty houses, nets strung between poles, boats dragged up above the tideline like exhausted animals. She told herself she would buy bread, sleep in the loft of some inn or other, and be gone by morning along the coast road, south, away, anywhere that wasn't on a page of Caspian's book.

She bought the bread. The baker's wife had flour on her forearms and a baby on her hip and charged Odile double, which was fair; strangers were trouble these days, and trouble should pay.

It was in the village square, waiting for a room, that she saw them.

A young woman sat on a bench by the well, rocking a bundle that wouldn't stop crying—a thin, red-faced infant with a voice far too large for its body. The mother looked hollowed out, the particular exhaustion of someone who hadn't slept a whole night in months. Her husband, presumably, was one of the men mending nets with the grim economy of people whose boats hadn't gone out in weeks because of the war news. The baby screamed. The mother rocked and shushed and rocked, and her eyes had gone somewhere far away, to a place where babies didn't cry and husbands weren't afraid and kings weren't mad.

Odile knew that faraway look. She had worn it her whole life.

*I could,* thought the old part of her, the valley part, the part that had kept her alive and alone and fed. *Just a taste. Just to get through the night. It's what the gift is *for*.*

Her rules rose up like old friends: *never steal pain, never steal from family, never leave the valley.* She had already broken the third. The second had been broken for her, by blood she never asked for. And this wasn't pain—that was the seduction of it, the lie she told herself every time. This was surplus. This was a lullaby the mother had sung ten thousand times, sung so deep it had become part of the woman's marrow. Take one singing of it and she'd never miss it. It was like taking a cup of water from a river.

That was what she'd always told herself. Standing there in the salt wind with the baby screaming and the mother's eyes a thousand miles gone, Odile heard her own excuses in a new voice—heard them the way Wick's confession had taught her to hear them, the way Sable's ledger had taught her, the way the whole cursed journey had been quietly re-teaching her everything she knew about herself.

Wick had no memories before age six. Someone, somewhere, had told themselves the same comfortable arithmetic.

But she was so tired. And she was so cold. And she had spent seven days being an heir, a quarry, a Covenant-Bearer, a name on an edict—and underneath all of it she was just a woman who had never once, in her entire remembered life, been sung to sleep.

She crossed the square. Sat down on the bench beside the mother. The woman glanced at her warily—strangers again, trouble again—but Odile had spent a lifetime being unremarkable, and she put on unremarkableness like a cloak.

"May I?" she asked, and held out her hands for the baby.

The mother hesitated, then surrendered the bundle with the gratitude of the truly desperate. The baby screamed directly into Odile's collarbone. She rocked it, badly. The mother laughed—a short, cracked laugh—and showed her the trick of it, hip angled, heel bouncing, and for a moment the two of them were just two women on a bench negotiating the ancient treaty between adults and infants.

And beneath the cover of the rocking, beneath the small human closeness of it, Odile reached out with the gift and touched the place where memories live.

She took one singing of the lullaby.

Just one. Just the melody and the low hum of it, the warmth of a kitchen, the feeling—it hit her like a wave, she hadn't braced for the *feeling* wrapped around it—the feeling of a small weight on your chest, of breath evening out under your palm, of being needed in the simplest possible way, of *this is my child and I would burn down the world for her*. The mother wouldn't miss it. It was one thread from a tapestry with ten thousand threads. It was a cup of water from a river.

Odile sat there on the cold bench with someone else's baby in her arms and someone else's love humming in her skull, and she began, very quietly, to sing.

The baby stilled. Its furious red face smoothed. Its eyes drifted shut under lashes still damp from crying, and its small fist closed around a strand of Odile's hair, and the lullaby poured through her, borrowed, warm, *held*, and Odile Eastwick—who owned a thousand first snows, a thousand weddings, a thousand dawns—sat in a strange village holding a stranger's child, crying silently into someone else's tenderness.

The mother watched her with softening eyes. "You've a good voice," she said. "Your mam teach you?"

"No," Odile said, and her voice broke on the word, and the mother, kind and tired, pretended not to notice.

* * *

The inn's loft was clean and smelled of lavender laid against moths. Odile lay on the narrow bed with her boots off and the stolen lullaby folded carefully in her mind like a letter she couldn't bring herself to reread and couldn't stop rereading.

This was the taste of it. This was rock bottom, and rock bottom tasted like someone else's kitchen.

She had thought the hunger was the curse. All those years in the valley, she'd believed the problem was wanting—that if she could just stop taking, stop filling the hollow, she'd be whole. Now, lying in the dark with a mother's love warm in her chest and her own heart colder than the sea outside, she understood it differently. The theft wasn't the disease. The theft was the symptom. The disease was the belief that belonging was something you could acquire. That if you just held enough of other people's lives, one of them might fit.

They never fit. That was the whole secret of the valley. You could own a thousand lives and live none of them, and the lullaby proved it: she had the melody note-perfect, the hum, the kitchen-warmth, the fierce animal devotion—and it passed through her and left nothing planted. In the mother's head it had been a living thing, rooted, growing new verses. In Odile's head it was a pressed flower. Beautiful. Dead. Wrong size for her hands.

And yet.

She pressed her palm flat against her sternum, where the warmth still lingered like a coal under ash.

And yet it had been *something*. For three minutes on a bench in the wind, she had been a woman singing to a child, and the child had slept, and the mother had smiled, and for three minutes the hollow had been quiet. Was that worth nothing? Was it worth exactly what it cost—a cup of water from a river?

She thought of Wick's face in Rotwood, asking her to promise. *Never take mine. Promise me, Odile.* She had promised. She had sworn it on the only thing she had, which was her word, and now here she lay having broken it in spirit within the week—not Wick's memories, never Wick's, but the principle, the line, the wall she'd built around herself after that night. Walls were for keeping things out. She'd forgotten they also keep you in.

She thought of Brannic giving up his door-hearing at the ninth gate without hesitation, because family was what you *gave*, apparently, not what you took. She thought of Sable burning her robes. She thought of Caspian's face when she'd walked away from him in Verance—not guilty, exactly. Wounded. As if she'd done to him precisely what he'd done to her, and he'd only just discovered how it felt.

Good, she'd thought then. Now, alone, the thought curdled. Because she missed him. Because the traitor had taught her to check the horizon twice and she caught herself doing it in this loft, listening for his footsteps on the stair, absurd, hopeless, angry at herself for the missing more than for the lying.

And she thought of the coast road going south, and the edicts nailed to posts, and the word *Covenant-Bearer*, and somewhere beyond the horizon an army marching on a court of empty thrones, and a usurper wearing nine dead lords' memories like a crown, and Wick's bandaged shoulder, and Sable's deciphered line—*she is simply no longer herself*—and all of it waiting, patient as tide, whether she walked toward it or away.

The fire in the grate settled. Downstairs, faintly, the innkeeper's actual daughter was being sung to sleep by the innkeeper's actual wife, and the sound drifted up through the floorboards, real and unstealable, and it hurt worse than any stolen thing ever had, because it wasn't hers and it never would be and no amount of reaching could make it so.

Family isn't what you're born to. Family isn't what you remember. Family is who shows up.

Wick had said it at the door of her hiding place—or would say it; the memory of that conversation was still ahead of her like a country she hadn't crossed—and Odile lay in the lavender-dark turning the sentence over like a coin she suspected was counterfeit but hoped wasn't.

Who shows up. Not who sings to you. Who *shows up*.

She had spent her whole life showing up for other people's moments and slipping away before anyone could ask her to stay. Steal the first snow, leave before spring. Steal the wedding day, skip the marriage. It was the thief's covenant with the world: take, don't touch; visit, don't live. And she had walked out of camp this morning and called it freedom, and it was the same old covenant, signed again in the same old ink.

The baby's fist closing around her hair. The mother's cracked laugh. *You've a good voice.*

Nobody had ever said that to her. Nobody had ever heard her sing, because she had never once sung where anyone could listen.

Odile stared at the ceiling beams until the black went soft and grey at the edges, and she made herself look honestly at the choice, the way Sable looked at scripture: without flinching. One road ran south along the coast into a life with no names on it, no ledger entries, no prophecies—safe, invisible, hollow, the valley stretched thin across the whole wide world. The other road ran back. Back toward the people she'd abandoned at dawn, toward the man who lied to her, toward a throne and a sacrifice and a version of herself she couldn't imagine surviving. Toward a family that had, every single one of them, shown up.

She didn't decide that night. Decisions were for morning people. She slept—badly, in snatches, the lullaby looping in her skull like a tide.

* * *

She woke to knocking.

Not on her door. On the inn's front door, downstairs, urgent, doubled-fisted, and then a voice she knew better than any lullaby, hoarse from roads and rain and worry:

"Is there a girl staying here? Dark hair, carries a knife in her left boot, lies beautifully? Please. I just need to know she's alive."

Wick's voice.

Odile sat bolt upright in the loft, heart slamming, the stolen lullaby still warm and useless in her chest, and listened to the innkeeper grumble, and listened to Wick refuse to grumble back, and understood with terrible clarity that the decision had been made for her after all—not by prophecy, not by fate, not by a century-old book, but by a stubborn, memoryless teenager who had walked away from a wounded shoulder and a war to come find her, because that was what family did.

Family is who shows up.

Footsteps on the stairs. Odile wiped her face with the heel of her hand, straightened, and waited, boots still off, for the knock on her door.
