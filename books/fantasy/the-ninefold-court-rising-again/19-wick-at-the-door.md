## 19. Wick at the Door

The coast smelled of salt and rot and old fish, and Wick hated it.

They had followed Odile for six days—six days of Brannic tilting his head at crossroads like a man listening to whispers behind walls, of Mother Sable muttering over maps gone soft with damp, of Caspian riding ahead and coming back each evening with his jaw set tighter, saying *she doesn't want to be found* as if that were news to anyone. Six days of Wick learning what it meant to track someone who had spent her whole life not being tracked.

But Wick knew something about disappearing. They'd done it professionally, once, before their memories ran out at age six and everything before became a locked room with no door. You didn't vanish by running. You vanished by becoming small, by folding yourself into the gaps between other people's attention. And Odile Eastwick, for all her thousand stolen lives, was very good at being small.

She was also, Wick had noticed, bad at one thing: she couldn't stop leaving traces of care. A coin pressed into a beggar's palm in Verance. An extra blanket folded over Sable's saddle when the priestess slept at watch. She left kindnesses behind her like breadcrumbs, and breadcrumbs led somewhere.

"Here," Brannic said, on the sixth morning, standing at the edge of a fishing village so small it didn't have a name on any map they'd stolen. The houses leaned together like conspirators, thatched roofs dark with sea-spray, nets hung out to dry like the skeletons of enormous fish. "She's here."

"You're sure?" Caspian asked. He looked terrible. Wick had decided around day three that this was deserved, but watching him now—the hollows under his eyes, the way his hand kept going to his coat where the prophecy book used to sit—he looked less like a liar caught and more like a man carrying something heavy uphill in the rain. Wick was still deciding how much sympathy to ration him. It was a small ration.

"She stopped hiding two days ago," Brannic said. His ears, slightly pointed beneath his hair, twitched. "Stopped listening for us. She's just... sitting. In the last house. The one facing the water." He paused. "It sounds like grief in there. Grief sounds like a door that won't shut all the way."

Wick shouldered their pack. "Then somebody better go knock."

"I'll go," Caspian started—

"No." Sable said it quietly, but with the finality of a church bell. She was mending a tear in her sleeve with clumsy fingers; she had burned her priestess robes in Verance and wore plain wool now, and somehow looked more holy for it. "You're the last face she wants at that door, boy. And I've said my piece to her already. She forgave me. What she needs now isn't forgiveness or confession." She nodded at Wick. "It's someone who shows up."

Wick blinked. "Why me?"

Sable smiled, and it was an old smile, worn smooth like a river stone. "Because you asked her for something once—asked her never to take your memories—and she gave you a vow. Vows make doors between people. Go through yours."

* * *

The house at the end of the lane had a blue door, though the salt air had eaten most of the paint, leaving it mottled like a bruise healing. Wick stood before it for a long time, longer than they'd ever stood anywhere, because the thing about doors was that Wick had always been good at them—locks, latches, hinges, the whole grammar of entry—but this door didn't have a lock. It only had a person behind it who had every reason in the world not to open it.

Wick knocked.

Nothing. Then, muffled: "Go away, Caspian."

"It's Wick."

A silence. Long enough that Wick heard the sea working at the stones below the cliff, patient as a thief picking a lock that would take ten thousand years.

"Go away anyway."

"No."

Another silence, and then, closer, right against the wood: "Wick. I mean it. I'm dangerous right now. I'm—" A breath. "I stole a memory yesterday. From a woman on the beach. Just a little one. A memory of her mother singing. I just wanted to know what it felt like." Her voice cracked down the middle. "That's what I am when nobody's watching. So go home."

Wick sat down on the doorstep. Deliberately. Back against the blue door, knees up, arms wrapped around them. The stone was cold through their trousers and the wind came off the water sharp enough to shave with.

"If you don't open the door," Wick called, "I'm going to sit here until the tide comes in, and then I'm going to get wet, and then Sable is going to fuss over me and wrap me in blankets and feed me soup, and it'll be your fault that I caught my death. She'll write you a very stern letter. She's a defrocked priestess; her stern letters are legendary."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

"Wick—"

"You stole from a stranger," Wick said. "Yeah. I heard. Want to know what I think?"

"I really don't."

"I think you went alone on purpose." Wick pulled their knees tighter. "Because if we were there, you couldn't do it. Stealing is easy when no one who knows you is watching. Believe me. I know. Before I lost everything, I was apparently very good at taking things from people who trusted me. That's what Sable says, anyway. She says it gently, which is worse."

Silence. But Wick could hear her breathing on the other side of the door, close, and they took that as encouragement.

"You told me once," Wick went on, "that you had rules. Never steal pain. Never steal from family. Never leave the valley. Three rules, and you said they were the whole architecture of your soul. Well. Look at you. You broke rule three for a prophecy. You broke rule one yesterday. There's only one left, Odie, and you're out here trying to break it too—by making sure there's no family left to steal from."

"Don't call me that."

"Why not? It's your name. Well—one of them. You've got a thousand lives in your head, so you must have a thousand names. Odie's mine. I made it up. You can't steal it back, it was a gift."

The door handle rattled. Wick's heart did something complicated. Then it rattled again and stopped.

"Why are you here?" Odile's voice came low and rough, like a blade dragged over stone. "Really. Not the joke about the soup. Why did you follow me?"

Wick considered lying. They were, historically, good at it. But lies were locks too, and some doors you had to walk through honest.

"Because I don't remember my family," Wick said. "Six years old, and then nothing. Maybe they sold me. Maybe they died. Maybe somebody like you—or somebody much worse—took it all. I used to lie awake trying to build them out of nothing. A mother with brown hair. A brother who snored. I built whole people out of guesswork and grief, and you know what I figured out?" Their voice wobbled and they hated it and kept going. "It didn't matter. Because even if I met them tomorrow, they'd be strangers. Family isn't the people who made you, Odile. It's not even the people you remember. It's the people who show up. That's all it is. That's all it ever was."

"And I left," she said through the door. "I showed up for exactly as long as it suited me, and then I walked away the moment it cost something. That's not showing up. That's the opposite."

"So show up again." Wick turned, still seated, and pressed their palm flat against the cold blue wood. "That's the whole trick of it. Nobody's counting. Nobody's keeping score except you. You walked away, fine. Walk back. People do it all the time. Sable walked away from her goddess for a hundred years and came back with burned robes and a sharper spine. Even Caspian—"

"Don't."

"—even Caspian is camped at the edge of the village right now, too ashamed to come down, sending me because he thinks I'm the only one you might not hate. Which is its own kind of showing up, maybe. Showing up by staying away. It's stupid, but it's something."

"He lied to me, Wick." The voice was shaking now, and it wasn't anger doing the shaking. "He lied twice. He knew I'd die for this prophecy. He knew I was the heir—he recruited me for my blood, not for me. My whole life I've been useful to people. The traders used me to carry contraband. The thieves' guild used me to carry grudges. And he—" A sound that might have been a laugh, if laughter bled. "He made me feel seen. Do you know what that's worth to someone whose entire gift is seeing everyone else? To be seen? And it was just recruitment."

"Yeah," Wick said quietly. "It was awful. You're allowed to be furious forever. That's not what I'm arguing about."

"Then what are you arguing about?"

Wick stood up. Brushed off their trousers. Faced the blue door like it was the ninth gate itself.

"I'm arguing about the part where you decide that being hurt means being alone. Because those aren't the same thing, and I think somewhere under a thousand stolen memories, you know they're not." They swallowed. "You asked me once to promise you'd never take my memories. Remember what I said? I said yes. And then I said something else—I said, *but if you ever forget me, I'll introduce myself again.*"

Silence. The sea filled it.

"You thought I was joking," Wick said. "I wasn't. I've got six years of self, tops. Six years, Odile. Everything else is a locked room. If I can live with that—if I can wake up every morning and choose to be somebody anyway—then you can survive losing whatever you're afraid of losing. Because here's the prophecy's dirty secret that Sable figured out: you're not afraid of dying. You're afraid of forgetting us. And I'm telling you—" their voice broke clean through, and they let it— "you can forget my name, my face, every single day we've ever had, and I will find you again. Every time. I'll track you across the Nine Realms. I tracked you here, didn't I? And I'm fifteen and half-trained and I did it with breadcrumbs and stubbornness. Imagine what I'll manage when I actually care."

The latch clicked.

The door opened a hand's width, and Odile Eastwick looked out at them through the gap, and Wick's chest hurt, because she looked wrecked. Hair unwashed, eyes rimmed red, wearing a borrowed fisherman's coat three sizes too big. The most competent thief in the Hollow Valley, undone by a teenager on a doorstep.

"You're crying," she said.

"You're hallucinating," Wick said, wiping their face with their sleeve. "Sea spray."

"There's no wind from that direction."

"Lucky spray."

Odile laughed—a broken, wet, terrible laugh, but a laugh—and opened the door the rest of the way. Inside, the cottage was bare: a table, a chair, a cold hearth, a bed she clearly hadn't slept in. She'd been sitting facing the water, Wick saw, in the one chair, for days. Watching the horizon like it owed her something.

"I stole a lullaby," she said, small. "From a stranger. Just to feel held. And it worked, and that's the worst part. It worked, and I sat here holding someone else's mother in my head, and I thought—this is what I am. A house full of other people's furniture."

Wick stepped inside. Closed the blue door behind them.

"Then throw out the furniture," they said. "Or keep it, I don't care. But a house isn't furniture either, Odie. It's whoever keeps knocking." They jerked a thumb at the door. "Speaking of which—there's three idiots at the top of the lane pretending to examine a fence post. One of them is fae-blooded and hears doors, so he definitely heard all of that, which means he's currently pretending very hard to be interested in post-related matters."

Odile wiped her face with the oversized sleeve. She looked at the door. At Wick. At the grey sea beyond the window, indifferent and vast.

"If I go back," she said slowly, "it's not forgiveness. Not yet. Not for him."

"It's not forgiveness," Wick agreed. "It's showing up. Forgiveness is a different door. We'll get to it."

"And the prophecy still says I die."

"Sable wants to talk to you about that, actually. She's been up three nights with the book and she's got ink on her nose and that look she gets. The one that means she's either found something or set fire to something." Wick grinned despite themselves. "Odds are both."

Odile stood in the middle of the empty room a moment longer—standing, Wick thought, exactly like someone at the edge of a high place, deciding whether falling and flying were the same thing today. Then she pulled the fisherman's coat straight, rolled up the ridiculous sleeves, and picked up her pack from beside the chair, where it had sat packed for days. Ready to run, even while she sat still. Old habits.

"Fine," she said. "But I'm walking in front. And if Caspian says one word—one word—about destiny, I'm feeding him to the tide."

"Noted," said a voice from the doorway, and there he was—Caspian, having abandoned the fence-post pretense entirely, standing hatless in the lane with windblown hair and the expression of a man who has been handed a sentence and intends to serve it without appeal. Behind him, Brannic waved cheerfully. Somewhere further up, Sable's voice carried down the hill, scolding them both for eavesdropping.

Odile closed her eyes. Breathed. When she opened them, she looked at Wick first—not at Caspian, not at the road, at Wick—and something in her face had shifted, some load set down or at least repacked.

"You'll introduce yourself again," she said. "If it comes to that. You promise?"

"On my life," Wick said. "And I haven't got many possessions, but my life is one of them, and I keep it in very good condition."

Odile huffed—almost a laugh—and shouldered her pack, and walked out into the grey morning with her found family falling into step around her like a tide coming back in.

And far to the east, past the drowned marshes and the burning borders, the roads filled with the dust of an army marching toward a court of empty thrones—and none of them, walking toward it, knew yet what it would cost to stand in its way.
