## 20. Veyran's Court

The city of Karvess did not welcome travelers so much as digest them slowly.

Caspian felt it happening from the moment he passed beneath the Gate of Nine Arches—the way the streets swallowed him, the way the crowds flowed around him like water around a stone that had always been there. He had walked into cities before and been seen; Bellharbor had looked at him with curiosity, Verance with suspicion. Karvess looked through him. That was the first art of the place, he realized. Not surveillance but erasure. The usurper had learned long ago that a man who feels invisible will never look for the eyes upon him.

And there were eyes. Caspian counted them out of old habit—a beggar with too-clean hands outside the tannery row, a woman selling roasted chestnuts whose gaze tracked him longer than any chestnut deserved, two soldiers in grey-and-silver livery who were not walking anywhere in particular. The Grey Wardens, Veyran's own. Their armor was beautiful in the way of well-made knives.

He pulled his hood lower and let the river-smell of the city take him: tar, fish offal, coal smoke, and beneath it all something sweeter and wronger, like honey poured over iron. He had smelled it once before, years ago, at the border of a fae mound that had been burned out by mortal mobs. It was the smell of magic curdling.

*You came here for a reason,* he reminded himself, threading down the Tallow Steps toward the harbor district. *Remember the reason. Remember everything else, too.*

That last part mattered more than it should have. In this city, memory was currency, contraband, and ammunition all at once, and Caspian carried more than most. He carried the face of every door in the Ninefold Court. He carried the sound of the nine lords swearing their oaths, a century ago, when the world still held its shape. He carried Odile Eastwick's expression when she'd caught him burning that page—hurt folding into fury folding into something colder, a door closing—and he carried the fact that he had not yet earned his way back through it.

He carried, too, the letter that had summoned him here. It sat against his ribs like a coal wrapped in silk.

*To the Court's herald, walking free among mortals:* it had begun, in ink so fine it might have been spider-spun. *The King of All That Remains requests your presence. Come unarmed. Come unaccompanied. Come knowing that refusal is also an answer, and we collect those.*

*The King of All That Remains.* Veyran had named himself that seven years ago, when he took the throne of Karvess with an army of inquisitors at his back and a smile that made even his generals step back half a pace. Caspian had spent the journey trying to decide whether the title was vanity or precision. He suspected, now, with the city's sweet-rot smell in his nose, that it was both.

The palace rose ahead where the river bent, black stone veined with white, and Caspian stopped in the shadow of a fishmonger's awning to do what he had promised himself he would not do: he hesitated.

Behind his breastbone, faint as a pulse under ice, he felt the tether. Every herald of the Court carried one—a thread of belonging spun into them at their making, running back across the broken miles to the ruined heart of the Ninefold Court, telling them where home was even when home was a mausoleum. For a hundred years the thread had hung slack, singing nothing. But since the night he'd shown Odile the prophecy book, since she had spoken her own name aloud in that candlelit room and the words had landed on the world like stones on a frozen pond, his thread had begun to hum.

It hummed now, here, in the usurper's capital, and Caspian did not know what that meant, and not-knowing was the most dangerous condition a creature like him could occupy.

He went on toward the palace.

* * *

They took his coat, his knives—he had brought only two, both ceremonial, both meant to be taken—and his satchel, and led him up through the black-stone halls barefoot on floors warm as skin. That warmth troubled him more than anything else. Stone should not be warm. Unless something inside it was alive.

The Warden escort said nothing. They did not need to. Their silence had the practiced quality of a ritual, and Caspian understood, walking the long ascending gallery lined with portraits of dead kings each defaced in exactly the same manner—the faces scraped smooth as eggs—that he was being shown these portraits deliberately. *This is what happens to kings here,* the gallery said. *All of them. Even the beloved ones. Especially the beloved ones.*

At the top of the gallery stood a door of pale wood, plain as a farmhouse door, absurd in all that black grandeur.

"Wait," said one of the Wardens, and then, unexpectedly, softly: "He likes it when people wait."

Caspian waited.

Through the door he could hear—faintly, the way you hear rain through a dream—a sound like many voices speaking at once, none of them quite in time with the others. It raised the hair on his arms. He knew that sound. Every fae-born knew it. It was the sound of a court in session: nine houses, nine voices, the old polyphony of the Ninefold.

But the Court was empty. He had stood in its ruins. He had counted nine thrones and found them cold.

The door opened inward without being touched.

The room beyond was small. That was the first shock. After the vastness of the approach—the galleries, the stairs, the hall of defaced kings—he had expected a throne room built to drown visitors in scale. Instead: a round chamber no larger than a miller's parlor, lit by a single hearth of coals that gave off no smoke, furnished with one table, two chairs, and a window that looked out not over the city but over something else entirely—a rolling green country under a gold afternoon sun, hills stitched with hedgerows, a road winding away toward towers that gleamed like mother-of-pearl.

It was a view of the Ninefold Court as it had been before the breaking. Caspian would have known it blind. He ached looking at it, an ache with teeth.

"You're staring at my window."

Veyran stood with his back to the hearth, and Caspian made himself look at him properly, because whatever else happened in this room, he refused to be a man who flinched.

The histories called Veyran tall and dark and beautiful, and the histories were lazy. He was of middling height, brown-haired, unremarkable in every feature except the eyes, which were the grey of a winter river just before it freezes over—and except for the crown.

Everyone knew the usurper wore a crown. No portrait of him showed it accurately, because no artist could render it and keep their mind. It was not metal. It was not light. It sat above his brow like a ring of frost made of moving images, and as Caspian watched, the images turned: a woman laughing in an orchard; a tower falling; hands weaving light into a loom; a child's voice counting to nine; a battle seen from above, from below, from inside the body of a dying man. Snatches of lives. Fragments of days. Memories, worn like jewels, turning slowly in a circle around the king's head.

Nine of them. Caspian counted, and his mouth went dry. Nine streams of imagery, nine distinct colors of remembering, braided together into a circlet that pulsed faintly, in rhythm, he realized with horror, with the warm stone under his feet. With the whole palace. With the whole city.

Veyran smiled, and it was a good smile, a warm smile, the kind of smile that had probably gotten him through doors that armies couldn't breach.

"The herald of a dead court," he said. His voice was ordinary, almost gentle, which was somehow worse than thunder. "Do you know how long I've wanted to meet one of you? Sit down, please. The chair on the left. The right one bites."

Caspian did not sit. "Your Majesty honors me."

"Honor." Veyran tasted the word. "Sit anyway. I dislike craning."

Caspian sat. The left chair was deep and upholstered in something soft and grey, and it accepted his weight with a sigh that sounded almost human, and he resolved not to think about what it might be upholstered *with*.

"You received my letter," Veyran said. Not a question.

"I did."

"And you came alone, unarmed, as asked. Which tells me either that you are very brave, very foolish, or that you already understand how little weapons matter in this particular conversation." He gestured, and the window-view rippled—the green country shivered like water disturbed by a finger. "Which is it?"

"I came," Caspian said carefully, "because the King of All That Remains does not summon the herald of the Ninefold Court out of idle curiosity. And because I have questions only you can answer."

"Oh, excellent. Questions." Veyran drew out the second chair—the biting one—and sat in it himself, leaning forward, elbows on knees, a man settling in for gossip by a fire. "Ask, then. I am feeling generous tonight. You'll find I'm often generous. It's one of the things people get wrong about me. They think a man who steals memories must hoard them like a miser, must be small and clutching. But a miser hoards coin because coin runs out." He touched one fingertip briefly to the turning ring above his brow, and where it touched, the images quickened, brightened, like embers breathed upon. "I hoard because hoarding is *plenty*. Ask."

The temptation to ask about Odile first was enormous, and Caspian strangled it, because asking about her first would tell Veyran exactly where to press. He chose the other question, the one that had been sitting in him since the door opened.

"The sound behind your door," he said. "A court in session. Nine voices. Your crown holds nine streams of memory. And the histories say—" he made himself say it plainly— "the histories say that when the Ninefold Court broke, the nine lords did not die. They vanished. Some said they slept. Some said they were scattered into the world like seed."

"Yes," Veyran said pleasantly.

Caspian waited. The fire crackled without smoke. Somewhere far below, the warm stone hummed.

"You want me to say it outright." The usurper tilted his head, and for a moment the good humor fell away like a mask slipping sideways, and what was underneath was older and hungrier and very tired. "Very well. A century ago, someone stole from the Court the one thing that bound it: the memory of its true heir. Without the heir remembered, the covenant could not renew. Without renewal, the lords began to forget themselves—do you understand what forgetting means, for creatures made of memory? They didn't die. They *dissolved*. Into the air. Into the borders where the magic rots. Into dreams. Into—" he spread his hands— "*me*. I was a minor nobleman with a talent for collection and a grudge, herald. Over thirty years I gathered them. Piece by piece. Stream by stream. The orchard-laughter of Lord Aurelian of the Summer House. The loom-hands of Lady Vesperine. The child-counting of the youngest lord, who was barely grown when the covenant failed." His voice softened on that one, and Caspian hated that it sounded genuine. "I wear them now. All nine. I am not their jailer. I am their reliquary."

"That's not what a reliquary is," Caspian said quietly.

"No?" Veyran laughed, short and sharp. "No. Perhaps not. A reliquary doesn't *use* what it keeps." He stood, and the crown turned faster, and the room seemed to lean toward him, walls and floor and hearth, the whole warm living palace orienting itself around its king like a flower toward a sun. "But consider what I've *built* with them, herald. The borders hold where I garrison them. The rot retreats where I march. The free cities squabble and starve and burn memory-witches in their markets, and meanwhile Karvess has bread, order, warm floors, and no wraiths within a hundred miles. Because I hold the nine. Because someone finally had the stomach to gather what was scattered and *wear* it."

"You hold them the way a thief holds a stolen coat," Caspian said. "It warms you. It isn't yours."

For the first time, the temperature of the room changed. Not literally—though perhaps literally; the hearth-coals guttered low. Veyran looked at him with the winter-river eyes, and the crown's turning slowed, and in the sudden stillness Caspian heard the nine voices again, clearer now, coming not from beyond the door but from the crown itself, from the king's own skull: nine melodies, worn down to fragments, circling and circling and never resolving into harmony.

"Do you know what I remember that isn't mine?" Veyran asked, softly. "I remember standing in an orchard at the end of summer, four hundred years ago, holding a pear I'd stolen off a tree that wasn't mine, while a golden-haired lord pretended not to see me because he thought thieves deserved mercy if they were young enough. I remember his laughter. I remember loving him for it." His jaw tightened. "I remember things I never lived, herald. Griefs I never earned. Joys I have no right to. Do you understand what that does to a man? I am a house with nine families living in it. I am never alone. I am never—"

He stopped. Recollected himself. The smile came back up like a drawbridge.

"But you didn't come to hear me confess. You came with questions of your own, and I promised generosity." He resumed his seat, casual again, and flicked two fingers toward the impossible window. "So. Ask about the girl."

The name tried to leap out of Caspian's mouth—*how do you know about*—and he bit it back, but he was too slow; his silence answered for him. Veyran nodded, satisfied, a collector confirming an entry in a ledger.

"Odile Eastwick," the usurper said. "Thief of the Hollow Valley. Carrier of a thousand stolen lives. Granddaughter of Marigold Eastwick, trader, thief, and—by my accounting—the woman who broke the world." He leaned back. "My inquisitors have chased rumors of her for months. A valley that cannot be found. A thief who lifts memories like purses. And now, suddenly, she walks the open roads with an ex-priestess, a fae-blooded foundling, a child cutpurse, and—you. My herald. Tell me, Caspian of the Ninth Door: does she know yet what she is? Does she know what her grandmother took? Has anyone had the courage to tell her that the heir the whole broken world is waiting for is sleeping in her blood?"

The room was very quiet. The crown turned. Through the false window, the pearl-towers of the old Court gleamed in a sun that had set a century ago.

"You already know the answers," Caspian said. "You didn't summon me to ask."

"No. I summoned you to make you an offer." Veyran leaned forward, and the firelight caught the turning memories above his brow, and for one vertiginous moment Caspian saw his own face reflected in them—saw himself multiplied nine times, nine small reflections swimming in stolen lives. "Here is what I know about heralds. You are made of belonging. Spun from the Court's own longing for connection—it's why you can find any door, why strangers trust you before they've heard your name, why the girl trusted *you*, gods help her, though you lied to her twice and burned the page that would have told her the price. You are a creature whose entire nature is *being kept*. And for a hundred years, nothing has kept you. Your court is rubble. Your lords are wearing my head as a hat. You walk the world carrying keys to doors that no longer exist." Veyran's voice dropped, gentle as a hand on a shoulder. "I am offering you a court, Caspian. A living one. These nine—Aurelian, Vesperine, the child, all of them—they speak to me. Through me. If you serve me, you will hear them properly again. You will be their herald in truth. First herald of the restored Ninefold, under a king strong enough to protect it. Not prophecy. Not sacrifice. Not a girl bleeding her childhood into empty thrones to patch a hole her grandmother made. *Order*. Bread. Warm floors. Belonging, given instead of stolen."

He let it sit. He was good at letting it sit; Caspian could feel the silence working on him like water on stone, finding the cracks, and the worst of it—the unforgivable part—was that the offer was not entirely a lie. The thread behind his breastbone hummed. The nine voices circled. Some traitorous chamber of him, the chamber that had been empty for a hundred years, *leaned*.

And Caspian saw, with terrible clarity, what the usurper's power actually was. Not the army. Not the inquisitors. Not the warm stone or the defaced kings. It was this: he wore stolen belonging so skillfully that lonely things mistook it for home. The crown wasn't a weapon. It was bait. Every memory in it was a life someone had loved from the inside, and love leaked, and hungry things came to the leak.

"You're offering me the nine lords as hostages dressed up as guests," Caspian said.

"I'm offering you dinner," Veyran said, "at a table where everyone you miss is already seated. Yes. Fine. Hostages. Guests. In a hundred years, herald, no one has managed to tell the difference, and I include myself." Something raw moved under his composure and was gone. "Think about it. Truly think. Not the answer you owe your absent queen-in-waiting. The answer you owe yourself."

Caspian thought of Odile at the covenant's edge, pouring herself out. He thought of Wick saying *if you forget me, I'll introduce myself again*—no. He hadn't heard that yet. He thought of Wick's stubborn face, of Sable's burnt robes, of Brannic listening to doors. He thought of the hollow taste of a lie told kindly.

"I have an answer," he said.

"Of course you do." Veyran smiled. "Heralds always do. But answers are heavy things to carry out of Karvess, and my city digests slow. So keep yours warm a moment longer, because there is one more item on tonight's agenda, and I confess I've saved it because I'm rather proud of it." He clapped his hands once, softly, and the plain farmhouse door opened, and a woman stepped through.

She wore grey-and-silver. Her ledger was chained to her hip. Her eyes had the flat polished look of a blade honed past usefulness into something else, and when she looked at Caspian, he felt catalogued, weighed, and filed.

"Inquisitor Maren Voss," Veyran said warmly. "My best. Voss—this is the herald I told you about. He's going to help us find the Covenant-Bearer."

Voss smiled. It did not reach anywhere near her eyes.

"She leaves at dawn," the usurper said, watching Caspian with the patience of a man who had already won, "for Rotwood. I do hope your answer keeps well on the road."

* * *

They gave him a room for the night, high in the warm black stone, with a bed softer than any he'd ever slept in and a window that showed the same impossible green country, the same gold afternoon, the Court unbroken forever behind glass. A guest-room kindness. A leash made of longing.

Caspian did not sleep. He sat with his back against the wall, watching the false view, and behind his breastbone his thread to the true ruin hummed and hummed, and he listened past it, past the humming, down into the deep of himself where he kept the thing Veyran's offer had been designed to dislodge:

Not duty. Not guilt.

Dinner. A table in a safehouse in Bellharbor. Wick stealing bread off Brannic's plate. Sable calling them all fools with unmistakable fondness. Odile laughing—actually laughing, surprised by her own laugh, as if it belonged to someone she was only just becoming.

Belonging, given instead of stolen.

Veyran was right about one thing: Caspian had an answer. It had stopped being a question somewhere on the road, maybe in Rotwood, maybe at Salt Bridge, maybe the moment a lockpick-child had looked at him and decided he was theirs.

Now he only had to survive long enough to deliver it.

Below, in the dark, he heard the stables waking early. Hooves. Grey-and-silver armor being buckled in the pre-dawn cold. Maren Voss riding for Rotwood with a company of Wardens and a chained ledger—and Caspian pressed his forehead against the glass, against the sun that had set a hundred years ago, and began, silently, desperately, to compose the fastest letter of his long, long life.
