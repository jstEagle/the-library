## 21. The Inquisitor's Hunt

The rain came down like a confession no one wanted to hear.

They had made camp in the shell of an old waystation on the Verance road—a pilgrim inn once, by the look of it, its roof half-collapsed and its hearth cold for a hundred years. Wick had found a dry corner beneath the stair and claimed it with the fierce territorial instinct of a cat. Brannic sat near the broken door with his head tilted, listening to the doors that weren't there anymore, the way he always did when they stopped moving. Mother Sable was mending a cloak that could not be saved, stitching anyway, because her hands needed work the way other people's needed prayer.

And Odile stood at the doorway, watching the rain stitch silver threads through the dark, and felt the memory she had paid at Salt Bridge still sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone.

A wedding. Some stranger's wedding—sunlight on a harbor deck, salt wind, a woman laughing so hard she had to hold the railing, a ring of braided silver going onto a finger. Odile had poured it out of her hoard into the inquisitors' toll-scales three days ago, and ever since, some part of her kept reaching for it the way a tongue finds a missing tooth. She hadn't known the woman's name. She never knew their names. That had always been the rule that kept her clean: take what they'll never miss, give what you can't keep, love nothing you carry.

It had never felt unclean before.

"You're doing the thing," Wick said from under the stairs. "Where you stand in doorways looking tragic."

"I'm keeping watch."

"You're brooding with extra steps."

Despite everything—the rotwood behind them, Veyran's edicts nailed to every post between here and the coast, the page of ash Caspian had tried to burn—Odile felt her mouth twitch. "Go back to sleep, picklock."

"Can't. Someone's humming." Wick's voice changed, lost its edge, went thin and careful. "Under the rain. You hear it?"

She did. She'd been hearing it for an hour and telling herself it was water in a broken gutter. A hymn, very low, very steady, sung in the old liturgical cadence that Mother Sable used only when she thought no one was listening. But this wasn't Sable. This came from up the road, from the dark where the rain fell thickest, and it was being sung by more than one throat, in unison, without variation, which was worse. People who sang like that weren't singing to feel something. They were singing to drown something out.

Brannic rose from beside the door. His eyes had gone wide and white-rimmed. "There's a door coming," he said. "Not a building. Not walls. A *door*—they're carrying one. It's been closed for years and it wants to open and there are people on both sides of it."

"Voss," Odile said.

Nobody asked how she knew. They all knew. The name had been following them since Bellharbor like a shadow cast by a light they couldn't see: Maren Voss, Senior Inquisitor of the Edict, keeper of ledgers, burner of memory-keepers. Sable spoke of her the way sailors speak of a storm they survived once and expect again.

"Out the back," Odile said. "Now. Quietly."

But even as she said it, she heard the hymn stop.

In the silence after, the rain sounded very loud, and under the rain she heard the thing Brannic had described: hinges. Old ones. Somewhere up the road, in the dark, a door was opening that had no house around it, and the sound of it went through her teeth the way a struck bell goes through a floor.

"They've seen us," Sable said simply. She set down her needle. "Or rather, child, they've seen what we are. Inquisitors don't find people. They find magic, and then they find whatever it's attached to." Her old eyes went to Odile, then to the satchel at her hip where a thousand stolen lives slept in glass and brass. "And you, my dear, shine in the dark like a bonfire to them."

* * *

They ran, and it didn't matter.

That was the lesson of the next hour, written in mud and lightning and the relentless geometry of pursuit: you cannot outrun an inquisitor, because an inquisitor does not follow your tracks. They follow your traces—the heat of your passing, the shimmer of stolen time leaking from a thief's satchel like oil from a torn sack. Every hour Odile had walked this road, she had left a wake of borrowed yesterday behind her, and Voss was reading that wake the way Odile read locks: fluently, professionally, with pleasure.

The waystation's back wall opened onto a sheep pasture gone to weeds, the pasture onto a wood, the wood onto a ravine with a rope bridge that Wick swore still held weight. They got as far as the bridge.

Then the lanterns bloomed.

They came out of the dark on both sides of the ravine—not torches, but the pale grey-white light of memory-lanterns, lamps that burned distilled recollection instead of oil, and the light they cast was wrong, was *familiar*, showed each of them not as they stood, mud-soaked and exhausted, but as they had been at their worst. Odile saw herself lit in the lantern-glow: a girl of nineteen crouched over a dying ferryman, hands in his hair, drinking his last snowfall out of him. She flinched from her own illumination.

At the head of the lantern-line stood a tall woman in grey, hood back, rain running off a face that might have been carved for courtrooms—sharp planes, thin mouth, eyes the color of slate before it breaks. She carried no weapon that Odile could see. She didn't need one. At her belt hung a ledger bound in white leather, and chained to the ledger hung a pair of long silver tongs, and Odile understood with a lurch what those were for, what they had always been for, and why the memory-keeper in Bellharbor had screamed the way she'd screamed.

"Maren Voss," Mother Sable said. Her voice was level. Her knuckles, around her walking staff, were bone-white. "You've come a long way from the seminary, girl."

"Sister Sable." Voss's voice was soft, almost kind, which was the most frightening thing about it. "Or should I say Mother? They tell me you burned your robes in Verance. I would have paid to see it. There is something almost holy in a woman finally telling the truth about herself."

"The truth," Sable said. "That's rich, from a woman who harvests the dying."

"I *archive* them." For just a moment the kindness cracked and something else looked out—something hungry and tired and terribly certain of itself. "Every memory the Edict takes is a memory saved. Preserved. Protected from thieves like your companion there, who gutted a ferryman on his deathbed because she was bored." Her slate eyes moved to Odile, and Odile felt them go through her like tongs through wax. "Odile Eastwick. Thief of Yesterday. Do you know what you're worth in the capital? Do you know what His Majesty pays for a live Covenant-Bearer?" She smiled without any warmth whatsoever. "More than you have ever stolen in your life. I almost envy you the discovery."

Behind Odile, she heard Caspian shift his weight. She heard Wick's breathing go quick and shallow. She heard Brannic make a small, hurt sound, because the carried door was close now, close enough for him to hear everything on both sides of it, and there were screams stored on the far side of that door. He'd told them that much before they ran. Screams, and a smell of smoke, and a child's voice calling a name over and over.

*Don't,* Odile thought at him, though she didn't know who she was warning. *Whatever's behind that door, don't listen.*

"There are four of you and thirty of us," Voss said, conversational, as if discussing weather. "Your foundling hears my door and it has unhinged him—I can see it. Your priestess is old and unarmed. Your lockpick is—" her gaze touched Wick and moved on with the terrible economy of a woman cataloguing inventory, "—negligible. And your fae escort—" her eyes found Caspian, and narrowed, and Odile watched recognition move across her face like frost crossing a window. "Well. His Majesty will want you alive, won't he. The Court's little messenger. How the mighty houses have fallen, to send errand boys."

"I've been called worse by better," Caspian said, and his voice was light, but Odile had spent enough weeks beside him now to hear the wire pulled taut underneath.

"So here is what happens." Voss unclasped the ledger from her belt and held it up, and the memory-lanterns turned its white leather the color of old teeth. "The thief comes with me, bound and tongued, her satchel intact. The others may walk away. I am not cruel, Sister. I am thorough. There is a difference, though I understand it is a difficult one for your generation."

"And if we refuse?" Odile asked.

Voss looked at her, and for the first time the courtroom calm flickered, and Odile saw the thing underneath the inquisitor: a woman who had spent so long handling other people's pasts that she had forgotten how to be inside her own present. It lasted less than a breath. Then it was filed away, and the smile returned.

"Then I open the door," Voss said, "and we learn what your foundling's gift costs him at close range. And then I take the thief anyway, along with everyone else's memories as a bonus. Refusal merely changes the order of operations." She gestured, almost graciously, toward the satchel. "I know what you are carrying. Nine hundred and forty-one vials by my count—which, incidentally, is off by six, and we will discuss the discrepancy during processing. Every theft you ever committed is itemized in my master ledger, Eastwick. Names. Dates. Victims. You are not a person to the Edict. You are an *inventory discrepancy*, and I do so hate discrepancies."

The rain fell. The lanterns hummed. Somewhere in the dark, Brannic pressed his palms flat against his ears and began, very quietly, to rock.

And Odile did the arithmetic she had been avoiding since Bellharbor. Four of them. Thirty inquisitors. A ravine, a rope bridge, no way through. Sable was too slow to run, Wick too small to fight, Brannic already bleeding out through his ears in a metaphorical sense, Caspian too valuable to kill and therefore doomed. There was no clever exit. She had checked every door in this situation, and every one of them was locked, and the keyhole was shaped like her own sin.

Which left only the thing she had sworn never to do.

Her whole life she had lived by the rules because the rules kept the theft clean. Never steal pain—that was the first law, older than the others, taught by her grandmother's ghost of a voice: *you can carry someone's joy, child, but their grief will carry you*. Joy was feather-light; it rode in a vial like pollen. Grief was lead. Grief had weight, and edges, and a gravity that bent the thief around it until the thief forgot which memories were hers. Every thief who ever stole pain ended the same way: drowned in other people's drowning.

But Voss's worst memory was standing right there, worn openly on her sleeve of a face, guarded by nothing but professional habit—and Odile had learned, in the Hollow Valley, to read a stranger's history the way a farrier reads a hoof. She could see the seam of it. Behind the slate eyes. Behind the courtroom calm. A door that had been closed for years and wanted to open, screaming, smelling of smoke—

"Oh," Odile said softly. "Oh, you *poor* woman."

Something flickered in Voss's expression. "What—"

Odile stepped forward, into the lantern-light, into the rain, and reached—not with her hands, though her hands rose anyway, an old habit, a thief's pantomime—but with the hollow place inside her where stolen things lived. She had done this a thousand times with joy. First snow. Weddings. Lullabies. She slid into the seams of Maren Voss the way she slid into locks, gentle, apologetic, invisible—

And found the door.

It was exactly as Brannic had described. Closed for years. Straining at its hinges. And through the gap beneath it: smoke.

She should have stopped. Later, on the rope bridge, shaking, she would tell herself a hundred times that she should have stopped, should have taken some small shame instead, a petty humiliation, a moment of private weakness—enough to wound, not enough to unmake. That was the craft. That was the ethics of it, such as they were.

Instead she put her shoulder against the door and pushed, and stole Maren Voss's worst memory entire.

* * *

Fire.

A house burning—not a waystation, not an inn, a *home*, with toys in the yard and a coat rack by the door and a child's drawing pinned to the kitchen wall, stick figures holding hands under a crayon sun. And in the fire: a small voice calling a name. *Mama. Mama.* And Maren—younger, so much younger, in novice grey, hair loose, running—held back. Held back by her own teachers' hands on her arms, because the house was lost, because the child inside was lost, because a memory-mage's working had gone wrong in the neighboring tower and the fire fed on recollection itself and could not be quenched, only *waited out*, and the doctrine said wait, and Maren waited, and the small voice called her name—it was her daughter, it was her daughter, she wasn't supposed to have a daughter, novices weren't allowed, she had hidden her, hidden her in the one place no one would think to look, and the fire had found her anyway—

*Mama. Mama. Why won't you come.*

And Maren Voss stood in the rain of her own burning and did not come, and the voice got smaller, and smaller, and then it wasn't a voice anymore, it was just fire, and after the fire there was silence, and after the silence there was the Edict, and the Edict gave her tongs and a ledger and told her that memory was a disease and feeling was a vector and the only safety lay in taking everything, archiving everything, sealing everything away behind white leather covers where it could never, ever burn anyone again.

Thirty years of sealed doors. Thirty years of archived grief. And Odile ripped the seal off all of it in one pull, and took it, and ran.

* * *

She was aware of pieces afterward, the way you're aware of fragments of a fall.

She remembered Voss screaming—not the controlled scream of pain but the raw, ruined scream of a mother at a window—and the memory-lanterns shattering one by one as her discipline broke, spilling thirty confiscated lifetimes across the ravine in pale ghost-light, and her inquisitors stumbling and weeping as stray memories washed over them like spray off a reef. She remembered Sable shouting her name with an entirely new tone in it—horror, or awe, or both. She remembered cutting the rope bridge anchor with Wick's knife while Wick stared at her with wide, unreadable eyes. She remembered the swing of the severed bridge over black air, the inquisitors stranded on the far side, Voss on her knees in the mud with her hands over her ears, keening a child's name into the rain.

She remembered that part most clearly. She suspected she always would.

* * *

The other side of the ravine was a pine wood, and the pines held the rain off in a way that made the silence underneath feel manufactured, like a room built specifically for the conversation Odile was about to have.

She made it twenty steps past the treeline before her legs quit. She knelt in the needles and was sick, violently, thoroughly, and when there was nothing left she knelt there anyway, gasping, the stolen memory sitting in her chest like swallowed coals.

Grief was lead. Her grandmother had told her true.

Because it wasn't just *in* her. It was *hers* now, the way every stolen thing became hers—that was the curse inside the gift, the fine print of the Hollow Valley. The little voice was in her head. *Mama, why won't you come.* She could feel the heat of the burning house. She could smell the crayon sun on the kitchen wall scorching. And layered under it, faint but unmistakable, she could feel the shape of the woman Maren Voss had been before the Edict got its hooks in her: young, defiant, secretly in love with a life she wasn't permitted, willing to break doctrine for a child.

*I did that,* Odile thought. *To her.*

Not killed her. Worse. She had taken the one sealed door in a woman built entirely of sealed doors and thrown it open, and then walked away, and left her kneeling in the mud with everything pouring out. Voss had spent thirty years turning grief into procedure. And Odile had undone it in a heartbeat because it was tactically convenient.

She was becoming what she hunted. No—she had always been what she hunted. She had simply never before had to watch herself do it in a mirror with a face and a name and a burned child behind the eyes.

"Hey." Wick crouched beside her. The teenager's face, in the grey pre-dawn, was carefully neutral in the way that only children who have learned not to need anything can manage. "You're leaking."

"What?"

"Your satchel. Since the bridge." Wick nodded at it. "It's glowing. All the vials are. Like they're... upset." A pause. "Is that normal?"

"No." Odile looked down. The thief's gold light was bleeding through the leather seams, pulsing faintly, arrhythmically—her whole hoard stirred and restless, nine hundred and forty-one stolen joys knocking against their glass like birds against a window. As if they knew what she'd done. As if they were afraid of her now.

Wick studied her for a long moment. Then, in the matter-of-fact voice that Odile had come to understand was the deepest tenderness Wick owned: "Was it bad? What you took?"

"It was the worst thing I've ever taken."

"But it worked. We're alive."

"Yes."

Wick nodded slowly, filing this away, and Odile realized the child was building a philosophy out of her, brick by brick, the way children do—and she couldn't tell yet whether the philosophy being built was *Odile does what must be done* or *never become Odile*. Both frightened her equally.

Sable came and stood over her, staff planted, rain dripping off her chin. The old priestess looked down at her for a long time, and Odile braced for judgment—for the sermon, the scripture, the righteous fury that chapter fourteen in Verance had proven Sable could summon like weather.

Instead Sable said, quietly: "I knew a woman like you once. A century ago. Who also believed that the ends could hold the means the way a vial holds a memory." Her voice cracked slightly on the last word. "We will talk of her soon, child. Before the Court. You deserve to hear it from me and no one else." She straightened. "But tonight—tonight you grieve what you did, and then you get up, because Voss's column will regroup by dawn, and there is worse than Voss behind her."

Caspian appeared at the edge of the firelight they hadn't dared light, and his face when he looked at Odile was strange—pride and pity and fear braided together, the look of a man watching a prophecy fulfill itself and hating the shape of it. He opened his mouth as if to speak—to explain, to apologize, to say *this is what the path requires*—and Odile turned away from him before he could choose.

Because she had caught it, in the moment before she turned. The way his eyes had gone not to her, but to the satchel. To the glowing hoard. With recognition. With calculation.

As if he had known all along what she would eventually have to pour out of it—and how much.

Brannic sat apart under the pines, hands still over his ears, rocking. But as Odile rose on unsteady legs and shouldered the burning, restless weight of everything she had ever stolen, he lifted his head and looked at her, and said, in a voice scraped raw:

"He's coming, Odile. Behind the inquisitor. Behind the army. The king with the crown of other people's faces." His blind-white eyes reflected the glow of her satchel. "He's heard about you. He knows what you carry." A pause, and then, barely a whisper: "He's *hungry* for it."

Far to the south, beyond the ravine, beyond the rain, horns began to sound—one note, repeated, patient as tide.

Not a hunt anymore, Odile thought, pulling the company together in the dark.

An invitation.
