## 22. Mother Sable's Confession

The fire in the ruined chapel had burned down to a red heart by the time Mother Sable could no longer pretend she was praying.

She knelt anyway, because her knees remembered the posture even if her vows had been struck from the rolls a hundred years ago. The chapel was a husk—roof open to the stars like a broken eggshell, altar cracked down the middle where someone had taken an axe to it during the first years of Veyran's edicts. Moss grew in the seams of the flagstones. Somewhere above, an owl turned its head and considered them all with the indifference of things that had outlived gods. Sable found that comforting. Owls at least were honest about what they were.

Behind her, the company bedded down among the fallen pews: Wick curled tight as a fist with their stolen cloak pulled over their head; Brannic sitting upright because he said sleep came easier when he could hear which doors in the dark were closed and which were merely pretending; Caspian apart from all of them, as he had been since Odile caught him burning prophecy pages. And Odile herself at the far edge of the firelight, sharpening a knife she did not need sharpened, the way other people paced.

Sable rose from her knees. Her joints announced every year of her long life—longer than any of them knew, longer than she had ever admitted aloud—and she crossed the chapel floor to where Odile sat.

"Child," she said. "Walk with me."

Odile looked up. In the firelight her eyes were flat and watchful, the eyes of a woman who had been used twice over and had begun to count everyone around her as a possible third. "If this is a sermon—"

"It is not a sermon." Sable almost smiled. "Sermons are for people who still believe the speaker. This is something rarer. It is a confession, and I have not made one of those in ninety-nine years."

* * *

They walked out past the chapel wall into the churchyard, where the graves had gone nameless, the stones worn smooth by weather until they might have been teeth in the earth's jaw. The moon was a thin paring of bone. Wind moved through the dead grass with a sound like paper turning, like a ledger being read aloud somewhere far away.

Sable stopped between two nameless stones and folded her hands, and for a long moment she simply breathed, because what she was about to say had lived in her chest so long it had grown roots.

"You know I was a priestess," she began. "You know I was defrocked—that Veyran stripped the old covenant clergy when he took the throne, called us relics, collaborators, traitors to the mortal realms. That part is true, but it flatters me. It makes it sound as though I lost my robes because I stood for something." She shook her head slowly. "I lost them because I ran. And before I ran, I did worse than run."

Odile had gone very still. It was the stillness of a thief, Sable thought—a woman listening not just to words but to the spaces beneath them.

"I told you I served the Ninefold Court in its last days," Sable continued. "That is true. What I did not tell you is how young I was. Younger than Wick. A novice in the House of the Ninth Door, sworn to keep the covenant's memory alive among mortals—the festivals, the tithes, the names of the nine lords and the cities bound to them. We were the hinge between worlds. Do you understand? When a fae lord needed a mortal voice, we spoke. When a mortal city needed the Court's ear, we carried the petition. The covenant was not a document. It was a conversation, and priests like me were the words going back and forth."

"And then?" Odile said quietly.

"And then the Court forgot its heir." Sable's voice did not waver; she had rehearsed this too many times in silence for it to waver now. "You know the story as the world tells it—one day the Court's memory of its true heir was simply *gone*, plucked out like a splinter, and without an heir the covenant could not be renewed, and without renewal the doors began to rot. Within a generation the thrones stood empty. The lords went dim. Magic curdled at the borders and the free cities learned to be afraid. Everyone believed the Court itself had failed some sacred test, or that the heir had died, or that there had never been an heir at all—only a story the fae told to make their power seem older than it was."

The wind gusted. Sable waited it out.

"But I was there," she said. "In the last season of the covenant, a trader came to our house. An old woman, sharp-eyed, with silver rings on every finger and a mark she pressed into wax on everything she sold—a little wheel with eight spokes. She traded in memories, openly then; it was not yet a crime, only a curiosity, a merchant's trade like spice or salt. And one night I saw her leave the Court's memory-vault carrying more than she'd declared. I saw her press her wheel-mark into the seal of a jar that hummed like a struck bell. And I—I was seventeen, and frightened, and I said nothing."

Odile's breath left her slowly. "The mark," she said. "Eight-spoked wheel. I've seen it. In Verance. On vials in the bazaar."

"Yes." Sable turned to face her fully. "Because the trader was your grandmother, child. And you are not the first thief in your line to walk into the Ninefold Court and walk out lighter than you came."

* * *

For a moment neither of them spoke. Above the churchyard the stars wheeled on, indifferent, ancient, the same stars that had watched the covenant break.

"I didn't stop her," Sable said at last. "That was the first sin. But hear the rest, because it gets worse, and you deserve the whole weight of it before you decide what I'm worth to you." Her hands tightened together until the knuckles whitened. "After the theft, the Court sent inquiries. Heralds went door to door through the nine cities asking who had entered the vault. There was to be an accounting. And I knew—if I spoke, the trader would be named, and the theft undone, and the heir restored, and I would have done my duty. One word. One word, Sable, that's all it required."

"But?"

"But I had seen what happened to novices who made trouble. I had watched the elders of my own house be silenced—not killed, nothing so clean—simply *set aside*, their voices no longer invited into the conversation, until they faded like letters no one answers. I was afraid of becoming a letter no one answers. So when the heralds came to me, I told them I had seen nothing. I swore it on the Ninth Door itself. I lied on holy ground with a holy oath in my mouth, and the inquiry closed, and the covenant died, and I lived." She laughed, short and bitter, a sound like a stick breaking. "Oh, I flourished, child. I rose. I became Mother Sable, keeper of keys, and for sixty years I kept the secret folded up inside my robes like a stone I had grown around, the way an oyster grows around grit. And when Veyran came and stripped our orders, I told myself it was justice arriving late. I let him take the robes. I thought: *now I am punished*. As if losing a title were punishment. As if exile were penance."

Odile's knife-hand had stilled entirely. "You knew," she said. "All this time on the road—you knew what my grandmother did, what I am, and you said nothing."

"I knew a trader stole from the Court a century ago," Sable said. "I did not know the stolen thing was a person until Caspian confirmed it. But yes. I suspected, when I saw your face by firelight the first night, and saw hers underneath it. Old women see the faces of the people who frightened them, child. They do not forget." She drew a slow breath. "And I said nothing again. Because that is what I do. That is the shape of my whole life: I see, and I am silent, and I call the silence caution, and the caution is just fear wearing its good coat."

"Why tell me now?" Odile asked. Not cruelly. Worse than cruelly—genuinely, with a thief's hunger for the true shape of a thing.

"Because tomorrow we reach the marshes, and after the marshes, the Court. And because you will stand in front of nine empty thrones and learn what must be paid to fill them, and when that hour comes you cannot afford to carry my lie as well as your grandmother's. Lies weigh, child. Even other people's lies. Especially other people's lies—they are the heaviest kind, because you never got to choose them." Sable unfolded her hands at last and let them hang open at her sides, palms out, the old gesture of a priestess offering blessing—or begging it. "So. Here I am. Ninety-nine years late. Confessing on unholy ground to a thief, because there is no one else left to confess to. Judge me as you like. I will wear whatever you hand me. It will fit better than the robes did."

* * *

Odile looked at her for a long time.

The wind moved. The owl called once, far off, and got no answer. Sable stood with her palms open and her heart hammering like a girl's, though her skin was older than most of the trees on the hillside, and she understood suddenly that she would rather have Odile curse her than forgive her cheaply—because forgiveness given easily would be one more silence, one more thing swallowed instead of spoken.

"I should hate you," Odile said finally. "It would be tidy. You let the world break and then you joined the crew repairing it, and nobody checked your hands."

"Yes," Sable agreed. "Tidy hatred. I've earned it."

"But here's the thing." Odile turned the knife over once in her fingers, then slid it home in her belt. "Caspian lied to protect his mission. He lied because the Court sent him and the mission mattered more than I did. That's the wound that won't close yet—I don't know how to be chosen for a *purpose*, Sable. I only know how to be used for one. But you—" She paused, and Sable watched her search for the words like a lockpick searching a keyhole. "You weren't protecting anything when you stayed silent. You were just scared. Seventeen and scared, and then old and scared, and the fear calcified. That's not better. But it's *human*. And I have spent my whole life taking memories from people, and I know the difference between a lie built like a fortress and a lie built like a hiding place. Fortresses are meant to keep me out. Hiding places are meant to keep the liar safe from themselves." She exhaled. "You built a hiding place. And tonight you walked out of it. That counts. I don't know how much it counts, but it counts."

"Odile—"

"No. Let me finish, because I'll lose my nerve." Odile stepped closer, close enough that Sable could see the firelight reflected doubled in her eyes. "I can't forgive Caspian yet. Maybe not ever, maybe not till the thrones are full, I don't know. But you—I can give you this, and I mean it: I believe you're sorry. I believe you'd take it back if the world offered returns. And I think—" her voice roughened, "—I think you've punished yourself harder than any defrocking ever could. Ninety-nine years carrying a wheel-marked jar in your chest. Mother Sable. You've been doing penance since before my mother was born. So yes. You're forgiven. Not because it's deserved. Because *I choose it*. Isn't that what you preach—the covenant isn't owed, it's chosen, freely, again each morning?"

Sable felt something crack in her chest that was not grief, or was grief in its final form, the husk splitting. "Yes," she whispered. "That is exactly what I preach. I had forgotten anyone listened."

"Well." Odile's mouth twitched, the ghost of her thief's smile, the one that meant she was covering something tender with competence. "Somebody had to steal the sermons, since you never gave any."

The laugh that broke out of Sable startled them both—wet and cracked and real, an old woman's laugh with a century of sediment in it. She reached out and took Odile's hands in her two dry ones, and pressed them, priest-fashion, between her palms.

"Then hear an old sinner's counsel, freely given and worth what you pay for it," she said. "When you reach the Court, they will ask you for a payment you will not want to make. The heralds' fragments still whisper there—I have heard them in dreams. And when that moment comes, remember this churchyard. Remember that a thing can be given up and still be yours—changed, but yours. Death and transformation are the same door, child. I walked through it ninety-nine years ago when I chose silence, and I have been walking back toward it ever since, and tonight I finally arrived." She squeezed Odile's hands once, hard, and released them. "Choose your door with open eyes. That is all any of us can do."

* * *

They walked back toward the chapel together, and Sable noted, with an old woman's quiet satisfaction, that Odile matched her slow pace without being asked, which was its own small covenant.

Inside, the fire had been fed; Brannic looked up from his post and nodded once, having heard, Sable suspected, every word—his gift did not respect walls or discretion. Wick stirred in their nest of cloak and blinked at them both with the frank, unguarded stare of someone with no past to hide behind. Even Caspian raised his head across the embers, and his eyes went from Sable's wet cheeks to Odile's steady ones, and something in his face shifted—hope, perhaps, or the beginning of it, cautious as a stray approaching a lit doorway.

But it was Odile who stopped, mid-chapel, and turned back to Sable with a frown gathering between her brows.

"Sable," she said slowly. "When the heralds came asking questions—a century ago. After the theft. Did anyone else in your order see the trader enter the vault? Anyone besides you?"

Sable went cold. "No," she said. "I was alone that night. Why?"

Odile held up one finger, and in her other hand she produced—from some hidden pocket, with a thief's effortless sleight—a scrap of paper. On it, in cramped ink, was copied a single line from the burned page of Caspian's prophecy book. A line Sable had never seen, because Caspian had fed it to the fire before any of them could read it.

Odile laid it on the chapel's cracked altar stone and turned it toward the light.

The ink was old. The handwriting was unmistakable—it was the same careful, disciplined script Sable had spent sixty years reading in the margins of the House of the Ninth Door's ledgers.

Her own.

"'The novice sees, and swears the empty oath,'" Odile read aloud, her voice very quiet. "'And the covenant waits on her confession.'"

The wind came through the broken roof and turned the page over, and beneath the line, in the same hand, dated a century ago, was written:

*I will tell her when she is ready. —S.*
