## 23. The Ninth Gate

They smelled it before they saw it.

The Ninefold Court, Mother Sable had told them, was built on a hill that drank the sky—a hill so old the mountains around it had learned to bow. What Odile smelled instead was rain that had never fallen anywhere else: cold stone and old honey and something underneath both, faint and wrong, like a lily left too long in a sealed room.

"Perfume," Wick said, wrinkling their nose. "Somebody's been burning perfume."

"Nobody's burned anything here in a hundred years," Brannic said quietly. "That's what a hundred years smells like."

They had climbed since dawn through country that grew stranger with every mile. The war-scarred lowlands behind them—burned farmsteads, refugee columns, the smoke of Verance still a bruise on the southern horizon—gave way to hills where the grass grew in colors grass should not grow: silver-green, wine-dark, one slope of pale blue that Odile refused to look at directly because looking at it made her feel like weeping for reasons she could not name. Broken covenant-magic pooled here like water finding its level, a hundred years of spillage from the wound at the world's heart.

And above all of it, the Court.

It did not look ruined, at first. That was the trap of it. From below, the silhouette against the morning haze was everything the songs promised: nine towers like nine fingers raised to heaven, walls pale as bone, banners—no. Not banners. Odile squinted. The long shapes hanging from the tower-tops were not cloth. They were ivy, dead and silvered, pouring down the stone like frozen waterfalls.

"Close your mouth, thief," Mother Sable said, without heat. "You'll want the breath."

"For what?"

"For what comes next." Sable's face was gray under her travel-stains. She had not slept well since Verance; none of them had, but the old priestess wore her sleeplessness like a penance she'd chosen personally. "I walked these grounds once. I was younger than you are now. And even then, even before the rot spread this far, the gate made my teeth ache."

Odile looked at Caspian. She had been looking at him in small, controlled glances for eleven days—not because she wanted to, but because she could not stop taking his measure, the way a lockpick cannot stop reading a lock. He caught her glance now and held it, steady, offering nothing and refusing nothing. *You knew*, she thought. *You knew what I was before I knew. You knew what my grandmother did.* The anger was still there, banked coals she fed every night when the camp went quiet. But beneath it, worse than the anger, was the terrible persistence of trust—the way her feet kept walking beside his without being told to.

He pointed up the slope. "There."

* * *

The gate was half a mile wide.

That was the first thing Odile understood, standing before it, and the understanding arrived not as sight but as a kind of vertigo, the way the sea makes you dizzy when you finally comprehend how much of it there is. The wall ran out of sight in both directions into the blue-grass hills, and set into its face was a gate—or rather, nine gates, each tall as a cathedral door, each carved from a single slab of some material that was stone and was not stone, each shut.

Eight of them were dead.

Odile felt it the way she felt everything now, with the whole surface of her skin, since leaving the valley: the eight leftmost gates were simply *there*, enormous and inert, like the ribs of an animal that had died standing up. Moss grew on them. A bird had nested in the hinge-crown of the fourth. Whatever had made those doors doors was gone, and had been gone long enough for the world to stop noticing.

But the ninth—

The ninth was on the far right, and it was warm. Odile knew it was warm before she touched it, knew it the way she knew a pocket held coin. The air around it shimmered faintly, like the air over a forge, and when she came within ten paces the stolen memories inside her chest stirred and turned over, a thousand borrowed lives waking like cats hearing a can-opener.

"It knows us," Brannic whispered.

Brannic had stopped twenty paces back. The foundling stood with his head tilted in the attitude Odile had come to know as listening—that slight cant, eyes unfocused, mouth parted—as though the world were a house and he could hear through all its walls. He had heard the memory-wraith in Rotwood three rooms of trees before it found them. He had heard the inquisitors' thoughts through the planks of the Salt Bridge tollhouse, or something close enough to thoughts that he'd gotten them past the toll by a margin of one breath. Now he was hearing whatever a nine-hundred-year-old door said when it dreamed.

"What does it say?" Wick asked. They had crept close to Odile without seeming to move; they did that, always ending up at her elbow as if proximity to her were a place they'd been assigned.

Brannic didn't answer immediately. His brow furrowed deeper. "Not words," he said at last. "Doors don't say words. Doors are all... intention. Wanting-to-open or wanting-to-shut." He swallowed. "This one wants. All of them want, a little, even the dead ones, the way a dead man's hand still wants to hold things. But the ninth—" His voice cracked, and he pressed his palm flat against his own sternum as if holding a door closed inside himself. "The ninth is starving."

The word went through the company like a dropped knife.

"Starving," Sable repeated. "For what?"

"For what was taken from it." Brannic's eyes found Odile. He didn't mean to look at her; she watched him try not to, and fail. "Everything here is starving for the same thing."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the wall itself, which Sable called blasphemy and practicality in the same breath, and Odile spent the afternoon walking the gate's length alone.

She told herself it was scouting. It was not scouting. There was nothing behind them for thirty miles but empty covenant-rotted country, and ahead there was only the wall, and the wall was all there was. She walked because her legs needed to be doing something while her mind did the real work, and the real work was this: standing in front of each of the eight dead gates in turn, laying her palm against the cold not-stone, and feeling—nothing. Truly nothing. Not the flat nothing of the Hollow Valley, which was a living silence, a held breath, a place where magic stood politely outside the door. This was the nothing of a room after the music stops.

Then the ninth gate.

She saved it for last. She made herself save it for last, the way she used to make herself save the best vial in a mark's house for the end of the job, discipline as self-bribery. And when she finally stood before it, close enough to see the carving—

Oh, the carving.

The other gates bore ornament worn smooth by centuries: vines, faces, heraldic beasts, all eroded into suggestion. The ninth was sharp. The ninth looked carved yesterday. Nine trees wound together into a single crown of branches, and beneath them, small enough that you would miss it unless you were a thief who read locks the way priests read scripture, a line of figures: nine tall, and one small figure standing before them, hands open, giving something away.

The small figure had no face. The stone had never given it one.

"You're going to touch it," said Caspian's voice behind her, "and I'd rather you didn't, actually."

She hadn't heard him approach. That was new, and she hated that it was new; for two weeks outside the valley her stolen senses had made her nearly impossible to sneak up on, and here he was, just a man walking across grass, and her skin full of a thousand lives and none of them had noticed him coming. The gate did things to the rules. She was beginning to understand that everything near the Court would do things to the rules.

"Why not?"

"Because the last person who touched it was my grandmother's grandmother," Caspian said, "and she came away with white hair and no voice for a year, and that was when the gate was merely sleeping, not starving." He stopped beside her, an arm's length away—an arm's length he maintained with visible care, the way you keep distance from a fire or from someone you've wounded. "Sable thinks the gate will test whoever approaches. I think she's underselling it. I think the gate has been waiting a hundred years to ask one question, and I think the question is going to cost somebody everything they have."

"The prophecy doesn't mention a gate."

"The prophecy doesn't mention weather either, and yet here we are, cold." He almost smiled. It didn't take. "Odile. If it opens for blood—"

"Sable said *keys*. Brannic said—"

"I know what they said. They're being kind." He looked up at the vast blank face of the ninth gate, and for a moment Odile saw him clearly: not the charming stranger from the valley, not the liar, but something wearier than both, a man carrying a book that had been writing him since before he was born. "Blood isn't a metaphor here. This is the Court of the Ninefold Oath. Everything is literal. Blood means blood. Sacrifice means sacrifice. The covenant was forged in a giving-over, and the covenant will only recognize a giving-over." He turned to her. "And you are the only one of us whose blood it could possibly want."

The wind moved through the blue grass, making a sound like distant applause.

"And if I give it?" Odile said. "What does it take? My blood? A cupful? A vein?" She kept her voice light, thief's patter, the tone that made marks underestimate her. Inside, the stolen memories had begun to press against the walls of her, restless, the way animals press against a barn before a storm.

Caspian was quiet a long moment.

"I honestly don't know," he said. "That's the truth, and I know it's worth less than it used to be." He held her gaze. "But I'll stand next to you when you find out. However much that's worth."

She turned away first. It was a small victory. She took it.

* * *

Night came down on the wall like a lid.

They built no fire—Sable forbade it; light drew wraiths, and wraiths drew inquisitors, and somewhere behind them Inquisitor Maren Voss was still hunting, half-mad, carrying the hole Odile had torn in her. So they sat in the dark under the ninth gate's faint warmth, sharing cold bread and colder water, and Odile listened to her family breathe.

*Family.* The word still startled her when it arrived uninvited. Eleven days ago it had been Sable and Wick and Brannic and, reluctantly, Caspian: assets, companions, a company. Somewhere between Rotwood and Salt Bridge and the terrible road after, it had become *them*, and she had become part of the them, and she had not noticed the exact moment any more than you notice the moment you fall asleep.

Wick was asleep sitting up, chin on chest, the way they always slept, as if napping were a skill they hadn't fully trusted yet. Odile remembered—would always remember, whatever the future took—the night on the river barge when Wick had confessed about the missing years, the six-year-old child at the center of their life with no road leading back to it. *Never take mine,* they'd said. *Even if it would help. Promise me.* And she had promised, and meant it, and filed away in the same dark drawer the knowledge that the mission might someday force the promise and the mission into the same room.

Brannic wasn't sleeping. Brannic was sitting closest to the gate, knees drawn up, listening.

"Tell me again," Odile said softly, settling beside him.

"There's not more to tell. There's just... more of it." He rubbed his arms. "Nine doors. Eight asleep. One awake. But it's not like the toll-house door, or the vault door in Verance. Those doors are *things*. This one's more like..." He searched. "Like a person who's been lying very still for a hundred years, pretending to be furniture, so nobody throws them out."

Odile looked up at the gate. At the faceless little figure with its open hands.

"Does it hear us?"

"Oh, yes." Brannic's whisper was certain. "It's been hearing us since the ridge. Maybe since Bellharbor. Maybe since the valley." He paused. "It likes Wick's laugh. I don't know how I know that. Doors don't like things. But it does."

From the darkness on her other side, Sable spoke, and Odile flinched; the old woman could still move like smoke when she chose. "In the old rite," Sable said, "the gate was opened every solstice. The heir of each house brought a gift-memory—a true one, freely surrendered—and poured it into the threshold. That was the key, in the days before keys. Not blood in the veins. Blood in the sense of *cost*. The gate opened for what you could not bear to lose." Her voice roughened. "I stood at this gate as a novice, the last year it opened. I watched the Lady of the Autumn House give up her memory of her own wedding day. She smiled the whole time. She was smiling because it was hers to give, and giving it proved it had been hers." Sable's hands folded, unfolded. "I have wondered for sixty years whether I would have had the courage. I am about to find out if any of us do."

Silence. Then, from the bedroll, Wick's voice, thick with sleep: "If it needs laughing, it can have mine. Brannic says it likes my laugh."

"Go back to sleep," Odile said, and her voice came out gentler than she'd planned.

* * *

She woke before dawn because the gate was singing.

Not loudly. Not even musically, exactly—it was a sound like wind through a bottle, like a finger round a wet glass rim, one note held so long it became the silence's spine. But everyone was awake within moments, all four of them, standing in the gray pre-light with their breath steaming, staring up at the ninth gate.

The carvings were moving.

Slowly, slowly, the nine trees of the crown were swaying, stone leaves stirring in a stone wind, and the line of figures beneath—the nine tall ones—had shifted, turning inward toward the small faceless figure at the center. And the warmth that had come off the gate like forge-air now pulsed, slow and vast, a heartbeat with a hundred-year pause between beats.

"It's asking," Brannic whispered. Tears were running freely down his face; he didn't seem aware of them. "It's been asking all along. We just couldn't hear the question."

"What question?" Wick's hand had found Odile's sleeve without either of them deciding it.

Brannic turned to Odile. Sable turned to Odile. Even Caspian, standing slightly apart as he had stood for eleven days, turned—and did not look away.

"Whose," Brannic said, "is the blood that broke it."

The gate's note deepened. The pulse quickened. And in Odile's chest, beneath her ribs, beneath her heart, the hoard woke all at once—a thousand stolen mornings, a thousand borrowed weddings and first snows and lullabies, pressing outward like water against a dam—and among them, for the first time, something that was not stolen at all. Something that was hers, that had always been hers, sleeping in her blood the way the heir slept in the prophecy: a memory older than herself, patient as stone, beginning to stir.

The ninth gate waited, warm as an open hand.

And Odile stepped forward.
