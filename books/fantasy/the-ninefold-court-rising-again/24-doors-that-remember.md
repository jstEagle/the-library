## 24. Doors That Remember

Brannic had heard doors since before he had words for them.

Not the creak of hinges or the groan of old wood—anyone could hear those. He heard what doors *meant*. A door was a held breath, a decision made of timber and iron, a line drawn through the world that said *this side* and *that side* and *never the twain*. Doors sang to him the way his mother must have sung lullabies to other children, though he had no memory of her voice, only of hers: the low hum of the foundling-house gate in Bellharbor, the bright chatter of cabinet doors in Mother Sable's chapel-kitchen, the long, mournful drone of the sea-doors at the harbor's mouth that opened twice a day and wept salt the whole time.

Magic lived in thresholds. That was the first thing he'd ever understood about himself, before he understood he was different—before Wick taught him the word *fae-blooded* and spat it like an insult until Odile made them apologize. Magic pooled in doorways like water in the low places, and Brannic had been born standing ankle-deep in it.

Now he stood before a door so vast it did not seem like a door at all.

* * *

The Ninth Gate filled the end of the valley like a cliff face that had decided, somewhere in its ancient anger, to become architecture. Black stone shot through with veins of silver that pulsed—slowly, arrhythmically, like a heart that had forgotten its proper rhythm over a century of neglect. The gate was nine doors wide if it was one door at all: nine arches carved into the living rock, each tall as a cathedral spire laid on its side, each sealed with stone that matched the mountain so perfectly there was no seam, no hinge, no handle. No way through that any lockpick in the Nine Realms could argue with.

Eight of the nine doors were dead. Brannic had known it from half a mile off—the silence of them, the flat nothing where a threshold should have sung. Eight graves dressed up as gates.

But the ninth—

The ninth was still breathing.

He heard it even now, standing at the company's fire while the others argued around the map Sable had unrolled across a flat stone. A sound beneath sound, felt more than heard, the way you feel your own pulse when everything else goes quiet. The ninth door was whispering to itself in a language older than names, repeating something over and over with the patience of stone:

*Who comes? Who comes? Who comes?*

"Blood," Mother Sable said, tapping a line of faded ink on her map. "The chronicles are clear enough on that much. When the Court sealed itself after the Breaking, the gates were warded to open for the heir's bloodline. Keys were considered and rejected—a key can be stolen." Her eyes flicked to Odile when she said it, and Odile had the grace not to smile. "A key can be copied. Blood cannot—at least, that was the thinking a hundred years ago."

"And the heir is me," Odile said flatly. "Which we've established. Which everyone keeps saying as though repeating it will make me feel better about walking up to a door that eats people."

"The doors don't eat people," Sable said.

"You don't know that."

"No," Sable admitted. "I don't know that. I know that three expeditions reached this valley during my lifetime, and none came back. I know that the Court has been dying by inches for a century, and desperate things guard desperate treasures." She rolled up the map with a snap. "I also know we have perhaps two days before Veyran's vanguard crests the Salt Hills, and no other road leads here. So we knock."

Brannic said nothing. He was listening.

The others had learned to read his silences by now—Odile especially, who had once told him, in the gruff way she delivered anything kind, that he thought out loud the way other people muttered. Wick was sharpening their knives with the particular aggressive rhythm they used when they were scared and wanted nobody to know it. Caspian stood apart, at the edge of the firelight, wearing the expression he'd worn ever since Odile caught him burning that page: present, useful, and fundamentally unwelcome, a man serving a sentence of his own sentencing.

Brannic listened past all of them, down into the dark under the world, to the ninth door whispering *who comes, who comes.*

And underneath the whisper—faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall—he heard something else.

A wrongness. A second voice, thin and hungry, coiled around the threshold like wire around a wrist.

* * *

They went up to the Gate at dawn, because Sable said wards answered best to morning light and Wick said dawn was when sentries slept, and because none of them said aloud what they were all thinking, which was that they might not have a dusk left to waste.

Up close, the Gate was worse. Better, too—but worse first. The black stone drank the morning instead of reflecting it, and the silver veins ran through it like frost across a window, branching and rebranching into patterns that hurt to follow with the eye. Brannic could feel the whole structure humming against the soles of his feet, a deep tectonic chord, and threaded through it the eighth dead doors sat like eight stopped clocks.

He put his palm flat against the cold of the nearest dead arch, just to be sure.

Nothing. Not even echo. Whatever had once lived behind those doors had either fled or been devoured, and the thresholds were empty as pulled teeth.

"It's the ninth," he said. His voice came out rough; he hadn't spoken yet that morning. "The others are gone. But the ninth—" He hesitated. He had learned, over the long road from Bellharbor, that the hardest part of hearing things no one else could hear was deciding how much truth the others could carry. On Rotwood he had waited too long to speak of the memory-wraith, and Wick had nearly paid for his caution. He didn't wait now. "There's something on it. Something waiting. It's been waiting a long time and it's very hungry."

"What kind of something?" Odile asked. She'd come to stand beside him, close enough that he could see the sleepless bruising under her eyes. Since the thrones' secret and the prophecy's price, she slept like a cat—one ear always cocked for the trap.

"A warding," Brannic said slowly, feeling for the shape of it the way you'd feel for the shape of a knot in the dark. "Or the corpse of one. Wards are doors too, in a way. They have a voice. This one's voice is... frayed. Like a rope that's held a hanging." He met her eyes. "It wants a password, and the password is blood, and I don't think it cares whose."

Sable had gone very pale. "Then we do this carefully. Odile opens a vein, lets a few drops onto the threshold, and—"

"It won't be enough."

Everyone turned. Brannic realized the words had come from his own mouth, in that same rough voice, with a certainty he hadn't authorized.

"How do you know?" Odile asked quietly.

Because he could hear it. Because he had spent his whole life listening to doors and he knew, the way sailors know weather, what a threshold sounded like when it was about to break its word. The ninth gate wasn't asking for a taste of blood. It was asking for a covenant—and covenants, as everyone in this miserable company had learned by now, were paid for whole.

"The ward is starving," he said. "It's been holding this door shut for a hundred years with nothing to feed on, and the Court behind it has been leaking magic like a gutted boat leaks water. It needs more than drops. It needs—" He searched for the word and hated the word when he found it. "*Anchorage.* Something bound to the threshold. Something that belongs to a door, given to the door, forever."

"Meaning what, exactly?" Wick demanded. They had their knives out now, actually out, which they did when talking failed them. "Meaning somebody stays? Meaning somebody dies? Say it plain, Brannic."

"I don't know," Brannic said, and that was the worst part. "I just know what I'm hearing."

* * *

They argued for most of the morning. Sable wanted to try the drops of blood first—*we test, then we commit*—and Odile agreed, because Odile would have agreed to anything that postponed sacrifice, having already been told she owed the largest one. Caspian volunteered himself three separate times and was refused three separate times, though Brannic noticed, with the strange double-vision of someone who heard both words and thresholds, that the ward's hunger bent toward Caspian's offers without ever quite taking hold of them. Wrong blood. Wrong nature. The gate didn't want a courtier's devotion.

It wanted a door-keeper.

By midday Brannic had walked the full length of the Gate alone, trailing his fingers along the stone, listening so hard that the world's ordinary sounds fell away from him entirely—the wind, the birds, Wick pacing and muttering, Odile and Sable's low conference. There was only the ninth door, and the starving thing wrapped around it, and beneath them both the vast slow breath of the Court itself, sleeping its ruined sleep.

He had heard ten thousand doors in his life, and he had never once heard one like this.

This door remembered.

That was the only way he could think of it. Ordinary doors forgot the moment they closed—they existed in the opening, in the passing-through, and between passages they were just wood and iron, dumb and patient. But this gate had been remembering for a century. It remembered every oath sworn before it. It remembered the last heir's small handprint, pressed into the stone at shoulder-height of a child, worn smooth by centuries of touching. It remembered the night the Court sealed itself, and it had been singing that memory to the empty valley ever since, over and over, a song with no audience:

*I held. I held. I held. Who comes?*

And the starving ward, wound around it like a starving dog around a dying master, snarling at every approach: *prove it. prove it. prove you'll hold too.*

Brannic took his hand off the stone.

His palm had gone numb. He looked at it without surprise. He had suspected since dawn, in the place where knowing lives before words do, what the price would be. You didn't give a door "anchorage" with blood alone. Blood opened accounts; it didn't keep them. To anchor a threshold—to *be* its keeper—you gave it what you carried that was door-shaped. And what Brannic carried that was door-shaped was everything. The gift itself. The hearing. The lifelong second language of thresholds and hinges and held breaths.

Give it, and the gate would open.

Give it, and he would never hear another door again as long as he lived—not the sea-doors of Bellharbor, not the foundling-house gate, not the soft click of Wick picking a lock or the chapel-kitchen cabinets of Mother Sable's kitchen. He would walk through the world's thresholds as deaf to them as any ordinary man, forever, and some part of him—the part that had been six years old and terrified in the dark of the foundling house until he realized he could hear the front gate singing him company—that part would simply die.

He stood there a long time.

* * *

He didn't make a speech. He'd watched enough of these people make speeches to know speeches were how you talked yourself into things you weren't sure of.

He walked back down to where they'd gathered—Odile and Sable at the map-stone, Wick whittling a stick into nothing, Caspian standing his usual exile's distance away—and he said, "I know how to open it."

Four faces turned to him.

"The ward doesn't want blood. Blood's just how it checks the paperwork." He kept his voice level. He'd practiced. "It wants a keeper. Someone bound to the threshold. Someone who gives it—" he turned his hand over, palm up, the numb palm— "the hearing. My gift. All of it. Permanently."

The silence that followed had weight and texture. He heard Wick's knife stop moving. He heard Odile's breath catch and hold.

"No," Odile said.

"It's my gift to give."

"I said no. We find another way."

"There isn't one." He almost smiled. "I've been listening to this door since yesterday, Odile. You steal memories. I hear doors. It's the only magic I've got, and it turns out it's exactly the coin this toll wants." He looked around at them, at the ragged impossible family the road had stitched together out of exiles and thieves and defrocked priests, and he found, to his own surprise, that he wasn't afraid. Or he was afraid, but the fear was small and sitting quietly in a corner, the way fear does when it knows it's going to lose. "You should understand what you're all refusing to let me do. This gate has been holding for a hundred years on nothing. If we turn back now, Veyran takes the Court, takes whatever's left of the fae lords, and the war is over by winter. If we go through, we might save something. I'm not the heir. I'm not the priestess. I'm not your prophet or your thief. I'm the one who hears doors." He shrugged. "Let me do the one thing I'm for."

"Brannic." Odile's voice cracked straight down the middle. "You'll never hear them again. Any of them. You told me once the harbor gates sang you to sleep. You'll be—"

"Deaf to magic. Not deaf." He touched his ears, and this time he did smile. "I'll still hear you all complaining about my cooking. I'll still hear Wick lie about winning at cards. I'll still hear Caspian apologizing, eventually, when he finally works up to it." Caspian flinched; Brannic hadn't meant it as mercy, exactly, but he saw it land like mercy, and let it stand. "I lose one sense of the world. The Court loses everything if we fail. That's not a hard sum, Odile. You taught us all how to count costs on the road here. Let me pay one."

Wick stood up. They crossed the space between them and punched Brannic in the arm, hard enough to bruise, which among the people gathered in that stony valley was a sacrament.

"If you do this," Wick said, and their voice was fierce and wet at once, "you're doing it as family. Not as the useful one. As family. Swear it."

"As family," Brannic said.

Mother Sable bowed her head over her hands and murmured something in the old liturgical cadence, the prayer of oblation, the giving-over. Even defrocked, even burned-robed, her prayers still had their music.

Odile looked at him for a long moment. Then she stepped forward and pressed her forehead to his, briefly, the way you'd greet a brother.

"When it's done," she whispered, "you tell me what the silence sounds like. I'll want to know everything."

* * *

They climbed to the Gate together as the afternoon light went long and gold.

The ninth arch waited, silver veins pulsing its slow broken rhythm. Up close, Brannic could hear the ward's hunger clearly now, could hear how far past reason it had frayed—a century of watching, a century of no one coming, a century of *who comes* asked to the empty air.

He laid both palms against the stone.

The stone was warm. After all that black cold, impossibly, it was warm as a living cheek, and he understood suddenly that the door had been waiting to be touched kindly for a hundred years.

*Here,* he said—not in words, in the language he'd been born fluent in, the tongue of thresholds. *Here is a keeper. Take what I carry. Hold as I held.*

The ward rose against him like a tide.

It did not ask permission. Nothing that starves for a century asks permission. It poured into him through his palms, through his skin, into the marrow of the gift itself, and Brannic braced and did not pull away as it began to drink. It took the harbor-gates first, their salt-weeping song, and he grieved them. It took the foundling-house gate, the first friend of his life, and he grieved it harder. It took the thousand small voices he had never told anyone about—cabinet doors and cellar hatches, garden gates and grave-yard lychgates, the great humming sea-doors and the tiny shy doors of birdhouses—and it took them all, and with each one a little of the world's hidden music went out of him like rooms going dark in a house at evening.

The last thing he heard, as the silence rose to meet him, was the ninth door itself.

It stopped whispering *who comes.*

It said—warm, vast, grateful, in the voice of something ancient that had finally been relieved of watch—*Welcome home, keeper.*

Then the gate opened, and Brannic Ravensworth's company passed through into the dark of the Ninefold Court, and Brannic went with them, hearing nothing at all.

He felt the threshold in his bones one final time—a shudder, a sigh, a door closing gently behind them like a blanket drawn up over a child.

And then, ahead, in the blackness beyond, a light no one had kindled began to grow—and with it, faint and terrible, the sound of a throne room remembering it had a king.
