## 25. The Empty Thrones

The gate closed behind them with a sound like a book shutting.

Odile felt it in her teeth first—a soft, final click, as though the mountain had swallowed them and was deciding whether to taste. Then the sound reached her ears: a deep, resonant boom that rolled away through stone and did not echo. Sound did not echo here. She noticed that within the first thirty steps, and it frightened her more than the wraiths of Rotwood ever had, because a place where echoes died was a place that ate things.

"Everyone breathe," Mother Sable said quietly. "Breathe slowly. The air is thin in the old ways."

It wasn't thin in the way of altitude or smoke. It was thin the way an unused room is thin—dust and stillness layered over something that once moved constantly. Odile drew a breath and tasted lilies and cold iron and, beneath both, a sweetness like fruit going soft on a windowsill. Perfume left behind by people a hundred years gone.

They stood at the mouth of a corridor so vast it made the word *corridor* absurd. Pale stone stretched ahead of them, veined with silver that might have been metal or might have been frozen moonlight, rising in gentle terraces toward a light source Odile could not name—there were no torches, no windows, no lamps, and yet the air itself glowed, a pale pearlescence that came from everywhere and nowhere, casting shadows that fell in nine directions at once.

Brannic stopped walking. His head tilted, the way it always did when he heard doors, but this time his face went strange—not focused but lost, like a man listening to an orchestra after years of silence and finding half the instruments missing.

"There are more than nine," he whispered. "There are hundreds. Thousands. Doors everywhere. But they're all—" He swallowed. "They're all unlocked. They've been unlocked for so long they've forgotten how to be shut. A door that can't close is just a hole."

"A hundred years of holes," Sable murmured. "A covenant is a door that swings both ways, child. Mortal to fae and fae to mortal. When it broke, every threshold between the realms became a wound."

Wick pressed close against Odile's side, and she felt their small hand find hers without looking, the way they'd learned to do on the road through Rotwood. They said nothing. Wick never said much when fear got big enough; words cost too much when you only had six years' worth of memories to spend. But their grip tightened, and that was its own sentence.

*Keep walking,* Odile told herself. *You've walked into worse rooms.* Except she hadn't. She had stolen from dying men in cozy ferry-cabins and slipped through the markets of Bellharbor while an inquisitor turned a woman into ash, and none of it had prepared her for the particular horror of a palace that was still breathing.

Because it was breathing. She could feel it now, the way she'd felt magic return at the valley's border—that prickle along her arms, the stirring of her thousand stolen memories like fish waking in a dark pond. The Court was alive in the way a body on a bier is alive: heart slowing, breath shallow, warmth lingering out of habit. Something enormous was dying here, and it had been dying for a century, patiently, magnificently, refusing to finish.

"The thrones will be at the center," Caspian said. His voice sounded wrong—too careful, too flat—and Odile remembered, with a fresh twist of anger, all the things he hadn't told her. He walked slightly ahead, the way he had since the gate, and she wondered if he was leading them or if he simply couldn't bear to look back at their faces. "Follow the terraces up. And whatever you see—whatever you *see*—"

He didn't finish. Perhaps he didn't know how.

* * *

The first husk was standing at a balustrade, watching nothing.

Odile nearly screamed before she understood what she was seeing. It had the shape of a fae lord—or what she imagined a fae lord should have, tall and terrible and lovely the way winter is lovely—but the loveliness had gone hollow, like a mask left out through too many seasons. Its skin was the grey-white of birch bark. Its eyes were open and fixed on the middle distance, and they were beautiful eyes, silver-shot, ancient, and utterly empty. Not blind. Worse than blind. Eyes that had forgotten there was anything to see.

It wore robes of state. That was the detail that broke something in her chest—the robes were magnificent, woven with scenes she couldn't read anymore, hemmed in gold thread gone green with age, and whoever this had been had dressed for a ceremony, a coronation, a covenant-day, and then simply... stopped. Mid-majesty. A century ago.

"It's not dead," Brannic breathed. He had crept closer than any of them dared, drawn perhaps by the door-sense in him, and his hand hovered near the creature's sleeve without touching. "I can hear it. There's a door in it, same as anyone. But the door's standing open onto nothing. There's no room behind it." His voice cracked. "Someone took everything out of the house and left the walls."

"The Court's memory," Odile said, and her own voice came out hoarse. "When Grandmother stole it—when the heir's memory was taken—" She stopped. Saying it aloud still felt like handling broken glass. *My bloodline did this. My grandmother's hands. My inheritance.*

"Not just the theft." Mother Sable had gone very pale beneath her weathered skin, and she gripped her walking staff like a weapon or a prayer. "The theft broke the covenant. The broken covenant cut off the flow between realms. Magic is memory here, child—it always has been. The fae don't store their pasts the way we do, in flesh and forgetting. They *are* their pasts, continuously, the way a river is its water. Cut the river off from its source and..." She gestured at the figure at the balustrade, helpless, furious, grieving. "This. This is what a century without covenant looks like. Not death. *Drainage.*"

More of them appeared as they climbed. That was the only word Odie could make herself use—appeared—because they didn't move, exactly. They occupied positions. One knelt in a garden court where fountains had run dry and moss had claimed the basin, hands cupped as if waiting to catch water that would never fall. One stood at a writing desk, quill lifted over a page blank for a hundred years, ink long since crusted black in the well. Two sat together on a bench facing a view of mountains through a window of unclouded crystal, shoulder to shoulder, one with an arm extended along the bench's back as though it had once rested around the other.

That one undid Wick. The boy—they'd told her once, in the dark outside Salt Bridge, that they thought they'd been a boy, though they weren't sure—stopped dead and stared, and Odile saw them doing arithmetic with their face, counting the emptiness in those two seated figures against the emptiness inside themselves.

"Wick," she said softly.

"I'm fine." Their voice was steady. Too steady. "I just—I know what's missing in them. That's all. I know exactly what's missing, because I've been missing it my whole life." They looked up at her, and their jaw set with a ferocity that belonged to someone much older. "Don't you dare feel sorry for me right now. Feel sorry for *them*. At least I got to build new things. They're stuck holding space for old ones that got poured out."

She wanted to argue. She didn't. There was a terrible wisdom in the child sometimes that shamed all of them.

Caspian walked faster than any of them, and Odile watched him carefully. He knew these halls. She was certain of it now—not from prophecy, not from study, but from the way his feet hesitated at turns and then chose correctly anyway. Muscle memory, or something older than muscle. Once, passing a doorway hung with tattered banners, he reached out and touched the frame—just briefly, two fingers, the way you'd touch a grave-marker—and Odile pretended not to see, and filed it away with all the other pieces of him she was keeping in the locked room of her anger.

*You'll deal with him later,* she told herself, as she had been telling herself since the burned page. *Survive first. Understand second. Forgive—*

She didn't finish that one either.

* * *

They climbed through terraces that had once, clearly, been gardens. Odile could read the bones of them: trellises of silver where climbing roses must have grown, beds laid out in patterns that hurt to look at because the geometry kept trying to mean something and failing, like a word in a language you almost remembered. Everything here was built to be meaningful. Every arch sang a chord when you passed beneath it—she felt the notes against her sternum, faint, detuned, a choir that had lost its music but not its instinct for harmony.

And everywhere, the husks.

By the time they reached the upper galleries, Odile had stopped counting. Hundreds. Maybe thousands. Fae lords and ladies, soldiers and singers, children—*children*, small figures frozen mid-game with wooden toys in their hands, and Odile had to walk past those quickly, jaw clenched, because her rule rose up in her like a reflex: *never steal pain.* But there was no pain here to steal. That was the horror. Pain would have meant someone home to feel it. These were vessels with the wine long spilled, and if she pressed her gift against them she would touch nothing but smooth, clean absence, polished by a century of nothing happening inside.

Except.

Near the top of the final stair, one of them turned its head.

Odile froze. So did the others. The movement was slow, grinding, like a hinge in a door long unopened—and maybe that was exactly what it was, because Brannic made a wounded noise beside her, one hand flying to his ear.

"It heard us," he whispered. "Its door—something knocked on its door. From inside. Something's still *in there*, just barely, just—"

The husk's silver eyes found Odile, and in them, deep down, at the very bottom like a coin at the bottom of a well, something flickered. Recognition? Hope? Hunger? She couldn't name it. It raised one hand—not reaching for her, she realized, but pointing, a gesture of terrible formality, arm shaking with the effort of a century's disuse—and its lips parted around a voice like wind through a ruined flute.

"*...the... thirteenth... hour...*"

Then the flicker guttered and died, and the arm sank, and the eyes went back to the middle distance, and Odile stood shaking on the stairs with her heart slamming against her ribs.

"What did it say?" Wick demanded. "What does that mean?"

"I don't know." Her voice betrayed her. "Sable?"

The old priestess was staring at the creature with an expression Odile had never seen on her—not fury, not grief, but a kind of appalled recognition, as though she'd met a ghost who'd greeted her by a name she'd buried. "The thirteenth hour," Sable repeated slowly. "In the liturgies of the old covenant, the twelve houses kept twelve hours of observance. But the texts spoke of a thirteenth hour—an hour outside the count. The hour of the *returning*. The hour when what was lost comes home." Her eyes moved to Odile, and there was no accusation in them, which somehow made it worse. Only awe, and dread, and pity braided together. "Child. I think you were just announced."

Nobody spoke. Somewhere far below them, in the drowned halls of the Court, water dripped into a dry fountain, one drop at a time, patient as a countdown.

* * *

The doors to the throne hall were the largest Odile had ever seen—each one carved from a single slab of white wood that gleamed faintly, grown rather than hewn, she was sure of it, because the grain spiraled outward from the center in patterns like fingerprints. Nine sets of doors, nine entrances, converging on a single antechamber. Brannic laid his palm against the nearest and listened for a long moment, eyes closed.

"These ones remember," he said finally. "These doors are still proud. They want to open." He glanced back at the company—at Sable with her staff, at Wick pressed to Odile's shadow, at Caspian with his face like a closed ledger. "They've been waiting a hundred years for someone worth letting in."

"Then let's not keep royalty waiting," Odile said, and was surprised to find that her voice could still carry irony. It was the last thing she had left that felt like herself.

Brannic pushed, and the doors swung inward without a sound, and the throne hall opened before them like the inside of a pearl.

It was vast beyond sense. The ceiling was lost in luminous haze, supported—if *supported* was even the word—by pillars that rose like trees, each one different, each one singing its faint detuned chord as their footsteps crossed the threshold. Light pooled on the floor in great soft discs. Banners hung motionless in air that had forgotten how to move. And at the far end, on a dais of nine ascending steps, stood the thrones.

Nine of them. No two alike.

One was wrought of living branches, silver-barked, budding even now with tiny white flowers that opened and closed in a rhythm too slow to be called breathing. One was a mass of dark iron shaped like interlocking chains, beautiful and brutal. One seemed to be made entirely of water held in place by sheer stubbornness, rippling gently though nothing touched it. One was carved from a single block of amber, and something small and winged was suspended inside it, mid-flight, preserved for a century. One was bone-pale coral. One was a heap of autumn leaves that had not decayed. One was woven moonlight. One was a door, upright, closed, its handle worn bright by ten thousand hands. And the ninth—

The ninth was empty in a way the others weren't.

Not bare—*vacant*. The other eight had the dignity of objects still serving their purpose, monuments awaiting tenants. But the ninth throne sat at the center, highest, closest to the sourceless light, and it had the look of a place where someone had *just* risen. A cushion still bore the impression of weight. An armrest gleamed where a hand had polished it over centuries. It faced the hall like an open question, and Odile's legs carried her toward it before she decided to move, and Sable's hand on her arm stopped her three steps from the dais.

"Don't," the priestess breathed. "Not until we know what sitting means here."

Odile nodded, but her eyes wouldn't leave the ninth throne, and something in her chest ached with a homesickness so violent and so impossible—for a place she'd never been, a seat she'd never known—that she had to press her knuckles to her mouth to keep it contained. *The heir's own memory of being heir.* The herald-fragment's words hadn't come yet, but she could feel them coming, feel the shape of the trap closing like weather on the horizon. Whatever she had inherited, she was standing in it now. The room knew her. The pillars' chords shifted subtly as she moved, resolving, trying to tune themselves around her presence, and failing, because the note they needed was the one her grandmother had stolen.

"They're not all empty," Caspian said suddenly.

His voice rang strangely in the vastness. He was staring out across the hall—not at the thrones but at the shadows flanking them, the deep alcoves where the pillars met the walls. Odile followed his gaze and her stomach dropped.

They were everywhere. Dozens of them. Standing vigil around the throne dais in ranks, armored in plate that had gone dull as pewter, helms under which no light showed, spears grounded with points down. The honor guard of the Ninefold Court, still at their posts. Still faithful. Still *empty*.

One of them, at the base of the dais, had fallen to one knee at some point in the last century and simply remained there, kneeling, spear still gripped, guarding a throne whose owner had never come back.

"Oh," Wick said softly. Just that. *Oh.* The smallest syllable carrying the whole weight of it.

Mother Sable walked forward alone, slow, her staff clicking on the pearl floor, and stopped before the kneeling guard, and did something Odile did not expect. The defrocked priestess, who had burned her robes in Verance and cursed the gods of two realms, gathered the frayed hem of her traveling cloak and inclined her head—deeply, formally, the bow of one clergyman to another's faith.

"You kept your watch," she said, and her voice carried to every corner of the hall, and the pillars caught it and echoed it for the first time since they'd entered—sound moving again, as if the hall remembered courtesy in the presence of devotion. "Whoever you were. Whatever you were. You kept it. May the thirteenth hour find you, and may it be kind."

Nothing answered. Nothing moved. But Odile would have sworn, then and forever, that the faint chords of the pillars resolved—one single moment of perfect harmony, nine notes finding each other—and then slid apart again, dissonant, unfinished.

A chord waiting for its final note.

She was still standing there, throat tight, when Brannic went rigid all over, his head snapping toward the far end of the hall, toward the shadowed gallery behind the nine thrones.

"Odile," he whispered. "There's another door. Behind the thrones. A little one, hidden, low—I never heard it before, it was silent, it was *nothing*—" His eyes were huge. "Something just knocked. From the other side."

From the darkness beyond the dais, slow and deliberate, came the sound of small mechanical feet. A whirring, a clicking, a creak of ancient joints. And out of the shadow, dragging one leg, trailing moth-eaten velvet, a figure tottered into the light of the throne hall—bent, tarnished, half its face a mask of tarnished silver and the other half simply gone—and fixed its remaining eye upon Odile Eastwick, Covenant-Bearer, thief, heir, and spoke in a voice like a music box remembering a song:

"*At last. At last. The Court has waited so long to be introduced.*"
