## 26. The Court's Last Voice

The husks did not sleep.

That was the first thing Odile understood as she walked the long colonnade of the Ninefold Court's inner hall—they simply stopped. They stood where they had stood when the memory left them, some with hands half-raised, some turned toward conversations no one would ever finish, and a century of dust had settled over them like snow that never melted. Their eyes were open. Their eyes had always been open. She made herself look at one—a tall figure in robes gone gray as ash, its mouth parted mid-word—and felt her stolen memories stir against the walls of her skull like birds in a shuttered room, all of them wanting out, or wanting in, she could not tell which.

Behind her, Mother Sable murmured a prayer in a language older than either of their griefs. Brannic walked with his head tilted, listening to doors that had nothing left to say. Wick stayed close enough that their shoulder brushed Odile's arm every few steps, and she found she was grateful for it in a way that frightened her more than the husks did.

The hall itself was still beautiful, and that was the cruelest part. The pillars were carved from a stone the color of moonlit water, and though the covenant was broken, something in the masonry remembered how to hold light—thin, sourceless luminescence pooling along the floor like spilled milk. Banners hung in rotted swags from the vaulting, nine of them, one for each house, their sigils faded to ghosts of themselves: the stag, the wave, the lantern, the thorn, the moth. She counted them twice because her hands needed something to do.

At the far end of the hall, before the first of the nine thrones, stood a single figure that was not a husk.

It moved.

"Careful," Sable breathed, and her old hands came up in a gesture Odile recognized—the warding sign of a priestess whose order no longer existed, protecting no one, from nothing, out of habit and hope.

But the figure only bowed. It was small and stooped, dressed in tabards layered like autumn leaves, each embroidered with thread so fine it might have been spider-silk. Its face was hidden beneath a hood stitched with ninefold patterns, and when it straightened, dust poured off its shoulders in slow gray rivers. Moths lifted from the folds of its sleeves—a dozen, two dozen, pale as paper, circling it once before settling again. A herald's chain of office hung around its neck, tarnished green, and every link was shaped like a tiny open door.

"Hail," said the herald, and its voice was the worst thing yet—not mechanical, not empty, but *worn*, like a book read aloud ten thousand times by someone who no longer understood the words. "Hail, and be welcome to the Court of Nine Thrones. Hail, and be welcome to the Court of Eight Empty Thrones. Hail, and be welcome—" it paused, head tilting with a dry rustle, "—to the Court of One Occupied Throne."

Odile's skin went cold. "One?"

"The ninth," the herald said. "For a hundred years and four days, the ninth throne has been occupied." It raised one sleeve, and beneath it Odile saw the faintest shimmer—a suggestion, an outline, the way heat shimmers above summer road. Something sat there. Something had been sitting there for a century, patient as rot. "You are expected, Covenant-Bearer. You have been expected since before your grandmother learned to write her name."

* * *

They gathered around the herald in the nave of the ruined hall, and it spoke to them the way a well speaks—only when approached, only what was asked, its answers rising up dark and cold from somewhere very deep.

"How are you still here?" Sable asked. Her voice cracked on the question; this was her faith's corpse they were standing in. "When the covenant broke, when the Court emptied—what kept you?"

"A fragment." The herald pressed both hands to its chest, and through the moth-eaten fabric Odile saw a dull gleam, like a coin sewn into a lining. "When the theft came—when the Court's memory of its heir was torn away—the tearing did not come clean. Memory does not cut neatly, mortal. It frays. And I was the keeper of thresholds, the announcer of names, and a frayed edge of the Court's remembering caught in me the way a splinter catches in a palm." It lowered its hands. "I am less than one percent of what this place knew. But one percent is enough to know why you have come."

"Then tell us," Odile said, and hated how steady she made her voice. "Tell us how to reforge it. The prophecy says the Covenant-Bearer reforges the pact. Fine. Show me the forge."

The herald was silent for a long moment. Moths stirred in its sleeves.

"You do not understand what was stolen," it said at last. "So let me tell you, because no one else alive can. Sit, if you wish. This is a story with weight, and weight should be carried sitting down."

They sat—or Odile did, on a step worn smooth by a century of absent feet, with Wick folding down beside her and Brannic looming behind like a wall she hadn't known she'd built. Caspian stood apart, arms crossed, and she watched him instead of the herald, because watching him had become a discipline lately, the way counting coins was a discipline. He looked like a man hearing a verdict he already knew.

"A century ago," the herald began, "the Nine Houses and the mortal cities were bound by the Covenant of Shared Breath. What one realm dreamed, the other could inherit. Mortal children born near a fae lord's border were given a gift of that lord's essence—a talent, a grace, a second sight—and in exchange the lords drew sustenance from mortal memory. Not theft. Gift. Memory freely offered at the turning of seasons, the way grain is offered at harvest. That was the breath we shared: they gave us wonder, we gave them past."

"And my grandmother stole from that," Odile said quietly.

"Your grandmother stole *the keystone*." The hood turned toward her, and though there were no eyes visible, Odile felt the regard land on her like a physical touch. "Every covenant has a linchpin—a single truth that all the oaths hang from. Ours was the memory of the true heir. The child born of both realms, in whom the pact was embodied. While the Court remembered its heir, the covenant held, because a promise made to a person is stronger than a promise made to an abstraction. Your grandmother understood this. Whether she acted from greed, from fear, from love—I cannot say. My fragment does not reach that far back. But she entered this hall under a trader's mark"—and Odile's hand went involuntarily to the satchel at her hip, to the vials bearing that same stamped sigil—"and she took from the Court the one memory it could not survive losing."

"The heir," Sable whispered. "She stole the Court's memory of its own heir."

"She stole the heir entire," the herald corrected. "Not merely the knowing. Understand: in this court, to remember a thing is to keep it alive. We did not store our past in books and graves as mortals do. Our memory was our flesh. When she took the heir's memory from us, the heir did not die. The heir was *unmade here*—and remade elsewhere. In the mortal world. Among thieves and ferry-crossings and magic-dead valleys, where the covenant's reach could never follow, because there was no covenant left to reach."

The silence after that was enormous. Somewhere high above, wind moved through broken vaulting and made a sound like a great hall full of whisperers.

Odile heard her own heartbeat. She heard Wick breathing beside her, quick and shallow. She heard, distantly, Brannic say, "There's a door opening somewhere," in a small, lost voice, and no one answered him.

"No," she said. It came out flat, almost bored, which was how she sounded when she was most terrified. "No, you don't get to do that. You don't get to stand there in your moth coat and tell me I'm—what? That I'm the missing piece of a fae court? I'm a thief. I steal memories out of dying strangers' heads for bread money. I've never seen this place before in my life."

"You have never seen it *while remembering it*," the herald said gently. "That is a different thing entirely. Look at the ninth throne, Covenant-Bearer. Tell me what you feel."

She didn't want to. She looked anyway.

The ninth throne stood at the end of the crescent, slightly apart from the others, carved—unlike its eight siblings—from wood rather than stone. Dark wood, grain swirling in patterns like riverwater frozen mid-turn, and across its armrests ran a carving of interlocking doors, dozens of them, hundreds, a whole architecture of thresholds. And the occupant—the shimmer, the outline, the patient presence—sat upon it the way frost sits on glass.

What Odile felt was homesickness.

It hit her sideways, without warning or permission: a bone-deep, wordless ache, the feeling of standing outside a lit window on a winter street, of smelling bread from a kitchen she couldn't find. Her stolen memories rose in answer—first snow, the ferryman's laugh, a hundred borrowed dawns—all of them leaning toward that throne like iron filings toward a lodestone. Even the ones she'd stolen from strangers. Even, gods help her, the wedding day she'd paid at Salt Bridge. Everything in her wanted to walk up those three steps and sit down and stop being tired.

"That's not mine," she said hoarsely. "Whatever that is, it isn't mine."

"It is the shape of the space you were carved from," the herald said. "A hundred years, the throne has waited with your absence sitting in it. Do you see now why Veyran hunts you? Why the usurper wears nine lords' stolen memories as a crown? He knows what you are. He knows that if you sit that throne *whole*, everything he has built on the covenant's grave comes down. And he knows—" the herald's voice dropped, and the moths went still, "—that you will never be whole. Because wholeness was sold before you were born."

* * *

Sable was the one who asked the question, because Odile couldn't. Her voice was very quiet, very old. "Reforge the covenant. Say it plainly. What must be returned?"

The herald folded its hands. For a moment it looked less like a relic and more like what it must once have been: a servant of a living court, announcing guests, opening doors.

"What was stolen must be returned," it said. "All of it. Every memory in the thief's line that belongs to this place. And above all—the keystone. The Court must remember its heir again. Not the idea of the heir. Not a description, not a name in a ledger. The heir's own memory of being the heir. The knowing, from inside, of what she was and what she owed and what she loved about this hall. That memory lives nowhere in these ruins." The hood lifted toward Odile. "It lives in her blood. Buried so deep even she cannot dig for it. It will surface only if she gives the covenant everything else—every stolen memory she carries, hers and her hoard's together—and lets the flood carry the keystone up from underneath."

"Everything," Odile repeated.

"Every memory you have taken. Every life you keep in your skull. The covenant will take them all as kindling, and from their burning, the true flame—the heir's own remembering—will rise." The herald spread its hands, apologetic, terrible. "And when it is done, Covenant-Bearer, you will sit your throne knowing yourself at last. You will know this hall. You will know your name in the old tongue, and your mother's face, and the taste of the fruit that grows on the terraces above the eastern gate. You will know everything you were."

"Except—"

"Except," the herald said, and did not flinch, because relics cannot flinch, "everything you became. The valley. The rules. The ferryman's last snow. The girl with no memories who walks like a lockpick and laughs like a bell. The people who followed you here. The man you—" it paused, delicately, "—the company you gathered. None of that was ever written in this court. None of that belongs to the covenant. When the flood comes, it takes what is yours to give and what is not, alike. The heir will wake on that throne. And the woman called Odile Eastwick will not."

Wick made a sound. Small, bitten-off, like a door closing on a finger.

"I'll forget them," Odile said. Her voice came from very far away, from the bottom of a well, from the Hollow Valley where magic couldn't follow and neither could anything else. "I'll pour out a thousand lives and my own with them, and I'll wake up a stranger wearing my face, and they'll—" she looked at Wick, at Brannic, at Sable, and could not make herself look at Caspian— "they'll be nobody to me."

"The covenant asks nothing dishonestly," the herald said. "It asks what your grandmother took. It happens that the price of her theft was paid in advance, a century early, by a granddaughter who has spent her whole life carrying other people's pasts because she could not bear her own." The moths lifted from its shoulders, circled once, and settled. "I am one percent of a dead court's memory, mortal. But I know a trap when I announce one."

* * *

Afterward, they walked out onto the eastern terrace, because none of them could breathe inside that hall. Below them the lands of the Court fell away in silvered ruin—fountains choked with moss, orchards gone feral, roads running straight toward horizons that flickered faintly at their edges, like paintings drying wrong. The border-rot was visible from here, a haze of unreality creeping inward year by year, patient as the husks.

Brannic stood at the terrace rail with tears running silently down his scarred face. "I can hear them," he said. "Underground. Under everything. Nine doors, and behind eight of them, nothing. Behind the ninth—" he shook his head, unable to finish, and Odile put her hand between his shoulder blades and left it there.

Sable had withdrawn to pray, or to argue with God, or both; her lips moved without sound.

Caspian came to stand beside her at the rail, and for once he did not speak first. That was how she knew he understood exactly what the herald had told her, and exactly what it meant, and had probably known since Verance, since the ledger, maybe since the first impossible dinner in the valley.

"Say it," she said finally, to the horizon. "Whatever you're not saying. Say it now, while I can still afford to hate you."

"Veyran's army crossed the Salt Bridge three days ago," Caspian said quietly. "Scouts confirmed it this morning. Ten thousand, maybe twelve, with the inquisitors riding vanguard. At their pace, they reach the outer gates in—" he closed his eyes, doing arithmetic he clearly wished would come out differently, "—four days. Five."

Five days. Odile looked at the ninth throne's distant glimmer through the colonnade, at the feral orchards, at the sky going amber over the rot-haze. Five days to decide whether to unmake herself for a world that had used her, lied to her, and sent a charming stranger to collect her like a debt.

"Wick," she said suddenly. "Where's Wick?"

They found the kid at the top of the western stair, sitting on a step with their knees drawn up, staring at nothing. When Odile touched their shoulder they flinched, then leaned into her hand like a cat deciding, against all evidence, to trust.

"I don't want you to do it," Wick said, without preamble, without looking up. "I don't care about thrones. I don't care about the stupid covenant. I care that you promised—" their jaw worked— "in Rotwood. You promised you'd never take what's mine. Well, I'm telling you the other half. Don't give yours away either. Find another way. There's always another way. You taught me that. Locks, you said. There's no lock in the world with just one key."

Odile crouched down until she was looking into the kid's eyes—gray eyes, wary and fierce, six years old forever at the bottom of them.

"There isn't another key," she said softly. "I checked. Believe me, kid. Checking locks is the one thing I've never been bad at."

Far below, at the edge of the flickering horizon, a smudge of dust rose against the amber sky—too orderly, too deliberate, too vast for weather. Brannic's head came up like a hound's.

"Doors," he said. "A lot of doors. Marching."
