## 27. Veyran at the Gates

The horns came at dusk.

Caspian heard them from the ninth gate's broken parapet, where he had been standing for the better part of an hour pretending to study the road when really he was studying the woman below him in the courtyard—Odile, arms crossed, watching Brannic trace the seams of a door that had not opened in a hundred years. The horns rolled up out of the western hills like thunder that had learned to count: three long notes, three short, three long again. The army's voice. Veyran's voice.

*They are here,* Caspian thought, and the thought landed in his chest with the specific weight of a debt come due.

He had known this day was coming since Verance. He had known it in his bones, in the old oath-scars along his forearms that ached whenever the Court drew near, in the way Mother Sable looked at the horizon each morning as though reading a ledger only she could see. Armies moved at the speed of their supply trains, and Veyran's trains were legendary—black-lacquered wagons drawn by oxen whose eyes had been replaced with glass so they would never flinch. Somewhere behind those hills was the man who wore nine dead lords' memories as a crown, and somewhere behind *that* man was the list of names Caspian had memorized and burned and memorized again, the list on which one name sat above all others like a knife laid flat across a page.

*Odile Eastwick. Covenant-Bearer. Priority of priorities.*

Below him, she glanced up—she always knew when he was looking, which should have frightened him more than it did—and mouthed something to Wick, who scrambled up the parapet stair two steps at a time, breathless, grinning the way teenagers grin at disasters.

"Horns," Wick reported unnecessarily. "Lots of them. Also there's dust on the west road like somebody set the sky on fire and is sweeping it toward us."

"How far?"

"Brannic says by tomorrow's middle. He can hear them through the ground." Wick's grin faltered into something younger. "He says they're singing."

"Singing?"

"Marching songs." Wick picked at the mortar between the stones. "Old ones. Sable got pale and went inside."

Caspian felt the cold arrive then, the particular cold he had been carrying folded up inside his coat since the day he'd walked into the Hollow Valley with a stolen prophecy and a smile he hadn't earned. It was the cold of arithmetic. Veyran's vanguard alone would number four thousand. The free cities, if every militia mustered tomorrow—and they would not, because he had seen to it, or rather his silence had—that might yield eight thousand scattered across three hundred miles, arriving too late by design. And here, at the Court's ruined heart, stood six people, a defrocked priestess, a boy who could hear doors, and whatever remained in the hollow thrones of the Ninefold Court.

*You did this,* he told himself. *Every mile you didn't send word, you marched them closer.*

* * *

He found Sable in the antechamber that had once been a chapel, judging by the scorch-shadow of a nine-pointed star still ghosted on the wall. She had built a small fire from broken pew-wood and was feeding it strips of parchment—the last of her maps, he realized. Burning her own cartography so it couldn't be captured.

"You've heard," he said.

"I've heard everything, boy. I'm old, not deaf." She didn't look up. "Four thousand, give or take the dead weight of the inquisitors' baggage train. Voss is with them. I can smell her from here—madness has a scent, like milk turning."

"We need riders. Now. Tonight. If Verance musters—"

"If Verance musters, Verance leaves itself open, and Veyran has second and third columns we haven't seen." She finally raised her head. Her eyes were wet-rimmed and iron-hard. "That was always his play, wasn't it? Pin the cities with fear, take the Court, take the Bearer, and let the covenant die officially, legally, with all the paperwork done proper."

"That was always his play," Caspian agreed quietly. "Yes."

"And you knew. You've known the shape of it since before the valley."

It wasn't a question, so he didn't insult her with an answer. The fire popped. Somewhere above them, faint through ancient stone, Brannic's voice rose in that low listening hum he made when doors spoke to him—a sound like a man humming along to a hymn only he remembered.

Sable studied him for a long moment. "There's a look on you," she said. "I've seen it twice before. Once on a young novice who was about to do a cowardly thing. Once on your grandmother's generation, the ones who broke the world." She poked the fire. "It's the look of a man deciding what he's willing to lose."

"Mother Sable—"

"Don't confess to me yet. Confess when it costs you." She turned back to the flames. "Go find the girl. Whatever you're about to do, do it while you can still look her in the eye."

* * *

He found Odile on the eastern wall, where the Court's gardens had gone over to wild thorn and night-blooming something-or-other that shouldn't have survived a century without gardeners but had, stubbornly, beautifully, the way everything fae survived—barely, wrongly, gorgeously. The moons were rising. Three of them tonight, out of phase, throwing three overlapping shadows from everything that stood still.

She didn't turn when he came up beside her. "Say it," she said. "Whatever you climbed all these stairs to say. Say it before I have to guess, because I hate guessing about you. Guessing about you has historically gone very badly for me."

"The army will reach the outer gates by midday tomorrow."

"I know. Brannic hears them."

"There may be more coming that even he can't hear. Second columns. The council in Verance won't move without confirmation, and confirmation takes days we don't have, and by the time they understand—" He stopped. Made himself stop. "Odile. The cities are undefended. Not undermanned. *Undefended.* Because I let them believe they had time."

Now she turned. In the triple moonlight her face was three faces at once—the woman who had trusted him, the woman who had caught him burning prophecy pages, and beneath both, faintly, the girl from the valley who owned a thousand lives and lived none. He had put that look on her face. Every version of it.

"You're telling me this now," she said slowly, "because?"

"Because I'm done keeping things from you that keep people alive."

"Is that what you call it? A late confession?" Her voice stayed level, which was worse than shouting. "Caspian, I have a ledger too, you know. Of things you knew and didn't say. It's longer than the inquisitors'. At some point you have to tell me what's actually in the newest entry, because if you make me guess—"

"Verance must be warned tonight. By someone they'll believe. Someone with standing." He reached into his coat and drew out the thing he had carried against his heart for eleven years: the signet ring of the Ninth House, the courier's seal, black silver worked in the shape of a moth with spread wings. The Court's own authority, still valid in the free cities because no one had ever formally declared it dead. "A message under this seal goes to the council's inner table by dawn, past every checkpoint, past every doubt. They'll muster. Not everyone—not enough—but thousands who would otherwise be home in bed when the second column arrives."

Odile stared at the ring. Then at him. "And how does a sealed courier get through an army that's camped between here and everywhere?"

"He doesn't go through it." Caspian turned the ring in his fingers, watched the moth catch moonlight. "He walks up to it. To the front rank. To Veyran himself." He heard his own heartbeat, absurdly loud. "Veyran knows me, Odile. He's been offering me a place at his right hand since before I ever found you. He thinks I'm his. Tonight I'm going to walk down that hill, kneel in front of ten thousand men like the traitor they think I am, hand him my credentials, ask for a private word—and while he's savoring the moment, thinking he's finally won, I'll have already sent three moths flying east."

"Moths." Her brow furrowed. "Messages? But the border-rot eats anything magical that—"

"Not magical. Blood-moths. Old courier craft, pre-covenant, older than the rot. They carry nothing written. They carry *pattern*—wingbeats. Sable taught me the code when I was a boy in her temple and still believed in something. Three moths, three wingbeat patterns: *army's true size*, *second column exists*, *move now*. The temple remnant in Verance still keeps the listening cages. Sable will confirm it." His voice wanted to shake. He wouldn't let it. "By the time Veyran realizes his new favorite courtier has no tongue for flattery, the birds are over the hills."

Silence. The thorns rustled. One of the moons slid behind cloud and gave her back only one shadow.

"No," she said.

"Odile—"

"*No.*" Now she was shouting, and he was almost grateful for it. "You are not walking into that camp. You know what he does to couriers of the Court? You told us yourself—you showed Wick the sketches. He doesn't execute them. He *unmakes* them. He peels their memories off like bark and wears them. You'd be handing him—" Her voice cracked clean through. "You'd be handing me another empty place at a table."

"That's not—"

"It's exactly what it is!" She grabbed his collar with both hands, and he let her, gladly, because her hands were shaking and it was the most honest thing anyone had done to him in days. "Listen to me. I have spent my whole life taking things from people and calling it survival, and the one lesson the valley never managed to teach me until recently is that some things, once taken, don't come back. You are not going to offer yourself up to a man who collects people. Not for Verance. Not for the covenant. Not for *me*."

"For you especially," he said softly.

Her grip tightened. "Don't."

"Somebody warned me once that love proven by loss is the only proof that counts. I didn't listen then. I'm listening now." He covered her hands with his own, gently, and did not pull away. "Odile. I lied to you in the valley. I recruited you like an asset. I hid the death-line in the prophecy. I hid what your blood meant. I have spent this entire journey being the man who tells you truths in installments, and every installment has cost this company something. So here is the whole truth, all at once, the last installment: I am going to that camp, and I may not come back, and I need you to let me, because the alternative is watching Veyran take the Court, take you, and take the cities while we stand here having protected *my* skin instead of *everyone else's*."

"You could send the moths without surrendering yourself. Fly them from the ridge—"

"The ridge is warded. Inquisitor glass, memory-snares strung like bunting. Anything magical dies crossing it—including blood-moths launched from outside the wardline. Inside the camp, though?" He smiled, thin and bitter. "Inside the camp, the wards exist to protect Veyran. Everything within them is shielded. Even, ironically, his enemies' pigeons. The only safe place to launch the warning from is the heart of the army that must never receive it."

She stared at him. He watched the arithmetic happen behind her eyes—he had learned to read her calculations the way Brannic read doors—and watched her arrive at the answer she hated.

"You've already decided," she said. "This speech was courtesy."

"This speech was the truth, given whole, for the first time." He took her hands off his collar and held them. "That's the redemption part. The rest is just logistics."

* * *

The war council—if five people and a herald's husk around a dying fire deserved the word—took place in the chamber of the ninth door, because Brannic insisted the doors should hear what was decided near them. Caspian laid it out plainly: the plan, the moths, the risk. He expected argument. What he did not expect was Wick standing up first, chin jutting, and saying:

"I'm coming with you."

"No," said Caspian and Odile, in perfect unison, and then glared at each other for agreeing.

Wick planted both palms on the table. "You need someone small and fast who isn't worth an inquisitor's attention. Someone who can slip away from the camp after the birds go up while everybody's busy looking at the dramatic kneeling traitor. That's *my entire skill set*, that's literally what I'm *for*—"

"Wick." Odile's voice had gone very quiet, which those who knew her understood was the dangerous register. "You have no memories before age six. Someone stole them. We don't know who. We don't know why. If Veyran's people get close enough to touch your mind, they'll find that hole, and holes like yours interest men like him."

"Then I'll be careful."

"You were 'careful' at Salt Bridge and came back with a burn scar."

"That was *cool*, though."

"*Wick.*"

Brannic rumbled from the doorway, where he leaned with his ear pressed, as always, half to the stone. "The little one speaks truly about the skillset." His deep voice filled the room like water filling a cellar. "But the doors here tell me other things. They say the camp's edge will be chaos tonight—supply wagons, forage parties, the usual disorder of an army arrived at its destination and full of its own noise. Two go in easier than one goes in alone. A pair reads as servants. A single stranger reads as a spy." He shrugged his enormous shoulders. "Also, I wish to hit something. It has been weeks."

Mother Sable, who had not spoken since entering, finally lifted her head from the map she was annotating in charcoal. "You'll all die," she observed. "Probably. But then, so will we all if the cities sleep, so I suppose the mathematics favor stupidity tonight." She dipped her pen. "The code, Caspian. Recite it. All three patterns. If you're going to carry my temple's voice into that camp, you'll carry it perfectly."

He recited. She corrected him twice. On the third pattern—the urgent one, *move now, now, now*—her hand trembled, and charcoal snapped against parchment, and for a moment the defrocked priestess looked like the young novice she had confessed to being, the one who had hidden an heir and lost her nerve and let the world break.

"They sang tonight," she said abruptly. "The soldiers on the road. Marching songs from before the breaking. My order wrote those songs, did you know? We wrote them so the mortal levies would march beside the fae hosts without fear. Veyran's army sings our songs while marching to bury the covenant forever." She straightened her spine with an effort that was visible. "Make the moths sing louder."

* * *

They left at the black hour before dawn, when the moons had all set and the stars alone kept watch, indifferent and beautiful. Odile walked them as far as the outer causeway, where the first of Veyran's picket-fires were visible as a smear of orange against the western dark, like a sunset that had stopped halfway and given up.

She had not spoken since the wall. Caspian had learned that this was how she processed: silence first, fury later, forgiveness—maybe, someday, if the world was kind—in some chapter neither of them had reached yet.

At the foot of the causeway she stopped him with a hand on his sleeve.

"When I crossed the valley border," she said, not looking at him, "everything I'd built came apart, and underneath it I found out the cage and the shelter had been the same thing all along. I chose the cage for years because choosing felt worse." Her thumb worried the fabric of his sleeve, back and forth, a thief's unconscious motion, checking a pocket that wasn't there. "What you're doing—it's the opposite of that. You're choosing the thing that feels worst because it's the only honest door. I want you to know I see it. Even if I never say it well."

"Odile—"

"Take this." She pressed something into his palm: a small glass vial, warm from her skin, stoppered with wax. Inside swirled a color he had no name for—gold shot with grey, moving like weather. "First snow," she said. "From a ferryman in the valley. The first thing I ever took. It's the closest thing I have to a beginning." She closed his fingers around it. "If you come back, give it to me, and I'll pretend I never gave it away, and you can pretend you never noticed I pretended. If you don't come back—" her voice held, barely— "then somewhere in that awful man's hoard, there'll be one memory he owns that was freely given instead of stolen, and maybe it'll choke him a little."

Caspian looked down at the vial, and found that his throat had closed entirely. He managed, eventually: "You realize you've just guaranteed I survive. No one gives me gifts. I'm not equipped."

"Then learn." She stepped back. Behind her, dawn was considering the horizon. "Come back and introduce yourself properly. You owe me a first meeting where you don't already know my name."

He laughed—it escaped before he could weigh it, surprised out of him—and for one moment on the causeway, with the enemy's fires glowing ahead and the ruined Court behind, Caspian of the Ninth House felt something he had no code-pattern for, something the temple hadn't taught and Veyran couldn't wear and the prophecy had never once mentioned.

Then he turned, and Wick fell into step beside him, and Brannic's footsteps shook the frost off the causeway stones, and the three of them walked down toward the fires together.

* * *

The camp was worse up close than it had been from the walls.

Caspian had seen armies before—had grown up in the margins of them, a temple orphan dodging baggage trains—but Veyran's host was different in kind, not degree. The discipline was absolute and joyless; the tents were pitched in geometries that hurt the eye; and everywhere, everywhere, the glass. Memory-glass lanterns on every fifth pole, humming faintly, drinking the ambient warmth of human recollection the way candles drank air. Men passed beneath them and their faces grew subtly smoother as the night went on. Serene. Emptying. An army being gently edited into perfect obedience, one evening at a time.

"Creepy," Wick breathed at his elbow, in a whisper pitched for a mouse's funeral. "The lanterns. They're like... hungry lamps."

"Eyes forward. Servants don't comment." Caspian adjusted the yoke across his shoulders—laundry bundles, their cover story, Brannic laboring behind them with the placid menace of a hired dray-man. "We're attached to the quartermaster's train. We got separated in the forage lines. Our papers are flawless because I wrote them."

"Your handwriting is *flawless*?"

"My forgery is."

The first checkpoint challenged them with bored eyes and a glass wand waved lazily across Brannic's chest. The wand flickered at the fae-blood in him—Caspian's stomach dropped—and then steadied, because Brannic's gift ran quiet as groundwater, and the bored soldier was already waving them through, already yawning, already forgetting. They passed. They passed the second checkpoint too. It was at the third, before the command pavilion with its banners of black and bone-white, that a voice like a file drawn across steel said:

"That one. The big one. And the children with him."

Inquisitor Maren Voss stood in the tent-gap with lamplight behind her, and up close the madness Sable had smelled was written plain: one pupil blown wide, one contracted to a pinprick, and a smile assembled from parts, worn like a mask worn slightly crooked. Caspian's skin tried to crawl off his body. He held it still.

"You're mistaken, honored one. We're quartermaster's people—"

"*Caspian.*" She tasted the name. Her pinprick eye fixed on him. "The Court's little moth. Veyran dreams of you. Did you know that? He dreams of you kneeling." The crooked smile widened. "Come to make his dream come true?"

Every plan Caspian had built in the last twelve hours collapsed at once, and in the rubble, with terrible clarity, he saw the only remaining structure: the truth, weaponized. He sank to one knee in the frozen mud, bowed his head, and said, loudly enough for the gathering guards to hear:

"Caspian of the Ninth House, courier of the Ninefold Court, comes to swear himself to King Veyran—and brings gifts fit for a king's ears alone."

Voss's smile hung there a moment longer, uncertain, cheated of its cruelty. Then she gestured, and the tent-flaps opened, and Caspian rose to follow her in—catching, in the last sliver of night beyond the fires, three small shapes rising silent from the laundry bundles' shadows, wingbeats already falling into rhythm.

*Move now. Move now. Move now.*

Behind him, the pavilion swallowed him whole, and Veyran's voice—warm, delighted, wearing nine dead lords' laughter like borrowed clothing—said:

"At last. Kneel properly, courier. Let us discuss what you've brought me... and what I intend to take."

Beyond the canvas walls, faint and fading eastward, the moths flew on.
