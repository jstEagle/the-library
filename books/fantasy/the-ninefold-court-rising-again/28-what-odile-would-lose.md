## 28. What Odile Would Lose

The covenant chamber had a way of making silence feel like an audience.

Odile sat alone in the gallery above the nine thrones, her back against a pillar of veined moonstone, and rehearsed.

That was the only word for it. The herald's moth-eaten voice still echoed in the hollows of the Court—*the covenant asks a return; the heir must give what was taken*—and she had spent three days turning the price over in her hands like a coin she did not want to spend, weighing it, biting it, testing whether it was real. It was real. She could feel it real in the same place she felt the stolen memories: behind her ribs, where a thousand borrowed lives lay coiled like sleeping birds.

*Give them all,* the herald had said. *Every memory you carry that was never yours. And with them—the heir's own past, which your grandmother stole, which lives in you as surely as your name.*

Her name. That was the cruelest part, when she let herself look at it straight. Not the hoard of strangers' first snows and wedding days and dying breaths. Those were weight, but weight could be set down. It was the rest of it—the memories that were hers, grown from her own soil—that made her hands shake now in the cold blue light of the empty thrones.

Because the covenant did not distinguish. She had asked. Three times she had climbed down to the herald's alcove and asked, and three times the tattered thing had unfolded its parchment face and said, in a voice like wind through a keyhole: *The heir returns herself entire.*

She closed her eyes and opened the first door inside herself.

Caspian.

Not the Caspian of the prophecy book, ink and dates and a stranger's unnerving familiarity. She went deeper, past the betrayal, past the night he burned that page and she saw his face by firelight doing it. Beneath all of it there was a smaller memory, one she had never told anyone she kept: their first dinner in the Hollow Valley, before everything. He had ordered two of every dish because he couldn't read the menu script and refused to admit it, and when she'd laughed at him he'd laughed too—unhurried, unguarded—and for one hour she had not been a thief or an orphan or a weapon waiting to be named. She had been a woman at a table with a man who knew her name and seemed, impossibly, glad of it.

If she gave herself to the covenant, that hour would go. Not just the memory of it—the *having-happened*. The herald had been clear on this, in its whispering way. Memory was not a record; it was the root. Cut the root and the tree never stood at all. She would stand in whatever came after and Caspian would be a handsome stranger with kind eyes and a liar's patience, and some part of her would lean toward him anyway, the way a scar leans toward rain, without knowing why.

*I would meet him twice,* she thought, *and lose him both times.*

The stolen memories stirred against her ribs like birds feeling a storm come. She pressed her palm flat to her sternum, as if she could hold them in by pressure alone.

* * *

Wick found her at dusk.

Not by searching—by sitting. The girl had a genius for it: choosing a doorway Odile would have to pass through and occupying it with the patient stubbornness of a cat who has decided the warm stone is theirs by ancient right. Wick sat cross-legged at the head of the ruined stair, sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening, and looked up when Odile's shadow fell across them.

"You've got the face," Wick said.

"What face?"

"The one Brannic gets before he does something noble and stupid." Wick blew a curl of dark hair out of their eyes. "You're going to do it, aren't you. Go down there and pour yourself into the thrones."

Odile leaned against the doorframe. Below them, the Court spread out in its desolate splendor—nine thrones under a vault painted with constellations no living astronomer could name, and in the shadows between the pillars, the husks. The fae who remained. She tried not to look at them. Tall figures of gray silk and hollow faces, drifting through the halls of their own majesty like smoke looking for a shape it used to have. One of them had spent the whole morning standing at a window, watching light move across the floor, with the absolute devotion of someone seeing it for the first time. Every time.

"Maybe," Odile said.

"That's a yes with a coat on." Wick snapped the knife closed and stood. They were sixteen and half a head shorter than her and they looked up at her with the flat, unflinching gaze of someone who had been robbed of their whole childhood and refused ever again to be lied to politely. "Sable says the covenant needs all of it. Your hoard and your own. She says the last line might not mean death." A pause. Wick's jaw worked. "She says *might*, Odile."

"I know."

"So here's my question, and I want you to answer it like I'm not sixteen and scared, because I am sixteen and scared but I'll fight you if you handle me gently." Wick stepped closer. In the blue gloom their eyes caught what little light there was. "When you pour it all out—do I survive? In you? Does any of it stay?"

"Wick—"

"Because I know what you keep." The words came fast now, tumbling, the way they did when Wick forgot to be guarded. "You think I don't notice things, but I notice everything, it's the one thing I'm good at, noticing, since I don't have anything else to notice *with*. You keep the sound I make when I get a lock open on the first try. You hum it sometimes when you think nobody's listening. It sounds like—" Wick stopped. Swallowed. Their voice dropped to almost nothing. "It sounds like somebody who's happy."

Odile's throat closed.

"And I don't remember being six," Wick went on, steadier now, gripping that truth like a banister on a long stair. "I don't remember my mother's voice or my birthday or whether I liked the rain. Somebody took it, or it burned, or it never was—I don't know which is worse. But you—" their hand came up, hesitated, landed on her sleeve, gripped, "—you're the closest thing I've got to proof that I existed before I woke up in Bellharbor with nothing. If you pour yourself out, then that proof goes too. And I'll still exist. I'll just be the only one who doesn't know I did."

The pillar at Odile's back felt suddenly load-bearing in more than architecture. She reached out and pulled Wick in, and the girl went stiff for a heartbeat—the old flinch, the foundling's flinch—and then folded against her, sharp elbows and bony shoulders and a smell of oil and river water and home.

"I don't know how to do this," Odile said into their hair. "Any of it. Everyone keeps telling me what the price is and no one can tell me how to pay it and still be standing after."

"You won't be standing," Wick said, muffled. "Sable says whoever comes out the other side won't be you. But—" they pulled back, wiped their face with the heel of their hand, furious at themselves for the wetness there, "—Brannic gave up his hearing for a door. He didn't stop being Brannic. Sable burned her robes and didn't stop being Sable. So maybe—" a shrug that tried very hard to be casual and failed, "—maybe whoever walks out of that chamber will walk out toward us. That's all I'm asking. Whoever you are after. Walk toward us."

And Odile, who could steal the last snowfall from a dying man's mind without leaving a mark, who could pick apart a person's whole history in an afternoon of careful theft—who could not, at this moment, manage a single honest sentence—just nodded, and held on tighter, and hated the covenant, and hated the truth, and loved this fierce ragged child with a love so large it frightened her, because it was exactly the kind of thing the sacrifice was designed to take.

* * *

That night she went up onto the Court's outer wall and watched the horizon burn.

Veyran's army was close enough now that its cookfires ringed the far hills like a fallen constellation, thousands upon thousands of orange points in the dark, and above them the usurper's banners hung black even against the black sky. Somewhere in that host rode Maren Voss, if Voss still rode anything, still wore a face. Somewhere in the capital, Caspian was bargaining with a king who wore nine fae lords' memories as a crown. Three days since he'd walked into the dark with nothing but his name to offer, and no word back. The silence had a texture. Odile carried it everywhere, like a stone in her boot.

Brannic joined her on the wall. He moved quietly for a big man, and since the ninth gate had taken his gift he moved quieter still, as though the world had gone slightly muffled for him too and he was matching its pace. He handed her a cup of something hot and bitter—Sable's tea, the stuff she brewed from Rotwood moss that tasted like forgiveness felt—and stood beside her looking at the fires.

"They'll breach the outer causeway tomorrow," he said. "Day after, the gate we paid for." His voice was gentle around the word *paid*, the way you speak near a wound. "Sable's been at the books all evening. Says there may be another line in the prophecy she hasn't cracked. She gets that look."

"She always has that look."

"This one's worse."

They drank in silence. Below, in the courtyard, Mother Sable moved among the free cities' refugees huddled against the Court walls—a few hundred souls, farmers and dockhands and two defrocked choirboys, armed with pikes and prayer and not much else. Her white hair caught the torchlight. A hundred years ago she had been a novice hiding an infant heir and letting the world break rather than speak. Tonight she blessed soldiers with the same hands.

"Can I tell you something cowardly?" Odile said.

Brannic turned. His face, seamed and fae-touched, with its faint silver tracery along the jaw, was the kindest thing on the wall.

"I don't want to be the heir." She heard her own voice go low and rough. "I never wanted the Court, or the oath, or the bloodline. I wanted—" she gestured helplessly at the valley beyond the hills, the direction of everything she had lost, "—a ferryman's cabin. First snows. A table with people at it. The covenant wants me to buy the world with myself, and everyone keeps saying *what a gift, what a sacrifice*, and I keep thinking: what if I refuse? What if I take Brannic and Wick and Sable and run east until the war can't find us? Nine realms can burn. I only need one valley."

The words hung in the cold air, ugly and true, and she waited for him to judge her.

Brannic took a slow drink of tea. "When they opened the ninth gate," he said at last, "and the deafness came, do you know what I lost first? Not the doors. Not the singing of the stones. I lost the sound of Wick's footsteps. There's a particular rhythm to how they walk—left foot drags a hair, from an old injury they won't talk about. Best sound I knew. Gone in a moment." He looked at her. "I'd give it again. Tomorrow. Every day. Because I heard a wraith coming for them once, and my ears bought us thirty seconds, and thirty seconds was Wick alive. That's the whole arithmetic. It's not noble, Odile. It's just the only sum that comes out right."

"But you got to stay you."

"I got to stay mostly me." His mouth twisted. "The Court takes what it needs. But hear me—" he set down the cup and took her shoulder, and his grip was gentle and immovable, "—you keep talking about pouring yourself out like it's dying. Sable doesn't think it is. I don't think it is. I think it's a door. And I've listened to doors my whole life, and every one of them sounds like an ending from the wrong side."

* * *

She went down to the covenant chamber alone, past midnight, because sleep had become a country she could no longer enter.

The nine thrones waited under their painted sky. She walked the length of the hall, her footsteps ringing off stone that remembered music, and stopped before the central throne—the ninth, the heir's, the one Brannic's gift had opened. Up close it was less majestic than sorrowful: carvings of harvests and oaths and linked hands worn smooth by centuries, a seat shaped for someone who was meant to grow old in it.

She reached into herself, carefully, the way she'd once reached into a dying ferryman's mind for a memory of first snow, and lifted the rehearsal to the front where she could see it.

Caspian laughing over two of every dish. Gone.

Wick humming her own delight back at her. Gone.

The valley at dawn, mist rising off the dead-water flats, the border stones humming their null-song, the small gray town where nobody could follow her and nobody could reach her either. Gone. All of it, the whole architecture of the self she had built brick by brick out of loneliness and skill and stubbornness—the rules, the hoard, the thousand lives she owned and the none she had lived. Poured out into nine empty chairs to knit a broken world back together. And what remained afterward would walk out of this chamber toward Wick's voice, Brannic had promised, toward the family—but it would not be *her* walking. It would be a stranger wearing her debt.

Belonging or self. The knife turned both ways. Keep herself, and Veyran's army rolled over the free cities with a crown of stolen belonging, and Wick's Bellharbor burned, and somewhere in the ash the girl who hummed would wake up with nothing again, this time forever. Give herself, and the world knitted whole, and the people she loved would grieve her and then, worse, *not* grieve her, because the woman they'd meet at the chamber door would smile at them kindly and feel nothing underneath but weather.

"There has to be another way," she said aloud, and the husk drifting near the eastern pillars turned its gray face toward the sound of her voice with terrible, vacant hope—as if a nameless thing still believed words could fill it up.

She put her hand on the ninth throne's armrest. Cold stone. Patient as a grave and hopeful as a cradle, which was the same thing here.

"One night more," she whispered to it. "Let me keep myself one night more. Then I'll decide."

Behind her, far off through the Court's ruined galleries, a bell began to ring—not the covenant's bell, nothing so clean. An alarm bell, cracked and desperate, rung by human hands. And threaded under it, rising from the direction of the outer causeway, a sound that turned her blood to ice water:

a woman screaming her name.

*Maren Voss,* Odile thought, already running. *Oh, gods. She's come for me early.*
