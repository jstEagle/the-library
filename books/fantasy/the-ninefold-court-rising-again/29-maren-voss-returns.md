## 29. Maren Voss Returns

The Court had learned to whisper again.

Odile heard it first as a sound beneath sound, a dry rustling through the ruined colonnades where the memoryless fae husks drifted like moths that had forgotten what light was for. The husks did not speak. But the Court itself—its shattered spires, its weeping walls of white stone, its nine thrones sitting empty as pulled teeth—seemed to murmur now that she had walked among them. As if the place remembered being listened to.

She stood at the edge of the covenant chamber's threshold, one hand resting against the doorframe, and tried not to think about what waited inside. What she would have to do inside. The rehearsal of her own memories still burned behind her ribs: Caspian's crooked smile, Wick's laugh like a snare drum dropped down a stairwell, the smell of rain on the Hollow Valley's clay. A hoard she had spent twenty-nine years gathering and four days learning to love.

"You're doing the thing," Wick said, appearing at her elbow with the particular silence of someone who had survived by never being noticed. They were sharpening a knife with a whetstone, the small rhythmic shink of it oddly comforting. "The thing where you stare at a doorway like it owes you money."

"It might."

"Everything in this place owes somebody something." Wick blew a curl of dark hair out of their eyes. "That's kind of the whole problem."

Odile almost smiled. Almost. "Where's Brannic?"

"Listening." Wick tipped their chin toward the great western colonnade, where Brannic stood with his head cocked, his fae-blooded ears tuned to doors no one else could perceive—the ghost-doors of the old covenant, thresholds that existed now only as memory in the stone. He had gone deaf to everything else days ago, trading his gift to open the ninth gate, but he still turned his face toward the far walls the way other people turned toward voices. "He says the gates are restless. Says something's coming up the causeway."

"The army?"

"He says it doesn't march like an army." Wick's whetstone paused. "He says it *hunts* like one."

The cold that slid down Odile's spine had nothing to do with the mountain wind moaning through the broken roof. She knew, before anyone said the name, whose shape that hunting would take.

* * *

They gathered on the terrace above the western stair because there was nowhere better to be, and because Mother Sable said that watching was a form of prayer when you had run out of anything else to offer. Below them the causeway unspooled from the Court's ruins down the mountain's shoulder, a road built by hands that had believed covenants were permanent, and along that road came firelight.

Not torchfire. Inquisitor-lanterns—the grey-white flame that fed on memory, that made the air around it taste of copper and forgetting. Odile could see them from this height as a river of pale light winding upward through the dark, hundreds of them, thousands, and at their head a single figure walking ahead of the vanguard with a stride that did not falter even on the ancient broken stones.

Even at this distance, Odile knew the set of those shoulders.

"Maren Voss," Sable said quietly. Her voice had the flat calm she wore over fury, the one Odile had learned meant someone was about to be told an uncomfortable truth. "Half the garrison at Verance reported her unfit after Salt Bridge. Raving. Scratching her own arms until they bled, calling for someone to give back what was taken."

"What was taken," Odile repeated. Not a question.

"You know what was taken." Sable's eyes, deep-set and tired under her cropped white hair, found Odile's and held them without mercy or accusation—only truth, which was sometimes worse than either. "You reached into her worst day and tore it out by the roots, child. You left a hole in her the size of a life."

"I did what I had to do to get us away."

"Yes." Sable folded her hands. "And now what you had to do is standing at the bottom of our stairs with three hundred soldiers and a grudge older than your friendship with any of us. These things have a way of finding each other again."

Caspian was quiet through all of it, standing slightly apart, his hand resting on the hilt of a sword he had barely used since defecting. When he finally spoke, his voice was low. "Veyran gave her the vanguard. That's not an honor. It's a disposal. He knows what she is now—half-mad, unpredictable—and he's pointed her at us like an arrow he doesn't mind breaking."

"She's not an arrow," Odile said. The words came out harder than she intended. "She's a woman I hollowed out. There's a difference."

"Is there?" Caspian asked, and it wasn't cruel; that was the terrible part. It was only honest. "From where the arrow lands?"

Odile didn't answer. She watched the lanterns climb.

Wick stepped closer to her side—a small, deliberate motion, shoulder nearly touching hers—and said nothing at all. That was its own kind of answer.

* * *

They came at moonset, when the sky went the color of a bruise fading and the Court's ruins threw long black shadows across the terraces. The husks scattered before the vanguard like startled birds, drifting up into the broken rafters, and the grey-white lantern-light flooded the outer courts and turned every statue into a witness.

Odile met them at the threshold of the covenant chamber, because some instincts ran deeper than strategy. If Voss wanted what was behind these doors, she would come through Odile to get it, and Odile would rather it happened here, where the walls still hummed with the last echoes of the oath, than anywhere the thief could be flanked.

Sable and Caspian fell in behind her. Brannic stood at the chamber's far side, head tilted, listening to doors that no longer existed. And Wick—

"I told you to stay back," Odile murmured.

"And I've decided to develop selective hearing." Wick's knife was out, held low. Their grin was ferocious and terrified in equal measure, which was, Odile had learned, simply what Wick's courage looked like. "Besides. Someone's got to watch your blind side. You collect everyone else's memories. You'd forget your own shadow."

The vanguard poured into the court beyond the doors like water finding a crack. Three hundred soldiers should have been deafening, but they moved strangely silent, boots muffled, lanterns held high, and above them all—in the space between the columns where the shadows pooled deepest—walked Maren Voss.

Up close, the change in her was worse than any wound.

Inquisitors were carved from severity: grey coats, grey eyes, faces planed smooth by discipline. Odile remembered Voss as a blade of a woman, precise, contemptuous, terrifyingly competent. The woman who stopped ten paces away was none of those things. She was gaunt. Her uniform hung off her like shed skin. And her eyes—her eyes moved constantly, darting, searching Odile's face, Odile's hands, Odile's throat, as though the missing thing might be written somewhere on her, as though it could be *read* if only the light were right.

"There you are," Voss said softly.

Her voice was the worst part. It was gentle. It was the voice of someone greeting a long-awaited guest.

"Do you know what you took from me?" She tilted her head, and the lantern-light caught the raw crescents scratched down both forearms, half-healed, reopened, half-healed again. "I've been trying to find out. I lie awake in camp and I reach for it and there's just—" her hand made a small, helpless opening gesture, fingers spreading like petals, "—this. This room with no furniture. This mouth with no word in it. I dream about a door and I can't remember what's behind it, but I wake up crying, so I know it mattered. So I *know*—"

"Voss." Odile kept her voice level. Kept her hands loose at her sides, though every stolen instinct in her body was screaming to reach out, to take, to *do*. "Stand down. Take your soldiers and go. What's behind these doors will burn your king's whole enterprise to ash, and I promise you don't want to die for his crown."

"My king." Something flickered across Voss's ruined face—not loyalty. Not anymore. Something colder and sadder. "Do you think I serve him? I serve the ledger. I serve order. Every memory in its proper place, every theft answered." She took a step forward, and the soldiers behind her shifted like a single animal. "You are the largest outstanding entry in my book, Covenant-Bearer. A hundred and forty-one thefts catalogued. Do you know how many I took to balance a fraction of what you carry? Do you know what it costs, standing in front of you, knowing the woman who emptied me is standing there *full*? Full of a thousand lives you stole, wearing them like jewelry—"

"They weren't given!" The words tore out of Odile before she could leash them, ragged with a guilt she had been swallowing since Salt Bridge. "I know what I am. I've known since the bridge, since the ledger, since your name went into my nightmares. I'm a thief. I've always been a thief. But Veyran wears nine lords' memories as a *crown*—he has hollowed out an entire Court—and you march for him, and you stand there telling me about theft—"

"I tell you about theft," Voss whispered, "because you *taught* me what it is."

She raised her hand, and the vanguard raised their lanterns, and the grey-white light bent inward like a tide turning.

* * *

It happened fast, the way terrible things do—all the slow dread beforehand and then a handful of seconds that lasted a lifetime.

Voss did not draw a weapon. She drew a memory—one of her own remaining ones, Odile realized, one she had hoarded against this moment, feeding it to her lantern like oil. The flame leapt from grey to a blinding silver-white, and she hurled it, not at Odile but at the doors themselves, at the covenant chamber's threshold, and the ancient stone screamed.

The wards that Brannic's sacrifice had opened flared and began to gutter. Odile felt it like a hand closing around her wrist—the chamber sealing, the oath going unsaid, everything they had crossed Rotwood and Salt Bridge and betrayal upon betrayal to reach for, sliding away.

"No," she breathed, and reached for her hoard without deciding to, the way a drowning person reaches for the surface. She pulled a memory up and out—a soldier's memory, a stranger's, a wedding-day bright as brass—and cast it into the nearest lantern. The flame swallowed it and guttered blue, confused, and two soldiers near it staggered, blinking, suddenly unsure why they were here, who had told them to raise their weapons.

"Stop her," Voss said, and there was nothing gentle in her voice now, only the howl behind the hole. "Take her hoard. Empty her. Empty her the way she emptied—"

The vanguard surged.

What followed was not a battle; battles have lines and orders and shape. It was a collapse. Sable's voice cracked out liturgical words that made three soldiers drop their lanterns and clutch their heads. Caspian's sword finally left its sheath, ringing, as he waded into the press to keep them off Odile's flank. Brannic stood unmoving at the chamber doors with his sightless-to-magic face turned toward the chaos, listening, listening—and then he shouted, sudden and urgent:

"Odile! The west door—it remembers being a door—she's going to seal it—"

But Odile wasn't listening to Brannic. Because Maren Voss had stopped throwing memories at walls, and started walking toward her instead, straight through her own soldiers, silver light dripping from her fingertips, and her ruined eyes had fixed on Odile with an expression Odile recognized from the inside.

Hunger. The specific hunger of a person reaching for the thing that was taken.

"You have it," Voss breathed, close enough now that Odile could see the tremor in her jaw, the tear tracks cutting through road dust. "I can see it. Behind your eyes. My worst day—I can almost see it—you're *carrying* it, you've been carrying it this whole time, give it *back*—"

"I don't keep what I steal from people I—" Odile caught herself. Lied. The oldest rule of thieves: deny the hoard. But Voss was already lunging, silver light flaring, and Odile felt the inquisitor's will slam against hers like a wave against a seawall, hungry and enormous and mad, scrabbling for purchase in Odile's mind, hunting through her hoard for one particular door—

And Odile, in pure reflex, in the survival instinct that had kept her alive in the Hollow Valley for twenty-nine years, slammed that door shut and opened another one instead, and *pushed*.

She pushed a memory into Maren Voss.

Not a stranger's this time. Not a shield. She reached into the inquisitor's fraying mind and gave her back a piece of what she had taken at the border of Verance—gave it back too fast, too hard, all at once, the way you'd throw a drowning man a millstone. Voss's worst day returned to her in a single blinding instant, uncompressed, unforgiven, and the inquisitor screamed and went down to her knees, lantern clattering, hands clawing at her own skull.

The pressure against Odile's mind vanished. Around the court, soldiers stumbled, lanterns guttering, some of them simply standing down in confusion as the silver spell-light died. Sable was shouting for retreat or surrender, Caspian bleeding from a cut above his eye, Brannic still singing out door-warnings—

And through the gap in the chaos, one of the vanguard broke ranks.

Later, Odile would try to reconstruct those seconds and fail. She would remember the silver flare of Voss collapsing, remember turning toward the chamber doors, remember thinking *we're through, we're through*—and then she would remember Wick.

Wick had been at her blind side the whole time. Of course they had. They had promised, hadn't they, in their sideways, lockpick way. And when the soldier came through the smoke—no lantern now, just a plain steel blade and the blank obedience of a person following the last order they'd understood—Wick saw the angle, saw that Odile had turned away, saw the blade's line running straight toward her spine.

Odile heard Wick shout her name. One syllable. Just her name, in that cracked-adolescent voice that had once demanded, *never take mine*, in a bunkhouse in Bellharbor with rain on the roof.

Then she heard the sound the blade made.

She turned in time to catch Wick as they fell. That was the whole of her heroism: she turned, and she caught them, and they both went down onto the cold white stone of the Court that remembered covenants, and her hands were full of Wick's blood faster than her mind could understand it.

"Wick. Hey. Hey, look at me—"

Wick looked at her. That was the unbearable part—they looked at her, eyes wide and shocked and already going glassy, and they tried to grin. Blood at the corner of their mouth. The wound was high, under the collarbone, angling down, and Odile had stolen enough dying men's last hours to know exactly what she was holding.

"S'their fault," Wick slurred, jerking their chin vaguely at the soldier, whom Caspian had already put down. "Bad... bad footwork. Left side open. I always say—always say—"

"Don't talk. Sable! *Sable!*"

Sable was already there, robes torn into bandages, hands pressing hard, her voice steady in the way that only breaks your heart: "Pressure. Keep pressure. Odile. *Odile.* Look at me. She needs a healer and the nearest one is inside that room, so you need to stand up, and you need to finish what we came here to do. Do you hear me? That's how you save her."

Around them, the vanguard was breaking—not routed, but dissolving, soldiers wandering free of the silver compulsion, some kneeling, some fleeing down the causeway. Voss knelt in the wreckage of her own returned memory, rocking, keening softly, a woman holding the pieces of her worst day and finding they cut worse whole than missing. No one moved to stop them. There was no one left with the will to give the order.

Odile knelt on the stone with Wick's blood soaking through her sleeves and understood, with perfect clarity, that she was looking at her own reflection. Armed. Uniformed. Hollowed out by the same hunger that had fed her for twenty-nine years, marching on the same door she was trying to open. Voss had come to take back a memory, and Odile had come to give away every one she had, and somewhere between those two intentions lay the entire rotten architecture of a world that taught the desperate to feed on each other.

"Odile." Wick's hand found hers, slick and small and gripping with all the strength left in it. Their voice was a thread now. "Hey. The thing I asked you. That night. Remember?"

Never take theirs. Never, no matter what.

"I remember," Odile said, and her voice broke clean in half. "I swear it. I'll never touch them. Even to save you. Especially to save you. They stay yours."

"'At's the..." Wick's eyes were closing. "Whole point... of having 'em..."

Sable's hands pressed harder. Caspian lifted Wick's head gently onto his knee, and his face, when Odile glanced at it, was stripped of every charm and cleverness she had ever resented in him—just a man watching a child bleed, promising himself something.

The covenant chamber's doors stood open behind them, waiting. Inside, the nine thrones. Inside, the price. Inside, whatever fragment of the Court's memory remained to tell her how it must be paid.

Odile laid Wick carefully in Sable's arms, stood up, and turned to face the doors.

Behind her, faint and failing, Wick whispered something that might have been a joke. Ahead of her, in the dark of the chamber, something ancient stirred on its moth-eaten throne and began, at last, to speak.
