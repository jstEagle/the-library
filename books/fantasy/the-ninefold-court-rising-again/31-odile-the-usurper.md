## 31. Odile the Usurper

The Court had gone quiet in the way that a house goes quiet after a funeral—when everyone has finally stopped whispering and the silence itself begins to grieve.

Odile walked alone through the great hall of the Ninefold Throne, her boots loud on stone that had not heard footsteps in a hundred years. Torchlight did not live here; it merely visited. The braziers along the walls burned with a pale green flame that gave light without warmth, and every shadow they cast was the wrong shape, too long, too patient, like shadows that remembered being something else.

Nine thrones stood on the dais at the hall's end.

She had seen them once already, on their first terrible walk through this mausoleum, when Brannic still had his gift and Wick still had all his blood inside him instead of pooled beneath the eastern colonnade where Sable had carried him. She had looked at them then with the awe of a pilgrim. Now she looked at them the way a thief looks at an unlocked door.

That was the change. That was what three days of grief and fear had done to her.

The ninth throne waited at the center, higher than its siblings by a single step, carved from white wood that might have been birch or might have been bone. Its seat was empty. It had been empty for a century. And it was not empty the way a chair is empty—it was empty the way a mouth is empty before it speaks, the way a cradle is empty before the child is laid in it.

*For blood, not keys,* Brannic had said, back when he could still hear doors. *It wants you.*

* * *

Three days since Veyran's riders had come through the shattered gate like a black tide with Maren Voss half-mad at its head. Three days since Wick had stepped between Odile and an inquisitor's memory-blade—a blade designed to cut recollection from the living, to leave a body breathing and hollow—and taken the stroke meant for her. Three nights of Sable working by candlelight over the boy's wound while Brannic sat outside the sickroom, deaf now to everything but ordinary sound, listening to Wick breathe the way other people pray.

And one day since Caspian had walked out the gate with his hands raised and his voice steady, offering himself to Veyran as the price of an army's withdrawal, and Veyran had laughed—that laugh Odile could still hear if she let herself, a sound like coins spilling across marble—and taken him.

They had watched from the ruined wall. Odile had watched Caspian go, and she had done nothing, because there was nothing to do, because the army was ten thousand strong and the free cities' militias were still two weeks away and the covenant chamber below the thrones required a price she could not bring herself to pay.

Or so she told herself.

The truth was uglier, and it lived here, in this hall, in the shape of nine empty chairs.

* * *

She climbed the dais slowly. Each step rang faintly underfoot, though the stone made no such sound anywhere else in the Court, and with each ring the air thickened around her like water deepening over a drowning woman's head.

Her hoard stirred.

It always stirred near the thrones—she had noticed it the second night, lying awake on a bedroll in the herald's antechamber, feeling her stolen memories shift against the walls of her mind like fish rising to feed. A thousand lives. A thousand borrowed yesterdays: the ferryman's first snow, a stranger's wedding day spent on Salt Bridge, a mother's lullaby she'd stolen on the coast road during the worst night of her life, lullabies and funerals and harvest festivals and the taste of forty different childhoods, none of them hers.

Here, in the heart of the Court that magic had abandoned, those memories were not quiet. They pressed outward. They wanted.

She reached the top of the dais and stood before the ninth throne, close enough to touch its armrest, and the throne looked back at her.

Not with eyes. Thrones do not have eyes. But there was attention in it, ancient and patient and terribly kind, the way a trap is kind to the animal it was built for. She felt it take her measure—the way Caspian had taken her measure that first impossible evening in the valley, except this thing had been measuring her for a century. Before she was born. Before her grandmother crossed the border with a trader's pack full of vials and committed the theft that broke the world.

*Heir,* the emptiness seemed to say. *At last.*

"Don't," Odile said aloud, and her voice cracked in the vast hall. "Don't call me that."

But the word had teeth, and it had found the soft place in her where every other name had failed to stick. Thief. Orphan. Covenant-Bearer. Stranger. She had worn them all like ill-fitting coats. But *heir*—heir fit. Heir had always fit, waiting for her here in the dark like a garment kept warm by a fire she hadn't known was lit.

She touched the armrest.

* * *

The vision came like a knife through silk.

One moment she was standing in a cold hall; the next she was standing in the same hall a century ago, and it was drowning in light. Banners of nine colors hung from rafters high as weather. The braziers burned gold. Music—she could not name the instruments, only that they sounded like rivers deciding to be glad—poured down from a gallery full of faces, fae and mortal mingled without distinction, and on the nine thrones sat the Nine, alive, luminous, each wearing a crown that was not metal but memory, visible thought woven into circlets of shifting light.

And at the foot of the dais, small and dark-haired and sharp-eyed, a girl of perhaps eight years ran laughing between the courtiers' legs, and the Ninth Lord rose from his throne to catch her, swinging her up onto his shoulders, and the whole hall turned to watch because she was—

Because she was the heir. Because the girl was the heir, and everyone loved her, and the light loved her, and the covenant sang through her like a struck bell, mortal and fae bound in one small laughing body.

Odile knew her face.

Of course she knew it. She saw it every morning in whatever water passed for a mirror on the road. The girl's face was her face, younger by twenty years and unshadowed by anything, a face that had never stolen anything because it had never needed to, a face that belonged.

The vision held her there for a long time—or no time; the Court did not keep hours anymore—and showed her everything the throne wanted her to see. The girl growing. The girl learning oaths. The girl at seventeen, standing on this very dais, being presented to both realms as the living knot that tied them together. And then—the vision flinched, blurred, went gray at the edges like a page left in rain—then nothing. A hole. A century of nothing where a girl should have grown into a queen.

*This is yours,* the throne said, without words, in the language of longing. *It was taken. I can give it back.*

* * *

"No, it can't."

Sable's voice, behind her. Odile came back to herself with her hand still on the armrest and her cheeks wet and the hall gray and cold again, the vision folded away like a letter returned unopened.

The old priestess stood at the base of the dais, leaning on her staff, her bare head silver in the green light. She had burned her robes in Verance; what she wore now was a traveler's coat patched at both elbows, and she wore it like armor.

"You saw it," Sable said. It wasn't a question.

"I saw a little girl." Odile wiped her face with the heel of her hand, furious at the tears, furious at how easily they came now, as if the last three days had broken some seal in her. "I saw myself. Before. When I was—" Her voice caught. "When I was someone who belonged somewhere."

"And the throne showed you how to have it again." Sable climbed the steps, slow, joint by joint, until she stood beside her. "Sit down, take your hoard, pour a thousand stolen lives into these nine chairs, and crown yourself with what's left. Rule the Nine Realms as the heir who came home. Yes?"

Odile said nothing.

"Child, I have served this Court since before your mother was born. I know what these thrones do when they are hungry. They do not lie, exactly. They show you true things and let you draw false conclusions. It is the oldest trick in any court, mortal or fae." She tapped her staff once against the white wood of the ninth throne, and the sound was like a knell. "Ask it the question it didn't answer."

"What question?"

"The one about Caspian." Sable's eyes were old and pitiless and kind. "You want to be queen? Then learn to think like one. If you sit that throne tonight—with Veyran's army at the gates and the covenant still broken and your hoard spent on crowns instead of oaths—what happens to the man who traded himself for you?"

* * *

After Sable left her—she had not asked Odile to leave; she had simply gone, trusting the lesson to do its work—Odile remained on the dais, and the temptation unfolded itself properly at last, the way it had been waiting to do, patiently, for three days.

Because here was the arithmetic, laid out plain in the green torchlight:

She had a thousand memories. The covenant required her to surrender them—all of them, the herald had said, every stolen yesterday poured out into the nine thrones to wake the sleeping lords, including the memories that were never stolen at all, including her own self, her own name, her own twenty-odd years of being Odile Eastwick. The Covenant-Bearer gives everything. The herald's moth-eaten voice had been very clear about that. *What was stolen must be returned, and the returner must return whole.*

And what did she get? A world saved. A war ended, maybe. A Court refilled with fae lords who would owe her everything and remember none of it. And Odile Eastwick—gone. Not dead, perhaps. But unwritten. A blank page walking around in a body that strangers would love and she would never recognize.

Versus the other road.

Sit the throne. Pour the hoard in—not all of it, never all of it, keep the keystone memories, keep the lullaby, keep the snow, keep herself—and rule. She had felt, in the vision's afterglow, exactly how much power was coiled in that seat. The Nine had drawn their strength from the covenant; a bearer who refused to dissolve it could hold its threads in her own fist instead. She could wake the lords as servants rather than kings. She could break Veyran's army in an afternoon. She could take Caspian back—not trade for him, *take* him, tear him out of the usurper's camp with a word and a wave of covenant-fire.

She would lose nothing.

That was the poison in it, and she drank the poison down and examined it honestly, because she was a thief and thieves know quality. She would lose nothing except the one thing the sacrifice was for.

She would keep every memory and be owed no love for keeping them. She would sit the ninth throne as a thief sits a vault—owner of everything, belonging nowhere. She would be Veyran with better hair and a sadder story. The realization arrived without drama, the way most truths do: quiet, complete, irreversible.

The usurper wore nine lords' memories as a crown. That was the whole of him, Sable said; strip the crown and there was nothing underneath but appetite. And Odile, standing before the throne with her hoard humming in her skull like a hive of golden bees, understood that the difference between her and Veyran had never been talent or opportunity. Only choice. Only the choice she was standing inside of right now, weighing, turning over, testing its weight like a purse of questionable coin.

The throne waited. It was very good at waiting. It had waited a hundred years.

"I know what you're doing," she told it softly. "You're showing me the door with no lock. Every mark I've ever worked, I worked the same way. You make the prize look free."

The throne said nothing, which was, in its way, an admission.

She thought of the Hollow Valley. Of the rules she'd built her life on there, scratched into the underside of her own conscience where no inquisitor could read them: *never steal pain; never steal from family; never leave the valley.* She had thought, crossing the border, that the third rule was cowardice. She knew better now. All three rules were the same rule, written three ways: *do not take what is not freely given.* Pain given is confession. Family given is love. A life given is a gift.

And a crown taken—even a crown that fits—is still theft.

Her whole life, she had owned a thousand yesterdays and lived none of them. If she sat this throne, she would own nine realms and belong to none. The loneliness would simply get bigger. It would get thrones.

*Everything except the one thing you want,* she thought. That was what the dark offered her, and the dark was honest about it, and that honesty was almost the worst part—that even damnation, even this, came itemized and signed.

Wick's voice floated up from somewhere below the dais, thin and reedy and gloriously alive: arguing with Brannic about whether deafness to doors counted as an excuse for losing at cards. Sable's low answering rumble. The ordinary music of the found family, leaking up through a century-old floor, into a hall built for majesty, and making majesty sound like nothing at all.

Odile took her hand off the armrest.

It resisted—not the wood, but the will inside it, a reluctance like the last inch of a boot coming off in mud. She felt the throne's attention withdraw by degrees, unhurried, unoffended, and she understood that it would wait. It would wait until her resolve was weakest, until Wick died or Caspian didn't come back or Veyran's fires lit the sky, and it would offer again, and the offer would be just as beautiful, and she would have to refuse it again, and then again, every hour of every day for the rest of a reign that would last exactly as long as she could bear the weight of everything she'd refused to lose.

Unless.

Unless there was a third road. Unless the herald had gotten the terms wrong, or hidden something, or unless a moth-eaten fragment of a dead Court's memory had simply not known everything—as who did?

She descended the dais. At the bottom step she paused, and—because she was who she was, because a thief leaves nothing unexamined—she looked back at the ninth throne one last time.

"You'll have to take my word for it," she told it quietly. "I'm going to find out what the prophecy actually says. The last line. The one nobody reads twice." Her jaw set. "Because I don't believe for one moment that a book clever enough to write down my dinner date a hundred years early forgot to mention whether the ending was death or something else."

Behind her, in the green dark, the empty throne sat silent on its single high step—and for the first time in a hundred years, it felt, very faintly, afraid.

Above, far off beyond the broken gates, a horn sounded from Veyran's camp. Long. Low. Answered by another, and another, until the whole horizon rang.

Dawn was coming. So was the end of everything—one way or the other—and Odile Eastwick went to find Mother Sable and the book that had started it all.
