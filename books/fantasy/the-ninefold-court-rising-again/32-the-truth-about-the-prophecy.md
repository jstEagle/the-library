## 32. The Truth About the Prophecy

Mother Sable had spent sixty years learning to read the way other people learned to pray: on her knees, in the dark, until her eyes ached and the letters began to speak.

The scriptorium beneath the Court's gate was not a room so much as a wound—a hollow where the mountain's stone had been scooped away by hands that remembered how to shape it like clay. Fae architecture. Even ruined, even dead for a century, the walls still hummed faintly under her fingertips, like a bell that had been struck once and never allowed to finish ringing. She had set up her work here because it was the only place left in the Nine Realms where ink did not fade. The covenant's death had rotted everything else—crops at the borders, roads, rivers, the memory of names—but the old words held. Old words always held. That was the trouble with them.

She spread the prophecy book open before her and weighted its corners with four smooth stones she'd carried from Verance, from the courtyard where she had burned her robes. The book was a century old, bound in grey leather gone soft as moth wing, and it smelled of dust and myrrh and something underneath both—something like snow falling on warm skin. The smell of magic dying slowly.

*The Covenant-Bearer shall stand at the ninth throne,* the last page read, *and there she shall pour out all that she has carried, and she shall be unmade, and the nine doors shall open.*

Unmade.

Sable had read that word a hundred times since they'd descended into the Court's shadow. Unmade. In every tongue she knew—and she knew eleven, some of them older than the free cities—the word meant the same thing. Ended. Undone. A candle pinched out between wet fingers. She had watched Odile read it too, two nights ago, by the light of a lamp Wick had rigged from rendered fat and a scrap of wick pulled from Sable's own sleeve. She had watched the girl's face do something very quiet and very terrible: nothing at all. The face of someone who had already said goodbye to herself and was simply waiting for the appointment.

That was what frightened Sable. Not the dying. Dying she understood; dying was honest work. It was the *waiting*—Odile moving through the ruined halls like a woman packing a house she'd already sold, touching nothing, claiming nothing, hoarding her last days the way she'd once hoarded other people's memories. As if she believed the prophecy had already finished writing her.

Sable turned the page.

There was no page to turn. The book ended. But she had noticed, three days ago, in the lamplight, something she had missed in a hundred readings before: a shadow along the gutter of the binding, a thickness where the leather met the paper, as though the book were swallowing something.

She took up her knife—the small bone-handled one she used for trimming wicks, for cutting bread, for all the humble surgeries of camp life—and she laid its edge against the spine.

"Forgive me," she told the book. "You've been read wrong for a hundred years. I mean to correct that."

The binding parted with a sound like a sigh. Inside the spine, pressed flat against a century of pressure, was a strip of vellum no wider than her thumb, folded over and over until it was thin as a wafer. The ink on it had gone brown. But it had not faded. Of course it hadn't. They were inside the Court now, where old words kept their promises.

She unfolded it with fingers that shook—not from age, though age helped, but from the specific trembling of a woman about to learn whether she had spent her whole life believing a lie or a truth, and which would be worse.

*And the unmaking is not the ending.*

Sable read it four times. Then she lit every lamp in the scriptorium, all seven of them, and read it again aloud, because the old covenant taught that words spoken in light could not lie.

*And the unmaking is not the ending. What is poured out is not lost but returned. The Bearer who stands at the ninth throne shall give up all that she is—and she shall not die. She shall simply no longer be herself who completes it.*

Sable sat down on the cold stone floor, quite suddenly, the way old bodies decide things without consulting their owners, and she laughed.

It was not a pretty laugh. It came out cracked and half-sobbed, echoing off the humming walls, and she pressed the back of her hand to her mouth to stop it and failed. Sixty years. Sixty years of carrying the covenant's failure like a stone sewn into her chest—she had been a novice when it broke, seventeen years old, standing in the cloister at Verance while the sky over the borders went the color of old pewter, while the first of the fae houses went silent, while her abbess wept and lied and said *it cannot be mended, child, only endured*. Sixty years of endurance, of defrocking, of burning incense to a pact everyone said was dead, and here, folded into a book's spine like a love letter no one bothered to send—

Here was the door.

Not an ending. A door.

*She shall simply no longer be herself who completes it.* Sable traced the words with one blunt fingertip, careful, the way you touch a sleeping animal. The distinction was fine and cruel and luminous. Not *she shall die*. Not *she shall be destroyed*. The prophecy's grammar—the old high grammar of the First Tongue, the grammar Sable had bled to learn in her novitiate—was precise in the way only merciless things are precise. *Herself.* The self ends. The woman continues.

A snake sheds its skin and lives. A river pours itself into the sea and is not murdered by it. A mother becomes a mother by ceasing, entirely and forever, to be only herself.

Death and transformation, she thought, are the same door viewed from different sides of the wall. The difference is whether anyone opens it from the far side.

*

She found Odile in the chamber of the thrones.

Of course she did. Where else? The girl had taken to sitting there the way penitents took to sitting in chapels—not praying, exactly. Auditing. Sable stood in the doorway of the vast hall and watched her, and felt her heart do the slow bruising thing it had been doing since Verance.

The hall had been beautiful once; you could see its ghost through the ruin. Nine alcoves fanned out from a central dais like the petals of a stone flower, each one cradling a throne, each throne carved from a different substance—there was one of black glass, one of living wood long since gone grey and brittle, one that might have been coral, one of iron gone to lace with rust. Above them the ceiling had been painted, and shreds of it still clung: nine banners, nine houses, nine colors bleeding down the walls like wounds that refused to close.

And in the thrones—or rather, slumped beside them, curled at their feet like dogs waiting for masters who would never come home—were the fae.

Memoryless husks, Caspian called them. Sable had another word, one from her order's oldest litanies: *the Unremembered*. Beings who had lived ten thousand years and now stood in their own halls without knowing why they were standing, without knowing their own names, their own shapes, staring at intruders with the mild blank courtesy of strangers at a funeral. One of them—a tall figure whose skin held a faint shimmer, like moonlight caught in oil—had spent the entire morning arranging pebbles into a spiral on the floor, over and over, as though the pattern almost meant something, as though the meaning were just behind a door he could no longer find.

Odile sat on the steps below the dais, knees drawn up, watching him.

"He does that every day," she said, without turning around. Sable's boots made no sound on the stone; she wondered how the girl had known. "Same spiral. Same number of stones. When it's done he looks at it like he's waiting for it to tell him something." Odile's voice was flat and careful, the voice of someone conserving herself. "Then he sweeps it apart and starts again."

"It was probably a sigil," Sable said. "Something from his house. Some working he did ten thousand times."

"Yeah." Odile hugged her knees tighter. "That's what I figured."

Sable came down the steps and lowered herself onto them beside the girl, joints complaining, cloak pooling. For a while neither spoke. The pebble-spiral builder reached the end of his spiral, regarded it with that terrible patient hope, and swept it apart.

"I've been reading," Sable said.

"I know. You haven't come to dinner in three days. Wick's offended. He won't say so, but he keeps making extra."

"There's a page hidden in the spine of the prophecy book. Folded into the binding. A century of readers missed it—including, I think, the people who wrote the summaries your friend Caspian memorized." Sable heard her own voice steady itself, the old pulpit cadence rising in it unbidden, and this time she let it rise. "I'm going to say it plainly, child, because you've had enough of people saying things to you sideways."

Odile turned. Her eyes in the dim hall were the color of the valley's winter streams, and guarded, and tired in a way sleep doesn't fix.

"The Covenant-Bearer does not die."

The silence after those five words was the loudest thing in the hall. Somewhere above them water dripped from the broken ceiling, counting time the old way.

"That's what it says?" Odile asked finally. Very quietly. "That's actually what it says?"

"Not in those words. In better ones. Listen—" And Sable unfolded the vellum strip and read it to her, all of it, twice, in the First Tongue and then in plain speech, and she watched the words go into the girl like water into ground that had been drought-stricken too long to trust rain. Watched her want to believe and refuse to want to believe, both at once, the way you watch someone test ice.

"'No longer herself who completes it,'" Odile repeated. "What does that mean, exactly? Because I've noticed prophecies like to be exact right up until the moment they're not."

"It means your self is the price, not your life." Sable took the girl's hand—it was cold, and scarred across the palm from years of picking locks and pockets and, gods forgive her, memories—and held it the way she used to hold the hands of the dying, firmly, so they'd know the grip was real. "Everything you carry. Everything you *are*, maybe. The thief goes into that fire, Odile. The woman walks out. I can't tell you which parts of you survive, because the prophecy doesn't know either. It speaks in doors, not rooms." She squeezed. "But it says you walk out."

Odile looked down at their joined hands. "You're sure it's genuine. Not something someone planted. Veyran plants things. Caspian—" She stopped on his name, the way you stop on a stair that isn't there.

"I've dated the vellum. It's cut from the same skin as the book's own pages. Whoever hid it hid it before the ink dried, a century ago, when hiding it served no one's purpose but the truth's." Sable smiled, and felt the smile crack open something in her own chest, sixty years of grief finding a hairline fracture to leak through. "Child, I have lied to congregations and been lied to by abbesses, and I know a forgery the way you know a loaded pocket. This is true."

"Then why hide it?" Odile's voice sharpened, and there she was—the thief, the survivor, the woman who trusted nothing because nothing had ever earned it. "Why fold the good news into a spine where nobody would find it? If the Court wanted its heir to walk in willingly, why bury the one sentence that would make it bearable?"

And Sable, who had asked herself the same question through three sleepless nights, gave the answer that had risen in her like sediment settling.

"Because whoever wrote it didn't want to lie to you, and couldn't bear to tell you." She gestured at the hall—at the husks, at the pebbles, at the nine empty thrones with their century of dust. "Imagine being one of *them*. Imagine knowing that your rescue requires a mortal woman to walk into a fire that every translation of the prophecy says will kill her. Imagine being able to add one line—one merciful, ambiguous line—that might save her from despair, but might also make her careless, make her hesitate at the crucial moment, make her try to keep pieces of herself back and so fail everyone." Sable's voice dropped. "Mercy is heavy, Odile. Sometimes the kindest thing you can hand someone is the whole weight of it, unlabeled. They chose the weight. I think they prayed, while they chose it. I think that's why they hid it in the binding—close enough to be found by whoever needed finding, far enough to never tempt a coward."

Odile was silent a long moment. Then she laughed—short, disbelieving, wet at the edges.

"So the message is: *you probably won't die, but don't count on it.*"

"The message," Sable said, "is that hope in this world has teeth. It always did. That's what the old covenant was *for*—not comfort. Teeth. Something to bite down on when the dark comes." She rose, joints crackling, and stood over the girl on the dais steps. "You'll lose yourself in there. I won't insult you by pretending otherwise—I watched my abbess lie about the covenant's breaking, and I swore I'd never lie to save someone's feelings again. You will pour out everything, and what remains will not remember the valley, or the thefts, or possibly any of us. That is real, and it is terrible, and you may grieve it as long as you need."

"But."

"But you will remain." Sable put her hand on the girl's head, an old blessing, awkward and unfashionable and utterly sincere. "Somebody will have to teach you your own name again. And child—I have been looking forward to that duty since the day I met you."

*

That night, for the first time since the descent, Sable went to dinner.

Wick had built the fire up big, the way teenagers do, as if heat were a form of defiance against the dark outside the gate. Brannic sat close to it, turning his ear toward the flames—since Rotwood he flinched at loud sounds, his gift fraying at the edges, though he pretended otherwise, and Sable let him pretend because pretending was also a kind of strength. There was stew, more or less. There was hard bread. There was Wick talking with his mouth full about the locks on the ninth gate, which he had mapped from memory in charcoal on a scrap of hide, in case anyone needed to leave quickly, and Sable saw Odile glance at that scrap and understand what the boy was really drawing: an exit for her. A way out that wasn't the fire.

And Caspian—

Caspian sat at the edge of the circle, where he had sat since the night of the burned page, eating little, speaking less, a man serving a sentence he had assigned himself. Odile had not forgiven him. Sable doubted she would. But she passed him the bread tonight, without meeting his eyes, and he took it with both hands like communion, and Sable thought: *there. That's the door too. Narrower than the others, but a door.*

Later, when the fire had settled and Wick slept with his charcoal map under his cheek like a talisman, Sable sat awake with the vellum strip and copied it out fair, letter by letter, in her best hand, onto fresh paper. Two copies. One for Odile, to hold, to burn if she needed, to keep if she could. One for herself—to read aloud at whatever came next, in light, where words could not lie.

Because the army was coming. Everyone could feel it now, a pressure behind the ears, a taste of iron on the wind off the plains. Veyran's banners would crest the hills within days, perhaps hours, and Maren Voss would be riding at the front of them with her stolen-wrong eyes, and somewhere beyond the hills Caspian's old king sat wearing nine dead lords' memories like a crown and calling himself whole.

Hope with teeth, Sable thought, folding the copies away. Well then. Let it bite.

In the deepest hour of the night, a sound woke the camp—a cry from Wick's bedroll, raw and cracking, and then the boy's voice, hoarse with fever, saying her name.

"Mother Sable. Mother Sable—he won't wake up—"
