## 33. Wick Wakes

Wick had always assumed that dying would feel like a door.

They had thought about it more than most children think about anything, because when you have no memories before your sixth year, the question of where you came from becomes a question of where you're going, and the two doors start to look suspiciously alike. So Wick had imagined it: a door, and a latch, and on the other side—what? Nothing? Everything? The thing they'd been stolen from?

What dying actually felt like was a fever made of light, and a weight on their chest like a hand pressing down, and a voice saying *stay, stay, stay* in a tone that brooked no argument.

The voice belonged to Mother Sable. Wick knew it even before opening their eyes, the way you know rain by its smell.

When they finally did open them, the ceiling above was not any ceiling Wick knew. It was high and vaulted and made of pale stone shot through with veins of silver, like frost grown backward into rock, and along its edges ran carvings of vines and moons and faces that might have been sleeping or might have been dead. The air smelled of cold stone and something sweeter underneath—night-blooming flowers, maybe, or candlewax from a temple no one had prayed in for a hundred years.

"Ah," said Mother Sable, from somewhere to the left. "There you are."

"There I am," Wick agreed. Their voice came out as a rasp, like a key dragged through a rusty lock. They tried again. "Am I dead?"

"Not yet. Though you gave it a serious attempt." Sable's face swam into view above them—brown and seamed and utterly unsentimental, her white-streaked hair tied back with what appeared to be a strip torn from her own sleeve. "Three cracked ribs. A wound in your side that I stitched twice because you tore it once thrashing. A fever that lasted four days and spoke to people who weren't there."

"Who'd I talk to?"

"A woman called Merrin. Repeatedly." Sable's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "You asked her to let go of the boat."

Wick stared at the ceiling. "I don't know anyone called Merrin."

"No," Sable said softly. "I don't expect you do."

That was the thing about having no memories before age six. Other people's ghosts wandered through your fevers wearing names you didn't recognize, and you couldn't even tell if they were yours.

Wick tried to sit up and immediately regretted every decision that had led to this moment. Pain bloomed along their right side, hot and specific, and Sable's hand pressed them flat with the gentle authority of someone who had been defrocked but never de-armed.

"The healer-priestesses of the old covenant had a saying," she said. "'The body keeps its own ledger.' Yours is badly overdrawn. Lie still."

"Where—" Wick's head throbbed. They pushed past it. "Where's Odile?"

And there it was—the change in Sable's face. Small, quickly smoothed away, but Wick had spent their whole remembered life reading faces, because faces were all they'd had. Faces and locks, and both opened the same way: patiently, with attention to the small give and catch of things.

"She's alive," Sable said.

"That's not an answer to the question I asked."

"It's the only answer I'm giving tonight." Sable rose, joints crackling, and poured water from a clay jug into a cup. "Drink. Sleep. In the morning, if the fever stays down, you may conduct whatever interrogations your heart desires."

"Sable." Wick caught her wrist. Their hand felt absurdly small around it. "Is she going to do it?"

Sable went very still.

The court around them breathed. Somewhere far off, water dripped in a slow rhythm, counting seconds in a place that had stopped counting years. The carved faces on the ceiling watched with their sleeping-dead eyes.

"I don't know," Sable said at last, and it sounded like it cost her something to admit it. "I told her what the last line means. What it truly means. She has not spoken since."

"What did you tell her?"

"Something you should hear from her first. Or from me tomorrow, if she can't manage it." Sable freed her wrist—not pulling away, just loosening Wick's grip with two fingers, the way you'd loosen a child's hold on something sharp. "But I'll say this much, Wick, because I think you've earned it, and because I am an old woman who is tired of carrying things alone: whatever she decides, it will not be a door closing. Do you understand me? Whatever she chooses, it will be a door opening. Doors only frighten us when we believe nothing is on the other side."

"And if there's nothing on the other side?"

"Then we will build something there," said Mother Sable, "the way we built everything else. Out of scraps. Out of showing up." She tucked the blanket under Wick's chin with surprising gentleness for hands that had burned her own priestess robes in a council square. "Sleep now, little lockpick. You've picked harder locks than sleep."

Wick slept.

* * *

They woke again to lamplight and the sound of someone failing to be quiet.

It was a particular kind of failure. Brannic moved through the world like a man perpetually apologizing to it—big shoulders folded in, big feet placed with exaggerated care—and he was failing at silence now in exactly his usual way, which meant Wick heard him from four rooms away, or would have, if there had been four rooms between. There weren't. This chamber opened onto a colonnade, and beyond the colonnade stretched a hall so vast that the lamplight didn't reach its far end, and Brannic sat on the floor against a pillar with his back to Wick, and he was crying.

Not loudly. Brannic never did anything loudly except fight. It was the quiet kind, the kind that shook the shoulders and kept the face turned away, and it went through Wick like a fishhook.

"Brannic."

He flinched hard enough to knock his elbow against the pillar. Then he was up and crossing the room in three strides, dropping to his knees beside the cot, and his huge hand hovered over Wick's arm without landing, the way it always did, as if he were afraid his touch was too heavy for the world.

"You're awake," he whispered. "Sable said—if the fever broke—you were awake, but I didn't believe—I had to see—"

"Well, don't look too long. I'm hideous when I convalesce."

A wet, broken laugh came out of him. He wiped his face on his forearm, leaving a dark streak across the tattoos there—old ones, fae-script ones, the marks of a foundling nobody had claimed until a thief and a priestess and a lockpick had claimed him by accident. "Four days," he said. "You were—there was blood, Wick, so much blood, and Voss's soldiers, and you—"

"I remember some of it." And here was the strange thing, the thing Wick turned over now like a lockpick turned in a stubborn tumbler: they did remember. Not before six—never before six—but the road, Rotwood, Salt Bridge, the smell of Caspian's burnt page, the weight of Odile's hand on their shoulder when she taught them the second rule of stealing (*never steal pain; pain remembers you back*)—all of it was there, vivid as fresh ink. Four days of fever hadn't touched it.

Which meant the memory-wraith in Rotwood hadn't gotten them after all. Which meant the only hole in Wick's head was the original one, the big one, the one shaped like everything before age six.

"Merrin," Wick said suddenly. "Brannic. When I had the fever. Did I ever say a name—Merrin—to you?"

Brannic frowned. "No. Should I know it?"

"No. No one does." Wick looked up at the vaulted ceiling, at the sleeping-dead faces. "Sable says I asked her to let go of the boat. Whoever Merrin is, she must've held on too long. Or I did." They swallowed. "Do you think memories can leak? From wherever mine went? Like water under a door?"

"I think," Brannic said carefully, "that you are the bravest person I know, and that you ask questions at three in the morning that should wait for daylight."

"That's not an answer either."

"No one in this company gives real answers anymore." His jaw tightened. "Caspian's gone to the gates. Odile won't eat. Sable talks to ceilings. We're all holding our breaths waiting for someone to decide something, and meanwhile you nearly died, and I—" His voice cracked clean through. "I heard nine doors when we came in, Wick. I heard them. And now I can't hear anything, and I keep reaching for them in my sleep, and they're *gone*, and I don't regret it, that's the terrible thing, I'd do it again, but I wake up and for half a heartbeat the world is just—quiet, just stone and air, and I understand now why hearing-less people go mad."

Wick reached up and took his hovering hand and pulled it down and pressed it flat against their own bandaged side, over the stitches, where it could feel the living proof of heartbeat.

"You're not mad," they said. "You're sad. Sad's different. Sad means the thing mattered."

Brannic bowed his head over their hand like a man at prayer.

* * *

She came at dusk.

Wick knew it was dusk because the great windows of the colonnade had gone the color of bruised plums, and the light that came through them lay in long amber bars across the floor, and dust drifted through those bars like slow golden snow. Wick had been awake for hours, propped on pillows, cataloguing the room the way they catalogued everything—a habit of a person with a hole in their head, building inventories in case the inventory was all they ever got.

Odile stood in the doorway of the colonnade, and she looked wrong.

Not hurt. Not aged. Wrong the way a room looks wrong when one piece of furniture has been removed and you can't say which. She was thin—thinner than the road had made her—and her dark hair hung loose and unbrushed, and her eyes had the flat shine of polished obsidian, reflecting everything, giving back nothing. She carried the prophecy book under her arm like a brick.

"Hey, thief," Wick said.

"Hey, picklock." Her voice scraped. She crossed the colonnade slowly, as though the floor were thin ice, and stopped at the foot of the cot, and looked at Wick lying there with their ribs strapped and their color bad, and something in her face crumpled and reassembled itself, crumpled and reassembled, like paper being folded and unfolded too many times.

"I'm fine," Wick said. "Mostly fine. Sixty percent fine."

"You took a sword for me."

"I took a sword *near* me. There's a difference. Ask any fencer."

"Wick." Odile sat down on the edge of the cot, careful of the ribs, careful of everything, and put her hand over theirs, and her fingers were cold. "I need to tell you what happens next. Sable says you already suspect. I want you to hear it from me."

"The last line of the prophecy," Wick said. "It doesn't mean you die."

Odile blinked. "Sable told you."

"She told me it wasn't a door closing. I did the rest of the arithmetic myself." Wick shrugged, winced, kept shrugging anyway, out of principle. "It's obvious, once you stop panicking. Prophecies lie by compression. 'The Covenant-Bearer dies'—dies to *what*, though? To whom? There's no army in that line, no blade. Just her, and the covenant, and the returning of what was stolen." They looked up at her. "You die to yourself. That's the toll. The heir's memory comes back to the Court, and everything else goes into the thrones. Including us."

The silence stretched long enough for the plum-colored light to deepen toward ash.

"Yes," Odile said finally. "Yes. That's it exactly. Every stolen memory I carry—every life I lifted in the valley, every stranger's wedding day, every ferryman's snow—it all goes back. And my own. My childhood. My mother's voice. The valley. The road." Her thumb moved over Wick's knuckles, back and forth, back and forth, a nervous metronome. "Caspian's face. Your laugh. The night you picked the salt-toll gate and got stuck halfway and Brannic had to push you through like a cork."

"That was dignified."

"It was extremely dignified. That's the memory I'll miss most." She laughed—a single cracked note, nothing like a real laugh—and then pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "I keep rehearsing it. Did I tell you that? I sit in the throne room and I rehearse. I take each memory out and hold it up to the light and try to imagine letting it go, and I can't. I get to your laugh and I stop. Every time. I stop at your laugh, Wick. It's the wall I run into."

"Then don't do it."

"Don't do it?" She lowered her hand. Her eyes were wet and furious. "Veyran's army is at the gates. The free cities are undefended. The nine thrones are empty and the fae are husks and the covenant rots a little more every day, and you're telling me—"

"I'm telling you what you'd tell me." Wick sat up as far as the ribs allowed, which wasn't far, but far enough to meet her eyes straight on. "You taught me rules, remember? First rule: never steal pain. Second rule: pain remembers you back. Third rule—your rule, the valley's rule—never steal from family." They held her gaze. "So don't steal from us. Don't take our faces and our laughs and shove them in a box and hand the box over like it's payment. That's not sacrifice, Odile. That's just theft with better manners. We're not yours to spend."

"They wouldn't be stolen," she whispered. "They'd be given. That's the whole point. The covenant takes back what was taken; the only way through is to return it freely—"

"Then let us give freely too." Wick's heart was hammering against the strapped ribs, and they didn't care. "You've had this backwards from the start, you know that? You've been sitting alone in that mausoleum deciding what our memories are worth, like you decide everything, like you decided in the valley that going hungry beat asking for help. But I don't *have* memories before six, Odile. Someone took mine—or lost them, or sold them, I don't know, I've never known—and do you know what I learned? What I learned on the road, with all of you?"

"What?"

"That family isn't what you remember. It's who shows up." Their voice shook and they let it. "Merrin let go of the boat. Whoever she was, whoever she is—she's gone, and I built myself anyway, out of scraps, out of showing up. You can do the same. We all can. That's the whole trick of people, Odile. We're not our pasts. We're what we do after."

"So I should forget you," Odile said, and it wasn't quite a question, and it wasn't quite steady. "I should pour you out with the rest and wake up tomorrow not knowing your name, and that's—that's supposed to be all right—"

"No," Wick said. "You should forget the *me in your head*. The collection-item. The vial labeled WICK'S LAUGH. That one goes in the thrones, sure, gladly. But me?" They grinned, and it pulled at the stitches, and they grinned wider anyway. "I'll still be here. Sitting right next to you. Annoyed about my ribs. And I'll introduce myself again. First thing in the morning. 'Hello, I'm Wick, I'm sixteen, I'm a lockpick, I once got stuck in a gate like a cork, and you're the woman who saved the nine realms, which frankly explains the terrible hair.'"

A sound escaped Odile—half sob, half laugh—and then she was leaning forward and Wick was wrapping their arms around her despite the ribs despite everything, and she was shaking, and Brannic had appeared in the colonnade doorway without either of them noticing and stood there with tears running openly down his scarred face, making no attempt whatsoever to be quiet.

"If you forget me," Wick said into her shoulder, "I'll introduce myself again. As many times as it takes. That's the deal. That's the only deal I've ever wanted from anybody." They pulled back and looked at her, at the thief of yesterday, at the girl who owned a thousand lives and lived none of them, and gave her the only gift a person with no past could give: a future. "Go empty the vault, Odile. We'll refill it. That's what family's *for*."

For a long moment she didn't move. The ash-light failed by degrees outside; the first of the court's strange eternal candles guttered awake along the colonnade, one by one, like the building itself remembering how.

Then Odile Eastwick wiped her face with her sleeve, stood, and squared the prophecy book under her arm like a soldier squaring a shield.

"All right," she said. "All right."

She walked toward the throne room. They watched her go—Wick from the cot, Brannic from the doorway—and the candles lit themselves ahead of her step by step, as if the dead Court knew its heir was coming home to pay its ransom at last.

Behind her, faintly, from the direction of the outer walls, came a sound none of them had heard in a hundred years:

a bell.

Then another. Then nine.
