## 37. A Stranger in the Court

Wick had counted the days by scratching marks into the marble floor beside Odile's bed, because the Court had no windows that opened onto anything honest and no clocks that still worked and no one else who seemed to care how long a body could lie sleeping before it stopped being sleeping and became something sadder.

Forty-one marks. Forty-one days of watching her breathe.

She lay on a cot they'd dragged from the servants' quarters into what had once been a herald's chamber, beneath a ceiling painted with nine crowns that someone — a century ago, maybe two — had tried to scratch out and failed. The moth-eaten herald itself stood in the corner, silent now, its voice spent, its velvet face gone soft as an old glove. Candle stubs ringed the bed like a ritual nobody had planned. Mother Sable said the light was for the covenant's sake. Brannic said it was so she wouldn't wake in the dark. Wick hadn't said anything about why. Wick just kept lighting them, one after another, when each burned down to nothing, because somebody had to.

"You should sleep," Sable murmured from her chair by the door. She'd been sitting there so long the chair had learned the shape of her. Her hands were folded around a rosary of river-stones, and her thumbs moved over them without stopping, like water worrying at the same stones forever.

"I sleep fine," Wick said.

"You haven't slept in three days."

"Then I'm very good at not doing things." Wick turned the mark-scratching knife over in their fingers. "You could sleep instead."

"I slept a hundred years ago," Sable said, "and look what happened while I did."

That was the thing about Mother Sable: she made jokes like a person dropping coins into a beggar's bowl, quickly, without looking, so you couldn't thank her properly. Wick had learned to catch them anyway. That was mostly what family was, Wick had decided. Catching things people threw away too fast to see if anyone wanted them.

Brannic sat cross-legged against the far wall with his eyes closed. He wasn't sleeping either. Since the ninth gate, since he'd poured his gift out into the stone and gone quiet to magic forever, he'd taken to sitting like this for hours, head tilted, listening to nothing. Listening to the absence. Once, Wick had asked him what silence sounded like, and Brannic had thought about it for a long time and said, *like a room where everyone left while I was talking.* After that Wick hadn't asked again.

The candles guttered. Wick got up and lit the next one, and in the small flare of the match, Odile's face looked almost alive — cheeks flushed, lips parted, a woman in the middle of a dream. Then the match burned down and the new candle took the weight of the dark, and she was only a stranger again.

Because that was what they'd all agreed to pretend they understood: the woman in the bed was a stranger. The covenant had taken everything. Every stolen memory, every borrowed life, every year of her own childhood — poured out into nine empty thrones until the fae lords woke gasping like drowned men given breath. And whatever was left behind, whoever was left behind, would open her eyes knowing nothing. Not her name. Not her valley. Not them.

Wick knew what that was like. Wick had woken at six years old in a Bellharbor alley with no before, just a name shouted by a stranger who turned out to be wrong about most things. So when Odile had asked — weeks ago, a lifetime ago, kneeling in the wreck of the Court with Wick's blood on her hands — *if I forget you, will you introduce yourself again?* — Wick had known the answer the way you know your own heartbeat.

*If you forget me,* Wick had told her, *I'll introduce myself again.*

It was easy to promise a dying woman things. It was going to be much harder to keep them to a living stranger.

* * *

She woke on the forty-first day, in the hour before dawn, when the candles had burned to a ring of soft red eyes and even Sable had finally dozed in her chair.

Wick heard the change in her breathing first. They'd spent enough nights listening for it — the shallow tide of sleep shifting, catching, turning into something with edges. Wick was across the room before their own mind caught up, and then stopped, three steps from the bed, because there was a question they'd never let themselves ask until it mattered:

What do you say to someone who has never met you?

Odile's eyes opened.

They were brown. That was the first thing. Wick had braced for something else — for the fae-light everyone said the true heir would carry, for eyes gone strange and silver with covenant-fire — but they were just brown, ordinary, human, blinking against the candlelight the way any eyes do when the world arrives too fast.

Her hand came up slowly and pressed flat against the mattress, testing it, the way you test ice. She looked at the ceiling with its ruined crowns. At the candles. At the herald in its corner, motionless, moth-eaten, terrible. Her breath went thin.

"It's all right," Wick said, before they could think better of it. Their voice came out rougher than intended, scraped raw from disuse. "You're safe. Nobody here is going to hurt you."

She turned her head. Looked at Wick. And there it was — the thing Wick had been dreading for forty-one days — the complete, courteous blankness of a stranger's gaze. No recognition. No wariness, even. Just a person meeting a person.

"Where—" Her voice cracked like dry wood. She swallowed and tried again. "Where am I?"

"The Ninefold Court," Wick said. "What's left of it. Which is more than there was a month ago, so we're calling that progress." They hesitated, then dragged the stool closer and sat down where she could see them without turning her head. Close enough to help. Far enough not to loom. Wick had been on the wrong side of looming; it was a thing you remembered even when you remembered nothing else.

"Do you know your name?" Wick asked gently.

Silence. Wick watched her reach for it — watched the actual reaching, the hand of the mind going into an empty drawer and coming out empty-handed — and saw the moment it landed on her. Not confusion first. Fear. The animal kind, cold and total, the fear of a person standing on air where they expected stairs.

"No," she whispered.

"That's okay." Wick said it firmly, the way you say *the rope will hold.* "That's okay. It happens. It happened to me too, when I was little. You wake up and the whole before-part of you is just... gone. Like a house where the rooms are there but the furniture's been carried out." Wick shrugged. "You learn to live in it. It's not as bad as it sounds. Mostly."

"Mostly," she repeated. A ghost of something crossed her face — humor or horror, hard to tell which.

"Mostly," Wick confirmed. "Now. First things first." They held out a hand, formal as a merchant sealing a deal. "My name's Wick. W-I-C-K, if you ever need to write it down, though honestly nobody spells it right the first time. I'm sixteen. I pick locks, lift purses, and until recently I was the second-best thief in this entire Court, which sounds impressive until you remember the Court was empty."

She looked at the offered hand. Looked at Wick. Slowly, carefully, like a person accepting a bridge over a gorge, she took it. Her grip was weak but real.

"Wick," she said, tasting it.

"Yeah." Something hot and tight climbed Wick's throat, and they talked past it, fast, because that was how you got past things. "And before you ask — yes, you knew me before. We were friends. Family, sort of, if you squint at it sideways, which we always did. But you don't have to take my word for any of that. Words are cheap. I'll prove it slow. That's the deal. Okay?"

"Okay," she said. And then, because some things survive the loss of everything: "Second-best?"

"There was a woman," Wick said, and had to stop, and start again. "There was a woman named Odile Eastwick. Best thief I ever saw. Stealing memories — you don't know what that is yet, don't worry about it. Point is, she taught me half of what I know and stole the other half back when I wasn't looking, just to keep me sharp. She saved my life more times than I can count, which isn't saying much, because counting's not really my thing either." Wick smiled, and found, to their own surprise, that it held. "You're going to hear a lot about her. People around here talk about her like she died. I want you to know up front: she didn't die. She's just... between introductions."

* * *

Brannic woke them all at dawn by simply standing up, which after forty-one days of stillness was announcement enough.

He came to the bed like a man approaching deep water, moving slow, giving her every chance to object. Up close his strangeness was plain — the faint silver tracery under his skin where fae blood ran too thin to be useful and too thick to hide, the ears slightly too pointed, the way his eyes focused on her face like it was a door he was trying to hear through.

"Can I sit?" he rumbled.

She nodded.

"My name is Brannic," he said. "I was a foundling. Raised in a temple doorway in Verance — literally, in the doorway, which the priests thought was funny until winter came. I used to be able to hear doors. All doors. What they wanted, what they feared, whether they'd open." His big hands folded together, unfolded. "I gave that away to get you through the gate. I don't regret it. I want you to know that too, because people are going to tell you a lot of stories about what you cost, and I need you to hear at least one story where the answer is *nothing.*"

"Nothing?" she said quietly.

"You didn't cost it," Brannic said. "I paid it. Freely. There's a difference, and the difference matters, and someday — not today — I'll explain it properly. Today I'm just going to tell you that you're among people who chose to be here, and being chosen is worth more than being owed."

She was crying a little by the end of it. Silently, the way people cry when they don't know what they're crying about, which Wick suspected was the worst way. Brannic produced, from somewhere in the depths of his enormous coat, a clean square of linen, and handed it over with the grave courtesy of a man presenting a medal.

"She cries like that too," he added. "Quiet. In case you wondered what you inherited."

* * *

Mother Sable took the middle of the day.

She came bearing bread and honey and a pot of tea brewed from pine needles, because the Court's larders were bare and the free cities' supply wagons hadn't yet learned the road, and she set it all out on the cot like a feast laid for a queen, which, Wick reflected, was exactly what it was, though nobody at this table would ever use that word.

"Eat first," Sable ordered. "Grief goes down easier with bread. Everything does. That's not scripture, it's just true, and I ought to know, because I was scripture's servant for sixty years and it never once fed me."

The stranger ate. Watched Sable over the rim of the cup with those brown, careful eyes.

"And you are?" she prompted, when she'd finished.

Sable drew a long breath, and Wick saw her decide something — saw the decision move through her like weather. "Mother Sable. Though the *Mother* was revoked a long time ago and the *Sable* was a name I picked myself, so between us, neither half of me has much claim to authority." She set down her cup. "I'm going to tell you something the others won't say yet, because they're kinder than I am, and old women get one honesty per day and I'm spending mine now. A hundred years ago, I helped hide a child. The true heir of this Court. I hid her because I was afraid, and my fear broke the world, and the world stayed broken for a century while I grew old telling myself it couldn't be undone." Her voice didn't shake. That was the terrible thing; it didn't shake at all. "That child was your grandmother. And the heir she bore, the heir the prophecy promised, was you."

The silence afterward had texture. Wick felt it press on their eardrums.

"So," the stranger said at last. Very evenly. Too evenly. "Everyone keeps telling me I was somebody."

"No," Sable said, and reached across the bread and honey and took the girl's hand in both of hers, old hands around young ones, priest's grip, unbreakable. "Everyone is telling you that you *did* something. Who you are is still yours. Still unwritten. Believe me, child, I have watched people try to make themselves into what history says they are, and it fits about as well as a dead man's coat. You are whoever you decide to be at that table tonight. History can wait its turn."

* * *

Caspian came last.

Of course he did. Wick had watched him circle the chamber for two days like a man pacing outside a confession booth, and when the door finally opened and he stepped through, he looked worse than he had walking into Veyran's camp to trade himself for an army. Pale. Hands trembling, and trying not to.

He stopped at the foot of the bed.

The stranger looked at him for a long moment. And something moved through her face — not memory, nothing so kind as memory, but the shape memory leaves behind, the dent in the cushion. Her brow furrowed. Her breath caught.

"Do I know you?" she asked.

"Yes," Caspian said.

"Did I— did we..." Her hand rose halfway toward him and stopped. "Why does looking at you feel like standing in a house where someone's been crying?"

Wick heard Sable shift in her chair. Heard Brannic go very still. Caspian stood in the wreckage of the question like a man in the ruins of a city he'd burned himself, and Wick waited to see if he'd lie — because he'd lied before, beautifully, catastrophically, pages torn from prophecy books and names withheld and a death sentence hidden behind a charming smile, and Wick had forgiven him for it exactly once and was not prepared to extend credit twice.

"No," Caspian said. "I'm not going to stand here and tell you sweet stories. You deserve better from me than stories." He came around the side of the bed, slowly, and knelt — actually knelt, on the cold marble, in front of everyone, which for Caspian was a gesture so extreme it bordered on theatrical, except his eyes weren't performing anything. "My name is Caspian. I came to find you a year ago because a book told me to, and I used you, and I lied to you, and when you found out you walked away from me into the dark, and you had every right. I loved you before I knew you and I love you now, when I don't know you at all, and I understand perfectly well that those might be the same failing." His voice frayed. "You don't owe me anything. You never did. I just needed you to hear it from my own mouth, because you've heard it from everyone else's, and I was the one who—"

"Caspian."

The whole room stopped.

She'd said his name. Not *learned* it — he'd introduced himself, technically — but she'd said it like she'd been carrying it somewhere deep, like a stone at the bottom of a drained well, and now that the water was gone she could finally reach it. She was frowning at her own mouth, at the fact of having spoken.

"Why do I know that name?" she whispered.

"Because some things," Sable said softly, from her chair, "are worn deeper than memory."

For a moment nobody moved. The candles breathed. Somewhere far off in the Court's waking halls, a door closed — an ordinary door, a mortal carpenter's door, one of the hundreds the restoration crews had hung that month — and the sound traveled through the stone like a pulse through a vein.

Then the stranger did the bravest thing Wick had seen in a life full of brave things, most of them Odile's.

She slid her hand across the blanket, palm up. Open. An offering, not a demand.

"Sit down," she said to Caspian. "All of you. Sit down and tell me everything, and don't stop until I fall asleep, and tomorrow do it again. Because apparently I'm somebody important, and I don't remember any of it, and I've decided—" her voice wobbled, steadied, "—I've decided that means I get to meet everybody twice. Once in the stories, and once like this. Like strangers. And I think—" she looked around the room, at the priestess, the foundling, the lockpick, the liar, at the ring of candle flames and the faces above them, "—I think I like the strangers better."

Wick laughed. Couldn't help it. It came out wet and cracked and it didn't matter, because Brannic was laughing too, low thunder in his chest, and even Sable had pressed her sleeve to her mouth, and Caspian — Caspian had taken the offered hand and was holding it like a drowning man holds driftwood, and laughing and crying in the same broken breath.

They pulled chairs close. They told her everything, starting from a ferryman's last snowfall, and she listened like a woman drinking after a long thirst.

And near midnight, when her eyes were finally closing and the candles had burned low, she caught Wick's sleeve one last time.

"Wick," she mumbled, already half under. "Tomorrow. Introduce yourself again. I want to see if you do it different."

"Oh, I will," Wick promised. "I've got material."

But later — alone in the corridor, in the dark that no longer frightened anyone, with the sound of four people breathing together in one warm room — Wick passed the herald's alcove and stopped. The old construct stood where it had always stood, moth-eaten and mute, its work done. Someone had laid flowers at its feet. Wildflowers, white ones, brought up from the valley road.

And pinned to its ragged lapel, in a careful hand Wick recognized as Sable's, a single card:

*Here stood the voice that kept faith, when faith had nothing left to keep.*

Below it, smaller, newer, in ink still fresh:

*The Ninefold Court rises. The free cities hold. Come home, all wanderers — the gates are open.*

Wick read it twice. Then went to fetch the others, because come morning, someone was going to have to tell the stranger in the bed that the world outside had heard she was awake — and the first delegation was already climbing the mountain road, carrying gifts, grievances, and questions no one in that little candlelit room would know how to answer.
