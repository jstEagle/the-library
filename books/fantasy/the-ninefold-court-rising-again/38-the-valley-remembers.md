## 38. The Valley Remembers

The road down into the Hollow Valley had not changed, which felt like a small cruelty.

Odile walked it in the last gold of autumn, her boots finding the old ruts without her asking them to—though of course they weren't the same boots, and the feet inside them were barely six months old, and the body carrying them had learned its walking again the way a child learns: by falling, by laughing at falling, by trying once more. The path remembered her even if she did not. It wound between two shoulders of grey stone and descended through birches gone the color of struck matches, and at every turn she half-expected the trees to lean away from her, to whisper *thief* the way they must have whispered it for years.

They didn't. They only rattled their leaves in the wind, indifferent and golden, and that indifference was so ordinary it nearly made her weep.

"You're stalling," Wick said behind her.

"I'm admiring," Odile said. "There's a difference."

"There really isn't." Wick came up alongside her, hands jammed in the pockets of a coat three sizes too large because Brannic had bought it for the person Wick might grow into rather than the one he currently was. "You've looked at those birches four times. I counted."

"You counted?"

"Someone has to keep track of things now that you can't." He said it lightly, but his eyes were careful, the way everyone's eyes were careful around her these days—as though she were made of spun sugar, or eggshell, or some other beautiful breakable thing. She was still deciding how she felt about that. Mostly she decided against it.

Brannic brought up the rear with Mother Sable, the priestess leaning on his arm more than she'd ever admit to needing to. Sable was old in a way that the covenant's return had not undone; whatever grace had poured back into the Nine Realms last spring, it had flowed around her like water around a stone, leaving her exactly as weathered as before. She claimed this was fair. She claimed she'd earned her age honestly, one cowardice at a time, and wasn't going to give it back now just because the world was mended.

Odile had come to love her fiercely for it.

At the top of the final rise, where the birches thinned and the land dropped away into the valley floor, they all stopped. Because there it was.

The Hollow Valley lay below them like a cupped green hand: terraced fields stitched with hedgerows, the river running slow and silver through the middle of everything, the ferry crossing marked by its crooked wooden posts, and beyond it the town itself—a scatter of slate roofs and chimney smoke, small enough to cover with two cupped palms if you were a god, which nobody was anymore, or at least nobody nearby. The air above it shimmered very faintly, the way air does over a summer road, except this shimmer never faded with the seasons. That was the valley's skin. Its hiding. The old magic-dead hush that had swallowed smugglers and runaways and one memory-thief and kept them all invisible to the Nine Realms for longer than anyone could count.

It was still there. Of course it was. Nobody had told the valley that anything had changed.

"Smaller than I imagined," Brannic said.

"It's not," Odile said. "It's exactly as big as it is. That's the whole point of the place." She heard the words come out of her mouth and realized she knew them—knew them the way you know a line from a song someone used to sing, without knowing who taught it to you. Her face must have done something strange, because Wick touched her elbow.

"What?"

"Nothing. I think—I used to say that. About this place." She laughed, and the laugh wobbled. "I keep meeting myself in doorways. Sentences I don't remember learning. A way of squinting at the light. It's like moving into a house where all the furniture is mine and I've never seen any of it."

"Does it hurt?" Sable asked, in her flat priestess voice that always sounded like a question even when it wasn't.

"No," Odile said, and found it was true. "That's the strangest part. It's like wearing a coat cut for me by somebody who loved me. It fits perfectly. I just never met the tailor."

She started down the hill. Behind her, unhurried, her family followed—the word still new in her mouth, still warm, still slightly unbelievable, like a coin she kept checking to make sure it was real gold.

* * *

The ferryman's name was Tam, and he was maybe nineteen, and when Odile stepped onto his barge he looked at her with the polite blankness of a boy who ferries strangers for coppers and doesn't ask questions.

"That'll be a penny," he said. Then he frowned, tilted his head, and looked at her properly for the first time. "Have we—no. Sorry. You have one of those faces."

"I've been told," Odile said, and gave him two pennies instead of one.

The crossing took no time at all. The river ran wide and brown-green here, dragonflies stitching the surface, willows trailing their fingers. On the far bank, the town began: first the tanner's yard with its sharp reek, then the laundry lines strung between buildings like bunting, then the crooked main street where an old woman was beating a rug off a windowsill and a dog was asleep in the exact center of the road, confident in its ownership of everything.

And then the town saw her.

Not all at once. First the woman with the rug went still, mid-beat. Then a man leaning in a doorway straightened so quickly he knocked his own pipe from his mouth. Then a shutter opened, then another, and a low murmur moved down the street ahead of Odile like wind through wheat, and by the time she reached the square by the well, half the town was standing in doorways looking at her, and the murmur had gathered itself into a word, and the word was her name.

"Eastwick," someone said. "It's Eastwick's girl. It's—it's Odile."

The name landed on her like snow landing on bare ground: soft, cold, melting immediately into something she couldn't hold. She had been told, patiently, many times, what her name was. Caspian said it gently. Wick said it like a joke he was letting her in on. But hearing it from the mouths of strangers—from people who had known the girl who owned it, who had watched her grow up, who could presumably tell her whether she'd liked honey on her bread, whether she'd cried easily, whether she'd been kind—

A woman pushed through the crowd. She was sixty-something, built like a fence post, with iron-grey hair pinned back and flour on her forearms, and she crossed the square at speed and stopped one pace from Odile and stared at her with an expression Odile had no shelf inside herself to put anywhere.

"You've got her chin," the woman said. "Her exact chin. And her way of looking at people sideways, like she's pricing them." Her voice cracked. "I'm Marta Hale. Your grandmother sold my mother wool every market day for thirty years. You used to sit on her counter and steal plums."

"I—" Odile's throat worked. "Did I get caught?"

Marta Hale laughed, wet and startled. "Never once. That was the marvel of you. Everyone knew. Nobody ever caught you." She wiped her eyes with the back of a floured hand, briskly, as though tears were a mess to be cleaned up before they stained. "We thought you were dead, girl. After Voss's inquisitors came through. We thought everyone who'd ever touched magic in this valley was dead, and then spring came, and the shimmer—" she gestured vaguely upward, at nothing, at the sky, "—the hush lifted, and we thought maybe, and then months, and nothing. And now you walk in off the ferry like you've been to market."

Odile became aware, distantly, that Wick and Brannic and Sable had arranged themselves behind her—not blocking the crowd, just *there*, the way stones are there in a stream. She drew strength from it the way you draw breath.

"Marta," she said carefully. "I need to tell you something, and I need you to hear all of it before anyone hugs me, because if someone hugs me right now I'm going to lose the thread entirely."

The crowd murmured. Marta folded her arms, which was clearly her listening posture.

"The girl you knew," Odile said, "gave everything away. Every memory she had. Her childhood, her family, her name—all of it went into reforging the covenant. What's standing in front of you is what grew back after. I know your faces are familiar in the way a song is familiar. I know my grandmother traded wool and stole the Court's memory of its heir, and that heir was me, and none of that is *mine*—it's all secondhand, it's all story. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I know you lost someone twice."

The silence afterward was long enough that Odile heard the well chain creak in the wind and a baby start fussing somewhere and Wick exhale slowly through his nose.

Then Marta Hale reached out and hugged her anyway.

"You absolute fool," Marta said into her shoulder, crushing her ribs. "You think we loved her for her memories? We loved her because she was ours. You're ours too. Sit down. There's pie."

* * *

There was, in fact, pie. There was also an entire afternoon of it—of tables dragged out into the square, of ale barrels broached, of old men competing to tell her stories about herself that she received like gifts from generous strangers: *you bit the miller's son when you were seven; you could gut a fish before you could write your name; you once sat three days with old Fen the ferryman while he died, and came down the hill gray-faced and wouldn't say why.* At that last one Odile set down her cup very carefully. She remembered the shape of that kindness from the outside—first snow, stolen gently from a dying man—and now she stood at the center of it, and both halves fit together like a lock and key, and neither of them was hers, and somehow both of them were.

Caspian found her at dusk, sitting alone on the ferry posts watching the water go bronze.

"You vanished," he said.

"I wanted to see if I could be alone without it hurting." She scooted along the post to make room. "Verdict: mostly. It helps that 'alone' apparently has a radius now. Wick followed me for an hour before giving up."

"He's learning restraint. Slowly."

They sat. Downstream, a heron stood in the shallows doing its patient statue impression. Odile watched it and turned a thought over and over, the way you turn a stone in your pocket until you decide to either throw it or keep it.

"Caspian. When you found me here—before all of it—did you know what this place meant to me? Even then? Even before the prophecy?"

He considered it honestly, which was one of the few things about him she had decided to trust absolutely: he considered things honestly, now, even when honesty cost him. "I knew it was a hiding place. The Court's records called it the Hollow—the one fold in the world where the Nine Realms couldn't reach. I thought of it as a hole. A gap in the map." He shook his head slowly. "It wasn't until we'd left that I understood it wasn't a hole to you. It was a home. Just one with the doors bricked up."

"That's the thing I keep coming back to." Odile pulled her knees up. "Everything I was—the thief, the rules, the thousand lives I carried—I built all of it inside a locked room. Never leave the valley. Never let the world find you. I told myself it was safety. And part of it was. But part of it—" she laughed, short and unamused, "—part of it was just a smaller cage with better lighting. I hid so long I forgot hiding was a choice. And then I gave up every memory I had, and do you know what the strangest discovery of my new life has been?"

"That pie tastes better the second time?"

"That I'm not afraid out here." She spread her hands at the valley, at the darkening hills, at the first stars. "New-made, empty, no past, no hoard, no tricks—and I walked across three realms to get home and I wasn't afraid once. Not of inquisitors, not of roads, not of people. Because I wasn't alone. The old Odile was the most capable person in any room and terrified of all of them. I'm the least capable person in any room and I'm fine." She looked at him sidelong—her grandmother's sidelong look, Marta had said, the pricing look, though she aimed it gently. "So I've been thinking about what this valley is *for*. Now."

* * *

They called a meeting in the town hall, because Marta Hale took one look at Odile's face the next morning and said, "You've got an idea. I can always tell. Your grandmother got the same look before she talked the coopers into rebuilding my mother's barn for free. Out with it."

The town hall smelled of lamp oil and old timber and held perhaps two hundred souls packed onto benches, and by the time Odile climbed onto the stage beside the mayor's vacant chair, word had run through the valley faster than floodwater, and farmers had come in from the terraces, and fishermen from the lower river, and the room was full and breathing and waiting.

Wick, Brannic, and Sable sat in the front row. Caspian stood at the back with his arms crossed, letting her lead, which was its own kind of gift.

"For as long as anyone can remember," Odile began, and her voice only shook a little, "this valley has survived by being invisible. Magic can't follow you here. Inquisitors can't find you here. Kings forget you exist. We told ourselves that was a blessing, and for a lot of people—for runaways, for the hunted—it was the only blessing left in the world. Myself included." She paused. "But I've seen what the world looks like when the free cities fall. I stood in Bellharbor when they burned a memory-keeper alive in the fishmarket. I've read the ledgers. Invisible isn't safe forever. Invisible is just *last*."

A murmur. Somewhere a bench creaked.

"So here is what I'm asking. Not ordering—asking, because it's your valley as much as it was ever mine." She stepped to the front edge of the stage. "The war is over, but it won't stay over. Veyran's remnant is scattered, not destroyed. The Nine Realms are knitting back together, and everywhere the seams are showing, there are people falling through them—fae-blooded children turned out of villages, memory-keepers with burned sanctuaries, folk whose gifts came back with the covenant and whose neighbors came back with torches. They need somewhere magic cannot follow. Somewhere a king's edict has no teeth. This valley has been a hiding place for centuries." She let the words stand a moment in the lamplight. "I'm asking you to make it a refuge. To take the walls down—not the hills, not the hush, those can stay—but the *rule*. The rule that says nobody finds this place and nobody leaves it changed. Open the borders. Let them in. Feed them. Teach them the paths. And when they're strong enough, let them go again—or stay, if they want, and plant things."

Silence. Long enough that she heard the lamp flames themselves.

Then an old man near the back—the miller, she guessed, from the flour in his eyebrows—said, "And when the next usurper comes with an army and asks us where the fugitives are? Hiding keeps *us* alive too, girl. That's not cowardice. That's arithmetic."

"You're right," Odile said, and he blinked, clearly expecting an argument. "Hiding kept us alive. I owe this valley my life twice over—once for raising me, once for sheltering me when I had nothing. I am not asking you to be brave for bravery's sake. I'm telling you the arithmetic changed. A century ago, this valley stayed hidden and the covenant broke and the world burned anyway—we were safe because everyone else was bleeding. Now there are nine thrones filled again, nine houses bound to nine cities, and the free cities held because a handful of people chose each other over safety. The world outside is stronger than it was. Which means the world outside can finally afford to protect places like this one—if we let it know we exist." She softened her voice. "Bellharbor sent militia to the Court's gates. Verance armed every street. Those are our friends now. Refuge isn't the opposite of safety. Done right, refuge *is* safety—with company."

Another murmur, different in texture this time. Marta Hale stood up in the third row, planted her fists on her hips, and addressed the hall as if addressing a stubborn mule.

"I've listened to fifty years of reasons to keep our heads down," Marta announced, "and I buried eleven of my neighbors under headstones during the inquisitors' pass-through, and not one of those headstones says *at least we stayed hidden*. The girl's right. The arithmetic changed." She turned and pointed a floured finger at Odile. "And besides—her grandmother broke the world from this valley. Least we can do is help mend it from the same spot. That's just bookkeeping."

Laughter rippled through the hall, loosening it, and Odile felt the moment tip—felt the whole room tilt from weighing toward wanting, the way rooms do, the way crowds do, the way a single held breath becomes a cheer.

The miller stood. "All in favor of opening the valley," he said gruffly, "stand up."

The benches emptied. Two hundred people rose, and the sound they made wasn't a cheer exactly—it was quieter and heavier than that, the sound of a decision settling into a place like a cornerstone, like something that would bear weight for a hundred years.

On the stage, in front of all of it, Odile Eastwick—who had spent her first life perfecting the art of being forgotten, and her second life learning the art of being known—put her face in her hands and cried in front of everyone, and discovered that this too was a kind of homecoming.

* * *

They worked through the following weeks the way valleys work through everything: steadily, without fuss. Paths were re-cut and widened so carts could manage them. The old smuggler trails were mapped and posted with markers. Marta organized a rota for feeding arrivals that operated with military precision; the miller grumbled about grain stores and then quietly doubled them. Word went out with traders, with pilgrims, with the first thin trickle of travelers who had begun appearing on the northern road since the covenant returned—refugees at first, hesitant, disbelieving, then more, then families, then a fae-blooded girl with moth-winged ears who arrived half-starved at the ferry crossing and was fed before she was questioned, which Odile counted privately as the true founding of the new valley, more than any vote.

Sable stayed a month and founded a small shrine to the old covenant in a converted granary, teaching anyone who wanted to learn the nine oaths. Brannic lingered longest of the three, walking the borders with his ruined magic-hearing and his excellent human ears, learning every path by heart so he could teach them to others. Wick declared he was staying indefinitely, then revised this to "until it gets boring," then was observed repairing the ferry dock with Tam for the fourth day running and revising nothing further.

Caspian had to leave first. The Court's summons came by white owl, formal as a knife: the first council of the reforged Ninefold convenes at season's turning, and the mortal envoys of the free cities are expected. He stood at the ferry posts the morning he went, dressed in traveling grey, and Odile came down to see him off with the autumn light making the river blaze.

"They'll want you there too," he said. "Verance's envoys have been asking after you. The woman who reforged the covenant. They want a name on the treaty."

"They'll get one," Odile said. "Just not mine. I'm needed here. Someone has to teach the refugees which berries lie about being ripe."

He smiled, then sobered. "You could come. As yourself. Whatever 'yourself' turns out to be—that's rather the point of the new covenant, isn't it? Chosen bonds. You could choose a seat at that table."

"And I choose this one." She nodded up the hill, toward the town, where hammering had already started on the first new cottages. "Nine thrones and nine cities will manage for a while without me. This valley managed without anyone for a hundred years, and look how that went." She held out her hand, and he clasped it, forearm to forearm, the way soldiers and shipmates do. "Go. Sign things. Argue with fae lords. Come back and tell me everything, because I'll want every detail—I'm building a collection. Honest ones this time. Given freely."

He was halfway across the river, standing in the bow with the wind pulling at him, when Tam the ferryman shouted from the bank and pointed upstream.

At first Odile thought it was geese. Then the sound resolved—hooves, many, coming fast along the northern ridge trail, and beneath the hoofbeats a stranger music, high and silver, the unmistakable bell-tone of fae horns. A column was descending the switchbacks: riders in livery she didn't recognize, deep blue and bone-white, perhaps thirty strong, with banners snapping, and at their head a rider on a pale mare who bore a herald's sash and carried, level with her heart, an unfurled scroll sealed with nine interlocking rings.

The valley's refugees froze in the fields. Mothers pulled children close. Old habits, old fears, rising like scar tissue.

Odile stood her ground at the water's edge, heart slamming, and watched the riders come down into the valley that had hidden from the world for a hundred years—and wondered, with her new-made courage and her old blood's instinct for trouble arriving in the same heartbeat, whether the Ninefold Court had come to bless what she'd built, or to test whether it would hold.
