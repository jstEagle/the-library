## 39. Ninefold Court Rising

The morning of the signing, the Hall of Nine Thrones smelled of cut stone and new rain.

That was the first miracle, Caspian thought, standing in the gallery above the floor where a century of dust had been scoured away by hands that remembered—newly, clumsily, joyfully—how to work. For a hundred years the hall had smelled of nothing at all. Magic-dead air, the long rot of an abandoned house. Now the fae lords had breathed on the stones and the stones had answered, releasing the scent they had held all along: quarry-fresh granite, mountain water, the green breath of the forest pressing in through windows that had been bricked shut since before Odile's grandmother was born.

Light came through those windows now, too. Real light, unfiltered by wards, falling in long amber bars across nine thrones that were no longer empty.

Caspian had arrived early because he could not bear to arrive late to anything again. He had spent too much of his life arriving precisely when it suited him and not one moment sooner—arriving with half a truth, arriving with a page already torn from the book. He stood at the rail with his hands folded like a penitent and watched the servants of the Court—memoryless no longer, their faces filling out day by day as returned memories settled into them like rain into cracked earth—lay the covenant table.

It was not a table so much as a bridge laid flat. Nine planks of wood, one from each mortal realm, joined without nails to nine slabs of Court-glass that had grown, not been cut, from the roots of the palace itself. Mother Sable had designed it, working from fragments she had carried in her head for sixty years, hidden even from herself behind vows she had finally spoken aloud in the ruined chapel at Verance. *I hid the heir,* she had said, her voice steady, her hands shaking. *I told myself it was mercy. It was fear wearing mercy's robes.* The company had forgiven her in different ways and at different speeds. Odile had forgiven her fastest, which Caspian still did not fully understand, and understood that he didn't, and let that be its own kind of lesson.

Below him, the nine fae lords took their seats—or rather, did not take their seats, but *arrived* at them, the way weather arrives, each in their own manner.

Lady Vessaline of the Spring Court came first, trailing the smell of thawed rivers, and sat with her chin propped on her fist like a bored child, though her eyes were ancient and wet and could not stop moving over the hall, over the light, over the mortals filing in below. Lord Corvane of Autumn arrived as a scattering of leaves that assembled itself into a tall figure with rust-colored hair. The twins of Summer came laughing, hand in hand, arguing about something that had happened three centuries ago and would apparently never be resolved. And at the center, in the ninth throne—the one Brannic's gift had opened, the one that had only ever answered blood—sat Lady Amaranth of the Hollow Court, who had been a husk for a hundred years and was now, terrifyingly, unmistakably, Odile's kin: same sharp jaw, same way of watching a room as if cataloguing what could be stolen from it.

Caspian's chest tightened when he looked at Amaranth, and he made himself look anyway.

*You knew,* Odile had said to him once, in the dark under Rotwood, and it had been the cruelest thing anyone had ever said to him because it was true, and because she had said it quietly, without raising her voice, which meant the wound went deeper than shouting could reach. He had known what she was. He had known why the Court needed her. He had dressed recruitment in the language of destiny and called a debt a calling. He would carry that. He had stopped asking the gods to take it from him somewhere on the road through Salt Bridge, when he'd watched her pay a toll with a stranger's wedding day and seen her carry the guilt like a stone she refused to set down. They were both carrying things now. That was, he supposed, what family was—a group of people agreeing to help each other hold what could not be put down.

The mortal envoys entered from the west doors.

They came in ones and twos, dusty from roads that had been battlefields a season ago. Old Magister Ferrow of Bellharbor, whose canals still stank of ash, walked with a cane but refused all offers of his arm. The delegate from Verance wore no colors at all—the council had nearly torn itself apart deciding whether to attend, and had finally sent a woman named Ilsa Corven who had lost two sons to Veyran's conscription and who had reportedly told the council, in words Sable repeated with relish, "If the fae are monsters, I will look them in the face myself, and if they are not, I will come home and look *you* in the face." Behind her came envoys from the smaller free cities, from the salt towns, from the hillholds, one of them barely more than a girl, clutching a scroll case like a weapon.

And then, last, when the trumpeters had given up and the herald-moths had begun to drift down from the rafters in confusion—

Odile.

She came through the west doors in traveler's clothes. Of course she did. Caspian laughed under his breath despite everything, despite the solemnity gathering in the hall like weather of its own. Every other envoy had dressed for history: brocade, ceremonial sashes, a fortune in embroidered silk on the Bellharbor delegation alone. Odile Eastwick walked into the reforged Ninefold Court in a patched coat and soft boots, her dark hair tied back with a plain cord, and every head turned toward her anyway, because the hall remembered what the world had mostly forgotten—that this woman had poured herself out into nine thrones and kept walking.

She caught him looking. She raised one eyebrow, the old expression, the valley expression, the one that said *yes, I know exactly what you're thinking, and I've already decided whether to charge you for it.*

He mouthed: *You're underdressed.*

She mouthed back: *I'm a thief.*

*Former,* he mouthed.

Her smile then was small and real and it undid him a little, the way it always did, and he turned to the rail and gripped it and watched her cross the floor to the table—not to the ninth seat beside Amaranth, which had been prepared for her with flowers and silver and which she had looked at exactly once before saying, "No. That chair is for someone the Court chooses. I'm here because the cities hired me,"—but to the modest envoy's place at the far end, between the salt-town delegate and the hillhold girl, where she sat down and immediately leaned over to whisper something that made the girl snort with laughter.

Not the heir. Not the Covenant-Bearer. Not the prophesied name in a century-old book.

Thief-advocate to the free cities. A job title that had not existed four months ago and that Odile had invented herself, deadpan, when the Bellharbor magisters asked what title to give her. "Someone has to make sure the fancy folk don't steal from the small folk," she'd said. "I'm an expert."

* * *

The ceremony began when the moths sang.

Caspian had forgotten—they had all forgotten, because forgetting had been the whole disease of the century—that the Court's herald-moths could sing. Their voices were thin and high and layered, a chord like wind through wheat, and when it rose, the hall's acoustics changed, became warmer, closer, as if the walls themselves leaned in to listen. Conversation died. Even Vessaline sat up straight.

Lady Amaranth rose from the ninth throne. Her voice, when it came, filled the hall without effort, the way a river fills a channel.

"A century ago, this court broke." She did not soften it. Caspian admired that; the fae lords had debated for weeks, he'd heard, about how much truth to speak at the signing, and Amaranth had ended the debate with a single sentence: *"We will tell them what we did, or we deserve nothing."* "We broke it not by war but by theft. Our memory of our own heir was stolen from us, and in our blindness we let the covenant starve, and the borders rotted, and your cities burned while ours slept. We are not innocent. Let this document say so."

A ripple through the mortal envoys. Ilsa Corven's jaw tightened; Caspian saw her knuckles whiten on the rail of her seat. But she nodded. Slowly. Once.

"The thief is dead," Amaranth continued, and Caspian watched Odile go very still at the far end of the table. "She paid with more than her life. She paid with her self. What remains of that debt is owed not to us but to her granddaughter, and it cannot be repaid in gold or apology, only in remembrance. So let the first line of the new covenant be this: *Nothing shall be taken that is not freely given.* Not thrones. Not names. Not memories. Not children." Her gaze swept the hall and landed, briefly, deliberately, on Odile. "Not even for good reasons. *Especially* not for good reasons. We have learned where good reasons lead."

Odile's shoulders lowered a fraction. Caspian hadn't realized they'd been up around her ears.

Then the mortal envoys spoke in turn, each naming their city's losses—Ferrow's voice breaking on the names of Bellharbor's memory-keepers, Ilsa Corven's flat and terrible recitation of Verance's conscript rolls—and each, one by one, setting their seal into the warm wax of the bridge-table. Nine seals for nine cities, pressed into nine planks. When the last seal fell, the fae lords rose together and pressed their palms to the glass slabs, and the Court's magic moved through the table like dawn moving across a field.

Caspian felt it from the gallery—a low hum in his teeth, in the scar tissue over his ribs where Veyran's inquisitors had worked him over in another life. The covenant was not a document. It had never been a document. It was a living thing, a woven thing, and what they were doing in this hall was less signing than *grafting*: mortal wood and fae glass, bound at the joints, fed from shared roots. The lines of the oath rose off the parchment in threads of gold-green light and sank into the table, and the table sighed, and outside the high windows every tree in the Court's dead gardens shivered and began, against all season, to bud.

The hillhold girl started crying. So, Caspian noticed, did Magister Ferrow. So, quietly, wiping his eyes with the sleeve of his formal coat as if annoyed at himself, did Brannic, standing guard at the west doors in his new livery that he kept insisting was itchy.

Brannic could not hear the doors anymore. That price had been paid at the ninth gate, freely, forever—he was deaf to magic's whispers now, would never again hear a lock confess its turning or a threshold murmur its history. He had done it grinning. *"Doors talk too much anyway,"* he'd said. But Caspian had seen him, twice since, standing in doorways with his head cocked, listening to nothing, wearing the expression of a man who misses a friend.

Now Brannic straightened, sniffed, and slammed the great doors open for the recessional, and the boom of them rang through the hall like a bell, and everyone jumped, and Odile's laugh carried clear across the floor, and the solemnity broke into something better than solemnity—into relief, into the giddy human noise of people who had expected to die and instead found themselves alive on an ordinary afternoon with work left to do.

* * *

The feast afterward was held in the courtyard, because Amaranth had declared that the new covenant should begin outdoors, under sky, where neither realm owned the roof. Trestle tables ran the length of the flagstones. Fae cooks and mortal cooks argued cheerfully over the same fires. Wick, restored enough to travel but still moving carefully, one arm bound, had appointed themselves unofficial taster of everything and was working through the tables with the methodical dedication of a professional, trailed by the hillhold girl, who had apparently decided Wick was the most interesting person in either realm.

Caspian found Odile at the end of a bench, apart from the crowd, watching.

"You're hiding," he said, sitting beside her.

"I'm supervising."

"From thirty feet away. With your hood up."

"It's not a hood, it's a collar. It's cold."

It was not cold. He let the silence sit between them for a moment, because he had learned—finally, at cost—that silence offered honestly was sometimes worth more than words offered cleverly. Around them the feast roared. Somewhere Brannic was teaching the Verance delegates a drinking song that Sable kept trying to stop. Firelight moved on stone. Above, the moths wheeled in slow spirals, singing softly to each other, keeping the hours of a court that would keep them again.

"I keep waiting to wake up," Odile said at last. Her voice was low, pitched under the noise. She wasn't looking at him; she was looking at the fire, at the tables, at Wick's laugh carrying down the length of the courtyard. "A year ago I had a room above a ferry landing and a thousand lives in jars and none of them mine. Now—" She gestured vaguely at all of it, the covenant, the courts, the budding trees. "Now there's a document with my grandmother's crime written into its first history, and my name in the margins, and a job title I invented, and people who—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "People who introduced themselves to me. Like I was someone worth knowing. Twice, some of them."

"Wick says they'll do it a third time if necessary."

"Wick," she said, with enormous tenderness, "is going to be insufferable about that promise until the day they die, and I'm going to let them."

Caspian turned his cup in his hands. There was something he had been carrying since the chamber beneath the thrones, something he had promised himself he would say today or not at all, and his mouth had gone dry around it twice already. He was, by training and temperament, a man of elegant deflections. He was trying, very hard, to become a man of plain speech.

"Odile. The page I burned. The one that said the Covenant-Bearer dies."

"I remember."

"I've been reading the restored text. Sable's translation, the full prophecy, every line." He made himself meet her eyes. "There's a passage after the sacrifice. Fourteen words. I burned that page too, that night, along with the rest. I want to tell you what it says, because you've earned every word of it and I stole them from you once already."

She went still. The firelight caught the edge of her face. "Tell me."

"'What she pours out, the world pours back,'" he said slowly, "'not as it was, but as it is chosen.'" He let the words land. "Sable thinks it means the erasure was never meant to be permanent. Not a restoration of the old memories—the old Odile, the valley, the childhood—that's gone, truly gone, poured into the thrones. But new ones. Made ones. Chosen ones. Given freely, kept honestly. A life that doesn't remember itself yet because it hasn't finished being lived." He swallowed. "The prophecy didn't kill you. It just demanded you become someone who could start over."

Odile stared at him for a long moment. Then she laughed—a short, disbelieving sound, almost angry—and pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes.

"Fourteen words," she said. "Fourteen words, and you *burned* them."

"I was afraid of what they meant. I was afraid hope would make you reckless. I was wrong about approximately everything that year; one more error hardly distinguished itself." He hesitated. "I'm sorry. I'll keep saying it. You can invoice me."

"Don't tempt me. My rates are ruinous." She dropped her hand. Her eyes were bright but level, and when she looked at him, really looked, he felt the familiar vertigo of being *seen*—the thing she'd done to him from the very first dinner in the valley, the thing he'd mistaken for suspicion and then for danger and then, eventually, for grace. "Caspian. The new memories. If they can be made—"

"They can. Sable's certain. The covenant itself will hold them, the way the thrones held yours. Anything given freely, witnessed, kept—"

"Then I want the first one to be tonight." She said it simply, the way she said most important things. "This. The feast. All of them. Brannic's terrible song. Wick stealing food off six nobles' plates. You, making that face you make when you think you've said something clever." Her mouth curved. "All of it. Freely given."

Across the courtyard, Brannic bellowed the chorus; a dozen voices, fae and mortal, bellowed it back; Wick stood on a bench conducting with a turkey leg; Mother Sable laughed so hard she had to sit down on a barrel, and no one had seen her laugh like that in sixty years, perhaps ever.

Caspian looked at all of it—at the architecture of the world being rebuilt not in stone but in *this*, in chosen bonds, in people showing up—and felt the last cold room inside himself finally, mercifully, go quiet.

"There's just one problem," he said.

"What?"

"If it's to be witnessed properly, the whole company has to consent. It's the rule. Your rule. *Nothing taken that isn't freely given.*" He stood, and offered her his hand, and smiled. "So come and ask them."

Odile looked at his hand. Then she took it, pulled him down onto the bench instead of up, and said, "Tomorrow. Tonight I'm busy supervising."

Above them, the herald-moths began to sing the evening hours, and in the east, past the budding gardens, past the mended borders, the road ran south toward a valley that was waiting—with its doors unlocked—to be opened.
