## 40. The First Memory

The canals of Bellharbor smelled of salt and frying onions and the green reek of weed that clung to the pilings in summer, and Odile stood on a bridge she did not remember crossing and thought: *this is what it feels like to be new.*

She had been new for forty-one days.

She knew this because Wick told her. Wick kept count the way other people kept calendars — notched into a strip of leather they wore around their wrist, one mark per day since the morning she had woken in the Ninefold Court with nothing behind her eyes but white. Forty-one marks now. Wick had shown her the strip that morning, holding it up like a fisherman showing off a catch.

"Forty-one," they'd said. "Old enough to walk. Not old enough to cross bridges alone."

"I crossed plenty of bridges before," Odile had said, and then heard herself say it, and stopped. Before. She said *before* the way other people said *the war* — a country she'd been to, whose language she no longer spoke. She had crossed bridges. Someone had taught her which stones were slick. That someone was gone, dissolved into nine thrones along with a thousand stolen lives, and what remained of her was a woman with quick hands, an unaccountable fear of open doors, and a name she'd been given secondhand: *Odile*, because it was written in a book, because it was hers once, because Sable said names should be returned even when everything else could not be.

The bridge shivered under the passage of a barge below. A boy on the deck waved at her. She waved back without deciding to, and the ease of it startled her — this body remembered kindness even when she didn't.

That was the strange arithmetic of her new life. The memories were gone, but their *shapes* remained, the way a river keeps the shape of a drowned tree. Her hands knew locks. Her feet knew rooftops. When Caspian laughed, something behind her sternum turned toward him like a plant toward light, though she had no idea why, and she had decided — this was her first decision as a new person, made on day three — that she would not interrogate it. Some things you were allowed to keep without knowing where they came from. That was the whole lesson of her old life, inverted.

"Odile!"

Brannic's voice carried across the water, deep and unhurried, from the direction of the Lantern Row market. He stood at the mouth of a crooked lane with his arms full — a basket, a bolt of blue cloth, a paper-wrapped bundle leaking the smell of cinnamon. Since Rotwood he had been deaf to doors, deaf to all the whispering architecture of the world, and he had come out of that silence gentler than any man she'd met, as if hearing had been a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten his own shoulders were strong.

"You're late," he called.

"I'm early," Odile called back. "Everyone else is late."

"That's the same thing said by a thief." He grinned. "Sable's orders: you're to stop by the fishmonger's. The good one. And don't pay."

"I always pay."

"You *used* to steal. Now you pay. It's very confusing for everyone."

She took half the load from his arms and fell into step beside him, and the lane closed around them in its warm disorder — awnings dripping, gulls arguing over a fish head, a cobbler's hammer keeping time somewhere above. Magic had come back to Bellharbor slowly, like feeling returning to a numb limb, and the city wore its small wonders shyly: a lamp that burned without oil outside the chandler's, a canal boat that never needed rowing, ivy on the temple wall that bloomed out of season because the ivy had missed flowering for a hundred years and had a lot to say about it. Children pointed at such things. Adults pretended not to see them, but smiled anyway.

Odile saw them and felt nothing but curiosity, clean and weightless. Whatever she had poured into the thrones, whatever power she had carried in her blood and her hoard, it had gone out of her like water through open fingers, and what was left was just a woman walking through a market with a fae-blooded foundling who smelled like cinnamon rolls.

It was enough. She kept checking, the way you press a bruise to make sure it's healing. It was enough.

* * *

Mother Sable had commandeered the back room of the Drowned Bell, a tavern on Lantern Row with low beams and a landlady who owed Sable a favor from the war and refused to discuss it. By the time Odile arrived, the room had been arranged into something between a dinner table and an altar: candles everywhere, none of them enchanted, because Sable had opinions about enchantment and expressed them mostly through candle choice. The blue cloth Brannic carried was spread down the center of the table. On it sat bread still steaming, olives, a wheel of pale cheese, roasted peppers blackened at the edges, two bottles of Verance red, and a third bottle of something amber that Wick was guarding with both arms and an expression of profound suspicion.

"What's that one?" Odile asked.

"Caspian brought it," Wick said. "Which means it's either expensive or cursed."

"It's from the Court cellars," Caspian said from the doorway. He came in carrying a stack of bowls against his hip, sleeves rolled, hair escaping its tie, looking — Odile noted with the mild astonishment that still caught her off guard most days — *happy*. The man she'd met across a dinner table in the Hollow Valley had worn happiness like a borrowed coat. This one's fit properly. "Nine hundred years old, give or take. The steward wept when I took it. I told him it was for the Covenant-Bearer's first birthday."

"It's not my birthday," Odile said.

"Day forty-two," Wick corrected, tapping their leather strip. "Close enough. You get one birthday a year and we missed the window, so we're inventing a new one. It's very efficient. Fewer cakes overall."

"The Court cellars survived?" Sable asked, pouring. Her hands were steady; her voice had that dry edge she used whenever she was moved and refused to admit it. She wore no robes anymore — had burned them in Verance, before the council, and had never replaced them with anything but plain gray wool — but she still held herself like a woman standing in a pulpit. Some postures outlive their institutions. "I'd have guessed Veyran drank them dry."

"He tried. He had no head for it. You cannot taste anything when your mouth is full of other people's lives." Caspian set down the bowls and, passing behind Odile's chair, let his hand rest on her shoulder for exactly as long as a hand may rest there without requiring explanation. Forty-one days, and they had not yet required explanation. She suspected they never would. Some debts are paid forward instead of back. "Sit, everyone. Sit. The bread's getting cold, and bread that waited a century for this table will not be kept waiting longer."

They sat. And Odile, who had eaten alone in a valley where no one could find her for years beyond counting — who had eaten a thousand dinners in a thousand stolen memories and tasted none of them — looked down the length of a scarred wooden table at the faces gathered around it, and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like grief and almost like sunlight, what all of this was.

They had planned it. Badly, and obviously, and with enormous love.

* * *

Dinner unfolded the way good dinners do: too loud, then too quiet, then loud again. Brannic told the story of the Salt Bridge toll — the version where he was heroic, which differed from history mainly in every particular — until Wick threw a pepper at him. Sable recounted, with liturgical gravity, the saga of the free cities' council finally ratifying the new covenant's mortal charter, three weeks of argument distilled into one devastating impression of the Verance merchant-prince who'd objected to the phrase *sacred obligation* on grounds of tariffs. Caspian described the Ninefold Court's first council in terms so diplomatic that everyone realized he was leaving out the part where two of the fae lords had nearly duelled over seating arrangements, and only confessed under pressure of refilled wine: the Autumn lord and the Tide lord had both insisted on the chair facing the door, because, respectively, tradition and spite.

"And who got it?" Odile asked.

"Neither. We built a ninth chair in the middle."

"A ninth chair for nine lords?"

"For ten." He looked at her when he said it. Just for a moment. Then he raised his glass. "To absent friends and present company, which tonight are the same thing."

They drank. The amber wine from the Court went down like honeyed light, and Odile felt it settle in her chest and warm her, ordinary and mortal and enough.

Under the table, Wick's knee bumped hers, deliberately, twice — their old signal, she somehow knew, though she couldn't remember learning it, the way she somehow knew the weight of a lockpick between two fingers. She glanced down. Wick slid something across the bench: a small tin, dented, tied with string.

"What's this?"

"Open it later."

"Wick—"

"*Later.*" But they were grinning, that gap-toothed ferocious grin she'd seen a hundred times in forty-one days and could not recall seeing once in a life she no longer owned, and she put the tin in her pocket and left it there, because family, she had learned, means letting people keep their small ceremonies.

It was Sable who finally leaned back, folded her hands, and said, in the voice she used for scripture and for asking people to pass the salt:

"We have a gift for you."

"You've given me dinner."

"Dinner is the wrapping," Brannic said.

Caspian reached into his coat and drew out a vial.

The whole table went quiet. Odile looked at it — at the little glass cylinder, stoppered with wax, faintly luminous, the way memory-vials were, the way she knew them to be down to her bones — and felt every hair on her arms rise.

"No," she said.

"Odile—" Sable began gently.

"No." She pushed back from the table. The chair scraped. In the corner of her eye the candle flames bent, though there was no draft. "You know what those are. You know what I did with those. What they *did to me*. I spent my whole — whatever my whole life was — eating other people's yesterdays because I had none of my own worth keeping, and I am not going to start again, not even — especially not—"

"It's empty," Wick said.

She stopped.

Wick held up their hands, palms out, the universal gesture of the unarmed. "Look. Look at it. There's nothing in it. It hasn't been *used* yet. It's just — glass."

Caspian set the vial on the table between the candles and the bread, and rolled it gently so it spun to a stop in front of her. Empty glass catching candlelight. Nothing more.

"A vessel," Sable said. "Nothing in it yet. Tonight, if you consent — only if you consent — this table becomes your first memory. Made, not taken. Given, not stolen. Every person here offers it freely, and it goes into the vial, and the vial goes wherever *you* decide it goes. Keep it. Give it away. Pour it into the harbor. It is yours in a way nothing has ever been yours, because you will have chosen it while it was still happening."

Odile stared at the vial. Her hands had gone cold.

"You want me to record this," she said slowly. "This. Peppers and bad storytelling and Wick guarding liquor like a dragon."

"This exactly," Brannic said.

"Why?"

And it was Caspian who answered, and he did not reach for her hand or soften his voice or do any of the careful things people did around her wound. He simply told her the truth, which was, she was beginning to understand, the whole of what love consisted of.

"Because you deserve a yesterday," he said. "You gave up all of yours so that everyone else could have their tomorrows. Nobody asked you whether that was fair. Nobody could. But this — this much we can do. We can't give you back your childhood or your valley or whoever you were before the thrones. But we can start the account again. First entry. One evening, Lantern Row, the Drowned Bell, day forty-two. Everyone laughing. You'll be able to hold it in your hand. And when you doubt — and you will doubt, some grey morning, that any of this is real, that you earned it, that you belong to it — you won't have to trust our word. You'll be able to open a little glass bottle and *know*."

Silence. The candles ticked. Somewhere out in the tavern, someone was singing badly and happily, a dockside song about a mermaid with expensive tastes.

Odile picked up the vial. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything.

Her old self — the thief, the valley-ghost, the woman with a thousand lives and none of her own — would have already known the craft of this. Fill the vessel, seal the wax, mind the bleed at the edges. She knew she had known it. The knowledge lived in her hands like a song in a struck bell after the sound fades.

But this was different. This was the thing she had never once done in all her years of taking: receiving.

"If I do this," she said, "it stays honest. No polishing. If I snort wine through my nose, it goes in the memory. If Sable cries during her own toast — and she will—"

"I will not," said Mother Sable, already wet-eyed.

"—then it goes in. All of it. I'm not building myself a pretty lie. I did enough of those for one lifetime."

"Agreed," Caspian said.

"Sworn," said Sable.

"On my life and my lockpicks," said Wick solemnly, and ruined it immediately by stealing an olive.

* * *

They filled the vial at midnight, when the candles had burned down to stubs and the bottles were empty and Brannic had fallen asleep sitting up with his chin on his chest, which everyone agreed must go in, *especially* the moment he woke mid-sentence to defend his honor and finished the sentence wrong.

There was no grand working. Sable spoke a few words of the old covenant tongue — words that had once sealed realms together and now sealed a dinner into glass, which she said was proof the old tongue had a sense of humor. Caspian held the vial. Wick narrated, quietly, everything that happened, so that the memory would know itself: *the peppers, the pepper thrown at Brannic, the story of the ninth chair, Sable crying, Brannic sleeping, the mermaid song from the next room, the way Odile laughed —*

"Did I laugh?" Odile asked. Genuinely asked. She hadn't noticed.

"You laughed," Wick said. "Like this." And they did an impression of it, terrible and loving, and Odile laughed again, right on schedule, and the vial filled with soft gold light, and it was done.

Caspian sealed the wax and pressed it with his thumb and handed it to her across the table, across the ruins of the meal, across the whole distance between the woman she had been and the woman holding a small warm bottle in two cupped hands.

"First entry," he said.

Odile Eastwick — new-made, day forty-two, owner of exactly one memory and no others — looked around the table one last time. Sable, gray wool and damp eyes. Brannic, blinking, asking what he'd missed, being told *everything*, believing none of it. Wick, already re-notching their leather strip for tomorrow. Caspian, watching her with an expression she was almost certain she'd seen before, in another life, across another table, when she'd been someone who stole feelings because she couldn't afford her own.

She slipped the vial into her pocket, next to Wick's mysterious tin, and decided she would not open either one tonight. Some things were better saved. She had a lifetime now, apparently, in which to save things. What a luxury. What an absurd, extravagant, ordinary luxury.

"Same time next year?" Brannic asked, yawning.

"Same table," said Sable.

"Same lies," said Wick.

"Same family," said Caspian.

Outside, the canals murmured under the lanterns, and the tide came in from the sea, and somewhere far north the Hollow Valley lay open under the stars for the first time in a thousand years, its borders wide, its lamps lit, waiting for anyone who needed somewhere to begin.

Odile blew out the last candle, and stayed.
