## 1. The Ledger of Borrowed Things

The candle had burned down to its last knuckle by the time Vesper Winterbourne finished the third column, and he did not relight it. He preferred to balance accounts in failing light. It kept him honest. In the dark, every entry looked like a debt.

The counting room of the Ninefold Court sat beneath the Lamplighters' Row tenements, behind a door disguised as a coal chute, and it held more wealth than the crown's treasury and less coin than a beggar's cup. Nine long tables stretched away into shadow, each one assigned to a keeper, each one stacked with ledgers bound in grey calfskin that smelled faintly of rain. Vesper's table was the smallest and the farthest from the door, which suited him. The other keepers called it the penitent's corner. He called it his.

He dipped his pen and wrote:

*Entry 214. One winter, borrowed entire, from a cooper of Saltmarket. Sold to a southern lady who has never seen snow. Condition on receipt: intact, with frost. Price: eleven marks and a lock of the buyer's hair.*

The ink dried slowly in the cold. Vesper watched it dry the way other men watched fire. There was a rightness to it—a memory written down could not lie about what it was. People lied. They said *I'm fine*, they said *I'll come back*, they said *you're the only one I trust*, and then they died or drifted or forgot your face in a market crowd. Paper did none of that. A page remembered exactly what you asked it to remember, forever, and never once made you wish it hadn't.

That was why he was a bookkeeper, he told himself, and not a thief. Thieves took things people wanted kept. He only ever wrote down what people wanted given away—or what they could no longer carry, which amounted to the same thing, most nights.

"Still awake, Winterbourne?"

Corvane stood at the end of the long tables, half in shadow as always, a man shaped like a question mark left too long in the rain. The guildmaster was sixty if he was fifty, with hands so soft they made no sound on paper, and eyes the colour of tarnished silver that never quite settled on what they were looking at. Tonight they were looking at Vesper's ledger.

"Balancing," Vesper said.

"At this hour." Corvane came closer, footsteps silent on the stone. That was another thing about the guildmaster—he moved like a man who had learned somewhere that sound was a debt, and refused to pay it. "You always balance at this hour. I've begun to wonder why."

"Because the entries are truest when no one's watching them."

"Hm." Corvane's gaze slid across the open pages—too fast for reading, Vesper noted, but not too fast for checking. Counting columns. Looking for gaps. "The Ashgrave account. You hold it personally?"

"The Ashgrave account is mine, yes."

"It should come through the Court. All extractions should."

"It will be entered," Vesper said evenly. "When it's finished."

Something passed over the guildmaster's face—not anger, nothing so warm as anger. A recalculation. "See that it is, Winterbourne. The Court keeps what the Court keeps. That is rather the whole of our purpose." He turned away, then paused, and without looking back added: "You've been with us nine years now. Long enough to know there are no private ledgers. Only ledgers we haven't read yet."

His footsteps made no sound going up the stairs.

Vesper sat very still until the coal-chute door clanged shut above, then exhaled slowly and turned back to Entry 214. His hand had gone slightly unsteady. He hated that. Ink forgave nothing—not hesitation, not fear, not the small betrayals of the body. He waited until his hand stilled, then drew a fine line beneath the column and summed it.

Nine years in the Court, and Corvane still checked his sums. Not because Vesper was careless—the guildmaster had told him once, drunk on plum wine at midwinter, that Vesper's books were the cleanest he'd ever seen, cleaner than the ones Corvane himself had kept in his own keeping days. No. He checked because Vesper was careful, and careful men, in Corvane's arithmetic, were either loyal or lying, and the two were difficult to tell apart until it was too late.

Vesper blew out the candle and worked by memory-light, which was, after all, his trade.

* * *

Mistress Ashgrave came at moonset, wrapped in a shawl the colour of old ash, escorted by her son who could not meet anyone's eyes.

They met in the extraction chamber—a low round room below the counting floor, walled in riverstone, with a single chair and a single lamp and a bowl of water on the floor for reasons no one remembered anymore. Vesper had asked about the bowl once, in his first year. Old Sef, who had trained him, had said: *So the memories have somewhere to go that isn't you.* Then he'd laughed, and Vesper had never learned whether the answer was doctrine or joke. He kept the bowl anyway. He liked the idea of it. Everything taken should have somewhere to rest.

"I don't want the whole life," Mistress Ashgrave said. She sat in the chair the way queens sit for portraits—spine straight, hands folded, daring the world to make her flinch. Only her knuckles betrayed her, white as bone under the skin. "Just the summer. The last one. Before he went into the water."

"You want me to take it out?"

"I want it out of my head." Her chin lifted. "I'm eighty-one years old, boy. I have carried that summer for sixty years, and it has grown heavy as sin. My son thinks I should keep it because keeping is grieving and grieving is loving." She glanced at the young man by the wall. "But I am tired of drowning every night in June. Do you understand? Every night I go down into that lake after him. Every night. Sixty years of swimming and never reaching him."

"And you want to sell it," Vesper said gently, "not discard it."

"Sell it. To someone who'll treasure it. Someone whose summers are all behind them or all ahead of them. Let some poor soul live one perfect summer by a lake, sunburn and all, instead of—" she gestured vaguely at herself, at the shawl, at the whole grey edifice of her grief, "—instead of this."

Vesper opened his ledger to a fresh page. This was the part he loved, though love was perhaps the wrong word; this was the part that fit, the way a key fits a lock built for it. "Then let's write the terms properly. What do you want for it? Coin?"

"No coin."

"Trade, then. Most sellers ask for something to fill the hole. A calm morning. A stranger's kindness. Some people want a wedding that isn't theirs—it takes the edge off wanting their own."

"I want nothing put in." She leaned forward, and her eyes were fierce and wet at once. "I want the space left empty. I want to wake tomorrow and feel the shape of where it was, like a tooth gone from the jaw. I want to miss missing him. Do you hear me? I want grief with the drowning taken out of it. Can your gift do that?"

Vesper set down his pen. For a long moment the only sound was the lamp's small combustion and, distantly, the river moving under the city like a slow thought.

"Yes," he said. "It can do that. But I'll need your permission twice. Once now, aloud, for the ledger. And once during—when you'll feel it going, and you may change your mind, and I will stop, whatever state we're both in. Do you consent to those terms?"

She consented. Her son did not watch; he turned his face to the wall and pressed his forehead against the cool stone like a child being punished, or praying, and perhaps there was no difference at that hour.

Extraction was not violence. Vesper wished people would stop expecting it to be. He took Mistress Ashgrave's hands in his, and he closed his eyes, and he went down into her the way a diver goes down a familiar rope—slowly, hand over hand, respectful of the dark.

Her mind was an old house with most of the lamps blown out. He moved through it quietly, past rooms of ordinary decades—weddings, harvests, arguments about money—and found the summer at last behind a door painted lake-blue, and he did not burst in. He knocked.

Inside, it was June, 60 years deep and still unbearable. A boy of nineteen laughing on a jetty, brown as a nut, showing off a dive. The lake green-gold in late light. Dragonflies stitching the air. The mother on the shore with bread and a knife and an enormous unguarded happiness, the kind that exists only in memories because no one recognizes it while living it. And then—the part she'd asked him to take, the whole of it, every hour from the moment the boy surfaced wrong to the moment they dragged the lake for three days—the cold, the searching, the terrible bright mornings of not-knowing, the night the knowing finally came.

Vesper gathered it the way he gathered everything: completely, gently, without leaving splinters. That was his particular talent, the reason Corvane's ledgers named him first among the extractors despite his corner table. Other keepers tore memories out and left ragged holes that scarred over crooked. Vesper cut along the grain. When he lifted a summer out of a woman, the woman remained whole around the absence—shaped by it, yes, marked by it, but not broken. He left her the shore. He left her the bread and the knife. He even left her the boy diving, one single image of the boy diving, because she had said *the last summer* and Vesper had decided privately that the last summer began after the dive. She would wake with one perfect picture and sixty years of weight gone, and she would never know he had edited the terms by a single image.

Some things a bookkeeper kept off the books.

He rose out of her like a man surfacing, gasping, and poured the summer—cold lake-water and dragonflies and grief entire—out through his hands into the waiting vessel: a slim vial of smoked glass, sealed with wax and a thread of her grey hair. It glowed faintly, the way all stored memory glowed, gold-green, like light through leaves underwater.

In the vial, the boy dived forever and never surfaced wrong.

Mistress Ashgrave sat breathing hard, gripping the arms of the chair. "It's gone," she whispered. "Oh. Oh, it's *gone*." And then she began to laugh, or cry, or both, rocking a little, touching her temple as though feeling for the missing tooth. "There's just—one picture. He's diving. He's *diving*, and I can almost hear him laugh before—"

"Before," Vester agreed softly. "That's yours. I didn't touch it."

She looked at him then, really looked, with eyes sixty years lighter, and said the thing they all said, the thing that was supposed to be gratitude and always landed somewhere worse: "How do you bear it? Carrying everyone's pasts like that. Doesn't it get heavy?"

"No, mistress," said Vesper Winterbourne, who was carrying, at that moment, four hundred and six lives in the vaults above them plus the summer currently warming his coat pocket, and whose own past contained precisely nothing worth the name. "Paper doesn't weigh anything."

* * *

He climbed the stairs to the vault alone, as he always did. The other keepers had gone home hours ago—to wives, to wine, to the loud bright lives they pretended were real and the Court pretended not to notice. Vesper stayed. Vesper always stayed. The vault was his favorite room in the world, if a room full of other people's yesterdays could belong to anyone.

Four hundred and six vessels sat in ordered rows on shelves of black oak, glowing their faint gold-green, gold-blue, gold-grey—each color the shade of what it held. Summers ran green. Winters ran white. Grief ran grey-silver, and love, rarest of holdings, ran the deep bruised red of embers under ash. He walked the aisles slowly, as he did every night, reading spines the way monks read scripture.

*One autumn, borrowed entire. One lullaby, sold thrice. One face, unknown, kept forty years.*

Every vessel was catalogued in three ledgers: the Book of Holdings, the Book of Sales, and the Book of Debts. Vesper kept all three himself. Corvane trusted no one else with the Debts—which was flattering, or ominous, depending on the hour—and tonight, passing the great iron cabinet where the oldest ledger lived, the one no one touched and no one explained, Vesper noticed something he had noticed before and never remarked upon.

The old ledger's chain-lock hung open.

Not unlocked. *Open*. Swinging loose on its staple, the way a door swings when someone means to come back shortly, or means never to come back at all.

He stood in the cold aisle with a dead woman's summer in his pocket and listened. Above him, muffled through floors of stone, the city slept its ordinary sleep. Somewhere a dog barked at nothing. The vessels glowed around him in their patient thousands, holding their little eternities, and none of them—none of the four hundred and six, none of the nine thousand sold across nine years—none of them hummed.

Except, very faintly, from behind the iron cabinet door, something did.

Vesper told himself it was the draft. He told himself it was his imagination, which was a tool of his trade and therefore prone to noise. He told himself that the Ninefold Court had kept its oldest ledger locked for longer than any living member could remember, and that chains did not open themselves, and that guildmasters did not creep down to vaults at midnight without reason.

Then he remembered the way Corvane had looked at his ledger earlier. Not reading. *Counting.* Checking for gaps. As if the guildmaster already knew where to look for what was missing—as if he needed to know whether Vesper had noticed yet.

Vesper closed the vault door behind him and did not chain it, because chaining it would mean believing there was something to chain against, and he was not ready to believe that. But he took the long way home through the sleeping streets, past the lamplighter dousing the last flames on Lamplighters' Row, and in his pocket the drowned boy's summer lay warm against his hip, humming very softly, like a word someone was trying to remember.

By morning, proclamations would be nailed to every door in the city.
