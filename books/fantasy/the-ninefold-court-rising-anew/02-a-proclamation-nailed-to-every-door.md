## 2. A Proclamation Nailed to Every Door

The hammering woke the city before the bells did.

Vesper heard it from his window above the counting room—three strikes, a pause, three more—and thought at first it was the shipwrights starting early. But shipwrights worked in rhythm, and this was arrhythmic, deliberate, spaced like punctuation. He leaned out into the grey morning and saw them: six soldiers in the king's black-and-silver moving down Lantern Row with a satchel of broadsheets and a boy carrying the nails.

They stopped at the chandler's door. The sergeant read the proclamation aloud in a parade-ground voice flat enough to drain all meaning from it, and Vesper caught only fragments across the distance—the words *Purge*, *Gifts*, *by order of Her Majesty*—before a shopkeeper's awning cut off his view. Then the hammer fell three times, and the paper stayed, and the soldiers moved on to the next door.

By the third house, people had begun to gather. By the fifth, they were reading over one another's shoulders in that particular silence Vesper knew too well, the silence of a counting room when a ledger refuses to balance.

He dressed quickly and went down.

* * *

The Ninefold Court kept its public face behind a moneylender's office on Coopers' Lane, and by the time Vesper arrived the guildmaster was already there, standing in the doorway of the back room with a proclamation crumpled in one fist as though he had torn it off a wall himself.

Corvane Ashgrave was sixty years old and looked carved rather than aged, all planes and shadow, with hands that had extracted ten thousand memories and never once trembled. This morning they were very still. That was worse than trembling.

"Read it," Corvane said, and thrust the paper at him.

Vesper read it twice, because the first time he could not make the words behave.

**BY ORDER OF QUEEN ISOLDE, FIRST OF HER NAME:** Let it be known throughout the realm that the practice of Gifts—of taking, holding, trading, or altering the memories of another—is hereby declared anathema to crown and church. All practitioners are commanded to surrender themselves for assessment within nine days. Those who harbor them share their guilt. The mercy of the crown is great; its patience is not.

Below, in smaller type, the justification: *The realm's children sicken. The realm's dead walk unquiet. The Gifted feed on what is not theirs. Let the realm be cleansed.*

"It's madness," Vesper said. "Half these claims—" He stopped. He had spent eleven years in the ledgers. He knew precisely which of the claims were true.

"Madness with an army behind it," Corvane said. "Proclamations went up in every quarter before dawn. Every door. Even the temple doors." His mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Her Majesty wants us to know there is nowhere to nail our own lies."

"What does the Court do?"

Corvane turned away, toward the shelves where the active ledgers stood—two hundred volumes, each one a life or a fraction of one, spines labeled in Vesper's own careful hand. For a long moment the old man simply looked at them the way a miser looks at a burning house.

"Silence," he said at last. "Every keeper goes dark. No extractions, no trades, no clients. We burn the working ledgers—all but the deep archive. And we wait."

"Burn them?" Vesper heard his own voice rise and hated it. "Those aren't inventory. Those are *people*. Half those entries are memories someone is still living without. If we burn the ledgers, no one can ever give them back."

"And if we don't," Corvane said quietly, "the queen's assessors learn everything. Names of every client who ever bought a stolen summer. Every keeper's true name. Every debt." He faced Vesper fully now. "Paper burns, Winterbourne. It always has. That was always the bargain—we keep the past, and the past can be destroyed. You knew that when you took your chair."

Vesper had known it. Knowing a thing and watching it happen were different countries, and no road ran between them.

"I'll need the burn list by tonight," Corvane added. "You know the holdings better than anyone. Mark what dies and what survives."

"The deep archive doesn't survive a fire either," Vesper said. "It just takes longer to die."

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes—quick, shuttered, gone. "The deep archive will be *handled*," he said. "See to the working ledgers."

* * *

The city spent the day reading.

Vesper walked home through streets where the proclamations hung on every door like pale leaves, and watched his neighbors read them, and watched them look at one another afterward. That was the poison in Isolde's method, he thought—not the soldiers, not even the threat. It was this: the moment a whole street begins wondering which of its own faces hides a Gift. A memory-thief leaves no mark. There was no way to prove what anyone had done—which meant, Vesper understood with a cold settling in his chest, that proof would hardly matter.

He learned how little it mattered before the day was out.

Old Marta lived two doors down from his rooms, a widow who mended sails for the harbor fleet and grew tomatoes in a cracked half-barrel because the soil of her homeland, she said, would grow nothing else. She had fed him soup his first winter in the city, when he was nineteen and newly bound to the Court and too proud to admit hunger. She had never asked what he did for the moneylender on Coopers' Lane. He had never told her. In eleven years, the sum total of their transaction was soup, tomatoes, and once—when she found him sitting on her step at dawn after a bad extraction, hollowed out and shaking—a cup of brandy and no questions at all.

They came for her at dusk.

Vesper heard it from his window again: voices, then boots on stairs, then Marta's voice, high and bewildered rather than afraid. *What have I done? What have I done?* By the time he reached the street, four soldiers had her in the lantern light between two mounted officers, and a crowd had gathered the way crowds do—close enough to see, far enough to deny.

"She's accused of what?" a fishwife demanded, and had the nerve to sound outraged. "She's seventy if she's a day!"

The younger officer unrolled a paper with the officiousness of a man enjoying his own authority. "A complaint was lodged. Witnesses report the widow Marta Kessel received payment for the restoration of lost recollections. Sailors' wives. Families of the drowned." He smiled thinly. "Grief makes poor customers, madam, but loyal ones."

"That's slander," Marta said. "I mend *sails*. Ask anyone. Ask—"

"Can you prove you've never handled another soul's memory?"

The question landed in the street like a dropped glass. Because that was the thing—no one could. Not Marta, not the fishwife, not the priest if one had been brought. The Gift left no scar, no tell, nothing the crown's assessors claimed they could detect except in the gifted themselves, and the test for that was a thing Vesper had heard described by a keeper who'd survived it in the southern provinces: nine hours of questions while a device of clockwork and crystal ground through your skull, and even then, even then, false positives ran one in five.

"I'm a sailmaker," Marta said, and her voice cracked at last. "I have been mending sails for forty years. I buried my husband. I buried my son. I have nothing left to take from me except—" and here she looked directly at the officer, direct as a blow, "—except whatever you're about to take."

Vesper stood in the crowd with his heart going like a trapped bird, and every rational part of him—the bookkeeper, the survivor, the man who trusted paper over people—said *stay still, say nothing, you cannot help her, helping her kills you both.* The Court had already given its order. Silence. Darkness. Wait.

But Marta had fed him when he was hungry and asked nothing, and the arithmetic of that did not fit in any ledger he owned.

He stepped forward. One step. Enough that she might see him, so that she would not stand alone in the lantern light believing her street had abandoned her.

It was a stupid, sentimental, unforgivable gesture, and it nearly killed him, because her eyes found his—and then the older officer's eyes found his a half-second later, and held, and Vesper felt the look like a hand closing around his throat. The officer was memorizing his face. Vesper knew the craft of memorizing; he could see the man doing it, deliberate as a scribe.

"Name?" the officer called to the crowd generally, meaning him specifically.

"He's nobody," the fishwife said loudly, bless her, stepping half in front of Vesper. "That's the ledger clerk from Coopers' Lane. Counts other people's coin. Never counts anything worth taking."

A ripple of agreement—nervous, but real. The crowd closed its ranks around him the way it had refused to close around Marta, because Marta was already condemned and he was not yet. He understood that too. He would have done the same, three days ago.

The officer looked at him a moment longer, then dismissed him with the smallest shrug in the world, and turned back to his work. They put Marta in the wagon. She did not cry out. Through the slats, as the wagon pulled away, Vesper saw her looking back at the street—at the tomatoes in their barrel, at her darkened windows, at the faces of her neighbors—and her expression was not fear. It was the expression of a woman doing sums.

He knew that expression. He wore it every day.

* * *

The Court convened at midnight, in the candlelit undercroft beneath the counting room, and Vesper stood at the back with twenty-odd keepers and brokers and watched the guildmasters decide who would live.

There were nine chairs at the long table—one for each master of the Ninefold Court, rank upon rank descending—and Corvane sat at the head with the proclamation flattened out before him like a specimen. The masters argued for two hours. Vesper listened, and took no notes, and remembered everything, because that was what he was for.

Master Quillon of the Third Chair wanted open defiance: strike the assessors, burn the palace archives, show the crown the Court's teeth. Master Ilseve of the Fifth wanted flight: scatter to the coast provinces, dissolve the Court into a hundred cellars, reassemble in ten years. Master Dree of the Seventh—fat, soft-spoken, kind-eyed, the one who had trained Vesper in extraction, who had taught him that a stolen memory should leave the donor *gentler* than it found them—wanted surrender of the guilty.

"We have members who traded carelessly," Dree said. "Members whose work drew notice. The crown wants heads. Give them the heads that earned the axe, and the body survives. In a year, in five, we buy back the prisoners or buy out the assessors. The Court has survived kings before."

"How many heads?" asked Ilseve.

"A dozen. Perhaps fifteen. The low-ranked. The visible ones." Dree's voice was gentle as ever. "The ones whose loss the Court can bear."

And the room—this was the part Vesper would carry to his grave—the room *nodded*. Not eagerly. Wearily, regretfully, the nodding of sensible people agreeing that surgery hurts. Fifteen names, discussed like entries to be written off against a bad year. He scanned the table for one face that flinched and found none. Corvane's face least of all; the guildmaster had not spoken during the entire debate, and his silence was not neutrality. It was assent. It had been assent since the beginning. Vesper realized, with a vertigo that had nothing to do with the candles, that Dree's proposal had not arisen tonight—it had been *arrived at* before tonight, and the debate was theater performed for the benefit of the junior members along the walls.

Himself included.

"Keeper Winterbourne." Corvane's voice cut across the room like a blade through silk. "You have something to add? You've been breathing audibly for a quarter hour."

Every face turned. Vesper could have said nothing. Could have bowed and murmured some clerical question about the burn lists. Fifteen names, and one of them might yet be his—low-ranked, visible, bearable—and the wisest thing in the world was to be furniture.

"Marta Kessel was taken today," he said instead. "Lantern Row. Accused on an anonymous complaint. She is not Court. She is not gifted. She mends sails." He let the silence stretch. "If we give them fifteen of ours, masters, they will take thirty. Then fifty. The crown isn't hungry for the guilty. It's hungry. Full stop. You cannot feed a hunger by deciding whose turn it is to be eaten."

"You mistake obedience for strategy," Dree said kindly, and that kindness was the worst of it.

"No," Vesper said. "I mistake it for what it costs."

Corvane rose. The candle flames leaned as if from a draft that wasn't there. "The motion stands," he said. "The list will be drawn by week's end. Keeper Winterbourne—you will draw it."

The room went very quiet. It was, Visper understood dimly, a test and a punishment braided together: make the objector write the death warrant, or break him, or both.

"With respect, guildmaster," Vesper said, "I keep records of things taken from people. I don't write lists of people to be taken."

"Then resign your chair."

Eleven years. Eleven years of careful columns and double-locked drawers, of being the man who could hold a hundred lives without dropping one, of trusting paper because paper never lied about what it cost. He looked at the nine masters in their nine chairs and saw, suddenly, with terrible clarity, a row of accountants balancing books in a burning house, arguing over which floor to sacrifice first.

"I'll draw the burn list of the ledgers," Vesper said. "Nothing else."

Corvane studied him for a long, cold moment. "As you like," he said softly. "Bring the list tomorrow. And Winterbourne—" the guildmaster's voice dropped, almost gentle, which from Corvane was the most frightening register of all, "—do be careful on the streets. The crown is taking people on anonymous complaints now. Anyone can be accused of anything." A pause, precise as a knife-point. "No one can prove otherwise."

* * *

He walked home through the emptying streets with the proclamation papers pale on every door, ghost-leaves in the lamplight, and the city behind its shutters holding its breath. Nine days to surrender. Fifteen names to feed the axe. And somewhere above it all, in a palace on the hill, a dying prince whom Vesper had never seen, whose sickness required—what? Whose mother had decided that the cure was other people's pasts, harvested by the hundred?

He climbed the stairs to his rooms and lit his lamp and sat at his desk with the day's work untouched, and found his hands were shaking. Not from fear, or not only. From the specific shame of a man who has just watched the institution that raised him reveal its arithmetic: *some lives are entries, and entries can be written off.*

Marta's face at the wagon slats. *Doing sums.*

Well. Two could keep accounts.

Vesper pulled a blank folio toward him, dipped his pen, and at the top of the first page wrote, in his small exact hand, not the burn list Corvane wanted but a different list entirely: the names of every keeper ranked below the seventh chair, every broker, every runner, everyone the Court would call *bearable*. Fifteen names. He would know them before Corvane did. Whatever was coming, they would not learn of it from a list drawn by their own guild.

At the bottom of the page, after the fifteenth name, he hesitated—then wrote a sixteenth line:

*Marta Kessel, Lantern Row. Taken without cause. Debt outstanding.*

Because if the Ninefold Court had forgotten what its ledgers were for, then someone had to remember.

Outside, far off toward the river, a dog began to bark, and then another, and then a whole chorus of them, rising and falling in the dark like an alarm no one had taught them—as if the animals could smell what was coming before any of the people could.
