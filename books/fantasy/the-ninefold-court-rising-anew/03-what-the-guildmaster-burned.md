## 3. What the Guildmaster Burned

The counting room smelled of tallow and old paper and, beneath both, something faintly sweet—like fruit going soft in a cellar. I had stopped noticing it years ago. That night I noticed everything.

It was the third hour past midnight, and the Court slept around me in its crooked warren of stairways and shuttered rooms, but I could not sleep, and so I did what I always did when sleep refused me: I went down to the ledgers. There were forty-one of them in my keeping. Forty-one books spanning two hundred and eleven years of traded pasts—first summers sold by farmers drowning in debt, last words of mothers copied out for children who had been too young to hold them, whole marriages extracted from widows who could not bear to wake beside a ghost. Every entry in my hand or the hands of keepers before me, every price recorded, every withdrawal signed. The Ninefold Court called it accounting. I called it the only honest thing in the city, because paper never lied about what it held.

I was balancing the autumn entries by candlelight—the dying woman's summer, the miller's guilt, a soldier's homecoming that had fetched an obscene sum—when I heard the fire.

Not the ordinary crackle of the hearth in the corner. This was lower, hungrier, the sound of a great deal of wood being fed at once. And beneath it, almost lost, another sound: a voice, or many voices braided into one, speaking in a cadence I knew without knowing where I'd learned it.

I set down my pen.

The counting room had one door and one window overlooking the courtyard, and through that window, past the frost-feathered glass, I saw light where there should have been none—a glow rising from the direction of Corvane's private study, gold-orange against the black brick, throwing the gargoyles' shadows long across the cobbles.

Corvane did not burn things. Corvane kept things. He kept them the way a spider keeps flies: wrapped, labeled, patient. In nineteen years as guildmaster he had discarded nothing, not broken quills, not failed apprentices, not debts of honor three generations stale. If Aldous Corvane was burning something at the third hour of the morning with no witness intended—

I took my coat. I did not take a lamp.

* * *

The study door stood ajar, which it never did, and heat rolled out through the gap like breath from a beast. I pressed myself to the wall beside it and looked through.

The room had been stripped. The shelves were bare—not emptied recently but emptied over days, I realized; the dust-shadows on the wood showed where books had stood for years and stood no longer. Everything portable was gone. All that remained was the fireplace itself, which was not a fireplace at all tonight but something older: the stones around it were carved, I saw now, carved with nine interlocking rings worn nearly smooth by centuries of hands, and inside it burned a fire far too large for any chimney, a fire that gave off no smoke, only light and that low braided singing.

And Corvane stood before it, feeding it a book.

He fed it page by page, tearing each leaf free with a deliberateness that was worse than haste would have been. He read each page first—I saw his lips move—and then he tore it out and held it to the flames until the edges caught, and dropped it in. He had been at this a long time. The floor around him was littered with covers, bindings split like husks, and the book in his hands was the oldest thing I had ever seen outside a museum: leather gone the color of wet slate, corners rounded to nothing, its spine stitched with thread that gleamed faintly silver even in the firelight.

I knew every ledger in the Court's collection. I knew their dates, their hands, their watermarks. I had never seen this book, because it had never appeared in any inventory—which should have been impossible, since I made the inventories.

"Vesper."

I hadn't moved. I hadn't breathed loudly. But Corvane spoke without turning, his voice level, almost gentle, the way it always was when he was about to do something irrevocable.

"You might as well come in. You'll only stand there freezing."

* * *

I came in because the alternative was worse than whatever waited inside. That has always been my arithmetic: choose the known cost over the unknown one, write the number down, move on.

"You're destroying records," I said. My voice sounded thin in all that heat. "Two hundred years of entries. Do you know what the Court is if the ledgers are gone? We're nothing. We're thieves with good handwriting."

"They aren't entries." He tore another page free, glanced at it, and paused. For a moment something moved across his face that I had never seen there—not guilt, exactly. Recognition, maybe. Grief's poor cousin. Then he held the page to the fire and let it go. "They never were. Sit down, boy. You deserve to hear it from me rather than from the ashes."

There was nowhere to sit. The chairs were gone too. So I stood, and he spoke while he burned, and this is what he said—or near enough, for I have replayed his words so many times since that they have worn smooth as river stones, and some of them may be mine now instead of his.

"I was twenty-six when I was raised to guildmaster," Corvane said. "You'll want to know why me. I asked the same question of the man who chose me, old Master Ellery, and he told me what I'm about to tell you, and I laughed at him. Remember that. Remember that I laughed.

"The Court is not a guild. It was never a guild. Guilds trade in goods and services, and we have certainly done that—we've grown fat and comfortable on the trade, fat enough to forget—but the trading was never the purpose. It was the maintenance. Every memory we extract, record, and hold in these books is a knot tied in a rope, and the rope runs between this world and the other one. The old world. The hungry one.

"You've felt it. Don't look surprised—you're the finest keeper we've ever produced, and you think that's craft. It isn't craft alone. When you pull a memory out of a living mind and lay it down on paper, you aren't copying it, Vesper. You're binding it. Ink and vellum hold what flesh cannot, and the Otherworld feeds on what flesh lets go. Every memory we take and keep is a meal withheld from that side. Every ledger is a chain-link. Two hundred years of small mercies and small thefts, all of it knotted together into a leash.

"There are nine seals in the old covenant. Nine anchors. Nine mortals, in every generation, who carry a fragment of the binding in their own remembered lives—memories so heavy with meaning that the Otherworld cannot digest them, cannot get past them, the way a throat cannot swallow a stone. The ledgers name them. Not plainly—the covenant protects itself—but plainly enough for a keeper who knows how to read. And you read better than anyone I have ever taught.

"The queen knows. She has known for six years, since the night her son was born wrong. He isn't sick, whatever her physicians call it. He is a vessel, an open door, and the thing behind the door has been starving since the last rebinding, four hundred years ago, when the seals were scattered and the ledgers begun. Isolde believes she can feed it selectively. Feed it the gifted, feed it the stolen memories, keep her son alive and the realm intact and pay the bill with other people's pasts. She has proclaimed her Purge, and half the city will cheer her for it, because people will always cheer the burning of someone else's house.

"And I—" Here he stopped. He looked at the book in his hands, the last of it, perhaps a hundred pages remaining, and the firelight made his face into something ancient and exhausted. "I have spent nineteen years telling myself that the Court could survive anything if it survived intact. That as long as the ledgers existed, the covenant held, and whatever bargain the crown struck, the balance would outlast Isolde and her son and every hound she breeds. Tonight I received word that her soldiers found the Bristol archive. They didn't burn it, Vesper. They *read* it. They took the memory-keepers alive and they took the ledgers away in wagons under seal of the privy council, and tomorrow they will come here.

"So I am choosing. Ellery told me the ledgers must never fall into royal hands entire—that a complete covenant in the grasp of a starving vessel is not a chain anymore but a key. I believed him the way you believe a story you were told as a child: completely, and without understanding. Now I understand. If I cannot protect the binding, I will break it into pieces small enough to hide. Three pages. That is all anyone should ever hold at once. Three pages, given to three keepers, scattered—and the rest to ash, so that no one, not the queen, not the Court, not the Otherworld itself, can ever hold the whole design again."

"Why tell me?" I said. It was the only question left standing after all the others had burned. "Why not just do it?"

"Because you already stole your three pages."

I looked down. I don't remember reaching for the fire. I don't remember choosing. But my hand was in my coat, against my chest, and through the wool I could feel them: three leaves of wet-slate leather-backed vellum, warm as skin, and trembling—no, not trembling. *Humming.* A note too low to be heard, felt instead in the bones of the wrist, in the teeth, in the hollow behind the sternum where fear lives.

"When?" I whispered.

"While I read the third one aloud," Corvane said, and for the first time all night he smiled, and it was the saddest expression I have ever seen on a human face. "You were always the best thief in the Court, boy. I only ever taught you bookkeeping to give the thieving somewhere decent to live. Go. Out the river door. Don't pack, don't warn anyone, don't go home. And Vesper—"

He turned back to the fire, to the last hundred pages of the covenant of the Ninefold Court.

"Don't read them by daylight. Some pages prefer to be read in the dark, and some pages read *you*. Now run."

* * *

I ran. Through the corridor that smelled of cold ash and snuffed candles, past the dormitory where my fellow keepers slept their last untroubled sleep, down the stair to the river door whose bolt I had oiled years ago for reasons I had never examined until now. The night outside was hard and clear, the Thames running black under ice-rimed banks, and I walked fast along the towpath with the three pages against my heart and the hum of them spreading slowly through me like ink through water.

In the lee of a warehouse, sheltered from the wind, I finally stopped and took them out.

Three pages. The first was written in a hand I recognized from the oldest ledgers—Master Ellery's own, though younger, fiercer, the letters leaning forward like runners. It described a place: *the ninth ring of the courtyard stones, where the fire burns without smoke,* and beneath it, a list of names, nine names, most faded past reading. One remained sharp as the day it was written, in fresh ink over old:

*Maren Ostrey, the miller's daughter of Harrow Ford, who keeps her brother's forgiveness and does not know its weight.*

The second page was older still, its script cramped and strange, half language and half notation, and where the ink pooled thick the page was not dark but *deep*—I looked into those pools the way you look into wells. At the bottom of the deepest pool, small and clear as a coin dropped in water, I saw snow falling. A child's mitten, red wool. Laughter I could not hear but somehow recognized, the way you recognize a voice through a wall.

The third page I did not read. I meant to. I held it up to the moon and got as far as the first line—*Let the keeper understand that what is given away*—and then the hum rose through my hand and up my arm and into my chest, and the Otherworld spoke.

I say it spoke. It was more like remembering a voice than hearing one: vast, patient, and terribly hungry, a voice that had been waiting four hundred years behind a door and had learned, in all that time, to be very polite.

*Little keeper,* it said, in the space behind my eyes where I keep the memories of others. *You smell of so many hands. So many yesterdays. Open.*

I closed my fist on the page until the edges bit my palm, and I said—aloud, to the empty towpath, to the black river, to the thing behind the world—"No. You are inventory. You are catalogued. You belong to the ledger, and I keep the ledger."

The hunger receded, amused, unhurried. Doors do not chase. Doors wait.

But my hands were shaking, and they did not stop shaking for a mile.

I walked until the Court's chimneys sank below the rooftops behind me, and then I sat on the frozen mud of the riverbank with my back against a bollard, and I thought about forty-one ledgers full of knots in a rope, and nine mortals walking the provinces with stones in their throats and no idea what they carried, and a queen feeding her son on other people's pasts, and a guildmaster laughing at a story he'd been told as a child and paying for the laugh nineteen years later.

And I thought: I am a bookkeeper. I balance entries. That is all I have ever been, all I ever wanted to be—someone who keeps faith with paper because paper keeps faith with me.

The pages hummed against my ribs like three small hearts, and somewhere across the dark city a bell began to toll the fourth hour, and I understood that the counting was over. Whatever I had been keeping, it had never been accounts.

Then I heard footsteps on the frost-hard ground behind me—light, quick, deliberately loud enough to announce themselves, which in this city on this night meant either a fool or someone who had learned caution the hard way—and a voice I knew, thinner and harder than it used to be, but unmistakable, saying my name like a question she wasn't sure deserved an answer.

"Vesper? Vesper Winterbourne?"

I turned, and the girl I had trained—the girl whose childhood I had once held in my hands and, alone among all the Court's keepers, had never sold—stood at the edge of the lamplight with snow in her hair and terror in her eyes.

"Wren," I said. "How did you find me?"
