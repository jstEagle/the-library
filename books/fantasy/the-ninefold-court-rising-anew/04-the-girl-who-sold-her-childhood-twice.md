## 4. The Girl Who Sold Her Childhood Twice

The city had taught Wren to count exits the way other children learned to count prayers.

Three in this room: the window with its rotten sill, the cellar hatch behind the flour sacks, and the front door, which was no exit at all but a mouth that opened only for soldiers. She sat on a stranger's bed in a stranger's garret above a chandler's shop on Candlewick Row, and she counted her exits twice because counting was what kept her hands still. Her hands were never still otherwise. They were small hands, quick hands, hands that had learned to lift memories out of skulls as lightly as lifting coins from a purse, and tonight they shook like a drunkard's.

She had been running for nine days.

Nine days since the proclamations went up nailed through every door in Lowmark like nails through coffin lids—*by decree of Her Majesty Isolde, all practitioners of memory-craft are to surrender themselves for assessment*—and she had learned in those nine days that *assessment* was a word the crown used the way butchers used *processing*. She had watched a tinker dragged from his cart on Tanners' Bridge while his wife screamed and held his boots and got trampled for it. She had seen the hounds.

That was the worst part. Not the soldiers, not the red-and-gold of Isolde's livery, not even the pyres they'd built in Coronation Square. The hounds. Great pale beasts with milky eyes and nostrils that flared at nothing, at everything—at the ghost-smell of yesterday. They didn't track you by where you went. They tracked you by what you'd touched. What you'd taken. What you'd left behind inside other people's heads.

Wren had left pieces of herself in forty heads across three districts, and every one of them was a scent-trail now, a ribbon of her laid across the city like breadcrumbs leading backward to a childhood she'd already sold twice.

She pressed her palms flat against her knees and made herself breathe.

*You're alive,* she told herself. *Alive is a start.*

Outside, the bells of Vespers rang from Saint Aldhelm's, six slow strokes, and the light through the garret's one window went the color of old honey. Down in the street a chandler's boy swept wax shavings into a pile, unhurried, unaware, and Wren watched him with an ache she couldn't name—the ache of watching someone who had never had to sell anything.

She had sold everything. That was the joke of her, wasn't it? That was why they'd called her Sparrow in the Court, because she'd pick herself clean and hand the pieces over smiling. First time she'd sold her childhood, she was eleven years old and her mother was coughing blood into a rag and there was a broker on Cordwainer Street who paid well for early memories, unblemished ones, the kind rich clients wanted because their own first snowfalls had been spent long ago. Wren hadn't even hesitated. She'd walked into the extraction room, lain down on the cold slab, and let a fat man named Odger pull the winter out of her head like drawing a splinter—and afterward she couldn't remember snow at all. Couldn't remember sledding down Gildersleeve Hill or the taste of the first plum off her grandmother's tree or her mother's face before the sickness. It was all gone, sold in portions, and she'd carried the coin home in her teeth like a dog carrying dinner to its dying master.

Her mother died anyway. In spring. Of something else entirely.

And then—because grief makes idiots of us all, and because the Court had taught her that anything in your head was inventory—she had gone back two years later and sold it again. Sold the memory *of selling it*. Sold the guilt, the shape of the empty place where winter used to be, the whole hollow architecture of loss. A second client, a second payment, and after that she woke some mornings genuinely puzzled by the weeping, because whatever had caused it was gone, and gone, and gone.

Three layers deep, the emptiness went. Like a well you kept digging hoping to reach water and finding only more dry stone.

Vesper Winterbourne had been the one who found her sitting on the Court's back steps afterward, fourteen years old and hollow as a struck bell, and he had crouched down to her level with his ledger under his arm and said, "Do you know what I do with what people give me?"

She'd shrugged. Everyone knew what keepers did. They kept, until keeping became inconvenient, and then they sold.

"I write it down," he'd said. "Exactly as given. And then I put it in the vault, and I don't take it out, and I don't sell it, and when the giver comes back—and they always come back, eventually—it's still there. Unspent." He'd looked at her with those gray eyes that seemed always to be reading something just over her shoulder. "You've been treating yourself as stock, girl. Stop it."

She hadn't stopped, not really. But she'd started giving things to *him*. Small things, worthless things—a memory of a bad oyster, a memory of losing a shoe in the harbor mud—because giving to Vesper felt less like selling and more like putting jewelry in a strongbox. He never took a fee. He never asked why. He wrote it in his ledger in that beautiful clerk's hand, and he kept it, and once, when she came back half-starved from a bad job in the Shambles, he'd fed her stew in the counting room and shown her the entry: *From Wren of Lowmark, received: one memory, the smell of bread burning, given freely.* As if she were someone whose gifts mattered.

He was the only keeper who had never sold what she gave him.

Which was why, on the tenth night of the Purge, she was going to walk across half of Lowmark to find him—if he was even alive, if the Court hadn't burned its own doors shut, if the hounds hadn't already found the trail of forty small Wrens scattered through the city like torn paper.

* * *

She moved at dusk, when the lamplighters were still making their rounds and shadows lay thick in doorways.

Candlewick Row south to the Tanner's Walk. Tanner's Walk west along the canal, walking the towpath where the mud would swallow her footprints and the water-smell would confuse any nose, even a nose that smelled yesterday. She wore a fishmonger's apron over her clothes and carried a basket of actual herring, because a girl hauling fish was invisible; a girl moving fast was a story soldiers told each other around fires. Twice she passed patrols and both times she slowed her walk and let her shoulders slump and thought hard about gutting fish, about scales and salt and the silver flash of knives, burying herself so deep in borrowed mundanity that even she almost believed it.

At the Mouth Bridge a proclamation flapped on the tollhouse door, and she made herself read it again, because fear demanded its meal.

*BY DECREE OF HER MAJESTY ISOLDE, QUEEN OF THE REALM AND ITS DEPENDENCIES: Let it be known that the practice of Gift-work—being the unlawful extraction, trade, or possession of the memories of others—is hereby declared a corruption of the natural order. All persons so gifted shall present themselves for assessment within the fortnight. Those who conceal the Gifted shall be assessed likewise.*

*Natural order.* Wren spat over the rail into the canal. There was nothing natural about a queen who built pyres. There was nothing natural about a city where a tinker could be dragged off his own cart for the crime of being useful to sad rich people.

It was full dark by the time she reached Guildhall Hill, and that was when she saw the smoke.

Not hearth-smoke. Archive-smoke—paper and vellum and leather bindings going up in a greasy black column against the stars, and beneath it the Ninefold Court's windows blazed yellow, and there were figures in the courtyard feeding the fire, armful after armful, and Wren stood in the shadow of a cooperage wall with her heart slamming and understood: the Court was burning its own ledgers.

Burning the records. Burning the proof. Burning—

Burning *her*, she realized. Every gift she'd ever placed in Vesper's keeping was written in those books, and the books were going into the fire, and if the entries burned did the gifts burn too? She didn't know. Nobody knew how deep the craft went, whether a memory lived in the skull alone or in the page as well, and the not-knowing hit her like cold water. Somewhere in that fire might be the smell of burning bread, given freely to a man who kept things safe, turning to ash along with everything else.

She should run. Every instinct she owned said *run*, the city was coming apart, the Court was dying, and here she stood at ground zero of the whole catastrophe like a moth asking the lantern's pardon.

But Vesper.

If Vesper was in that courtyard, she wasn't leaving without knowing which side of the fire he stood on.

She circled wide, through the cooperage yard, up the drainage lane that ran behind the Court's east wall—the same route she'd used a hundred times as a apprentice thief slipping out past curfew—and found the postern gate standing ajar. Standing ajar was wrong. Everything about the Court was locks and ledgers and locked ledgers, and an open gate during a purge was either an invitation or a grave marker.

Inside, the courtyard was chaos lit orange. Guildmasters she half-recognized shouted at porters. Carts were being loaded—not with ledgers but with strongboxes, plate, the Court's movable wealth—and between the carts and the archive door ran a chain of clerks hurling armloads of books into a bonfire that roared like something alive.

And there, apart from all of it, at the edge of the firelight, stood a tall thin man with ink-stained fingers and a ledger clutched against his chest the way a soldier holds a wounded comrade.

"Vesper!" The name tore out of her before caution could catch it.

His head snapped around. For one heartbeat he simply stared, as if she were a figure stepped out of one of his entries, and then he crossed the courtyard in six long strides and caught her by the shoulders, and up close she could see what the firelight had hidden: exhaustion carved into his face, a burn blistering across one knuckle, eyes that had seen something in the last day that had broken a piece of him.

"Wren." Her name in his mouth sounded like a ledger entry read aloud. "Saints' bones, child, what are you doing here? The whole city knows this hill by morning."

"I came for my things." Her voice cracked and she hated it. "Whatever you kept. Whatever's still—I heard they're burning the—" She gestured at the bonfire, at the black flakes of vellum rising like obscene snow.

Something crossed his face. Guilt? Grief? With Vesper it was always hard to tell; he wore his feelings the way clerks wore smocks, buttoned up and underneath.

"Not yours," he said quietly. "Nothing of yours is in that fire. I pulled the personal holdings this afternoon—all the freely-given vault, mine and three others who refused the order. It's—" His hand tightened on the ledger against his chest. "It's with me. Wherever I'm going."

Relief hit her so hard her knees nearly buckled, and she covered it by scowling, because Wren of Lowmark did not buckle, Wren of Lowmark counted exits. "Then I'm coming with you. Wherever you're going. Because I'm done, Vesper. Done running alone. The hounds found my trail at the Shambles two nights ago and I only lost them by swimming the Grange culvert in freezing water, and I can't—" Her voice did the cracking thing again. "I can't keep being inventory. I want to give myself somewhere safe. One last entry. Freely given. You understand?"

He looked at her for a long moment. Around them the Court burned its own history, and the guildmasters loaded silver, and somewhere beyond the wall a hound began to bay—a long, low, hungry sound that raised every hair on her arms.

"I understand," Vesper said. "But Wren—there's something you need to know first. About the ledgers. About what they actually are." He glanced toward the fire, and his voice dropped to almost nothing. "They were never accounting books. And there are pages missing from the oldest one. Pages I stole out of the flames. And when I touch them, they *speak*."

The hound bayed again, closer.

"And here's the worse news," he went on, grim and fast. "One of those pages—the fourth seal of nine—it isn't humming from the vault, Wren. It's humming from *you*. From something you gave me years ago and forgot you gave. Something about snow."

Wren's whole body went cold, colder than the Grange culvert, colder than the well she'd dug in her own head down through three layers of sold childhood.

Because she remembered, suddenly, the one thing she had never sold to anyone else, the one gift she'd brought to Vesper's vault and nowhere else, back when she was sixteen and desperate and didn't know why: a memory she'd found wedged in her mind like a splinter of glass, a memory of snow falling upward, of a white field where a voice like a starving choir had whispered *keep this, little sparrow, carry it, we will come for it someday*—

The hound broke into a run.

"Tell me later," Wren said, grabbing his sleeve. "Run first."
