## 5. Nine Seals, One Debt

I did not sleep that night. I sat in the counting room with the three rescued pages spread before me on the scarred oak table, and I read them by candlelight, and the more I read, the colder the room became.

The pages were not written in Corvane's hand. They were not written in any hand I recognized from the guild's archive—none of the careful clerk-scripts, none of the flowing ledger-cursive we used for entries of trade. This was older. The letters looked as though they had been pressed into the paper while it was still wet clay, then fired, then somehow made to remember being paper again. The ink was not ink. It moved when I was not looking directly at it, the way water moves under ice.

And the pages hummed.

I had noticed it in Corvane's study, snatching them from the fire—the vibration against my fingertips like a struck tuning fork. Here, alone, laid flat on my table, they hummed together, a low chord just below hearing, felt in the teeth and behind the eyes rather than in the ears. My gift, which had never once failed me, could not get a grip on them. When I reached for the memories stored in those pages—if there were memories stored there—I touched something vast and cold that recoiled from me like a hand pulled back from a stove.

So I did what I have always done when the world refuses to make sense. I treated it as an entry.

*Item: three pages, recovered from destruction. Provenance: the oldest ledger, burned by order of Guildmaster Corvane. Nature: unknown. Value: unknown.*

The words looked absurd even in my own head. Some things do not fit in ledgers. I had built my whole life on the belief that everything does.

The first page began without preamble, mid-thought, as if I had opened a conversation already centuries underway:

*...and because the Otherworld cannot cease its hunger, being made of hunger as the sea is made of salt, the covenant binds it not by force—for nothing can bind hunger by force—but by feeding. Nine seals shall be set into nine mortal keepers, one for each door of the Court, and each seal shall be fed on memory freely given, and so long as the nine hold, the starving heart shall be satisfied with smallness.*

*But let one seal fail, and the heart shall grow larger. Let four fail, and the heart shall reach across the veil. Let all fail, and the two worlds shall become one world, and there will be no more sleeping, no more forgetting, no more mercy of any kind.*

I read it three times. Then I lit another candle, because the first three seemed to be guttering in some wind that did not exist indoors, and I read it a fourth time.

Nine seals. Nine mortals. The guild had nine masters. I had always assumed this was administrative convenience, or vanity—nine chairs for nine egos around the long table in the hall above. Now I understood the number had come first, and the Court had been built around it like a scabbard forged to fit a particular blade. The Ninefold Court was not named for its founders. It was named for its function. We had not been a guild at all. We had been a lock, and every member of it, from the masters down to the lowliest extractor working the taverns for pennies, had been a tooth in the mechanism.

My hands were steady. I noted that with distant professional interest, the way I would note a client's pulse during an extraction. Steady hands, racing heart. A body preparing for work while the mind caught up.

The second page was worse. The second page was a list.

Not a list of names—though names were there, worn half away, in a script that changed every few lines as though different writers had added to it across generations. It was a list of *keepings*. Entries, in the old covenant-script, of what each seal had been fed. And the entries were memories. Human memories. Ordinary ones.

*A miller's grief for his brother, given freely, 3rd year of King Aldous.*
*A lullaby, sung by a mother who died before her child could walk, given freely.*
*A confession spoken once and never again, given freely.*
*A life, freely given.*

The last entry stopped me. I read the surrounding lines twice, hunting for context, and found only the same refrain: *given freely*. The covenant did not take. That was the whole architecture of it, the thing Corvane had spent his life obscuring, perhaps the thing he no longer understood himself: the Otherworld could not seize what it needed. It could only receive what was surrendered. Every stolen memory in our vaults, every extraction I had ever performed for coin, was a counterfeit of the only currency that mattered—and the counterfeits did not feed anything. They only *replaced* the real thing, clogging the channels, leaving the true debt unpaid beneath mountains of false payment.

We had not been keeping the covenant. We had been smothering it.

And the Otherworld, starved of what it could only be given, had begun to reach.

*

I knew about the prince, of course. Everyone in the city knew about Prince Aldric, the Queen's only son, who had been sick since birth with an illness no physician could name. The court physicians called it wasting sickness; the priests called it a trial of faith; the gossip in the wine-shops called it a curse on Isolde's line. The boy was eleven now and had never been seen in public. The Queen had ordered prayers in every temple, tithes remitted to every healing order, and—three years ago—had begun asking questions that made the guildmasters very nervous indeed.

Questions about gifts. About memory-wielders. About whether borrowed lives could mend a failing child.

When the Purge was proclaimed, the guild's official position was that it was persecution, pure and simple—crown superstition turned against useful people. I had believed that. I had repeated it. I had felt the righteous anger of the wrongly accused, safe in my counting room, invisible and indispensable.

The third page took that belief and broke it over its knee.

It was a record of a bargain. Dated eleven years ago—Aldric's birth year. The handwriting was Queen Isolde's; I recognized it from the few royal writs I had been required to copy in my early years with the Court. Her script was beautiful and ruthless, every letter identical to its neighbors, a hand that allowed no personality.

*To the Keepers of the Ninefold Covenant, greeting. My son was born silent, and in his silence I heard the other country calling through him. Your masters came to me in the night and told me what he is. I did not believe them until I held him and felt him reaching for my memories as a babe reaches for milk. He is not sick. He is empty. Something old abandoned its vessel in my womb and left the shell hungry, and the shell is my child, and I will feed it whatever it requires.*

*I am told the covenant can be unbound. I am told that if the seals are starved, the heart will come to where it is called. I am told my son may be made whole if the vessel is filled. Name your price. I will pay it in whatever coin you ask.*

Beneath, in the older covenant-script, a reply:

*There is no filling an abandoned vessel. There is only feeding the heart that wore it. What you ask will drown both worlds. But you are a mother, and mothers do not hear refusal. Take this instead: the Purge you contemplate will gather memories enough to quiet the boy for a season. Each gifted one your soldiers take will buy him a month of seeming human. And each one taken will crack a seal, for the gifted are the keepers' kin, and the covenant knows its own blood. You will save him monthly and end him yearly. We wash our hands of it. Do what mothers do.*

I put the page down. My candle had burned down to a stub without my noticing. The humming of the pages had changed pitch—or perhaps I had changed, and could now hear it properly. It sounded almost like breathing. Like something enormous asleep nearby, turning over in dreams.

Prince Aldric was not sick. He was a doorway standing open with no one home, and the thing that had abandoned him was still out there, still hungry, still pulling. The Purge was not persecution. It was provisioning. Every memory-wielder dragged from their home, every gift stripped and carried to the palace in lead-lined boxes, was a meal for the hollow prince—a month of borrowed humanity purchased with a cracked seal.

And the guildmasters knew. Corvane knew. The bargain was eleven years old; the Purge had been in preparation for years; the proclamations had been printed with the guild's tacit cooperation, I would stake my ledgers on it. Which meant the burning of the oldest ledger was not panic. It was housekeeping. Corvane was destroying the evidence of the original covenant so that the new arrangement—with himself as sole broker between crown and Otherworld—could stand unchallenged.

He had kept me ignorant for nineteen years. Not because I was unimportant. Because I was *useful*, and useful things work best when they do not know what they are building.

I thought of the dying woman whose last summer I had extracted for her grieving daughter. I had held that summer in my hands like a bird, gently, and sold it, and told myself the gentleness mattered. It had gone into a vault. From the vault, per the new arrangement, it might already have traveled to the palace in a lead box, to be poured down the throat of a hollow child so that a queen could pretend her son was human for another month.

Every entry in every ledger I had ever balanced. Every one of them a stone in a wall I had not known I was building.

*

Dawn came gray through the counting room's high windows. I became aware, gradually, of sounds in the street below—the city waking, but wrong. Fewer carts. More boots. Somewhere a woman was crying, and somewhere else men were shouting an order I couldn't make out, and the shouting had the flat rehearsed quality of proclamation.

I gathered the three pages. I did not put them in a drawer; drawers had locks, and locks implied confidence. I cut open the binding of my own personal ledger—the one book I had kept apart from the guild's records, containing only my private entries, my own small history of trades—and I sewed the covenant pages flat against the boards beneath the lining, where a bookbinder's eye would see only an old ledger swollen with use. Then I wrapped the whole thing in oilcloth and considered where to hide it, and realized the answer had been sitting across the table from me all night in the form of my own reflection in the darkened window.

No vault. No hiding place. Every hiding place in this city could be found, because every hiding place had been designed by people who thought like me. The pages hummed against my palms, patient, ancient, faintly amused, as if to say: *you are thinking like a keeper of records again. We are not records.*

They wanted to be carried. I understood that suddenly, the way you understand a door is locked not by studying it but by trying it. The seals lived in mortals. Mortal flesh was the only vault the covenant trusted. And these pages—fragments of the binding itself—wanted keepers too.

Very well. I would carry them. I, Vesper Winterbourne, who trusted paper more than people, would spend the coming days trusting my own ribs to do what iron and oak could not.

But carrying was not enough. The list on the second page had given me more than dread. It had given me a map. Nine seals, nine keepers, and the entries recorded what each keeper had *given*—and I knew memories the way a priest knows scripture. A miller's grief for a drowned brother. A lullaby. A confession. I had seen entries like these pass through my own books. Some of them I had extracted myself.

The keepers were clients. The keepers were people whose pasts I had handled, priced, and sold. The covenant had scattered its seals among ordinary lives, and I had spent nineteen years walking through those lives with my hands in their pockets.

Somewhere out there was an old miller who had forgiven his drowned brother, and I was the man who had stolen that forgiveness and sold it. If the seal lived in the memory, and the memory lived in my vault—

I stood up so fast the chair went over backward. In the gray light, with the city sounding its wrong notes below, the plan assembled itself with terrible clarity: the vaults must be opened. The memories must go home. Not because restitution was noble—because restitution was *survival*. The seals could not hold while their keepers were hollowed. Every memory I returned was a seal mended.

The guild would call it theft. The guild was calling a great many things theft these days, and hanging the word around the necks of innocents.

I took up my coat, my ledger, my three humming pages hidden against my heart, and I blew out the candles. On the table, in the wax, my reflection stared back at me from the polished wood: a thin, precise, forgettable man. Invisible. I had treasured that invisibility for nineteen years, believing it was my nature.

It was not my nature. It was my cage, and someone else had built it, and someone else had chosen what it should contain.

The door of the counting room opened behind me without a knock, and Wren stood in it—soaked to the knees, soot on her cheek, eyes wild, holding a knife she clearly hoped she wouldn't need.

"They're burning the low-quarters," she said. "Vesper—they're burning us first."
