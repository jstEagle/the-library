## 7. Hounds That Smell Yesterday

The hounds came at dawn, and they came smelling of yesterday.

Wren heard them before she saw them—a low, wet keening from the direction of Tanner's Row, the sound of animals grieving something they could not name. She was on the roof of the cooperage with a stolen loaf and a view of the canal when the first handler rounded the corner below her, and her mouth went dry around the bread.

Not dogs. Not really. They had the shape of mastiffs stretched long, the color of river mud, but their heads were wrong—too broad, too smooth, ears absent as if hearing were a sense beneath them. Where a dog's nose would wrinkle at the world, these creatures' faces rippled constantly, skin moving like water over stone. And trailing behind them, visible only to someone like Wren—someone who'd spent six years learning to see the seams of memory—came the *smell*.

That was the horror of it. She didn't watch them track; she watched them *taste*. Each ripple of that wrong face pulled a thread of yesterday out of the air: the ghost of a baker's flour-dusted joy, the residue of a child's tantrum in the doorway of the chandler's shop, the bright oily smear of a man's fear where he'd paused to read the proclamation nailed to his door. The threads streamed backward into the hounds' open mouths like smoke returning to a fire.

They were eating the city's memories off its own streets. And somewhere in all that flavor, they would find hers.

She thought of Vesper's face last night, gray in the lamplight of the flooded quarter's safehouse, saying, *They'll come for us by what we've touched. Go east along the canal. Don't argue.* She hadn't argued. She was good at not arguing; it was the first thing he'd ever taught her, back when she was eleven and starving and he'd caught her lifting memories off drunk sailors for pennies. *Never run from a keeper's question,* he'd said. *Answer it slowly, and take something while you talk.*

But there was no talking to these things.

The loaf slipped from her fingers and rolled down the roof tiles. She let it go.

Below, the handler raised one gloved hand—not toward the roof, thank every quiet god, but toward the alley mouth—and the lead hound poured itself into the dark after some older scent. The column moved on: four handlers, six beasts, moving through morning fog like a funeral that had lost its corpse.

Wren counted to two hundred, the way Vesper had taught her, then went down through the skylight into the loft where their packs waited. Vesper stood at the narrow window, ledger pages wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like a newborn.

"They're on Tanner's Row," she said.

"I know." His voice was flat, which meant it wasn't. "They crossed the fish market an hour ago. Whatever they're following is old—a week, maybe more." He turned, and for a moment his eyes did the thing they always did when he was frightened, going still and bookish, as if he could file the fear away between entries. "It's not us. Not yet."

"Yet."

"Wren." He set down the bundle and looked at her properly, and she hated him a little for how gently he did it. "If they catch our scent—the real one, the one we leave behind every time we touch each other's thoughts—we won't outrun them on foot. So we don't try to outrun them. We make them chase something else."

"The flooded quarter."

"The flooded quarter," he agreed. "Six years of my work is soaked into those streets. Every extraction I ever ran in the tenements, every trade in the waterside taverns. A thousand flavors of somebody's yesterday." Something flickered across his face—regret, maybe, or its accountant's cousin, guilt. "I'm sorry. I know what I'm asking you to walk through."

She should have asked him what he meant. She should have made him say it plain: that the flooded quarter was where he'd taken *her* childhood, or part of it, the winter she couldn't remember except as a shape of snow. But the hounds were keening closer, and there was no time in the world for questions that hurt, so she just said, "I'll go west along the canal. You go east."

"No."

"You said don't argue."

"I said don't argue about small things." He pressed the wrapped pages into her arms before she could refuse them. "You carry these. If they take me, they get nothing but a tired bookkeeper who can't remember why he's tired. If they take you—" he stopped, and she watched him decide not to finish the sentence, watched him choose a different door out of it—"if they take you, the covenant loses its courier. That's all this is. Bookkeeping."

"That's all anything is, with you."

He almost smiled. It was the worst thing she'd seen all morning, worse than the hounds.

"West," he said. "Stay in the water where you can. Water eats scent—even theirs. And Wren—" he caught her shoulder as she turned, "—don't be brave. Be gone. There's a difference, and I need you to be the second thing."

Then he was down the stairs and out into the fog, east, and she stood alone in the loft holding the covenant of two worlds in oilcloth, feeling like a girl again, feeling like the girl he'd trained, feeling—beneath the fear, in a place she refused to examine—like someone who had been trusted.

She went west.

* * *

The canal at dawn was the color of pewter, scummed with coal dust and cabbage leaves. Wren waded in up to her thighs before she started moving, boots and all, because dry stones left prints and prints left stories. The cold climbed her body like a slow reader. She kept the oilcloth bundle above her head, held in both hands, and told herself the shivering was just the water.

For half a mile, it worked. The canal bent south past the tanneries, past the drowned steps of Old Ferry Lane where the poor had once lived until the river took their street and the crown took their compensation. She passed windows at eye level now, green glass thick with silt, and behind one of them a memory hung like a moth—some housewife's ordinary Tuesday, decades dead, still fluttering against the pane because no one had ever come to collect it. The flooded quarter was full of such things. Abandoned yesterdays, drifting in the current. Vesper called it the richest fishing ground in the city and had never once seemed ashamed of casting nets there.

She was thinking about that—about him, about shame—when she heard the keening change key.

It dropped. Went low and hungry. Went *interested*.

She turned in the water, and there, on the embankment fifty yards back, stood a single handler with two hounds, and both of their rippling faces were pointed at her like compass needles finding north.

They'd found her trail anyway. Dry crossing, earlier, before she'd learned better—or maybe they didn't care about footprints at all. Maybe they could taste her straight off the air: six years of stolen goods hanging around her like perfume. Every memory she'd ever lifted and carried and sold left its grease on her hands, and she suddenly understood, with a clarity that felt like drowning ahead of schedule, that to these creatures she must smell like a butcher's shop.

"*There!*" the handler shouted, and his voice cracked with something that wasn't duty. It was delight. These men got paid per head, she'd heard. Per *gifted* head.

Wren ran.

Not along the bank—along the bank she was a rat on a wall—but *into* the flood itself, plunging chest-deep into the canal and striking out for the sunken lane beyond, where the water spread wide over what had been streets. Her legs went heavy. The bundle rode high in her grip. Behind her, splashing, shouting, the handler ordered his beasts into the water, and she heard—one instant, horribly—the sound of a hound entering the canal, and the sound it made doing it, which was not a bark but a long satisfied exhale, like a man sitting down to a meal.

She swam. She was a strong swimmer; the harbor rats had taught her before Vesper ever taught her anything. But the water here was treacherous, full of submerged walls and gateposts and the black mouths of doorways, and she was swimming blind through other people's pasts. Memories rose around her in the murk like bubbles—a wedding, a funeral, a fistfight outside a tavern forty years gone—and each one brushed her skin with its warmth or its cold, distracting, disorienting, a whole drowned parish clamoring to be remembered.

Her foot caught a gatepost. She pitched forward, went under, came up gasping with the bundle somehow still aloft, and the hound was *close*, so close she could hear the wet working of its face, taste the air it tasted. It wasn't even swimming hard. It was letting her tire.

So she did the only clever thing left in her, the thing Vesper would have done, the thing that made her stomach turn even as she did it: she grabbed a passing memory out of the water—a fat, juicy one, some fisherman's decades of dawns—and she tore it open and scattered it behind her like chum.

The effect was immediate and obscene. Both hounds wheeled toward the cloud of released yesterday, faces rippling in ecstasy, and for three precious seconds the canal belonged to her alone.

Three seconds. Then the nearest beast shook itself like a dog leaving a bath and turned back to her, unfooled, because chum is chum and she was the living thing, and living things smell of tomorrow too.

She never knew afterward whether it was the gatepost or the hound's lunge that took her under. There was a moment of impact against something solid, a bloom of pain along her ribs, and then the canal closed over her head and the world became brown roar.

The bundle. She had the bundle. Her fingers were still locked around the oilcloth, her arm straight up, straining it toward a light that shivered far above. Her lungs began their argument. Her coat dragged her down. Somewhere overhead, muffled to nothing, men were shouting, and she thought with strange detachment: *they'll pull me out and I'll be worth money, and Vesper will be east with no one carrying the pages, and the covenant will rot in an evidence locker between confiscated knives.*

No. No, that was—

Her boot wedged. A doorway, a lintel, some iron hook of the drowned past, and now she was truly caught, anchored in nine feet of gray water by one stupid foot, and her vision was going soft at the edges, soft and warm, and a memory rose to meet her that wasn't hers—it was the canal's own, the river's memory of rain, of falling forever in silver threads—and she thought, distantly, *so that's what it feels like from the sky's side,* and her grip began to loosen,

and a hand closed around her wrist.

Not a gentle hand. A hard one, rope-scarred, certain, the hand of someone who had pulled drowning things out of water before and resented having to do it again. It hauled. Her boot tore free with a sound she felt in her ankle's bones, and then she was rising, rising, breaking the surface into gray morning with a gasp that felt like being born angry.

The embankment wall. Someone had dragged her along it, underwater, thirty feet, to where the old ferry stairs broke the surface. Hands lifted her onto wet stone, and she curled there, coughing the canal out of herself, clutching the bundle to her chest, alive, alive, *alive*.

Above her, on the far embankment, the handler stood with his beasts, looking directly at the ferry stairs, looking directly at her.

And doing nothing.

He looked for a long moment. The hounds rippled and tasted the air and found—what? Wren would wonder about this for weeks. Perhaps the stranger's presence confused the scent. Perhaps the flooded quarter's thick soup of abandoned memories masked her. Or perhaps—and this was the version she eventually chose to believe—the handler looked at the shivering, half-drowned girl on the stairs, and the girl he saw was not a bounty but a daughter, someone's, anyone's, and whatever the crown paid for gifted flesh, it was not enough to buy the look in his own memory that her face put there.

He turned away. He whistled his hounds off the water's edge, and they flowed after him, east, toward richer hunting, and Wren lay on the wet stone and shook.

"Can you stand?"

The voice came from beside her, gravelly, unhurried. She twisted to look and found an old woman crouched at the top of the ferry stairs, mending net, of all things—mending net on the flooded stairs as if the world hadn't ended, as if proclamations weren't nailed to every door. Gray plait, forearms like dock rope, eyes the flat honest color of the canal itself.

"Can you stand," the woman repeated, not unkindly, "or shall I keep mending?"

Wren tried. Her leg held. Her ribs screamed. She stood, dripping, and the woman looked her over—the bundle clutched to her chest, the water streaming off her, the terror she couldn't scrub from her face—and asked exactly none of the questions a person asks.

"Not from round here," the woman said instead.

"No."

"Running from the dog-men."

"Yes."

"Mm." The woman bit her thread and tied it off. "My sister was gifted. Could find lost things. Keys, rings, people." She said it the way you'd report weather. "They took her in the spring, before the dogs. Men with papers." She wound the finished net around her fist with terrible neatness. "Nobody asked me any questions either. Nobody ever does, when they're taking people. Questions are only for the ones left standing." She jerked her chin eastward, along the sunken lane. "Old Ferry runs dry under the arches for another mile. Cold, dark, and empty of everything except the past, which nobody official wants. You can walk it to the tannery sluice and come up beyond the checkpoints."

"Why?" Wren's voice came out ruined, half water. "Why help me?"

The old woman considered her for a moment, and then she smiled, and it transformed her whole craggy face into something young.

"Because you were carrying that bundle over your head," she said, "even while you drowned. Whatever's in it, you thought it was worth more than breathing." She stood, joints cracking like kindling, and handed Wren the last thing Wren expected: a strip of dried fish, wrapped in cloth. "People who hold on like that are rare. My sister was one." She turned back to her nets. "Go on, then. Before the water changes its mind about you."

Wren wanted to say something worthy of the moment—something about debts, about return, about the new guild Vesper dreamed of building where nothing taken stayed taken. But her throat wouldn't make the words, so instead she bowed, clumsy and dripping, the way you bow to someone who has just rewritten your idea of strangers, and went down into the dark under the arches.

* * *

Old Ferry Lane, dry under its stone ribs, was exactly what the woman had promised: cold, dark, and crowded only with the past. Memories hung in the blackness like lanterns no one had lit—doorway after doorway glowing faintly with the lives that had ended here when the river came. Wren walked through them with her hand on the wall and the bundle against her heart, and she did not take a single one.

It felt like a vow. It felt, absurdly, like the easiest thing she'd ever done.

At the tannery sluice she climbed into gray daylight, wrung out her coat, and got her bearings: south and east, two hours' hard walking, to the ruin of the old counting house where Vesper had said—if he lived, if he made it, if the hounds hadn't tasted his particular flavor of careful loneliness—that they would meet at moonrise.

She walked. The sun climbed. Her ribs throbbed in time with her steps, and the dried fish was the best thing she had ever eaten, and twice she passed crown patrols and twice she became, through sheer concentrated effort, nobody: a tanner's drudge, a widow, a girl with errands. Being unseen was a craft, Vesper always said. You don't hide from people's eyes; you hide in their assumptions.

Moonrise found her at the counting house, in the roofless room where the stars fell through the ceiling like the world's most patient snow. She built no fire. She sat against the wall with the bundle in her lap and listened to the city breathe.

He came an hour late, which meant something had gone wrong, and he came limping, which meant it had gone more wrong than that. But he came—gray-faced, coat torn at the shoulder, one hand pressed to his side, and alive, alive, *alive*—and when he saw her sitting upright in the starlight with the covenant still in her arms, he stopped in the doorway and looked at her for a long time without speaking.

"You're wet," he said finally.

"I drowned a little."

"You were supposed to stay out of the water."

"I was supposed to do a lot of things." She tossed him the rest of the dried fish. "Eat your dinner, bookkeeper. And then tell me why there's blood on you, and don't say it's accounting."

He lowered himself carefully against the opposite wall, and in the starlight she saw that his hand, when it finally left his side, was red to the wrist—and that he was smiling, faintly, terribly, in the way of a man who has opened a ledger and found something in it that changes every entry that came before.

"It's not accounting," he said. "Wren—the ledgers. The ones Corvane burned. I found what was written on the missing pages." He looked up at her, and his eyes were the eyes of a man reading his own death warrant and finding it interesting. "The seals aren't hidden in vaults. They're hidden in *people*. Nine keepers. And I think—" he hesitated, and she braced herself, because she had known him six years and knew what came after that particular hesitation, "—I think one of them might be you."

Outside, far off across the sleeping city, a hound began to keen.
