## 8. The Court Splits

The summons came at dusk, folded inside a ledger page the way Corvane liked to send everything—disguised as bookkeeping.

*Convening of Masters. The counting room. One hour. Bring nothing you cannot burn.*

Vesper read it twice by candlelight, then held the paper over the flame until it curled into a black moth that drifted down among the ledgers. Bring nothing you cannot burn. As if any of them carried anything else. As if the Court itself were not a building made entirely of things that could burn—the memories of ten thousand strangers pressed between boards, the covenant humming in its oldest spine like a struck bell that had not yet finished ringing.

He took the three stolen pages out from beneath the floorboard where he'd hidden them, wrapped in oilcloth beneath a false row of debt entries for a spice merchant who had never existed. They lay in his palm, thin as moth wings, warm as living skin. Even after weeks they had not cooled. Sometimes, when he woke before dawn, he could hear them: not words exactly, but the *shape* of words, the way you can hear someone speaking through a wall without catching a single syllable. The Otherworld, murmuring on the other side of the vellum.

*Nine seals,* he thought. *Kept by nine mortals.* And one of those mortals was a girl who had sold her childhood twice and didn't know what she carried.

He put the pages back. He locked the floorboard. He straightened his cuffs the way his mother had taught him before she died, before he learned that grief could be weighed and sold by the ounce, and went down to be counted.

* * *

The counting room had never held so few people or so much silence.

Once, the long tables would have been crowded with forty keepers, sixty, their fingers ink-stained, their voices overlapping in the low arithmetic of trade—one summer here, three winters there, a first kiss against a second funeral, always balancing, always weighing. Now Vesper counted eleven. Eleven masters and keepers seated around the master table where Corvane presided, and every chair between them empty like pulled teeth.

Candles burned down the table's center, and beyond them, at the head, Guildmaster Corvane sat with his hands folded on a single closed ledger. He had not burned that one. Vesper noted it the way he noted everything: automatically, uselessly, an entry written in a mind that no longer trusted itself.

Corvane looked older than he had a month ago. Not weaker—older the way a blade looks older after it has been through a fire and come back harder. His gray eyes moved down the line of faces with the unhurried precision of a man auditing accounts.

"Eleven," he said. "There were thirty-one when the Purge began."

No one answered. Master Ondrell, fat and trembling at Corvane's right hand, studied his own knuckles. Mistress Sable, who had taught Vesper the extraction cant before he could grow a proper beard, sat very still, her silver rings clicking once against each other and then going silent.

"Three taken in Ashmarket. Four fled south—we have heard nothing since. Five dead." Corvane said the numbers as if reading them aloud helped him believe they were only numbers. "And eight more unaccounted for. Which means, masters, that we are past the point where silence is a strategy. Silence is now simply the sound we make while they find us one at a time."

He opened the ledger. Vesper's stomach tightened. He knew that binding—that cracked green leather, that brass clasp worn smooth by two hundred years of thumbs. It was the registry of members. Every name the Ninefold Court had ever enrolled, entered in the hand of whoever kept the books at the time. Vesper's own name was in there, in his predecessor's careful script, dated nineteen years ago.

"I have been offered terms," Corvane said.

The word *offered* landed strangely, like a coin dropped in a quiet room.

"Queen Isolde does not want the Court destroyed." A pause; Corvane let the sentence breathe, let hope lean into it the way he'd once let clients lean into their own desperation across this very table. "She wants the gifted delivered. The crown will suffer the guild to continue—as a chartered house, licensed, inspected—if the Court surrenders its working members. Those who carry the gift of taking." His eyes came up. "The masters, the archivists, the bookkeepers—those whose talents are merely clerical—will be spared. Protected, even. Under charter."

The candles flickered. Somewhere above them, in the rafters where the oldest ledgers slept in their iron cabinets, something creaked, and everyone flinched, and everyone pretended they hadn't.

"Hand over our own," said Master Ondrell at last, his voice a wet whisper. "That's the bargain. That's what you're telling us."

"I'm telling you what I was told," Corvane said. "I am telling you that the alternative is what happened to the Lanternkeepers last month. You all saw the smoke. Their archive burned with them in it because their guildmaster mistook pride for duty. I am telling you," and here his voice dropped into the register he used for closing sales, the register Vesper had heard him use to talk grieving widows out of their mothers' voices, "that the Ninefold Court is older than every person in this room. Older than the crown. It has survived purges before. It survives by being *necessary*. If we are emptied of thieves, we become necessary again. We become the crown's memory-brokers instead of the city's. And the gift—" his gaze slid, deliberately, and rested on Vesper, "—the gift survives too. In safer hands. Hands that understand stewardship."

*In your hands,* Vesper translated silently. *You'll take the gift for yourself, sell it back to us at interest, and call it salvation.*

He kept his face still. Twenty years of keeping other people's secrets had built him a face like a locked drawer.

Mistress Sable spoke without raising her voice. "How many names do they want?"

"All of them."

"And if we refuse?"

"Then they take them anyway," Corvane said, "and burn the Court around the refusal." He turned the ledger around so its open page faced the table. Blank columns waited under the headings: *Delivered. Retained.* "I did not call you here to debate. I called you here to record. Each master signs beside the names of their own people. That is the arrangement—the crown wants the guilds to police themselves. They want to know we chose it."

*They want complicity, not just obedience,* Vesper thought. *A proclamation nailed to every door is an order. A signature in our own book is absolution.*

Ondrell signed first. Of course he did; Ondrell had never risked anything in his life except his digestion. His pen scratched, quick and guilty, and Vesper watched him write the name of a boy named Tam Wicke whom Vesper had trained himself—a stuttering, sweet-natured boy who could pull a memory as gently as drawing a thread from cloth. Tam, who had once spent an entire winter secretly restoring a blind client's memory of her wedding day, unpaid, because he couldn't bear to charge her for sunlight.

Sable hesitated longer. Her rings clicked. When she finally took the pen, her hand shook so badly the signature came out broken—but it came out. She did not look at anyone afterward. She looked at the candle flames as though they might contain instructions.

One by one, five of the eleven signed. Five refused—not with speeches but with stillness, with folded arms, with the particular silence of people deciding which death they preferred. Old Master Ferrow, who had kept the debt-books for forty years, pushed his chair back from the table with a scrape like a bone breaking, and stood, and left the room without a word. Two others followed him. That left Vesper, and two keepers whose names he barely knew anymore because lately all his knowing had gone into the pages under his floorboards.

Corvane watched the defectors go without anger. Then he turned the ledger toward Vesper.

"Keeper Winterbourne."

"Guildmaster."

"You have people under you."

"I have ledgers under me," Vesper said. "People are what's *in* them."

Something flickered across Corvane's face—irritation, or perhaps recognition; they had both been made by this room, shaped by its arithmetic until they thought in entries. "Your apprentices. Your clients. The girl Wren, formerly of your training." The name dropped into the room like a stone into deep water, and Vesper felt the ripples go through him and kept his breathing level. "Sign, and she falls under the charter's protection along with the rest of the clerical staff. Refuse, and she is listed as unaccounted. Hunted. You know what the hounds do with the unaccounted."

*She doesn't even know what she carries,* Vesper thought. *You're bargaining with a seal you don't know exists, using a life you don't own.*

"The crown's protection," Vesper said aloud, "lasted exactly as long as the crown found it convenient. Ask the Lanternkeepers."

"The Lanternkeepers were fools."

"They were *ours*," Vesper said, and heard his own voice crack on the word, and hated it, and let it stand. "That's the whole of it, isn't it. That's the entry. Thirty-one of us, and we've just agreed to sell half, and the price is that the other half gets to keep the furniture." He stood. Around the table, the signatories would not meet his eyes—except Sable, who finally did, and her look was worse than any hatred. It was apology. It was *I'm sorry, boy, I'm old, I'm tired, I want to live.*

"Sit down, Keeper," Corvane said quietly.

"No."

"Sit down, or be marked."

The word went through the room like cold water through cloth. Everyone knew what marking was; it hadn't been used in living memory, but the Court kept the ritual in its oldest volumes, and Vesper had catalogued those volumes himself. He had read the procedure the way he read everything—curiously, clinically, certain it was history.

He should have known better. Everything he had ever filed as history had a way of climbing back off the page.

"Mark me, then," Vesper said.

Corvane rose. He drew from his sleeve a stylus—not steel but bone, yellowed, older than the table, older probably than the Court—and he came around the table with the unhurried gait of a man doing nothing remarkable, and Vesper stood his ground because running would have meant admitting it mattered.

"It needn't hurt," Corvane murmured, almost kindly. "It's only a binding. You of all people understand bindings."

"That," Vesper said, "is precisely why I refuse yours."

Corvane caught his wrist. The grip was stronger than an old man's had any right to be, and the bone stylus touched the back of Vesper's hand, below the knuckles, where the skin is thinnest, and *wrote.*

It was not ink. It was not pain, either—or not only pain. Vesper felt the mark go in like a hook going into deep water: a small bright sting at the surface, and then something vast turning over underneath. The stylus traced a character from the covenant-script, the same script that hummed on his stolen pages, and he understood, with the sick clarity of a bookkeeper reading a fraudulent entry, what Corvane was actually doing. Marking wasn't punishment. Marking was *registration*. It wrote Vesper into the crown's ledger through the Court's own hand—it made him a debt outstanding, a line item awaiting collection. Any hound, any soldier carrying the queen's writ, could feel the mark like heat through a wall. And worse: the mark was drawn in covenant-script, which meant it touched the part of Vesper that was bound to the Otherworld. It gave whatever held the other end of that thread a handle.

Corvane released his wrist and stepped back, and for one unguarded moment his face was not triumphant but *grieved*, and Vesper realized with a lurch that the old man believed he had just saved the world the only way it could be saved. That was the most terrible thing about Corvane: he had never once done evil for evil's sake. He did accounting.

"You were the best of them," Corvane said softly. "Remember that I said so, later, when it matters."

"Mark that down somewhere," Vesper said. His voice came out steady. His hand did not; he hid it in his coat. "You're good at records."

He walked out of the counting room past the bowed heads of the men who had signed, past Sable's wet eyes, down the stairs that smelled of tallow and dust and twenty years of his own life, and out into the street where the first snow of the season was coming down fine and dry, settling on the proclamations still nailed to every door, softening the crown's seal into something almost gentle.

* * *

He did not go home. Home was the first place they would look, and besides, home had stopped being a word that fit anywhere the moment the bone stylus lifted from his skin.

He went instead to the flooded quarter, walking fast, hood up, left hand buried in his pocket where he could press his thumb against the mark and confirm what he already felt: it pulsed. Faintly, slowly, like something sleeping under ice. And it was *pulling*—not at his flesh but at the oilcloth-wrapped bundle hidden in the lining of his coat. He'd brought the three pages with him. He couldn't say why, except that leaving them behind in a building where Corvane had just begun selling souls by signature had seemed unthinkable, and the mark had approved of the decision, had *leaned* toward the pages like a compass needle finding north.

Which meant Corvane could find him by it. Which meant Corvane could find *the pages* by it.

Vesper stopped under the broken arch of an old tollhouse, snow gathering on his shoulders, and forced himself to think in entries, because entries were calm, entries were safe:

*Item: the Court has split. Five signatures, six refusals, one defection in progress.*
*Item: I am marked. The mark tracks.*
*Item: four seals remain unaccounted for beyond my knowledge; the girl carries the fourth and does not know it.*
*Item: Corvane said "when it matters," which means he expects a later. Men like Corvane always expect a later. That is how they justify the present.*

And beneath the entries, in the space where a honest bookkeeper would write his true feelings and never did:

*Item: I loved them. All of them. The room, the candles, the arithmetic. It was the only church I ever had, and tonight it sold its congregation.*

Snow hissed softly on the canal water. Down the flooded lane, a lantern bobbed—watch patrol, or hound-handlers, or nothing—and Vesper pressed himself into the arch's shadow and watched it pass, and in that moment of stillness he felt the mark pulse hard, twice, like a knock.

Not pulling toward the pages this time.

Pulling *south*. Toward the river district. Toward, though he could not have said how he knew, the narrow streets where a girl who sold her childhood twice was hiding.

The mark wanted him to run. So, of course, he ran the other way.
