## 9. The Second Seal: The Drowned Choir

The harbor at Saltmere did not freeze in winter. It only went still—a skin of grey water stretched over black depths like the lid of an eye that had decided not to look at anything anymore.

Wren crouched at the edge of the flooded quarter with her boots off and her trousers rolled to the knee, testing the water with two fingers. It was cold enough to make her knuckles ache instantly, cold enough that she understood why no one swam here, why even the fishermen rowed out past the breakwater before they dropped their lines. Beneath the piers, beneath the silt, beneath the bones of three drowned districts that had slid into the sea a century before either of them was born, something was singing.

She couldn't hear it from up here. Not exactly. But she could feel it, the way you felt a tooth before the pain arrived—a low pressure behind her sternum, a hum in the fillings of her teeth. Memory-thieves learned early that the body heard things the ears refused.

"You're going to take your pack," Vesper said behind her. It wasn't a question. He was standing in the shadow of a collapsed customs house with his ledger satchel strapped across his chest like armor, and he had the particular expression he wore when he'd already lost an argument with himself and was trying to lose it with her too.

"I'm going to take my pack."

"The seal-keepers don't want visitors. The pages say—"

"The pages are written by men who never went anywhere." She pulled her boots back on. "You read me the passage again last night. *Beneath the harbor, the second voice keeps its watch.* That's not a warning, Vesper. That's directions."

He didn't smile, but something around his eyes loosened by a degree, which for Vesper was practically laughter. He had been losing pieces of himself for six days now—not fast, not all at once, but in slivers. This morning he'd called her *girl* instead of *Wren*, and when she'd corrected him he'd gone white as gull-bone and written her name on his own wrist in ink, like a man marking a door so he could find his way home. She hadn't let herself cry about it. Crying was for people with time.

They went down together.

* * *

The way into the drowned quarter was through a customs tunnel that had half-collapsed and half-flooded and somehow done both without dying completely. They waded in to their waists, then their chests, then found a ledge of old masonry where the ceiling dipped low and the water opened below them into darkness. Wren lit a stub of tallow candle and held it over the edge. The flame bent sideways, drawn down toward the water as if the sea were inhaling.

"There," Vesper whispered.

Below, far down where the candlelight gave up, there was light anyway. A green-gold shimmer, slow and rhythmic, pulsing like the glow of deep water fish—or like the glow behind a closed eyelid.

And now she could hear it. The singing.

It came up through the stone and through the water and through the soles of her feet, and it was the most terrible thing Wren had ever heard, because it wasn't frightening at all. It was beautiful. Dozens of voices, layered, patient, weaving in and out of each other like threads in a loom—and every thread was a memory. She could taste them as the sound touched her. A wedding morning. A fever breaking. The smell of bread in a kitchen that had burned forty years ago. The moment someone's child first said their name. The drowned did not sing songs. They sang everything they could never forget, because forgetting was the one mercy death had denied them.

"They've been down here a hundred years," she said. Her voice came out small.

"The districts sank in the Sinking of Aldermoor." Vesper had gone bookkeeper-pale, the color he turned when the numbers stopped adding up. "Three parishes. Over a thousand souls. The crown called it an act of the Otherworld and used it as justification for the first covenant restrictions, and nobody—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Nobody asked what happened to the bodies."

Wren looked at him. "We're going down there."

"We're going down there," he agreed, and the fact that he said *we* and not *you* was the whole reason she'd crossed half the country to find him.

There was a stair, once. Now it was a ladder of iron rungs bolted into the rock, furred with white mineral bloom, descending into water that glowed faintly from within. Wren went first because she was lighter and because if the drowned wanted to drown someone, better it be her than the man currently holding what remained of the covenant in a leather satchel against his heart.

The water took her at the fifth rung from the bottom. Cold punched the air out of her lungs. She forced herself down anyway, hand over hand along the rusted rail, eyes open in the stinging dark, until the green-gold light rose to meet her and she saw—

They were everywhere.

Figures in the deep water, drifting upright, hair moving like weed, clothes rotted to lace. Their faces were unmarked. That was the horror of it: a century under the sea, and they looked like people sleeping in a current. Their mouths moved. The light poured from between their lips, from their open eyes, from the spaces between their fingers, and every pulse of it carried another memory into the water, and the water carried it to her, and she drank it whether she wanted to or not.

A boy learning to whistle. An old woman burying a dog. A sailor counting stars. A mother—

Wren's hand slipped off the rung.

The memory hit her like a wave: a kitchen, steam on the windows, flour on a wooden table, and a voice, low and cracked with love, singing *hush now, hush now, the tide won't take you, the tide only takes what the tide is given—*

Her mother's lullaby.

Not a memory of it. The memory itself—the real thing, the original, the one she had carried since she was six years old and sold everything else she owned rather than give this up. It lived in her the way her heartbeat lived in her, the last intact room in the ruined house of her childhood, and she had guarded it through training that taught her to trade everything, through hunger, through the Purge, through watching friends dragged into carts with sacks over their heads.

She kicked for the surface and broke through gasping onto the lowest landing, a shelf of stone barely above the waterline, and Vesper hauled her up by the arm with more strength than his thin frame advertised.

"Wren. Wren. What did you see?"

"The choir." She coughed brine. "It's not a ward, Vesper. It's not a trap. They're not guarding the seal the way guards guard a door." She looked back down at the glowing figures, drifting in their slow wheel beneath the surface, mouths open, giving and giving and giving to water that never gave anything back. "They're guarding it the way mourners guard a grave. Because no one else will come."

Vesper sat down slowly beside her on the wet stone. Above them the tunnel mouth was a coin of grey daylight, impossibly far away. Below them the dead sang.

"Then we ask permission," he said finally. "We don't take. We ask."

* * *

They waited for the change of the tide, because the oldest rules of the guild said that the dead keep honest hours and dishonest doors, and Wren figured the drowned counted as both. When the water dropped a handspan, exposing a lip of stair that ran down into the flooded nave of a submerged church, they descended side by side, walking where the singers had walked a hundred years ago.

The church was intact in the way dreams are intact—arches holding, pews soft with silt, an altar furred white as a frost-covered field. And in the nave, in the green-gold light of their own voices, the drowned had gathered.

Dozens of them. Close now, close enough to see that their eyes were open and aware and unbearably sad, close enough to see that some of them wore the expressions of people mid-sentence, interrupted a century ago and still waiting to finish. The nearest was a woman in the rotted remains of a good dress, her hands folded like a lady at prayer, and when Wren met her gaze the choir's song bent around her like wind around a corner.

The woman's mouth moved, and though the singing continued from every other throat, her voice came through the water alone, clear as a bell rung in an empty house:

***Little thief. You brought nothing to sell.***

"I'm not—" Wren started, then stopped. There was no point lying to the dead; they had seen everyone lie. "I am. I was. I stole memories for the Ninefold Court. I sold my childhood twice, once to the Court and once to a man in Corvane's district, and I'd have sold it a third time if the price had been right." Her voice shook and she let it. "But I'm not here to sell. I'm here to trade. Fairly. If you'll hear the terms."

***Terms,*** the drowned woman echoed, and around her the choir rippled, voices rising and falling, and memories washed over Wren in waves—a christening, a hanging, a harvest, a war—the whole drowned parish pouring its century into the water between them. ***No one has come to us with terms. Only thieves, taking shards of us for trinkets. Only divers, stealing our dead. Tell your terms, little thief.***

"The second seal of the old covenant," Wren said. "It's here. Down here. In your keeping. The realm above is burning—there's a queen hunting everyone like me, feeding them to a vessel that isn't her son, and if the covenant fails entirely, the Otherworld comes through the cracks and this city drowns again, and this time nobody gets to stay dead and sing about it." She made herself breathe. "We need the seal. We need it to pass to living hands. And we know better than anyone alive that nothing passes without a price."

Silence, except for the singing, except for the sea.

***And what would you pay?*** the drowned woman asked. ***What does a thief carry that the sea could want?***

Wren had known the answer since the water first touched her. She had known it on the ladder, known it on the stairs, known it in her bones the way you know a tooth before the pain arrives. She had been carrying the answer across half the country without letting herself look at it directly, the way you don't look at the sun.

She reached into herself.

Every gift-worker did it differently. Vesper went quiet and still, like a man balancing entries. Wren went inward the way you'd go into a house you grew up in blindfolded—past the burnt rooms, past the empty shelves, down the corridor to the last intact door. Behind it: flour on a wooden table. Steam on the windows. A voice, cracked with love, singing *hush now, hush now, the tide won't take you.*

Her mother had died when she was six. This was all that remained of her. Not a story, not a description—a presence, a sound, a warmth, the actual weight of being loved by a specific person on specific evenings in a specific kitchen. Everything else Wren had ever sold had hurt. This would do more than hurt.

She lifted it out of herself gently, the way you'd lift a sleeping child, and held it in both hands where the drowned could see it. It shone. Of course it shone. It was the brightest thing in the sea.

"This," she said, and her voice broke clean in half, and she kept talking anyway. "My mother's lullaby. The original. Never copied, never sold, never shared. One hundred years your parish has sung everything it can't forget to water that doesn't listen." She knelt on the altar step, in the silt, in the cold, and held the memory out over the water like an offering at a shrine. "So listen to this one. Take it into your choir. Let her voice be one of yours. Let her sing with you, down here in the dark, so that somewhere under this harbor there's one song that somebody chose to give."

***Little thief.*** The drowned woman drifted closer. Her face was kind, which was somehow worse than any cruelty. ***This will cost you more than the other seals will cost your friend. Do you understand that? You will leave here lighter, and you will not grow the missing piece back.***

"I know what I'm offering."

***And you pay gladly?***

And here was the strange thing, the thing Wren would remember later when remembering was all she had: the answer came easily. Not without grief—grief was the whole currency of the transaction—but without resistance. Some prices you paid because you had to. And some prices you paid gladly, because paying them was the first honest thing you'd done in years.

"Gladly," she said.

The choir changed key.

She felt it happen—the memory lifting out of her hands, out of her chest, out of the last locked room in the ruined house—and it didn't tear the way stolen memories tore. It unfolded. It went willingly, a bird leaving an open palm, and as it spread into the water the entire choir caught it, hundreds of drowned throats opening to receive one small human voice, and suddenly the song beneath the harbor had words Wren knew, a melody Wren knew, *hush now, hush now, the tide won't take you*, woven through a century of the dead like a single golden thread pulled through grey cloth.

Her mother's voice joined the drowned, and they made room for her.

Wren knelt on the altar step and wept—not the ragged crying of fear but the deep, silent kind, the kind that empties you out so something new can settle. Vesper's hand came to rest on her shoulder, awkward and careful, a man touching something he was afraid of breaking, and she let him, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing in either of their vocabularies equal to the moment.

When she could stand, the drowned woman drifted near again, closer than before, close enough that Wren could see the wedding ring still on her finger, fused to bone.

***The seal,*** the woman said. ***Living hands, as was promised in the old days. Come.***

They followed her through the drowned nave to the base of the altar, where a slab of stone had been carved long ago with nine interlocking circles, eight of them worn blank. In the center of the second circle, something glowed: a disc the size of a coin, pale as moonstone, humming with a note that harmonized with the choir above. It had not been hidden. It had been held. For a hundred years, by a thousand singers, because no one else would come.

***Take it,*** the drowned woman said. ***And tell the ones who rebind the covenant what we told the first binders: the Otherworld is not a wolf to be chained. It is a guest to be fed. Starve it, and it eats the table.***

Wren lifted the seal from its setting. It weighed nothing and everything, and the moment her fingers closed around it the choir's song shifted again—simpler now, quieter, a watch relieved—and she felt the second circle in the stone go dark, its duty passed on, its keeping ended after a hundred years.

* * *

They climbed back into the grey daylight of Saltmere harbor like people surfacing from a dream, dripping, silent, wrung out. Wren kept reaching for the lullaby out of habit and finding the room where it had lived swept clean and open to the sky, and each time the absence hit her fresh, and each time, underneath the grief, she found the same stubborn coal: *she's singing. Wherever she is now, she's singing, and she's not alone.*

Vesper spread the rescued covenant pages on a fisherman's overturned boat and laid the second seal beside them. The pages drank its light, and ink that had been faded rose to the surface like a bruise coming up, and Vesper read aloud, slowly, translating the old binding-script:

"*Two seals gathered. Seven remain. The third sleeps in the east, with a keeper who cannot die and wants to. The fourth walks the roads in the keeping of a child of snow.*" He paused. Frowned. "*And the seventh lies in the breast of the crown, heavier than any jewel, and the vessel-starved prince shall wear his mother's regret like a second crown—*"

He stopped reading.

"Vesper?"

"He's going to the palace." Vesper's voice had gone flat and fast, the voice of a man doing sums that frightened him. "Isolde. The seventh seal is inside the queen herself—her memory of the bargain, the choice she made—and if the Purge feeds the vessel much longer, if the Otherworld finishes hollowing that boy out, the seal dies with whatever she becomes." He was already repacking the satchel, already moving. "Wren, I have to go to the palace."

"The palace is the most guarded place in the Nine Realms, and you're forgetting your own name—"

"That's why it has to be tonight." He looked at her then, and she saw it plainly: the man was afraid, properly afraid, and going anyway. "While I'm crawling through the queen's corridors, you take the fourth seal road east. Find the girl with snow in her memory." A ghost of his old dryness surfaced. "You trained her, after all. Apparently I only trained you."

He shouldered the satchel and turned toward the palace heights, where Isolde's banners hung limp over a city full of nailed proclamations—and Wren stood on the dock with a seal warm in her fist, her mother's voice ringing in a choir under the sea, and watched the only person she trusted walk alone toward the hungriest house in the world.
