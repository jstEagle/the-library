## 10. Isolde's Empty Cradle

The candles in the nursery were never allowed to gutter. Isolde had given the order herself, on the third night after the physicians stopped pretending, when she had watched the flame beside her son's bed shrink and flicker and seen—she would swear this until her dying day, though no one living would believe her—that the boy's breathing kept the candle's rhythm. When Aldric exhaled, the flame bowed. When he drew breath, it stood up straight as a soldier at review.

So the candles burned now, forty of them, a small obedient sun in the western tower, and Isolde sat in their light and watched her son sleep, if sleep was the word for it.

He was nine years old. He had been nine years old for three years.

Not frozen—not exactly. The physicians had a gentler vocabulary: *arrested development*, *the wasting stillness*, *a soul that has forgotten to move forward*. But Isolde had eyes, and what she saw was not stasis. It was appetite. Aldric did not simply remain nine; he consumed. Each morning the boy in the cradle-sized bed was a little smoother, a little less particular, the way a river stone is smooth, worn by something passing over it again and again. His face kept its shape the way a glove keeps its shape. And some mornings—this was the part she confessed to no one, not to the priests, not to Marshal Vane, not to the mirror—he woke with someone else's habits. A lilt of northern accent that faded by noon. A way of cracking his knuckles that belonged to a fisherman drowned off the Cape two winters past. Once, terribly, he had looked up from his breakfast and said, in a voice like wet gravel, *Where is my wife?*—and then blinked, and smiled, and was nine years old again, asking for more honey.

She had learned to count the candles instead. Forty flames. If one guttered, she called for the memory-tithe early.

*You are doing this for him*, she told herself, as she told herself every morning, kneeling where the physicians used to kneel. *Every thief you burn is one more day of your son.*

Outside, the city rang with hammer-blows. The proclamations were going up on every door in Vantage—she could hear the nails driving home, a sound like rain on a tin roof, like the beginning of the world's most pious storm. PURGE OF GIFTS, the edicts read, in letters tall enough to be read from horseback. By order of the Crown, all wielders of stolen memory shall surrender themselves or be taken. The Queen's mercy extends to those who come quietly.

*Mercy.* She turned the word over the way she once turned coins as a girl in the border provinces, checking for shave and clip. There was none left in it. She had spent it all.

* * *

The council met at midday, and Isolde presided from the iron chair because kings sat in iron chairs and queens must sit higher.

"The tithe reports are favorable," Marshal Vane said, laying out the ledgers with the crisp satisfaction of a man who enjoyed other people's arithmetic. "Since the Purge began in earnest, deliveries to the palace have tripled. The prince's color improves. The physicians say—" and here Vane permitted himself a smile, thin as a knife laid flat—"they say he may see his tenth birthday."

*His tenth birthday.* As though the boy were a ship approaching harbor, as though time were carrying him somewhere instead of holding him under. Isolde folded her hands. On her right sat Lord Chancellor Merek, who had drafted the legal machinery of the Purge with such elegant precision that warrants now issued themselves, more or less; on her left, the empty chair where Master Corvane of the Ninefold Court had begun appearing two months ago, uninvited, indispensable, smelling always faintly of smoke.

"Your Majesty hesitates," Merek observed gently.

"I weigh," she corrected. "It is different."

"Is it?" He said it kindly, which made it worse. Merek had a gift—no, not a gift, she refused to use that word anymore—a talent for saying cruel things in the voice of a confessor. "The Court's own guildmaster assures us the memory-wielders number fewer than four hundred souls in all the realm. Four hundred, against a kingdom. Against the succession. Against him." He nodded toward the tower above them, toward the small window through which, at this hour, a small face sometimes watched the courtyard. "Weighing seems already done."

"Four hundred souls," Isolde said. "Some of them children. Wren-the-nameless was eleven when the hounds first caught her scent, I'm told. Eleven." She had not meant to say that aloud. She watched it land in the room like a dropped glass.

Vane's jaw tightened. "The gifted are not children, Majesty. They are thieves of the worst kind. They steal what cannot be returned. A man robbed of his past is robbed of his grave, his mother, his very self. The people fear them. The Purge gives that fear a shape, and the shape has Your Majesty's face upon it, and the people love You for it."

*The people love me for it.* She thought of the neighbor woman from the Tanner's Row account—the one dragged into the street last week while her lodgers watched from windows, accused of a theft of memory no accuser could name, no victim found, no proof offered beyond a neighbor's spite and a soldier's quota to fill. Isolde had signed the warrant herself. Her signature had grown fast and fluent these past months, like a weed.

"Master Corvane sends word," Vane continued, consulting a slip of paper, "that the Ninefold Court will honor its compact. The guild delivers its low-ranked members first—the extractors, the couriers, the street-thieves—and retains its masters under crown protection. He calls it *pruning*. His word."

*Pruning.* Isolde rose. The council rose with her, a field of bowed heads, and she hated each one of them a little, and hated herself more, because they were only mirrors.

"I will inspect the tithe myself tonight," she said. "Alone. Clear the tower."

"Majesty, the vessel should not be disturbed during feeding—"

"The tower, Marshal." She let a degree of frost into it. "I am the Queen. I will visit my son."

* * *

They fed him at dusk, and she made herself watch.

The tithe arrived in a lacquered box no bigger than a hymnal, carried by a palace servant whose hands shook so badly the lid chattered against the base. Inside, packed in gray wool, lay a vial of quicksilver-bright liquid that moved against gravity, climbing the glass when Aldric stirred. The memory-tithe, rendered down by Court alchemy from whatever raw stuff the arrests produced. Isolde had asked once, early on, whose memory it was. No one would tell her. She had stopped asking, because the answer was always *does it matter*, and the answer to *does it matter* was always *yes*, and she could not afford yes.

The nurse poured it into the silver cup. The boy drank.

For a moment nothing. Then the forty candles leaned toward the bed like an audience, and Aldric arched his back, and his eyes opened—not the gray of her husband's line, but a deep water-color, green going on black—and behind those eyes something *turned over*, vast and slow, the way a leviathan turns over beneath a boat, and every hair on Isolde's arms stood up in witness.

*My son,* she said to the leviathan. *That is my son.*

The convulsion passed. The boy settled. His eyes closed. His breathing slowed. And Isolde knelt beside the cradle—too small for him now, but he could not bear to sleep in a proper bed, he screamed in proper beds, he screamed in any room without candles—and she took his hand, and his fingers curled around hers with a grip too strong for a child, and she whispered the thing she whispered every night:

"Hold on, my love. Mama is finding enough. Hold on."

And the doubt came, as it always came, in the silence after, and she silenced it as she always silenced it, with the old litany. *The Otherworld is fading. Its hunger spills over into our realm like water through a cracked dam, and it found the weakest vessel in both worlds, and that vessel was my child. The memories feed the hunger so the hunger does not eat him whole. Every thief taken is a wall raised. This is not cruelty. This is a mother bailing out the sea.*

It was even true. That was the horror of it, the thing she could never say to Merek or Vane or the priest who came on rest-days: every word of her justification was true, and it changed nothing about what she did with her days, which was hunt frightened people to feed a thing wearing her son's face.

She was still kneeling there, his hand in hers, when the candle nearest the door went out.

Not guttered. *Out*, snuffed between one heartbeat and the next, and then the next candle, and the next, walking the row toward her like footsteps, and Isolde was on her feet with the letter-knife from her sleeve before her mind caught up to her body. Forty candles. Thirty-one. Twenty-six.

"You shouldn't be here," she said to the dark, because the dark had a presence now, a pressure, someone standing in the doorway where the guard should have been standing. "The tower is sealed. Guardsman! Guards—"

"They won't hear you." A man's voice, quiet, unhurried, pitched low the way bookkeepers speak in counting rooms. "I've borrowed their last quarter-hour. They're remembering a tavern evening. They'll think it was pleasant."

Twenty candles. Twelve. In the failing light she saw him: a lean man in a clerk's gray, moving along the candle-row with his palm out, and where his palm passed, flame died like a snuffed argument. Not a soldier's build. A scholar's stoop. Eyes that held the particular exhaustion of someone who had looked at too much of other people's lives.

"A keeper," she breathed. "One of the Court's keepers." Her knife-hand steadied. "Then you know why I do this. You of all people—you've seen what's inside him. You've felt it. Tell me I'm wrong. Tell me there's another way, and I will hear it, I swear it on my husband's grave, I will hear any way at all—"

"There is," he said. "But not the one you want."

Six candles. Three. He reached the bedside and stood looking down at the boy, and Isolde saw the moment his composure failed—saw it happen, a crack running through the man like ice on a pond. Because Aldric had opened his eyes. And the eyes were not a child's eyes, and they were not entirely the leviathan's either; they were *hungry* in a way that had no age at all, and the boy-thing in the cradle looked up at Vesper Winterbourne—I knew his name then, though she would not learn it until later—and said, in a voice layered like sediment, nine years old at the top and centuries deep beneath:

"*Keeper.* You smell of ledgers. You smell of pages that hold. Come closer. We remember when there were nine. We remember who broke the ninth." A smile spread across her son's face, using her son's mouth, and Isolde's heart tore cleanly down the middle. "Bring Us what Corvane stole. Bring Us the pages. Or We will stop being gentle with this little body, and Your Majesty will bury what remains of her boy in a box lined with lead."

The last candle died.

In the blackness, Isolde heard the window open, heard cloth take wind, heard the clerk's voice one final time—soft, shaken, human:

"He called himself *we*, Your Majesty. Pray to whatever you pray to that we find the seals before he stops pretending to be your son."

Then the night air moved, and he was gone, and Isolde stood alone in the dark with her knife and her litany, and for the first time since the wasting began, she could not make herself kneel beside the cradle. Behind her, in the dark, her son began to laugh—an old, old laughter, patient as erosion—and somewhere below in the courtyard, a bell began to ring, and a voice was shouting news from the gates: the Court had been betrayed from within, a guildmaster had taken two seals and fled to the palace, and he was coming up the hill even now, smiling, with his hat in his hands, to offer the Queen everything she needed to end the Purge forever.
