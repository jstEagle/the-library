## 11. Corvane's Bargain

The message came by raven, which was Corvane's way of saying he considered himself above the city now—above ink and paper and the ledgers that had made him. The bird arrived at dawn, soaked through, a scrap of vellum strapped to its leg with wax the color of old blood.

*Come alone. Bring what you carry. Come before the bells of terce, or the girl pays for your pride.*

No signature. He didn't need one. Corvane's hand had signed every entry I had ever balanced in the counting room; I would have known it blindfolded, the way you know a voice that taught you your letters. The *g* with its long descending tail, like a hook. Like something meant to catch.

I read it three times while the rain worked at the shutters of the safehouse, and each reading made my hands colder.

Wren had been taken. Of course she had. She had gone out two nights past to scout the flooded quarter for the hounds' patrol lines—I had told her not to go, and she had gone anyway, because Wren did not survive by doing as she was told, and I loved her for it the way you love a blade for its edge: knowing it will cut someone, someday, possibly yourself. Now the edge was in another man's grip.

*Bring what you carry.* Three seals. The miller's forgiveness, warm as bread in my coat's inner pocket. The drowned choir's offering, cold and singing faintly if I held it near my ear. And the confession we had bought on the road from a dying customs officer who had wanted, more than anything, to be believed once before the end. Three seals against nine. Not enough to rebind anything. Enough, perhaps, to buy one life.

Corvane knew that arithmetic better than anyone alive. He had invented half of it.

* * *

The meeting place was the old tithe-barn beyond the tanners' row, a building I remembered from my first year in the Court, when Corvane had stood new apprentices in its rafters and shown them how memory was weighed like grain. *Everything has a bulk,* he used to say, tapping the great brass scales that hung there still, green with verdigris. *Everything can be measured. Anyone who tells you otherwise is trying to cheat you.*

He was waiting beneath those scales now, and the symmetry was not lost on me. He had put Wren in the pan where the grain went. He stood where the weigher stood. And he expected me to come stand where the merchants stood, haggling, because haggling was the only language he believed I spoke.

I almost didn't go. I sat in the safehouse with the seals spread across the table like three small suns, and I thought about all the other lives balanced against hers—the miller, who had waited forty years for his brother's drowning to be given back; the choir below the harbor, who had sung their lullaby into Wren's keeping because they trusted her; the customs officer's widow, who slept easier now knowing her husband's last words were true. Every seal was a debt already paid. To hand them to Corvane was to unpay it, to reach into other people's healed wounds and tear the stitching out.

But Wren was not an abstraction. Wren was a girl who had sold her childhood twice so no one else would have to sell theirs at all, and she was in a barn with a man who had burned the covenant and called it prudence, and the bells of terce were four hours off.

So I went. But I did not go empty-handed, and I did not go honest.

* * *

The barn smelled of tallow and wet rope. Corvane had cleared it of everything but a single table, and on the table, arranged with his usual terrible neatness, lay instruments I recognized from the extraction chambers: the silver hooks, the wax seals, the little furnace for softening binding-wax. Beside them, bound to a chair with her wrists behind her and her ankles lashed to the legs, sat Wren.

She looked smaller than I remembered. That was the first thing grief does—it shrinks people, compresses them into the size of the hole they'll leave. Her hair was matted where someone had dragged her by it. There was blood at her temple, dried brown, and her left eye was swollen nearly shut. But her right eye found me across the barn the moment I entered, and it was steady, and it was angry, and it said without moving: *don't you dare.*

"Don't I dare what?" Corvane asked pleasantly, though I hadn't spoken aloud. He had always been able to read faces the way I read pages. It was why he'd kept me close, and why he'd never trusted me. "Don't I dare trade? Vesper, please. Sit."

There was one chair. I remained standing.

"You've brought them." His eyes went to my coat, weighing the bulge of it the way he once weighed sacks of grain. "Three. I counted the hum. You should have hidden them better—they sing, Vesper. They've sung since the night you pulled them from my fire. Did you think I wouldn't hear?"

"I hoped you wouldn't," I said. "You never could resist listening."

"Flattery." He smiled, and the smile was the worst thing in the room, worse than the hooks, worse than Wren's blood. "You always flattered when you were about to refuse me. I've watched you do it for eleven years. You're refusing me now. So let us skip the part where we pretend this is a negotiation between colleagues, and arrive at the part where it is a negotiation between a man with three seals and a man with a girl."

He turned the chair slightly, angling Wren toward me like a merchant presenting damaged goods. Her swollen face caught the light from the high window. She was conscious. She was watching everything. Of course she was. I had trained her to watch everything, back when watching was the difference between keeping a memory whole and cracking it.

"The terms are simple," Corvane said. "All nine seals. You have three. You'll tell me where the others are kept, or who keeps them, or both. In exchange, the girl walks. Unharmed. I give you my word."

"Your word," I said slowly, letting contempt season every syllable, "burned the oldest book in the world rather than be inconvenienced by it."

"My word kept the Court alive for thirty years." His voice rose a fraction, and beneath the rise I heard the thing I had come to hear: fear. Corvane was afraid. He had defected to Isolde with two stolen seals, and Isolde did not reward failure, and the Otherworld's hunger was growing faster than either of them had planned, and somewhere in the palace nursery a vessel-child was flickering between boy and appetite, and Corvane needed all nine seals the way a drowning man needs air—not to save the realms, but to save himself from the queen's disappointment. "You think I don't know what you say about me in the safehouses. The traitor. The burner. Fine. Call me traitor. But I am the only traitor offering you a living apprentice, and the queen is the alternative, and the queen does not offer."

I let the silence stretch. Silence was a tool I had learned from him, and there is a particular pleasure, cold and clean as well-water, in turning a master's own instrument back upon him.

"Walk me through it," I said finally. "Mechanically. If I give you nine seals, what do you actually do with them?"

"That's not your concern."

"It's entirely my concern. I'm being asked to price them. I want to know the buyer's intent." I began to walk, slowly, along the edge of the barn, circling him the way I circled a difficult ledger entry, and he tracked me with his eyes and hated it, because Corvane liked to be the one standing still while others moved. "You told the guildmasters the covenant was superstition. You told them the ledgers were accounting. You burned the binding and called it housekeeping. And now you want nine seals. For what, Corvane? Balance? You don't believe in balance. You believe in ownership. So either you mean to rebind the covenant with yourself as sole keeper—which the pages say cannot be done—or you mean to sell them to Isolde, whose son eats memories the way fire eats rope. Which is it?"

His jaw tightened. "The rebinding requires a keeper willing to remain," he said, and stopped himself, and I saw the sentence die in his mouth, because the rest of it was: *and I am not willing, and neither are you, and that is the whole problem.*

"There it is," I said softly. "You've read them too."

"I've read enough."

"No," I said. "You've read enough to want them and not enough to use them. That's worse than ignorance, Corvane. That's appetite without comprehension. You're a man holding a lit fuse because it's warm."

He laughed then—a short, ugly bark—and reached out and laid two fingers on Wren's shoulder, and she flinched, and something in my chest went very quiet and very clear, the way it goes before an extraction. Cold clarity. That was the phrase the old keepers used. When the gift opened fully and the trembling stopped and you could see the shape of a memory entire, its edges, its weight, its seams—cold clarity. I had felt it over dying women and grieving clients and stolen summers. I had never once felt it as a weapon.

I felt it now.

* * *

Because here is what Corvane did not know, could not know, had never bothered to learn, though he had signed off on my training himself: the gift runs both directions. Extraction is only half the craft. The other half is return—the laying of a memory back into a mind, gently, seam by seam, so the recipient cannot feel the join. I had spent eleven years returning memories to clients: the miller's guilt, the widow's comfort, the dying woman's summer given to her grieving daughter. Return was considered the humble half of the work. Bookkeeping. Nobody feared the man who gave things back.

Nobody fears the lockpick disguised as a gift.

Wren had sold me a memory once, years ago, when she was sixteen and desperate and I was the only keeper in the Court who refused to resell what children brought me. It was a small thing. A market morning. Her mother buying pears, haggling shamelessly, laughing at her own shamelessness. Wren had sold it because she needed coin for a roof, and I had taken it into my keeping and never traded it, and I had carried it for six years like a stone in the boot, a small constant ache, proof that even a bookkeeper keeps some things off the books.

I carried it still. And a returned memory, laid properly, settles into the mind like water into ground—unfelt, unnoticed, unless you know to look for it. Unless you know that inside the pear-market morning, folded flat beneath the image of her mother's laughing face, I had pressed a single object: a sliver of tension-wire, thin as a hair, hard as a vow. Not physically. Memory is not a warehouse; you cannot hide wire in it the way you hide coin in a mattress. But memory is a *language*, and languages can carry cargo. I had shaped the recollection so that its deepest fold—the place where the mind stores what it refuses to look at directly—held the exact sensory grammar of that wire: its spring, its bite, the way it sang when bent. Given time and privacy and a fingernail, Wren could draw it up out of the fold and into her hands. The Otherworld's craft had always blurred that line—what is remembered and what is real. The seals themselves were proof. The covenant's pages hummed. Why should a memory not hold a tool?

It was a gamble. It was the first truly dishonest thing I had ever done with the gift, and I found, examining myself with that cold clarity, that I felt no remorse at all. Only purpose. Corvane had spent eleven years teaching me that everything was a transaction. Very well. This was a transaction. He was selling me a girl's life; I was paying with a memory I had no right to keep anyway.

* * *

"All right," I said, and stopped circling, and came to the table. "Let's talk terms."

Corvane's smile returned. He thought he had won. I let him think it; a buyer who believes he's winning signs quickly and reads slowly.

"Three seals now," I said, laying my hand flat on my coat. "The locations of the remaining six, verified. In exchange, she walks out of this barn ahead of me, free, and I walk out behind her, and you get your seals when I'm satisfied she's clear of your reach. Not before."

"Half now."

"Half of what? Half the seals? Then you have one and a half and I have no reason to speak further." I leaned on the table, close to the hooks, close to him. "You're negotiating with a bookkeeper, Corvane. I know every trick of partial payment ever devised, because I invented a third of them. You will not drip-feed me. Full trade, simultaneous, or nothing."

"And if you simply lie about the locations?" His fingers drummed the table. "You'd love that. Six false addresses and a dead girl's ghost between us."

"If I lie, you kill her, and then you have nothing, because I will scatter the remaining seals into the harbor before your men finish tying the knots. You know I will. You trained me to destroy stock rather than let it be robbed—we did it twice, the winter of the Ash Tax, do you remember? Or has burning the covenant burned away your memory of your own lessons?" I watched his face do the arithmetic. He was good at arithmetic. It was his tragedy that he thought everyone else's soul was also a ledger. "You need me cooperative, Corvane. Not compliant—cooperative. There's a difference, and the difference is that a compliant man can be double-crossed and a cooperative man has to believe the deal serves him. So make it serve me. Give me the girl. All of her. Breathing."

He studied me for a long moment. Rain ticked on the roof. Somewhere outside, a bell rang the quarter-hour—not terce yet, but near enough to press on both of us.

"Simultaneous," he said finally. "You put the three seals on the table. I cut her bonds. She walks to the door. You give me the locations as she walks—one per step, if you like, poetic. When she's out, you write the remaining three and go."

"She doesn't walk to the door," I said. "She walks to *me*. Then we leave together, and the locations come tonight, by raven, from a distance you can't reach."

"Then I keep one seal as surety until—"

"No." And I said it the way he had taught me to say it, thirty-one months ago, when a client tried to renegotiate a completed extraction: flat, final, immovable as a posted balance. "One offer, Corvane. I'm not haggling over her. Take it or burn the offer."

And then, because the moment required closeness—because the trade had to appear to happen, had to have weight and ceremony and the smell of surrender—I stepped around the table and stood beside Wren's chair, and I took the three seals from my coat and set them down one by one on the wood where Corvane could see them gleam.

And while his eyes devoured them—while his pupils widened with the hunger I had seen a hundred times in clients' faces, the hunger that makes people stupid—I put my hand on the back of Wren's chair, and I bent as if to whisper the first location into her ear, and instead I gave her back the pear-market morning.

I laid it in gently. Seam by seam. Her mother's laugh, the smell of ripe fruit, the cobbles warm underfoot, the fold beneath the fold where the wire sang. It took perhaps four seconds. To anyone watching, I was a broken man whispering treasured secrets to save a child's life, my lips at her ear, my hand gripping her chair like a drowning man grips driftwood.

Her breath caught. I felt it catch—felt the memory land, felt her mind recognize it the way a locked room recognizes its key. Six years I had kept her childhood safe. Now it was going home armed.

I straightened. "First location," I said aloud, giving Corvane a street name in the Tanner's Quarter that was true but useless—the keeper had fled weeks ago; I'd see Corvane waste a day on it. "Second comes when she's untied."

Corvane was already reaching for the seals. His fingers hovered over them, trembling slightly—he didn't notice, but I noticed; I noticed everything; it was the whole of my gift—and then he mastered himself and drew a knife instead and sawed through Wren's wrist-bonds with two efficient strokes.

"Go," he said to her, jerking his chin at me. "To him. Walk."

She stood. Her ankles were still bound; Corvane gestured impatiently and cut them too, and she took one step, and then another, swaying, her swollen face turned toward me, and I opened my arms and she fell into them and I held her upright and whispered, barely a breath against her hair:

"*Wire. Under the pears. Left pocket of the morning.*"

She didn't nod. Trained girls don't nod. But her hand, closing on my sleeve, tightened once—*received*—and Corvane, gathering his seals into a leather case with the reverence of a man handling relics he intended to melt down, never looked up.

"The second location," he said.

"Tanners' Row, the blue door under the sign of the scales—" I began, feeding him the dead address in a steady clerk's cadence, and Wren leaned her weight against me, and I walked her toward the door, step by step, location by location, a payment in installments, exactly as negotiated.

At the threshold she stumbled. I caught her. Corvane glanced up, annoyed—"Mind her feet, Vesper, she's worth nothing limping"—and in that glance-away, that single contemptuous flicker of attention, Wren's free hand slipped into the fold of the returned memory and drew the wire up into the waking world, into her palm, into the space between her bound and bleeding fingers where no one thought to look.

Cold clarity. The whole barn went sharp as an etching.

We crossed the yard. The rain met us like a curtain. Behind us, Corvane's voice followed, already hoarse with triumph, already counting: "Tonight, Vesper! The remaining three, by raven, or I'll find your safehouses faster than you can empty them!"

Let him count. Let him gloat over three seals that would sing their betrayal the moment he tried to use them—pages pulled from a fire remember the fire; they remember who fed them to it. Let him spend tomorrow chasing a blue door that hadn't answered its bell in a month.

And let him learn, slowly, in the hours ahead, what I had learned in the seconds it took to give a girl back her mother's laugh: that a bookkeeper's gift, turned at last to its true purpose, is not a weapon that cuts.

It is a weapon that *returns*—and there is nothing in any world, ours or the Otherworld's, that can defend against a thing coming home.

Wren's fingers closed around the wire in the rain, and I felt her begin, very quietly, to work.
