## 12. The Third Through Fifth Seals: Roads Remembered

### VESPER

The third keeper lived in the province of Marrowmere, where the roads were paved with river-stone and the dead were said to walk them at dusk, retracing routes they had loved in life.

His name was Aldous Crane, and he was dying of a rot that had begun in his lungs and was working its way outward like frost through a windowpane. He lay in a bed in a farmhouse that smelled of tallow and turned earth, and when Wren and I came through his door—uninvited, unannounced, because we had learned by now that the seals did not wait for courtesy—he laughed until the laugh became a cough that stained his lips red.

"The ledger-keeper," he wheezed. "I know what you are. The pages told me you'd come."

He nodded toward the shelf above his hearth, where a book sat swollen with damp, its cover furred with mold. One of the rescued pages I had stolen from Corvane's fire had been missing for two days. We had searched every saddlebag twice. And here it was, wedged between a psalter and a farmer's almanac, as if it had grown there.

"They come to me," Aldous said. "Since the burning. They come to whoever is meant to keep them next." His eyes were bright with fever and something older than fever. "Cruel, isn't it? To hand a man a burden just as he's losing the strength to carry it."

"We've come to take it," I said. "If you'll give it."

"Give it." He tasted the words. "You say that so gently, bookkeeper. Like a man asking to borrow a cup of flour." He struggled upright against his pillows, and I saw how thin he had become, how the blanket pooled in the hollows where muscle used to be. "But the third seal isn't given free. Nothing in this covenant is. You read the pages. What does mine ask?"

I had read them. I had read them until the words printed themselves on the inside of my eyelids, and still the covenant's grammar resisted me—it did not state prices, it implied them, the way a wound implies the blade.

"It asks for truth," I said. "Yours or mine. It doesn't specify."

Aldous smiled with cracked lips. "Then let it be yours first."

And because I had learned, somewhere on the road between the drowned choir and here, that the covenant could smell a withheld thing the way hounds smell yesterday, I gave it.

"I stole from you," I said. "Eleven years ago. You came to the Court to sell a memory—you wanted to forget your brother, the one who drowned in the millrace while you watched from the bank. You couldn't swim. You told yourself if you'd gone in, there would have been two bodies instead of one, but the telling didn't help, so you sold the watching itself." My voice was steady. That was the horror of my gift; it made confession easy, made it *clerical*. "But I kept a copy. We all kept copies, back then—that was the corruption Corvane built into us, that nothing given was ever fully surrendered. I held your worst hour in my ledgers for eleven years, and I profited from it twice: once when you paid me, and once when I sold the echo of your grief to a playwright who needed material for a tragedy."

Wren went very still beside me. She had not known this. None of them had known how deep the rot went, how much of my own archive was stolen from people who believed they had been paid fairly.

Aldous looked at me for a long time. The fire ticked. Somewhere outside, a dog began to bay at the coming dusk, and I thought of the Otherworld pressing close beneath the skin of things, hungry, listening.

"You're right," Aldous said at last. "That's exactly what happened. I'd forgotten the playwright." He laughed again, softly this time, without blood. "Isn't that strange? You stole the worst thing I owned, and somehow that freed up room. Afterward I could remember my brother alive instead. His laugh. The way he cheated at cards." He reached beneath his pillow and drew out something small and dark: a seal shaped like a millstone, no bigger than a coin, warm as a living thing. "Take it. But understand what you're taking, bookkeeper. The covenant doesn't want treasure. It wants truth. And the truth is that I forgave you years ago—I just never had anyone to tell."

I took the seal, and it burned going into my pocket, burned like a coal of shame, and I understood that this was its way of saying *received*.

We buried Aldous Crane three days later, in the yard of the farmhouse, under a stone Wren carved with her knife. She wrote only: *HE CHEATED AT CARDS.* He would have liked that, I think. I never got to ask him.

### WREN

The fourth seal's keeper was mad, and the maddest thing about her was that she was right about everything.

Her name was Sister Orla, and she kept a shrine at the crossroads of the province of Vell, where three highways met over an old battlefield, and she had been standing watch there for thirty years. When we found her, she was arguing with the air—and the air, I realized as we got closer, was arguing back.

"Don't look," she snapped at us before we'd even stopped walking. Her eyes were white as boiled eggs. "Don't look at it directly. It preens when it's looked at."

"What is it?" Vesper asked. He always asked first, always wanted the shape of a thing before its price. Bookkeeper to the bone.

"A fragment," Sister Orla said. "Of the old hunger. A splinter of the Otherworld that got stuck in this crossroads when the covenant was young, like a splinter in a thumb, and it has been whispering to travelers ever since. Telling them lies. Telling them they're owed something." She turned those blind white eyes on me, and though she could not see, I felt seen more thoroughly than I had ever felt in my life. "It tells the girl she was abandoned. Doesn't it? It's been telling you that since you walked into earshot."

My throat closed. Because it was true—or rather, the whisper was true-shaped, which is worse. My mother *had* left me. At a shrine door, wrapped in a coat that was too big for me, with a memory of snow tucked against my chest like a charm. The fourth seal slept inside that memory, and I carried it everywhere, and some nights I unwrapped it and looked at it and hated everyone who had ever let go of anything.

"The seal is hers," Sister Orla said. "Not the girl's—the mother's. The woman left it with the child as payment. As if a child could be *paid for*." Her mouth twisted. "The splinter has guarded it all these years. Guarded it jealously. It will not release the seal to truth, because truth is not its currency." She leaned toward us, and her voice dropped to the register of conspiracy. "It deals in lies. So lie to it. Lie beautifully. Lie with your whole heart. It cannot tell the difference between a lie and a wish, and it has been wishing for someone to come home to this crossroads for thirty years."

So I stood in the middle of the crossroads where my mother had set me down, and I addressed the empty air, and I lied.

"I forgive her," I said.

The air shivered.

"She had reasons," I said, and the reasons bloomed in my mouth like flowers growing out of a grave—reasons I invented on the spot, debts and threats and a sickness, a war, a promise, a king's men at her heels, any reason at all, every reason, and the strange thing, the terrible thing, was that as I spoke them I began to believe them. Not because they were true. Because I *wanted* them true, and wanting is a kind of architecture, and I built a mother out of excuses the way other girls build castles out of clouds.

"She loved me," I told the hungry air. "She left the seal because it was the most precious thing she had, and she gave it to the most precious thing she had, and that was me. She didn't abandon me. She *entrusted* me. And I came back. Look—I came back, after all these years, to this exact crossroads, to finish what she started. Let it go. Let it go home."

And the air—wept. There is no other word for it. A sound came off the battlefield grass like wind through a keyhole, high and grieving and relieved, and the pressure that had sat on my chest since I entered the province lifted all at once, and something small and cold dropped out of nowhere into my open palm.

The seal. Shaped like a snowflake, if a snowflake were forged in iron and had been falling for a thousand years.

Sister Orla's blind eyes had turned toward the horizon. "It sleeps now," she murmured. "First peace in thirty years. Go quickly, before it wakes and remembers it's hungry."

I went. But at the edge of the crossroads I turned back once, because I am not made of stone no matter how hard the Court tried, and I said—not to the splinter, not to the seal, but to whatever might be listening in the space between—

"And if she's alive anywhere, tell her I built her good reasons. Tell her they hold weight."

Then Vesper took my elbow and we walked, and neither of us spoke for six miles, and that was the right number of miles.

### VESPER

The fifth seal cost a life freely given, and I want to write plainly about it because plainness is all I have left, but I find my hand hesitating, and I am a man whose hands have never hesitated over a page in twenty years.

The fifth keeper was complicit. That was the covenant's cruelest joke. Of all the mortals entrusted with its seals, the fifth had sold hers to Queen Isolde's ministers years ago—had traded the keeping of it for her family's safety during the last famine, believing the seal inert, a trinket, a relic. Her name was Dame Marguerite Voss, and she was the Purge's chief magistrate for the province of Callowmoor, and she signed the orders that sent memory-wielders to the palace in wagons with covered wheels.

Wren wanted to kill her. I want to record that honestly too. Wren stood in the shadows of the magistrate's garden with a knife in her hand and every name she had ever lost written on her face, and I put my own body between her and the woman, and I said: "The seal won't pass through blood. Read the pages. It only passes through *truth*."

So we did the hardest thing either of us had done on that whole breakneck road, harder than the flooded quarter, harder than the choir: we knocked on the front door of the woman hunting us, and we asked to be arrested.

Dame Voss was smaller than her reputation. Grey, precise, tired around the eyes in the specific way of people who sign papers they don't read closely enough. She recognized me within a minute—I had balanced her household accounts once, years ago, in another life—and she recognized what I was within two, and she called for her guards, and I stood in her study and did not run.

"Before you take us," I said, "read this."

I put the covenant page on her desk. The one that named the fifth seal and its keeper. She read it standing, the way magistrates read, and I watched her face do the arithmetic of decades: the famine, the bargain, the trinket she'd locked in a drawer and forgotten, the wagons, the children in the wagons, the boy in the palace eating borrowed lives because the seal that should have bound him sat unclaimed in her desk drawer not forty feet from where she stood.

Her guards came. She waved them out.

Then Dame Marguerite Voss, chief architect of the Purge in Callowmoor, opened the drawer, took out the seal—a small brass thing shaped like a set of scales—and held it out to me without letting go.

"What happens to them?" she asked. "The ones already taken. Is there any—" Her voice failed and restarted. "Is there any returning them?"

"Some," I said. "If the covenant holds. If the vessel stops feeding. If the queen stops."

"If the queen stops." She almost laughed. "I've seen her. I've stood in that nursery. Do you know what she says? She says *every life I take is a winter my son survives*. She makes it sound like mercy. She makes *me* sound like mercy." The seal trembled in her grip. "I told myself the same. For years. That's the trick of atrocity, bookkeeper—it arrives wearing mercy's coat, and it fits so well you forget it isn't yours."

"The seal requires a price," I said. "One of three truths, the pages say: a confession, a lie, or a life freely given."

"I know what it requires." She had gone very calm, the terrible calm of a decision long deferred finally arriving. "I have known since I read your page. Confession is too cheap—I could confess and sleep tonight. And I haven't a lie left in me; the queen took them all when she took my signature." She looked at me then, straight on, and her eyes were clear. "A life freely given. Mine is the only one on offer that balances the scales. I weighed them my whole career. Let me weigh true, once."

"Wren," I said quietly. "*Wren.*"

But Wren had lowered her knife. Later, on the road, she told me why: *"Because I've watched people die holding on to their lives like ledgers that wouldn't balance. I've never once watched someone set the books down and say: this column ends here, and it ends clean. You can't steal a gift like that, Vesper. You can only witness it."*

Dame Voss gave her life at dawn, in her own garden, by her own hand, with the seal passing from her fingers to mine in the moment between—one breath a magistrate of the Purge, the next breath a keeper of the old covenant, the next breath nothing at all but a weight in the grass and a morning coming up grey over Callowmoor.

Five seals rode in my coat now, humming faintly against each other like struck bells remembering harmony. Wren carried the fourth. Two remained beyond our reach: the seventh, locked in a queen's regret; the sixth, lodged—though I had not yet told her—in the oldest theft of my own life.

We rode hard for the city. We were three days out when we met a refugee on the road, a guild archivist with burns on his hands, who grabbed my stirrup and said the words that turned my blood to well-water:

"The Court has fallen. Corvane opened the gates himself. They're calling it Ash Wednesday—and he took two seals west, toward the threshold, and the queen's hounds are burning every archive between here and the sea."

Behind us, five provinces smoldered. Ahead, the ninth part of everything waited.

I put my heels to the horse, and the road remembered us, and we ran.
