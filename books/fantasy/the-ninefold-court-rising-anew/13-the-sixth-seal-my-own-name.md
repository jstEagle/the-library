## 13. The Sixth Seal: My Own Name

The map of the seals had led us through a drowned choir and a confession in a miller's kitchen, through provinces where the roads themselves seemed to remember our footsteps and turn to watch us pass. Five seals now rode in the oilcloth packet against Vesper's ribs, and each of them was warm in a way that paper had no right to be—warm like a sleeping bird, like a hand held too long, like something alive and patient.

The sixth seal, according to the rescued pages, was kept in the city of his birth.

That was all the pages would say. *The sixth seal dwells where the first theft was made. The keeper knows the debt. The keeper has always known.*

Vesper read the passage eleven times by candlelight in a rented room above a chandler's shop, while Wren slept sitting up in the corner with her knife loose in its sheath, the way she always slept now, the way the Purge had taught her. The candle guttered. The words did not change.

*Where the first theft was made.*

He had been nine years old when he first felt the pull of another person's past. He remembered it—or he remembered remembering it—with the terrible clarity of a thing told too many times: a winter market, a juggler with frostbitten fingers, and the sudden, uninvited flood of the juggler's childhood pouring into him like water into a cracked cup. The smell of another boy's mother's bread. The sound of another family's door. He had not meant to take it. He was a child, and the gift had simply opened, the way a mouth opens when it is hungry.

And he remembered what came after, because it was the one memory he had never sold, never traded, never written into the ledger under a client's name. He remembered the counting room of the Ninefold Court, and Corvane's long fingers turning his chin up toward the candlelight, and the old man saying, *Well. Here is a vessel that holds without spilling. We will make a bookkeeper of you.*

He had thought, for twenty years, that the story began there. That he had walked into the Court empty and been filled with purpose.

The pages in his hands hummed against his palms, and he understood, with the slow vertigo of a man reading his own death warrant, that the story began earlier. That the first theft was not the juggler's childhood.

The first theft was his own.

* * *

He did not wake Wren. He sat with the pages until the candle drowned in its own wax, and then he sat in the dark, and the dark sat with him.

Here was the shape of it, as the covenant's language laid it out in its old, patient grammar: when the Nine Seals were first scattered—when the original binders broke the covenant apart so that no one hand could hold it—they had not hidden the fragments in stones or wells or the hearts of mountains. They had hidden them in the one place the Otherworld could not reach uninvited. They had hidden them in mortal memory, folded into lives, sewn into the fabric of ordinary days, so that a seal was not a thing a man *carried* but a thing a man *was*.

Six keepers, six mortals, each holding a fragment without knowing it.

Five he had found in others.

The sixth had been lodged, all this time, in the first memory Vesper Winterbourne had ever extracted and kept.

His own childhood.

He could feel it now that he knew to feel for it—that faint wrongness at the center of his oldest recollections, the way you can feel a splinter only once someone tells you it's there. The winter market. The juggler. His mother's kitchen with its copper pots. His father teaching him to write his name in the condensation on a cold window. *V-e-s-p-e-r.* Evening star. The last light before dark.

All of it, he saw now, had the faintly glassy quality of a thing handled too often. A memory kept for twenty years in a man whose gift was keeping. A memory that was not a memory at all, but a vessel. A seal wearing the costume of a boy's life, and Vesper had lived inside it, or beside it, or through it, for so long that he could no longer tell where the boy ended and the binding began.

The pages told him what extraction would require. The covenant's grammar was mercifully plain on this point, as though even the old binders had flinched from dressing it up.

*The keeper who yields the sixth seal yields with it the vessel entire. What is drawn out takes with it all that grew around it. The keeper will stand empty of his own beginning. He will be a man with no first day, no mother's kitchen, no name given at a cold window. He may be given a new name. He will not be able to feel that it was ever his.*

Vesper set the pages down. His hands were steady. That was the worst part—he was a bookkeeper, and bookkeepers' hands did not shake over figures, even figures like these.

* * *

Wren woke at dawn the way she always did, all at once, knife first. She found him at the window with the packet open on the sill, the five warm seals steaming faintly in the cold morning air like five small mouths breathing.

"You didn't sleep," she said.

"I read."

"That's worse." She came and stood beside him, shoulder almost touching his, the closest distance either of them ever allowed. She was seventeen and had been hunted for a season and it had made her old in the eyes and young in everything else. "Which one is it? The sixth. Tell me where we're going."

Vesper looked at the city below the window. Smoke rising from a hundred chimneys. A bell tolling the hour in a tower he had climbed as a boy—except he had not, had he? That memory had the glassy look too now. Everything behind a certain line had the glassy look.

"We're not going anywhere," he said. "It's here. It's been here the whole time." He turned the pages toward her, and watched her read, and watched her face do what his had done—confusion, then the cold drop of understanding, then the look of someone standing at the edge of a high place.

"It's in your childhood," she said. "The first thing you ever kept."

"The only thing I ever kept." He heard his own voice go dry and ledger-flat, the voice he used for balancing entries. "Everything else I extracted, I eventually sold or returned or traded. Clients' pasts, strangers' pasts, the miller's guilt, the choir's grief. I moved them through. But this one—" He touched his own temple, lightly, as though knocking on a door. "This one I held. Twenty years. I told myself it was because a bookkeeper needs to know his own history. I think now the truth is simpler. I couldn't sell it because it was never mine to sell. It was collateral. It was a lock, and I was the door it was set in."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. Below them, the chandler's boy began hauling buckets, and the ordinary sounds of an ordinary morning went on, indifferent.

"What does it cost?" she asked, because she was Wren, and she had been trained to always ask the price first.

"You know what it costs."

"Say it anyway. Out loud. So it's real."

He said it. "My name. My history. Everything before the Court. If I take the seal out, I take out everything that grew around it. I'd still be able to walk and talk and keep a ledger. I'd still know the work. But I wouldn't know why I was the man who did the work. I'd be—" he searched for the word and hated every candidate— "unauthored."

"Vesper—"

"Don't." He said it gently. "Don't say my name right now. It sounds different when you say it, and I can't—" He stopped, and started again. "I need to think about this like a figure in a column. Not like a name."

"That," Wren said, "is the saddest thing you've ever said, and you once told me grief was an accounting error."

* * *

They spent the day in the city, because the pages required it—required him to walk the streets where the seal had been sewn into him, to stand in the places the fragment had anchored itself, the way a splinter anchors in flesh. It was a strange pilgrimage: a man visiting the shrines of his own life to determine whether he was willing to burn them down.

The winter market was still there, smaller than memory made it, or perhaps memory had always made it larger because memory was not memory at all but architecture. The juggler's corner stood empty. A fishmonger shouted prices. Children ran between the stalls, and Vesper watched them with the peculiar grief of a man watching a species he might soon stop belonging to.

"Tell me something true," Wren said, walking beside him. "Not from the ledgers. Not from the covenant. Something true from before."

He reached for his mother's kitchen. Copper pots. Bread on the sill to cool. The smell of—

He reached, and the memory came, and it was beautiful, and it was glassy, and for the first time in twenty years he watched it with a keeper's eye instead of a son's, and he saw the seams. The way the light fell slightly wrong, the way his mother's face turned always at the same angle, the way the whole scene resolved like a page written by a very skilled forger. Not false. But *made*. A seal does not simply hide in a memory; it *becomes* the memory, builds a life around itself like a pearl around a grain of sand, and everything tender in that kitchen—the bread, the light, the love—had grown around the fragment like pearl around grit.

"Here's what's true," he said finally. "I don't know anymore which parts of my childhood are mine and which parts are the binding wearing my childhood as a coat. That's what the pages don't say. They say I'd lose my name and my history. They don't say that maybe I lost the right to call it mine the day the seal was put in. Maybe I've been a stranger to my own first twenty years all along, and extraction would only make it honest."

Wren stopped walking. In the middle of the market, in the noise and the fish-smell and the cold, she took his sleeve—Wren, who touched no one—and said, "Listen to me. I sold my childhood twice. You know that. I sold it to a broker in the Marsh Ward, and then when the grief got too loud I went back and stole the memory of selling it, so I could forget I'd sold it at all. I know what it is to stand inside a life with holes in it. And I'm telling you: a life with holes is still a life. A name with a hole in it is still a name. Don't you dare decide you were never real."

The fishmonger shouted. The children ran. Vesper stood very still, and somewhere behind the glassy surface of his oldest memories, something that was not memory at all pulsed once, patiently, like a second heart.

* * *

Night. The rented room. The candle. The pages open on the table, and the five warm seals arranged in a row beside them, waiting with the terrible patience of things that have waited nine hundred years and can wait nine hundred more.

Wren slept. Vesper did not.

He thought about the miller, who had wept when his drowned brother's guilt was returned to him—not because the memory was sweet, but because it was *his*, and a man would rather drown in his own past than float on someone else's. He thought about the drowned choir, singing their one unbearable memory forever, because to forget it would be to stop being themselves. He thought about the queen, whose regret he had not yet reached but whose shape he could already see coming, and he thought: *everyone in this story is being asked the same question. What will you pay to hold the world together? And the world only accepts payment in the currency of the self.*

He picked up the extraction needle. It was a small thing, bone-handled, older than the guild, older than the covenant's breaking. He had used it a thousand times on a thousand other people, and his hands had never shaken, because bookkeepers' hands do not shake.

He held it to his own temple.

And he found—he was not surprised, not really—that he could not.

Not because of fear. He turned the fear over and examined it and set it aside; fear was a figure in a column like any other. What stopped him was something else, something the pages had not accounted for and the covenant had not priced.

It was Wren's voice: *a life with holes is still a life.*

It was the shape of a girl's name he could not yet say. It was the knowledge that if he emptied himself now, tonight, in this room, he would do it as a bookkeeper balancing a ledger—and everything that had happened to him since the Court had been teaching him, slowly, at the price of blood and fire, that the ledger was never the truth. The truth was the miller's tears. The truth was the choir's song. The truth was a girl who had sold everything and still, somehow, had things left to give.

If he surrendered his name, it would be as a transaction. And transactions, he understood at last, were how the world had gotten into this ruin. Corvane traded. The queen bargained. The guild sold its own. Every atrocity in this war was a transaction wearing a justification.

The rebinding, when it came, could not be a transaction. It had to be a gift. And a gift given by a man who no longer knew his own name was not a gift at all—it was a stranger's coin, spent from an empty purse.

*Not yet,* he told the seal, pressing his palm flat against his temple, against the glassy beautiful forged kitchen of his childhood, against the second heart beating there. *Not as a payment. If you must have me, you will have me whole, and you will have me willing, and I am not yet either of those things.*

The pulse beneath his palm slowed. Not in anger. In patience. It had waited nine hundred years inside a boy's winter, inside a mother's kitchen, inside a name written on a cold window. It could wait a little longer.

Vesper set down the needle. He blew out the candle.

In the dark, Wren's voice came softly from the corner, and he realized she had been awake the whole time, the way she was always awake, the way the hunted are always awake.

"You didn't do it."

"No."

"Are you going to?"

"I don't know," he said, and it was the truest entry he had ever made. "But if I do, it won't be tonight, and it won't be because the pages told me to."

She was quiet a moment. Then: "There's smoke on the north road. I saw it at dusk. More than cookfires."

Vesper went to the window. Against the stars, the northern sky held a dull orange stain, and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, came the sound he had learned to dread in another city, in another life: the belling of hounds.

They had run out of provinces. The Purge had found the road to the city of his birth—and somewhere behind that smoke, moving fast, was the man who had made him a bookkeeper, carrying two stolen seals and a grudge nine hundred years in the making.

"Wren," Vesper said, already reaching for the packet. "We're leaving. Now."
