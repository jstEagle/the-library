## 14. Ash Wednesday in the Guildhall

### VESPER

They came back for the ledgers.

That was the thought that kept circling Vesper's skull as they ran through the north gate with the hounds' music swelling behind them and ash already sifting down like grey snow: of all the things the Ninefold Court had ever taught him, the first lesson had not been extraction or binding or the careful arithmetic of other people's pasts. The first lesson had been *the ledger is the Court*. Burn the ledger and you burn nothing; carry it out, and the Court survives anywhere—a cellar, a cart, a grave. Corvane had drilled it into every apprentice with a switch and a sermon: *Paper outlives flesh. Be the paper.*

Now the paper was burning, and the flesh was staying behind to argue with the fire.

The guildhall rose against the smoke-stained sky like a black tooth. Vesper had known it his whole adult life—the counting room where he'd balanced ten thousand entries by candlelight, the archive stairs spiraling up nine floors into the dark, each landing guarded by a door with a lock only its keeper knew. He had spent years believing those stairs led to accounting. He had spent three provinces learning they led to the hinges of the world.

"They're already inside," Wren said, breath ragged. She pointed with her chin. At the great doors, which had never once in living memory been forced, soldiers were prying with iron bars, and the wood was giving—not to the bars but from within, because someone inside had unbarred them.

"Corvane," Vesper said. The name tasted like swallowing a coin.

"He opened the doors himself?" Wren stared. "Why would he—"

"Because he wants them to find exactly what he leaves them." Vesper looked up at the ninth floor, where a single window still glowed candle-yellow against the fire-light spreading below. Someone was still up there. Someone was still working by lamplight as though it were an ordinary Wednesday and the world had not come to collect its debt. "Maren."

He was running before he finished saying it.

* * *

### MAREN

Maren Ostrow had kept the ninth floor of the archive for forty-one years, and she intended to keep it until the end of this one, however long the crown chose to make it.

She worked with her sleeves pinned back, the way she always did, feeding pages into the iron strongbox one bundle at a time while the stairwell below filled with shouting and the sweet-rot smell of burning vellum. Her hands did not shake. Forty-one years will cure a person of shaking. She had survived three purges before this one—in her girlhood they had called them corrections, in her mother's day cleansings—and she had learned the only law that mattered: *what is written down cannot be wholly killed.* A memory burned in a skull is gone forever. A memory written down can be carried, hidden, buried in lead and dug up again by hands not yet born.

"Keeper Ostrow." A boy stood in the doorway of the ninth landing—Tally, sixteen, a counter from the fourth floor, soot on his cheek and terror in his eyes. "You have to come. They're on the sixth stair. Master Quillon says—Master Quillon is dead, they shot him on the fifth landing, and the others are falling back to—"

"I heard." She did not look up. "Take this box. Both handles. Go down the east chute to the cistern tunnel and do not open it until you reach the tanner's yard. There's a man there with a white beard who owes the Court a debt. Say my name and he'll take you wherever you need to go."

"But you—"

"Tally." Now she looked up, and gave him the face she used when apprentices wept over botched extractions: calm as a closed book. "I am seventy-three years old. I have read everything in this archive, and most of it twice. Do you understand what that makes me?"

He swallowed. "The richest woman in the world?"

A smile flickered under the soot. "The least afraid. Go."

He went. She heard the chute clatter, heard the box thump away into the dark, and turned back to the last shelf—the oldest shelf, the one Vesper Winterbourne had once stood before with wide eyes and asked what it held, and she had told him *taxes*, because some truths are given only when they're asked for properly. The covenant pages. What remained of them. Three rescued from Corvane's fire, and beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth, the copies Maren had made in secret across thirty years, because she had never trusted Corvane Ashgrave further than she could throw his inkwell.

She wrapped the bundle against her chest and bound it with the cord she'd prepared months ago, since the night the proclamations went up. Then she took the lamp from its hook, walked to the head of the stairs, and poured the oil down the top twelve steps.

Below her, boots on stone. Many boots. And above the boots, a voice she knew, smooth as a ledger line:

"Maren. Step aside and live. The Queen has use for archivists."

Corvane. Coming *up*. With them. Walking through his own house at the head of the wolves.

"The Queen," Maren called down, striking flint to steel, "has use for very few people these days, from what I hear. Mostly she uses them up." The flint caught. "As do you."

"You were the best of us."

"No," she said. "I was the most faithful. It isn't the same thing, and I'm sorry it took me sixty years to learn the difference." She dropped the lamp.

Fire ran down the stairs like a living thing let off its chain, and Maren Ostrow turned her back on it and walked, unhurried, toward the window at the end of the hall, holding to her chest the whole remembered history of two worlds.

* * *

### WREN

Wren came through the cistern tunnel coughing, Tally staggering beside her with the strongbox biting grooves into both their palms, and burst out into the tanner's yard just as the ninth floor of the guildhall bloomed orange against the night.

She stopped. Everyone stops, the first time they see a place they love begin to die. She had done it in her own village at eleven years old, watching her childhood sold twice over go up in a magistrate's brazier, and she did it now, twenty-two and harder and no wiser, watching the archive windows crack and glow.

"She's still in there," Tally whispered. "She told me to go. She—she just told me to go."

"I know." Wren set the box down. Her arms were shaking now that it was safe to shake. "She wrote the exit orders herself. All of them. Every route out of this building tonight has her handwriting on it somewhere." She looked at the boy—at his soot-black face, his bleeding palms, the way he was staring at the fire like it might apologize. "Which means she planned this. Which means she decided what it cost before we ever arrived, and she decided it was worth it. Our job is to make sure she was right."

"How? How is any of this—" His voice cracked. "There were two hundred of us."

"There were two hundred of us," Wren agreed, "and half of them voted in the guildhall to hand the rest over, and the ones who didn't are carrying paper out of a burning building right now, which means the ones who didn't are winning." She hefted the strongbox onto her shoulder. "Vesper says the ledgers aren't records. He says they're chains. If they're chains, then every page we save is a link the Queen doesn't get to melt down. Come on. The tanner's man is waiting, and we've got six more boxes coming down that chute if anyone's left to send them."

Behind them, part of the roof fell inward with a sound like a giant clearing its throat, and sparks climbed into the smoke and hung there, drifting, almost gentle, like the Court letting go of itself one ember at a time.

* * *

### QUILLON'S COMPANY — THE FIFTH LANDING

Sera Quillon had been dead for perhaps four minutes when her sister-knives reached her body.

They found her where she'd fallen, across the width of the fifth landing, arranged by chance or stubbornness so that no soldier could pass without stepping over her. Two of the Queen's men lay with her. A third sat against the wall with a knife in his thigh, humming through his teeth, waiting to bleed or be helped—he hadn't decided.

Brannoc, who had trained beside Sera for nineteen years, knelt and closed her eyes with two fingers, the old gesture, *your ledger closes, your story opens.* Around them the stairwell thundered with fire from below and boots from above and the strange, ringing quiet between.

"Hold the seventh," Brannoc said. "One more hour. Maren needs one more hour, and then everyone goes down the chute."

"And after the hour?" asked Odile, the youngest of them, seventeen, gifted with the finding of lost things, currently holding a stolen sword much too big for her.

"After the hour," Brannoc said, "we are the hour." He looked around at them—the six who had stayed when the vote split the Court in two, who had chosen sisters over survival. None of them would say the word *die*. Gifted folk learned early that some words, spoken aloud, become bargains.

They climbed. On the seventh landing they built their wall of overturned shelves and waited for the soldiers, and Brannoc thought, absurdly, of the counting room where Vesper Winterbourne used to work—how the boy had trusted paper more than people, how they'd all teased him for it—and understood suddenly, with a clarity the smoke gave him like a gift, that Vesper had been right all along. Paper was the only promise a frightened world could keep.

He put his back against the shelf and kept the promise.

* * *

### CORVANE

Corvane Ashgrave walked out of his burning guildhall through the front doors, past the soldiers who parted for him like water around a keel, and did not permit himself to look up at the flames.

Two seals rode against his heart in a lead-lined case: the miller's forgiveness, taken from its keeper's cold hand three nights ago; the drowned choir's song, bought from a harbor priest who had never known what he held. Two of nine. Enough, with the Queen's hounds and the Queen's son, to bargain from strength. Not enough to rule. That would come later, when the vessel opened its eyes fully and the starving heart of the Otherworld learned whose hand fed it.

Behind him the Court burned—the halls where he had been apprenticed, the chair he had occupied for thirty years, the archives he had ordered emptied and then, when the order came too late, ignited. Let it burn. A building is only the shape a habit makes. He had been the Ninefold Court's spine long enough that he could walk away from the body and remain standing.

"You let them take the ledgers," said Captain Vere, falling into step beside him. Not quite an accusation. Vere had learned which questions could be asked and in what tone.

"I let them *try*," Corvane said. "Ledgers are paper. Paper is patient. What matters tonight is what the paper binds, and I hold the only loose ends that matter." Ahead, the palace rose white and blind above the city, its highest tower lit by a light that was not torchlight—that pale, hungry shimmer the courtiers pretended not to see. The prince's window. The vessel's window. Even at this distance Corvane felt it noticing him, the way a dog feels a stranger's eyes.

At the palace gates a herald met them, young, sweating despite the cold. "Lord Ashgrave. Her Majesty awaits. She asks—" the herald hesitated, glancing back at the orange stain eating the northern quarter, "—she asks whether it's done."

Corvane considered the question with the care he gave all entries.

"The Court is ended," he said. "Tell her the account is settled." And then, softer, to no one, to the fire behind him, to the dead on the stairs who had refused his offer and died for the refusal: *you were the Court, and I am sorry, and I would do it again,* which was the truest sentence he had spoken in thirty years, and the reason he would never speak it aloud.

The gates closed behind him. Above the city, the prince's window brightened.

### VESPER

Vesper watched the guildhall burn from the tanner's yard, and understood that he was watching the death of the only home he had ever trusted.

Around him, survivors gathered in knots—counters and thieves and binders, soot-faced, clutching strongboxes and bundles and each other. Tally wept without noise. Old Fenwick from the counting room sat on a stack of hides reciting inventory under his breath, names and numbers, a rosary of ordinary things. And down the chute, one after another through the long hours, came the carriers: six boxes, then seven, then a gap, then an eighth carried by a girl with burned hands who collapsed the moment she set it down and could not say whether anyone else had been behind her.

No ninth box came. No tenth. Near dawn the fire settled into a red ruin, and the chute delivered nothing but smoke, and Vesper counted what they had saved the way he had once counted everything—carefully, in columns, because numbers were honest even when nothing else was.

Eight strongboxes. Fragments of the covenant, Maren's secret copies, the guild's nine-hundred-year memory. Against that: Sera Quillon, dead on the fifth landing. Brannoc's company, unaccounted for. Maren Ostrow, last seen alive at the head of a burning staircase. And two seals gone west with the man who had raised him, traded to a queen for a throne's worth of silence.

"We should go," Wren said quietly. She stood at his shoulder, ash in her hair, looking older than her years and younger than her grief. "Soldiers will sweep the yards at first light. Vesper—we got people out. Thirty-some. We got the paper out. Whatever else happened in there, that part worked."

"Did it?" His voice came out strange to him, flattened. "Wren, I keep trying to remember the first thing Maren ever said to me. My first day. She must have said something—everyone does—but there's just—" he pressed two fingers to his temple, "—a gap. Like a page torn out. And yesterday I couldn't remember the name of the street I grew up on, and I told myself it was exhaustion, and this morning I tried to recall my mother's face and I got the kitchen she stood in and a dress and nothing above the chin."

Wren went very still. "That's not exhaustion."

"No," Vesper said. "It's the covenant reclaiming its collateral. The pages I pulled out of Corvane's fire—they're calling in debts, and I'm the one who holds them." He looked at the ruin of the guildhall, at the sun coming up grey behind the smoke, at the survivors beginning to stir and pack and look to him without knowing why. "Every hour, I'll lose something. Maybe small things first. Maybe not."

"Then we write them down," Wren said. It was not a comfort; it was a battle plan, delivered in the voice of a girl who had sold her childhood twice and learned exactly what a memory is worth. "Everything you start to lose, you tell me, and I write it, and when the covenant takes it I give it back. You trust paper more than people—fine. Be the paper."

For the first time since the roof fell, Vester laughed—or made a sound near enough to laughter that Wren accepted it. He took out the little notebook he had carried through five provinces, opened it to a blank page, and handed it to her with the pencil.

"First entry," he said. "My name is Vesper Winterbourne. I was born on Candle Lane, in the house with the blue door. Write it large. I have a feeling we're going to need it."

Above the smoking ruin of the Ninefold Court, the morning bell began to toll—once for the dead, twice for the burned, and then, unaccountably, a third time, though no hand touched the rope, and the sound that rolled out over the city was not bronze at all but something older, hungrier, and awake.
