## 15. The Stranger Who Knew Me

The first thing he forgot was the smell of the mill.

I noticed it at breakfast, three days out of Ashford, when he lifted his bowl and said, "Whose flour is this?" as if we hadn't spent an afternoon in a miller's loft while an old man wept into his hands and forgave us both. Vesper had eaten that man's bread. He had watched him take back his drowned brother's summer like a man taking back a limb. And now he looked at the porridge with polite, stranger's interest, the way you look at food in an inn where you've never slept.

"It's good," I said carefully. "The miller milled it himself."

"Which miller?"

The spoon went cold in my hand.

"Vesper." I made my voice light, the way he'd once taught me to make my voice light over a mark. "You remember Ashford. The loft. The—"

He frowned, and I watched him reach for it. I actually watched it happen, the way his eyes went somewhere behind his own face and came back empty-handed. "There was a fire," he said slowly. "A ledger burning. Pages I pulled out of the flames. After that—" He gestured at the road behind us, at the hills, at me, vaguely, like a man describing weather in a country he'd never visited. "After that it gets... smudged."

Smudged. That was the word he chose. Not gone. Smudged, as though someone had dragged a wet thumb across the ink of him.

I told myself it was exhaustion. We'd been running for weeks—the flooded quarter, the drowned choir under the harbor, the three provinces behind us with their keepers dead or mad or complicit, three more seals wrapped in oilcloth against my ribs. Nobody's mind survives that kind of road intact. I told myself that all through breakfast, and then I tested him, casual as shuffling cards.

"What did I sell you? The first time. In the counting room."

And Vesper Winterbourne, who never forgot an entry, who kept ledgers in his head more accurate than any archive, looked at me with courteous blankness and said, "Did I buy something from you?"

* * *

That night he didn't set the watch correctly.

It was a small thing. A nothing thing. But he'd taught me the watch himself, back when I was thirteen and all elbows and stolen goods, taught me the way he taught everything: precisely, without praise, as if precision were a form of kindness. *Third watch is the dangerous one,* he'd said. *Tired men see ghosts, and honest men stop looking.* So we always split the night unevenly—him longer, because he claimed he needed less sleep, which was a lie, but a lie he'd been telling so long it had grown roots—and I lay there listening to him sit by the fire and get the hours wrong. He woke me too early. He woke me twice. At some point past midnight he shook my shoulder and said, "Is it your turn?" with the earnest confusion of a child, and something in my chest cracked quietly, like ice on a pond, no sound anyone else would hear.

In the morning I asked him what nine plus four was, and what the third seal had cost us, and what color my eyes were. He got the arithmetic. He hesitated on the seal—he remembered a confession, he thought, someone dying, or maybe lying; the provinces had blurred together. He got my eyes right, but only after a pause long enough to hurt.

"Covenant," I said. "It's the covenant doing this."

We were walking the ridge road above the Vale, autumn flattening the bracken to rust, and he stopped mid-stride. I saw him assemble the thought before he spoke it, carefully, the way he handled other people's memories—by the edges, never the center.

"The pages I pulled from Corvane's fire. I read them. I bound myself to the work." His jaw tightened. "Collateral. Every binding wants collateral. I've written enough of them to know."

"You wrote them as records."

"I wrote them as records." He laughed, short and awful. "Corvane let me believe that for twenty years. Maybe I let myself." He started walking again, faster, and I had to trot to match him. "The seals are being gathered into living hands. Mine is one of them. The covenant isn't stealing from me, Wren. It's collecting on a loan I signed the day I pulled those pages out of the fire."

"So we give them back. Put them down. Let someone else carry—"

"To whom?" He didn't say it cruelly. That was the worst part. He said it the way he'd say *the sum doesn't balance*, patient with my error. "Every keeper who held one is dead, mad, or Isolde's. It's us. You know it's us."

I did know it. That was the problem with being trained by Vesper: he taught you to see the true shape of a thing, and then you carried the shape forever, even when it cut.

* * *

By the fifth day he'd lost the mill entirely, and the harbor, and half the road between.

He took it the way he took everything—neatly, without complaint, cataloguing each loss aloud at dawn like a merchant auditing spoilage. "This morning I still have the counting room," he told me, stirring the fire. "Candles. The smell of wax and dust. Corvane's voice reading entries. I have my mother's hands—I think they're my mother's; they're kneading dough on a table I don't recognize. I do not have your training. Apparently I trained you. Was I any good at it?"

"You were—" My throat closed. I tried again. "You were exact. You never touched what wasn't yours to touch. You used to say a keeper's whole virtue was restraint." I wrapped my arms around my knees. "You found me stealing bread off a guild courier when I was thirteen. You didn't turn me in. You bought the bread from me, overpaid, and then asked if I wanted to learn a trade where nobody got hurt."

He listened with his head tilted, hungry, drinking me in. That was the thing I couldn't get past, that first week: he listened to his own life like a story told by a stranger, grateful and a little embarrassed, the way you feel hearing a drunk uncle describe you at a wedding. "Go on," he'd say. "What happened after?" And I would tell him about the counting room, the candles, the first memory he ever let me hold—a woman's wedding day, worn soft as cloth, safe in my palms—and he would nod and say, "Yes. That sounds like something I would do," and the distance in it would go through me like a splinter.

Because here is what nobody warns you about loving a person: their memories of you are half of you. When Vesper looked at me, he was looking at eleven years of evidence—every lesson, every argument, every time he'd stood between me and the Court's worst judgments. Take that away and what remained was a man's bare courtesy toward a girl traveling with him for reasons he couldn't fully reconstruct. He was kind to me the way you're kind to anyone. I had gone from being known to being believed in. Belief is colder. Belief has to be renewed daily.

On the seventh morning I woke and found him sitting up already, watching the grey light come into the trees, and he said, without turning around:

"I dreamed about snow. A girl in the snow, selling her childhood for the second time. She was angry at me, but she trusted me anyway. Wren—that was you, wasn't it. Chapter four of my own life, and I only have it because I dreamed it."

"Yes," I said. "That was me."

"And I'm losing the rest." He finally turned. His face in the early light was calm, and that calm was the most frightening thing I'd ever seen on him, because Vesper frightened was Vesper fighting, and Vesper calm was Vesper accepting terms. "I can feel the edges going. Like frost creeping across a window. By the time we reach the threshold, Wren, I won't know why any of this mattered. I'll walk into that heart a stranger holding seals he can't explain."

"Then I'll explain it," I said. "Every morning. Whatever's left, I'll tell you the rest."

He studied me for a long moment. Somewhere behind his eyes the covenant was unpicking him thread by thread, and he knew it, and he still managed to smile—the old dry almost-smile, the one that used to mean *you've surprised me, well done.*

"A keeper of my story," he said. "That's fitting. I kept everyone else's."

"That's not funny."

"No," he agreed softly. "It isn't. It's the only fair trade I've ever been on the right side of."

* * *

So it became our ritual, and rituals are just grief wearing its good clothes.

Each morning, before we walked, I told him who he was. I kept it orderly, because disorder would have frightened us both—I made it a ledger, spoken aloud. *Your name is Vesper Winterbourne. You keep accounts. You were born in a house that smelled of tallow and river water; your mother baked bread on Thursdays. You joined the Ninefold Court at nineteen because you thought paper could be trusted more than people. You were wrong, but you stayed anyway, because the people inside the paper needed someone careful.* And so on, entry by entry, eleven years compressed into ten minutes of morning road.

Some days he'd interrupt with questions, sharp ones, testing my account against whatever fragments survived. Some days he'd just listen, eyes closed while he walked, and I learned to steer him around stones without breaking the telling. Once he stopped me and said, "Say the part about the lockpick again," and I realized he was memorizing—not remembering, memorizing, laying my words down like planks over water. He was building a false memory out of my true ones, and if that wasn't the family trade I don't know what was.

The losses kept coming. His mother went next, then the taste of her bread, then the fact that there had been a mother at all. Then Corvane—though I confess I grieved that loss least, and Vesper, hearing my version, said, "He sounds terrible," with such cheerful conviction that I laughed for the first time in days, and he looked pleased, and for a moment we were just two people on a road laughing at a villain, and the covenant could take whatever it liked.

But then it took the counting room.

I knew before he said anything. He stood at the edge of camp that morning with a lost expression I'd never seen on him, a man standing in a house where all the furniture has been removed overnight, and he said, "Wren. There's a room missing. Candles. I can feel the shape where it was. Tell me about the room."

So I told him about the counting room. All of it. The candle-wax and the dust, the ledgers stacked like city walls, the way he'd roll his sleeves exactly twice before touching a client's memory, the dying woman's last summer balanced against a grieving husband's coin. I told it as litany, and he received it as scripture, and somewhere on the road behind us the real room burned down to ash in his head and only my telling remained, and I understood—with a clarity that felt like falling—that I was no longer describing him to himself. I was writing him. Every word I chose became the man walking beside me. If I lied, he'd be a lie. If I left things out, they'd never have happened.

God help me, I have stolen memories. I know exactly how much power lives in the telling. And every morning I stood over a man who trusted me absolutely and rebuilt him from raw material, and every morning I had to decide, again, whether love meant the truth.

It did. It does. That's the whole of what I learned on that road. I gave him everything, including the parts that shamed him—including the part where he refused the sixth seal because it lived in his own childhood and he wasn't ready, including the part where he'd profited off a miller's grief for twenty years. I gave him his flaws back before I gave him his virtues, because a man built from only his best pages wouldn't be Vesper, and I hadn't survived this far to lose him twice.

One evening he sat across the fire from me, turning a stick in the coals, and said quietly: "Do I deserve you? The man in your stories. Does he?"

"He'd hate that question. He'd say deserving has nothing to do with belonging."

"Did he say that?"

"You're going to say it tomorrow, when I tell you. You always do."

He smiled down at the fire. "Then I look forward to meeting him."

* * *

We came down out of the hills on the ninth day, toward the grey line of the Otherworld's bleeding edge, where the sky had the wrong color in it, bruise-purple where blue should be. The seals against my ribs had begun to hum. Behind us, somewhere in the ruined country, Corvane was moving with his two stolen seals, moving fast, and ahead of us the threshold waited like an open mouth.

Vesper walked beside me with his ledger-face on, calm and precise, and I knew—we both knew—that the man I'd been talking to all week was mostly my making now. The covenant had taken nearly everything original. What remained was my telling of him, walking upright, keeping pace.

At the treeline he stopped and turned to me, and for a moment his eyes cleared—not remembered, just clear, present, wholly here—and he said:

"Wren. Before we go in. Tell me the last entry. The one you always save for the end."

My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "*You have a name,*" I recited. "*It's Vesper. It means evening star. Your mother chose it because you were born at dusk, and she said the evening star is the one that stays visible longest after dark falls, and stays longest before dawn. She said it was a keeper's name.*"

He nodded slowly, absorbing it, filing it among the planks. "Good," he said. "Whatever happens in there—if the ninth seal takes everything, if I walk out of that heart knowing nothing—keep saying it. Promise me. Even if I don't know what it means. Especially then."

"I promise."

He shouldered his pack and stepped out of the trees onto the bruised ground, and I followed, and behind his eyes the frost crept on, and ahead of us the Otherworld opened like a wound full of starlight—

—and that was when we heard the bells of the city behind us begin to toll, all of them at once, wrong, discordant, a sound I'd only ever heard described in a ledger I'd hoped never to read: the sound of a half-made binding tearing, and a realm beginning to bleed.
