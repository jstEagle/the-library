## 16. The Seventh Seal: The Queen's Regret

The palace of Aldermere had a hundred doors, and I had spent three nights learning which ones lied.

Guards told the truth with their boots—the double patrol at the moonrise bell, the gap of eleven minutes when the eastern colonnade emptied for the changing of the watch. Servants told the truth with their hands—a tray carried to the nursery wing meant the prince still ate, and that was worth knowing, because everything else about Prince Edric was rumor wearing a crown's reflection. But the doors themselves lied most beautifully of all. The great oak of the throne room said *power*. The gilded arch of the ballroom said *joy*. And the small door at the end of the nursery corridor, painted the blue of a winter morning and no taller than a grown man's shoulder, said *child*—when what lived behind it, if Wren's whispers and my rescued pages were right, was the reason two realms were starving.

I went in through the laundry chutes. It is undignified work, descending into a palace through the same passage as its dirty linens, but thieves are not remembered for their dignity. Only for what they carry out.

Only—I kept correcting myself on the climb up the shaft, fingers finding mortar seams worn smooth by decades of other people's secrets—only tonight I was not carrying anything out.

Tonight I was a witness.

The distinction mattered more than it had any right to. For twenty years I had entered rooms like this one with my gift coiled in my chest like a second heartbeat, tasting the air for memories worth taking: the chamberlain's shame over his brother's debts, the captain's terror of open water, the cook's grief for a daughter lost at sea. Every room was a ledger entry waiting to be made. Every person was a page.

But the pages had taught me better. Three scraps of burning covenant, rescued from Corvane's fire, humming against my ribs even now beneath my shirt, bound in oilcloth like relics—which was exactly what they were. Nine seals, they said. Nine keepers, mortal and scattered, each one a living lock upon the Otherworld's hunger. Six seals gathered already, paid for with a miller's forgiveness, a drowned choir's lullaby, a confession, a lie, a life freely given, and—for now refused—one fragment lodged in my own stolen childhood.

And the seventh, the pages whispered in their wordless way, lay in the heart of the woman who had set the whole country hunting us.

Queen Isolde.

* * *

I found her not in the nursery but in the chapel behind it, kneeling where the queen of a nation should never have to kneel: on bare stone, before an altar stripped of candles. She had ordered them removed herself, I learned later. She could not bear light that reminded her of watching.

She heard me come in. Of course she did. Queens grow ears like foxes.

"If you've come to kill me," she said, without turning, "do it quietly. He sleeps at this hour."

Her voice was not what I expected. I had built her out of proclamations and ash—out of the nailed decrees, the hounds, the burned quarter where Wren had nearly drowned. I had given her a voice like a drawn blade. Instead it was tired. Tired the way mine was tired, the way every voice in this story had become: worn down to the grain by something too heavy to set down.

"I've come to see him," I said. "Your son."

That brought her around. She rose from the stone slowly, one hand pressed to the small of her back, and I saw her properly for the first time—not the portrait on the proclamation, stern and crowned, but a woman of perhaps forty with grey coming in early at her temples and shadows under her eyes deep enough to lose things in. A ring of keys hung at her belt. Ordinary keys. Nursery keys.

"See him," she repeated. "Everyone who sees him wants something."

"Yes," I said. "That's fair."

Something flickered across her face at that—surprise, maybe, or the ghost of amusement, the expression of a woman who had not been agreed with in years. "You're one of them. One of the gifted." Her hand moved toward the bell-pull by the chapel door and stopped halfway. "Why would you walk into the one place in Aldermere where your death is a signature away?"

"Because you can't take what I know to the pyre," I said. "And because—" I reached into my shirt and drew out the oilcloth bundle, unwrapping it just enough that the pages could feel the open air. They hummed. I saw her hear it; I saw her whole body flinch toward the sound like a plant toward sun, and then flinch away from its own flinching. "Because I think you already know what these are."

The chapel went very quiet. Somewhere far off, a guard coughed. The palace breathed around us, enormous and indifferent.

"Where did you get those?" she whispered.

"From the man who was burning them." I let the cloth fall closed again. "Corvane. Your guildmaster. Your partner in the Purge—or did he let you believe he was yours?"

I had rehearsed cruelty on the road. I had composed speeches full of accusation, armed myself with the names of the taken—the miller hanged from his own beam, the choir-singers' families questioned, Wren hunted through flooded streets by dogs bred to smell yesterday. I had planned to make her kneel in truth.

None of it came out. Because standing there in the candle-less chapel, watching her look at the bundle in my hands the way a drowning woman looks at driftwood, I found that the hatred I had carried since the first proclamation had sprung a leak somewhere along the road, and what remained was something far more uncomfortable.

Pity.

"Come," she said at last. "If you must see him, see him."

* * *

The blue door opened onto warmth.

That was the first wrongness—the warmth itself, a nursery kept hot as high summer in the dead of winter, air thick enough to drink. The second wrongness was the silence. No child's clutter, no wooden horses, no half-finished drawings pinned to walls. The room was furnished like a museum's memory of childhood: a cradle too fine to have been slept in, toys arranged rather than played with, a rocking chair with its cushion uncreased.

And in the great bed at the center, a boy of perhaps nine years old, asleep—or something like asleep.

He flickered.

I have no better word. His body was a boy's body, brown-haired, thin as winter grass. But the light in the room did not sit right on him. When he breathed, the shadows around the bed breathed a half-second late. When he turned his head in sleep, for the space between one blink and the next he was *older*—a face beneath the face, vast and hollowed and hungry as a well—and then the boy returned, and the well closed over.

My skin knew what my mind took longer to accept. I had felt that hunger before. In the pages against my ribs. In the threshold visions that had haunted me since the guildhall burned. This was the Otherworld looking out through borrowed eyes.

"He's dying," Isolde said from behind me. "They tell me he's dying. The physicians use words like *wasting* and *failure of vital humor*, and they bow very low, and none of them will say what I know."

"And what do you know?"

"That he is being eaten." She crossed to the bed and stood over her son with her hands clasped so tightly the knuckles had gone white. "From inside. By something old. And every life we feed the pyre buys him another week."

There it was. The arithmetic of atrocity, spoken plainly, in a mother's voice. I thought of the neighbor dragged out in chapter after chapter of this long nightmare—old Tam Broadhurst, who could coax a headache out of your skull with his thumbs, hanged while his neighbors watched because no one could prove the theft of memory the crown had accused him of. I thought of the archive stairs, and Sera holding them with a kitchen knife until the smoke took her. All of it purchased, week by week, with this boy's breath.

"You could stop," I said quietly. "You could stop tomorrow."

"Then he dies *tomorrow*." Her voice cracked down the middle like river ice. "Do you have children? No. Of course not. You keep ledgers." She laughed, and there was nothing of laughter in it. "You cannot understand what it is to stand at that bed every night and count. Count the hours the physicians promise. Count the lives the Purge has bought. Count how many weeks of my son's laughing I have traded for strangers whose faces I will never have to see. Fourteen months. Two hundred and six souls. I know the number. Do you understand? I *know the number*. I make myself know it, because the day I stop counting is the day I become her—" and here she gestured at some invisible portrait, some queen of storybooks, cruel and crowned, "—and I swore I would never be her."

"But you are," I said. Not cruelly. Gently, the way you tell someone a wound is infected. "You counted, and the counting became the thing itself."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she sat down in the rocking chair that had never been rocked, and put her face in her hands, and the Queen of Aldermere wept like any mother in any gutter in her city.

* * *

It was a long time before she could speak. I used the time to study the boy—to watch the flicker, the older face beneath, the hunger moving in its sleep. And as I watched, the seventh page against my ribs began to warm, gently, insistently, the way a coal warms a pocket. It knew. The seal was close. Closer than close.

When Isolde finally lifted her head, her eyes were red-rimmed and strangely calm, the calm of a woman who has decided something terrible and been relieved by the deciding.

"The night he was born," she said, "there was a storm such as the city had not seen in a century. The midwives said the thunder sounded like voices." Her hands folded in her lap, gripping each other. "He was born silent. Not dead—his heart beat—but silent, and cold, and his eyes, when they finally opened on the third day, were not a newborn's eyes. They had already seen things."

"The bargain," I said. "The original covenant. Tell me what you know of it."

"I knew nothing of covenants then. I know only what was offered." She spoke now in the flat voice of testimony, the voice of someone reciting a confession learned by heart. "A visitor came on the ninth night. Not a man. It wore a man's shape the way I wear this gown—it fit poorly, it shifted. And it looked at my son and it said: *the vessel is ours by oldest law, abandoned and empty, and we starve.* And I said, he is not empty, he is my son. And it said—" her voice caught, held, went on, "—it said, *then feed us, and we will let him keep the body a while longer.*"

The pages against my chest burned cold now instead of warm. The fifth seal-keeper, the one who had died freely giving, had told us fragments of this on the road between provinces: the covenant's first terms, the vessel promised, the realm's hunger leashed by nine locks. What no page had told us was that a human hand had signed those terms willingly.

"What did you give them?" I asked, though I already knew.

"My regret," she said. "No. Worse. I gave them the *keeping* of it. There was a moment—I will tell it truly, since you came all this way to witness—there was a moment, before the visitor came, when the physicians told me the child would not live out the month, and I prayed. I knelt where I knelt tonight and I prayed, and what I prayed was not *save my son*. What I prayed was *take this cup from me. Let it be anyone's burden but mine. Let the world pay, if the world must, only spare me the choosing*."

Her voice dropped to almost nothing.

"And something answered. And it said: *very well. We will choose for you. And you will forget that you asked.*"

The chapel-warmth of the nursery seemed suddenly glacial. I understood, then, with the sick clarity of a lock turning, what the seventh seal was. Not a treasure hidden in a vault. Not a relic guarded by knights.

A memory. The memory of the choosing—the moment Isolde had asked the world to decide for her, the moment her love had reached for any price except her own hands. The Otherworld had taken it as payment and collateral both, and it had lived in her ever since, a splinter under the skin of a kingdom, poisoning outward: the Purge, the pyres, the hounds, all of it the slow fever of one suppressed wound.

"They didn't take it from you," I said slowly. "They left it in you. Sealed. That's why you can't stop. That's why fourteen months of feeding hasn't quieted anything—the seal isn't satisfied, it's *supplicated*. Fed but never resolved. Because the debt underneath it was never acknowledged."

"Speak plainly," she said. "I am too tired for riddles."

"You have to remember it," I said. "All of it. You have to take back the choosing. That's what the seal asks. That's the seventh lock—not a key I can steal. A truth only you can unlock."

For a long moment she simply looked at her sleeping son—at the boy-face, and beneath it, in the flicker of a breath, the vast patient hunger that wore him.

"And if I remember," she said, "if I take it back—if I say aloud that I asked the world to pay for my son's life—what then? Does the bargain break?"

"No," I said honestly. "The bargain stands. Debts don't vanish because they're named. But a sealed debt festers, and a confessed debt can be *paid*—paid in kind, paid in truth, paid forward. Your son may yet live, Majesty. But not on a diet of other people's pasts. On yours."

She was silent for a long time. Outside, the changing of some distant watch rang faintly through stone. The boy breathed. The shadow beneath him breathed a half-second later.

Then Queen Isolde did the thing I will carry longest of everything I saw in that palace, longer than the flicker, longer than the weeping. She climbed onto the narrow bed beside her son—the queen of a nation, folding herself down into a child's bed, gathering his too-light body against her—and she kissed his hair, and she said:

"Show me."

I sat on the edge of the bed. I did not draw out my gift the way I had ten thousand times before, the thief's grip, the extraction. Instead I opened myself the way the drowned choir had taught me, the way the miller's forgiveness had taught me—not taking, but *witnessing*. Holding the door. Letting her walk through it herself.

And she walked.

I felt it leave her—not ripped, but rising, like floodwater lifting a boat off a riverbed that had held it for years. The memory came whole: the storm-night chapel, the young queen's knees on colder stone, the prayer, *let it be anyone's burden but mine*, and the darkness leaning close with something that was not quite mercy, saying *as you wish*—and the seal cracking open like ice at thaw, and the truth flooding home:

*I chose this. I chose this. God help me, I chose this.*

She sobbed once, hugely, a sound that would have brought guards running if I had let it travel farther than the warm air of that room. Then, slowly, the seventh page in its oilcloth against my chest went quiet—cooler, heavier, settled, a lock falling open somewhere in the deep architecture of two worlds.

Isolde held her son and shook, and outside the window the frost on the glass, I noticed, had begun—very slightly—to recede.

"It doesn't hurt anymore," she whispered, wonderingly. "Fourteen months, and it doesn't hurt."

"No," I said. "Confession rarely does. It's the sealing that hurts."

She looked at me then across her sleeping son, and there was something new in her face—not forgiveness, nothing so cheap, but recognition. One keeper of ledgers acknowledging another.

"The Purge," she said. "Tomorrow I will—"

The windows exploded.

Glass inward, a black wind, and the boy's body arched off the bed with a sound that was not a scream but a *bell*—a tolling, vast and wrong—and in the shattered frame stood a shape wearing Corvane's coat and none of Corvane's face, its smile too wide and its eyes like wells, and behind it, filling the winter sky above the palace gardens, the Otherworld poured through a wound I had not known was open.

"*Six seals,*" it said, in a voice made of every voice I had ever catalogued, "*is not enough. And now you have shown me where the seventh sleeps.*"

Isolde screamed her son's name. I grabbed the pages and ran.
