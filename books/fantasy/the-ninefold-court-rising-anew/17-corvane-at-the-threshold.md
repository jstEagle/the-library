## 17. Corvane at the Threshold

The threshold was not a door. Vesper had imagined it a hundred ways on the long road north—a gate of black iron, an arch of living stone, the mouth of some patient beast—but it was none of those things. It was a wound in the air above the drowned chapel on Graywater Hill, a place where the world simply stopped pretending to be whole. Rain fell upward through it in silver threads. The light that leaked from beyond was not light but *remembering*, a glow like the last moment before sleep, when everything you have ever lost comes back to you all at once, unasked.

Vesper stood at the edge of the chapel's ruined nave with Wren's hand locked in his, and he understood, with the terrible clarity of a man reading his own ledger's final page, that they had arrived too late.

Corvane was already there.

The guildmaster stood beneath the wound in the world on a platform of fused memory-glass—Vesper could see faces in it, hundreds of them, the extracted pasts of every gifted soul Corvane had sold to the crown to buy his passage here. The glass caught the Otherworld's glow and threw it back in colors that had no names, and Corvane himself seemed lit from within, as though he had already swallowed a piece of the other side and let it burn him hollow.

Seven seals hung around his neck on a chain of woven hair—hair Vesper recognized with a lurch as Wren's.

"Put them down," Vesper said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "Corvane. You don't know what you're holding."

"I know exactly what I'm holding." Corvane did not turn. He was watching the threshold the way a starving man watches bread. "For forty years I balanced other people's books. I kept the ledgers. I taught keepers like you to believe that extraction was service, that surrender was sacred, that the covenant demanded we give and give and receive nothing but the privilege of continuing to give." Now he turned, and his eyes were wrong—not cruel, not mad, but *empty*, scoured clean by whatever the Otherworld had shown him when he first read the pages he'd tried to burn. "I am done being a bookkeeper, Vesper. I am going to be the book."

Wren's grip tightened. "You can't bind it alone," she said. "The pages say it plain. Nine seals, nine keepers, held *in common*. That's the whole design. Balance isn't a chain with one link."

"Balance." Corvane smiled, and it was the smile Vester had trusted for twenty years across counting-room tables—the smile of a man about to close a favorable entry. "Balance is what the weak call their share of the strong. The covenant failed because it was *communal*, girl. Nine mortals, each free to walk away, each free to die, each free to be hunted by queens with dying sons. Look what your shared keeping bought us." He gestured at the city below the hill—at the smoke still rising from the guildhall quarter, at the bell towers ringing the Purge alarm, at the proclamations nailed to doors where families had been torn apart. "I offer it something better. One keeper. One will. No seams for hunger to leak through."

"You're not sealing it," Vesper said quietly. "You're feeding it. Can't you feel it? It's drinking you already."

And Corvane *could*—Vesper saw it, the flicker of doubt crossing the guildmaster's face like a shadow over a ledger line, there and corrected, there and corrected. Because to admit it would be to admit forty years of theft had been for nothing, that the man who had taught Vesper to trust paper more than people had never once read his own account honestly.

"I feel *power*," Corvane said. "Stand aside, or stand there and watch."

He raised the seals above his head.

Vesper moved without deciding to move. Later—if there was a later—he would not remember choosing; only the cold air of the nave, the glass platform slick under his boots, his hands closing on Corvane's wrist. The seals were screaming. Not metaphorically: they made a sound like a choir heard underwater, seven voices singing against what was being done to them, and the sound went through Vesper's bones and out the other side into the wound in the sky.

The threshold *noticed* them.

It was like being seen by something with no face. The upward rain stopped midair, hanging in thousands of silver beads, each one holding a reflection that was not Vesper's—a boy in a counting room, an old woman's summer, a miller weeping at a riverbank, snow falling in a memory that belonged to Wren. Everything he had ever taken, everything he had ever carried, laid out and *read*.

"Speak the binding!" Corvane roared at him. "You've read the pages—you know the words! Speak it with me and I'll let the girl live!"

"There are no words for one voice," Vesper shouted back over the rising hum. "That's the entire covenant, you fool—it only holds if it's *shared*. You're not rebinding anything. You're tearing it wider!"

And then it tore.

There was no thunder. That was the horror of it—the end of the world arriving politely, without drama, like an entry posted to the wrong column. A seam opened along the underside of the wound, thin as a knife-cut in silk, and through it came the Otherworld not as invasion but as *overflow*: a flood of unanchored memory spilling into Graywater Hill like wine from a knocked cup.

Vesper watched it take the hillside. Grass remembered being a forest and surged upward in a wave of gray trunks. A stone wall recalled two centuries of weather in a single heartbeat and crumbled to sand. And people—people in the lanes below stopped where they stood and began to *live other lives*, their faces cycling through griefs and joys that were never theirs, a fishwife suddenly weeping over a child she had lost in someone else's century, a soldier reliving a wedding from a country that did not exist.

"They're drowning," Wren breathed. "They're drowning in borrowed pasts—"

"Because he gave them nothing to hold onto." Vesper wrenched the chain from Corvane's neck. The guildmaster let him—let him, and laughed, a sound with no bottom to it.

"Do you think taking them changes anything? The binding is *already spoken*. It's already half-made, boy—I spoke my half standing on this glass while you were still climbing the hill. Seven seals given freely to a willing master. All that remains is the ninth." His empty eyes found Vesper's and held them, and for one moment the old Corvane surfaced in them, the mentor, the teacher, the man who had once told a lonely apprentice that paper was more trustworthy than people. "And the ninth seal has been inside you since the day you stole your own childhood, hasn't it? I read the sixth page too, Vesper. I've always read further ahead than you."

Below them, the city screamed in ten thousand borrowed voices.

"The eighth seal," Wren said suddenly. She was pulling at her satchel, pulling out everything they had gathered—the miller's forgiveness wrapped in oilcloth, the lullaby pressed between sheets of wax, the confession, the lie, the life freely given, every protected memory the survivors had carried across three provinces. "We pool them. We hold them together, right now, in front of the threshold—we show it what keeping is supposed to look like—"

"It won't be enough without the ninth," Vesper said. And he looked at the wound in the sky, at the memories flooding through it into the world, and he understood at last what the covenant had been asking of him from the very beginning. Not a battle. Never a battle. Only a bookkeeper, closing his books honestly, paying out the final column.

He turned to Wren. Her face was streaked with rain that fell both up and down, and behind her eyes he could see her already beginning to grieve, because she understood too—she had always been the one who understood fastest.

"Keep telling me who I was," he said.

"Vesper—" His name cracked in her mouth. "Vesper, wait—"

But the threshold was opening its ledger now, its pages the color of remembering, and Vesper Winterbourne stepped toward it with eight seals in his arms and nothing left to lose except everything he had ever been—and somewhere far below, in a city drowning in stolen pasts, a queen looked up from her son's bedside at the silver light pouring through her windows, and knew that the bill for her mercy had finally come due.

* * *

The ninth seal waited for him at the center of the wound, and it was shaped like a question.

Not fire, not darkness, not any of the torments the old stories promised. Just the question itself, vast and patient and terribly gentle: *What will you give?*

Everything, Veper thought. I give everything.

And the Otherworld, starving for so long, began—to his astonishment, to his horror, to his unbearable relief—to *eat*.
