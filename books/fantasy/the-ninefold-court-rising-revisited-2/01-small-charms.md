## 1. Small Charms

The night market of Corvale opened the way all good secrets did: quietly, and only for those who already knew where to look.

Casper Vasquez arrived an hour before dusk to set up her stall, because invisibility was not a talent you were born with. It was a discipline. It was arriving early enough that the market guards grew used to you, blending into the routine of canvas awnings and lamp oil until you became as unremarkable as the smell of fried dough. It was dressing in the particular gray-brown of a woman who had nothing worth stealing. It was smiling exactly enough to seem harmless and never enough to seem memorable.

She had been perfecting it for fifteen years, and she was very good at it.

Her stall was three planks of weathered wood on two barrels, draped with a cloth she'd dyed herself a dull, apologetic green. On it she arranged her wares in careful rows: small glass bottles stoppered with wax, each one holding no more than a mouthful of something that caught the lamplight strangely—not liquid, not smoke, but the suggestion of both. To anyone walking past, they looked like perfume samples from a failing perfumery. That was the point.

"Evening, Vasquez." Old Maro from the pickle cart next door didn't look up from his brine vats. "You're early."

"You're late," Casper said.

"I'm always late. You're always early. This is how we know the world hasn't ended." He waved his ladle at the sky, where the first stars were pricking through the smog of the tannery district. "Cold one coming."

"Every one's a cold one coming, this time of year."

"And every night you complain about it like it's news." He grinned at her, gap-toothed and easy, and Casper felt the familiar small warmth of being known just slightly—known as the memory-woman with the green cloth and the early habits, known for nothing true about her at all. That was the trick of it. People could only hurt you with what they knew, and Maro knew a woman who sold bottled forgetfulness and complained about the cold. The rest of her—the real weight of her—lived somewhere no cart vendor would ever find it.

She uncorked a bottle, checked the color, re-stoppered it. Good. The memories kept better in cold weather. Heat made them restless; she'd once lost an entire summer afternoon to a stall left too near a brazier, the memory evaporating out through the wax seal like steam off tea. She'd spent a week afterward feeling vaguely lighter and couldn't say why, which was, in its way, the whole business model.

The first customers came with the lamps.

They always did. Not the desperate ones—that was later, after midnight, when shame had had time to ferment—but the ordinary ones, the ones who wanted what everyone wanted: a little less weight.

A journeyman stonemason, still dusty, bought *the sound of his foreman's voice* for four coppers, because he'd been hearing it in his sleep. A widow bought *the last argument*, carefully decanted so she'd keep the love and lose the shouting—decanting was delicate work and Casper charged double for it, and the widow paid without blinking. Two apprentices pooled their coin for *the smell of the tannery* between them, splitting it like a bottle of wine, and walked away lighter by a smell and heavier by friendship, or so Casper liked to imagine.

This was the part of the work she let herself enjoy, if enjoyment was the word. She told herself it was honest trade. People came to her carrying things too heavy to hold and too precious to throw away, and she took the weight and gave them back their hands. What she did with what they gave her—kept some sealed for resale, released others into the gutter where they dissolved into the general damp melancholy of Corvale—was nobody's business but hers.

There were rules. She'd written them on the inside of her own skull years ago, in letters she checked nightly the way other people checked locks.

*Small things only.* Never take a whole life. Never take a name. Never take anything someone would tear a city apart to get back.

*A little pain, a little comfort.* First kisses, grudges, the smell of rain on hot stone. Grief in thimblefuls. Joy in thimblefuls too—there were people who needed to stop loving someone, and she didn't judge them for it, though she always charged them more, because it cost her more to hold.

*And never, ever be interesting.*

Fifteen years, and she had never broken a rule. She was proud of that. It was a fragile kind of pride, the pride of a mouse who has learned exactly how far from the trap to walk, but it was hers, and she polished it every night like silver.

* * *

Midnight brought the harder trade, and with it, the man who smelled of church.

He came to the stall wringing his hat in his hands like he was trying to strangle it, and Casper knew before he spoke. She always knew. There was a look people got when they wanted to forget something sacred—it was the look of someone standing at the edge of a warm house in winter, wanting in and ashamed of wanting.

"You're the memory-seller," he said. Not a question. Word traveled in Corvale the way water traveled downhill, finding every crack.

"Forgetting is my trade," Casper said, which was technically true and legally necessary. Magic itself was outlawed—had been since before she was born—and what she did was magic, thin and diluted as it was. But the Hollowers didn't come for charm-sellers. They came for the ones who took big things. Whole histories. Names. She stayed small, and so she stayed free.

"My name's Odell," the man said. "I'm a lamplighter. Third ward."

"Lamplighter Odell of the third ward," she repeated pleasantly. "What can I take off your shoulders?"

He glanced over both shoulders, which was answer enough, then leaned close. He really did smell of church—tallow and old incense, the particular staleness of buildings where people had been begging for things for centuries.

"It's my prayers," he said. "Someone's answering them."

Casper kept her face pleasant and empty, the shopkeeper's face. "That's usually considered good luck."

"Not when I asked for rain and my neighbor's well went dry." His voice dropped further. "Not when I prayed my sister would stop drinking, and she woke up three days ago not knowing she'd ever touched a bottle. Not knowing her husband either. Smiling at him like a stranger." His hands worked the hat. "I pray for small things, miss. Little mercies. And lately everything I ask for comes true, sideways, wrong. Like something's listening that doesn't understand people." He swallowed. "I want the prayers gone. All of them. Every prayer I've said since—" he counted on cracked lips, "—since the frost month. Can you do that?"

Every instinct Casper had, honed over fifteen years of staying alive by staying boring, sat up and paid attention.

Prayers weren't supposed to work. Everyone knew that. Prayers were what you did with your fear when there was nothing else to do with it, like humming in the dark. The gods were gone—all nine of them, gone so long and so thoroughly that half of Corvale thought they'd been a story and the other half thought they'd been a mistake. Whatever was listening to Lamplighter Odell, it wasn't any god she'd ever heard preached about.

But he was offering coin, and he was offering a burden, and taking burdens was what she did.

"Prayers are tricky stock," she said slowly. "They're braided through everything else. Your mother's voice teaching you the words. The feeling of your knees on chapel stone. I can take the recent ones clean, but if I go deeper I'll take the kneeling with them. You might stand in a chapel someday and feel nothing, and wonder why."

He looked at her with wet, grateful eyes, and she understood suddenly that he'd already decided. Whatever she offered, whatever she warned, he was going to hand it over. Some pains you carry so long you forget they're optional.

"The frost month forward," he whispered. "Just that much. Please."

So Casper took off her right glove—she wore gloves because skin-to-skin made the taking messy, memories sticking like burrs—and pressed two fingers to the man's temple, under cover of steadying his hat.

Taking a prayer was strange work even by her standards. Most memories came loose like ripe fruit, but prayers were knotted things, tied to hope, and hope fought. She felt the frost-month prayers come away one by one: *let the roof hold, let the cough pass, let the price of oil drop, let my sister be well.* Ordinary, humble, human. She gathered them gently, coiled them into a fresh bottle with practiced hands—

And the bottle hummed.

Not the faint restless shimmer of stored memory, which was normal, which was stock-in-trade. This was a note. A low, sustained tone, like a finger run around the rim of a glass, rising up through the cork and into her palm and along her arm until her teeth ached with it.

Odell heard it too. His eyes went wide and white-rimmed. "What—what is that? What's in there?"

"Quality merchandise," Casper said, and her voice came out steadier than her hands felt.

She stoppered it fast, wrapped it in flannel, and shoved it deep in the locked box beneath the stall, and the humming dulled to almost nothing. Almost.

"That's it?" Odell asked. "It's done? They're gone?"

"They're gone," Casper said. "You'll sleep tonight. Whatever's been listening will have to find someone else's ear."

He paid triple what she asked—he insisted—and hurried off clutching his ruined hat, lighter by twenty years of kneeling, and Casper stood in the lamplight of her little stall feeling the shape of the bottle through the floorboards like a coal through an iron stove.

Because here was the thing she hadn't told him. Here was the thing she barely admitted to herself.

When the prayers had come loose, all tangled together in her grip, something had answered. Not out loud. Not in words, exactly. It had been more like pressure against the inside of the memory, the way a bird presses against a window from the outside—a presence, vast and patient and terribly glad, pushing against the membrane between what was and what remembered.

And it had said—or sung, or pulsed, or however a thing like that speaks—one syllable of sound that landed in her chest like a key turning.

*Casper?* it had seemed to say. Except not *Casper*. Something older, smaller, softer. A name from before her name. A child's name, a name she hadn't heard since she was seven years old and the world had ended politely around her while everyone pretended nothing happened.

No one living knew that name. She'd buried it so deep she sometimes doubted she hadn't invented it.

The market noise washed over her. Maro was packing up his pickles. Somewhere a fiddle started up badly. The night went on being an ordinary night in an ordinary city, and Casper Vasquez, master of invisibility, breaker of no rules, keeper of small charms, smiled at her next customer and sold her a grudge against a landlord, all the while feeling the locked box at her feet humming its one long note.

*Come out,* the note said, in the language underneath language. *I know that name. I've waited so long.*

Casper sold memories until dawn, and she did not open the box again, and she told herself—with the fragile, polished, practiced pride of a woman who has survived fifteen years by believing her own disguises—that whatever was in the bottle, it had gotten mixed up. Mistaken her for someone else. Someone from a country that wasn't on any map anymore.

She was almost successful at believing it.

Almost was going to have to do, because across the square, three figures in gray coats had stopped at the mouth of the night market, and they weren't shopping.
