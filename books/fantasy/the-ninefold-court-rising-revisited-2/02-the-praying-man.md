## 2. The Praying Man

The trouble with selling forgetfulness, Casper had learned over eleven years in the trade, was that people always wanted to keep the box.

Not literally — though she'd had her share of clients who tried to haggle over the little glass vials themselves, as if the bottle were where the memory lived rather than just its shipping container. No, what they wanted was to hold on to the *shape* of it. The idea of having once been hurt. They wanted to be able to say, at dinner parties or funerals or three in the morning when sleep wouldn't come, *I used to grieve so badly I paid a thief to take it from me*, and to feel, in saying it, that they were still the sort of person who could grieve that badly.

They never understood that grief you can talk about is grief you kept.

Casper kept this observation to herself, mostly, because explaining it never made anyone happier and occasionally made them cry into their tea, and crying clients didn't tip.

So on the third night of the week, in her corner of Corvale's night market — wedged between a woman who sold luck by the pinch and a man whose pickled eels were rumored to cure cowardice (they did not; they cured appetite) — Casper arranged her wares with the particular care of someone who had long ago stopped expecting to be understood and settled instead for being useful.

Her stall was a folding table draped in indigo cloth gone soft at the edges. On it: rows of small glass vials, each stoppered with wax the color of its contents. Pale gold for warm things. Grey-blue for sad ones. A single deep red vial she'd priced high out of spite, because the memory inside it belonged to a man who had cheated her on a delivery two years ago and she felt no obligation to make his heartbreak affordable.

"Charms for forgetting," she said to the woman who drifted past sniffing candles. "Small ones only. A bad winter. An embarrassing wedding toast. The smell of a hospital corridor."

"The smell of rain," the candle-woman said, not really asking. She'd been by twice before. "You still have it?"

Casper lifted a vial the color of wet slate. Inside, if you knew how to look, something moved like weather. "I do. But I'll warn you again — it's not mine to sell twice. Once it's yours, it's yours."

"I know." The woman counted out coins with the solemnity of a communicant. "My mother used to stand in the doorway when it rained. I lost her voice years ago, but I think — I think if I could remember the rain properly, I might find my way back to her door."

*You won't*, Casper thought, and handed over the vial anyway, because hope was not her department. Memory was. Hope was between the client and whatever gods still took appointments.

She watched the woman walk away cradling the little bottle like an egg, and felt the familiar ache-and-release of the work. This was the part nobody warned you about, being a memory-thief: you spent your life handling other people's most tender possessions, and after a while your hands forgot how to be empty.

"Vasquez." The word came from behind her, low, in the flat tone market-folk used for customers who meant business. It was Tam Reeve, leaning against the eel cart with his collar up and his grin conspicuously absent. "Got a live one for you. Been circling your stall for an hour like a moth that's read too much philosophy."

"Where?"

"Don't look." Tam examined his nails. "Green coat, third stall down, pretending to compare eels. He asked me about you first, which means he's nervous, which means he pays well or he's trouble, and in my experience the categories overlap more than I'd like."

"What's the job?"

"He wouldn't tell me. Said—" Tam dropped his voice further, which for Tam was nearly subterranean, "—said he needs a memory taken tonight. Before dawn. And he said, and I quote because it gave me a chill I'm still working off, *it has to be someone who won't listen to it.*"

Casper went still over her vials.

That was not how the trade worked. Of course you listened — you had to, to find the edges of a memory, to cut clean. Taking a memory blind was like defusing a bomb by mail. Every thief she knew listened first. It was rule one, rule two, and roughly half of rule three.

"And you told him," she said slowly, "that listening is the whole job."

"I told him you'd say that." Tam pushed off from the cart. "He said fine. He said *she'll understand when she hears it.* Which, again, chill. Still working it off." He glanced at her sidelong, and beneath the market-hoodlum polish there was real worry, the old kind, the kind Tam carried for people the way other men carried knives. "You don't have to take it, Wren."

*Wren.* Not Vasquez. Tam was one of perhaps four living people who used her true name, and every time he did it, it landed like a hand on the shoulder — grounding, unwelcome, necessary.

"I know," she said. "But let's hear him out. Nervous men with secrets pay double."

"They also," Tam said, "die interestingly."

"That's why we charge double."

* * *

His name was Odell Brann, and he was a lamplighter by trade, forty-ish, with the kind of face that made you want to check whether he'd eaten recently. He sat across from her stall on an upturned crate with his hat wrung in both hands like laundry, and he did not compare eels, and he did not haggle, and when he finally spoke, his voice came out sanded down to almost nothing.

"It started in the spring," he said. "I pray. I've always prayed. My wife taught me how — she died four years back, and I kept doing it because stopping felt like a second funeral. Just little prayers. For the lamps to light easy. For bread to rise. Nothing grand. You don't ask grand things of gods you're not sure are home."

Casper nodded along, professional, patient, already reaching with her senses toward the shape of what he carried. Every person's head had a texture. Most were like attics — cluttered, dusty, full of furniture nobody needed. Odell Brann's attic had something in it that hummed.

"You'll want the whole story," he said.

"I'll want the edges," she corrected gently. "The middle's your business."

"No." He looked up at her then, and his eyes were the eyes of a man who has been awake too long in a house that creaks. "No, miss, I think you'll want the whole story, because here's the whole story: three weeks ago, something started answering."

The market noise seemed to step back politely, the way crowds do around a fall.

"Answering how?" Casper asked, and hated that her voice had gone quiet, hushed, as if she were the one in a sickroom.

"Not words. Not at first. At first it was — my prayers would come back *warm.* Like letters returned with someone else's handwriting added at the bottom. I'd pray for the lamps to light easy, and they'd light easy, and then they'd light *too* easy, all down the street at once, in order, like someone walking ahead of me lighting them. I'd pray for bread and the loaf would come out perfect and there'd be — I don't know how to say it — there'd be *enough.* More than enough. Enough for neighbors. Enough for strangers." His hands twisted the hat. "And then last week it started using words."

"What words?"

"One word." He swallowed. "It says my name. But not — not *Odell.* It says it like it's reading it off a list it doesn't trust. Like it's checking whether I'm the right one. And when I don't answer right — because I don't know what the right answer *is* — it goes quiet, and the quiet has a shape, miss. The quiet has a shape like a door."

Casper's hands, resting on the edge of her table, had begun very slightly to tremble. She noticed this the way you notice a tooth going bad: distantly at first, then all at once.

"Take it," Odell said. "Please. Whatever it is, whatever it wants, it wants it through *me,* and I'm just a lamplighter, I'm nobody's door, I'm nobody's anything, I light lamps and I miss my wife and I want to go back to praying into a silence that stays polite." He leaned forward, and up close she could see he hadn't slept properly in weeks; the tiredness had gone past grey into something like translucence. "I heard about you from a man whose grudge you took. He said you were careful. He said you only steal small things. Please be small with this. Please make it small."

Here was the moment — and she would think about this moment later, in the bad hours, turning it over like a stone — here was the moment when Casper Vasquez, who had survived fifteen years in Corvale by treating every strange thing as a pricing problem, should have named an outrageous fee and sent the lamplighter home to his humming attic.

Instead she heard herself say: "Tell me your wife's name."

"Why—"

"Because memories stick better to names. It's technique. Humor me."

"Maren," he said. "Maren Brann. She sang off-key on purpose, to make the children laugh. She said a prayer was just a letter you mailed to somebody whose address you'd forgotten."

Something cold walked down Casper's spine wearing her own footsteps.

Because that was — that was almost word for word — no. No, lots of people said wise-sounding things about prayer. It was a common shape of sentence. It meant nothing. It was coincidence, the ordinary kind, the kind that happened every day to people who weren't marked, who hadn't been hiding for fifteen years, whose childhood names hadn't been buried so deep even they sometimes fumbled for them.

"Right," she said briskly, before the cold could finish its walk. "Here's how this goes. I touch your temple, I go in, I find the prayer-memory, I lift it whole. You'll feel a gap. It'll itch for a few days, like a word on the tip of your tongue, and then it won't. You'll keep your wife. I take the answering and everything attached to it. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Odell said, with the relief of a drowning man signing anything.

"One condition." She held up a finger. "When I pull it out, you're going to want to watch. Don't. Look at the eel man. Count eels. If you watch me work, you'll try to help, and helping mid-extraction is how people end up remembering things backwards."

He counted eels. He was, she noted with distant approval, quite good at it — got to thirty-one before she was done.

* * *

Going into another person's memory felt, to Casper, like stepping into a house at night with the owner's permission. You moved carefully. You touched nothing you didn't need. You left the furniture exactly where grief had put it.

Odell's interior was warm-lit and modest, and near the center of it, exactly where he'd said, sat the prayer. She found it by its heat. Memories have temperatures, and this one was feverish — a small, worn-smooth shape like a river stone, glowing faintly gold at the core, and wrapped around it, unmistakably, warmth that did not belong to him.

Someone had been holding it.

She reached out — carefully, professionally, with the lightest possible grip —

—and the prayer opened under her fingers like an eye.

*Little thief.*

The voice was not loud. That was the worst of it. It was not thunder, not a choir, none of the instruments gods were supposed to arrive with. It was a voice like a man speaking in the next room, low and patient and terribly glad, and it said:

*There you are.*

Casper, who had been inside hundreds of heads, who had handled betrayals and deathbeds and one truly remarkable wedding night she still thought about professionally, felt every hair on her body attempt to leave.

*I know that grip,* the voice continued, warm as banked coals. *I have waited a long time to feel that grip. Fifteen years, little thief. Fifteen years of doors, and you open like no one else.*

"Who are you?" she said aloud — aloud, in the market, in her body, her lips moving while Odell counted eels and Tam watched her with rising alarm, because her eyes had gone somewhere else entirely and everyone could see it.

*You know,* the voice said, gentle, amused, unbearably intimate. *You have known since you were small enough to fit through the gap under the garden gate. Say it. Say the name you were before you chose the name you hide in.*

"I don't—"

*Say it, Wren.*

Not Vasquez. Not the market name, the safe name, the name stitched onto her stall and whispered by clients. Her *other* name. The one from before. The one from a house with a garden gate, in a town that — that—

That she could not picture.

She reached for it and found smooth blankness, the particular polished absence of a memory taken cleanly, and the horror of that was so complete and so quiet that for a moment she simply floated in it, a thief discovering a theft in her own house, committed by her own hand, so long ago she'd hidden the evidence from herself.

*Yes,* the voice said softly, and there was sorrow in it now, real sorrow, the kind that has waited so long it has become furniture. *They took it from you. Or you took it from yourself; the order hardly matters now. But I kept it. All this time, while you sold pieces of other people's rain, I kept yours. Come to the white door, little thief. Come home, and I will give you back every name they made you bury.*

"Wren." Tam's voice, sharp, close, his hand hard on her shoulder. "Wren, come back, you're scaring the eels."

She surfaced gasping, wrenched her fingers from Odell's temple, and sat down hard on her own stool like a puppet with a cut string. The vials on her table rattled. Somewhere in the row, the deep red one — the spite-priced one — had gone dark.

Odell Brann blinked at her, mild and empty-eyed and *lighter*, visibly lighter, a man from whom a great weight had been lifted by persons unknown. "Did it work?" he asked. "I feel — I feel like I've forgotten something important, but in a good way? Is that normal?"

"That's normal," Casper managed.

"Will it — will the answering stop? Will the quiet stay polite?"

"Yes," she said, and it was even true; the voice had gone with the memory, packed up and carried off in the vial she now held clenched in her fist, stoppered with wax she'd sealed with shaking hands. "Whatever it was, it's mine now. It'll bother me and not you."

"Thank you." He pressed coins into her palm — too many, far too many, she'd quote him a fair price later or die of shame — and stood, and put on his ruined hat, and paused. "Miss? One thing. While you were working, I counted thirty-one eels. And for just a second, I thought I heard singing. Off-key. On purpose." He smiled uncertainly. "Was that Maren?"

"I couldn't say," Casper said honestly.

He nodded, satisfied, and walked away into the market crowd a smaller and happier man, and Casper sat at her stall holding a vial that hummed against her palm like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing.

Tam crouched beside her, out of the sightline of customers, and his face had gone carefully blank in the way that meant it was working very hard. "What happened in there?"

"A voice talked to me."

"Voices talk to you all the time. Clients. Me. The eel man owes you money."

"This one knew my name."

Tam went very still. "Which one?"

"The first one." She turned the vial in her fingers. Inside, the stolen prayer glowed like a coal refusing to die, and written through its warmth, in a hand that was not Odell Brann's, she could almost read a message that had been waiting fifteen years for someone to carry it. "Tam, it called me *little thief.* It said it had been waiting. It said—" She stopped. Swallowed. "It said come to the white door."

"Whose white door?"

"I don't know." She looked down at the vial, and the vial, impossibly, warmly, seemed to look back. "But I think," she said, and her voice came out steady in the way of things that are steadied by terror, "I think it knows where I hid myself. And I think it's been knocking for a very long time."

Across the market, three figures in grey coats had stopped at the mouth of the lane, and they were not looking at eels.
