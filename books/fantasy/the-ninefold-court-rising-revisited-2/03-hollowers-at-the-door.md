## 3. Hollowers at the Door

Casper had a rule about Hollowers, and the rule was: don't be interesting.

It was a good rule. She had built her whole life on it, the way other people built lives on houses or marriages or honest work. Be forgettable. Be small. Sell charms that took away hangnails and awkward silences and the memory of your own wedding toast going badly, and never — *never* — take anything big enough to hum.

The three figures in grey coats were not looking at eels.

She knew them without looking directly at them, the way you know a draft in a warm room. Hollowers moved differently from everyone else in Corvale. Market people flowed; they jostled and haggled and drifted like weather. Hollowers cut. They walked through crowds the way a knife goes through bread — not fast, exactly, but with a terrible certainty about where they meant to end up.

There were three of them. That was bad. One Hollower was a rumor you could outrun. Two was an inspection you could talk your way past if your papers were clean and your hands were empty. Three meant they hadn't come to check on anyone.

They'd come for something specific.

*Don't be interesting,* Casper told herself, and began, very calmly, to pack up her stall.

This was harder than it sounded, because Casper's stall was a masterwork of deniability, and deniability takes time to disassemble. There was the tray of "perfumes" (bottled memories, diluted, harmless as milk). The jar of "candied ginger" (memories of summer, sweetened, sold to old men who missed being young more than they'd ever admit). The little locked case she kept under the counter with the actual inventory — forty-odd vials of stolen weather, first kisses, grudges, the smell of rain on hot stone — and beneath all of that, wrapped in oilcloth and tucked into a false bottom, the one vial she should have thrown into the canal weeks ago.

The one that was humming.

She could feel it through three layers of wrapping, faint as a cat purring against her boot. The white door. The praying man's memory, which was no longer his, because the moment she'd touched it, something on the other side of it had said her name — not *Casper*, but the other name, the small soft name from before, the one nobody living knew.

She picked up the vial.

The nearest Hollower turned its head.

Not quickly. Not dramatically. Just a smooth, deliberate rotation, like a weathervane finding wind, and even across thirty feet of crowded market lane, even through the smell of frying eels and the babble of a hundred bargains, Casper felt the attention land on her like a hand on the back of her neck.

*Oh,* she thought. *Oh, no.*

Because here was the thing about Hollowers that every child in Corvale learned before they learned their letters: they didn't hunt people. They hunted *weight*. A stolen memory was heavier than an honest one — it had been lifted out of one life and crammed into another, and that extra weight sang, faintly, in frequencies that hollowed men and women had been tuned to hear. Casper had spent fifteen years learning to muffle her hoard, wrapping each charm in misdirection and mundane packaging, keeping her total cargo light enough to pass as ordinary clutter.

But the door-memory wasn't hers. It had never been hers. And whatever lived behind that door had been calling her name into the dark for who knew how long, and a called thing *rings*, and a ringing thing is the loudest object in any market in the world if you happen to have ears made for listening.

The Hollower started toward her stall.

Casper did the math. Thirty feet. Four seconds at a Hollower's walk, less if it decided to run, and Hollowers ran like things that had forgotten how to be tired. Behind her: the fishmonger's awning, the drainage ditch, the low wall of the tanner's yard. Above her: the striped canvas of the awning over Madame Serrano's hat shop, and beyond that, the rooftops of Corvale's crooked middle district, which Casper knew the way other women knew their mothers' faces.

"Closing early," she announced to no one, cheerfully, sweeping her perfumes into a satchel. "Family emergency. Terribly sorry."

"You," said the Hollower.

Its voice was the worst part. Hollowers weren't demons; that would have been simpler. They were people — or they had been, once, before the Ministry of Memory emptied them out and refilled the space with procedure. What spoke through them now was polite, and flat, and patient as arithmetic.

"Vendor," said the Hollower, stopping at her stall. Up close it smelled of nothing at all, which in a market thick with eel-fat and charcoal smoke was somehow louder than any stench. "You are required to open your inventory for inspection."

"Of course." Casper smiled the smile of a woman with nothing to hide and slightly aching feet. "Though I'll warn you, it's mostly perfume and candied ginger. My aunt makes it." Her aunt, as far as Casper knew, did not exist. If she did exist, Casper apologized to her silently. "Very popular with the older ladies."

The Hollower looked at her. And then — this was the moment, the hinge of the whole evening, though she wouldn't understand it until much later — the Hollower looked slightly *past* her, at the satchel at her hip, and its blank face did something she had never seen a blank face do.

It hesitated.

"You have been marked," it said.

"I'm sorry?"

"A resonance has been logged against this location." The Hollower's head tilted, a degree, two degrees, like a bird examining a beetle. "Awakened magic. Class four." Another tilt. "No. Class five."

"There's been some mistake—"

"The resonance is coming from *you*."

And here is what Casper Vasquez did not do, though every instinct screamed it: she did not run. Running was confirmation. Running was interesting. Instead she laughed — a short, baffled, market-woman laugh — and said, "From *me*? Friend, I sell ginger. I can barely afford the stall. Whatever your instruments heard, it's probably the eels. Eels do strange things near the full moon, everyone knows that."

It was, objectively, a magnificent performance. She would be proud of it later, in the way you're proud of a beautiful letter that arrives too late.

Because while the Hollower was tilting its head at her, deciding whether a woman who sold ginger could plausibly be a class-five resonance, the vial in her satchel — the white door, the knocking thing, the memory with her childhood name folded inside it like a letter in an envelope — grew warm.

Then warmer.

Then, unmistakably, it *answered*. A sound came out of the satchel, thin and silver, like a bell rung underwater, and every Hollower in the lane turned at once, three grey faces swinging toward her with the smooth synchronized hunger of compass needles finding north.

"Well," said Casper. "That's my cue."

She grabbed the edge of Madame Serrano's awning and swung herself up onto the hat-shop roof with the ease of fifteen years' practice, and behind her the market erupted — shrieking customers, toppling stalls, the first Hollower moving with a speed that no longer bothered pretending to be human.

* * *

Corvale's rooftops at night were a second city, and Casper was a citizen of it.

She ran the ridgelines the way water runs a gutter — automatically, joyfully almost, despite everything, because there was a part of her that had always loved this, loved the tiles under her boots and the chimney-smoke braiding the air and the whole glowing sprawl of the night market spread out below like spilled jewelry. The middle district's roofs were uneven, gossipy things, leaning together in places as if sharing secrets, and Casper used those leanings, hopping gaps she'd mapped years ago, dropping into the alley between the cooper's and the candlemaker's where a stack of barrels made a staircase only she and the rats ever used.

The Hollowers followed.

Of course they followed. But they followed like men reading a map, and Corvale's roofs were not a map, they were an argument — a chaotic, centuries-long dispute between builders, patched and sagging and full of traps for anyone who hadn't grown up memorizing them. Casper heard the first one hit the tiles behind her, heavy and wrong, heard slate crack under a weight that shouldn't have been so heavy, and she grinned, breathless, wild, alive in the stupid animal way she only ever felt when running.

"Come on, then!" she shouted back, because she was an idiot, because terror and exhilaration are twins who share a wardrobe. "Mind the third chimney! It bites!"

She went left along the dyer's ridge, right down the rope of a laundry line (apologies to whoever owned the sheets), vaulted the gap above Tanner's Walk that had killed a rival thief named Oskar when she was nineteen — she had stolen the memory of Oskar's fall once, briefly, just to know what it felt like, and had given it back within the hour, shaken. She knew these roofs. These roofs, in a sense, knew her.

Which was why she couldn't understand, until she was nearly at the edge of the middle district, looking down at the canal road and freedom, why they were still behind her.

Three of them. Paced evenly. Not gaining, not falling behind. Keeping pace.

Keeping *her* pace.

Cold slid down her spine and settled in her stomach like a swallowed stone. Hollowers chased. They did not pace. Chasing was effort, and effort implied doubt, and Hollowers did not doubt — they simply arrived, sooner or later, at whatever the Ministry had aimed them at. Unless.

Unless they didn't need to catch her tonight.

Unless tonight was not the point.

Casper dropped off the roof's edge onto the canal towpath, rolled, came up running, and ducked under the arch of the Weeper's Bridge, into the deep shadow where the bridge's stone met the water. She pressed herself flat against the cold granite, heart slamming, and listened.

Footsteps on the towpath. Unhurried. They stopped directly overhead.

"She is marked," said one voice, flat as ledger paper. "Resonance class five. Confirmed."

"The vessel responds to the summons. Retrieval is not required. Observe and report."

"The Ministry will want—"

"The Ministry wants what it has always wanted." A pause. Somewhere in that pause, Casper got the distinct, skin-crawling impression that the speaker was smiling, though she'd have sworn on her own grave that Hollowers couldn't. "Let the door knock. Doors are easier to follow than thieves."

Then the footsteps moved on, unhurried, and did not come back.

* * *

She stayed under the bridge until her legs stopped shaking, which took longer than she cared to admit.

The canal breathed its slow green breath around the pilings. Somewhere a barge coughed and settled. Casper sat with her back against wet stone and the satchel in her lap, and took out the vial.

Up close, in the dark, she could see it properly for the first time: the memory inside wasn't sitting still the way memories did. Memories, bottled well, were like pond water — still, settled, clear. This one moved. Silver light coiling and uncoiling, pressing against the glass from the inside, and every few seconds it pulsed, gently, patiently, the way knuckles meet wood.

*Knock. Knock. Knock.*

"You're going to get me killed," she whispered to it.

The pulsing did not stop, but it changed — softened, somehow, apologetic, the rhythm of someone who understands they're causing trouble and can't help it.

"And don't give me that look. You don't even have eyes. I checked."

She should throw it in the canal. She knew that. Any sensible thief would. It was hot cargo, marked cargo, the single most dangerous object in her possession since — since —

Since fifteen years ago. Since the night she didn't think about. Since the job she'd taken as a girl with quick hands and no questions, the one that paid enough to vanish on, the one whose details she had — very deliberately, very carefully, at enormous cost — arranged never to remember. She remembered taking the job. She remembered finishing it. The middle was gone, excised by her own hand, the one theft she'd committed against herself.

The vial pulsed. *Knock.*

"You knew him," she said quietly. It wasn't really a question, and it wasn't really to the vial. It was to the field in the memory, the empty field she'd glimpsed when she touched it, the white door standing alone under a sky that was the wrong color, a sky with too many stars arranged in patterns no astronomer of Corvale had ever charted. "Whoever's knocking. You knew him, and he knew me, and I took that away too, didn't I? Along with everything else."

The light in the vial flared — not a pulse this time but a bloom, brief and warm, like a lamp lit in a window of a house you'd thought long empty.

Casper looked at it for a long moment. Then, because she was exhausted and frightened and the canal offered no counsel, she did the bravest and stupidest thing available to her.

She pulled the stopper.

Not all the way. A crack. Enough.

The field rose around her like fog off morning water — grass to her knees, wind she could feel but not hear, that vast wrong-colored sky — and the door stood in front of her, white and plain and patient, and this time she noticed what she hadn't noticed before: footprints in the grass. Hundreds of them. Thousands. All leading *to* the door, none leading away, as if everyone who had ever reached it had stepped through and never needed to come back.

And on the door, at the height of a child's reaching hand, someone had carved letters. Old letters. Letters in an alphabet that predated Corvale, that predated the maps, that predated — she understood suddenly, with a vertigo that had nothing to do with rooftops — predated the country itself.

She couldn't read them. But her mouth, entirely without her permission, shaped the sounds.

"*Vessin,*" she whispered, and the word fit her tongue like a key fits a lock it was cut for.

Under the bridge, in the dark, in the city that had outlawed every god and forgotten every country, the vial in Casper Vasquez's hands went quiet at last — not silent, but *settled*, the way a held breath settles when it's finally, finally answered.

Somewhere far away, on the other side of a white door in an empty field, something that had waited fifteen years heard its own name spoken in a thief's voice, and began — gently, gratefully, with the patience of mountains — to push.
