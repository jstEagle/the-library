## 4. The God Who Waited

Casper had hidden in a lot of places in fifteen years. Under a tanner's awning during a Hollowing sweep. Inside a memory of somebody else's wedding—wearing the groom's mother's eyes for six hours while enforcers walked past her face. Once, memorably, inside a wine barrel that turned out not to be empty.

She had never before hidden inside her own skull from something that was already in there.

She crouched behind a stack of rain barrels on the roof of the dye-works, three stories above the night market, lungs burning, the stolen memory clutched in her fist like a coal. The white door. The field. She could feel it through the glass of its little bottle, humming against her palm like a wasp trapped in a jar.

*Come out, Casper,* said the voice. It came from everywhere and nowhere and, most alarmingly, from slightly to the left of her own thoughts. *You're bleeding.*

"I'm fine," she whispered, which was true only if you didn't count the scrape along her forearm where a Hollower's hook had kissed her on the way over the gutter. "And I don't talk to voices. House rule."

*A wise rule.* The voice paused, and she could have sworn it was smiling. It had a smile the way a cathedral had a smile—large, old, and faintly smug about its architecture. *I'll wait. I'm rather good at it.*

"Great," she muttered. "Wait forever."

*I did, actually. You were there for part of it.*

The memory-bottle pulsed. Warmth crawled up her wrist, gentle as sun through a window, and Casper made the mistake of looking at it. The white door stood in an empty field in her mind's eye, patient as a held breath, and she understood—with the sick, sliding certainty of a floorboard giving way—that it had always been *her* door. That was why the voice used her childhood name. That was why the bottle hummed. That was why, when she'd stolen it off the praying man's shelf two nights ago, she hadn't felt the usual small tug of taking. It had felt like picking up something she'd set down.

"No," she said out loud, to the roof, to the city, to whatever was listening. "Nope. Whatever this is, I steal memories. Small ones. Forgettable ones. A grudge here, a bad haircut there. I do not—" she gestured at the sky with the bottle, "—do *doors*."

*You stole a god's memory fifteen years ago and hid it in your own head for a decade and a half,* said the voice mildly. *I think we can agree you do doors.*

*

Vessin had waited so long that waiting had become less a state and more a country he lived in. He knew its weather: the long grey centuries of silence after the Court fell. The seasons of almost—hearing his name half-spoken in a drunk's prayer, feeling a door-shaped absence ache in the world and then close again. He had learned patience the way drowning men learn water.

And then, fifteen years ago, in one blinding instant, everything had gone *quiet*. Not silent—quiet. The difference mattered. Silence is nothing. Quiet is something holding its breath. Eight gods' war had been roaring across the Ninefold Court like a fire through a granary, and then a thief's hands—small, quick, terrified, human—had closed around the whole of it, every prayer, every name, every stone of every temple, and tucked it away somewhere no god could reach.

Inside a girl. A market-thief with rain-smell charms and a talent for being overlooked.

He had spent those first years shouting into the dark of her. She couldn't hear him then; the theft had sealed too well. But gods are patient, and prayers leak, and lately—oh, lately—the seals had begun to thin. Something in Corvale had started answering prayers again. He suspected it was him, leaking. An embarrassing habit, but there it was.

Now she crouched on a rooftop arguing with him, which he found delightful. Most of his worshippers had either wept or groveled. This one negotiated.

*You're going to have to come down eventually,* he told her. *Your arm is still bleeding, the Hollowers have your scent now—you've been marked; surely you noticed the way their hooks bent toward you—and I make excellent tea.*

"You're a god."

*I contain multitudes. Some of them are tea.*

There was a long pause. He felt her weigh it—not whether to trust him, because she clearly didn't, but whether trusting him beat the alternatives. He respected that. Faith given freely was worth having; faith extracted was just obedience with better posture.

"If this is a trap," she said finally, "I want you to know I've escaped worse."

*The wine barrel?*

Her whole body went rigid. "*How do you know about the barrel?*"

*I live in your head, Casper. It's very cozy. You should clean it more often.*

*

The house Vessin sent her to shouldn't have existed. That was, she would later understand, the entire point of it.

It sat at the end of Cordwainer Lane, which Casper had walked a thousand times, and there had never been a house at the end of Cordwainer Lane—just a wall with ivy and a smell of cats. Now there was a narrow building with blue shutters and smoke rising from its chimney, and when Casper touched the door handle it was warm, as though someone had been expecting her hand specifically.

Inside smelled of tea and old paper and, underneath both, something vast and green that reminded her of a forest she had never visited but somehow missed.

"Sit," said a man by the hearth.

He looked ordinary, which was her first clue he wasn't. Ordinary people worked at looking interesting; this one worked at looking forgettable, and he was doing it badly on purpose—a courtesy, maybe. Brown hair going grey at the temples. A cardigan with one elbow darned in a different color wool. Hands wrapped around a teapot that steamed without ever seeming to be poured.

"You're the praying man," she said.

"I borrowed him." He shrugged apologetically. "Poor Aldous has been praying to me for eleven years about his daughter's cough, and I finally needed a mouth. I gave him back his prayer afterward. And cured the cough, incidentally. He thinks it was the new physician." He poured tea into two cups. "Milk?"

"You're a god who drinks milk."

"I'm a god who *was* a god," he corrected gently, "of a country that currently does not exist. Milk is the least of my indignities."

Casper did not sit. She stood in the doorway with her back to the street, because old habits, and looked at him properly. Up close, the ordinary kept slipping. When he blinked, the blink took a fraction too long. When the firelight moved across his face, it moved the wrong direction, like reflections seen in deep water. His shadow on the wall had nine corners. She counted twice.

"Vessin," she said, trying the name from the voice in her skull.

Something flickered across his expression—relief, hunger, grief, all arriving together like relatives at a funeral. "You remember my name."

"It came with the voice. Free of charge." She still didn't take the cup. "Start talking. What court? What country? And what do you mean, mine is the only door?"

Vessin set down the teapot and folded his hands, and for a moment he let the disguise fall entirely, and Casper's breath left her in a small involuntary sound, because what remained was not a monster or a radiance but something worse: a tired man who had once been worshipped by millions and now darned his own elbows. There was dignity in it. There was also, unmistakably, loneliness.

"The Ninefold Court," he said, "was a country east of everywhere. Past the Saltreach mountains, past the maps that stop saying *here be honest borders* and start saying *here be guesses*. Nine gods shared it—we were siblings, of a sort, though 'siblings' undersells both the love and the war. Each of us kept a door. Nine doors, nine domains: harvest and storm, grief and mirth, the tide, the forge, the dream, the law, the wild green, and me." He tapped his own chest lightly. "Doors. Thresholds. The space between one thing and the next. Somebody had to hold the hinges of the world, and it might as well be the youngest sibling."

"And your door was white."

His head came up sharply. "You've *seen* it."

"In a stolen memory. Two nights ago. A field, a white door, standing alone." She swallowed. "It hummed at me."

"It would." He rose, crossed to a cabinet, and drew out something that made Casper's skin prickle: a map. Not a printed thing—an actual map, inked on skin-pale vellum, and the landmass it showed was shaped like nothing she'd ever seen, ninefold, like a flower pressed flat. In the center, where a capital should be, someone had drawn a small white rectangle and written beside it, in a shaking hand: *gone.*

"Fifteen years ago my siblings went to war. Over worship, over borders, over pride—all the reasons gods go to war, which are the same reasons men do, with better lighting. Their war was unmaking the Court, Casper. Country and people and prayer, all of it burning as fuel. So I did the only thing left." He met her eyes. "I hid it."

"Hid—"

"Everything. The Court. The other eight. The land, the language, the prayers, fifteen million souls' worth of devotion. I folded the whole of it into a single memory and I—" his voice caught, and he steadied it with visible effort, "—I asked a thief to take it."

The room tilted. Casper gripped the doorframe.

"I was nineteen," she said. Her voice sounded far away, like it belonged to someone standing in a different room. "I don't—I don't remember any of that. I remember Corvale. I remember learning the trade. I remember—"

"Because that *is* your life. All real. All yours." Vessin's gentleness was unbearable. "But think, Casper. Think about how good you are at disappearing. How you can walk through a crowd and leave no impression at all. How memories slide off you. You thought it was skill."

"It *is* skill."

"It is also," he said quietly, "what happens to a person who is carrying an entire hidden country. The world agreed not to see you, because seeing you meant seeing what you hold. You didn't master invisibility, dear thief. You *are* invisibility. You're the hiding place. Every quiet corner of your life, every year nobody asked about, every friend who drifted away without reason—they weren't failures of your craft. They were load-bearing walls."

Casper sat down. Her legs made the decision without consulting her, which she supposed was fitting, since apparently nothing else in her life had consulted her either.

She drank the tea. It tasted like milk and honey and, distantly, like a festival she couldn't name—lanterns, drumming, someone laughing her childhood name across a courtyard that had never existed on any map she'd owned.

"That's why they're hunting me," she said. "The Hollowers. They can feel it waking up."

"They can feel *me*, leaking through the seals. And through you." Vessin knelt across from her—not looming, kneeling, which somehow made it worse. "The war isn't over, Casper. My siblings aren't dead; they're *stored*, furious, and slowly remembering they exist, the way you remember a debt. When they wake fully, they will tear the hiding place open from the inside, and everything your world has built on top of the wound will come apart. Unless—"

"Unless I give it back." Her mouth was dry. "Open the door. Un-steal it. Fifteen years of—of *everything*—poured back into the world."

"Yes."

"And what happens to me? The hiding place, when it's empty?"

Vessin was quiet for a long moment. The fire ticked. Somewhere outside, Corvale went about its evening, selling fish, cheating at cards, forgetting things in small safe ways.

"I don't know," he admitted. "No one has ever carried a country out again. You may remain yourself. You may come apart. You may become the doorway itself, which I am told is a mixed experience."

"Wonderful." Casper laughed, and it came out cracked. "Fifteen years I built a life on the principle that nobody gets hurt by what I take. Small things. Forgettable things. And it turns out I'm sitting on the largest theft in history and the whole world's been paying interest." She looked at her scraped arm, at the dried blood, at the small careful life she suddenly saw from the outside: the stall, the bottles, the clients who wanted to forget a first kiss. A hiding place furnished with such love and so little foundation.

"The oldest bottles," she said slowly. "In my inventory. The ones I've never dared open. The ones that hum."

"The Court," Vessin said. "Piece by piece. Harvest and storm. Grief and mirth. Tide and forge and dream and law and the wild green. You took them all that night, one by one, because I asked, because there was no time, because you were brave and terrified and nineteen." His voice roughened. "I have regretted asking every day since."

"Regretted it enough to tell me the truth straight away?"

Another pause. Longer. Casper watched a god decide how much truth a mortal could carry, and filed the hesitation away, because she was still a thief, and thieves notice what people hide.

"There are things you must see first," Vessin said carefully. "Memories you must open in the right order, or they'll open you instead. Start with the harvest. The green fields, the first festival. Let the Court introduce itself kindly, before—" he stopped himself.

"Before what?"

Before the war, his face said. Before the burning. Before the part where a nineteen-year-old girl ran through collapsing temples with a god's voice screaming *faster* in her ears.

"Tomorrow," he said aloud. "Sleep tonight. Real sleep. And tomorrow, we begin returning what you took." He hesitated, then added, softer: "There's a cartographer on Tanner's Row. Ilsa Vane. If anyone alive can chart a road into a country that isn't on the map, it's her. And Casper—she'll ask a price. Cartographers of impossible places always do."

Outside, the bells of Corvale rang the hour, and beneath them, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, Casper heard—or imagined she heard—nine bells answering from somewhere that had no towers.

She drained her tea, stood, and found her legs mostly willing.

"For the record," she said at the door, "when this is over, I'm retiring. Candles. Something with wicks. Nothing that hums."

"Noted," said Vessin, and smiled his cathedral smile, and for the first time in fifteen years, Casper Vasquez walked home knowing exactly what she was carrying—and dreading, with every step, the weight of setting it down.
