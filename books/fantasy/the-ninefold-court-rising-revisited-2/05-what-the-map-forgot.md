## 5. What the Map Forgot

Ilsa Vane had three rules for her shop, and she recited them to every customer the way other shopkeepers said good morning.

One: I sell maps of places that exist. Two: I sell maps of places that used to exist. Three: if you want a map of a place that will exist, you'll have to wait like everyone else, because prophecy is just cartography with bad manners.

The third rule was new. She'd invented it Tuesday, after a man in a grey coat asked her to chart the coastline of his dreams, and she'd needed something clever to say while she reached for the poker under the counter.

The morning Casper Vasquez came in, Ilsa was re-inking the coast of Vel Sarran for the eleventh time that month, which was ten times too many.

"Your eastern shore is wrong," Casper said, by way of hello. She had the particular rumpled look of someone who had slept on a rooftop, or possibly not at all. There was salt-dust in her hair and a bruise going yellow along her jaw, and she was carrying herself with the careful lightness of a woman who had recently discovered that the world could see her.

"My eastern shore," Ilsa said, "is a masterpiece."

"It's wrong. It's different from the survey charts. And it was *right* two months ago, because I checked." Casper set a folded paper on the counter — one of Ilsa's own maps, bought secondhand, dog-eared within an inch of its life. "Vel Sarran has moved inland."

Ilsa put down her pen. This was a thing she did perhaps once a week, putting down the pen, and each time it felt like setting down a small sleeping animal she wasn't sure would still be breathing when she picked it up again.

"Casper. Lovely to see you. You look terrible. Tea?"

"No tea."

"Tea makes everything survivable."

"Ilsa."

"Yes, all right." Ilsa poured two cups anyway, because rules were for customers and Casper had long since graduated into the category of *problem*, and problems got tea whether they wanted it or not. She slid one across the counter. "Now. Say the impossible thing slowly, so I can enjoy it properly before I have to ruin my whole career confirming it."

Casper wrapped both hands around the cup but didn't drink. "Corvale moved. The city. Not the buildings — the ground under them. And the borders with it. I've been walking routes I've walked for fifteen years and they don't match anymore."

"How much don't they match?"

"The Tanner's Row bridge is forty feet east of where it was."

Ilsa laughed. It came out thinner than she intended. "Bridges settle. Ground shifts. Corvale is built on river silt and the ambitions of people who never met a load-bearing wall they trusted—"

"Ilsa." Casper's voice did something quiet and terrible. It went flat. "I steal memories for a living. I know what forgetting looks like. This looks like forgetting."

The shop was silent except for the tick of the great orrery clock in the corner, which Ilsa had bought at auction because it kept time for nine cities simultaneously and only agreed on any of them twice a year.

She should have laughed it off. That was the sensible move, the move she'd made eleven times already this month when clients brought her surveys that didn't reconcile, when harbor pilots swore the lighthouse had drifted, when a farmer from the Weald insisted his village had traded places overnight with an orchard. Eleven times she'd found an error. A mislabeled datum, a drunk surveyor, a printing plate set crooked. Errors were the mortar of her trade; she'd built a respectable living on the gap between what the world was and what people remembered of it.

But Casper didn't bring her errors. Casper brought her the things too strange to sell, back when she'd been a client — the map of a country Ilsa couldn't find, the request for a chart of a place that answered to no name on any ledger. And Casper had a thief's ear for the truth, which meant she could hear it even through walls of her own making.

"Stay here," Ilsa said. "Touch nothing. Especially not the globe, it bites."

* * *

The archive room behind the shop held forty years of Ilsa Vane's work, organized with a rigor that her mother would have called obsessive and Ilsa preferred to call *defensive*. Every survey she'd ever taken or verified. Every chart, cross-referenced three ways. She'd spent her life on the principle that the world might lie, but measurements, done honestly, would eventually gang up on it and tell the truth.

She pulled the Vel Sarran coastal surveys first. Twelve years of them, seasonal, meticulous.

She laid them out across the big table in chronological order and weighted the corners with ink pots, and then she stood over them and felt the particular cold that scholars get, the one that starts at the base of the skull, where the understanding lives before the words do.

The coastline was walking inland.

Not eroding — erosion was honest work, gradual, explicable. This was something else. Year over year, the surveys showed the high-water line retreating from the sea in small, patient increments, as though the entire peninsula were being pulled gently backward by something with infinite time and very good manners. And the town with it. The harbor soundings showed the seabed shallowing. The road surveys showed the distances lengthening. Everything consistent. Everything *internally coherent*, which was the horror of it, because nature was sloppy but forgetting was thorough.

"Show me Corvale," Casper said from the doorway.

Ilsa hadn't heard her come in, which hadn't happened in twenty years, and she decided not to think about it.

She cleared the table and brought out the city series instead — the great project, the one she was quietly proud of and never mentioned at parties because it made people's eyes glaze: a complete measured survey of Corvale proper, redone every quarter for thirty years. Streets, structures, bridges, walls. The city rendered in ink at a scale of one inch to fifty feet, generation after generation after generation.

She laid out the last eight quarters.

Casper leaned over the table. For a long moment neither of them said anything. The orrery ticked.

"Forty feet," Casper said finally. Her voice was very steady, the way voices get when steadiness is being held up with both hands. "I told you. Forty feet."

"It's not just the bridge." Ilsa traced the Tanner's Row district with one finger, quarter by quarter. The street grid was the same. The buildings were the same. But the whole district had crept, quarter by quarter, like a slow animal migrating — eastward, always eastward, dragging its bridges and gutters behind it. And it wasn't alone. The Saltgate quarter had drifted south. The Weavers' Terraces had compressed by a full street-width, as if the city were a hand slowly closing around something in its palm.

"When did you last check the outer markers?" Casper asked.

Isla went cold all over again, because she knew exactly when, and she knew exactly what she'd found, and she knew exactly what she'd written in her logbook about it: *survey error, remeasure.*

The boundary stones. The old imperial markers that ringed the Corvale hinterlands, placed four hundred years ago and unmoved, supposedly, since. She'd surveyed them every spring. This spring, seven of the nine had been somewhere else entirely — not toppled, not stolen, but *elsewhere*, sitting plumb and level in fields where no marker had ever been, and the farmers nearby had looked at her strangely when she'd asked how long they'd been there.

*"Always been there,"* the farmer had said. *"You all right, miss?"*

And she'd written *survey error* in her own neat hand, because the alternative had been a door she didn't have a key for.

"They moved," Ilsa said. "Seven of nine boundary stones, relocated, undamaged, unremarked. I logged it as instrument drift." She laughed, and it sounded awful even to her. "Instrument drift. As if the theodolite had opinions about where Corvale ends."

"So it's real." Casper sat down heavily on the archive stool. "The land is—"

"Don't say it yet. Let me be the one to say it. It's the only perk of my profession." Ilsa braced her hands on the table. "The land is moving. No — worse. Moving implies intent or mechanism, tectonics or sorcery, something with a direction. This isn't movement." She pulled the quarterly surveys together into a single stack, and the edges fluttered like something breathing. "This is *drift*. The kind of drift a thing does when nobody's holding it in place."

"You sound like you've seen this before."

"I've read about it." Ilsa crossed to the locked cabinet — the deep cabinet, the one with the iron fittings, the one containing the collection she officially denied owning: the impossible geographies. Charts of lands that weren't. Surveys of countries that had stopped answering letters. She'd bought most of them from refugees and madmen and once, memorably, from a woman who paid in buttons and wept the entire transaction. Scholars called the collection *Vane's Folly*. Ilsa called it *the evidence*.

She found what she was looking for third drawer down: a thin folio, water-stained, bound in fishskin, titled in a dead dialect *A True Account of the Country Called Ninefold, Being Its Measures and Bounds, Taken in the Last Season of Its Being.*

She set it on the table between them and didn't open it right away.

"There was a country," Ilsa said. "Northeast of here, past the Saltmarshes, where the maps go polite and vague. Old accounts describe it — a rich place, nine great cities, a court of gods, the works. Every account agrees it existed. Every account also agrees on almost nothing else, which is unusual. And then, roughly fifteen years ago, the accounts stop. Not fade — stop. Travelers' diaries end mid-sentence. Trade ledgers show shipments arriving from ports that no longer appear anywhere. And the maps—" she tapped the folio, "—the maps of the region from before are detailed. After, the same region is blank. Not unexplored. *Blank.* Cartographers didn't write *here be dragons*. They wrote nothing. Which takes effort, Casper. Leaving a space empty takes more ink than filling it."

"Fifteen years," Casper said.

"Fifteen years." Ilsa watched her face do something complicated. "You knew that number already."

"I've been having a strange week."

"Weeks are the natural habitat of strange. Go on."

Casper turned the fishskin cover open. Inside were the measures of the lost country — leagues and bearings, river-widths, the heights of nine hills, the depth of a harbor — all recorded in a careful hand that deteriorated toward the end, the numbers getting shakier, until the final page held only a single line in a different, hurried script:

*It will not stay measured. The country forgets itself from the edges inward. Pray someone remembers the middle.*

Ilsa read it aloud, because some things needed air. When she finished, the silence in the archive room had texture, like wool packed in the ears.

"That's not possible," Casper said.

"I agree entirely. And yet." Ilsa flipped back to her own quarterly surveys of Corvale, spread beside the dead country's dying measurements, and made herself look at them side by side. The handwriting differed. The century differed. The *pattern* didn't. Drift from the edges inward. Small at first, patient, thorough. A land losing its grip on its own coordinates.

"Here's what keeps me up at night," Ilsa said, "and I want you to appreciate that I am a woman with many competitors for that title." She began stacking the surveys, squaring their edges with unnecessary precision. "Geography doesn't happen in the ground, Casper. It happens in agreement. A border is a thing a million people agree to remember. A city stands where it stands because everyone who knows it knows the same place. Take away the agreement — take away the *remembering* — and the ink is just ink. The stone is just stone. Nothing holds it anywhere."

"You're saying Corvale is forgetting itself."

"I'm saying something is forgetting Corvale. Or—" Ilsa hesitated, because the next part was the part she'd been circling for eleven surveys now, the part she'd refused to write down even in her private log, "—or something took the remembering away, and the land is only now getting around to noticing. Like a man who loses his leg and keeps walking for a few steps before the falling-down part."

Casper had gone very pale. "How long?"

"For Corvale? Based on the rate of drift?" Ilsa did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding for a month. It wasn't hard arithmetic. It was just heavy. "Years. Maybe five. Maybe less, if it accelerates. Borders first — the farmland goes vague, the roads start disagreeing about distance. Then the outskirts. Then—" she gestured at the fishskin folio, at the blank pages where a country used to keep its address, "—then the middle. Then the maps go polite and vague, and some poor fool in a shop like mine buys the last account off a refugee who paid in buttons."

"And nobody else has noticed? The Hollowers? The magistrates? Anyone?"

"Oh, they've noticed *something*. Why do you think the raids started? Why do you think they hollow anyone caught with waking magic?" Ilsa shrugged, and hated how casual the gesture came out. "Fear is a kind of memory too, and they're hoarding it. But they're treating symptoms. Burning the fever while the wound underneath gets wider." She fixed Casper with a look. "Which brings us to the obvious question, which I have been politely not asking since you walked in looking like a rooftop's regret. What exactly happened to you this week? And why does my professional instinct insist it's standing in my archive room wearing your face?"

Casper opened her mouth. Closed it. Looked, for one naked moment, like a woman deciding how much truth a friendship could carry.

And then the lamps guttered. All of them, all at once, the way flames lean when something vast passes near — and the orrery in the corner stopped ticking, its nine city-dials spinning briefly out of agreement before settling, and every map on every wall rustled as though a wind had gotten inside the paper.

Casper pressed a hand flat against her sternum, the way a person does when something inside them has spoken.

"Not here," she whispered — and Ilsa genuinely could not tell whether she was talking to Ilsa or to whatever lived behind her breastbone. "Not yet. Ilsa — I need you to do something for me. If I ask. Someday soon."

Ilsa Vane had spent her whole life refusing to chart the impossible. It was, she realized, watching her friend shake in a room full of proof, a policy due for revision.

"What," she said.

"Take me to the edge," Casper said. "Before Corvale forgets where it keeps me."

* * *

That night, alone, Ilsa updated the master survey. New quarter, new measurements, new drift logged in her precise, defensive hand. At the bottom of the page, below the figures, she paused — pen hovering — and then wrote the line she'd been refusing to write for eleven surveys:

*Confirmed. The land is forgetting itself. Cause unknown. Rate increasing.*

Then, because she was who she was, she added a footnote:

*Note: subject C.V. arrived today asking the correct questions. Note further: subject C.V.'s shadow fell the wrong way against the lamplight, and the lamplight seemed to be consulting with her about it.*

*Note further still: I believe I am about to become involved.*

She blotted the page, locked the cabinet on the fishskin folio, and stood a long moment in the dark of her shop, listening to the orrery tick its nine unequal cities back into reluctant agreement.

Outside, far off toward the Saltmarshes, where the maps went polite and vague, something on the horizon flickered — once, twice — like a lamp being carried, patiently, through a country that was trying very hard to remember it existed.

Ilsa began packing for a journey she hadn't agreed to take.
