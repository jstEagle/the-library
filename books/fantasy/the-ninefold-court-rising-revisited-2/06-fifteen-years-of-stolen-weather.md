## 6. Fifteen Years of Stolen Weather

The cellar under the tannery smelled of old leather and older lies, which Casper had always found comforting. Lies kept well down here. They didn't rot like fish or sour like milk; they just sat in their little glass bottles on their little wooden shelves and waited to be sold to someone who wanted to be rid of them.

She lit the lamp—oil was expensive, but she'd earned enough at the market tonight, before the Hollowers turned everything sideways—and set her satchel on the workbench with the reverence of a woman handling a sleeping animal.

"Right," she told the room. "Inventory."

Talking to herself was another skill she'd mastered. Invisibility, she'd learned long ago, wasn't about being unseen. It was about being unremarkable, and unremarkable people muttered to themselves over their accounts like everyone else.

She began pulling the bottles out one by one and lining them up in rows: the small stock, the everyday wares. A first kiss, taken off a boy in Salt Row who'd wanted to stop blushing every time he passed the girl's door. A grudge, fifteen years old, harvested from a ferryman who couldn't sleep for hating his brother. The smell of rain on hot cobblestones—she'd never actually sold that one. Some things you took because taking was what your hands knew how to do, and some things you kept because they were too lovely to let go of and not yours to keep anyway.

That was the whole dirty secret of her trade, if anyone had ever cared to dig for it: nobody came to a memory-thief to *give*. They came because something hurt and they wanted it out of their heads, and Casper Vasquez was very good at lifting hurt out of heads without dropping any of it on the floor. She bottled the pain, sold it back to no one, and occasionally, when the weight got heavy enough, buried a few bottles in the marsh beyond the north wall where even Hollowers didn't like to walk.

She counted as she worked. Forty-one small charms. Three mid-grade sorrows—a dead husband's cough, a betrayal by a business partner, the specific shame of a man who'd once struck his daughter and could no longer look at her. Those went in the locked case under the bench. High-risk inventory. If a Hollower ever cracked this cellar, those were the bottles that would hang her.

And then there was the bottom of the satchel.

Casper's hands stopped moving.

There were nine bottles down there. She knew the number without looking, the way you know the number of your own teeth. Nine bottles wrapped in oilcloth, packed in wool, sealed with wax she'd pressed her thumb into so hard it had left the whorls of her print like a signature on a confession. Nine bottles she had never opened, never sold, never shown to a living soul.

Nine bottles that had been down there for fifteen years, since the night the sky over Corvale had gone the color of a bruise and a country had stopped existing, and a girl of seventeen had run through streets that were forgetting themselves around her, snatching memories out of the air like sparks off a bonfire because if she didn't catch them they would be gone forever—

*Or,* said a small voice in her head, the voice that sounded uncomfortably like Ilsa Vane drawing a line through a map, *or you caught them because you were already running with your hands out, thief, and catching is what thieves do.*

"That's slander," Casper told the empty room. Her voice came out thin. "I was helping."

The lamp guttered, though there was no draft. Down in the cellar. Under three feet of stone and forty years of spilled tanning brine.

Casper looked at the nine bundles of oilcloth and did not touch them. That was the rule. That had always been the rule, the only rule she'd never broken: *don't open the old ones.* Not because she didn't know what was in them. Because she did.

* * *

She made tea instead, which was the traditional response of Corvaleans to problems too large for the kitchen. She climbed the ladder to the loft above the cellar, put the kettle on the little iron stove, and sat on her cot wrapping her hands around nothing while she waited for it to whistle.

Vessin's voice had been quiet since the market. Two days now. She might have been grateful, except silence from a god felt less like peace and more like a held breath.

"You're sulking," she said to the rafters.

*I am waiting,* the voice said, mild as milk, arriving somewhere behind her ears rather than in them. *You are sulking. There is a distinction.*

"I'm doing inventory. It's called bookkeeping. Respectable people do it."

*Mm.* A pause with texture to it, the particular pause of an ancient being deciding whether to be patient. *And how does the bookkeeping go?*

"It goes fine." The kettle began its low climb toward whistling. "Forty-one small charms, three sorrows, one smell of rain I'm keeping for myself, and none of your business."

*Casper.*

"Don't." She stood up, and the cot creaked, and the kettle screamed, and she poured the tea over all of it like noise could drown out a god. It couldn't. Gods, she was learning, were waterproof. "Don't say my name like that. Like you know it better than I do. It's mine. I've had it fifteen years and it fits me like my own boots."

*It does fit you,* Vessin said, and there was something in his tone she hadn't heard before—not patience now, not amusement. Something gentler and much worse. *That is what I have been trying to tell you. Doors fit their frames, little thief. That is how doors work.*

She drank her tea standing up, which meant she wasn't going anywhere, which meant she was arguing with a god in a garret over a tannery at two in the morning, which was, when she thought about it honestly, the most remarkable thing that had happened to her in fifteen years of careful unremarkability. And she'd spent those years being so *good* at it. She'd built herself a life the way other people built card houses—one careful piece at a time, never breathing on it, never letting anyone lean close enough to topple it. Market stall. Cellar. Cot. Tea. The same route home every night, varied only on Thursdays, because even invisibility needed a maintenance schedule.

And underneath it all, at the bottom of the bag, nine bottles she'd never dared open.

"You could just tell me," she said finally. Quieter now. "What's in them. You were there. Apparently you're always *there.*"

*I was there,* he agreed. *But this is not a thing you should hear. This is a thing you must remember. There is a difference, and it matters more than anything else I will ever ask of you.*

"That's ominous."

*I am a god. Ominous is most of what we do.* But the joke landed soft, without its usual dryness, and then he was quiet again, and Casper was alone with her tea and her rules.

She finished the cup. Washed it. Dried it. Put it away handle-out, facing left, because that was where it went.

Then she went down the ladder into the cellar, because she had known since she lit the lamp that she was going to, and pretending otherwise was just a slower way of walking down the stairs.

* * *

The oilcloth crinkled under her fingers like old skin.

She laid all nine bundles out on the workbench in a row, unwrapped them, and for the first time in fifteen years actually *looked* at the oldest part of her hoard. She'd expected—what? That time would have changed them? That the glass would have clouded, the contents faded to gray?

No such mercy. Each bottle held weather.

Not metaphor. Actual weather, folded small. The first bottle held fog—real fog, sea-fog, thick and silver, rolling slowly against the glass like something alive and patient. The second held snow that fell upward. The third held sunlight, but wrong sunlight, gold shot through with green, the light of a place she had never seen and yet recognized the way you recognize your own reflection in a stranger's window.

The fourth bottle hummed.

Casper went still. Not the ordinary faint buzz a strong memory gave off when you handled it—that was a thing she'd felt ten thousand times, the little tickle against the palm like a moth's wings. This was deeper. Lower. A note she felt in her sternum, in her teeth, in the fillings of her back teeth, a note like the one a cathedral bell keeps humming long after the rope has stopped swinging.

She touched the fifth bottle. It answered the fourth, harmonizing. The sixth picked up the chord. One by one, as her fingers moved down the row, the nine bottles woke, and the cellar filled with a sound that was not quite sound—a resonance, a chord, a *name being almost spoken*—and the lamp flame stood up straight and burned white, and every hair on Casper's arms rose like the fur on a cat that has seen something in an empty doorway.

"Oh," she said. Very quietly. "Oh, you're awake."

They were. All of them. Fifteen years she had kept these bottles dark and cold and wrapped, and they had lain there like seeds in winter soil, and now something was calling through the ground and every seed had broken open.

Divine residue. That was what Ilsa would call it, if Casper ever worked up the courage to bring her one, which she absolutely was not going to do. Residue, like ash in a grate. Except residue didn't *harmonize*. Residue didn't roll over in its bottle and press itself against the glass toward her hand like a dog hearing its owner's step on the stair.

These weren't memories of weather. She saw that now, with the kind of clarity that arrives precisely when you least want it. These were memories *of a country*—of harvest festivals where the fog came in off a sea that no map admitted to, of snow that fell upward because the god of small mercies had liked to watch children chase it, of light that came green-gold through leaves of trees that had been old when the world was new. Nine bottles. Nine pieces of weather. Nine—

Her hand hovered over the ninth bottle, the smallest, the one wrapped in three layers instead of one, and stopped.

Because the ninth bottle was humming a name.

Not aloud. Nothing so crude. But the resonance of it bent as her fingers drew near, curved toward her, shaped itself around her the way water shapes itself around a stone dropped in a pool, and in the curve of it she heard—or almost heard, or *felt*, the way you feel a word forming in your own mouth before you speak it—a sound like *Ves-sin* and also like something older, and beneath both of them, faint and terrible and unmistakable, a child's name.

Hers. The first one. The one from before Corvale, before the market stall, before the boots and the tea and the card-house life. The one she had not spoken in fifteen years because she had told herself she couldn't remember it.

She remembered it now. It came up out of her like a drowned thing breaking the surface—gasping, dripping, alive after all this time at the bottom.

"No," Casper said. Her voice shook. She pressed her palms flat on the workbench to steady them, and the wood hummed back against her skin, sympathetic as a struck string. "No, no, no. I stole *memories.* Little ones. Small pains. Kisses and grudges and the smell of rain. That's the trade. That's what I *do.*"

The bottles sang on, gently, mercilessly.

"Whoever I was before—" Her throat closed. She pushed through it. "Whoever I was before, she drowned. She's gone. I built someone out of the pieces and the pieces are *good*, they're decent, they pay their taxes—well, mostly—and they don't open bottles that hum."

*Little thief.* Vessin's voice, very soft, from everywhere in the cellar at once. *What do you think you were carrying out of that country, the night it fell? Souvenirs?*

"They were going to be lost! Everything was going under, I could see it happening, the streets forgetting their own names—I *caught* them, I saved them—"

*Yes,* he said. *You did. Every one. Ninefold, complete, hidden so well that eight gods have spent fifteen years tearing the world apart looking for them.* A pause, and when he spoke again there was something raw under the gentleness. *Do you understand yet what you are, Casper Vasquez? Do you understand why the Hollowers can feel you coming now? You are not carrying stolen goods.*

The ninth bottle rolled a half-turn toward her hand. Against the wax seal, from the inside, something pressed—lightly, patiently, the way a child taps on a window.

*You are the vault,* said Vessin, *and the vault has started to sing.*

Casper snatched her hand back. The chord faltered, held, swelled again—hungry now, or hopeful, she couldn't tell which was worse. Upstairs, faint through the floorboards, a bell in the Temple district began to ring. Then a second bell. Then a third, out-of-step, urgent—the alarm pattern, the one that meant the Hollowers were working tonight, sweeping, hunting, following a sound only they could hear.

Following, perhaps, a sound exactly like this one.

Casper looked at the nine bottles singing on her bench in the lamplight, looked at the ceiling as though she could see through it to the bells, and felt the last comfortable lie of her life go quiet in her chest—the lie that whatever she'd done fifteen years ago, she'd gotten away clean.

She hadn't gotten away. She'd just been *stored.*

She blew out the lamp, swept the bottles into their oilcloth with hands that were not entirely steady, and reached for the satchel.

Outside, somewhere over the rooftops, dogs had begun to bark—and beneath the barking, if you knew how to listen, and she did, came a rhythm of boots. Many boots. Moving toward the tannery district.

Moving, she was almost certain, toward her.
