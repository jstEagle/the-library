## 7. The Hollowing of Tam Reeve

Tam Reeve had stolen exactly one hundred and nine memories in his life, and he could tell you the number because he kept a ledger.

Not a written ledger — that would be idiocy, a confession bound in leather. A ledger in his head, one notch per theft, tallied every morning while he shaved. He was proud of the discipline of it. Other thieves got greedy. Other thieves took a wedding, took a whole childhood, took something with weight to it, and then they were carrying ballast they couldn't set down. Tam's rule was simple: never take anything heavier than an afternoon.

His best work, he'd tell you over a pint if you bought the first two, was a man's fear of geese. The fellow had been crippled by it since boyhood — couldn't cross the park, couldn't walk past the pond, wept once at a funeral when the mourners passed a goose on the lane. Tam had lifted it out clean as a splinter. Charged fair. And now that man fed the geese by hand every Sunday and had named the meanest one Gerald, and somewhere in Corvale there was a goose named by a man who no longer knew why he loved them, and Tam considered this the finest thing he'd ever done.

He was thinking about Gerald that morning, oddly enough, when the Hollowers came for him.

He'd been working the fish market, which was against his own better judgment — fish markets were crowded, and crowds meant witnesses, and witnesses meant questions — but a dockhand had approached him directly, sweating, pressing coins into his palm before Tam had even agreed. *My brother's grief,* the dockhand said. *He lost his daughter in the spring and he can't get out of bed.* And Tam, who had rules, heard himself say yes before his rules could clear their throats.

Grief was heavier than an afternoon. Grief was a stone you carried in your chest, and pulling it out meant bracing yourself against the puller. But the coin was good, and Tam had been telling himself for three years that he'd retire soon, that the ledger would stop at some round number, and a hundred and ten felt like a fine place to stop. A hundred and ten and done. He'd open a shop. He'd always thought he'd be good at selling hats.

The memory sat heavy in his satchel, wrapped in waxed cloth the way Casper had taught him years ago — *wrap it like it's alive, Tammy, because it is* — and he was halfway down Rope Street, whistling, already composing the hat shop's name in his head, when he saw the cordon.

Hollowers did not do things quietly. That was rather the point of them. They wore gray coats and faces like unfinished sketches, and where they walked, people forgot to look at them, which was worse than looking. The cordon on Rope Street was six of them, standing in a ring around a small man in an apron, and the crowd around *that* was doing the terrible thing crowds do, which is nothing.

Tam should have walked away. Every rule he had said walk away. But the small man in the apron turned his head, and Tam recognized him — the dockhand, the brother — and the dockhand's face was not afraid. It was worse than afraid. It was *hopeful*, the way a face looks when it believes the men in gray coats have come to help.

"Tam Reeve," said the tallest Hollower, and its voice was like paper being unfolded. "You are carrying what does not belong to you."

"Now, see," Tam said, because he had never in his life met a silence he didn't fill, "that's a matter of interpretation, that is. Everything belongs to someone until it doesn't. That's just commerce."

"You stole a grief."

"I *relieved* a grief. There's a difference, and the difference is the widow I paid rent to last month." He was backing up as he talked, which was also part of his method — talk backward, keep your feet honest, look for the gap in the cordon. There was always a gap. Crowds were water and Hollowers were stones; water went around.

But the gap did not come. And the Hollower stepped forward, and it did the thing Tam had seen done twice before from a safe distance and had spent both nights after drinking until his ledger blurred: it reached into the air beside the dockhand's head and *pulled*.

What came out looked like smoke and sounded like a name. The grief of a father for a dead daughter, unwrapped in public, and the whole street flinched from it — even the Hollowers swayed slightly, even they had to brace — and then the tallest one opened a jar, and the grief went in, and the dockhand stood up straighter.

"There," said the Hollower, to the crowd. "Cleaned."

And Tam understood, in the slow cold way understanding arrives, that the dockhand had reported him. Had sold him. Because a grief removed is a grief owed to whatever authority claims all griefs, and Corvale had decided long ago that sorrow belonged to the state now, and returning it was called justice.

"That's mine," Tam said, and his voice cracked on it, which embarrassed him even then, even with the gray coats closing. "That's my stock. You can't requisition stock, that's not — there are *rules* about this, even for you lot—"

"Tam Reeve," said the Hollower, unfolding closer, sketch-face tilting with something that might have been curiosity in anything with a real face, "you are carrying one hundred and nine items. We will be taking all of them."

And the last thing Tam Reeve thought, before they reached into him, before the pulling started and the street tilted and a hundred small mercies came loose in him like buttons off a coat — the last thing, held tight as a coin in a fist, was not a prayer or a curse or even the number one hundred and nine.

It was the memory of a girl with ink-stained fingers teaching him to wrap a theft in waxed cloth. *Like it's alive, Tammy.*

He tried to give that one away first. To anyone. To the crowd, to the cobbles, to the gray sky over Rope Street.

They took it anyway. They took it third.

* * *

Casper heard about the arrest before she heard about the hollowing, and she heard about neither — she read it, in the way of her trade, in the texture of the market itself.

The night market had gone quiet in a way that wasn't quiet. The charm-sellers still called their wares, the lantern-sellers still argued about wick-oil prices, but under it ran a hush like a held breath, and Casper had survived fifteen years in Corvale by trusting hushes the way other people trusted friends. She closed her stall early. She told herself it was caution. She told herself it had nothing to do with the fact that Tam worked the day markets and she hadn't seen his stupid cheerful face in four days, and Tam without his face in four days was a Tam in trouble, because that man would sell his own reflection if he thought it would make somebody laugh.

She found out where they were holding the hollowing from a candle-girl who traded gossip for the smell of her grandmother's kitchen, which Casper had bottled years ago and kept mostly out of sentiment. Execution square, sundown. Public. The criers were calling it *a restoration of civic feeling*.

Casper went anyway.

This was, by any professional standard, insane. Going to a public hollowing was how you ended up *in* a public hollowing; the Hollowers used the crowds the way fishermen use nets, dragging them through everyone present, tasting for awakened magic, for stolen goods, for anything that hummed. And Casper was humming these days whether she liked it or not. Ever since the praying man, ever since the voice in his prayer that had said her childhood name like it had been keeping it warm — the oldest charms in her satchel had started to sing, faintly, at odd hours, like kettles deciding whether to boil. She'd taken to sleeping with the satchel in a bucket of river water, which muffled it somewhat and did nothing at all for her peace of mind.

So going was insane, and she went, and she pulled her hood up and put on the face of a woman with nowhere better to be, which was easy, because it was true.

The square was fuller than she expected. Corvale loved a punishment; that was the bargain the city had made with its own fear. Magic had been outlawed so long that watching it punished was the only way most people ever got to feel near it. Families had brought snacks. A man near the front was selling roasted chestnuts and doing brisk business, and Casper hated him with a purity that surprised her.

They brought Tam out at sundown, and the sound the crowd made was not a boo or a cheer but something hungrier, a collective inhale, and Casper's hand found the knife in her sleeve without her permission. As if a knife could do anything against six Hollowers. As if a knife could cut smoke.

Tam looked smaller. Not beaten — Tam had been beaten plenty in their line of work and always bounced back like a dropped cat — but *smaller*, diminished in advance, as though they'd already begun and he'd simply learned to stand inside less of himself. His eyes found the crowd and moved across it, and Casper shrank back, and then — she would swear this later, swear it on her satchel, on the white door, on everything — his eyes stopped moving. Found her hood. Held there.

And Tam Reeve smiled.

It was his market smile, his *two pints and I'll tell you about the goose* smile, and he aimed it at her across forty feet of hostile crowd like a man tossing a coin to a beggar he liked, and Casper understood what he was doing, and it broke her heart in a place she hadn't known was load-bearing. He was saying goodbye. He was saying *don't you dare feel guilty*. He was saying it with a smile because words would get them both killed, and because Tam had never in his life said a serious thing except through a joke, and now he had one serious thing left and no time to wrap it.

The tallest Hollower raised its hands.

What followed took eleven minutes. Casper counted, because counting was something to hold onto, because counting was a rail to grip while the world went over a waterfall. The Hollowers worked in a ring, palms out, and Tam's memories came out of him one at a time, visibly, each one a little curl of light and color that the crowd *oohed* at, because the crowd oohed at anything. Some of them were ordinary — you could see the ordinariness, a birthday, a bad tooth, a woman laughing in a doorway — and some made the crowd gasp, because a few of Tam's thefts were beautiful, and beauty reads as magic to people who've forgotten the difference.

And between them — this was the part Casper would carry, the part she'd wake up holding for weeks — between the extractions, Tam *talked*. Mouth working, voice getting thinner, but talking. Joking. She was too far to hear it, but she could see the shape of it, the rhythm, the pauses where the crowd laughed or didn't. Even being unmade, even being emptied into jars in front of his city, Tam Reeve was doing his act. He was giving the performance of a lifetime because it was his last, and the horrible grace of it was that it *worked* — the crowd laughed twice, actually laughed, and for two seconds the square was full of people laughing at a dying man's jokes instead of feeding on his death, and Casper thought: *that's the trade. That's what he stole for. That's what it was all for.*

By minute eight he had stopped joking. By minute nine he had stopped standing, propped up by two Hollowers like a puppet whose hand had gone slack. By minute ten there was very little of Tam left to extract, and the Hollowers slowed, thorough, patient, scraping the jar's edge.

At minute eleven they finished, and a man stood in the center of the square who had Tam's body and none of Tam in it, and the man smiled.

That was the worst part. Not the emptiness — the emptiness was horror, yes, a person-shaped absence with the lights left on. But the *smile*. The Hollowers fitted their shells with smiles, Casper knew, a small civic mercy, so that families wouldn't have to look at blankness. And so Tam stood there in the sundown wearing a borrowed grin, and the crowd began to applaud — some of it pity, most of it relief, all of it obscene — and the shell of Tam Reeve bowed, because the shell remembered bows even if it didn't remember why.

Casper did not remember walking out of the square. She remembered chestnut-smoke. She remembered the count reaching eleven. She remembered thinking, with a clarity that felt like falling: *they weren't searching for stolen magic. They were searching for me. One hundred and nine items, and they took all of them to find out which one was mine.*

Because that was the arithmetic underneath the horror, and she had seen it the moment the tallest Hollower spoke: they hadn't caught Tam poaching. They had caught Tam *knowing her*. Fifteen years of small forgettable thefts, and she had one friend in the world, and the friend was a ledger, and ledgers could be read. Somewhere in those jars on Rope Street was the memory of a girl with ink-stained fingers teaching Tam to wrap thefts in wax, and the Hollowers had taken it *third*, and when they finished reading it they would know her name, her face, her haunts, her habits, the exact weight of everything she'd ever taken.

She made it three streets before she had to stop and put her hand on a wall and breathe.

The wall was warm from the day's sun. Small mercy. She took it.

*He smiled at me,* she thought. *He emptied out smiling at me, and I'm going to stand here feeling it, and then I'm going to stop feeling it, and then I'm going to be useful.* Because grief, in her experience, was like anything else in her satchel: you could hold it, or you could spend it. And Casper Vasquez had never once in her life let a debt go unpaid, and this — this smiling shell in the square, this third-taken memory of her own young hands — this was a debt with interest accruing.

She straightened. She pulled the hood lower. And she became aware, gradually and then all at once, of the humming.

Her satchel. Against her hip. Singing.

Not the faint kettle-whistle of the last few days — this was louder, urgent, a chord of nine notes that she did not know how she knew were nine, thrumming against her ribs like a second heartbeat that had opinions. The oldest charms, the sealed ones, the ones she'd never dared open. They had hummed since the praying man. Now they were practically *shouting*, and when she pressed her palm flat against the waxed cloth to hush them—

The voice came again. Through her own skin this time, through the charms, patient as tide.

*They will read him,* it said, gently, terribly. *And then they will come for you, thief. You have perhaps three days. Less, if they are diligent.*

"Who are you?" Casper whispered, into an empty street, like a madwoman, because the alternative was admitting she was asking a satchel.

*A friend,* said the voice, which was what every dangerous thing in Corvale said first. *Or nearly. Come somewhere safe, Casper. There are names you need to hear. Nine of them. And one of them, I think, is going to hurt.*

Down the street, a lamp flickered in a window that had no lamp.

Casper looked at it for a long moment. Then she tightened her grip on a satchel full of singing gods, and walked toward the light.
