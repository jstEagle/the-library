## 8. Nine Names

Vessin taught her the first name at dawn, in the ruins of a shepherd's hut three days out from Corvale, while Ilsa slept with her boots on and a knife under one hand like a woman who had been disappointed by mornings before.

"Say it," Vessin said. "But not carelessly."

"I don't know how to say anything carefully. It's never been my problem." Casper turned the name over in her mouth. He had given it to her as a shape before a sound — drawn in spilled tea on the floorboards, a curling line like smoke deciding to be a letter. "What happens if I'm careless?"

"You'll know."

"That's not reassuring."

"It isn't meant to be. It's meant to be honest. You'd be surprised how rare that is among gods."

She said the name.

It was *Oreneth*. And it did not go into the air so much as it went into her — down through the roof of her mouth and along the spine of her, into the place where she kept her oldest charms, the ones wrapped in grey silk at the bottom of her pack that she had carried fifteen years without once opening, because opening them felt like digging up something that had been buried for a reason.

The first charm woke.

Not literally — it didn't rattle or glow or do any of the things charms were supposed to do when they misbehaved. It simply stopped being a charm and started being a memory, hers again whether she wanted it or not. She was standing in a harvest field under two moons — *two* moons, one silver and one the color of weak tea — watching women with wheat braided into their hair sing a song about the patience of rivers. The smell was barley and woodsmoke and someone's grandmother's bread. A child ran past her laughing and Casper's throat closed around a grief that belonged to no one alive anymore.

The country had a name. It rose in her like water finding its level: *Saravelle*.

"Saravelle," she whispered.

"Yes," Vessin said softly. "One."

---

They walked all day, because walking was what you did when the world had stopped making sense and sitting still only made it worse. Ilsa navigated by instruments of her own invention — a compass that pointed at belief instead of north, which she had built, she explained, "as a joke, and now I hate it."

"The borders moved again last night," she said, tapping the needle, which swung lazily between two directions like a bored cat. "East is where east was yesterday. East is also, apparently, forty miles south-southeast. This country doesn't have geography, it has moods."

"It's not forgetting itself," Casper said quietly. "It's remembering. That's worse."

Isla looked at her. "You want to explain that?"

"Not yet. Ask me after the ninth name."

"A ninth name." Ilsa's laugh was dry as old paper. "Of course. Why would there be eight names? Eight would be reasonable."

"There are nine," said Vessin, from everywhere and nowhere, the way he always spoke — a voice like a door opening in an empty room. "There were always nine. Your cartographer's maps are simply too small to hold us."

"My maps," Ilsa said with dignity, "are excellent maps. Your country is an unreasonable country."

Casper found herself smiling, which felt almost obscene given what was waking up inside her, charm by charm, name by name. But that was the thing about grief, she was learning. It didn't replace ordinary life. It just sat down next to it and refused to leave, and eventually you made room, the way you make room at a table for a relative you can't turn away.

*

The second name came at noon, at a crossroads shrine so old the god it honored had been forgotten even before Saravelle fell. *Ysolde-of-the-Ford*, Vessin called her, goddess of crossings, and when Casper spoke the name the second charm opened like a hand uncurling.

A wedding. Hundreds of guests, every one of them wearing ribbons of the same impossible blue. A bride from the river towns and a groom from the hill clans, and the whole point of the wedding — she understood this suddenly, the way you understand things in dreams — was that the two families hadn't spoken in sixty years, and Ysolde's priests had decided that a marriage was the best kind of treaty. There was dancing on a bridge over a river that sang back. There was a moment when both mothers wept at the same time on opposite banks, and the river carried the sound across so each heard the other crying, and that, someone said beside young-Casper's ear, *that* is why we keep her name.

"They shared," Casper said aloud, blinking back to the road, the dust, the afternoon light. "Your court. They shared everything. Weddings as treaties. Gods who — who lent each other their names."

"For a thousand years," Vessin said.

"And then?"

Silence. The particular silence of a man deciding how much truth a rope bridge will hold before he steps onto it.

"And then," Casper repeated, "what?"

"Say the third name first."

"You're stalling."

"I am pacing myself. It's different. Stalling is cowardly. Pacing is architectural."

"You," said Ilsa, without looking up from her compass, "are going to be very annoying to travel with, aren't you?"

"He already is," said Casper, and said *Marrow-and-Mirth* — the third name, god of cellars and feasts and the particular holiness of feeding people — and the third charm bloomed open, and this time she laughed even as her eyes burned, because the memory was of a midwinter feast in a hall lit entirely by candles made of rendered memories — legal ones, blessed ones, memories freely given by families who wanted their dead honored rather than grieved — and the whole city had eaten until the tables groaned, and the poorest quarter had eaten *first*, because Marrow-and-Mirth had exactly one law and it was this: the last plate goes to the hungriest table.

"We had laws like that," Casper said. "We. I mean — the country. Saravelle."

"You lived there," Vessin said gently. "For eleven years. Before you took us away."

The word *us* landed like a stone dropped down a well. She listened for the splash. It took a long time coming.

*

The fourth name was *Haleth-the-Doorwarden*, and his memory was of walls — but kind walls, walls built low enough to talk over, gates that were never locked because locking them would have been an insult to travelers and travelers were holy. The fifth was *Sereth-of-Coins-Turned-Over*, patron of second chances, whose great temple was a debt-forgiveness house where every seventh year the ledger fires burned and the whole economy of Saravelle began again from zero, voluntarily, joyfully, and merchants danced in the ash of their own account books.

"This is a fairy tale," Ilsa said, watching Casper come back from the fifth memory with tears running freely and no attempt to hide them. "You understand that. No country forgave debts every seven years. No real country."

"No *surviving* country," Casper said.

And the wonder, which had been carrying her for two days like a warm current, curdled right there on the road, because the sentence arranged itself in her head before she could stop it:

No surviving country. So what happened to this one?

She looked down at her pack. At the grey silk bundle. Four charms still sleeping inside it. Five names remaining.

"Casper," Vessin said, and his voice had changed — careful now, the way you're careful around a wound. "Before the sixth—"

"No." She sat down in the middle of the road, dust and all, because her legs had opinions and they were all *no*. "You've been pacing yourself. Architecturally. I've watched you do it since the shepherd's hut. Every time I get close to the question, you give me another beautiful memory — weddings and feast halls and unlocked gates — and I think, oh, what a lovely country, what a tragedy that it's gone, some terrible war must have taken it, some plague, some disaster that happened *to* these poor people. But that's not the shape of it, is it?" Her hands were shaking. She watched them shake with distant interest, the way you watch someone else's hands. "The Hollowers hunt me specifically. Tam Reeve was hollowed in a public square as bait. The land remembers me. The priestess at the village — she barely looked at anyone else. And your voice, the first time, used my childhood name, my *real* childhood name, the one I stole from myself so thoroughly I didn't know I'd stolen it until you said it."

The road was very quiet. Even the wind seemed to be holding its breath.

"So say the sixth name," Casper said. "Or tell me. One of those. But you don't get to keep being kind at me, Vessin. Kindness with information withheld is just theft with good manners. I should know. I'm a professional."

A long pause. When he spoke again, the door-in-an-empty-room quality of his voice had gone strange — smaller, somehow. Older.

"Sixth," he said. "*Vessin*."

Her own mouth went dry. "That's your name."

"It is. And the sixth charm is mine. Say it."

She said it, and the world folded.

---

She stood in the Court itself. Not a building — she understood that immediately — a *place*, a ninefold ring of thrones under a sky that was more sky than sky, each throne radiating its god like heat off stone. Oreneth's wheat-gold presence. Ysolde's river-light. Marrow-and-Mirth's candle-warmth. Haleth's gate-shadow. Sereth's coin-bright mercy. And six others, and then—

Then the eighth and ninth thrones, and the memory flinched away from them like a tongue from a broken tooth, and in the center of the ring stood the white door, the door from her oldest stolen memory, standing alone in a field of grass that grew in perfect silence, and around the door the nine gods were arguing.

No. Not arguing. *Fighting.* The word arrived with the full weight of the memory behind it: fighting the way only family fights, with everything personal and nothing held back, because they had shared a country for a thousand years and sharing had finally, finally gone wrong. Oreneth wanted the harvest given freely; Sereth insisted free gifts corrupted the giver. Ysolde demanded all borders dissolve; Haleth answered that a warden with no walls wards nothing. And underneath it all, older and colder than the rest, the two thrones at the ring's heart — the eighth and ninth, whose names the memory still refused to give her — were pulling the whole Court apart like children fighting over one toy, except the toy was a nation and the nation was tearing.

And Vessin — her Vessin, the quiet one, the god of doors and waiting, the one whose throne stood slightly outside the ring because he had never wanted a country, only a way through — Vessin had looked at the white door, and then at a girl crouched in the grass beyond it, a thief-girl with quick hands and a hungry heart who had slipped through a crack between worlds chasing something shiny —

— and Vessin had *chosen* her.

Not caught her. Chosen her. Had poured the whole Court — its names, its lands, its ninefold singing — into the mind of a child who could carry stolen things better than anyone alive, and had let her run, because the Court could not survive its gods' war, but it might survive *hiding*, hidden in the one vessel no god could reach into without permission: a memory-thief's hoard, locked behind locks only she could open.

He had needed a thief. So he had made sure there was one nearby, and opened the door, and let her steal the biggest thing anyone had ever stolen.

The country hadn't fallen. The country had been *withdrawn* — tucked away inside a teenage girl's pack like a charm wrapped in grey silk — and everyone in it, everyone who had ever lived there, had been unwritten from the world, scattered into forgetting, and Casper Vasquez had been the hand that took them.

She came back to herself on the road, on her knees, actually, though she had no memory of kneeling, and Ilsa was crouched in front of her saying her name in the sharp repeating way people say names when the named person has been somewhere else.

"I didn't witness it," Casper heard herself say. Her voice came out level, which felt like the worst possible outcome. "All this time I thought — I told myself I was a witness. A survivor who got out with souvenirs. But witnesses don't cause things. And I caused it. He put the whole country in my hands and I *ran* with it, I ran for fifteen years, I sold pieces of it, I sold *the smell of its rain* for three coppers a bottle—"

"Casper." Ilsa's hand was on her shoulder, firm, unpoetic, grounding. "Breathe. Whatever you saw—"

"She's not wrong," Vessin said.

Ilsa's head snapped up toward the empty air. "Oh, wonderful. The invisible roommate weighs in."

"She is not wrong," Vessin repeated, and there was no architecture in his voice now, no pacing, just a tiredness so vast it sounded almost human. "I chose her. I opened the door. I let her take us because the alternative was watching my siblings burn ten million souls to fuel their war. If that makes me a thief, then she and I are colleagues. If it makes me something worse—" the pause stretched, "—then she has been carrying my sin as well as her own, and I have no right to ask her to carry it further."

"But you're going to anyway," Casper said. "Because there are three charms left, and two names left, and the eighth and ninth thrones were the ones your memory wouldn't let me look at."

"Yes."

"And those two are the reason you've been pacing yourself."

"...Yes."

Casper sat back on her heels. The evening was coming down gold and ordinary around them — dust, dry grass, Ilsa's ridiculous compass spinning its lazy circles. Somewhere out there, Hollowers were hunting her. Somewhere out there, a crater waited where a white door used to stand. And here, in the middle of it, sat a woman holding a country hostage in a silk bundle, being asked — by a god, no less — to finish the job.

"Three days ago," she said slowly, "my biggest problem was a client who haggled."

"Three days ago," said Ilsa, "you were rich. Relatively speaking."

"Give me the seventh name," Casper said, and held out her hand for the pack as if it weighed nothing, as if it weren't the heaviest thing in the world. "And then the eighth and the ninth. All of it, Vessin. Tonight. Because if I'm the Thief of the Ninth Door, I want to know exactly what I stole — and I want to know it from *you*, before whatever's left of your family finds me first."

The wind moved through the grass. And far off, at the edge of hearing — or maybe at the edge of the world — something with nine voices, minus however many were still speaking to each other, turned its attention toward the road.

Vessin said, very quietly, "Then hear the seventh name, and know why I waited."

And the name he gave her was one she recognized — not from any charm, not from any dream, but from the priestess's mouth in the village that prayed itself real, spoken with reverence and terror in the same breath:

*"Ninth Door."*
