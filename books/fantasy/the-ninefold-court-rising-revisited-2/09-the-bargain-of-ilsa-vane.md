## 9. The Bargain of Ilsa Vane

Ilsa Vane had three rules about her workshop, and she recited them to every visitor the way a priest recites liturgy: *Don't touch the maps that hum. Don't ask about the sea room. And don't—under any circumstances—spill tea on the western shelf, which is older than the city and holds grudges.*

Casper Vasquez had broken rule one within four minutes of arriving.

"Sorry," she said, snatching her hand back from the drawer of shifting coastlines. "It tickled."

"It does that." Ilsa adjusted her spectacles and tried very hard not to be charmed. Charm was how thieves got in. She'd spent forty years making herself difficult to rob: locks within locks, false drawers full of angry wasps, and a front door that only opened for people who knocked in the rhythm of a specific sea shanty her father had sung badly. "That's the coast of Vel Amurra before it drowned. It likes attention. Everything in this room likes attention. That's what makes them dangerous."

"And me?"

"You," said Ilsa, "are the most dangerous thing currently standing on my rug, and my rug has survived two fires and a flood." She set down the compass she'd been pretending to clean. "Sit. You look like a woman who hasn't slept since the moon changed its mind."

"The moon did change its mind."

"Yes," Ilsa said. "I noticed. It's been rising in the wrong quarter for six nights, and every chart in this room has quietly rearranged itself to pretend it hadn't. Sit down, Thief of the Ninth Door."

Casper flinched at the name. Good, Ilsa thought. A thief who liked being called a thief would have been far less useful, and far more trouble.

* * *

The girl—for she seemed a girl when she sat, all elbows and exhaustion, though her eyes were old in the way of things that had been dropped too many times—took the offered chair and wrapped both hands around the tea Ilsa pushed toward her, as if it were something that might be taken away.

"I need a map," Casper said.

"Everyone needs a map. Most people want one of somewhere that exists. Those are cheap."

"Not one of somewhere that exists." Casper turned the cup slowly in her hands. "The opposite. I need to find the borders of a country that isn't there anymore. One that got—" She hesitated, hunting for the word.

"Erased," Ilsa supplied. "Unmade. Forgotten out from under itself." She rose and crossed to the western shelf—the old one, the grudge-holder—and drew out a tube sealed with wax the color of dried blood. "I've been tracking the symptoms for years. Corvale's eastern boundary moved nine hundred paces south last month. The farmers out past the Tallow Road woke up one morning in a different district, legally speaking, and no records anywhere could tell them otherwise. The river Kess now runs where a granary used to stand, and the granary's owner remembers building his house beside water he swears wasn't there. Every night the land forgets a little more of itself, like an old woman losing nouns." She laid the tube on the table between them. "And you're the noun-thief, aren't you? Or so my sources say. And my sources are mostly maps, and maps don't lie. They only sulk."

Inside the tube was a chart Ilsa had made over eleven sleepless nights: Corvale rendered in ink that shifted as you watched it, borders drawn and redrawn, and beyond them—a vast pale absence, shaped roughly like a country, labeled in her own hand: *Here There Was Something.*

Casper stared at it for a long time. When she spoke, her voice came out smaller than before. "You know what I did."

"I know what the shape of your crime looks like on vellum." Ilsa tapped the pale absence. "Fifteen years ago, this whole region stopped appearing in records. Not destroyed—destroyed leaves ruins, refugees, ash in tree rings. This left nothing. No grief. No graves. People who should have lost daughters remember having never had them. It's the cleanest erasure I've ever studied, and I have studied some filthy ones." She took off her spectacles, polished them unnecessarily, and put them back on. "So. Yes. I'll guide you to the borderlands. I've wanted to walk that blank space my whole professional life, and I am not so old that curiosity has stopped outrunning sense. Though honestly, it's a close race these days."

Casper looked up sharply. "Just like that? No haggling?"

"Oh, there will be haggling." Ilsa smiled, and knew from long experience it was not her warmest smile. "But not over coin. Coin is vulgar, and I have enough of it, and neither fact protects anyone from anything. My price is heavier than coin."

* * *

She made Casper wait while she lit lamps. Partly because the workshop deserved proper light for what came next; partly because her hands needed the excuse.

From the locked cabinet beneath the sea room door—the one room no visitor asked about, because asking meant Ilsa stopped being polite—I brought out a single charm bottle. Small. Green glass. Ordinary as a plum.

"This," Ilsa said, setting it on the table, "is the only memory I have ever considered stealing. Mine, I mean. Taking it out of my own head and putting it somewhere I wouldn't have to live inside it anymore." She laughed, short and dry. "Do you know why I never did it? Pride. Same reason I learned eleven dead languages. I told myself: *Ilsa Vane does not run from her own head.* Forty-one years of pride." She sat down across from the thief. "Pride is a terrible landlord. Charges rent every day and fixes nothing."

"What's in it?" Casper asked quietly. She already knew. Thieves always knew; it was the first thing they saw, the way sailors see weather.

"My daughter," Ilsa said. "Mariel. Died at seven. Fever, the winter the Hollowers burned the Lantern Quarter, because the healers who could have saved her were ash, and the magic that could have saved her was illegal, and I was too busy protecting my precious maps to smuggle her to someone who'd break the law for a child." Her voice stayed level. She had practiced level. "I remember everything. That's the curse of my profession. I remember the exact weight of her in my arms the morning she couldn't wake. Fourteen pounds, two ounces, like carrying a sack of flour that loved you. I remember it every day. Some days several times. Memory doesn't knock, you understand. It just moves in."

Casper's hands had gone still around the teacup.

"I'm sixty-three years old," Ilsa continued. "I am going to walk into a country that eats travelers' memories for breakfast, guided by a god who calls you his door, hunted by men who hollow people out in public squares for entertainment and civic order. If I die out there, fine. I've made peace with dying; I've drafted my will in triplicate and it is a masterpiece. But if I die carrying her—if Mariel's face goes wherever I go when the Hollowers take me—then they get her. They get the last of her, the weight of her, the flour-sack that loved me. And I find I cannot permit that." She slid the green bottle across the table. "So here is my price, Vasquez. Take it. Take her out of my head, and carry her. Keep her safe the way you keep all your stolen things—which, by reputation, is better than any of us keep the things we love honestly. And give me back a head quiet enough to do my work, and hands steady enough to draw the borderlands, and a heart light enough to survive what's coming."

The silence afterward had texture. The maps on the shelves rustled softly, the way a crowd goes quiet.

"That's not a small thing to ask," Casper said finally. "Taking a memory like that—it doesn't leave a clean hole. It leaves a *shape*. You'll feel her absence like a missing tooth. You'll reach for her every day and find nothing, and you won't even know why it hurts, because I'll have to take the knowing too, or the theft won't hold."

"I know exactly how it works," Ilsa said. "I've read every treatise on extraction ever written, including three that were banned for accuracy. I know you'll have to take the grief, and the guilt, and the eleven years of birthdays I celebrated alone with two slices of cake because that was our joke. I know I'll wake tomorrow and feel a wound with no story attached to it, and part of me will spend the rest of my life pressing on that bruise trying to remember what hit me." She met the thief's eyes. "I have thought about this every single night for fifteen years. I did not arrive at this price lightly. I arrived at it the way one arrives anywhere after a long march—limping, and certain."

"And you trust me?" Casper said. "A stranger. A thief. You've known me two days."

"Three days, and yes." Ilsa allowed herself the smile again, softer this time. "Because yesterday, in my workshop, you apologized to a map. Thieves who apologize to maps are rare, Vasquez. I collect rarities. Besides—" she nodded at the air beside Casper's shoulder, where the temperature had dropped half a degree and the lamplight had gone the color of deep water, "—your god is listening, and gods make excellent witnesses. Even forgotten ones. Especially forgotten ones."

The voice, when it came, seemed to arrive through the floorboards and the marrow simultaneously, patient as tide:

*SHE IS WISE TO BARGAIN WITH YOU. YOU HAVE NEVER ONCE PAID A FAIR PRICE VOLUNTARILY.*

"Thank you, Vessin," Casper said through her teeth. "Very helpful."

*I OFFER PERSPECTIVE.*

"You offer interruptions." But there was no venom in it—or rather, there was, but worn thin, the way a coin wears thin from being carried everywhere. Ilsa watched the girl glance at the empty air the way one glances at a husband of thirty years: exasperation braided tight with something that had no business being tenderness yet.

"Does it bother you?" Ilsa asked. "Being spoken through?"

"He doesn't speak *through* me. He speaks *near* me. Loudly." Casper rubbed her temple. "It's like living next to a cathedral bell that's decided it loves you."

*ACCURATE,* said Vessin, sounding pleased.

Ilsa laughed despite herself—a real laugh, surprising in its own throat—and pushed the green bottle the last inch across the table. "Well then. Do we have a bargain, Thief? My guidance, my charts, my stubborn old legs on the road to the borderlands—in exchange for my daughter's ghost, carried safe in stolen glass, out of reach of hollowing men and forgetting country alike."

Casper looked at the bottle for a long moment. Then she reached out—not for it, but for Ilsa's hand, turning it palm-up, gentle as a nurse. "Last chance to keep your pride."

"Pride and I are dissolving the partnership," said Ilsa Vane.

* * *

It didn't hurt, exactly. That was the horror of it.

Ilsa had expected pain—had almost counted on it, some penance, some toll. Instead there was a coolness at her wrist where Casper's fingers rested, and a sensation like a page turning somewhere behind her eyes, and then—

Then the workshop was quieter. Not silent. Quieter. The way a house is quieter after a guest who lived with you for years finally leaves, and you stand in the doorway wondering why the rooms suddenly hold so much echo.

"There," Casper said softly, capping the little bottle. Her face was wet. Ilsa touched her own cheek and found hers was too, though the crying seemed to belong to someone standing slightly behind her, waving from a distance. "It's done. She's—she's safe. She's warm in there. They stay warm, the ones we carry carefully. I promise you."

"Do they?" Ilsa asked. Her voice sounded fine. Steady. Professional. Somewhere below the steadiness, a bruise was already blooming, tender and sourceless, and she found she was pressing her thumb against her sternum without meaning to.

"They do." Casper held up the green bottle to the lamplight, and for a moment something flickered inside it—small and bright, a spark the size of a child's laugh. "Hello, Mariel," the thief said, to a bottle, to a dead girl, with the easy courtesy of someone greeting a colleague. "Your mother's a terrifying old woman and I like her enormously. We're going to go fix a country. Try to get some rest."

And the spark—this was the part Ilsa would write down later, that night, in the margin of her charts, in ink she'd have sworn was dry—*the spark flickered, twice, like a nod.*

"Well," Ilsa managed. Her thumb kept circling the ache under her breastbone, a bruise looking for its story. "Well. That's settled, then."

"Are you all right?" Casper asked. "Truly. People lie about this part. Tell me the truth."

Ilsa considered. The truth was that she felt lighter and robbed in the same breath; that the workshop already seemed larger, emptier, a museum of a life instead of a life; that somewhere in her chest a tooth-shaped absence throbbed in time with her pulse. The truth was that she had gotten exactly what she bargained for, and bargains, in her considerable professional experience, were how people acquired precisely what they asked for plus interest.

"I'm sixty-three, I've sold my daughter to a thief, and tomorrow I march into a hole in the world," she said briskly, rising to fetch her traveling case. "I'm marvelous. Now help me decide which maps come. We can't take the humming ones—they attract Hollowers like jam attracts wasps—but I refuse to leave Vel Amurra behind. She's earned the trip."

* * *

They worked until dawn, choosing and packing, and it was almost pleasant, the two of them arguing over vellum weights and ink fastness, the god contributing opinions nobody asked for (*THE RIVER CHART. TAKE THE RIVER CHART. RIVERS REMEMBER LONGER THAN PEOPLE DO.*), the lamp burning low and gold.

It was near the end, while Casper wrestled the last drawer shut—the one that tickled—that Ilsa paused over her newest chart, the one charted fresh that evening from the night's observations, and went very still.

"Vasquez," she said. "Come look at this."

Casper came. On the paper, the pale absence—the blank country, the Here-There-Was-Something—had changed. Along its southern edge, faint as breath on cold glass, new markings had appeared overnight. Not borders. Not roads.

Lights. Tiny inked points of illumination, scattered through the emptiness like embers in ash.

And beneath them, in handwriting none of them had written—delicate, looping, unmistakably a child's—two words:

*come find*

Ilsa looked at the thief. The thief looked at the bottle in her coat pocket, where a small green light was glowing steadily through the glass.

"Well," said Ilsa Vane, reaching for her boots. "It seems my daughter has opinions about the itinerary."
