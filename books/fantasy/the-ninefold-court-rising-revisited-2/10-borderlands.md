## 10. Borderlands

### Casper

The border of Corvale was, according to every map Casper had ever seen, a river. According to the land itself, it was apparently more of a suggestion.

They crossed at dawn, where the Vane cartography said the Sere should run wide and brown and slow. Instead they found a dry channel full of pale grass, bending like hair under wind that didn't touch anything else. Ilsa stood at its edge for a long time, saying nothing in the particular way she had of saying nothing loudly.

"That's wrong," she finally announced, to no one and everyone.

"It's grass," Casper said.

"The Sere is a river, Vasquez. Two hundred miles long. I have drawn it eleven times." She stepped down into the dry bed, crouched, and pressed her palm flat against the earth. "And this ground remembers water. Feel it."

Casper knelt beside her. The soil was cool and dense, packed the way mud packs after a flood. It smelled faintly of silt. Somewhere underneath, something was holding the shape of a river the way an empty coat holds the shape of shoulders.

"Vessin," Ilsa said, without looking up, "explain."

Vessin had been walking behind them all morning as a man of about fifty with a farmer's forearms and a face you'd forget on purpose — his idea, not theirs. He said he wore forgettability like Casper wore invisibility: as a craft, honed over centuries. It was, she had to admit, well made. Even now, looking directly at him, she felt her eyes slide toward the horizon instead.

"Countries are held up by belief," he said. "Yours is held up by habit. Habit is thinner stuff. Out here, past the last census, past the last tax roll, there's nothing to hold the land steady except what people remember. And people out here remember the Court." He smiled, and it was a strange thing to see on a face designed to be forgotten. "So the land keeps trying to be the country it used to be. Then it forgets again by morning. Then it tries again."

"So everything out here is—" Casper started.

"A rumor with weather," Vessin agreed. "Yes."

Isla rose, brushing off her knees with the brisk violence of a woman who has decided a thing is offensive and will therefore be catalogued. "Then we walk fast," she said, "and we trust nothing we slept in."

That was how they spent their first day in the borderlands: walking fast, trusting nothing.

By evening the dry riverbed had filled itself back in — they heard it behind them, a long silver rush like applause — and ahead of them, where Ilsa's map showed only scrubland, stood a village.

It was small and golden-lit and utterly unremarkable: twelve houses, a well, a shrine with nine candles burning in nine clay niches. Smoke from cookfires. A dog trotting down the lane with the self-importance dogs reserve for home turf.

"There was no village there," Ilsa said. Her voice had gone very careful, the way it did around impossible geography, which was increasingly the only kind there was.

"There was no village there," Casper agreed, "and yet I'm starving, so."

"You want to sleep in a rumor."

"I want to sleep somewhere that isn't standing in a field pretending to be brave about it." She looked at Vessin. "Is it safe?"

Vessin considered the village with an expression she couldn't read — tenderness, maybe, or homesickness, or both wearing each other's coats. "It's real tonight," he said. "Real enough. Everything that exists one night at a time is still existing. Ask any thief."

"That," said Casper, "is the most comforting thing anyone's ever said to me, and I resent it."

* * *

### Vessin

Vessin had watched humans build things for a very long time, and he had learned that you could tell almost everything about them from how they lit their fires.

The village — it called itself Ostmere tonight; tomorrow it might answer to something else — lit its fires low and close, huddled, the way people do when light is precious. But in the shrine, the nine candles burned tall and unbothered, in air that had no draft. He stopped in the doorway of the shrine and let himself feel that. Nine flames, burning straight as pins. Someone in this place remembered carefully.

An old woman was tending them, trimming wicks with a knife smaller than her thumbnail. She looked up when he entered, and her face did something complicated — recognition, disbelief, and then a decision not to make a fuss, which was a very old-country reflex and it caught him in the chest like a fist.

"Evening, grandfather," she said. "Pilgrims?"

"Travelers," he said. It wasn't quite a lie. "Three of us."

"We've bread. The bread's real tonight. Tomorrow it may just be a smell." She snorted at her own joke, then went serious. "You'll forgive me if I look at you too long. You have a face like—"

"Don't," Vessin said gently.

She nodded, unsurprised, and returned to her candles. That was the old country for you. Half its people could feel a god in the room and had the good manners to keep trimming wicks.

He found the other two at a table outside the inn-that-was-real-tonight, arguing over a map by lamplight. This had become their evening liturgy. Ilsa drew what the map should say; the land disagreed; Ilsa took it personally.

"If the Sere fills in at night," Ilsa was saying, tapping the paper, "then the crossing point moves. Which means the Hollowers following us either know something we don't or are drowning politely somewhere behind us."

"They're not behind us," Casper said.

"How do you know?"

"Because I can feel them." Casper said it lightly, but her hand was flat against the table, fingers spread, the way she did when she was listening through her own skin to the stolen things she carried. Fifteen years of charms, humming. "Like teeth. Farther east. They're pacing us."

"Pacing us," Ilsa repeated.

"Herding," Vessin said, and sat down. Both women looked at him. He took the bread the old woman had sent and tore it, because it seemed important, in that moment, to be a person eating bread. "Hollowers don't chase. Chasing implies urgency, and urgency implies that what they hunt might escape. They herd. They keep you moving along a line until the line ends somewhere convenient for them."

"Convenient for them meaning?"

"Meaning open ground. Meaning nowhere to hide a door."

Casper set down her cup. "So we're being walked."

"You're being walked," Vessin said. "You specifically. Ilsa and I are luggage." He meant it as a joke. It landed wrong — he saw it land wrong, saw Casper's jaw tighten with the guilt she carried like a stone sewn into her coat — and so he did the thing he had been practicing for fifteen years of waiting, the thing that did not come naturally to gods: he told the truth plainly. "I chose you because you were strong enough to carry this. I have never once regretted the choice. Whatever comes at the end of this road, it will find us walking toward it, not driven. Do you understand me?"

Casper looked at him for a long moment. Lamplight moved on her face.

"Yeah," she said finally. "But I'm going to steal your dessert later anyway, on principle."

"Principle," Ilsa muttered, "is not what I'd call it."

But Vessin noticed — and he suspected Casper noticed too — that Ilsa shifted her chair so it sat closer to Casper's, and that neither of them mentioned it, and that this, also, was a way of building a country: two people deciding, night by night, to exist near each other.

* * *

### Ilsa

Ilsa Vane had been awake since the third hour of the morning, because the wheat field was singing.

Not the village. The village was gone. She had known it would be — she'd lain in a real bed in a rumored inn and stared at the ceiling rehearsing the loss, so that when she woke to birdsong and a breeze moving through ten thousand wheat heads where twelve houses had stood, it would hurt less. It hadn't worked. She'd still gone outside barefoot into the dew and stood among the wheat and felt, absurdly, like a mourner at a funeral for someone who'd only been alive one evening.

But the wheat was singing. Faintly. A work song, four voices, in an accent from a country that no longer existed.

She knelt and put her ear practically into the grain, and yes — voices, woven into the rustle, singing about harvest and a girl named Maret and a god whose name ended in a sound like a bell underwater. The wheat was remembering being sung to. Or the country was remembering having grown things worth singing about, and the wheat was simply the nearest available mouth.

"Cartographer," Vessin said, from somewhere above her. "People will talk."

"People already talk. They talk about villages that don't survive breakfast." She stood, brushing dew from her knees, and pointed at the grain. "Listen."

He listened. His expression did something she was beginning to recognize: the softening he tried to hide whenever the old country surfaced anywhere, even in wheat. Grief, she thought, looks different on something that's had centuries to practice it. On him it looked like patience. Terrible, endless patience.

"It's Maret's song," he said quietly. "There was a valley. There was always a valley."

"Where does it go?" Ilsa asked. "This line the Hollowers are herding us along. Where does it end?"

Vessin was quiet for a moment. The wheat sang. Somewhere behind them, miles east, Casper was sleeping badly in the ruins of a village that had never existed, dreaming other people's dreams.

"There's a place," Vessin said. "A crater, now. Once it held the first door — the white door. The Court came through it, in the beginning, before the Court was a court at all. If the Hollowers mean to pen us anywhere, they'll pen us there. It's the one place the land never forgets. It's the scar."

"Then we shouldn't go."

"No," Vessin said. "We absolutely should. It's the only place the door can open. Which means it's the one place they cannot afford to let us reach — and also the one place they cannot stop us reaching, because the land itself will pull us there. It pulls everything, out here. Every rumor runs downhill toward that crater." He smiled, thin and tired. "The whole borderlands is a funnel, cartographer. We've been inside it since the dry river."

Ilsa thought about her maps. Eleven renderings of the Sere. Twelve years of borders that wouldn't sit still, rivers that migrated like herons, mountain passes that closed for decades and opened overnight. She had called it bad surveying. She had blamed instruments, weather, her own aging eyes. She had written, once, in a private margin she'd never shown anyone: *the country is lying to me.* And she had felt, writing it, like a madwoman.

"I spent my life mapping this," she said. "Every error. Every shift. I thought I was failing."

"You were the only one telling the truth," Vessin said. "Your maps weren't wrong. They were honest. Nothing else in Corvale was."

That was, Ilsa reflected later, the most anyone had ever comforted her, and she resented how much it worked.

Behind them, Casper emerged from the ruined doorway, squinting, hair like a crow's nest, carrying three tin cups. "You're both being solemn without me," she said. "Rude. Also I dreamed about a bell underwater and I'd like it noted that I hate this country."

Ilsa took the cup offered to her. It was warm. The tea in it was terrible and real, this hour, and she drank it looking at the wheat, which was still singing about Maret, and thought: *we are three people and a god, walking into a funnel, and somehow this is the closest thing to family I have had in nine years.*

She didn't say it. Cartographers marked things; they didn't confess them. But she moved closer to the fire, and she noticed that Casper moved too, to keep the distance the same, and that Vessin — watching them both with that patient, hidden grief — pretended not to see.

### Casper

On the fourth night, the Hollowers stopped pacing them and sent a message.

Casper felt it coming an hour before it arrived — the hum of her stolen charms souring, all fifteen years of small thefts turning restless in her pack like animals smelling a storm. She was on watch. She woke the others without ceremony, and they climbed a ridge above their campsite and looked east, where torchlight was moving through a village.

Except it wasn't a village. Not anymore. It had been one — she could see the shapes of it under the torches, doorways, a shrine, a well — and the Hollowers were standing in it doing their work, and as she watched, the work finished, and the village simply... stopped. The torchlight revealed empty fields. Wheat. Wind. As if the village had been a drawing and someone had rolled up the page.

"They hollowed a whole village," Ilsa whispered. Her voice had gone flat, which was how she shouted.

"They hollowed the memory of it," Vessin said. His voice had gone quiet, which was worse. "Out here, that's the same thing."

One Hollower remained standing in the wheat where the village square had been. Even at this distance, across a mile of dark country, Casper felt it turn its head and look directly at her. She felt the look land on the pack at her hip, on the oldest charms inside it, the sealed ones, the ones that hummed with divine residue — and she understood, with a clarity that arrived cold and total, that the message was for her.

*I know what you're carrying,* the look said. *Bring it to the crater.*

"They're not trying to catch us," she said aloud. Her own voice sounded strange, steady. "They're driving us. Vessin's right. They're walking us straight to the door — because they need a thief to open it. They can't touch the Court. But I can."

Nobody answered right away. Below them the wheat sang its four-voiced song about Maret, oblivious, faithful, remembering a harvest that had happened a hundred and fifty years ago in a country with no name on any map.

"Well," Casper said at last, and found, to her surprise, that she was smiling — the fierce, cracked, road-worn smile of a woman who has decided that if the world insists on using her, it will at least find out what she costs. "Good news. For once, everybody wants the same thing."

"That's never good news," Ilsa said.

"No," Casper agreed. "But tomorrow, if the land cooperates, we pass a village that prays itself real. Vessin says they're holding a piece of the Court." She turned east, toward the torchless dark where a mile away nothing at all stood in a wheat field. "And I want to know why its priestess has been sending letters addressed to the Thief of the Ninth Door. Because I never told anyone that name."

The wheat sang. Far off, something answered — a bell underwater, tolling once.

"And I never wrote those letters," Vessin said softly.
