## 11. The Village That Prayed Itself Real

Mother Sable had been lighting the evening candles for forty-one years, and in all that time she had never once doubted that the village of Emberlow would still be there when the wicks caught.

This was not a small thing to be sure of. Emberlow was not on any map. It had not been on any map for fifteen years, because for most of those years it had not been anywhere at all. The village existed the way a held breath exists — sustained entirely by the stubbornness of two hundred people who refused to forget what had been taken from them.

Every night, Sable lit nine candles. Every morning, the village woke up inside its own borders instead of dissolving into somebody's barley field or, on one memorable occasion, the middle of a river. She did not know precisely how the trick worked. She only knew that it was her job to keep the wicks trimmed and the prayers said, and that faith, like bread, required someone willing to do the daily work of it.

So when the dog began barking at the western road at dusk, and Sable looked up from her candle-tray to see three travelers walking out of a landscape that had been empty wheat an hour ago, her first thought was not *strangers*.

Her first thought was: *the ninth candle just flickered.*

It had flickered. She was certain of it. Forty-one years of trimming wicks teaches a woman things about flame, and that flame had bent toward the door of her chapel as if greeting someone.

"Brannic," she called to the blacksmith's boy loitering by the well, because Brannic was always loitering by the well; it was practically his vocation. "Fetch the kettle. We have guests."

"Is it bandits?" Brannic asked, with the hopeful tone of a sixteen-year-old who had never once been permitted excitement.

"It is never bandits. Bandits cannot find us. Nobody can find us." Sable set down her candle-snuffer and squinted at the road. "That is rather the point of us."

* * *

The travelers were an odd trio, even by the standards of a village that had once been found by a traveling menagerie that absolutely should not have been able to cross its borders (the elephants had been very polite about it).

The first was a tall woman with a cartographer's weathered face and a way of looking at Emberlow's rooftops as though measuring them for grief. She walked like someone carrying something heavy that no one else could see, which, Sable would later learn, was exactly what she was doing.

The second was a god.

Sable knew this immediately, the way you know rain is coming in your knees. He wore the shape of a lean, gray-eyed man in a traveler's coat, but the candles in her chapel window leaned toward him as he passed, every one of them, like flowers after sun. Her knees ached. Her heart did something complicated. She had spent her whole life praying to gods who had stopped answering, and here was one strolling up her lane looking mildly embarrassed about the whole affair.

And the third —

The third was a young woman with dark hair going gray early at the temples, a thief's quick eyes, and the posture of someone trying very hard to be nobody. She kept her hood up. She kept her hands in her pockets. She looked, in short, exactly like a person who hoped very fervently that no ancient priestess would recognize her.

Sable recognized her instantly.

She dropped the kettle.

Well — she handed the kettle to Brannic with great dignity and then her hands shook so badly she had to sit down on the well's edge, which amounted to the same thing.

"You," she breathed.

The young woman went very still. "I think you've mistaken me for—"

"The Thief of the Ninth Door," Sable said, and the words came out of her the way water comes out of a cracked dam, forty-one years of them at once. "You're her. You're *her*. You came back. Oh — oh, merciful Nine, you actually came *back*—"

"I really think—" the thief began.

"She stole the Court," Sable told Brannic, who was holding the kettle and understanding nothing. "Fifteen years ago, when the gods went to war and the country burned, *she* took it away. She took it somewhere the other eight couldn't reach. She hid it inside herself and walked out of the fire and the whole country went with her, and we—" her voice cracked, and she hated it, and let it, "—we are what's left. We are the splinter under the world's fingernail. And you *came back*."

The thief looked at the god. The god looked at the sky with the expression of a man who had rehearsed this conversation differently.

"I don't want worship," the thief said quietly. "Whatever you think I am, I'm not that."

"No," Sable agreed, standing, wiping her palms on her apron, gathering herself the way she gathered herself before funerals. "You're worse. You're *guilty*. Worship I could forgive you. Guilt takes longer." She picked up her candle-tray. "Come inside. All of you. The kettle's already on, and in Emberlow we do not discuss the end of the world without tea. It's a rule. I made it myself, after the Year of the River Incident."

* * *

The chapel of Emberlow was a low stone building that had been a granary, then a chapel, then briefly a granary again during a lean winter, then a chapel with conviction. Its walls were hung with nine hundred small offerings — ribbons, buttons, carved animals, a truly alarming number of wooden spoons — each one tied to a prayer that someone in the village still remembered saying.

The tall cartographer stood in the doorway and did not come in for a long moment. When she did, she touched one of the ribbons — a child's ribbon, faded pink — very gently, and said nothing at all, and Sable understood that this woman also carried a dead country, just a smaller one, one person large.

The god sat down at the table like he'd sat there before. Perhaps he had. Time worked strangely around the old ones; Sable had learned to stop asking.

The thief sat last, at the edge of the bench, spine straight, hands wrapped around a tea cup she hadn't drunk from.

"So," Sable said, pouring. "Talk. Start with why the ninth candle flickered tonight, because it hasn't flickered since the night you left, and I would very much like to know if my knees are lying to me."

"They're not," the god said. His voice was quiet and dry as old paper. "The Court knows its door, Mother. Even a fragment of it. Even here."

"A fragment?" The cartographer — Ilsa, the thief had called her — leaned forward. "You mean the village itself?"

"I mean," Sable said, "that Emberlow is the last piece of Vessia that still remembers being Vessia. When the Thief took the Court away, everything else went with it — the cities, the roads, the rivers, the names. Gone. Erased. The maps forgot, the neighbors forgot, the *land* forgot. But two hundred of us were in the temple of the Nine that night, praying, and our prayer got... folded in. Caught in the hem of whatever she carried off." She looked at the thief over her cup. "We woke up the next morning in a wheat field that wasn't ours, in a country that didn't exist, and we decided — collectively, stubbornly, and against all available evidence — to keep existing anyway."

"How?" Ilsa asked. It was the question of a scholar, hungry and horrified in equal measure.

"By remembering," Sable said simply. "Every night, nine candles. Every morning, the names. Every child learns the Ninefold Litany before they learn their letters. Every wedding, every funeral, every harvest — we say the names, we tell the stories, we argue about whether Aldous the Smith-God preferred his ale dark or light—" ("Dark," muttered the god.) "—and the village stays. Mostly. Some nights we drift. There was a week in '09 when we were a hillside in Corvale's borderlands and a very confused shepherd tried to shear one of our goats. But we hold. Memory holds."

The thief finally drank her tea. Sable watched her do it and thought: *there. That's the look of a woman swallowing something bitter and calling it tea.*

"You've been praying to us," the god said softly, "for fifteen years. In a world where prayer goes unanswered."

"We weren't praying *to* you," Sable said. "We were praying *about* you. There's a difference. To-praying asks for things. About-praying just says: *you mattered, you mattered, you mattered.*" She set down her cup. "Though I'll confess, lately, some of the younger ones have started to-praying. Tam Reeve's cousin swears the Well-God answered her. Said her name back." She paused. "You wouldn't know anything about that, I suppose."

The god and the thief exchanged a glance that contained an entire unwritten chapter.

"Mm," said Sable, who had raised four children and buried two husbands and was not fooled by anyone, ever. "I see we'll be getting to that."

* * *

After supper — stew, bread, and the goat cheese Emberlow was unjustly famous for among the perhaps six people aware it existed — the villagers gathered in the chapel square, because word traveled fast in a place with two hundred souls and nothing better to discuss than miracles.

They came out to see the god, and they did, and they pressed close and wept and laughed and one elderly man fainted directly into the horse trough (he was fine; he pronounced the experience "bracing"). They sang the Litany, all of them, unbidden, two hundred voices in the dark with nine candles burning, and the air itself seemed to thicken, to *remember*, and above the chapel the stars rearranged themselves into patterns that belonged to no Corvale sky.

But Sable watched the thief.

The thief stood at the edge of the crowd, hood down now, and the villagers saw her too — saw her and knew her, the way Sable had known her, because the Litany included her. Fifteen years of nightly remembering had done its work. Somewhere between the fourth candle and the seventh, the Thief of the Ninth Door had become part of the story: the one who carried the fire out of the burning house. The children had a skipping rhyme about her. Sable had heard them sing it a thousand times without knowing the woman in it was real.

*Thief, thief, little thief,*
*what did you carry under your sleeve?*
*Nine bright names and a door of white —*
*carry them far, carry them tight.*

The thief's face, as she listened to children skip-rope to a rhyme about the worst thing she had ever done, was something Sable would remember for the rest of her life. Which, given recent events, might be a shorter duration than previously assumed, but never mind.

Later, when the crowd had gone and the candles were burning low, Sable found her in the chapel, alone, standing before the wall of offerings.

"This one," the thief said, touching a small carved fox. "Who left this?"

"A girl named Merrin. She prayed for her father to come home from the war. He did. Missing two fingers, but home." Sable came to stand beside her. "Most of these are like that. Small prayers. We never asked the Nine for much. Just to be *real*."

"I took that from you." The thief's voice was flat, careful, the voice of someone holding a wound closed with both hands. "All of it. Your country, your gods, your — your *reality*, apparently. I didn't know. I swear I didn't know what I was taking. But knowing doesn't un-take it."

"No," Sable agreed. "It doesn't."

"So say what you need to say. Curse me, if it helps. The others will want to, eventually. I'd rather hear it from you first."

Sable considered her for a long moment. Then she reached up — the thief flinched, expecting a blow — and straightened the girl's collar, which was crooked, which had been crooked all evening, which had been driving Sable quietly mad.

"Child," she said, "I have been a priestess for forty-one years in a village held together by spite and candlelight. Do you know what I prayed for, every single night, into the dark, to gods who never answered?"

The thief shook her head.

"*Send her back.*" Sable's voice did not crack this time; she had used up her allowance of cracking. "Every night. *She took it, wherever she took it, she can bring it back. Send her back.* And now here you stand in my chapel smelling of road dust and bad decisions, and you're asking me to curse you." She snorted. "Absolutely not. I have waited too long. You don't get my curse. You get my *expectations*, and believe me, those are far heavier."

"That's not—"

"Oh, it very much is. You think guilt is a burden? Guilt is *cozy*. Guilt lets you sit still and suffer elegantly and change nothing." Sable took her by the shoulders. The girl was thinner than the rhyme suggested; the rhyme gave her broad heroic shoulders, and life had given her sharp ones. "Expectation makes you move. So here is what I expect. I expect you to open that door you stole. I expect you to give my country back. And I expect—" her grip tightened, gently, terribly, "—you to survive doing it, because I did not spend fifteen years praying for a corpse. I prayed for a *return*. Do you understand the difference?"

For a moment the thief said nothing. Then, very quietly: "Everyone keeps telling me what this will cost me."

"Yes," Sable said. "And everyone keeps forgetting to ask what it cost *us*. Two hundred people, fifteen years, holding a country together with their bare hands and their bedtime stories. You think we did all that so you could feel appropriately sorry?" She released her and stepped back, and her voice gentled. "You were a thief. Fine. Be a thief. Steal it *back*. Steal the whole country back from wherever you hid it. That's not penance, girl. That's just finishing the job."

The thief laughed — a startled, broken, real laugh, the first honest sound Sable had heard from her all evening.

"You're terrifying," she said.

"I'm a grandmother. It's the same thing."

* * *

It was near midnight when the trouble came, because trouble has excellent timing and no manners whatsoever.

Sable was banking the chapel candles when the ninth flame stood straight up — rigid, unnaturally tall, burning blue-white — and every offering on the walls rustled at once, nine hundred small prayers shivering like leaves.

Outside, the dog began to bark. Not at the road, this time.

At the dark past the road.

Sable stepped out into the square. The god was already there, standing very still, his gray eyes fixed on the western fields where the wheat had begun — there was no other word for it — *forgetting itself*, row by row, dissolving into gray nothing. And moving through that nothing, patient as frost, came shapes. Tall. Smooth-faced. Carrying instruments that gleamed like surgical steel and hurt to look at directly.

"Hollowers," the thief said from the chapel doorway, and her voice had gone cold and flat. "They followed us."

"They cannot cross the boundary," Sable said. "They never could. Memory holds—"

But even as she said it, the nearest Hollower raised its instrument, and the wheat where it stood simply *stopped being remembered*, and the boundary — the hem of prayer that had held Emberlow safe for fifteen years — tore with a sound like a hymn snapping mid-note.

The god turned to Sable, and for the first time since his arrival, he looked afraid.

"Mother," he said. "How many of your villagers can recite the full Litany from memory?"

"All of them. Why?"

"Because," said Vessin, forgotten god of the Ninefold Court, drawing himself up as the Hollowers came on through the ruined wheat, "tonight, they're going to need every word."
