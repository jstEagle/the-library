## 12. What Vessin Withheld

The last charm was the ugliest thing I owned.

That's not a figure of speech. In my trade, beauty is a kind of quality control — a well-stolen memory bottles clean, settles like wine, catches the light of whatever it remembers. A first kiss glows amber. The smell of rain comes up pale blue-green when you tilt the vial. Even Tam Reeve's stolen grudge against his landlord had a certain smoldering garnet elegance to it, before he sold it back to himself twice and wore it out.

But this one — this one sat at the bottom of my satchel like a dead thing. Cloudy white, opaque as spoiled milk, its stopper fused to the glass with something that wasn't wax and wasn't resin. Fifteen years I'd carried it through six rented rooms and one memorable flood. Fifteen years of telling myself I'd open it someday, when I was braver, or drunker, or both.

Fifteen years of knowing, in the way you know a load-bearing wall is cracked, that everything wrong with the world lived inside that little bottle.

"You're going to open it," Vessin said.

He sat across the fire from me in the shape of a tired man with ink-stained fingers, because that was the shape he'd worn when I'd met him, apparently, and gods are sentimental about their clothes. The village of Saint Aderyn's Rest slept around us, its priestess's fragment of the Court humming faintly under the floorboards like a hive that hadn't decided whether we were bees or bears.

"I'm going to sleep," I said. "I'm going to sleep for eight hours like a person with a functioning relationship to time."

"Casper."

"Don't say my name like that. You sound like my mother."

"I don't know your mother."

"Neither do I, technically." I turned the bottle over in my hands. "That's sort of the whole problem, isn't it."

He had the grace not to answer. That was the thing about Vessin that kept catching me off guard — for a god, he was remarkably good at shutting up. The other eight, according to every account the priestess had given us, were the kind of divine beings who filled silence the way a burst pipe fills a cellar. Vessin just waited in his, patient as furniture.

The fire popped. Somewhere down the lane, a dog was having opinions about the moon.

Eight names I'd learned. Eight sealed memories opened, one by one, each name a key turning in a lock I hadn't known was in my own head. Ashava of the First Door. Oren and Miel, twins of the Second and Third. Sefa who kept the rivers honest. Brannoc of the forge-light. Yeva of thresholds small and large. Old Corvus himself, the ninth-and-a-half god, the patron of thieves, who had laughed in my skull like a drain unclogging. Each memory had shown me a piece of the Ninefold Court: harvest festivals where the wheat bowed on its own, courts of law where the accused wept truth like rain, children taught the nine names before they learned to count.

And each memory had ended the same way. With fire. With the sky opening. With eight gods deciding, all at once and none together, that one country could not hold them all.

With me, fifteen years old and terrified, standing in a wheat field next to a white door, doing the only clever thing I have ever done in my life.

I stole the Court.

Not the land. Not the people. The *idea* of it — the nine names, the worship, the shape of the divine that made the country cohere. I bottled it the way I'd bottle a grudge, and I ran, and behind me the country forgot itself so thoroughly that maps grew blank spaces, and borders started wandering like drunkards, and Corvale outlawed magic mostly out of embarrassment.

I had spent three days believing I understood what I'd done. It was almost restful, guilt. Guilt has edges. You can grip it.

Then there was the ninth bottle.

"Casper," Vessin said again, gentler now. "You should know before you look. Whatever you find—"

"Oh no," I said. "No, no. You don't get the 'whatever you find' speech. That speech is for surprises. I've known this bottle existed since before you knew *I* existed. If there's a surprise in here, that's a structural failure of honesty, and I want it on record that I noticed."

"Noted," he said gravely. "For the record."

I hate that he can be funny. It makes everything so much harder.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being a thief: the hard part isn't the taking. The taking is easy, if you're any good at it. The hard part is that afterwards, the thing you took lives with you. You carry it past markets, past sleeping lovers, past your own reflection in shop windows, and every single day you make the same choice again — keep walking, or put it back.

I had been putting it back for fifteen years. Every morning. Keep walking, Casper.

I pulled the stopper.

It didn't fight me, which frightened me more than fighting would have. The fused seal dissolved like sugar in warm water, the cloudiness cleared, and the memory rose up around me the way all my stolen ones did — total, immersive, smelling of wheat dust and hot iron—

Except this one smelled of *him.*

* * *

I am fifteen, and my hands won't stop shaking, and the sky over the Court is coming apart like wet paper.

I know what's happening above me because I've felt it building for weeks — the nine names grinding against each other in the air like millstones, the prayers going up tangled and unanswered, the priests in their nine-colored vestments starting to whisper that perhaps the gods were never meant to share one sky after all. Ashava wants the doors sealed. Brannoc wants them thrown open for war. Sefa has flooded two valleys already, grieving. The twins have stopped speaking to each other entirely, which for twins who share a single worshipped body between them is a theological crisis nobody knows how to file.

And below all of it, in a wheat field beside the First Door — the white door, the original, the hinge of the whole country — stands a girl with a thief's calluses and a satchel full of practice charms, crying too hard to see straight.

Because a voice had spoken to her three nights ago. A quiet voice, ink-stained, tired. A voice that said:

*They will burn it all down fighting over it. Unless someone takes it away from them.*

*I can't take it. I'm one of them. If I touch it, they'll feel my hand in it instantly, and then it becomes another move in their war.*

*But you — little thief — you're nobody. That's your genius. Nobody watches nobodies. Take the Court out of their reach. All of it. The names, the worship, the whole shining idea of them. Bottle it and run, and don't look back, and I swear to you by nothing, because I'll have nothing left to swear by — I will find you. However long it takes. I will find you and we will give it back together.*

And the girl, who is me, who has never once been chosen for anything except punishment, says yes.

Of course she says yes. That's the part that breaks something loose in my chest as I watch my own young face through the memory's glass. She says yes because someone finally looked at her and saw a genius instead of a nuisance. Because he made it sound like rescue instead of theft. Because she was fifteen and the world was ending and the kindest voice she'd ever heard asked her for help.

She steals the Court. It's the hardest working of her short criminal life — bottling a nation's worth of devotion while gods scream overhead — and when it's done she staggers back with nine heavy vials cradled in her arms, and the voice says, *good, good, now run, run south, run now,* and she runs.

She does not see him turn toward the other eight. She does not hear what he shouts at them — some diversion, some accusation, something to send their burning eyes chasing each other instead of her. She does not see the moment the war finds its focus, or the door shatter behind her, or anything else, because he made sure of it. He curated even her escape. Even her guilt.

The memory ends with her running through wheat, clutching nine vials, and the tenth — the ugly one, the cloudy one — already sealed in her satchel.

The tenth bottle held the memory of the theft itself.

He'd taken it from her. Sealed it away so she couldn't relive it, couldn't regret it, couldn't ever quite be sure what she'd done. He'd left her a life built on a hole and told her it was kindness.

*So you could live*, the memory whispered, in his voice, in the margins where he'd handled it. *Guilt is a weight, little thief, and you were carrying enough.*

* * *

I came back to myself sitting in the dirt beside the fire, and I was laughing.

I want to be clear that it was not good laughter. It was the laughter of a woman discovering that the floor of her life was a trapdoor, and it scared the dog down the lane into a fresh round of opinions.

Vessin didn't move. He watched me laugh myself hoarse with an expression I'd seen on clients' faces a hundred times — the careful stillness of a man waiting to learn how bad the news is.

"Fifteen years," I said, when I could talk. "Fifteen years I thought I was the monster of this story. I lay awake inventing new ways to hate myself. I got *good* at it. I had a system."

"Casper—"

"And the whole time—" My voice cracked, which was humiliating, so I leaned into it, went loud, went bitter, because loud and bitter is armor and I have always dressed well. "The whole time it was a *job*. Somebody hired me! I wasn't even the villain, I was a *contractor!* Do you know what that does to a person? I had a whole tragedy going. It had *structure.*"

"Yes," Vessin said quietly. "It did."

"And you let me believe—"

"I let you live."

"That's not the same thing!"

"No," he said. "It isn't. And I knew it wasn't, and I chose anyway, and I have spent fifteen years watching you flinch at your own reflection and calling it mercy." He set his ink-stained hands flat on his knees. "Ask me the question you actually want to ask, Casper. Not why I hid it. Ask the real one."

I wanted to throw the empty bottle at his head. Instead I asked it, because he was right, damn him, and the real question had been clawing up my throat since the wheat field faded:

"Why did you need a thief at all? You're a god. You could have hidden it yourself. Buried it, scattered it, done anything but hand a child a satchel and a suicide mission." My hands were shaking again, fifteen years older and shaking exactly the same. "Unless hiding it wasn't the point. Unless you needed it hidden somewhere *deniable.* Somewhere the other eight could never trace back to any of you. Somewhere blameless and small and—" I stopped.

"Human," Vessin said. "Yes."

The relief hit me sideways, and I hated it, hated it, hated it. Because that was the terrible arithmetic underneath the betrayal: if he had engineered it, then I was still a thief. Just a thief. Not a god-killer, not the woman who unmade a country — a tool, a mark, a girl used by something vast and tired. And somehow that was *easier*. Somehow being deceived hurt less than being guilty.

"That's the worst part," I whispered. "I'm relieved. You lied to me for fifteen years and my first feeling is *relief.* What does that make me?"

"Honest," he said. "Which among gods is practically a miracle. Sit with the relief, Casper. But don't live there." He leaned forward, and the firelight did something strange around him, the way it does, the way it keeps forgetting he's wearing a man like a coat. "Because here is what your relief is hiding from. The other eight are still warring. They felt the Court stir when you opened those eight memories, and they felt *you* — the thief-shaped hole in the world where their worship used to live. They are coming for the door, and for whoever can open it. And Iliya's Rest sits on the road to that door, and Ilsa Vane has a daughter's grave she cannot visit, and you—" he gestured gently at the empty bottle in my hands, "—you now know exactly what you are."

"A con job," I said. "A patsy with excellent taste in vials."

"A door," he said. "My door. The only one I have."

I looked at him for a long moment. The fire crackled. The dog gave up on the moon.

"You know what the truly unforgivable part is?" I said finally, standing, brushing off my skirt, tucking the empty tenth vial into my satchel where the others rode. "You waited fifteen years to tell me the truth, and you're still not sorry."

"I am sorry about nearly everything," Vessin said. "Just not that. If I had left it with you — the guilt, whole and unlabeled — it would have hollowed you faster than any Hollower ever could. Some mercies are lies. I've made my peace with being damned for this one."

"Well, isn't that tidy for you."

"It's tidy for no one. That's rather the theme."

I shouldered my satchel. Down the lane, toward the eastern fields, the air had gone strangely still — that pressed, listening quiet that in my experience preceded either weather or disaster, and lately only disaster had been keeping appointments.

"The priestess said the crater's four days east," I said. "The First Door. Where you found me. Where you *made* me."

"Yes."

"Then we leave at dawn. And Vessin?" I looked at him straight on, and let him see all of it — the anger, the relief, the fifteen years of flinching, and underneath, stubborn as a weed in pavement, the girl in the wheat field who had once said yes. "When we get to that door, you're telling the other eight what you did. To their faces. I didn't steal this alone, and I'm not going to be hollowed for it alone."

Something moved across his borrowed face — pride, maybe, or fear, or the particular expression of a god realizing his tool has grown opinions.

"Dawn," he agreed.

And far to the east, over the horizon, the sky flickered — just once — white as a door.
