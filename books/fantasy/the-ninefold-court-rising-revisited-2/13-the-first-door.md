## 13. The First Door

They came over the last ridge at dusk, and Casper's first thought was that someone had taken a bite out of the world.

Not a small bite. A bite like a god had leaned down and scooped the earth out with a spoon the size of a cathedral. The crater spread below them in a wide gray bowl, half a mile across, its slopes terraced by erosion into something almost beautiful—almost, if you could forget that it used to be something else. Grass had crept partway down the inner walls, tentative as a guest testing whether it was welcome. At the center lay a floor of pale stone, cracked but whole, and in the middle of *that*, unmistakable even from this distance even in failing light—

The door.

It stood alone. White, tall, framed by nothing. No wall behind it, no building around it, no hinge-side anything. Just a door planted in bare stone like an exclamation point at the end of an empty sentence.

"There it is," Ilsa said quietly.

"Yes," said Vessin, who was not visibly present except when he wanted to be, which lately was more often than Casper would have liked. He stood beside her now in the form of a lean man in a traveler's coat, his face the kind of face you forgot the moment you looked away, which she suspected was deliberate courtesy on his part. "There it is. Hello, old friend."

"You've seen it before?" Tam asked. Tam had been quieter since the hollowing—since watching what the Hollowers did to people they caught—and when he spoke now it was in the careful voice of someone handling glass.

"I built it," Vessin said.

"Right," Tam said. "Silly question."

Casper crouched at the ridge top and studied the crater floor, because looking at the door directly made her chest do an odd thing, like a key turning in a lock she hadn't known she carried. What she looked for instead were the shapes moving along the rim of the bowl.

Hollowers. Six of them, maybe seven, spaced evenly around the crater's edge like beads on a string, their gray coats drinking the twilight. They walked slow patrols. Every so often one would stop, raise a hand palm-outward, and stand perfectly still for a long moment—*listening*, Casper thought, or whatever passed for listening in a body that had been emptied and refilled with law.

"Seven," she murmured. "One for each day of the week they can ruin."

"They're not searching," Vessin said. "They're guarding."

"That's worse."

"It is worse," he agreed, with terrible mildness.

Isla unrolled a scrap of map on a flat rock, weighted the corners with pebbles, and frowned at it in the way she did when the map was lying to her, which, this far into the borderlands, was most of the time. "The patrols cross here and here. The gap between them is—" She measured with two fingers. "Forty breaths, if nobody sneezes."

"I never sneeze," Tam said.

"You sneezed yesterday when a moth flew near your ear."

"That moth was *hostile*."

Casper let them argue about moths and kept looking down at the white door, because there was something happening to her hands. They had gone cold. Not cold like weather—cold like the first minute of a fever, when the body knows something before the mind consents to it.

Because here was the thing she hadn't said out loud yet, the thing that had been sitting in her ribs since they crossed into the shifting country: the memory fit. The stolen memory—the one she'd carried fifteen years without opening, the one that hummed against her ribs like a struck bell—was of standing exactly where that door stood. And in the memory, she had not been afraid.

She had been *welcome*.

That frightened her more than the Hollowers did.

"Casper." Vessin's voice, close. "Whatever happens down there—"

"Don't do the thing where you say something ominous and then refuse to finish it," she said. "You've been doing that for three days and it's unbearable."

He smiled. It was a strange smile, patient and sad, the smile of someone who has waited fifteen years for a person to arrive and knows better than to rush her at the threshold. Literally, in this case. "Then I'll say it plainly. The door is yours. But doors open both ways, and what comes through will know your name."

"My name," Casper said, "is currently worth nothing, seeing as half the country wants me hollowed and the other half doesn't remember there was ever a country at all."

"Not that name."

She didn't ask him what he meant. She wasn't ready, and he knew it, and mercifully—for once—he let her not be ready.

They went down in the blue hour, when the light goes soft and forgiving and everything looks kinder than it is.

Tam took the eastern slope, moving through the grass like a rumor, because Tam was the best sneak Casper had ever known and because he'd insisted, in his careful glass-handling voice, "Let me be useful. Let me be *useful*, Casper, or I'll start thinking about things." So Casper let him, and pretended not to understand what things.

Ilsa held the ridge with the map and a signal lantern shuttered down to a coin of light. Her job was simple: watch the patrol rhythm, flash twice if it broke. Ilsa was good at watching. Ilsa had been watching borders her whole life, professionally and then obsessively and then, since the daughter, since the memory Casper now carried wrapped in wool at the bottom of her pack—Ilsa had been watching the way people watch a fire that already took the house once.

And Casper went down the western slope alone, with a god at her shoulder like a coat she couldn't take off.

The grass whispered against her knees. The crater walls rose around her, terraced and gray, and the air changed as she descended—it got *thicker*, somehow, the way air gets thick before thunder, charged with a pressure that had nothing to do with weather. Her charms stirred. That was the strangest part. The little bottled memories she carried—a grudge, a first kiss, the smell of rain on hot stone—all of them woke at once inside their vials and began to hum, softly, in harmony, like a choir clearing its throats.

"Hush," she whispered to them. "None of this concerns you."

Every single charm disagreed.

The stone floor of the crater was warm under her boots. Warm, in the evening cold. She noticed that and decided not to think about it, and then thought about it anyway, thoroughly, the way you itch a scab.

Twenty paces from the door, a Hollower on the far rim stopped walking and raised its hand.

Casper froze behind a shelf of broken stone. Across the bowl, Ilsa's lantern blinked once—*hold*—and the Hollower stood there, palm out, listening, its head tilted like a dog hearing a whistle pitched too high for human ears.

It turned. Slowly. Its blind gray face swept across the crater, across the grass, across the shelf of broken stone where Casper pressed herself flat and breathed in increments, and Casper felt the attention pass over her like a searchlight, like a hand patting down her pockets, like fingers leafing through her—

—and stopping.

On the charm at her breast pocket. On the sealed one. The oldest one. The one that had been humming louder every night since the praying man, since the rooftops, since the name spoken through a stolen prayer in a voice like low bells: *Vessin*.

The Hollower took one step down the slope.

Then the wind moved.

There was no reason for wind—the evening had been still as held breath—but a gust came up out of the crater's heart and flattened the grass in a long silver ripple, and the Hollower staggered, caught itself, and stood blinking at nothing like a man who'd lost his place in a book. By the time it found its page again, Casper was moving, low and fast across the warm stone toward the door, and if anyone asked later she would say the wind was luck, but she could feel Vessin at her shoulder wearing the smug silence of a god who has just done a small illegal kindness.

*Thank you,* she thought at him.

*I have waited fifteen years,* he answered. *I am capable of waiting another four minutes. But not five.*

The door was bigger up close. Of course it was. Everything true was bigger up close. It stood twice her height, white wood gone faintly gold at the edges, carved all over with nine interlocking rings that her eyes kept sliding off of, the way they slid off Vessin's face. And set into the wood at the height of her heart was a keyhole shaped like nothing she'd ever seen—not a slot, not a circle, but a small empty space in the shape of a hand.

A left hand. Fingers slightly crooked at the second knuckle.

Casper looked at her own left hand, whose second knuckles had been crooked since childhood, since a fall down stairs she only half remembered, and felt the whole architecture of her careful little life shudder like a table with one short leg.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, you absolute *door*."

She touched it anyway. Because she was a thief, and thieves touch things; it was the whole of her religion.

The moment her fingertips met the wood, the door lit from within.

Not brightly. Not dramatically. It glowed the way coals glow when breathed on—a soft pulse of white-gold light rising under the grain, running out along the nine rings until all of them spun slowly, silently, like the innards of some vast patient clock. Warmth flooded up her arm, past her elbow, into her chest, and every charm she carried sang aloud—one clear note each, nine notes, ten, twenty, a chord—and the sealed memory at her breast beat like a second heart.

And then the door *reached*.

There was no better word for it. Something in the wood reached back along her arm, gentle and enormous, the way a tide reaches for a footprint, and Casper felt it find the edges of her—find the place where Casper Vasquez ended and everything she had ever stolen began—and pull.

It didn't hurt. That was the horror of it. It felt *wonderful*. It felt like setting down a pack after a hundred miles, like the first swallow of cold water, like sleep. Fifteen years of weight began to lift off her, memory by memory, and she heard herself make a sound—half laugh, half sob—and understood with perfect clarity that if she did nothing, if she simply stood here and enjoyed it, the door would drink her dry by moonrise and leave a smiling shell in gray robes, and the last thing to go would be her name.

*Casper.* Vessin's voice, sharp now, all patience burned away. *Step back.*

She stepped back. Her hand came off the wood with a sensation like tearing free of a web, and the glow dimmed—but it did not go out. It settled into a low ember-light, breathing, waiting, and the door was different now: the keyhole-space in the shape of her crooked hand had darkened, as though the wood had tasted her and marked the spot.

From the crater rim, a Hollower cried out—a thin mechanical sound with nothing human left in it—and all seven of them turned at once toward the door. Toward her.

"CASPER!" Ilsa's voice, high and furious, from the ridge. The lantern flashed twice, three times, four—*run, run, run*—and somewhere on the east slope Tam burst from cover and started sprinting a diversionary line through the grass like a glorious idiot, waving his arms and shouting something about hostile moths.

Casper didn't run. Not yet. Because the door was speaking—or not speaking, but *offering*, the way a shopkeeper offers, the way she herself had offered bottled forgetfulness to strangers in the night market for fifteen years. She could feel what it wanted laid out plain as a market stall:

*A little more. Just a little more of you. Your first kiss, thief—you don't need it. Your mother's lullaby—you haven't sung it in years. Give me a memory and I'll hold the light a moment longer. Give me enough, and I'll open.*

It was her own con. Turned inside out. All those years of taking small forgettable things from people who wanted to forget—and now a door was quoting her prices back to her, and the currency was her.

"No," Casper told it, and her voice shook, and she said it again louder. "No. You don't get to nibble. When I pay, I pay all at once—that's the deal, that's always been the deal—"

The door pulsed. Almost petulant. Almost *hungry*.

And beneath her feet, the warm stone of the crater floor shifted, and Casper realized with a lurch that the entire basin was beginning to glow, faintly, ring by ring, nine great circles rising through the rock like sunken lamps being relit one after another—and the Hollowers on the rim had stopped advancing, stopped entirely, standing rigid with their palms raised as though the light itself had grabbed them by the collars.

Vessin appeared beside her, solid as grief, and his face—for the first time since she'd known him—looked afraid.

"It's waking faster than it should," he said. "Because you're closer than you should be. Casper—the other eight will feel this. They will feel it *tonight*."

"Good," Casper said, and surprised herself, because she meant it. Somewhere in her chest the drowned guilt of fifteen years had finally surfaced, gasping, and it turned out that underneath the guilt had been anger all along. "Let them come. I stole from all nine of you, and I'm done paying in installments."

Above them the stars came out hard and cold, and on the crater's rim, seven gray figures stood unmoving as fence posts while the ninth ring kindled beneath the stone—and far away, at the edge of hearing, at the edge of the world, something ancient and enormous turned over in its long war-sleep and began, slowly, to wake.
