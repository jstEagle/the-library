## 14. Ilsa's Choice

Ilsa Vane had spent her whole life measuring things that could not be measured, and she had learned one truth above all others: the world was badly drawn.

Not carelessly. Badly *on purpose*. Every map was an argument, and every border a lie told with a straightedge. She had built her career on that lie, and she had built her grief on it too — because her daughter had died on the wrong side of a line that moved in the night, in a village that existed on Tuesday's charts and not Wednesday's, and no cartographer alive could tell her whether she'd buried Annet in Corvale or somewhere else entirely.

So when Casper Vasquez had come to her with impossible geography and a god in her ear, Ilsa had not hesitated. She had named her price, ugly and honest: carry my daughter's memory for me. Take it out of my head so I can walk again.

Casper had taken it. It lived now in a little glass vial against Ilsa's heart, warm as a sleeping bird, and every night Ilsa checked it the way other travelers checked their flint.

She did not regret it. That surprised her most of all. She had expected to feel hollowed already, half-emptied by the loss of it, but what she felt instead was *lighter* — like setting down a pack she'd forgotten she was carrying up a hill she'd forgotten she was climbing. Grief was heavy. Even shared grief was heavy. But grief carried by someone else, carried carefully, carried like something holy —

Well. That was almost bearable.

The three of them made camp that night in a fold of land that should not have existed. Ilsa knew this professionally. The valley appeared on none of her charts, which meant either it was new or it was very old, and out here past the borders, old and new were the same thing. The wheat grew in rows too straight to be wild and too crooked to be farmed, as if the land couldn't quite remember what farming was.

"Bad ground," Tam said, spitting into the fire. "Ground that doesn't know itself. You can feel it under your boots."

"You can feel it under my boots," Ilsa corrected. "You've been complaining about your feet since the borderlands."

"My feet are a barometer."

"Your feet are a liability."

Tam grinned at her — that easy, gap-toothed grin that had survived even his years of thieving, though nothing else about him had survived intact — and Ilsa felt the small, familiar warmth she'd been trying not to name for two hundred miles. Belonging, maybe. Or its drafty cousin.

Casper sat apart from them, as always, turning a sealed memory over in her fingers. The last one. The one Vessin had told her to open alone, and she hadn't yet, and the not-opening hung around their campfire like weather.

"You should sleep," Ilsa told her. "We move before dawn."

"I don't think I sleep anymore," Casper said, and there was a joke in it somewhere, but neither of them found it.

Ilsa took first watch anyway, because she slept least and complained most, and because someone had to count the stars and make sure they were all where they ought to be. Out here they weren't. There was an extra constellation shaped like nothing at all, and it watched her back.

*

It was the extra star that warned her.

She had been tracking it idly for an hour — watching it drift, which stars did not do, watching it *descend*, which stars especially did not do — when she understood what she was looking at. Not a star. A lantern. A Hollowing-lantern, white-flamed and cold, and beneath it, coming through the wheat in a wide silent crescent, figures in grey.

"Hollowers," she said quietly, and then, louder, with the full authority of a woman who had spent thirty years shouting at apprentices: "*Hollowers. Up. Now.*"

Tam came awake the way thieves do — all at once, hand already on his knife, eyes already moving. Casper came awake the way hunted things do, which was worse to watch.

"How many?" Tam breathed.

Isla counted the lanterns. Nine. Of course nine. Everything out here came in nines lately, like the country was taunting them.

"Nine lights. Crescent formation. They're herding us toward the ridge."

"The ridge is a dead end."

"The ridge is what they want us to think is a dead end." Ilsa was already rolling her charts, already stuffing them into her coat, because whatever happened next, the maps went with her. A cartographer without her maps was just a woman lost in a field.

Casper stood frozen, the sealed memory still clutched in her fist, and Ilsa saw it happen — saw the girl's face go through fear and arrive at something past it, some cold clearing where decisions get made.

"They're here for me," Casper said.

"They're here for all of us," Tam said. "Hollowers don't take appointments."

"They're here for me." Casper looked at Ilsa, and her voice dropped into something raw. "If I run east, they follow me. You two can go south. Back to Corvale. Back to—"

"If you say 'safety' I will hit you with a chart tube," Ilsa said.

"Ilsa—"

"Do you know how long it took me to find people worth traveling with? Thirty years of apprentices who rolled their maps wrong and colleagues who thought latitude was a personality trait, and then along comes a thief with a god in her skull and a footpad with a barometer for feet, and suddenly—" She stopped. Her hands were shaking. Not from fear.

From the memory rising.

Because here is what Ilsa had understood for days now, walking beside them, and had said to no one: the vial against her heart had been growing warmer. And the Hollowing-lanterns behind them had been growing brighter. And she had done the mathematics that cartographers are trained to do — distance over time, cost over benefit — and she had arrived at a number she didn't like.

They wanted the thief. But they would settle for anything the thief had touched. And the thief had touched everything Ilsa carried.

"Casper." Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "How much does a trade weigh? Your trades. When you sell a memory, when you take one — how much can you carry?"

Casper blinked. "What? Why—"

"How much?"

"A... a lot. More than you'd think. Why?"

Ilsa smiled. It felt strange on her face, like a map drawn upside down. "Because I've got something they want more than you."

"No," Tam said immediately. He'd followed the arithmetic faster than Casper had; thieves always could. "No, absolutely not, Ilsa—"

"It's just arithmetic."

"It's *you*."

"Yes," she agreed. "That's the point."

The lanterns were closer now. She could hear the wheat parting around grey coats, that soft shushing sound, like the country itself making way for them. Somewhere out in the dark, a Hollower spoke a word of unmaking, testing it, and the nearest stalks of wheat simply forgot they were wheat and became dust.

Casper grabbed her wrist. "Whatever you're thinking, stop thinking it."

"I'm thinking about my daughter," Ilsa said gently. "Which, incidentally, is exactly the problem."

*

Here is what Ilsa had never told them, because it had seemed beside the point:

When she'd given Casper the memory of Annet — her laugh, her terrible singing, the way she'd once redrawn her mother's entire atlas with crayons and improved it — Ilsa had kept one thread of it deliberately. A cartographer's trick. You always keep a reference copy. She had kept the sound of Annet laughing, folded small, tucked deep, and she had told herself it was for professional reasons.

It wasn't. Nothing about Annet had ever been for professional reasons.

And now that thread was burning in her chest like a lit fuse, and the Hollowing-lanterns were drinking its light across the field, and Ilsa Vane, who had spent her life measuring unmappable things, finally measured herself and found she weighed exactly enough.

"Listen to me," she said, and took both their faces in her hands, one cheek each, because fairness mattered even now. "In a minute, I am going to do something stupid and generous, and you are both going to want to argue. Don't. Arguing wastes light, and we need light for running."

"What are you going to do?" Casper whispered.

"I'm going to pay a toll." She laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of her, because of course, *of course* it would end like this, the woman who dealt in impossible geography paying passage with the only territory she'd ever truly owned. "Do you know what I figured out, out here? Borders aren't lines. They're prices. Everything costs something to cross. I just never thought I'd be the coin."

"Don't," Tam said, and his voice cracked clean down the middle. "Ilsa. Please."

"Oh, hush. You'll remember me. That's the trick of it — they can't take what I am to *you*. They can only take what I know." She pressed her forehead briefly to his, then to Casper's. "I won't know why I'm here. I won't know what we're doing or where we're going or why my heart keeps checking a glass vial like it's a sleeping child. But I'll know *you*. Do you understand? Memory is the map. People are the country. They can burn every chart I own and I will still find my way to the two of you, because some things don't live in the head."

"That's not how hollowing works," Casper said, crying now, furious about crying.

"It's exactly how hollowing works. I've read the reports. They empty the *why* and leave the *who*. Everyone thinks that's the tragedy." Ilsa wiped Casper's face with her thumb, briskly, the way she'd once wiped Annet's. "It's the mercy. Someone has to survive this journey knowing why we matter. Might as well be me, even if I won't know the reason."

Behind her, the first lantern crested the wheat.

She turned to face them. Grey shapes fanned out across the field, unhurried, patient as tide. The lead Hollower raised its lantern, and the white flame leaned toward Ilsa like a plant toward sun, tasting the bright burning thing inside her.

"South," she said clearly. "There's a fold in the land two hundred paces southeast — it isn't on any map, which means it's on mine. Run when I fall. Don't look back; it wastes time. And Casper —"

"Yes."

"When you open that last memory — the one you're strangling in your fist — remember that some of us give things away on purpose. It changes what they mean."

Then Ilsa Vane stepped forward, spread her arms like a woman greeting rain, and said, in a clear carrying voice, to the lantern, to the grey men, to whatever ledger they served:

"*Take the toll.*"

The white flame leapt.

It did not hurt. That was the strange, tender truth of it. It was like being read aloud — every memory of the journey lifting off her in pages: the night market where she'd met Casper, the argument about the extra star, Tam's feet, the fold in the land, the reason, the reason, the reason — page after page after page flying out of her into the light, and she stood in the middle of it feeling lighter and lighter and lighter, until—

Until she was standing in a wheat field at night, arms out, facing two strangers who were staring at her like the world was ending.

One of them was crying. The other had blood on his knuckles from punching a Hollower, apparently quite recently, though she had no memory of any Hollower at all.

"Well," said Ilsa Vane, lowering her arms, patting her coat to make sure her charts were there (they were; some instincts outlive everything). "You two look lost. Come on, then. I know a fold in the land southeast of here — it isn't on any map."

She was already walking. Behind her, she heard the big one let out a sound like grief and relief sharing a body, and the crying one laugh through her tears, and Ilsa frowned slightly, because she did not know these people at all.

Except that her feet, entirely without instructions, turned to check that they were following.

And when the crying one caught up and took her hand, Ilsa squeezed it back, warm and certain, and said:

"I don't suppose either of you knows my name? It seems I've gone and misplaced it."

"Casper," said the crying one. "Your name is Casper. No — sorry. *She's* Casper. You're Ilsa. Ilsa Vane. You draw maps."

"Ilsa Vane," Ilsa repeated, trying it on. It fit like an old coat. She smiled. "Good name. I must have earned it somehow."

The grey coats were shouting behind them. The two strangers ran, pulling her along, southeast, toward the fold in the land, and Ilsa ran with them, laughing at she-didn't-know-what, light as a woman with nothing left to carry but company.

*

They did not stop until the sky went grey at the edges. Then they collapsed in the hidden fold, breathless, safe by inches, and Tam — the big one, the one whose hands shook whenever he looked at her, which she chose not to remark upon — built a fire with the exaggerated competence of a man avoiding conversation.

Ilsa dug out her charts and began, out of pure habit, to sketch the terrain they'd crossed. Her pen slowed. Stopped.

"I drew this," she said slowly, staring at the contour lines. "This shading style. This is mine. I know my own hand." She looked up, troubled. "But I don't remember drawing it. I don't remember any of it — the borderlands, the villages, the..." Her hand drifted, without her permission, to the glass vial at her heart. Warm. Warm as a sleeping bird. She frowned at it. "Why do I keep touching that?"

Neither of them answered fast enough.

"Why," Ilsa said again, quieter, "does it feel like something I lent out?"

Casper opened her mouth. Closed it. And Ilsa watched the girl decide — watched her choose, with visible cost, not to fill in the blank.

"You'll remember when it matters," Casper said. "I promise. Just — not yet. Can you trust that?"

Ilsa considered her. This stranger with stolen eyes and a god-shaped shadow, holding a secret the size of Ilsa's whole missing year.

"I trusted you with something once," she heard herself say. "I don't remember what. But I trust you now, and I suspect that's the same thing wearing different clothes."

Casper made a small broken sound and hugged her, hard, and Ilsa held on, bewildered and certain, while beyond the rim of their hiding place the erased country stirred like something remembering itself — and far to the north, at the edge of the crater, the white door began, very faintly, to glow.
