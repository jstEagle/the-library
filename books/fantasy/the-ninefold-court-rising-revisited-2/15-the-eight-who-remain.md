## 15. The Eight Who Remain

I have waited fifteen years for this night, which is not so long for a god but felt longer than any of my centuries of waiting before it, because this time I had something to lose.

Let me tell you what it is like to watch a thief sleep.

She slept badly, as she always did near the door — the white door that was not quite a door yet, standing in the shallow bowl of the crater like a tooth in an empty jaw. She had made camp at its edge against all my advice, and Ilsa had agreed with her against all sense, and so there they were: two mortal women and a god who could do nothing but listen to them breathe, wrapped in blankets that smelled of woodsmoke and Ilsa's terrible tea.

Casper Vasquez dreamed, and in her dream she argued with me again. She was very good at arguing. Fifteen years of carrying my voice inside her stolen memories had given her plenty of practice.

*You knew,* she said in the dream, the way she had been saying it for three days now, since the final sealed memory opened and showed her the truth. *You put the theft in my hands. You picked me out of the crowd.*

*I picked you out of a burning courtyard,* I reminded her gently. *You were already stealing. You were stealing from a god, which took either tremendous courage or tremendous stupidity, and I have never been able to determine which.*

*Cowardice,* she said, which was unfair, and then she turned over in her sleep and muttered something about candles, because even in dreams the woman had commerce on the mind.

I loved her. I want that stated plainly, here, before everything else happens, before the sky opens and makes it difficult to think of anything except the end of the world. I loved her the way a lock loves a key — not sentimentally, not softly, but with the wholehearted relief of a mechanism finally fulfilling its purpose. And also sentimentally. Also softly. A god can hold contradictions; it is one of the few advantages of the position.

The door needed more of her. That was the arithmetic we had all been avoiding. It flickered when she touched it, guttered like a lamp low on oil, and each time it drank from her she came back a little thinner around the eyes, as if someone had sanded a layer off the woman and left the shape intact.

She would give it everything. I had known since the market in Corvale, since the moment I spoke her childhood name through a stolen prayer and watched her go pale and furious and curious all at once — curiosity winning, as it always did with her. Casper Vasquez was a hoarder of small mercies, and she would spend her entire hoard on a country she barely remembered taking.

Which is why, when the sky began to boil, I was afraid.

Not for myself. Gods do not die the way mortals die; the worst we suffer is being forgotten, and I had already survived that once. I was afraid for her, and for the cartographer snoring beside her with half a journey's worth of memory gone and no regret about it, and for the thin, stubborn thread of hope that had carried us three hundred miles into a country that maps kept refusing to admit existed.

They were coming. All eight of them. I felt them the way a house feels a storm coming up the valley — a pressure in the walls, a rattle in the glass.

"Casper," I said aloud, into the firelight. "Wake up. They're here."

* * *

She woke the way she always woke: instantly, silently, with a knife already in her hand. I have watched soldiers wake slower than Casper Vasquez waking from a nap she wasn't finished with.

"What," she said.

"The Eight." I poured myself into visibility beside the fire — a man-shaped convenience, grey-cloaked, faceless only when I chose to be, which tonight I did not. I let her see my face, because I wanted her to know I was afraid, and because I had learned that Casper trusted fear more readily than reassurance. "All of them. They've stopped fighting each other long enough to come stop you."

"I thought they couldn't leave their war."

"They can't. That's what makes this remarkable." I looked up. The stars over the crater were going out one by one, not behind clouds but *behind intentions*, occluded by things still deciding what shapes to wear. "A war between gods is not fought with armies, little thief. It is fought with attention. Each of them has spent fifteen years pouring every scrap of themselves into unmaking the others' claims. For all eight to look away from the battlefield at once—"

"Means they're more scared of me than of each other." She stood, brushing ash off her knees, and I heard the grin enter her voice before it reached her face. It was a terrible grin. It was the grin of a woman about to negotiate. "Good."

"That is not the word I would use."

"You'd use 'doomed.' You've used 'doomed' four times today. I counted." She nudged Ilsa awake with her boot, gently, the way you wake someone whose memories you are carrying in your pocket. "Ilsa. Company."

Ilsa sat up, pushed grey hair from her eyes, and looked at the darkening stars with the expression of a scholar encountering a citation error in the fundamental laws of reality. "That's not astronomically possible," she said, with mild interest. "Stars don't blink in sequence."

"These aren't stars anymore," I said. "They're eyes."

"Ah," said Ilsa, and reached for her tea.

* * *

They came down as light first. That is how gods arrive when they mean business — not in thunder, not in glory, but in a silence so complete that your own heartbeat sounds like trespass. The crater filled with it, eight colors bleeding up out of the ground where the Court had stood, and the white door at the center began to hum like a struck bell.

Then they wore shapes. Of course they did. Gods cannot bear to meet mortals without dressing for it.

Ashvael came as a mountain wearing a crown. Mereth came as a flood frozen mid-crash. The twins Oren and Oris came as two mirrors facing each other, showing everyone present a slightly better version of themselves. Sul came as winter, actual winter, a localized season with opinions. Kellivar came as a judge, robed and bored. Yara came as a harvest field burning in slow motion, forever almost-ashamed. And last, largest, most tired of them all came Brannoc, who had once been god of hearths and home and now looked like a house after a fire — still standing, nothing left inside.

Eight gods. Nine thrones, eight claimants, and the ninth seat empty because its occupant had begged a thief to hide him in her head while his siblings tore the country apart over who deserved to sit where.

"CASPER VASQUEZ." The voice did not come from any one of them; it came from the pressure itself, from the air being squeezed by eight wills agreeing on exactly one thing. "THIEF OF THE NINTH DOOR. GIVE BACK WHAT YOU CARRY AND BE UNMADE QUICKLY. WE ARE NOT CRUEL, BUT WE ARE THOROUGH."

"Thorough," Casper repeated, conversationally. "He says thorough, Vessin. That's generous. I'd have gone with 'hasty.'"

"Do not provoke them."

"I'm not provoking. I'm stalling. There's a difference and it's called tactics." She stepped forward, into the light, arms crossed, five feet of unremarkable woman standing before eight manifestations of divine fury, and I swear on my empty throne she looked *bored*. "You want your memory back. Fine. But you should know it comes with interest."

"SPEAK."

"Fifteen years of compound interest," she said. "Every prayer answered in secret. Every village that prayed itself back real. Every border that shifted because the land couldn't forget you even when the maps did. You didn't erase yourselves when the Court fell — you just stopped being *responsible* for anything. And the people kept believing anyway. Sloppy work, gods."

The mountain shifted. Winter deepened by a degree. Somewhere in the twin mirrors, two better versions of Casper flinched on her behalf.

"YOU DARE ADDRESS US—"

"She dares," I said, stepping up beside her, because there are limits to how long a god can watch a mortal pick a fight alone without joining in. It's a matter of professional courtesy. "She dares because she is right, and you all know it, which is why eight of you who cannot share a country managed to share a journey here."

And then, because it was time — because the door was humming, because the truth had been mine to carry as long as hers — I told them.

* * *

"You fell," I said, "because you could not share."

The light went very still. Even gods, it turns out, do not enjoy hearing the diagnosis spoken plainly.

"I watched it happen. I sat the ninth throne and I watched eight siblings who had built paradise together decide, one by one, that paradise was too small. Ashvael wanted the highlands consecrated. Mereth wanted the rivers freed of Ashvael's consecration. Kellivar wanted law, Yara wanted growth, Sul wanted rest, the twins wanted everything duplicated, Brannoc wanted simply to be left alone — and every want was reasonable. That was the horror of it. No villain. No madness. Just nine thrones and eight appetites and a country only large enough for one of each."

"WE KEPT THE PEACE FOR A THOUSAND YEARS," rumbled the mountain.

"You kept a truce for a thousand years. Peace is different. Peace means wanting less. None of you wanted less." I spread my hands. "So when the wars began — small ones, polite ones, prayers redirected, blessings withheld — the country cracked along the seams of your grievances. And I understood before any of you did that if the Court fell, it would not fall to enemies. It would fall to family. And that whatever rose from the rubble would be eight kingdoms at permanent war, forever, with mortals as the soil you farmed."

"WELL?" The judge's voice, dry as old parchment. Kellivar had always been the one who asked the questions everyone else avoided. "WHAT DID YOU DO ABOUT IT?"

"What I did," I said, "was find a girl in a burning courtyard who was stealing from me while her city came down around her ears, and I judged — correctly — that a thief with no allegiance, no name worth keeping, and no respect whatsoever for gods was the only creature in the Ninefold Court capable of doing what needed doing."

"You hid yourself," Casper said quietly. It wasn't a question. She'd had three days with the memory; she knew its shape. "You had me steal the Court — all of it, the whole country's memory of you, the whole *country* — and you hid inside the theft."

"A wound stops bleeding when it is forgotten," I said. "I could not stop my siblings from warring. But I could make the prize disappear. No Court, no throne, no reason to fight. Let them exhaust themselves against each other over nothing. Let the land heal under a new sky with no gods in it. And wait — wait for the one person alive who knew where the treasure was buried, and hope she would someday choose to bring it back."

The silence afterward lasted eleven seconds, which for gods is roughly a geological age.

It was broken by Ilsa.

"So let me summarize," she said, wrapping both hands around her cup of terrible tea as though eight apocalypses were a mildly unusual weather pattern. "Nine gods. One country. The ninth god solved the problem of his siblings' war by having a pickpocket steal reality itself, thereby erasing a nation from existence, including — and I want to be clear I'm following correctly — including everyone in it, or at least everything they remembered being."

"The people remained," I said. "Unremembered by their own history. They rebuilt. They became Corvale. They made new lives in a country that had forgotten it was ever theirs."

"And the plan was always to eventually undo that."

"The plan," Casper said, before I could answer, "was always for *me* to undo it. With my whole self. He just neglected to mention the invoice until after delivery." She turned and looked at me, and her eyes in the godlight were bright and hard and — this is the part I did not expect, the part I will carry longer than my name — *not surprised*. "But here's the thing, Vessin. Here's the thing I worked out somewhere around the third sealed memory. If he'd asked, I'd have said no. Anyone would. So he didn't ask. And I've spent three days being furious about it." She looked back at the Eight. "And then I realized: you eight would have done worse. At least he gave the country back a chance. You lot gave it a war."

"THE THIEF LECTURES THE HEAVENS," said winter.

"Somebody has to," said Casper. "You clearly haven't listened to each other in fifteen centuries. So listen to me instead, because I'm the only neutral party here — I robbed all nine of you equally." She raised her chin. "Here's my offer. And before you refuse it, remember that I'm the door. The door doesn't open unless I pour myself through it, and I don't pour myself anywhere for gods who won't deal."

* * *

What she offered them, she offered loudly, in the teeth of eight kinds of divine weather, and I will not spoil the terms by summarizing them poorly here — they deserve their own telling, and she will tell them better than I would, because Casper Vasquez negotiates the way rivers carve canyons: patiently, irresistibly, and with an eventual result that looks inevitable only in retrospect.

What I will record is what happened while she talked.

Brannoc, god of hearths and home, the one who had only ever wanted to be left alone, knelt down. A mountain of a god folding himself small at the edge of a crater, to be nearer the level of a mortal woman listing her demands. He did not speak. He had perhaps forgotten how; he had not been addressed gently since the fall. But he listened the way the starving watch bread being broken, and when Casper glanced at him mid-sentence — mid-*demand*, really, she was demanding — he nodded, once, slowly, like a house settling.

And Sul, who was winter, softened by one degree. And the twins, who were mirrors, briefly showed everyone not better versions of themselves but true ones. Small movements. Cracks in eight armored egos. But I had watched these beings refuse compromise for a thousand years, and I knew what I was seeing.

The war was ending. Not because they had grown wise. Because a thief had made restitution sound like common sense, and because fifteen years of being forgotten had done what fifteen centuries of warfare could not: it had made them lonely.

The door hummed louder. It needed her. It had always needed her, and the Eight could feel it drinking at the edges of their awareness, and their fear of it curdled slowly into something adjacent to hope.

"IF WE AGREE," said the pressure-voice, and it was smaller now, more voices in it, less thunder, "WHAT GUARANTEE—"

"You get *me*," Casper said. "That's the guarantee. I'm a thief, not a liar. Ask anyone whose kiss I stole — I always return what I take. Eventually. When I'm good and ready." She smiled that terrible grin. "Now. Do we have terms, or do I stand here all night? Because I'll stand here all night. I once haggled over a candle for six hours."

The Eight drew together above the crater, eight lights becoming one argument, and I felt the shape of eternity tilt.

Casper leaned toward me, not taking her eyes off them, and murmured, "Vessin. If this works — if they say yes — how much of me is going to be left?"

"Enough," I said.

"Liar."

"The best kind of liar," I said. "One who intends to be proven right."

Above us, the eight lights stopped arguing, turned as one, and began to descend — and I saw in the foremost of those lights, in Ashvael's ancient mountain glare, not wrath but a question, and the question was aimed past Casper, past the door, directly at me, and it was:

*Brother. Was it worth it?*

I did not have an answer yet.

I suspected, watching the thief roll up her sleeves, that I was about to get one.
