## 16. Every Name I Ever Stole

The eight gods of the Ninefold Court did not arrive so much as *remember themselves into being*, which was somehow worse.

One moment the crater's rim was empty — just ash and the patrol of Hollowers frozen mid-stride like clockwork toys someone had forgotten to wind — and then there were eight figures standing in a circle around us, and the sky above them had eight different opinions about what color it should be.

Casper stood very still at the edge of the white door's flicker and did what she had always done best in impossible situations: she took inventory.

*One thief,* that was her. *One cartographer who no longer remembered why she'd come but had refused to leave.* Ilsa stood a few paces behind her, arms crossed against the cold, watching the gods with the frank professional interest of a woman surveying terrain she intended to map later, if anyone could explain to her why it mattered. *One god of doors,* Vessin, standing between her and the rest of them like a wall made of patience. And *eight gods who had spent fifteen years at war over a country that didn't exist.*

"Well," said the god of harvests — or Casper assumed he was the god of harvests, because wheat was growing out of his footprints in real time and also his coat appeared to be woven from bread — "the door is trying to open."

"It is not," Vessin said. "It is breathing."

"It is *breathing*," repeated a tall woman whose hair moved like water finding its level, "because the thief has been feeding it."

Eight faces turned to Casper.

She had been looked at by angry clients before. She had been looked at by Hollowers, by magistrates, by one memorable innkeeper who'd caught her lifting the memory of his mother's lullaby out of a locket. None of it prepared her for being looked at by divinity in octuplicate. It was like being x-rayed by weather.

"Before we do anything," Casper said, "I want it on record that I was invited here. By him." She pointed at Vessin. "Whatever this is, it's technically his fault."

"Casper." Vessin's voice was gentle and exhausted, the voice of a man who has been waiting fifteen years for a meeting and dreading it in equal measure.

"No, I mean it. I'd like minutes taken. Is there a god of minutes? There should be a god of minutes."

The god of harvests blinked. Somewhere in his beard, a small bird nested.

*Good,* Casper thought, and felt the old familiar thing rise in her chest, the thing that had kept her alive through every raid and every close call since she was nine years old: the certainty that even cornered, even outnumbered, there was always a deal to be struck if you could just find the seam. She was a thief. Thieves survived by knowing what people wanted badly enough to trade for.

She just had to figure out what eight gods wanted badly enough to let the world keep existing.

* * *

They had come for the door, obviously. That much Vessin had explained on the long road from the village of Ashenmere, in the low urgent voice he used when the truth was too large for volume: the other eight believed that if the Court rose again, the war would resume where it left off. Fifteen years of stalemate — an entire country quietly deleted rather than let any single god hold it — and now the thief had gone and found the hinge.

"They will not destroy you," Vessin had said. "You are the only vessel the Court's memory fits inside. Destroying you would be destroying it forever, and none of them can bear that. But they will take you apart looking for another way in."

"Take me apart how?"

"You've seen what the Hollowers do."

Casper had thought about Tam Reeve, smiling his empty smile at the crowd while they cheered, and had not slept well after.

Now, standing in the crater with the sky arguing overhead, she watched the eight gods regard her the way scholars regard a locked box. The tall woman with the river-hair drifted closer. When she spoke, her voice arrived slightly before she did, like thunder with ambitions.

"You are the one called Casper Vasquez. That is not your first name."

"No," Casper agreed. "But it's the only one I paid rent under, so I'm fond of it."

"You stole from us." The river-woman's voice carried grief the size of a coastline. "Fifteen years ago, on the night the Court fell, something walked into our sanctum and took the whole of it — our country, our worshippers, our names from the mouths of the living. We have been gods without a nation. We have been *words without mouths.* Do you know what that is, thief?"

"I steal memories for a living," Casper said. "So yes. Actually. I do."

Something flickered across the goddess's face — surprise, maybe, or the beginning of an unwelcome respect.

"We searched for the theft," said a god whose skin was quarried stone, each sentence arriving with the finality of a landslide. "We tore the world along its seams. The Court was simply *gone*, folded away so cleanly that even we could not follow the fold. And now it breathes again, through you." His stone eyes narrowed. "How."

Casper opened her mouth.

"He asked me to," she said instead, and pointed at Vessin again, because if she was going down she was going down with company.

The change in the crater was immediate and total. Eight divine attentions swung to Vessin with the weight of falling architecture. The temperature dropped. The wheat god's little bird fled.

"Brother," said the stone god, very quietly, "what have you done."

Vessin, to his eternal credit, did not flinch. He stepped forward until he stood beside Casper, shoulder to shoulder, and Casper realized with a small vertiginous jolt that he was positioning himself between her and them not as a shield but as a *co-signer*.

"The Court was dying," Vessin said. "Not from outside. From within. Nine gods, one country, and not one of you willing to share a single worshipper. The war had already begun — you all remember the first century of it, the villages burned over whose name got spoken first. I did not stop the war. No one could stop the war. So I did the only merciful thing left." He spread his hands. "I hid the patient from the doctors."

"You erased a *country*," snarled the stone god.

"The war would have erased it *loudly*. With everyone awake." Vessin's voice cracked, just barely, and Casper heard beneath it fifteen years of solitary vigil. "I found a child with a thief's gift and a thief's conscience, and I asked her to carry the Court somewhere no god could reach it — including me. Including *me*, brother. I sealed my own knowledge of it inside her. I have spent fifteen years as ignorant as a mortal, waiting at a door I couldn't remember why I built. Do not speak to me of cost."

Silence. The kind of silence that has tectonic implications.

Casper cleared her throat.

"So," she said. "Everyone caught up? Great. Because I have a counteroffer, and I'd like to make it before somebody decides to smite somebody."

* * *

Here was the thing about stealing, which Casper had never told another living soul: you learned things about people that they didn't know about themselves. You learned what a grudge weighed. You learned that a first kiss was mostly nerves and the smell of someone's laundry soap. You learned that the memories people most wanted rid of were almost never the ones causing their pain.

And you learned, above all, that taking was easy and giving back was the hard part.

She looked out past the crater's rim, toward the dark horizon where Corvale glowed like a banked fire. Fifteen years ago, when she'd folded the Court away, the world hadn't ended. It had done what worlds do — it had healed crooked and kept going. People had rebuilt. New cities on land that used to be the Ninefold's heartland. New lives stacked on top of the absence like houses built over a filled-in well. Ilsa's maps, shifting nightly. Ilsa herself, who had given up the memory of her dead daughter to walk this road, and who was currently standing behind Casper with no idea why, trusting her anyway.

If she gave everything back — if the Court rose and the old country flooded home onto every map — what happened to the new world? To the people sleeping tonight in towns that had never existed in the Court's time, holding children who'd been born into lives the old gods had never sanctioned?

That was the question underneath the question. That was the seam.

"I know what you want," Casper said to the eight. "You want your Court. You want your names back in living mouths. You want your *country*. And here's the part you don't want to hear: you can't have all of it clean. The world grew over the wound. Pull it open without care and you bury thousands of people who never wronged any of us."

"They are squatters on sacred ground," began the stone god.

"They're *people*," Casper snapped, and her voice came out louder than she'd meant, ringing off the crater walls. "People with new memories. A baker in Corvale remembers her grandmother teaching her to knead — a grandmother who never existed in your country, who existed because the world needed a grandmother there. Take the Court back and erase those memories, and you're doing exactly what I did. Exactly what the Hollowers do. You'll be thieves. Divine ones, with better lighting."

The river-goddess tilted her head. "Then what do you propose, Thief of the Ninth Door?"

Gods, she hated that title. And loved it. Both at once, which was probably the most honest thing she'd felt all year.

"I propose a bargain." She made herself breathe. Her heart was going like a pickpocket's feet. "Every memory I stole — the whole Court, all of it, every name and street and harvest-song and temple bell — I give back. Freely. Completely. I open myself and pour it out until there's nothing of it left in me and the Court stands whole again on its own ground."

Vessin went rigid beside her. "*Casper.*"

"But." She held up one finger, the gesture she'd used on a thousand clients to stop them agreeing too fast. "The new memories stay. Every life built in the last fifteen years stays in the heads that earned it. Your country comes home *around* them, not through them. The baker keeps her grandmother. The mapmakers keep their maps. You get your nation back and the world gets to keep the scar tissue. Both true at once. That's the deal."

"That is not possible," said the stone god flatly. "Memory does not divide so neatly. The land remembers—"

"The land," Casper said, "will remember what I tell it to remember. Because I'm the one holding it." She swallowed. Here it came, the part that terrified her more than the Hollowers ever had, and she said it anyway, out loud, in front of gods. "There's a catch. Obviously. I'm a thief; there's always a catch. The Court's memory doesn't live in a jar I can unstopper. It lives in *me*. Fifteen years, it's grown into me like roots into a wall. To give it back, I have to be emptied. All the way. Not just the Court — everything I am is tangled up in it. My name. My mother's face. The smell of rain." Her voice wobbled and she hated it and pushed through. "I go into that door hollow, and I come out the far side as nobody. Someone new. No debts, no stolen goods, no history worth prosecuting."

The crater was silent except for the white door, breathing.

"And in exchange," Casper finished, "you swear — all nine of you, sworn on your own names, which I'm told is the only oath that binds a god — that the restored Court leaves the new world alone. No reclaiming. No punishing. No holy wars over whose worshipper kneels where. You rise, and you *share*, or the door stays shut and I die in it with your country still folded in my chest, and you can spend eternity explaining to the dark how you lost it twice."

She stood there, shaking slightly, having just threatened eight gods with a hostage situation involving her own soul.

Ilsa, behind her, said quietly, "I don't know who you are. But I think I'd have liked to."

* * *

The gods conferred.

They did not confer in words, which was fortunate, because Casper suspected the words would have melted her eyebrows off. They stood in their circle and the air between them thickened and shimmered, and the sky cycled through eight seasons' worth of moods, and the wheat god wept golden tears that sprouted where they fell. It lasted perhaps four minutes. It felt like a geological era.

Vessin leaned close. "You understand what you're offering."

"Emptying myself into a hole in the world while eight deities watch? Yeah, it came up."

"You may not survive as anything. The person who walks out the far side might be no one at all. Or worse — someone with nothing, in a body that remembers suffering without remembering why."

"Yeah," Casper said softly. "But here's what I know, Vessin. I've spent my whole life carrying things that weren't mine. Other people's kisses, other people's grudges, other people's gods. And it turns out the biggest thing I ever stole wasn't even for me — it was for *you*, you secretive bastard, and neither of us got to choose honestly." She looked up at him. "This is the first honest choice I've ever made. I'm not giving that up because it might hurt."

Vessin looked at her for a long moment. Then he did something she'd never seen him do: he laughed, short and helpless, like a man realizing the punchline of a joke fifteen years overdue.

"You know," he said, "of all the thieves in all the world, I chose a nine-year-old girl because she had the gentlest hands I'd ever sensed. I told myself it was practicality." He shook his head. "It was never practicality."

"Aww," said Casper. "Don't make it weird."

The circle broke. The eight gods turned as one, and the stone god spoke for them, his landslide voice softened to gravel:

"The Ninth Door's terms are heard. Restitution offered. Conditions named." A pause like a mountain deciding. "We accept — *if* the vessel survives the return. If she empties truly, and the Court rises whole, the oath holds. If she fails halfway..." His stone eyes were almost kind. "Then we take what remains. By force, if the door allows."

"Then pray for me," Casper said. "I hear it works now."

The river-goddess made a sound that might, in a lesser being, have been a laugh.

Vessin turned to her, and his face was the door's face, and the door was glowing brighter than it had ever glowed, drinking in her decision like light through a lens. "It will need all of you, Casper. Every memory, given willingly, in order, oldest first. It will be—" he searched for a word and failed to find a gentle one "—it will be like being unwoven."

"Okay." Casper rolled her shoulders. Cracked her neck. Did the little breathing exercise she did before every job, the one that had never once calmed her down but marked the occasion. "Oldest first. Which means..."

She felt it before she said it. Deep in the cellar of herself, behind the seals she'd never dared touch, the oldest memory stirred — the first thing she'd ever taken, on a night of fire and falling bells, when a god's voice had said *gently, child, gently* —

"—the night of the Court," she whispered.

Behind her, Ilsa reached out and gripped her hand, brief and fierce, a stranger's blessing. "Whoever you are," Ilsa said, "come back as someone good."

"No promises," said Casper Vasquez, for the last time, and stepped toward the breathing door.
