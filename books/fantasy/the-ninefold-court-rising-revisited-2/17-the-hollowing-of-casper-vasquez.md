## 17. The Hollowing of Casper Vasquez

The white door stood in the crater like a tooth in a wound, and Casper Vasquez had come to pay for it.

Not in coin. Not in blood. The Hollowers circling the rim of the pit would have taken blood gladly—had taken plenty from Tam Reeve and a hundred others whose names the city no longer remembered—but this door wanted something rarer. It wanted her hoard. Fifteen years of stolen weather, bottled and corked against her ribs, humming now like a hive that had finally smelled spring.

"Tell me again," she said to Vessin, "the part where I survive this."

"You survive it," he said. His voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the way it always did when he was frightened enough to stop pretending at embodiment. He wore his borrowed shape tonight—a lean man in a traveler's coat, gray at the temples, hands that looked like they'd held things gently once and never learned to stop. "The shape survives. What you are after this—" he paused, and she appreciated that he didn't lie to fill the pause— "after this, you get to choose."

"Choose what?"

"What remains."

* * *

She knelt at the door's threshold and took out her charms.

They were small things, mostly. That was the art of it, the pride of it—the whole craft of Casper Vasquez built on the principle that nobody misses what's tiny. A first kiss, lifted off a boy on Tallow Street who'd been desperate to be rid of the nerves. A grudge, sour as green plums, taken from a widow who slept better without it. The smell of rain on hot stone, which she'd stolen from no one in particular because it had been sitting in the open air, unclaimed, and theft seemed kinder than letting it evaporate.

She uncorked the first one.

The grudge went first, because grudges are heavy and she was tired of carrying other people's. She pressed the little vial to her lips the way Vessin had taught her—not drinking, exactly, more like exhaling into the glass—and then turned it upside down over the door.

The memory poured out of her instead.

That was the trick of willing hollowing: you didn't give the memory away, you gave yourself *into* it. The widow's grudge unfurled through her chest—her sister's name, the will, the winter neither of them spoke through—and Casper held it the way you hold a bird with broken wings, and then she let go, and the door drank.

Stone that had been dead for fifteen years warmed under her palms.

"Oh," said Vessin softly, behind her. "There you are."

* * *

The second charm was the kiss.

She had kept it longer than any client's property ought to be kept, and she'd told herself for years it was professionalism—inventory discipline, nothing more. Lying to yourself is easy work when your whole trade is making people forget what they felt.

The kiss tasted of stolen cherries and someone else's nervousness, and when it left her she felt it go all the way down her arms and out through her fingertips into the door, and the door *shivered*, and somewhere in the crater's dark the Hollowers stopped moving, every one of them at once, like hounds catching a scent too old to hunt.

"Casper." Vessin crouched beside her. His borrowed face was doing something complicated. "You should know what happens now, so you can't say I tricked you twice."

"You're going to tell me the order matters."

"The order matters. Whatever you pour out last is what holds you together longest. Save the ones that make you *you*. If you spend them early—"

"I'll unravel before I finish."

"Yes."

She looked at her little collection spread across her skirt. Fifteen years of careful, cowardly, magnificent thievery. And beneath the small charms, wrapped in oilcloth, the nine sealed ones—the memories she'd taken the night the Court fell, the ones with gods' names stitched through them like veins.

And beneath those, at the very bottom of the bundle, one thing that wasn't stolen at all.

*Later,* she told herself. *Everything else first.*

* * *

She spent the smell of rain on hot stone, and the crater filled with the scent of a summer storm fifteen years gone, and somewhere above them actual clouds began to gather, because the sky had forgotten weather could feel like that and wanted to try again.

She spent a baker's memory of his mother's hands kneading dough. She spent an argument between brothers about a fence line, resolved at last by being given to a door. She spent the color of the sea, which she had stolen off a sailor in a tavern for the sheer wicked joy of it, and the water pooling in the crater's low places turned briefly, impossibly, salt-blue.

Each one took more than the last. Not the memories—they came out easy, eager, homesick for their owners. It was *her* that got thinner. By the twentieth charm she was noticing gaps. She reached for the name of the street she'd lived on at nineteen and found a smooth blank place where it should have been, like a tongue finding a missing tooth.

"That one wasn't yours to keep anyway," Vessin murmured, and she couldn't tell if he meant the street or herself.

* * *

Somewhere past midnight—Ilsa's voice, from the crater's edge, counting Hollowers—"Four moving west. They're waiting, Wren. They know what she's doing. They know what it means."

Ilsa had started calling her Wren three villages ago, some half-remembered affection from a journey she'd sold most of, and Casper had stopped correcting her. There was something in being named wrong by someone who stayed anyway.

"They'll come when I'm nearly empty," Casper called back. "That's when I'm easiest to finish. So don't let them."

"We won't," said Ilsa, and Tam's old knife caught starlight in her hand, and Casper loved her so much it hurt, and she made a note to spend that feeling last of all.

* * *

Then the sealed ones.

She unwrapped the oilcloth and the nine vials woke up all at once, singing, and the crater rang like a struck bell. Down in the dark, the door began to glow through its cracks, gold bleeding out like yolk from a broken shell.

Vessin put his hand on her shoulder. "One at a time. Say the name as you pour. The name is the key and the payment both."

"Aurenth, of the First Door."

She opened the first vial and the country came home.

Not around her—in her. Fields she'd walked once as a girl thief fleeing a burning court, wheat the color of struck brass, a river with nine names depending which bank you stood on. She poured it into the door and the door drank and beyond the crater's rim, faint as breath on glass, hills appeared where there had been flat scrubland an hour ago. Ilsa made a sound like a woman watching a miracle or an eclipse; with Ilsa it was hard to distinguish.

"Seryn of the Second Door. Omma. Kel. Vereth." Each name a vial, each vial a province, each province a grief, because she remembered now—she had always remembered, she'd just been standing on the memory to keep it shut—she remembered the war. Eight gods tearing one country apart because none of them could bear to share it, and a young thief in the smoke offering the only solution anyone had thought of: *hide it. Hide all of it. Give it to me.*

"You didn't cause the fall," Vessin said quietly, reading her face the way he always could. "You were the bandage. The wound was already there."

"Bandages get thrown away."

"Bandages get *thanked*," he said, "by anyone with sense," and there was such fierce tenderness in it that she had to look away and pour the sixth name before she cried and diluted anything.

* * *

By the eighth vial she was mostly ghost holding a lantern.

She knew this because she could see the crater floor through her own knees. Interesting. She filed that away under things to mention to whoever she became, and then realized she might not have a whoever, and laughed—a thin sound, like wind through an empty house, which was fair, since that's what she was.

"Casper." Vessin was kneeling in front of her now, both hands on her face, holding her together the way you hold water in cupped palms. His borrowed shape was flickering, spending itself to anchor her. "Last one. The ninth. You know what it is."

The Ninth Door. Her door. The memory she'd never dared open, the one with her own beginning folded inside it—the night a god had knelt in the ruins of his family's court and asked a terrified girl to carry everything, and she had said yes, and the saying-yes was the oldest thing she owned.

"If I pour it out," she said, "there's no me left to walk through."

"There's no you left either way. The question is only what walks out the far side." His thumb traced her cheekbone, gathering light. "I can keep one thing. One only—it's how much I am, now, it's all the room in my hands. Your name. Your truest one, the one you haven't spoken since the Court fell. Or the memory of this—of us, of the road, of Ilsa swearing at horses. Choose."

And here is the thing about Casper Vasquez, the thing the whole city had missed for fifteen years while she sold forgettable weather in a market stall: she had never once, in her entire long career of taking, kept anything for herself. Every charm she'd ever lifted had gone back to someone eventually. Even her invisibility had been a kind of gift—everybody else got to keep their pain; she just carried the receipts.

"No," she said.

His face did something terrible and hopeful.

"Keep nothing. Hold nothing back for me." She smiled, and her smile was coming apart at the edges, luminous. "I've been a hiding place my whole life, Vessin. Let me find out what I am when I'm not hiding anything."

* * *

The ninth vial she didn't pour. She simply opened her hands and let it go, and it went through her, and she went with it.

The door opened.

Gold flooded the crater, and the country flooded the map, and eight distant presences that had been screaming toward them across the night suddenly went quiet, sated mid-scream, full for the first time in fifteen years. Somewhere Corvale's borders snapped taut around a landmass that had never actually stopped existing, only been folded, and cartographers in a hundred cities would wake tomorrow with ink stains and headaches and maps that had grown a heart.

Casper stood at the threshold, nearly empty, held together by a god's two hands and the last unspent thing in her—the one item at the bottom of the bundle, the one that had never been stolen.

It was small. It was a single memory, her own, freely made: standing in this exact spot, choosing this.

She looked at Vessin. He looked back, wearing his borrowed face like a man wearing his heart outside his coat, and beyond him Ilsa was shouting something joyful and profane at the rising light.

"Well," said Casper Vasquez—or the shape that had been her—"open the door. Someone new is waiting on the other side, and I'd hate to keep her."

The threshold brightened. Vessin opened his mouth—to argue, to bless, she'd never know—

And the Hollowers, remembering themselves all at once, came down into the crater with knives made of nothing.
