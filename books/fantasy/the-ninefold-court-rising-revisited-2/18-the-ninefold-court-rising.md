## 18. The Ninefold Court Rising

I had waited a long time for this moment, and I will tell you honestly: I spent most of it watching her hands.

Not the crater. Not the eight gods circling above it like storm-crows with crowns. Not the sky, which had begun to remember colors it had no business remembering — a green along the horizon that belonged to orchards drowned fifteen years ago, a gold threading the clouds like light through honey. I watched Casper Vasquez's hands, because they were shaking, and because she was trying to hide it, and because in fifteen years of loving a thief I had learned that the truth of a person lives in what they try to conceal.

"You're doing it wrong," I said.

"I'm doing it *exactly* right," she said. "You just don't like how it looks."

She stood at the lip of the crater with her palms open and her hoard pouring out of her — that was the only word for it, *pouring*, as though someone had tipped her sideways and everything she had ever stolen was running out of her like water from a broken jug. Fifteen years of small charms. A first kiss. A grudge. The smell of rain on hot stone. The sound of a mother's laugh through a wall. Nine great sealed memories, each one a country's worth of morning. She gave them back one by one, and with every memory that left her, she grew fainter at the edges, the way a candle grows fainter as it drowns in its own wax.

She was right, of course. She was doing it exactly right. I simply did not like how it looked, because I am a god, and gods are permitted their small dishonesties.

* * *

Let me tell you what returning memory looks like from my side of things, since no one has ever asked and I have been waiting an age to say it.

It looks like weather.

The first charm she released — the smell of rain, the oldest small thing she owned, older even than the theft itself, something she'd taken as a girl before she knew what she was — went up out of her like steam off summer pavement. And the air over Corvale, forty miles distant, changed. I felt it change. Farmers who had never once in their lives thought about the old country stopped mid-chore and stared west, homesick for a place they could not name, weeping without knowing why, and then laughing at themselves for weeping, and then going back to their chores with the laugh still caught in their chests like a fishbone of joy.

That was one charm. One small, forgettable thing.

Then she opened the nine.

* * *

The first name was Aureth, who kept the gates of morning, and when Casper spoke it — she had to speak them now, there was no other way to pour a sea out of a cup — the eastern sky split open with a dawn that arrived out of order, out of season, out of *obedience*. The Hollowers on the crater's rim fell to their knees. Some of them wept. A few of them laughed, which frightened me more; hollowed men do not laugh, and yet something in them remembered how, the way a riverbed remembers water.

The second name was Marenne-of-the-Tides, and the dry riverbeds of the borderlands filled overnight, though no cloud had passed overhead. Fish appeared in them. Old fish, patient fish, fish that had been holding their breath for fifteen years and were extremely put out about it.

I could give you all nine names and all nine wonders, but here is the one that mattered, the one I want you to carry:

With every name she spoke, the war paused.

My siblings — the Eight Who Remain, the eight proud, grieving, furious powers who had torn the Court apart because none of them could bear to share a country with the others — stopped mid-blow. They hung above the crater in their terrible aspects, fire and frost and the slow grinding weight of mountains, and they *watched her*, this small gray woman with shaking hands giving back what she had taken, and I saw something move across their faces that I had not seen since the fall.

Shame. And beneath the shame, worse and better both: *hunger*. The hunger of the starved at a table suddenly, impossibly full.

"She is emptying herself," said Ossian, whose aspect is winter and whose voice is the creak of glaciers, "to feed *us*."

"She is restoring what was stolen," said Yara, whose aspect is plague and whose voice is kinder than you would expect, which is its own horror.

"She is doing," I said, "what none of us were able to do."

They turned to me then, all eight, and I confess I enjoyed it more than a god should. Fifteen years I had been the forgotten one, the ninth name struck from the roll, the coward who hid his country inside a thief rather than watch his siblings burn it. Now they looked at me and could not decide whether I was the wisest of them or the worst, and I have built a long existence on being exactly that ambiguity.

"The Court rises," I told them. "The question is what you do when it does."

* * *

Here is what the rising looked like from the ground, which is where the truth of things always lives.

Casper spoke the ninth name — her own door, the Ninth Door, the name she had carried sealed so long it had grown into her like a splinter into wood — and the crater answered.

Stone moved. I do not mean it crumbled or shifted; I mean it *moved*, deliberately, the way a sleeper turns toward a familiar voice. From the bottom of that scar in the earth rose first a single white door, standing upright on nothing, exactly as it had stood in the stolen memory she'd fled with across the rooftops of Corvale. Then around the door rose walls. Then towers. Then bridges that connected to nothing and would, within the hour, connect to everything. The Ninefold Court came up out of the earth like a breath held too long finally let go, and it came up *hungry for its country*.

And the country answered.

I have described the borders of Corvale shifting nightly, the land forgetting itself, villages becoming wheat fields between one dusk and the next. What I did not say — what Ilsa Vane's maps had only whispered, what the priestess of the praying village had known in her bones — was that the land was not forgetting. It was *waiting*. Every field that had ever belonged to the old country had stayed exactly where it was, folded flat beneath the new world like letters beneath a floorboard. And now the Court rose, and the letters came open, and the map of the world tore gently down the middle and made room.

The erased country flooded back the way floodwaters return to a dried lakebed: fast, total, indifferent to what had been built in the meantime. Roads unspooled toward towns that had been ruins yesterday and were whole by moonrise. Bells rang in steeples that had been rubble at breakfast. People woke in Corvale with two sets of memories settling over them like snowfall — the life they'd built, and the life that had been taken — and here is the miracle I will spend the rest of eternity trying to deserve: most of them did not scream. Most of them sat up in bed, held both lives in their hands like two halves of a broken bowl, and began, quietly, to fit them together.

Because that was the bargain she had struck. Her defining act, the act I will remember her by when I have forgotten the faces of my own siblings: *you may have your Court back, but the people keep what they built.* Restitution without erasure. A thief's justice — take from the takers, never from the poor.

The Eight felt it settle over them, the returned weight of worship, of morning-gates and tide-halls and hearths lit in their names again. And one by one — Ossian first, because winter is honest even when it is cruel — they laid down their war.

Not surrendered. Laid down. There is a difference, and gods know the difference, even if men rarely do. They set their grievances down the way a traveler sets down a pack too heavy to carry another mile, and they looked at the risen Court, and Yara said, in her kind, dreadful voice:

"Who taught the thief mercy?"

"No one," I said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

But I promised you cost, and I will not flatter you with a cheap miracle.

Come back to the crater's edge. Come back to her hands.

By the time the last tower cleared the earth, Casper Vasquez was nearly gone. I do not mean wounded. I mean *nearly gone* — translucent at the wrists, thin as smoke at the shoulders, a woman-shaped suggestion holding itself together out of habit and stubbornness and, if I am honest, out of me. Because while she poured, I held. That had always been the design, the terrible engineering of it: a god cannot carry the Court himself, but he can hold a thief together while she spends herself, the way a hand holds a coin being paid out.

She was lighter than I had ever held her. Lighter than the night market, lighter than the rooftops, lighter than the night she'd first heard my voice speak her childhood name out of a stolen prayer and hadn't run, which still astonishes me. Curiosity. Of all the things that survived fifteen years of hiding, curiosity had been the load-bearing wall.

"Vessin." Her voice was already half echo. "Is it done? Did it work?"

"Look," I said.

She looked. At the Court rising silver-white against the remembered dawn. At the Eight descending slowly, warily, like great birds coming in to land on a branch they aren't sure will hold. At the horizon, where the drowned orchards had turned the air green. At the far side of the crater, where Ilsa Vane stood — Ilsa, who had traded away her memories of the journey and yet had followed us anyway, following not the why but the *whom*, because belonging outlasts memory — and Ilsa raised one hand, not in greeting exactly, more in the manner of a cartographer confirming a landmark. *There you are,* the gesture said. *I don't know why, but there you are.*

Casper made a sound that was half laugh and half something else.

"It worked," she said. "Of course it worked. I'm very good at my job."

"You are," I said. "You were always — "

"Don't." She held up a hand that I could nearly see the stars through. "Don't eulogize me while I'm standing here. It's rude."

So I didn't. I did what I had promised myself I would do, the one thing I had kept back through all of it, the single coin clutched in a fist while the whole treasury poured away: I held onto her. Not her memories — those were spent, given back, scattered across a country waking up whole. *Her.* Whatever a person is after you subtract everything they remember. It turns out to be very little and very much: a stubbornness. A warmth. An instinct, when the world asks her to be afraid, to make a joke instead.

I held that, the way you cup a flame in wind.

And the threshold stood before her — the white door, the First Door, the Ninth Door, all the doors being one door tonight — open now, breathing out the scent of a country reborn, waiting for the thief who had hidden it to come home.

"Casper," I said, and her name in my mouth was almost more than she had left to answer to. "The door is open."

She turned to look at it. And I saw — gods help me, I saw it plain — that she was not looking at the door the way a survivor looks at shelter.

She was looking at it the way a prisoner looks at an open gate, and past it, at the wide ordinary world beyond, where nobody knew her face and nobody owed her a history and the night markets were just opening their lanterns.

"Vessin," she said slowly, in the voice she used when she was about to steal something, "what happens if I walk *through*?"
