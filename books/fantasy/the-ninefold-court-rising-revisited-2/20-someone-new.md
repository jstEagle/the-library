## 20. Someone New

The door was white, and it was not a door.

That was the first thing Casper understood as she stood before it with nothing left inside her but the shape of a person. A door implies two sides. This had one side and then everything else, folded like a letter waiting to be opened.

Vessin's presence wrapped around her—not hands, exactly, not arms, but something that remembered what hands were for. *You don't have to do this,* he said, and his voice was gentler than any voice had ever been with her, because she could no longer remember whether anyone had ever been gentle with her at all.

"Yes I do," Casper said. Her own voice sounded strange. Thin. Like a borrowed coat. "I gave everything else back. It would be rude to keep the last of me."

*It wouldn't be rude. It would be human.*

"I've never been very good at that."

She felt him laugh—or something adjacent to laughter, the way a mountain might be adjacent to a hill. Then, quieter: *I will remember you. All of you. Every version. The girl who stole small things because she thought she deserved only small things. The woman who bargained with gods. The hollowed thing standing in front of me right now, terrified and stubborn about it.*

"Stubborn is the only part worth keeping," she said.

*Then stubborn it is.* A pause, vast and careful. *Walk through, Casper Vasquez.*

"That's not my name anymore."

*No,* Vessin agreed, warm as banked coals. *But it was. And someone ought to have kept it somewhere safe.*

---

Stepping through a door, Casper discovered, felt like being born, if being born were done politely and by appointment.

There was no falling, no light, no choir. There was simply a moment when she was on one side, and then a moment when she was on the other, and between those moments something had been gently removed from her—though she couldn't say what, because whatever it was, she no longer had it.

She stood in an alley in Corvale. She knew this because the alley told her so: the smell of frying onions from the Night Market three streets over, the particular gray of Corvale stone, the sound of a cart wheel that needed grease. The city poured itself into her like water into an empty cup, filling all the spaces where a life used to be.

Except there was no life there. She checked. Twice.

She knew what rain smelled like. She did not know why she knew that, or whether she'd ever stood in it. She knew how to count coins by touch, how to read a crowd for exits, how to tell a lie so smooth it felt true in her own mouth. These skills sat in her like furniture in an unfurnished house—useful, present, belonging to no room in particular.

She looked down at her hands. They were a thief's hands, calloused in the right places. Whose thief? Hers, apparently. But the *her* was a technicality.

"Oh," she said aloud, to no one. "Oh, that's what empty feels like."

*Not empty,* said Vessin, from everywhere and nowhere, soft as dust settling. *New.*

"It feels empty."

*Empty is just new with bad marketing.*

Despite everything—the crater behind her, the country flooding back into existence somewhere east of here, the nine gods laying down their war like tired children setting down heavy toys—Casper laughed. It came out rusty and surprised her on the way out.

"I'm talking to a god in an alley."

*You've done worse in alleys. I remember.*

"You would," she muttered. "Somebody has to."

---

The Night Market was in full swing when she reached it, and Casper stopped at its edge like a swimmer testing cold water.

Lanterns strung between stalls. The smell of spiced wine and hot oil. A woman arguing cheerfully about the price of eels. Children darting through the crowd with the specific lawlessness of children after dark. Everything ordinary. Everything continuing, as though a country hadn't been erased and restored overnight, as though the sky hadn't briefly filled with nine enormous presences agreeing to stop.

Most people didn't know. That had been the bargain. The people who rebuilt their lives in the fifteen years without the Court got to keep them—their memories, their marriages, their grudges, their small charms. Only the land itself remembered, and the land, Casper was learning, was discreet.

She walked into the market and nobody looked at her.

This should have been comforting. For fifteen years—in some other woman's life, a life Casper now knew about only the way you know about a book you read long ago—invisibility had been the whole art. Be forgettable. Be small. Be a charm-seller with a stall and a smile and nothing underneath.

Now nobody looked at her because there was genuinely nothing to look at. No history. No reputation. No debts, no enemies, no one who owed her a favor or wanted her head. She passed a fruit stall and the vendor glanced up, saw a stranger, and dismissed her in half a second. It was the most thorough erasure imaginable, and it had been administered by Casper herself, voluntarily, and it felt—

It felt like taking off boots after a very long walk.

She sat down on the steps of a fountain she didn't remember but which remembered plenty, apparently, because the water tasted faintly of old prayers when she cupped it in her hands. Around her the market hummed. Somewhere a fiddle. Somewhere a couple having the low, intense argument that precedes either a breakup or a proposal.

*How does it feel?* Vessin asked. He had been quiet since the alley, watching, the way he'd promised to watch. Not hovering. Just... present, the way weather is present.

"Strange," Casper said. "I keep reaching for things that aren't there. Not sad things. Just... I went to worry about something and there was nothing to worry about. I went to feel guilty and there was no guilt. There's all this room." She turned her cupped hands over, letting the fountain water fall. "I don't know what to do with all this room."

*Fill it slowly,* Vessin said. *That's my professional advice. I'm a god of doors; we believe in thresholds. You don't have to be anyone yet. You get to be no one for a while. It's underrated.*

"No one," Casper repeated. She tested the word like biting a coin. Fifteen years she'd been afraid of being no one. She'd stolen memories by the fistful partly, she suspected, just to prove she existed—that someone, somewhere, had thought her worth robbing. And now here she was: no one, officially, by choice, sitting on a fountain step eating a plum she'd bought from a startled vendor, and the world had not ended.

The plum was excellent. That seemed important to note. Whatever else had been emptied out of her, her ability to appreciate a good plum remained fully intact.

"The Hollowers," she said suddenly. "Are they—"

*Gone. Disbanded this morning. Their whole purpose was policing the absence of magic, and the magic came home. Some of them are reportedly very lost. One of them opened a bakery.*

"A bakery?"

*He makes excellent rolls. I remember. I'm remembering everything now—it's most of what I do. Would you like to hear about your funeral?*

"My—" Casper nearly dropped the plum. "I had a funeral?"

*A small one. Tam Reeve organized it. He cried the entire time and insisted everyone take home candles. Ilsa Vane attended even though she couldn't remember why she was there—she just knew she'd lost someone, and she decided the feeling was trustworthy. She was right.* A pause, gentle. *They buried an empty box on the hill above the city. Tam said some very kind things about you. You'd have liked hearing them. I can recite them whenever you want.*

Casper found she was crying. It took her a moment to identify the sensation—tears were unfamiliar the way a language is unfamiliar after years of disuse—but once identified, they came freely, and she let them, sitting on the fountain steps in the lantern light, weeping over a funeral she'd attended as an empty box.

"They think I'm dead."

*They think you're dead,* Vessin agreed. *Is that a problem?*

She considered it. Really considered it, turning it over with her new, uncluttered mind.

Tam Reeve, alive, hollowed and then un-hollowed when the Court returned—he'd been restored along with everything else, every stolen person poured back into themselves. He was out there somewhere lighting candles. Ilsa Vane was out there missing a daughter and trusting the shape of her grief. Neither of them knew that the woman they'd mourned was sitting forty feet from the eel stall with plum juice on her chin.

"They're better off," Casper said at last, and found it was true. "If I walk up to Tam and say 'hello, it's me, also I caused the fall of civilization and then dissolved myself,' that's a lot to put on a man who already cried at one funeral this month."

*So this is the choice,* Vessin said softly. *You could go back. Knock on doors. Say my name and watch them remember you—I could give them every memory of you, restored, tonight. You'd be Casper Vasquez again by morning. Or you stay new. And they grieve someone who isn't gone, and you live free of a name that was mostly fear anyway.*

"That's a terrible bargain."

*It's the same bargain you made for everyone else,* Vessin said. *They keep their new lives; you keep yours. I only held the door. You wrote the terms.*

Casper sat with that. The fountain murmured. The fiddle player launched into something faster, and a child spun past her, laughing at nothing, at everything.

"Stay new," she said. Quietly, but without trembling. Mostly without trembling. "Let them keep the grief. Grief means they loved me. I'd rather they have that than have me back and lose the loving part."

*You're sure?*

"No," Casper said honestly. "Ask me again tomorrow."

*I'll ask you every day for a hundred years if you want.*

"Don't you dare. Let a woman eat her plum."

---

She wandered after that, because wandering was free and she had nothing else scheduled.

Corvale at night, seen fresh—which was the only way she could see anything now—was a miracle of small lights. She watched a lamplighter work his way down the canal road and thought: *I don't know if I've ever watched a lamplighter. Maybe I have. Maybe I did it every night for fifteen years. Isn't that strange? Either way, it's lovely.* Every experience came to her twice: once as itself, and once as a mystery of whether it was the first time.

She caught herself doing thief-things without deciding to. Reading exits. Noting which guards were bored and which were attentive. Clocking a cutpurse working the wine stalls—a kid, twelve maybe, clumsy, about to get caught—and before she'd consciously formed a plan, her feet had carried her over, and she'd stumbled into the boy, spilled his mark's drink apologetically, and murmured "left pocket, move now" through a friendly yawn.

The kid vanished. The mark never noticed. Casper stood in the crowd afterward, astonished at herself.

"I still know how to do that," she said.

*You'll always know how to do that,* Vessin said. *Skills aren't memories. They're deeper than that. Like rivers—you can drain the lake, but the riverbed remembers which way the water ran.*

"Was that a metaphor or a threat?"

*From me? Both. I'm a complicated god.*

She laughed again. Twice in one night. She was keeping count, because counting things was apparently a pleasure when you weren't counting sins.

Near midnight she found herself at the top of the terraced hill where the market district met the residential quarter, and she stopped, because below her, at the corner of Lantern Row, stood a small stone building with a dark window and a sign she could just make out:

**CHARMS & CANDLES — CLOSED BY DEATH OF PROPRIETOR**

Someone had left flowers against the door. A lot of flowers. And among them, dozens of little candles in glass jars, burned down to stubs—people had been coming here to leave them, night after night. A city's worth of small remembrances for a charm-seller whose name most of them probably hadn't even known until the funeral.

Casper stood looking at her own shop—someone else's shop now, or no one's—for a long time.

*We can walk another way,* Vessin offered quietly. *There's always another way.*

"No," she said. "I want to look."

She went down and stood before the door. Through the window she could see shelves, dusty, and the counter where she'd weighed out bottled forgetting for people who needed it. On the counter, someone had left a card in a careful hand. She pressed close to the glass to read it.

*Tam's handwriting,* she realized. *I know that from—* and then the knowledge slipped away, because the memory it lived in belonged to the woman in the ground, and Casper was only borrowing her eyes.

The card said: **Thank you for the small mercies. — T.R.**

And beneath it, in different ink, shakier: **For the candle that let me sleep after the funeral. Whoever you were, you were kind. — A stranger**

Casper put her hand flat against the glass. The glass was cool. Behind it, her whole former life gathered dust in the dark, and none of it hurt, and that was the strangest thing of all. She had expected this moment to hurt.

*Well?* Vessin asked.

"She was kind," Casper said. "That stranger. The one who owned this shop." She smiled, and it wobbled, and held. "I hope wherever she is, somebody's remembering her properly."

*One somebody,* Vessin said. *At minimum. Forever. That was the deal.*

"That's a terrible deal for you. I'm nobody."

*You're my favorite nobody,* said the god of doors. *I waited fifteen hundred years in an empty country for you. I can afford to be sentimental about the return on investment.*

---

Dawn found her on the roof of the tannery—she hadn't chosen it; her feet had, and her feet clearly had opinions about rooftops—with the city turning gold beneath her and the eastern horizon glowing over hills that, she now understood, hid a restored kingdom full of waking villages and nine gods learning to share.

She hugged her knees and watched the light come and felt the terrifying, wonderful lightness of having no past. Anyone she met today would meet her exactly as she was. No reputation preceded her. No sin trailed her. When she spoke, her words would be judged only as words, not as the words of the Thief of the Ninth Door, the woman who broke a country, the saint or monster depending on which priest you asked.

She was, for the first time in possibly her entire existence, innocent. Not forgiven—innocent. There was a difference, and the difference was everything.

"So," she said to the morning, to the god woven through it. "What does a person with no past do first?"

*Whatever you want,* Vessin said. *That's the whole answer. It's a short religion. I think it'll catch on.*

"I should get a job."

*You should get a job.*

"Not thieving."

*Not unless you want to. Though I notice your feet disagree.*

"My feet are reformed."

*Your feet picked a lock on the tannery door to get up here.*

"That was a fire exit," Casper said with dignity.

*It was a padlock.*

"A locked fire exit."

His laughter moved through the sunrise like warmth through floorboards. And under the laughter, steady as a heartbeat, the promise he'd made at the threshold: *I will remember you. When no one else can, when you yourself can't, when the world sees a stranger—I will see you. Always. That's what I'm for now. It's a good job. Better than waiting in a crater.*

Casper Eriksen—no. That wasn't it either. Casper Nothing. Casper Plum-on-the-Fountain-Steps. She'd need a name eventually. Names were how the world held onto you, and she'd spent one whole lifetime slipping out of the world's hands, and perhaps it was time, just this once, to be holdable.

But not yet. Not this morning. This morning she was no one, and no one was the freest person in Corvale.

She stood, brushed off her trousers, and looked out at the city—her city, a city she'd never seen before, a city she'd saved, a city that had buried her under a hill of candles.

Below, the Night Market was packing up for the day. And she noticed—because a thief notices, because some things live deeper than memory—that one stall near the fountain stood empty. Small. Good foot traffic. A faded awning, a scarred counter, a hook for hanging wares.

The vendor was rolling up his mat and calling out to a passing friend: "Aye, it's available again, poor woman who had it died, no family, no one to claim it—"

Casper looked at the empty stall.

The empty stall, in the way of certain objects in a world freshly stitched back together by a god with a sense of humor, seemed to look back.

"Huh," said Casper, who was no one, who was new, who had absolutely nothing planned for the rest of her life. "Huh."
