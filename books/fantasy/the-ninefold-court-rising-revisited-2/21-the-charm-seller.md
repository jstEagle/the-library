## 21. The Charm Seller

The stall had been a fishmonger's once, and before that—according to the woman at the spice cart who remembered everything about everyone because remembering was her whole personality—a fortune-teller's, and before *that*, nobody knew, which Casper felt was fitting. She rented it for four coppers a week from a landlord who asked her name, wrote it down wrong twice, and gave up.

"Doesn't matter," she'd said. "I'll answer to most things."

Her name, the one she'd chosen herself, off a list of names she'd made up in an afternoon because choosing had turned out to be the single most luxurious activity available to a person: Wren.

It wasn't clever. It wasn't a reference to anything. She had not stolen it from anyone, inherited it from anyone, or been called it by any god in any field in any life. A wren was a small brown bird that sang louder than its size suggested and mostly wanted to be left alone. She had read about them in a book she bought with money she earned honestly, which still gave her a small private thrill, like getting away with something.

She sold candles.

That was the whole business. Candles. Beeswax and tallow, some scented with lavender, some with the smoke-and-cedar smell she'd spent three weeks learning to make without cheating, which is to say without buying a memory of cedar smoke off anyone, because she didn't do that anymore. She poured them in the mornings in the little room above the stall, burned her thumb on the wax pot regularly, and swore words that would have scandalized a god if gods were easily scandalized, which hers, thankfully, was not.

*Hers.* That was a word she was still careful with. Possessives in general were careful territory. But some evenings, when the candle smoke curled just so, she felt watched in a way that was less like being hunted and more like being *kept* — the way you keep a letter you can't throw away. And on one memorable occasion, entirely unprompted, all nine of her candle flames had bent sideways toward the door at once, and a voice like warm stone had said, in tones of profound grievance:

"You're out of the good honey ones."

"They sell fast," she'd told the empty room. "People like honey."

"I liked honey first."

"Then buy some next time instead of haunting my stockroom."

He hadn't answered. He never did, when she talked back. Vessin, god of doors and thresholds and apparently sulking, had made her one promise and kept it absolutely: he remembered her for everyone else. What he did *not* do, as far as she could tell, was drop by for tea. Gods were terrible guests. They ate all your metaphors.

So: candles. Four coppers a week. A sign she'd painted herself, slightly crooked, reading WREN'S — and then nothing after the apostrophe, because she'd run out of paint and decided the mystery was good marketing. The woman from the spice cart had squinted at it and said, "Wren's what?"

"Wren's," said Casper.

"That's not a sentence."

"It's an invitation."

* * *

The night market of Corvale had changed in small ways since the Court came back. Not dramatically — there had been no thunderclap, no mass conversion, no one walking around in robes. The Ninefold Court rose into the world the way water rises into dry ground: quietly, capillary by capillary. People lit candles now where they used to buy charms. They prayed in the old shapes without knowing they were old shapes, cupping their hands around flames the way their great-grandmothers had. There was a shrine going up by the tannery, three stones stacked by children, and nobody could explain why it felt right.

And charms — bottled memories, stolen forgettings, the whole gray trade Casper had lived inside for fifteen years — had gone strange. The bottles still worked. But sometimes the memories inside them woke up, the way hers had, and started calling out to their owners, and a client would come back to a charm-seller demanding a refund because his purchased grudge kept whispering his mother's name at him.

Half the charm stalls had closed. The other half had moved to selling luck knots and love powders, which were frauds but honest frauds, and Corvale respected that distinction deeply.

Casper had considered, briefly, whether the world needed another charm seller who actually refused to steal anything, and had concluded that the world needed exactly one, and it might as well be the only person alive who understood the trade from the inside.

Besides, she was good at it. Invisibility was a skill, and skills didn't vanish; they just changed jobs. She could still read a face across a crowded market. She could still tell grief from guilt from boredom at forty paces. Fifteen years of studying what people wanted to forget had taught her, as a side effect, exactly what people wanted to keep.

They wanted to keep the small things. That was the discovery that had rearranged her. Everyone came to the market wanting to hold onto the small things — the smell of a kitchen, the weight of a sleeping child, the sound of a specific laugh. The big memories, the weddings and funerals, those people carried fine. It was the small ones that slipped through their fingers like water, and it broke their hearts.

So she sold them vessels for the small things. Candles, mostly. A candle burned for six hours, and while it burned, the room smelled like the thing you loved, and you got to sit inside the remembering instead of running from it. She'd learned that trick from the inside too — from fifteen years of carrying the smell of rain in a bottle, never daring to open it, until the night she finally did and wept for an hour over weather.

The first week, she'd sold eleven candles. The second week, thirty. By the third week, the spice-cart woman was telling everyone that the new girl at WREN'S made candles that "put your own life back in front of you, but nicer," which was, Casper thought, better advertising than anything she could have paid for.

* * *

On a mild evening in the fourth week, with the market strung in lantern light and the air smelling of frying dough and woodsmoke, a man stopped at her stall.

She saw him coming, the way she always saw them coming — the particular walk of someone circling a decision. Middle-aged. Clean hands. The look of a clerk or an accountant, someone orderly whose orderliness had recently failed him. He picked up a candle, put it down, picked up another, and read the crooked sign twice.

"You're the one who used to do charms," he said. Not a question. "Before."

Casper set down her wax knife. "I never did charms here. I've been here five weeks."

"You have her hands." He said it flatly, like reporting a measurement. "The thief. Vasquez. You have the same way of standing, like you're listening to a room through the floor."

Casper looked at him for a long moment. There had been a time — not long ago, though it felt like a previous geological era — when this moment would have meant flight. Rooftops. Adrenaline. A name spoken in a voice that shouldn't know it.

Now she just felt tired in an old, familiar place, and curious in a new one.

"Sit down," she said, and pulled out the stool customers used.

He sat. Up close he had the raw eyes of a man who hadn't slept properly in weeks. "My daughter," he began, and then stopped, and pressed his knuckles to his mouth, and tried again. "My daughter drowned. Two years ago. The canal, in spring. And I—" His jaw worked. "There are sellers in the east row. They bottle forgetting. I went last month. I paid twelve silver for a charm that was supposed to take the worst night. And it worked, that's the thing, it *worked*, I couldn't remember her face at the end anymore, the water part, I couldn't see it—"

"But," said Casper gently.

"But she started talking to me." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious. "In my sleep. Not nightmares — *her*. Saying Da. Saying Da, why does the house smell like someone erased me? And I smashed the bottle, and it all came back, worse, because now it came back knowing I'd tried to throw it away." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, angry at himself for the tears. "Everyone says pray now. Pray to the Nine, they'll hold it for you. But I don't know how. I don't know the names. I never learned the names, nobody my age learned the names, and the priests at the new shrine talk like you should already know them, like you should already—"

"Breathe," said Casper.

He breathed.

She reached under the counter and took out a candle. Not a fancy one — plain beeswax, unremarkable, except that she'd made it on the morning after she'd opened her own sealed hoard, when her hands had known things her mind was still catching up to. She didn't know what was in it, exactly. Something of the Court's patience. Something of the way the restored country felt when you stood very still in it: held.

"What's her name?" she asked.

"Hetta."

"How old?"

"She was nine."

Casper lit the candle with a spill from her lamp and set it between them. The flame stood straight and calm. "Here's what I can do," she said. "I can't take it. The memory, the night, any of it. If someone offered me twelve silver to hollow out that hurt, I wouldn't, and you shouldn't let anyone else either, no matter how much it screams at three in the morning. Grief isn't a splinter. It's a load-bearing wall. Take it out and the whole house comes down, even if you can't see the house anymore."

"That's not comforting."

"No. But here's what is." She nudged the candle an inch toward him. "You burn this tonight, in the room where it's worst. And you say her name while it burns. Just the name. You don't need the nine names, you don't need rites, you don't need to know how to pray — Hetta will carry it up for you. Children are good at that. Better than the rest of us. We get embarrassed; they don't."

He stared at the flame. "That's it? That's your grand magic? Say her name?"

"The oldest one I know," said Casper. "Everything else is decoration."

His mouth twisted — half a scoff, half a sob, the exact expression of a man discovering that the thing he'd dismissed as nonsense was the thing he'd been starving for. "How much?"

"For that one? Nothing." She held up a finger before he could argue. "Not charity. Trade. You come back in a week and tell me if the house smells like her again. That's the payment. Stories are legal tender at this stall."

"And the others?" He nodded at the rows of candles. "If it works."

"Then you pay full price like everyone else, and complain about it, and buy three more. That's commerce." She wrapped the candle in brown paper, folded it into his hands, and closed his fingers over it. "Say her name tonight. All night if you have to. The walls can take it."

He stood, clutching the paper bundle like contraband, and paused at the edge of the stall. "You really aren't her. Vasquez. The thief everyone talks about. The one who stole a whole country and gave it back."

"No," said Casper. "I'm nobody. Ask my landlord — he's given up writing my name down."

But he was already looking past her, at the candle flame, which had bent — all nine flames along the stall bending together, gently, unmistakably, toward the man with the paper bundle — and straightening as he walked away.

Casper waited until he was out of earshot.

"Show-off," she said.

*You said his daughter carries prayers well,* said the warm-stone voice, everywhere and nowhere, smug as a cat in cream. *I merely agreed.*

"You bent my merchandise."

*Your merchandise is beeswax and a good wick. Everything else was always mine.*

"Then invoice me." She picked her wax knife back up and went back to trimming, because gods could sulk and glow and speak through fire, but they could not trim a wick, and somebody had to keep the lights on. Literally. Somebody had to keep actual physical lights on, in an actual stall, in a city that had almost forgotten how to burn things for joy instead of forgetting.

Vessin laughed — she felt it more than heard it, a low rumble under the market noise, the sound a threshold makes when it's happy.

* * *

Business was steady through the evening. A young couple bought a lavender pair and bickered charmingly about whose room they'd burn them in. An old woman bought the cedar-smoke kind and held it to her chest like a returned letter, and Casper didn't ask, because some stories you let arrive on their own schedule. The spice-cart woman came by to report that the accountant with the candle had told the entire east row about "the priestess at WREN'S," and Casper had to talk her out of repainting the sign to say PRIESTESS, because the mystery was good marketing but accuracy was bad theology.

"He called you a priestess," the woman insisted. "That's free advertising."

"I sell candles. I am aggressively not clergy."

"Mm. And yet." The woman tapped her nose, which was her universal gesture for *I know everything and will share none of it*, and rolled back to her cart.

It was near midnight when the last customer of the night appeared — a girl, maybe sixteen, hood up despite the mild air, hovering at the edge of the lamplight the way Casper had hovered at edges her whole first life. Casper knew that hover. She'd invented it.

"We're closing soon," she said, kindly. "But I'll stay open for you."

The girl came forward slowly. Under the hood: sharp eyes, chapped hands, the specific hungry wariness of someone who has been invisible for a living. A thief's eyes. Casper's old eyes, looking back at her from a younger face.

"You're the one who doesn't steal," the girl said. It came out accusatory. "Everyone says. The candle woman who won't touch memories. Even the east-row charm sellers say it, and they say it like it's disgusting, but they say it quiet, like it's also—" She stopped, jaw tight. "Like it's something."

"It's something," Casper agreed. "What do you need?"

"I don't need anything." Which was a lie, delivered badly, by an amateur. The girl's eyes flicked to the counter, to the lockbox beneath it, to the shadows behind the stall — casing the place, out of pure habit, and hating herself for it. "I heard — people say you used to be someone. Before. That you know what it's like when the things you took start waking up. When they call you by names you never—"

She stopped dead. Went pale.

Because every candle on the stall had leaned toward her at once. Not flickering — *leaning*, nine small flames tilting like flowers toward sun, and the air over the counter went warm and dense and attentive, the way a room gets when something vast leans in close to listen.

"Oh," whispered the girl. "Oh, no. Did I— I didn't mean to—"

"It's not you." Casper's voice came out steadier than her hands. She laid her palm flat on the counter, over the wood, the way you'd press a hand to a restless animal. "Easy," she murmured, to the flames, to the warmth, to whatever was listening. "She's not yours. Nobody here is anybody's."

The flames straightened, reluctantly, like a dog told to stop begging at the table.

The girl hadn't run. That was interesting. That was very interesting — a frightened thief's first instinct is always the rooftops, and this one had planted her feet. Casper looked at her properly, and under the hunger and the wariness she saw something else, something that made her chest ache in a place she didn't have a name for yet: recognition. Not of a face. Of a *shape*. A kid carrying more than she could hold, doing the math every night on whether tomorrow was survivable, and doing the thieving that math required.

"Sit down," Casper said, and pulled out the stool.

"I haven't asked for anything."

"You will. Sit anyway. First one's free — candle or conversation, your pick."

The girl hesitated. Then she sat, perched on the edge like a bird ready to bolt, and pulled back her hood, and said, in a voice that shook only a little:

"The thing I want to ask — how do you give it back? What you took. Everybody says you can't, everybody says once it's in your head it's yours till you're hollowed, but the stories say—" Her eyes came up, fierce and terrified. "The stories say somebody gave a whole country back. So how? How do you start giving things back?"

Over the stall, all nine flames stood perfectly, reverently still.

Casper reached for a candle — the plain one, the beeswax one — and began, carefully, to wrap it in brown paper, while somewhere behind the night sky a god who remembered her for everyone else waited to hear what she would say.

"Well," said Wren, who had once been no one, and was now someone with a stall and a crooked sign and a story worth trading, "it starts with saying a name. Whose do you want to begin with?"
