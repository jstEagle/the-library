## 22. Remembered

The stall had been hers for six weeks, and in that time she had learned three things about selling candles.

First: people bought more when the wicks were crooked. Straight wicks looked manufactured, and manufactured things made Corvale nervous. Crooked ones looked *made*, by hands, by someone who might be having a slightly bad day, and there was nothing the city trusted more than a stranger's small imperfection.

Second: never sell a candle to someone crying. They wanted the flame for what they could burn away, and Casper—she was not Casper anymore, but the woman behind the stall had not yet decided what to call herself, so the market had done it for her, and the market called her Wren, because of the birds that nested in her awning—Wren had learned that grief should not be handed fire.

Third: the smell of rain could be put into wax. Not stolen. *Put.* There was a difference, and the difference was the whole of her new life.

She was trimming wicks at dusk when the first customer of the evening arrived—a boy of maybe ten, holding a coin too large for whatever he intended to ask.

"You're the memory lady," he said. "The old memory lady down the row says you used to do charms."

"The old memory lady," Wren said, "is a gossip with excellent hearing." She set down her knife. "What did you want to forget?"

"Nothing." He said it too fast, which meant something. He looked over his shoulder the way children do when they've rehearsed being brave and are running out of rehearsal. "I wanted to know if you could keep one. Just—keep it safe. My gran's dying. Everyone keeps saying *remember her*, like remembering is easy, like you just hold on. But I can already feel it going soft. Her laugh. It's going soft."

Wren's hands went still over the candles.

Once, this would have been the easiest work in the world. She would have touched his temple, drawn the laugh up like thread from a spool, sealed it in glass, and sent him home lighter. She had built fifteen years on exactly that transaction. She had built a country's disappearance on it, though she hadn't known that at the time, and some nights—the ones when the awning birds were quiet—she still woke with her palms aching from the weight of everything she'd poured back into the world.

"I don't steal memories anymore," she said gently.

"I'm not asking you to steal it. I'm asking you to *save* it."

And there it was. The distinction a child could make in one breath that she had spent a lifetime failing to see: taking and keeping were different verbs. A thief emptied. A keeper filled.

Wren reached under the counter and brought out an unmarked jar—not one of her bottled charms, those were long gone, every last one returned or spent—but a plain preserve jar she'd cleaned and kept for reasons she hadn't examined until now.

"This won't take the memory out of your head," she said. "It'll make a copy. An echo. You'll still have your gran's laugh, and someday it will go soft in you, because that's what memories do when we carry them in bodies. Bodies are terrible jars." She smiled. "But this one will stay loud. And when it goes soft in you, you come back, and I open it, and you remember how it sounded."

"That's not magic?"

"It's wax and glass and a trick I know." Which was true. It was also, quietly, a small miracle—the kind that had crept back into the world through the cracks the Ninefold Court had left open behind it, thin as candle smoke, harmless as a crooked wick. Magic wasn't outlawed anymore. It was licensed, inspected, taxed. Corvale had responded to the return of the gods the way Corvale responded to everything: by forming a committee.

The boy pressed his big coin across the counter. Wren pushed half of it back.

"Crooked wicks cost a copper," she said. "Echoes are free. Keeper's rule."

He didn't understand, but he grinned anyway, and when she laid two fingers against his temple and drew the sound of his grandmother laughing into the jar—warm, cracked, ending in a wheeze that was clearly a family inheritance—he laughed too, because it was impossible to hear it and not.

"There," she said, sealing the lid. "Name it. Jars hold better when they're named."

He thought hard. "Gran's Terrible Laugh."

"*Wonderful* Laugh," Wren corrected, and wrote it on the label herself.

* * *

The market closed late these days. Since the Court rose—since the crater at Harrow Field became a palace of white doors and the maps had to be redrawn twice in a single season—Corvale had become a city of insomniacs. People came out at night to look at the sky, where sometimes, if the wind came from the east, you could see the light of the restored country bleeding over the hills like a second dawn that refused to commit.

Wren packed her unsold candles into crates and let the birds have the awning, and walked home through streets that no longer felt like a hiding place.

That was the strangest part, she thought. Not the god, not the door, not even the hollowing—that had hurt less than expected, more like pouring out a jug than like dying. The strangest part was that she had spent her whole first life learning invisibility as a discipline, mastering it, priding herself on it, and now she could not walk four steps without someone nodding to her. The fruit seller. The lamplighter. A Hollower, once—one of the gray-masked enforcers who had hunted her across rooftops—and the Hollower had bowed. Actually bowed, hand to chest, in the middle of Copperlane, and said, "Ma'am," in a voice gone soft with awe, and Wren had needed a sit-down and a pastry afterward.

No one remembered Casper Vasquez. That had been the bargain's shape: the people whose lives she'd touched got to keep their new memories, their rebuilt selves, their fifteen unhaunted years; and the woman who had held all their stolen pieces simply dissolved, spent like coin, gone from every mind that ever knew her.

Except one.

Vessin had promised. *I will remember you,* he had said, in the white roar of the door opening, holding together the last shreds of her while she emptied herself into the world like light leaving a lamp. *For as long as I am, you were, and you are.*

Gods, it turned out, kept their promises with terrible precision.

She felt him the way you feel weather changing—not often, not loudly. Some evenings, passing a shrine (there were nine shrines now, one per god, and Vessin's was the smallest and always full), she'd feel a warmth at the back of her neck, patient as a hand not quite touching. He never spoke first. That was their arrangement, unwritten: he had waited fifteen years for her once already; he could wait for her to turn around.

Tonight, walking home under the bleeding eastern sky, she stopped at the little bridge over the Corve and leaned on the rail, and let the warmth arrive.

"You're hovering," she said aloud.

*I am attending,* said the voice, everywhere and nowhere, in the space between the water-sound and the wind. *There is a difference. Hovering is anxious. Attending is dignified.*

"You're a god. You're supposed to be beyond dignity."

*Beyond dignity is the other eight. I was always the small one. The ninth. The door-keeper.* A pause, and then, with the gentle humor she remembered from the road, from the borderlands, from the worst night of her life when he'd held her together with nothing but his attention: *Dignity is mostly what's left when your siblings have taken the thunderstorms and the harvests and the war.*

She watched the water carry lamplight downstream. Six weeks of this—his presence arriving like a guest who always knocked, who always waited, who asked for nothing. She kept waiting for the debt to surface. For the bill. Fifteen years of theft teaches you that every gift has a ledger somewhere.

"You're not going to ask me for anything," she said. "Ever. Are you. It's driving me mad."

*Would it help if I did?*

"Immensely."

*Then: I require you to eat something besides pastries. I am a god, and I am worried about your diet.*

She laughed—out loud, alone on a bridge, and didn't care who heard. Invisibility was a skill she'd mastered once. Belonging, she was finding, required practice, and she was a slow student, but willing.

* * *

Later, in her rooms above the candle shop—a rented room with a crooked window and a bed she'd chosen herself, first object of her new life, purchased before food or coat—Wren sat at the table and did the thing she did most nights: tried on names.

This was harder than it sounded. Names, she had learned, were not labels but doors. *Casper Vasquez* had been a door she'd built herself, board by stolen board, and walking out of it had been the only free choice of her first life. She could not walk back through it. But a person needed *something*. The market's *Wren* fit well enough for transactions, but it was a name given by birds, and there were mornings she woke and felt the shape of herself like an unfurnished room.

She had a list. It lived in her head, since writing things down felt too much like forging documents. *Maren.* Too soft. *Odile.* Too much like a swan, and she had never trusted anything that graceful. *Sera.* Pretty, and borrowed from a girl she'd charmed once, so no.

The trouble, she was beginning to suspect, was that she kept shopping for a name the way she used to shop for charms: looking for something small enough to hide inside.

A knock at the door. Small, polite, human-sized—which ruled out gods, who knocked on the inside of your skull.

It was Ilsa.

The cartographer stood in the hallway with her satchel and her tired eyes, and Wren's heart did the complicated thing it always did around her. Because Ilsa had traded away her journey-memories to buy their escape from the Hollowers—had forgotten why she traveled, though not whom she traveled with—and then, weeks later, standing in the night market, had seen Wren's face and felt something she couldn't source, an attachment with no story attached, grief-shaped love with the grief missing.

"I was mapping tonight," Ilsa said, without preamble, because preamble was for people with patience. "The eastern quarter. And my hand—" she turned her palm up, frustrated. "My hand drew a road I've never surveyed. Out past Harrow Field. Through the new country. To a village with a bell tower, and a ford, and—" her jaw tightened. "I know the number of steps in the bell tower, Wren. I've never been there. Or I have, and it's gone out of me, and my *hand* kept the receipt."

Wren stepped aside to let her in. "You want me to open the jar."

Ilsa had come to her three times before with fragments like this—muscle-memory, dream-shapes, a song she hummed without knowing the words. Each time, Wren had opened a small sealed echo, one of the few she'd kept from the borderlands, and each time Ilsa had gotten a piece of herself back and wept in a businesslike way and left.

"Not tonight." Ilsa sat down heavily at the table, uninvited, which was also familiar. "Tonight I want to say something, while I still have the nerve." She looked at her own hands. "I don't remember why I went east. I don't remember my daughter's name—I gave it away, I know I gave it away, I chose it, everyone tells me I chose it, and I believe them, and I hate it. But I remember *you*." Her eyes came up. "Not the details. Just—you're the reason. Whatever the reason was. My hand draws roads to you. So I've decided that's enough. I've decided you're my reason, retroactively, and you don't get a vote."

Wren found she couldn't speak for a moment. Somewhere in the unfurnished room of herself, a window opened.

"You could come to the Court," she managed finally. "Vessin could—"

"No." Sharp, immediate. "Whatever I lost, I paid it for something. I'm not digging up the price tag. I'm keeping the purchase." She stood, briskly, before the moment could get any larger than either of them could bear. "Open the bell-tower jar tomorrow. Bring tea. You make it too weak."

She paused at the door.

"And Wren—or whatever you're calling yourself this week—it suits you. The birds picked well. Birds always know."

* * *

After she left, Wren sat a long time in the dark with the candle she'd lit for company.

*The birds picked well.*

Maybe that was the answer she'd been shopping for. Not a name hidden small. A name grown into—like the awning nest, like the crooked wicks, like Ilsa's hand-drawn roads. A name that arrived because a life had happened underneath it, and kept arriving, nightly, the way Corvale's borders once shifted: not forgotten, but becoming.

She blew out the candle and, in the dark, felt the warmth arrive at the back of her neck—polite, patient, waiting its customary beat.

"You can talk," she said. "I'm decent."

*You are always decent,* said Vessin. *It is one of the many things I remember about you.*

"How is the Court? How is the country?" She asked it lightly, but she meant it with her whole chest. Every morning she checked the sky's eastern edge. Every morning it bled a little more gold. The erased country was filling back in—rivers remembering their courses, villages praying themselves solid—and every bit of it had passed through her hands once, and been given back.

*Growing. Arguing. My siblings are laying down the war the way children lay down toys: badly, and with relapses. But they lay them down. Your bargain holds. Every life rebuilt in the new world stays built. They grumble about it. Grumbling is how gods say thank you.*

"And you?"

A pause. When he answered, the humor was still there, but underneath it ran something older and quieter, the thing he'd carried through fifteen empty years beside a white door in an empty field.

*I am remembering you. As promised. It is my favorite work. I have worshippers again, and temples, and a bell tower with a hundred and eleven steps—do not tell the cartographer I counted—but none of it is my favorite work. My favorite work is a woman on a bridge, and a candle stall, and a name she is almost ready for.*

Her throat tightened. "I think I might have it," she said. "The name."

*May I hear it?*

"It's not finished. Names aren't finished. That's the point, isn't it? You told me once—doors aren't doors until someone walks through, and names aren't names until someone answers to them."

*I said that about doors. But yes.*

"So it's provisional. Draft form." She took a breath, and said it aloud to the dark room, to the god, to herself: "Casper Wren Vasquez Eriksen."

All of it. Not the old name abandoned, and not the old name restored—but carried forward, folded into the new, the way Ilsa's love had survived its own forgetting, the way the smell of rain could live honestly in wax. The thief and the bird and the novelist's surname she'd worn like a coat through her first life, kept now not as a hiding place but as a history. Hers. Earned, not found.

The silence that followed was not empty. It was the silence of a very old, very patient being setting down the last weight he'd been carrying.

*Casper Wren Vasquez Eriksen,* he repeated, tasting every syllable, and the room warmed like noon. *Provisional. Growing. Answerable.* And then, softly, not as debt, not as duty, not as the god claiming his door:

*Hello, Casper. I am Vessin. I have waited a long time to meet you properly.*

She smiled in the dark, and outside, the eastern sky bled gold over the hills, and the birds in tomorrow's awning stirred in their sleep.

"Hello, Vessin," she said. "Come in. I'll make tea. I make it too weak—Ilsa says—but I'm told that's charming."

And far away, in a rising court of nine thrones, a small god laughed with joy, and the sound came all the way to Corvale as the first warm wind of spring.
