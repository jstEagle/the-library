## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The stars were still there when Lior Oyelaran checked them at dusk.

She made a habit of it — a guide's superstition, her grandmother would have called it, though Adaeze had taught her the habit herself. Stand at the highest point of the pass before full dark. Count what you can see. The Shepherd's Crook low over the western shoulder of Mount Vaelin. The Sisters in their tight little dance above the Thorn itself. If you know where the sky is, you know where you are. Maps lie. Rivers move. But the stars, her grandmother had said, pressing a calloused finger against Lior's seven-year-old brow, the stars are the one country that never gets erased.

Lior stood on the shelf of rock above the caravan's camp and counted. Fourteen she could name, another dozen too faint to bother with. The air smelled of cold pine resin and the woodsmoke of six small fires banked low, because smugglers knew that smoke carried and Wardens' eyes were sharp even this far from any border anyone admitted to. Below her, the caravan was settling into its nightly ritual: mules hobbled, crates counted twice, dice rattling in a tin cup near the second wagon while Ferro Ashkani cheated a drover named Pell out of his week's wages with the serene patience of a man shelling peas.

Fourteen named stars. She remembered the number the way other people remembered prayers.

She went down to the fires.

"The pass splits at moonrise," she told Ferro, crouching by his dice game and stealing his cup for a drink of wine. "Left fork is the old pilgrim road. It's watched — there's a toll station at the narrows that the Crown pretends isn't there, which means it's there, and bored, and taking bribes from everyone but us. Right fork drops through the Kestrel Cut. Steeper, narrower, no tolls, and the rockfall from two winters back has finally been cleared enough to take mules through if we walk them single file and don't sing."

"Who'd sing?" Ferro said. He took his cup back without looking at her, sleight of hand so smooth it was practically a greeting between them. "You're saying the Cut's open. Last time you said the Cut was open, we lost a wheel and half our dignity."

"We lost your dignity. The wheel was fine."

Pell snorted. Ferro gave him a look that would have curdled milk, then turned back to Lior, and beneath the lazy humor his eyes did the thing they always did when business turned real: they went still as well water. "Wardens?"

"Not on the Cut. Not yet." She turned the cup in her hands. "There've been patrols on the low roads all month. More than usual. And the wards along the border have been — " she hunted for the word and didn't like any of the honest ones — "loud. I can hear them when I sleep near them. Like a struck bell that won't finish ringing."

Ferro frowned. "You Oyelarans and your blood-sense. Useful trick. Unsettling dinner conversation." But he said it lightly, and Lior let him, because that was their arrangement: he didn't ask how much of Thornfell's old magic ran in her veins, and she didn't ask where he'd learned to forge seals good enough to fool a provincial magistrate. Everyone in the caravan carried something they didn't talk about. That was the price of admission. That, and a share of the profits, and absolute obedience on the road.

"Kestrel Cut at moonrise," she said, standing. "Get them moving quiet. We'll be through and down into the valley before dawn if the mules behave."

"And if they don't?"

"They will. I bribed them."

"With what?"

"With not being eaten. Mules respect me."

She heard him laughing behind her as she walked the camp's edge, and the sound settled into her chest like a warm stone. This was hers — the road, the route, the small empire of eleven wagons and thirty-one souls who trusted her to read the mountains the way priests read scripture. She was twenty-four years old and the best guide on the smugglers' roads between Emberhold and the coast, and if the name Oyelaran opened fewer doors than it once had, if the great houses of Thornfell had forgotten the family that had helped found the Ninefold Court, well — the mountains hadn't forgotten. The mountains kept better books than archivists did.

She checked the sky again out of habit before turning in.

That was when she noticed the bell.

* * *

It started as a taste. Iron, faint, at the back of her throat, as though she'd bitten through the skin of a plum and found a nail instead. Lior sat up in her bedroll beneath the wagon, heart already quickening, because she knew this taste. Every child raised on an oath-bound family's stories knew it. It was the taste of old wards working hard — wards strained past their design, wards chewing through something they weren't built to chew.

The horses felt it first. They came up off their pickets in a chorus of screams, all of them at once, hauling against their lines with their white eyes rolling toward the northeast, toward the border, toward home. Then the dogs, if you could call the caravan's three mangy trackers dogs; they went silent instead of loud, pressed flat to the ground with their ears pinned, which was worse. Ferro burst out of his tent with his coat half-on and a knife already in his hand, because Ferro woke the way soldiers wake, all at once and armed.

"Lior. What is it?"

"I don't — "

The bell rang.

Not with sound. With *absence* of sound — a pressure wave that passed through the camp and through her body and left her ears ringing with a silence so total that her own heartbeat sounded like a hammer on an anvil. The fires guttered flat. The wind died mid-breath. Even the river two hundred feet below the shelf seemed to hold its water still, and Lior had guided this road nine times and had never once heard the Kettle River stop talking.

Then the light began to die.

Not all of it. The fires still burned, though they burned wrong now, their flames standing up straight and colorless like candles seen through glass. But the lantern-glow on the snowfields above them faded. The faint phosphorescence of the lichen on the northern cliff face — lichen she had used as a landmark since she was twelve — winked out like a snuffed lamp. And overhead —

Overhead, the stars went out.

Not dimmed. Not veiled by cloud. They went out the way lamps go out when someone pinches the wick: one, then ten, then all of them, in a sweeping tide from the northeast to the southwest, a darkness rolling across the sky like a shutter drawn over creation. The Shepherd's Crook vanished. The Sisters vanished. In the space of four heartbeats the night above Thornfell's high passes became a ceiling of perfect, seamless black, blacker than any cloud, blacker than any cave, a black that had texture to it, that seemed almost to press downward, and Lior stood in the middle of her own camp with her mouth open and her grandmother's voice screaming in her memory:

*The stars are the one country that never gets erased.*

"Down!" Ferro roared. "Everyone down! Mules loose, wagons ringed, blades OUT!"

But there was nothing to fight. That was the horror of it. The camp dissolved into chaos anyway — thirty-one people trained for ambush, for raiders, for avalanches, for every violence the high passes could offer, and not one of them trained for the sky breaking. Drovers cut mule-lines and the animals bolted into the dark, or tried to; several simply refused to move, standing rigid with their noses pointed northeast, trembling like struck tuning forks. Someone was praying. Someone else was sick behind the third wagon. Pell stood in the open with his sword drawn and his face tilted upward, whispering, "*where*, where did they go," as though the stars might answer, as though the stars had merely stepped out of the room and might be coaxed back with courtesy.

Lior grabbed her compass.

It was her father's compass, brass-cased, needle ground true by a craftsman in Emberhold who had charged her father a month's wages for it and worth every coin. It had never lied to her. On the foggiest day, in the deepest gorge, when every landmark was swallowed, the needle had swung home to north with the dumb faithful certainty of a dog returning to its master. She had trusted it over her own eyes more times than she could count, and it had never once — not once in twenty-four years and nine seasons on the road —

The needle spun.

Not trembled. *Spun*, fast and smooth and endless, whipping around its pivot like a living thing trying to escape its housing, and when Lior held it flat in both palms and stared at it, willing it still, it slowed, shuddered, and pointed straight down. Into the earth. As if north were below her. As if the whole frame of the world had rotated ninety degrees and buried its directions.

Her maps were in her head. That was the truth of her trade that she told no one: the charts were for clients, for comfort, for the illusion of a plan written down. The real map was the one Adaeze Oyelaran had burned into her granddaughter's memory — every switchback of the Kestrel Cut, every spring, every slope that held snow past midsummer, every place the rock sang hollow underfoot. And that map had just developed a hole in it, a blind spot shaped exactly like everything she had ever known, because if the stars were gone and north was down and the wards on the border had rung like a cracked bell, then the world had stopped agreeing to be the world, and no map survived that kind of disagreement.

"Lior." Ferro's hand closed on her shoulder, hard, grounding. His face was a pale smear beside a shielded lamp. "Talk to me. What are we looking at?"

"I don't know." Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. Somewhere underneath the terror, the guide was still working, still doing what guides do, which is stand upright and speak clearly while everything else falls over. "The border wards just — ended. All of them, all at once, I'd stake my name on it. Whatever held them up is gone. And the sky — "

"Yes. The sky."

"The sky doesn't do this." She looked up despite herself, into that seamless pressing black, and felt vertigo roll through her stomach like seasickness. She had spent her whole life navigating by lights older than kingdoms. Without them she was — she was —

*I am a guide whose maps have lied,* she thought, and the thought had teeth.

Around them the camp was reassembling itself out of panic by sheer drilled habit: wagons dragged into a defensive ring, torches relit, watchers posted facing outward in all directions, though outward meant nothing now. Ferro got them sorted with short barked orders and then pulled her aside, into the lee of the lead wagon, away from listening ears.

"Options," he said. It wasn't a question. It was the word that meant *you're the guide, earn your keep.*

Lior breathed. Once. Twice. Made herself look at the black sky without flinching, because if she flinched here, in front of Ferro, she'd flinch forever.

"Option one," she said. "We turn around. Back down the way we came, reach the lowlands, find out what happened from someone with a newspaper and a roof." She hated the option as she said it. Retreating meant giving up a season's contract and, worse, admitting that the mountains had beaten her. "Option two, we stay put till dawn and see if the sky comes back. Maybe it's weather. Some kind of — " she gestured helplessly at the heavens — "atmospheric event. Ash cloud. Something."

"You don't believe that."

"No," she admitted. "Ash clouds don't put out stars in straight lines. Option three — " and here her voice dropped, and she was suddenly aware of the oath in her blood the way you become aware of your heartbeat when you lie still in the dark — "we keep going. Down through the Cut, into the valley, to Emberhold. Because whatever broke those wards broke them from the inside, and the answers aren't back the way we came. They're inward. Toward Thornfell."

"Toward the country that hasn't existed for a hundred years," Ferro said dryly. "Forgive me if my enthusiasm requires a moment."

"It exists." The words came out of her before she could weigh them, low and fierce, and Ferro blinked at the heat in them. Lior never talked about Thornfell. Lior laughed when clients called her routes *the old kingdom roads*. Lior was a smuggler, a professional, a woman who had traded her grandmother's dreams for a commission percentage years ago. But standing under that murdered sky, with her compass pointing into the dirt and the taste of iron still coating her throat, she found she couldn't perform the laugh. "It exists. It has to. Wards that old don't break clean, Ferro — they *shatter*, and shattering means something pushed them, and things only push at borders if there's something on both sides of them. There's still a this-side. I can feel it." She pressed her fist against her own sternum. "In here. My blood can feel it."

Ferro studied her for a long moment. The shielded lamp threw shifting amber light across his face, and beyond the wagon-ring the darkness waited, patient and total, a darkness with intent.

"Moonrise was an hour ago," he said at last. "No moon. No stars. You want to take thirty-one people and whatever mules we have left through a slot canyon neither of us can see the bottom of, guided by your *feelings*."

"Guided by the only map in the world that doesn't depend on the sky," Lior said. "My grandmother put it in my head before she died, and whatever just happened up there — " she jabbed a finger at the black — "it didn't happen inside my skull. Yet."

"*Yet.* Charming."

"Ferro." She caught his sleeve. Behind her calm her pulse was slamming, and maybe he heard it in her voice, because he went still. "Something erased the stars. Over Thornfell. Only over Thornfell — look east, look west, the horizon's still lit on both sides. We're standing inside the edge of something. And I am not going to learn what it is from a newspaper in the lowlands. I'm sorry about the season. Dock my pay. But I'm going down that mountain tonight, and anyone who wants to follow the only guide whose map still works is welcome to come."

Silence, except for the restless stamping of frightened animals and the far-off mutter of prayer.

Then, from across the fire, Pell's voice, hoarse but clear: "I'm in. Sky's gone mad either way. Rather go mad following somebody."

A beat. A second voice. A third. The caravan, deciding, the way caravans always decided — not by vote but by gravity, each soul falling toward whoever seemed least likely to get them killed. Ferro watched the gravity work with the expression of a man watching a landslide he'd predicted, then sighed through his nose and pulled out his knife to cut the knot on the lead mule's line.

"Kestrel Cut," he said. "Single file, no singing. And Lior — " he glanced up, just once, at the vast and starless dark, and for the first time since she'd known him she saw fear move openly across his face — "if your grandmother's map starts lying too, you tell me. First sign. Promise me."

Lior looked up at the empty sky where fourteen named stars should have been, and felt the hole in the world like a wound in her own chest, and understood with terrible clarity that she was promising something she might not be able to keep.

"I promise," she said.

They moved out into the dark, and the mountain received them without comment, and somewhere far below in the valley town of Emberhold, people who no longer knew the name of their own country slept soundly beneath the same starless black — and did not dream of anything at all.
