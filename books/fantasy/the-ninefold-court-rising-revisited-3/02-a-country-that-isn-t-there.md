## 2. A Country That Isn't There

Lior came down into the valley at dawn, and the dawn was wrong.

Not the light itself — the light was ordinary enough, grey-gold and thin as broth, pooling in the hollows where the mist had not yet burned away. What was wrong was the way the valley received it. She had walked this descent a hundred times. The road from the high passes switchbacked down through black pine and scree to the overlook above Emberhold, and from that overlook — she knew this the way she knew her own heartbeat — you could see the whole town laid out below you like a coin dropped in a green palm: the slate roofs, the smelter smoke standing up straight in the still air, the river Vane cutting its bright line through the middle of everything, and beyond it, on the far slope, the terraced orchards that made Emberhold famous in three kingdoms for its bittergold pears.

She stood on the overlook. The town was there.

The orchards were not.

Where they should have been — where she could have drawn them blindfolded, every terrace, every leaning ladder-stile, every stone wall her grandfather's generation had stacked by hand — there was only forest. Old forest. Dense, unhewn, unbroken, the kind of forest that takes two centuries to make. It climbed the far slope without a single gap, as if no one had ever set an axe against it, and the morning fog lay among its trunks like something asleep.

Lior's legs went strange beneath her. She sat down hard on the cold rock of the overlook without deciding to.

"That's not possible," she said aloud, and her voice sounded very small under all that empty sky.

Because it wasn't only the orchards. She made herself look, made herself inventory, the way her mother had taught her to inventory a pass before committing a caravan to it. The bridge over the Vane: gone. Not ruined — *unbuilt*, the banks smooth and grassy on both sides, no footings, no ferry-house, no rope-marks worn into the stone where a century of ferrymen had hauled their lines. The watchtower on the near ridge, the one the smugglers called the Old Widow because of the way its broken crown leaned: gone, and with it the ridge itself seemed subtly reshaped, gentler, as though the mountain had been smoothed over like a bed after someone got up. The shrine-road that branched east toward the hot springs — a road she had used herself, twice, in the last year — was a deer track at best.

The town remained. Slate roofs, smelter smoke, the river running silver. But it looked smaller than she remembered, or perhaps it was simply that everything around it had grown back over the space where the rest of its life used to be, the way flesh closes over a wound when the thing that made the wound is taken out of the world.

Above her head, where stars should have been fading into daylight, there was nothing at all. Not cloud. Not haze. Nothing — a clean absence, a sky scrubbed blank, and looking at it too long made her teeth ache the way biting foil did.

*The stars went out,* she thought, *and my country went out with them.*

Her compass was still in her pocket. She took it out without much hope. The needle lay flat and dead, pointing nowhere, trembling occasionally as if it were trying to remember what north was and failing. She closed her fist around it until the brass bit her palm. Her maps were worse. She had unrolled the high-pass chart at the last camp, the one her own hand had inked, and found the roads still drawn but the labels wrong — not crossed out, not faded, but *wrong*, the names replaced with other names, older-sounding names, names in no alphabet she knew, written in what was unmistakably her own handwriting.

She had rolled it up again and not slept.

Now she stood, brushed off her knees, and started down toward Emberhold, because there was nowhere else to go, because the caravan was scattered somewhere behind her in those passes — Toma and his wagons, old Brannic, the Kellin sisters, eleven souls who trusted her to know the way through a country that had apparently stopped existing overnight — and because a guide with no answer still has to walk toward the question.

* * *

Emberhold's gate was open. That was the first strangeness inside the strangeness: the gate stood open at dawn, unguarded, and the guardhouse beside it was a bakery now, and had clearly been a bakery for longer than a year. The stone around its door had settled into new cracks. Ivy had opinions about the eaves.

Inside, the town was alive. That was the second strangeness, and the one that hurt worst. Alive, and ordinary, and utterly unafraid. Women carried water up the lane, arguing cheerfully about a wedding. Boys chased a dog between the market stalls. A smith was already at work, and the sound of his hammer rang out over the roofs exactly as it had rung out over the roofs every morning of Lior's childhood, when she'd been small enough to think the whole world was one town and one hammer and one river.

Except the market stalls sold things Emberhold had never made. She stopped at one and stared at bolts of cloth dyed in patterns she didn't recognize, and when she asked the woman minding them whose weave it was, the woman said, "Southron, love, off the river barges," and Lior said, "There are no river barges; the Vane isn't navigable past the falls," and the woman looked at her with mild, untroubled puzzlement and said, "The barges come up from Lowmark every week, dear, they have since before my grandmother."

Lowmark. Lior turned the word over. It wasn't a name from any map she owned. And yet when she tried to say *that's impossible*, the word *Lowmark* sat in her mouth with the terrible confidence of a thing that had always been true, and she understood, with a lurch, that the erasure didn't just take things away — it left replacements behind, little load-bearing lies, propping up the shape of the world so no one would notice the hole.

"Can I ask you something?" Lior said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "What country is this?"

The cloth-woman smiled. "Why, the Marchlands, of course." She said it the way you'd say *the sky*, or *bread*. Then she frowned slightly, as if the question had snagged on something, and touched her temple. "Funny. For a moment there—" The frown passed like a cloud. "Cold this morning, isn't it?"

It was not cold. It was a mild grey morning, and the pear trees — Lior looked, desperately, at the nearest garden — were apple trees, heavy with fruit no one in Emberhold had ever grown.

Lior thanked her, bought nothing, and walked on with her skin crawling.

She tried it again at the well, with a carter. Again outside the temple — and the temple was wrong too, dedicated to gods whose names rang faint bells and struck no deeper, its icons painted over what might have been older frescoes, scraped but not quite erased, a suggestion of wings under the whitewash, a hint of nine crowned figures in a processional frieze, defaced so thoroughly that only their number survived. Nine. She counted them three times. Nine.

"What happened here?" she asked a boy sitting on the temple steps, peeling an apple. "A long time ago. Do you know what this place used to be?"

The boy considered the question with the honest slowness of the young. "Used to be?" he said. "It's just Emberhold. It's always been Emberhold." Then he shrugged, and went back to his apple, and Lior walked away from him feeling as if she had spoken to someone through a wall of glass — close enough to touch, sealed away entirely.

They weren't harmed. That was the thing she kept circling, the thing that kept the horror from resolving into simple anger. They weren't grieving. They weren't captives. They laughed, quarreled, married, buried their dead, and loved their town, which was real, which was *there* — only the country around it had been lifted out of the world like a stitch pulled from cloth, and the cloth had been rewoven so smoothly that even the people who lived in the pattern couldn't feel the missing thread.

Only she could feel it. Only she walked down these streets feeling them double, seeing the baker's oven and the guardhouse both, tasting two histories in the same mouthful of air. She pressed her hand against the temple's whitewashed wall and felt, under her palm, a faint warmth — no, not warmth. A kind of *resistance*, the way a scar resists the finger that finds it. Something under the surface. Something remembered by the stone even if no living mind held it.

And when she drew her hand away, her fingertips had gone cold. Cold as the dead compass brass. Cold as the starless patch of sky.

Oath-bound blood, her grandmother used to call it — half joke, half warning, the Oyelarans having guided travelers through Thornfell's passes for as long as Thornfell had existed, bound to the land by some bargain old enough that the telling of it had worn smooth. *The land knows us, little wren,* Adaeze used to say. *That's why we can never lie to it.* Lior had thought it was a story to make children careful with fire on the dry slopes.

Now she stood in a town that had forgotten its own nation, and her blood was the only part of her that refused to forget, and it burned cold in her veins like a brand held against snow, and she finally let herself understand what she was perceiving:

This was not destruction. Destruction leaves ruins, griefs, graves. This was *subtraction*. Someone had reached into the world and taken Thornfell out entire — its name, its borders, its orchards and bridges and shrines, its place in the memory of everyone born under its vanished stars — and stitched the hole shut so neatly that only the stitches themselves, glimpsed sideways, gave the lie to the fabric.

Records burn cold to oath-blood. She didn't know how she knew that phrase, but she knew it, the way she knew the passes: something inherited, arriving in her all at once. She tested it. In the temple there was a chest of parish records, and when she lifted the lid and laid her fingers on the oldest ledger, the cold bit deep enough to sting — the paper remembering something, holding something, a name inked and scraped and inked again, and under her thumb, faint as breath on glass, letters that were almost legible: *Th—*

She snatched her hand back.

*Thornfell.* Her homeland. Her mountains, her passes, her grandmother's shrine-road and the Ninefold Court that had ruled it, the nine seats and the nine houses, all of it dissolved a hundred years ago — dissolved by oath, the stories said, sworn by the heads of the nine houses themselves, though no story agreed on why, and no story had ever mentioned anything like *this*. Erasure was supposed to be a fable. A threat grandmothers made. *Swear nothing lightly, child; look what oaths once cost.*

Someone had paid that price. Someone had fed a whole country into the dark to buy something, and the purchase had held for a hundred years, and last night — the night the stars went out — the stitching had begun to fail.

Lior stood in the temple doorway with her cold hand clenched at her side, and the grief arrived at last, fully armed. It came for her not as tears but as fury wearing grief's face: fury at whoever had done this, fury at the neatness of it, fury at the cloth-woman's mild smile and the boy's honest shrug, fury most of all at the orchards — the bittergold pears her grandmother had described to her a thousand times, fruit from trees that had never gotten the chance to be cut down because they had never, in this version of the world, been planted. A hundred years of harvests, unwitnessed. A hundred years of Emberhold lives lived inside a lie so comfortable they'd never once felt its walls.

*I'm the only one who remembers,* she thought. *I'm the only witness the country has left.*

And then, quieter, in her grandmother's cadence, the thought that changed the grief into something with edges: *Then be worth witnessing.*

Resolve doesn't arrive like lightning. It arrives like frost, she thought later — silent, incremental, everywhere at once, until one morning the ground will bear weight that it wouldn't yesterday. By the time she left the temple she was already making lists. Toma and the others would shelter at the Hollow Camp, two days back up the trail; she'd send word. She needed to find anyone else who felt the cold in the records, anyone else whose blood argued with the world. She needed to learn who had held the needle that pulled the stitch — and whether the pull could be reversed.

She was halfway across the market square, moving fast now, when she heard the humming.

It came from a narrow lane behind the chandlery, thin and sweet and absolutely familiar, and it stopped her mid-stride as if she'd walked into a wall. It was the anthem. The *old* anthem — not the Marchland hymn the temple choir would sing, but the anthem of the Ninefold Court, the rising-falling melody every Thornfell child learned, the one about the nine lights and the mountain keeping them. No one alive should know it. She barely knew it herself, only fragments, from her grandmother's humming on winter nights.

But someone in that lane was humming all of it. Note-perfect. Unhesitating.

Lior turned toward the sound. At the mouth of the lane, half in shadow, a small figure sat on a step with a stick of charcoal and a piece of slate, drawing — drawing, even from this distance, what looked unmistakably like a ring of nine chairs — while the anthem poured out of her as naturally as breathing.

A girl. An orphan, by the size of her and the patched state of her coat. Seven years old, maybe eight.

Singing a country that did not exist.

Lior's cold hand opened slowly at her side, and for the first time since the stars went out, hope hit her like sunlight after a long passage underground — fierce, painful, and impossible to look away from.
