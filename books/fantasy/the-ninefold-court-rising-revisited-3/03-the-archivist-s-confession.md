## 3. The Archivist's Confession

Wren Vale had spent eleven years learning how to disappear inside the Archive of Ash, and he had thought himself very good at it.

He had been wrong about many things that year, but this was among the most instructive: a man who can vanish among shelves is not a man who can vanish on a road. The road had wind in it, and weather, and other bodies moving through it with their own purposes, and Wren — pale, ink-stained, soft-footed from decades of stone corridors — moved through all of it like a stain on clean linen. He felt conspicuous as a candle in a crypt. Every grey-cloaked figure in Emberhold's market square might be a colleague. Every child might report him. Every bell tolling the hour reminded him that somewhere above the valley, catalogued in his own careful hand, there existed a ledger entry for *this day*, *this hour*, and what was scheduled to happen in it.

So he waited instead. Waiting was something archivists did well.

He had been waiting three days in the taproom of the Drowned Lantern, in the corner seat where the lamplight failed and the plaster sweated in winter, nursing a single cup of cider gone vinegar-sour, watching the door. The town of Emberhold went about its business around him with the terrible smoothness of the erased: bakers baking, smiths hammering, mothers calling children home to supper, none of them able, if pressed, to say what country they stood in. He had tested it twice, gently, the way one tests a bruise. A fishwife had told him she lived "here." A carter had said "the valley." An old man had wept without knowing why when Wren asked what his grandfather had fought in, and then forgotten his own tears before his cup was drained.

The forgetting did not hurt them. That was the worst of it. It sat over Emberhold like snowfall, soft and total, and beneath it the people went on living lives that had been quietly amputated and did not bleed.

Wren had helped write the amputation order. Entry 4471-C. His hand. His seal. *Catalogue complete; dissolution confirmed; memory-ward laid per the Ninefold Accord.* Eleven years ago, and he could still feel the quill's scratch in his fingers like a splinter working toward the bone.

And now the wards were falling apart at the seams, the stars had gone out over Thornfell like snuffed candles — he had seen the darkness rolling up the sky two nights past, had dropped his cup in the inn yard and stood gaping like a fool while the heavens emptied — and someone was walking the valley roads whom the forgetting could not touch.

He had seen her from the window that morning. Tall, dark-skinned, weathered as old rope, with the walk of a woman who knew passes by their smell and carried a sword the way farmers carry scythes — an extension of the arm, nothing ceremonial. She had stopped in the middle of the square and stared up at the chapel spire, and her face had done something no face in Emberhold had done since the erasure. It had *grieved*. Openly. At a building. As though the building had been taken from her.

Lior Oyelaran. He had read her name in a genealogy so old its pages shed brown flakes when turned. The last line of a house sworn into silence.

Which was why, when she finally pushed open the Drowned Lantern's door that evening, shaking rain off her shoulders, Wren stood up from his corner before his courage could reconsider, crossed the room, and said the words he had rehearsed for three days and which still came out wrong:

"You're looking for your country. I know where they buried it."

* * *

She turned on him the way a hawk turns — the whole body involved, the hand already finding the hilt without seeming to decide to. Up close she smelled of pine resin, wet wool, and road dust, and her eyes went over him with the frank inventory of a guide assessing a client: weight, fitness, likelihood of trouble.

"Say that again," she said, "slower."

"I know where they buried it," Wren repeated. His voice came out thin. He made himself stand straighter. "Thornfell. Your country. It wasn't destroyed, mistress Oyelaran. It was filed away."

Her eyes narrowed. "Who told you my name?"

"No one alive," Wren said, and gestured toward his corner table. "May I buy you a drink? What I have to tell you goes down easier with something bitter in hand."

For a long moment she simply looked at him, and Wren had the sensation of being read — thoroughly, mercilessly, the way he had once read ledgers. Then she said, "You're the one who's been watching me. Corner seat, three days. You hold your cup like a clerk and you flinch every time a Warden's boots go past."

Guilt has a smell, he thought. I must reek of it.

"Yes," he said. "I've been watching you because I've been trying to find the courage to do this." He set his hand flat on the table between them, palm up, the way one offers peace or surrender. "My name is Wren Vale. For eleven years I served as an archivist of the Regency of Ash. And I am the man who catalogued the end of your world."

* * *

She did not strike him. He had braced for it — had almost wanted it, in some shameful corner of himself, a punishment to balance the ledger — but she only took the chair across from him, set her sword-hand flat on her knee, and said, "Talk. From the beginning. And Wren Vale, if I catch you lying to me, I'll know. I grew up reading faces on smuggling roads, where a lie costs blood."

"The beginning," Wren said, and laughed, a dry sound with no humor in it, because the beginning was not where people thought it was. "Everyone believes Thornfell fell to conquest. That's the shape the forgetting leaves behind, when it leaves any shape at all. A country erased must look like a country defeated, or the story doesn't sit right in the mind. But no army crossed your borders, Mistress Oyelaran. No city burned. The Ninefold Court was dissolved by *oath*. Sworn willingly, by nine voices, in the throne hall beneath the mountains, one hundred years ago this autumn."

The taproom noise seemed to recede. Rain ticked against the shutters. Lior had gone very still.

"Nine voices," she said slowly.

"Nine houses. Oyelaran among them." Wren kept his eyes on her because flinching would be unforgivable; she deserved the truth delivered by someone who could meet it. "Your grandmother Adaeze was the first to swear. Her name heads the accord. I've read the original — I *handled* the original, that was my work, verifying signatures against the house seals." He pulled a folded paper from his coat, worn soft as cloth from handling, and unfolded it on the table: a page copied in his own small, precise hand. "I stole a copy. It's the only crime of my career I don't regret."

She looked down at the page. Wren watched her read it and saw the moment it landed — a small contraction around her eyes, a breath drawn through the nose, the stillness of a woman standing in deep water feeling the bottom drop away.

"'Let the wars end by our forgetting,'" she read aloud, barely voicing it. "'Let the borders close and the names go dark, that our children may eat in peace.' " She looked up. "What is this?"

"It's called the Ninefold Accord," Wren said. "A century ago Thornfell was dying. Not by sword — by famine and by war. Nine great houses, nine endless border feuds, and the harvests failing three years running. The neighboring kingdoms were circling like gulls after a gutted fish. And so the nine lords and ladies of your Court met beneath the mountain and made a bargain with powers I only ever saw described in the margins of older books — bargains of unmaking. They swore to dissolve the Court, to fold the country out of the world's memory, to make Thornfell a hole in every map and every mind outside its borders — because a country no one remembers is a country no one marches on. The wars couldn't follow them into oblivion. Neither could the famine trade, nor the feuds, nor the ambitions of kings. Your people would live small and safe and *unnamed*, and the price—" he tapped the page— "the price was everything else. History. Anthem. Flag. The stars themselves, apparently, since the warding drew on starlight; I never understood that part fully. The Court's magic was written in the sky."

"And my grandmother swore it first."

"She drafted it first, I believe. The accord's language carries her cadence. I'm a cataloguer, not a poet, but I've read enough oaths to hear a hand in the phrasing." He hesitated. "There's more, and it will be worse. Do you want worse?"

"Give me all of it."

"Wardens keep the forgetting maintained. Grey cloaks, ash-grey sigils — you'll have seen them. They are not soldiers exactly; they're *clerks* with power. They walk the roads and renew the erasure where it frays, and they hunt anything that remembers. Songs, records, dreams. Especially dreams." He lowered his voice further. "But the erasure isn't perfect. Blood resists. Oath-blood most of all. Which is why you can see what these people cannot — why the records burn cold under your hands instead of lying to you. The oath that unmade Thornfell was sworn by Oyelaran blood, and Oyelaran blood stands outside the spell it created. You are the flaw in the design, Mistress Oyelaran. Or the key to it. The Wardens haven't decided which yet, and neither, I think, has the Regent."

"The Regent of Ash." She said the title like something spat. "The one who unmade us?"

"No." Wren shook his head, and here he had to be very careful, because the next part was the part that had broken him, eleven years ago, at his desk, alone, with a lamp burning low. "The Regent didn't unmake Thornfell. The Regent *maintains* it. The unmaking was your own Court's doing — freely chosen, desperately, with love, if you want to call it that. The Regent's line inherited the maintenance the way a son inherits a debt. Every generation since has renewed the ward rather than let it lapse, because letting it lapse means the wars come back, and the neighbors remember there's a fat undefended valley sitting in a hole in the map." He spread his ink-stained hands. "I was one of the maintainers. Small work. Cataloguing what was erased so the erasure could be checked for completeness. I inventoried your country, Mistress Oyelaran. I numbered its songs. I wrote descriptions of its paintings so that if anyone ever glimpsed one, the Wardens would know what to confiscate. Entry four thousand four hundred seventy-one. My hand. My seal."

The silence stretched. Somewhere behind the bar, a keg thumped; laughter rose and fell, oblivious. Lior stared at the copied page as though she could burn holes in it with attention.

"You regret it," she said at last. Not a question.

"Every day since the stars went out." Wren heard his own voice crack and hated it, and continued anyway. "Eleven years I told myself it was mercy. That was always the word we used. *Mercy.* The forgetting keeps them safe. The forgetting ends the wars. I filed it under mercy and slept at night. But I've watched this town for three days, and I'll tell you what mercy looks like, Mistress Oyelaran. It looks like an old man crying over a question he can't remember asking. It looks like a choir that's lost one note and hums around the gap without knowing it. It looks like children drawing suns and rivers and houses and never, not once, drawing a flag." He swallowed. "I came to find you because I can't file it anymore. Whatever it costs me. Whatever it costs *you*. The ledger needs balancing, and I'm the only clerk willing to do it."

Lior was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was low and level and more frightening than shouting would have been.

"My grandmother bound her whole bloodline into this. A hundred years of my family, sworn to silence, and I walked into a valley of ghosts last night thinking some enemy had done it to us. And you're telling me *we* did it to ourselves." Her jaw tightened. "Do you understand what you're telling me? That the woman whose ghost stories raised me — that she chose this. Chose to erase her own granddaughter's name before I was ever born."

"I'm telling you she chose it the way drowning people choose to push others toward a plank," Wren said. "It may even have been the right choice, a hundred years ago. I've read the famine records. I've counted the dead of the border wars. I can build the argument better than the Regent's own clerks — I built half of it." He leaned forward. "But here is what I also know, and what drove me out of the Archive with a stolen copy and a forged leave-pass. Three nights ago the border wards shattered. The stars went dark. Something is *failing*, and whatever the forgetting was bought to prevent, it's coming back through the cracks — and this time it won't find a Ninefold Court ready to meet it. It will find a valley full of kind, gentle, amputated people who don't remember how to fight for a country they cannot name." He held her gaze. "The choice your grandmother made is expiring. And someone has to decide what comes next. I'd rather it were the last Oyelaran than the Regent's clerks."

She looked at him for a long moment. Outside, a patrol passed — the rhythmic grey of Warden boots on cobbles — and both of them, without agreeing to, stopped breathing until it faded.

"Why trust me?" she asked finally. "You don't know me. I could be anyone. I could be a smuggler using a dead noble house's name for cover."

"I know your name is true because the forgetting slides off it," Wren said simply. "And I trust you because you came back. Everyone else who touches the edges of this runs. Merchants feel the wrongness and move along. Pilgrims dream strangely and blame the wine. But you walked *inward*, toward the pain, alone, with no plan I can see. That's either madness or inheritance, and I've spent my life among documents, so I believe in inheritance." A ghost of a smile touched his mouth, the first in days. "Also, you haven't stabbed me, which puts you ahead of my last three allies."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth too — not quite a smile, but the ground where one might take root. Then it passed, and she folded his copied page with great care and put it inside her coat, over her heart, and Wren understood that he had just handed her the most dangerous object in the valley and there was no taking it back.

"All right, archivist," she said. "Here's what happens now. Tomorrow I go to the orphanage on Chapel Row. There's a girl there — small, maybe eight years old — who was singing a song in the market yesterday that no one in this country should know. A song I learned at my grandmother's knee. If your Wardens hear her before I do, she's dead, and if your Regent's clerks hear that *I* heard her, then we're all dead, so tonight you're going to tell me everything: where the Wardens sleep, how they renew the wards, what they watch for. And then, Wren Vale—" her eyes held his, and there was iron under the weariness in them— "you and I are going to see whether a guilty clerk and an angry guide can steal a child out from under the Regency of Ash."

Wren's stomach dropped through the floorboards and kept falling. Everything in him that had survived eleven years in the Archive screamed *no* — stay small, stay shelved, stay filed.

He picked up the sour cider instead and drank it to the dregs.

"Chapel Row," he said. "I know the matron takes bribes. God help me — yes."
