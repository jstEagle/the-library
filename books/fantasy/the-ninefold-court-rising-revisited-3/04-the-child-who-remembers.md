## 4. The Child Who Remembers

The orphanage of Emberhold had once been a counting house; you could still see it in the bones of the place. Iron brackets where ledger-shelves had hung. A great hearth built to warm clerks bent over columns of figures, now crowded with small bodies instead. The children slept in rows on straw ticks like entries in some account no one bothered to balance anymore.

Lior stood in the doorway of the long room and felt the wrongness of it the way a sailor feels a storm before the clouds come — not with her eyes but with something older, something that lived in her blood and had been awake ever since the stars went out.

The matron, a broad woman named Odile with flour to her elbows, watched her with the wary patience of someone who had fed many strangers and trusted few. "You said you were looking for a child."

"I'm looking for a song," Lior said.

Odile's hands stilled over the bread she was portioning. Outside the window, Emberhold went about its evening as if nothing in the world had been cut out of it: lamplighters, a dog barking at geese, the smell of woodsmoke and river mud. But under all of it, Lior could feel the hollow — a country-shaped absence pressed into the valley floor, like the dent a body leaves in a bed after it has risen.

"There was a woman came through last month," Odile said slowly. "Traveling scholar. She hummed something in the yard and one of mine picked it up. I've told her to stop. It upsets the little ones." She did not say why it upset them. Lior suspected Odile herself didn't know. That was how the erasure worked, Wren had explained on the road down from the pass — not like forgetting, which leaves a hole and knows it's a hole, but like a scar that itches and cannot be scratched because the skin around it insists nothing ever happened there.

"Which one?" Lior asked.

"The girl by the window."

* * *

She was drawing when Lior found her. Seven years old, maybe eight, thin in the way of children who give away half their supper without being asked. Knees drawn up against the sill, a stub of charcoal moving across the back of an old tax form — paper still printed with Emberhold's name, though the seal above it was a smudge of nothing, ink that refused to resolve into any crest at all.

Lior looked down at what the girl was drawing and stopped breathing for a moment.

Nine chairs. Drawn in a child's wobbly hand, but unmistakable — nine seats arranged in a crescent on a dais, each one different. One carved with wheat-sheaves. One with a river running through its arms. One so tall its back became a mountain peak. One that was simply a stone block, plain as a grave-marker, and yet the girl had shaded it more carefully than all the others, pressing hard enough to snap the charcoal twice.

"You've seen this," Lior said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Where have you seen this?"

The girl looked up. She had ash-grey eyes, unusual in the valley, the color of the sky the night the stars vanished. "In my dreams," she said, matter-of-fact, the way children report impossible things. "Every night. Same ones. The tall one talks sometimes." She frowned. "It doesn't say words. It says *names*."

"What's your name?"

"Sefi." Then, with the sudden fierce precision of a child reciting something learned by heart: "Sefi Anara Okonkwo-Vale of the Ashline Road, daughter of Tamar and Jule, born under the Nine Stars in the year of the Long Thaw."

Lior's knees nearly gave.

There was no Ashline Road anymore. There had been no Long Thaw in any calendar Wren knew. And yet the names rang through her like a bell struck in an empty hall — true, old, *hers*, in the way that a country is hers even when the country has been stolen out from under the word.

"Say it again," Lior whispered.

Sefi shrugged, pleased to be asked. "Sefi Anara—"

"No. Not that." Lior crouched, bringing herself level with those grey eyes. "The song. The one the scholar taught you. Sing it for me."

For a heartbeat Sefi hesitated, glancing toward the door where Odile had gone. Then she drew breath, and out of that small body came a melody that made the hair rise along Lior's arms.

She knew it. Of course she knew it — every child of Thornfell knew it, had been carried to sleep by it, had sung it badly at festivals until the elders laughed. It was the anthem of the Ninefold Court, the *Rise-O-Song*, the one that began low as a river under ice and climbed and climbed until it broke open into light. Except no child of Thornfell could know it, because there had been no Thornfell for a hundred years, and no Court, and no anthem, and the woman who'd taught it to Sefi must have been someone like Lior — oath-blooded, remembering, walking through a country that insisted it had never existed and singing anyway.

And here was the thing that undid Lior entirely: as Sefi sang, the other orphans turned their heads. One by one. They didn't know the song. Their faces said they didn't know it. But their faces also said they *almost* did, the way a face almost remembers a name called across a crowded market twenty years ago. A boy of ten began tapping his foot without knowing he was doing it. A toddler hummed a bar behind the tune, a half-step off, catching up.

The erasure had taken the country. It had not quite taken the children.

Lior sat down on the cold flagstones right there, skirts and all, and let the anthem wash through her until her eyes burned. When Sefi finished, the silence afterward felt full rather than empty, the way a held note feels full.

"You're crying," Sefi observed.

"A little."

"Why?"

Because your dream-chairs are real, and I have walked among them in my grandmother's stories, and the world was robbed of them while everyone smiled. Because you are singing a dead country back to life and don't know it. Because I am going to have to keep you alive, and I have never kept anything alive except myself and a bad-tempered mule.

"Because it's a good song," Lior said. "Good songs make me cry. Where's the scholar who taught you? Did she say where she was going?"

"She went north." Sefi pointed with the charcoal, out the window, toward the passes. "She said she was going home, but she looked sad when she said it. Like maybe home wasn't there anymore." The child tilted her head. "Is that silly?"

"No," Lior said. "That's the least silly thing anyone's said to me in weeks."

* * *

She stayed the night. She told herself it was caution — the roads were bad, the innkeepers of Emberhold asked questions with their eyes even when their mouths stayed polite, and Wren needed another day to copy what he called *the parish registers of nonexistence*, his phrase for the town's records, which burned cold under his fingers the same way they did under hers. All true. None of it was the real reason. The real reason was that Sefi had fallen asleep mid-sentence, mid-drawing, charcoal still in her fist, and had slumped sideways against Lior's arm with the boneless trust of a child who has decided, without consultation, that this stranger belongs to her now.

Lior sat very still for a long time, listening to the orphanage breathe.

Wren found her there near midnight, a candle in his hand and guilt already arranged on his face, his default expression. He looked at Sefi asleep against her shoulder, then at the drawings scattered on the sill — nine chairs, and beneath them, newer, a caravan of stick figures walking between mountains with a star drawn above each head.

"You know what she is," Wren said quietly.

"I know what she has."

"That's worse. Or better. I haven't decided." He lowered himself onto a bench with the groan of a man whose joints predated him. "Lior — the Wardens will have heard the same rumor we did. A child who sings the erased songs. If the Regent's people learn that the erasure can be *undone* in a single mind — one mind that spreads it, that teaches, that—" He didn't finish. He didn't need to.

"Then we leave at first light," Lior said. "North, through the quarry cuts. No Warden patrols the quarry cuts; they think the rockfall closed them."

"They did close them. Twenty years ago."

"I unclose things," Lior said. "It's becoming my whole profession."

Wren almost smiled. Almost. Then the candle guttered in a draft from the corridor, and both of them heard it at the same moment — boots. Many boots. On the street outside, unhurried, evenly spaced, the cadence of soldiers who own whatever street they walk down.

Grey cloaks. Grey hoods. In Emberhold's memory-less streets, the Wardens moved like ink through water, and no one looked at them, because looking required a reason and the erasure had eaten all the reasons.

Lior was on her feet with Sefi in her arms before the thought finished forming.

* * *

The back door of the counting house opened onto a drying-yard strung with laundry, and beyond it the tannery lane, and beyond that the dark shoulder of the quarry hill. Lior counted four Wardens crossing the front square, lanterns hooded, moving with that terrible patience. Two more peeling off toward the alley behind the chandlery — cutting off the lane. They weren't searching the town. They were closing a net around one building.

They had known. Someone in Emberhold had whispered — not from malice, probably, just from the deep human itch to hand the frightening thing over to whoever claimed authority over it.

"Wren. The yard wall."

"I see it." He was already boosting himself up, forty years old and graceless and utterly determined. Lior passed Sefi up to him — the girl woke halfway, went rigid, then saw Wren's face and clung. Lior took the wall after them, boots finding purchase in old mortar, and dropped into the tannery lane's reek of lime and hides.

They made thirty yards. Forty. The lane bent past a cooper's shed, and Lior could see the quarry path rising ahead, black against marginally less-black sky, freedom shaped like a wound in the hillside —

—and the two Wardens stepped out of the alley mouth ahead of them, unhurried, lanterns raised.

Grey cloth to the wrists. Grey masks beneath the hoods, smooth as river stones, with no features but two dark slots. Under the lantern-light their shadows fell long and clean, shadows that seemed too sharp for the light that cast them, as if the erasure itself stood there wearing men's bodies.

"Hold," said the nearer one. Its voice was flat, official, almost kind. "The child comes with us. The others may go. This is mercy. Understand that this is mercy."

Behind her, Wren breathed: "*Names,*" — a warning or an instruction, Lior couldn't tell.

Sefi stirred against Wren's shoulder. And Lior saw the child look at the masked faces, and saw recognition move through her small body like lightning finding ground — not fear, recognition. Whatever Sefi dreamed every night, these grey shapes belonged to it. They had been in the dreams before they were ever in the lane.

"You're the empty chairs," Sefi said clearly. "You're the ones who took the seats away."

The nearest Warden actually flinched. Lior would remember that flinch for the rest of her life: the tiny stagger, the lantern dipping, the smooth mask turning as if the words had struck it physically. Because they had. Wren had tried to explain it on the road — *the erasure is a sentence spoken over the world, and sentences can be answered* — but understanding it and watching it happen were different creatures entirely.

A seven-year-old had just spoken truth into a lie strong enough to eat a nation, and the lie had bled.

"Take her," the second Warden said, and its voice cracked in the middle of the word, and it advanced, and Lior moved.

Everything after happened in the space of three heartbeats, the way violence does — too fast to narrate, only fast enough to survive. The first Warden's hand closed on her wrist; she broke the grip with the turn her grandmother had drilled into her before either of them knew why such drills might matter, drove her elbow into the slot of its mask, felt cartilage give. The second one lunged for Wren and Sefi and Lior caught its cloak and hauled, spun it off-balance into the cooper's shed hard enough to crack boards, and then its knife was out, silver-grey, drinking the lantern-light, and it came at her low and professional and meant to open her belly.

Her sword cleared the sheath with the sound a river makes under ice.

She had killed before — smugglers' roads collect debts — but always bandits, always men whose names she never learned and whose deaths sat in her like stones she'd learned to carry. This was different. This was a soldier of the thing that had erased her country, wearing a mask with no face, sent to steal a child who sang, and when the point went in under the grey cloak's edge — high, angled, the fastest killing she knew — she felt the whole hundred years of the lie shudder down the blade.

The Warden folded. The mask rolled sideways on the cobbles, and beneath it there was a face after all, young, astonished, ordinary — a farm-boy's face from some village that no longer knew its own name — and Lior understood in one sick flash that the Wardens were not the erasure's authors. They were its victims too, marching in grey, holding lanterns over a country's grave.

The first Warden — nose broken, mask askew — looked at its dying fellow, then at Sefi, then at Lior, and did something none of them expected.

It ran.

Boots hammering away down the lane toward the square, toward its brothers, toward the net it would now tighten with a story: the child speaks, the words burn, one of ours is down. They had minutes. Perhaps fewer.

"*Quarry path,*" Lior gasped. "Now. Don't look back at him — Sefi, love, eyes on me, that's it, eyes on me—"

But Sefi was looking at the dead man on the cobbles. Looking at his ordinary, astonished face with those ash-grey eyes that had watched nine empty chairs every night of her life, and when she spoke, her voice was very small and very old.

"He remembered something. Just now. When he died. I felt it come off him like heat." She reached up and took Lior's hand, charcoal-stained fingers lacing through sword-stained ones. "He remembered his mother's kitchen. It had yellow tiles."

Lior's throat closed. There was no time for grief, and she grieved anyway, for half a second, walking fast — for the boy in the grey cloak, for the yellow-tiled kitchen, for every name ground smooth by the Regent's great machine of forgetting.

Then the first horn sounded from the square, and the second answered it, and the night filled with the sound of a lie organizing itself to defend its own existence.

Wren ran beside her, Sefi bouncing on his hip, the old archivist's breath tearing in ragged rhythm, and as they plunged into the quarry cuts — into the dark wound in the hillside that led, if the maps in Lior's blood still told the truth, inward, toward the buried heart of everything — Sefi began, softly, to sing.

Not the anthem this time. Something else. A walking song, a road song, one Lior hadn't heard since she was small enough to ride her grandmother's shoulders through the high passes — and it should have been erased, it *had* been erased, and yet it rose out of the child like water out of rock, and Lior found her own lips shaping the words she did not remember knowing, and Wren, stumbling, weeping openly now and pretending it was the wind, hummed the harmony he had catalogued and mourned and never dared to sing.

Three voices in the quarry dark, one of them seven years old, carrying a country on their backs into the mountains while horns bayed behind them.

Whatever waited inward — the buried seats, the oath, the cost her grandmother's ghost had not yet shown her — it would have to reckon with this: the erasure had missed. Somewhere, in an orphanage that used to be a counting house, it had missed a child.

And she was singing.
