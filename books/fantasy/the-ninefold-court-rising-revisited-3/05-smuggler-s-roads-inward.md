## 5. Smuggler's Roads Inward

The road Lior chose did not exist.

Not on any chart, not in any Warden's ledger, not even in the memory of the mountains themselves — or so she liked to tell herself on the nights when pride was all she had left to carry. It was a smuggler's road, which meant it was a rumor wearing boots: a thread of goat-track and dry riverbed stitched along the spine of the Ashen Reaches where the old wards had never reached because no one had ever thought a country worth warding would bother to hide from itself.

She led them up out of Emberhold before dawn, when the town's chimneys still breathed their small grey prayers into the cold, and by the time the sun cleared the eastern crags they were three switchbacks above it and already ghosts. Behind them, the valley lay pale and ordinary, its people sweeping doorsteps around a nation none of them could name. Lior did not look back. Looking back was a luxury for people whose maps still told the truth.

"Is this a road?" Sefi asked.

It was the first thing the girl had said in an hour. She stood ankle-deep in scree with her bundle of drawings clutched against her chest like a shield, staring at the slope ahead — a chaos of broken slate and frost-shattered stone that rose toward a notch in the ridge so narrow it might have been a wound rather than a pass.

"It's a road," Lior said, "if you're willing to be the kind of traveler who doesn't need one."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Lior agreed. "It's better than one."

Behind Sefi, Wren Vale was already breathing hard. The archivist climbed like a man who had spent thirty years being carried by chairs and staircases, his satchel of salvaged documents swinging against his hip with every lurching step, his face the color of old tallow beneath the wind's red slap. He caught Lior watching him and lifted his chin with what dignity he could salvage.

"I catalogued eleven hundred leagues of borderland," he said. "I never once walked any of it."

"Now you have a chance to correct the record."

"My knees decline the amendment."

Lior almost smiled. Almost. Her mouth had grown out of the habit; grief had set into her jaw like frost into stone, and everything warm had to break through it slowly. She turned back to the slope instead, testing the first handholds, finding the line she remembered — the seam of darker rock, the cairn her mother's people had stacked generations ago and that smugglers had rebuilt every spring since, each generation adding a stone as if the mountain could be taught to hold its shape by stubbornness alone.

The cairn was there. That small mercy nearly undid her. In a world where countries could be deleted overnight, at least someone had kept stacking stones.

* * *

They climbed through the morning, and the mountain taught them its lessons in order: first humility, then hunger, then the particular silence of high places where sound goes to die and a person can hear their own heart apologizing.

Lior moved at the front and listened with her whole body. This was the craft her grandmother had beaten into her — *a guide is not a pair of legs, girl; a guide is an ear with a destination* — and the ear heard things now that made the skin crawl along her arms. Wind moving wrong through the notch below. A rhythm under the scree-slide that wasn't water. Once, faint and terrible, the flat mechanical cadence of voices speaking in unison, the way Wardens spoke when they recited the erasure litany over ground they suspected of remembering.

Wardens in the high passes. Wardens, plural, organized, chanting. The Regent of Ash was not content to let Thornfell stay forgotten; he wanted it *confirmed* forgotten, patrolled into permanence, its very geography taught to agree with its absence.

She held up a fist and the column stopped — Sefi freezing instantly with a soldier's instinct no orphan should own, Wren colliding gently with both of them and whispering an apology to the rocks.

Below them, maybe four hundred feet down and across a gully of blue shadow, three grey cloaks moved along the true road — the honest road, the cart-road that wound down from Emberhold toward the interior. Even at that distance Lior could feel the wrongness coming off them like heat off a forge. Where they walked, the air went smooth. Birds went quiet. The scree itself seemed to stop settling, as though the mountain were holding its breath in their presence, embarrassed to exist.

"They're checking the wards," Wren breathed. "Or what's left of them. The border collapsed, but the Regent's people are re-lacing it with something new. Something that doesn't just hide the country." His throat worked. "Something that hunts the memory of it."

"In people," Lior said.

"In people."

Sefi was staring down at the grey cloaks with an expression Lior couldn't read — not fear exactly, though fear lived in it, but something older and more personal, the way a child looks at the person who burned down a house they can't quite prove they lived in. Her small hands had gone white around her drawings.

"We go up," Lior said. "There's a shelf above the notch. Old shepherd track. We cross there tonight, when the Wardens bed down. They don't climb after dark. Their magic needs light to read by — some superstition about the written word, I think."

"You know that how?" Wren asked.

"I've been dodging the Regent's men since I was fourteen years younger than her." Lior nodded at Sefi. "Different uniforms. Same grey."

* * *

The shelf crossing took until moonrise and cost Lior more than she admitted.

It was a ledge barely wider than a coffin, running two hundred feet across the face of the cliff, crumbled in places to a hand-width of crumbling stone over a drop that the darkness made bottomless. She crossed it first with the rope, anchored it to a spur of granite that had held fast since her grandmother's day, and then brought them across one at a time — Sefi first, because the child crossed like a spider, fearless in the way of those who had already survived worse than falling; then Wren, who crossed like a man being audited by gravity, eyes shut, murmuring archival citations under his breath as though the Dewey classification of his own terror might somehow file it away.

Lior came last, unroped, walking the ledge the way other people walked a hallway.

Halfway across, the wind found her. It came up the cliff face in a long cold exhalation, and it smelled wrong — ash and ink and something sweeter underneath, something like the smell of a book burning closed. For one vertiginous instant the ledge wasn't under her feet at all. She was standing in her grandmother's kitchen, seven years old, watching Adaeze Oyelaran seal a letter with wax the color of dried blood, and her grandmother had looked up at her with an expression Lior had never understood until this season of her life —

— and then the wind passed, and the kitchen was gone, and the ledge was stone again, real and indifferent, and Lior Oyelaran breathed until her hands stopped shaking.

Ghosts didn't haunt houses, she was learning. They haunted weather.

At the far side, Sefi sat wrapped in both their blankets, awake, watchful. Wren dozed sitting upright like a horse. And beyond the notch, spread out below them in the starless dark, the interior of Thornfell waited — the deep country, the Ninefold country, the land that had been unmade and yet somehow, impossibly, still rolled away under her boots when she walked it.

That was the thing the Wardens hadn't managed to explain, Lior thought. You could erase a country from memory, from record, from the tongues of its own children. But you couldn't erase the ground. The ground stayed. The ground remembered being walked on. Every smuggler's trail was a scar the forgetting hadn't reached.

"Can I ask you something?" Sefi said quietly, when Lior settled beside her.

"You can ask. I might lie."

"Why are you doing this? Taking us inward. You could've gone over the border. Out. Everyone who remembers is trying to get out."

Lior looked at the dark country below. Somewhere out there were nine empty seats in a buried hall, and an oath her blood had helped swear, and a century of quiet that had been bought with a price nobody alive had agreed to pay.

"Because I'm a guide," she said. "And my country just became the one place on earth no map will take anyone. Somebody has to know the way in."

Sefi considered this with the grave thoroughness of the young. "That's not the whole reason."

"No," Lior said. "But it's the part I'm giving away free."

* * *

They fell into a rhythm over the days that followed, the way travelers do, the way families accidentally do.

Dawn: cold rations eaten standing, Lior reading the sky and the wind while the others broke camp. Midmorning: movement, always movement, along routes that existed only in the muscle of her memory — a smuggler's inheritance of hidden springs and hollowed cairns and cave mouths screened by hawthorn, each one left stocked or marked by some nameless predecessor in the trade, a chain of kindness stretching back through decades of people who had never met but trusted each other anyway. It occurred to Lior, somewhere around the fourth day, that this network was itself a kind of court. An unchartered, unwritten court of the dispossessed, holding its sessions in waypoints and leaving its laws carved into tree bark.

Noon: rest, and lessons.

It was Wren who started them. She shouldn't have been surprised — guilt makes teachers of the worst men sometimes, and Wren wore his guilt the way Sefi wore her bundle of drawings, close and constant and slightly too heavy. He would sit on whatever flat stone the mountain provided with Sefi cross-legged before him, and he would teach her the old law of the Ninefold Court, reciting it from memory in the singsong cadence of a man who had once been forced to memorize the very constitution he'd helped destroy.

"A seat is not inherited," he would say. "A seat is *owed*. To the river that fed you, the road that carried you, the stranger who fed you when the harvest failed. The Court sits for the country, not above it."

"The Court sits *for* the country," Sefi repeated, solemn as a funeral.

"Nine seats. Nine duties. Hearth and Harvest. Road and River. Sword and Song. Law and Ledger." He paused, always at the same pause, his voice going strange. "And Memory."

"And Memory," Sefi echoed. Then, with the merciless directness of children: "Which seat did you betray?"

Wren never flinched. Lior, watching from ten paces off while she pretended to mend a strap, saw him take the blow full in the chest and keep his voice level, and she revised her opinion of him by a small and grudging degree.

"Memory," he said. "I was archivist to the Court of Emberhold, which was Memory's last living fragment. When the Regent's writ came, I catalogued what we were told to catalogue, and I shelved what we were told to shelve, and I told myself paper is patient, that someday the truth could be retrieved." He looked at his hands. "Paper is patient. Archivists are not required to be brave. That's the flaw in my profession, and I am its finest specimen."

Sefi absorbed this. "You're brave now."

"I'm terrified now. It looks similar from the outside."

Lior looked away before either of them caught her listening. She mended the strap badly. She had been mending things badly for a week now — straps, fires, plans — and she suspected it had nothing to do with her skill and everything to do with the fact that some traitorous part of her had begun to arrange these two, the shamed archivist and the remembering child, into positions around her life, like stones in a cairn. Like family. Like the beginning of a thing she had sworn off building, because everything she built seemed destined to be erased or betrayed by the blood that raised her.

*A guide is an ear with a destination.* Her grandmother's voice, in the wind again. But her grandmother had also said: *never sleep facing your companions, girl. People you love become hostages.* Adaeze Oyelaran had died alone in a house full of locked doors, and everyone had called it dignity.

Lior slept facing the fire, and the fire faced the stars that weren't there, and she pretended not to hear the lesson-drone behind her, and she pretended not to store every word of it away in the vault where she kept everything that mattered.

* * *

On the sixth night they nearly died, and the night after that, they became something.

The trouble was a patrol — five Wardens this time, working a ridgeline sweep with lanterns that burned a color lanterns shouldn't burn, a grey-white flame that made Lior's teeth ache and turned her sense of direction to porridge. The company was caught in open heath between two granite tors with no cover closer than a quarter mile of rolling, exposed ground.

"Down," Lior hissed, and they went down into the heather, bellies to the frozen earth, breath held, hearts loud enough to give them away to any god paying attention.

The lantern-light swept toward them. Under it, Lior felt her own memories start to smear — her mother's face softening at the edges, the name *Thornfell* dissolving on her tongue like sugar, and she understood with cold clarity what the lanterns were for. Not searching. *Erasing.* Sweeping the land clean of anything that had begun, however faintly, to remember.

Beside her, Sefi began to hum.

It was the anthem — the erased one, the impossible one, the melody that existed nowhere in any surviving record because Wren had personally catalogued its destruction. Four notes, rising, then falling like a bird deciding against flight. Lior's whole body went rigid. *Stop*, she wanted to say. *They'll hear you.* But the girl hummed it into the heather, soft as breath, and something happened.

The lantern-light stuttered.

One of the Wardens, forty feet away, made a small confused sound — the sound of a man whose mind had just snagged on something, a splinter, a name. He lowered his lantern. He looked at the heather, at the hills, at his own gloved hands, and Lior watched his face do what faces did in Emberhold when the erasure slipped: the blankness cracking, the confusion flooding in, the terrible dawning question — *what was I guarding? why does this ground feel like home?*

"Sefi," Lior whispered.

"I know," the girl whispered back, and hummed louder.

Two more Wardens faltered. The other two grabbed their comrades' arms and dragged them away up the ridge, quick now, frightened, and the lanterns dwindled into the dark like sinking stars — the only stars this sky had left — and then the heath was silent and empty and humming with a song that should not have existed.

For a long moment nobody moved.

Then Wren sat up out of the heather, and his face in the darkness was transformed, lit from within by the expression of a man watching his life's work reverse itself. "Her voice," he breathed. "It's not just that she remembers. Her memory is *contagious*. Spoken names, sung names — she can burn the erasure right off—" He stopped. He looked at Sefi the way scholars look at manuscripts that rewrite history, and Lior stepped, without quite deciding to, between them.

"She's a child," Lior said, "before she's a weapon."

"I know." Wren's voice cracked. "Gods forgive me, I know. But Lior — do you understand what she is? Do you understand what we're carrying?"

What they were carrying, Lior thought, was a nine-year-old girl asleep against her shoulder within the hour, worn out from singing a country back into existence one note at a time. What they were carrying was an archivist who sat up half the night by dead-coal firelight sketching diagrams in his journal — *transmission vectors, resonance ranges, the mechanics of spoken remembrance* — while pretending he wasn't looking at the sleeping child with something dangerously close to hope.

What they were carrying, Lior admitted to the dark, to the missing stars, to the ghost-wind that smelled like her grandmother's sealing wax, was a home. Small, ragged, mobile, absurd. Three people and a stolen cause, moving through a country that officially did not exist, along roads that officially led nowhere.

She caught herself smiling at the fire. She let it stand.

* * *

On the ninth day, the road gave them a gift, which was how Lior knew the mountain was warning her.

It came at a waypoint — one of hers, a hollow beneath a leaning slab of granite where generations of smugglers had left supplies, and inside it, tucked in oilcloth against the damp, she found a packet that stopped her breath. Maps. Fresh ones, drawn within the year, in a hand she knew better than her own heartbeat: her grandmother's. Adaeze Oyelaran had walked these passes. Adaeze Oyelaran had cached charts here — detailed, current, annotated in her cramped iron script — marking not just trails but *Warden patterns*, patrol schedules, the locations of new grey-cloak garrisons that had not existed when Lior fled Emberhold's valley.

Her grandmother, who had sworn the founding oath. Her grandmother, who had helped erase the country.

Had spent her last years mapping the way back in.

Lior knelt in the cold hollow a long time, the oilcloth open across her knees, grief and fury and a wild, wordless tenderness all fighting for the same throat. At the map's edge, in the margin, one line of annotation, underlined twice:

*If you are reading this, the oath has cost more than it bought. There are nine seats. Come home, child. Bring whoever remembers.*

She did not show the others. Not yet. She folded the maps away against her ribs, next to the place where her heartbeat lived, and led them on — east and down now, following her grandmother's iron script toward the deep country, toward the buried hall, toward whatever waited among the nine empty seats.

Behind them, far to the west, the horizon flickered once, softly, like a lamp being carried through a distant house.

Sefi saw it too. The child went still on the trail, her head cocked, her small face suddenly ancient.

"Lior," she said. "Someone's singing at a shrine. Someone who isn't me."

The wind shifted, and carried it to them across nine miles of stone: faint, rising, unmistakable — four notes, then a fall, the anthem sung by a voice that was not a child's, from a place no living soul should have remembered.

Lior put her hand on her sword and turned them toward it.
