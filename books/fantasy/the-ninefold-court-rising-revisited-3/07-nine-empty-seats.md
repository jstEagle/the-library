## 7. Nine Empty Seats

The door in the mountain had no handle, no hinge, no seam that any eye could find. It opened, as Wren Vale had always known it would, to a name.

Lior spoke it — a word in the old court-tongue, worn smooth as riverstone from a century of disuse, and the rock simply *agreed* to be elsewhere. Stone slid against stone with a sound like a held breath released after a hundred years, and the dark beyond exhaled cold air scented with dust and something older: lamp-oil gone to resin, cedar, the faint mineral sweetness of silver left untended.

Wren stood at the threshold with his lantern raised and did not move.

He had drawn this doorway once, in ink, on vellum, in the Archive of the Regency, cataloguing it under *Structures, Subterranean, Ceremonial — Access Protocols Pending*. He had written *pending* because writing *sealed by oath* would have required him to ask questions his superiors had forbidden, and Wren Vale had been very good, for thirty-one years, at not asking forbidden questions. The ink of his own hand stared up at him now in memory, neat and small and complicit, and he felt it like a stone swallowed whole.

"Archivist." Lior's voice came back to him through the dark. "You're going to want to see this."

That was not true. That was precisely what he did not want. But shame, he had learned, was a country you could not refuse entry to; you could only walk through it.

He went in.

* * *

The passage descended in long switchbacks carved with such precision that no tool-mark remained, only a polish like the inside of a shell. Sefi's small boots rang ahead of them, her breath making little ghosts in the lantern-light. She had stopped asking questions an hour ago — a bad sign in a child, Wren thought, or perhaps the best sign possible. Children who stopped asking questions were children who had begun to understand how much there was to be afraid of.

The rescued soldiers walked behind them, six of them now, moving with the careful silence of men relearning their own names. Marta Corvane kept touching the wall as they went, dragging her fingertips along the stone as though confirming the world still had texture. Since the ford, since Sefi had spoken the old names into her and burned the erasure away, she had been quiet in the way of someone listening to a sound only she could hear.

"The walls are singing," she said suddenly, and then looked embarrassed.

"They're not singing," Wren said, before he could stop himself. His voice echoed strangely. "They're *remembering out loud*. It's different."

Lior glanced back at him. In the lantern light her face was all planes and patience, the face of a woman who guided people through mountains for coin and had lately been asked to guide them through something with no far side. "You've been here before," she said. Not a question.

"I catalogued this place," Wren said. "I never set foot in it. There's a difference, and I've spent three weeks learning how wide that difference is."

"How can you catalogue a room you've never entered?"

"Because the people who erased Thornfell needed to know what they'd erased. You cannot forget something properly unless you first write down everything it was. Otherwise it leaks back. Otherwise someone finds a song, a ruin, a name, and starts pulling the thread." He heard his own voice flatten into the archivist's cadence, the one he'd used for eleven years over ledgers and lists, and hated it, and could not stop it. "I wrote the inventory. Nine seats. Fragmentary enchantments, classification uncertain. Significance: historical. I gave it two pages." His laugh came out wrong. "*Two pages.*"

Sefi turned around and walked backwards to look up at him, which children could do in the dark without tripping, which Wren had never understood and envied. "What's significance historical mean?"

"It means important but finished," he said. "It means dead things we keep records of so we don't have to feel anything about them."

"Oh," Sefi said, with the terrible economy of children. "Like me."

"No," Wren said, too fast. "No. Like — nothing is like you. That's rather the entire point of you."

She considered this, nodded once, satisfied, and turned forward again. Behind them Marta Corvane made a low sound that might have been a laugh strangled in its cradle.

The passage opened without warning.

* * *

Wren had imagined the throne hall a thousand times, the way a man imagines a room he has described but never seen: nine seats, he had written, arranged in a crescent facing a dais, dimensions estimated. He had imagined grandeur. He had imagined decay. He had prepared himself for both.

He had not prepared himself for the hall to be *lit*.

There were no lamps. There was no fire. But light lived in the stone itself, rising out of the floor in slow veins like dawn coming up under ice, pale gold shot through with green, and where it touched the walls the carvings woke. Wren stood at the mouth of the descending stair with his useless lantern and watched Thornfell's history move through the rock: harvests, coronations, rivers given names, a woman setting her crown on a child's head, a line of nine figures joining hands beneath nine stars. The carvings flowed like water frozen mid-pour, and every few heartbeats the whole cycle began again, patient as seasons.

"It knows we're here," Lior said softly. Beside her, Adaeze Oyelaran's ghost stood with her hands folded, and even the ghost seemed struck silent — a woman who had died bound to an oath, looking at the thing the oath had hollowed.

The nine seats waited on their crescent dais, and Wren's inventory, that miserable accurate document, had not lied: nine chairs of grey mountain-stone, each carved from a single block, each distinct. No two bore the same emblem. A seat marked with wheat and a sickle. One with a sword broken across a shield. One with an open book. One with scales whose pans had been carved as cups, as if justice were meant to hold water, or wine, or tears. One with a lark in flight. One with crossed keys. One with a mother's face ringed by hands. One with a hammer and a sprouting seed. And the ninth, highest, plainest — unmarked, smooth-backed, waiting.

Empty. All of them empty. A century empty.

But not *dead*.

As Wren watched, the light in the floor gathered itself toward the seats in slow tides, pooling around their bases, climbing their legs, and each time it reached a seat's arms it flared — briefly, hopefully, like a dog hearing footsteps at a door it has been told no one will ever come through again — and failed, and sank back, and gathered itself to try once more.

"Fragmentary enchantments," Wren whispered. "Classification uncertain." He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "I sat at a desk in Ashfall with a cup of tea going cold beside me and I wrote *classification uncertain* about *this*."

"You didn't know," Lior said.

"I didn't want to know. There's a difference, and I've spent three weeks learning how wide *that* difference is." He stepped down onto the floor of the hall and the light rose to meet his boots, curious, tasting him. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? The erasure didn't kill the Court. It couldn't. An oath can dissolve a government, it can scatter a people, it can take stars out of the sky — but it cannot make stone stop loving what it was carved to hold. So they buried it instead. They put the body under the mountain and wrote two pages about it and called the wound a grave."

Marta Corvane had stopped at the base of the dais. She was staring at the seat with the broken sword, and her jaw was working. "My grandmother sang," she said. "Songs with no words in them. We thought she'd gone soft in the head. She'd stand at the wash-line and sing sounds, just sounds, and when I asked her what the song was called she'd say—" the soldier's voice caught, steadied, "—she'd say *it doesn't have a name anymore, little one, but it remembers having one.*" She looked back at Sefi. "The anthem. She was humming the anthem. They took her words and left her the tune."

"Yes," Sefi said simply. The girl had walked to the center of the crescent, below the ninth seat, and stood there with her chin lifted, listening to something none of them could hear. "This is where the speaker stands. When the Court sits. This is where you say the names and everyone says them back."

"You know that?" Lior asked. "From your dreams?"

"Not dreams." Sefi frowned, searching for the word, finding it. "Inheritance."

* * *

They split, as exploring parties do, into the natural orbits of their curiosities. The soldiers drifted toward the martial carvings, touching swords rendered in stone. Lior climbed to the ninth seat and laid her palm flat on its back, and whatever passed between her and it made her shoulders drop half an inch, like a knot loosening. Adaeze's ghost moved down the crescent from seat to seat, and Wren noticed — filed, helplessly, in the old professional reflex — that she did not touch any of them. She walked the row like a woman passing the houses of creditors.

Wren went to the seat with the open book.

Of course he did. He was what he was. He knelt before it the way he had once knelt before the Archival Seal of the Regency, and found the carving worn differently than the others — not by age but by use, by centuries of hands gripping the armrests, by readers leaning in. The book's stone pages were blank. As he watched, the underlight pulsed through them, and for one heartbeat he saw script: columns, marginalia, a hand he almost recognized.

His own.

Not his own. A predecessor's. Some archivist of the living Court, ten decades dead, keeping the same records he had kept, except these had been *for* the country instead of about its corpse. The realization went through him like cold water: there had been a chair for what he did. What he did had once mattered. It had been one of nine pillars holding up a sky full of named stars.

And the Regency had taken that and made of it a shovel.

"Wren." Lior's voice, close. She had come down from the ninth seat. "You're doing the thing where you punish yourself in advance. I've watched you do it for three weeks. It's very efficient. You save time by skipping straight to guilt."

"This room deserves someone better than me standing in it."

"Probably." She said it without cruelty, which somehow made it land harder. "But it got the nine who swore the oath, and the heir who keeps the forgetting running, and a hundred years of nobody at all. So it seems to me the standard is low." She crouched beside him. "Tell me what you're seeing."

So he told her. About the inventories. About the Erasure Catalogue, forty-one volumes, of which he had written seven. About the night his superior — a kind man, a tired man, a man who brought biscuits to junior staff — had explained that the work was mercy, that forgetting was a bandage, that a clean record of the amputation prevented infection. About the moment, three years in, when Wren had cross-referenced a grain-shipment ledger from a century ago against a casualty roll and understood, in one white-hot instant, exactly what price the founding oath had bought and what it had cost, and had closed the ledger and said nothing and requested more biscuits.

"I was good at my job," he said. "Do you know how much evil requires that? How much of it runs on competent, punctual, well-rested people who write *classification uncertain*?"

Lior was quiet a moment. Below them, Sefi had begun, very softly, to hum.

The tune filled the hall the way water fills a bowl. The light in the floor answered it — brightened, gathered, leaned toward her like grass toward sun — and on the dais the nine empty seats flared together for the first time, all at once, gold-green fire standing in the air above each armrest like candles lit for absent guests. For three heartbeats the buried throne hall was a throne hall again. Wren could see it: figures in those seats, the speaker below with her lifted chin, the anthem sung by nine hundred throats, the stars visible through some high shaft now sealed, nine lights answering nine seats.

Then the flare guttered. The hall dimmed to embers. Sefi's humming trailed off and she looked around, bewildered, betrayed.

"Why did it stop?"

"Because one voice isn't nine," Adaeze's ghost said, from the foot of the dais. Her voice carried oddly, like wind through a keyhole. "Because a Court is not a room. It is a vow, spoken by many, kept by many. And yours, granddaughter-of-my-blood, is not yet sworn." The ghost's eyes found Wren, and there was no accusation in them, which was worse. "Nor is the debt of the one who catalogued us paid. Though I begin to think he intends to pay it."

"That's the trouble with confessions," Wren said, getting to his feet. His knees cracked. His voice, to his own surprise, did not waver. "They're only the down payment."

He looked up at the ninth seat, the plain one, the empty one, where Lior's hand had rested. Then past it, to the vaulted dark above, where a round shaft of stone rose out of sight — the chimney through which, a century ago, the Court had watched its stars.

"One more thing I catalogued," he said quietly. "A mechanism. Sealed with the rest. In my notes I called it *the astronomical aperture, function unknown*." He laughed, and this time it came out almost honest. "Function unknown. I knew exactly what its function was. Every archivist did. We just weren't allowed to say the word."

"Which word?" Sefi asked.

Wren looked at her, at the child who remembered a sky she'd never seen, and gave her the truth like a man handing over contraband.

"Stars," he said. "The word was *stars*."

And as he spoke it, high above, behind a hundred years of sealed stone, something faint and far and impossibly patient knocked — once — as if against the other side of the ceiling of the world.

Above them, muffled by the mountain, by the oath, by the dark that had eaten the heavens: singing. Hundreds of voices, growing louder, coming down the smugglers' roads and the buried passes, carrying a tune with no words that was remembering its way back to having them.

The Wardens' horns sounded behind it, distant and furious.

"They followed the marchers," Lior breathed. "They followed the anthem *here*."

Wren turned to face the stair, and found, to his astonishment, that his fear had been replaced by something older and sturdier — the particular calm of a man who has finally, after eleven years, located the correct page.

"Well," he said. "I suppose we'd better decide quickly what this room is going to be. A tomb, or a beginning. Because in about an hour, everyone who ever forgot us is going to know exactly where we're standing."
