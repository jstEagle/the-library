## 8. The Usurper's Offer

The throne hall breathed cold.

Lior felt it before she saw it — a draft rising through the mountain's bones, carrying the smell of deep stone and old ash, the way a cellar smells of earth that has never once seen sun. The tunnel Wren had charted from Adaeze's murmured directions opened without warning into darkness so complete that her lantern seemed to shrink inside it, a single seed of light swallowed by a mouth.

"Nine seats," Wren whispered behind her, his voice cracking on the second word. He had not spoken above a whisper since they'd entered. "I catalogued them. I stood in this room with my ledger and my ink and I wrote down what each seat held, and I — I did it *neatly*, Lior. Neat handwriting. That was my contribution to the end of a country."

"Later," Lior said, though there was nothing left to do later but feel it.

She lifted the lantern high.

The hall unfolded in tiers of pale stone, pillars carved as trees that had never grown anywhere on earth — their branches spreading into vaults hung with stone fruit, stone birds mid-flight, stone stars. And there at the center, on a dais worn smooth by a century of nothing, nine chairs arranged in a crescent like arms opening or closing, depending on how you looked at them. Eight stood empty, their surfaces dulled with dust. The ninth —

The ninth held a man.

He sat with his elbows on his knees and his hands folded, an old man in a grey coat buttoned to the throat, and he was watching her with the patient expression of someone who had waited out longer vigils than hers. No crown. No guards that she could see, though the shadows between the pillars had the particular density of places where Wardens might be standing and choosing not to breathe.

"You brought a lantern," the man said. His voice was dry and unhurried, a clerk's voice, a voice for reading inventories aloud. "Most who come here bring swords. Or prayers. The lantern is more honest. You came to *see*."

Lior set Sefi gently behind her. The girl had gone rigid, her small hand fisted in Lior's coat, and when Lior glanced back Sefi's eyes were wide and fixed on the seated man with something worse than fear.

"That's him," Sefi whispered. "That's the one who turned the page."

"Hush now." Lior drew her sword anyway. It felt absurd — steel against a tired official in a dusty chair — but absurdity had been the shape of every day since the stars went out. "Wren. Take her back to the tunnel mouth."

"I wouldn't," the Regent said mildly. "The child is safer in the open than either of you understands. The wards are thin here. What sleeps beneath this hall does not distinguish well between intruders, and your sword—" he nodded at her blade, courteous as a host remarking on weather "—would only make noise. Sit, Lior Oyelaran. Or stand. But hear me. You have come a long way through roads that no longer exist to reach a room that is not officially here. Let me at least be worth the walk."

* * *

She did not sit. She climbed instead — up the shallow steps to the dais, past the eighth seat, until she stood close enough to see the Regent of Ash plainly, and what she saw unsettled her more than any tyrant's face would have.

He looked like a man at the end of a very long shift. Grey at the temples, grey in the coat, grey in the skin around his eyes, which were the color of slate after rain. His hands, folded in his lap, were ink-stained at the fingers. Not blood-stained. Ink. She had imagined — in the fevered hours on the smugglers' roads, watching Warden fires from ridgelines — she had imagined the man who unmade Thornfell as something vast and burning. A storm given a body.

Instead he was an administrator. And somehow that was the most frightening thing she had ever looked at.

"You know my name," she said.

"Of course." He said it without apology. "You are the last Oyelaran who can still hear the word *Thornfell*. Do you know how short that list has become? There is you. There is the ghost bound to the shrine at Vael's Crossing. There is a child." His gaze slid, gentle and terrible, to Sefi at the tunnel mouth, and Lior stepped sideways to break the line of sight. "Three people. A country remembered by three souls, one of whom is dead. When you die, the list shortens again."

"The list doesn't matter." Her grip tightened on the sword. "The country matters."

"Does it?" He unfolded his hands at last and gestured — a small, weary motion that took in the hall, the seats, the dark beyond the stone. "Show me its borders. Name its neighbors. Recite the treaty lines. You can't. No one can, because the world's memory of Thornfell was the treaty, and we burned it to keep a greater fire from catching. You've walked the passes your whole life, guide. Tell me — when the wards fell and the stars went out, what did you lose? Your maps lied to you. Yes. I know. I am sorry for that; it was never meant to touch those born under the wards. But consider what the maps would have told the world if we had let them read true."

Lior thought of Emberhold — the baker who could not name his nation, smiling politely as though she'd asked him about the weather. The records cold under her hands. The anthem that lived only in a child's humming.

"You erased them," she said. "Living people. Their mothers' names. Their dead."

"We fed them." For the first time his voice moved, a hairline fracture in the clerk's calm. "Do you know what the borderlands were, a hundred years ago? A knife-fight that had lasted three generations. Every harvest burned by raiders from the east, every winter a ledger of children who didn't see spring. Nine houses, nine armies, nine proud banners, and grain enough for none of it. Your grandmother stood where you're standing and did the arithmetic, Lior. War, or forgetting. Blood without end, or peace bought with memory. She chose the peace. So did eight others. So did the world's own archivists — men like your friend there, who signed the instruments and wrote neatly." A glance at Wren, who flinched as if struck. "We have all been paying the installments ever since."

"And the stars?" Lior's voice came out low and rough. "Was the sky part of the arithmetic?"

"The sky was collateral. The oath needed power, and the oldest power in this land was starlight sworn to the Court's keeping. We spent it." He said it flatly, without ornament. "I will not pretend it was anything but a wound. I have kept the bandage on for sixty years. That is my whole office, girl. I am the bandage."

* * *

Silence pooled in the hall. Somewhere far above, water ticked through stone, counting seconds no one had asked it to keep.

Lior became aware, distantly, that Wren had crept forward to the base of the dais and was staring at the ninth seat — not at the man in it but at the carvings along its arm, the oath-marks worn nearly smooth. His lips moved, reading. She watched him go pale.

"You were the ninth heir," Wren said hoarsely. "In the catalogue. Seat of Ashes and Accountings. The line of the ninth speaker—"

"Ended with me, yes." The Regent smiled, and it was such a small, tired smile that Lior's sword arm actually loosened. "No children. I made sure of it. One does not pass a debt like mine down out of cruelty. Which brings me—" he rose, slowly, joints audibly protesting, an old man standing up from a hard chair "—to why I waited for you instead of sending the Wardens."

He descended one step. Lior raised her blade level with his chest, and he stopped, unbothered, hands spread at his sides.

"Kill me if you like. Genuinely — consider it. My death ends the succession of the forgetting; the oath would fray within a year, and everything you want would happen *messily*. The borders tear open. The neighbors I keep blind come flooding over passes they suddenly remember wanting. Emberhold burns first, because Emberhold sits on the road everyone wants. You would get your country back the way a drowning woman gets the river's bottom." His slate eyes held hers, steady, sorrowful, absolutely reasonable. "Or."

"Or," Lior echoed.

"Or you take the seat." He gestured at the ninth chair behind him, and lamplight slid along its worn arm. "Not the old seat. A new one, reforged to the same purpose. Inherit the forgetting as the Oyelarans always meant it to be inherited — as stewardship. Keep the bandage on, but *you* keep it, with your living hands and your conscience, instead of me and my failing heart. Restore what can be restored without waking what must sleep. Give Emberhold its festivals back. Its songs — the safe ones. Let your grandmother's ghost rest knowing her bargain is kept by willing blood." He paused, and his voice dropped to something almost kind. "You could give them back ninety percent of what they lost, Lior Oyelaran, and no one would ever burn for it. No war. No flood across the passes. A throne, a country, a people who mostly remember being happy. All you must carry is the ten percent. The names. The stars. The truth that the peace is rented, not owned. That is the whole price. It fits in one person, if the person is strong. Your grandmother proved it. She carried it for forty years and died loved."

Behind her, Sefi made a small sound.

Lior did not turn around. If she turned around she would see the child's face, and the child's face would ask the question the Regent had carefully not asked aloud: *what about me?* What about the girl who hummed the erased anthem, whose dreams drew the nine seats, whose mind was the last living archive of everything the ten percent contained? Where did Sefi fit in a kingdom built on her silence?

And the horrible thing — the thing Lior would lie awake examining later, turning it over like a stone with something alive beneath — was that the offer was *good*. It was good the way well-water is good after a long road. She could feel herself drinking it in despite everything: a restored homeland, no war, her people's laughter returned, and all it cost was her complicity, forever, in the erasure of her grandmother's sin. To become the next link in a chain her blood had forged. To sit where the Regent sat, ink-stained and grey and sorry, explaining kindly to some future Lior why the stars had to stay dark.

Temptation, she thought, does not wear a monster's face. It wears a tired clerk's coat and offers you tea and tells you the truth in a reasonable voice, and that is how you know it is dangerous.

"My grandmother," Lior said slowly, "died bound to an oath she regretted. Bound. I've spoken with her ghost, sir, and bound is the word — she cannot leave the shrine, cannot rest, cannot even properly *die*, because the founding oath holds her to anchor it. Is that your inheritance too? Is that what you're offering me — forty years of being a bandage, and then eternity as the scar?"

Something flickered across the Regent's face. Pain, quickly filed away.

"Yes," he said simply. "That is the whole of it. I won't gild it. I have spent sixty years learning exactly what your grandmother learned: that a lie told to save a people becomes a cell with the savior inside it. And I would still sign again tomorrow." He straightened his coat with two precise tugs, a man squaring paperwork. "Because the alternative, every time I have priced it, is graves. Thousands of them. Most of them children's. Choose your grave-digger, Lior. Me, or the eastern kings."

* * *

"No."

The word came from the tunnel mouth, small and clear as a bell struck once. Sefi stepped out from behind Wren before Lior could catch her sleeve, marched across the hall floor with her chin up and her fists at her sides, and planted herself at the foot of the dais directly below the Regent of Ash.

"You forgot the singing," she said.

The Regent looked down at her. For the first time, the clerk's composure shifted — not much. A settling, like dust disturbed. "Child—"

"In Emberhold nobody knows the anthem. Nobody knows the *names*. My mother sang me the river-names before—" Sefi stopped, jaw tight, and started again, louder. "Before. And when I sing them, the old people cry without knowing why. That's what you don't put in your ledgers. You saved the bread and you threw away the *songs*, and the songs are half of what a country *is*, and I'm the only jar left holding them, and you want to hand the country to her"—she jabbed a finger up at Lior—"and lock me in a room somewhere quiet so the jar doesn't spill." Her voice cracked, and she let it. "Well. I heard you say the oath would fray in a year if you died. Fraying means cracks. Cracks mean light gets in. I've *seen* what comes through cracks."

"Sefi." Lior crouched, caught the girl's shoulder. "It isn't yours to answer."

"It's everybody's," Sefi said fiercely, wiping her nose with the back of her wrist. "That's the whole point of a country."

The Regent exhaled — a long breath, and Lior realized with a strange lurch that he was looking at the child the way a man looks at a ledger entry he has dreaded for decades. Not with hatred. With resignation.

"So it begins already," he said quietly. "The fraying. I told myself I had another decade." He turned back to Lior, and now, at last, the tiredness in him showed through completely — no mask of office, just an old man standing in the dark under stone stars that were only stone. "You will refuse me. I can see it assembling behind your eyes; you have your grandmother's stubborn chin and none of her patience. So hear the last thing I came to say, freely given, because you deserve honest counsel from your enemy: the founding oath is not paper, girl. It is not words in a book you can cross out. It is load-bearing. Nine speakers swore it and nine fragments of power hold it up, and when you pull it down — and you will, I see that now — you must have something ready to bear the load, or the whole roof of the world's forgetting comes down at once, on Emberhold first, on the child perhaps firstest of all." His slate eyes flicked once, involuntarily, toward the deepest shadow between the pillars, and whatever slept there. "Find your counterweight before you cut the rope. That is not counsel from the usurper. It is from the bandage, to whoever tears it off next."

He gathered his coat about him and turned toward the ninth seat — and stopped, as if remembering something almost human.

"For what it is worth, Lior Oyelaran: your grandmother, at the end, told me she regretted nothing except that she had sworn alone. Remember that. Whatever you build—" he lowered himself into the chair, folding his ink-stained hands, becoming again a monument of patience, "—do not swear alone."

The shadows between the pillars emptied. The Wardens had gone, or had never been, or had walked backward out of the world's attention. Only the four of them remained in the great buried hall — a guide, an archivist, a child, and a ghost's absence — and overhead the stone stars hung carved and lightless, waiting to learn whether they had ever really been anything else.

Lior looked at the eight empty seats. Then at her grandmother's daughter-line ending in her own two hands. Then at Wren, who had gone very still, staring at the ninth chair as though the oath-marks on its arm had begun, faintly, to glow.

"Wren," she said. "How many of those fragments are still anchored? All nine?"

His answer, when it came, was barely a whisper:

"No, Lior. Eight. One of the seats broke its oath a hundred years ago — and I think we've just been talking to what's left of it."
