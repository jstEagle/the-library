## 9. What the Oath Bought

The dead do not sleep. This is the first thing I learned, in the first hour of being dead, when the shrine stones were still warm from the last pilgrim's hands and my granddaughter's blood had not yet come walking up the smugglers' road to wake me.

We linger. That is the truth no one sings about. The priests say the dead pass beyond the veil, into the quiet country where names are not needed and hunger does not follow. They are wrong, or they are kind, or they have never been dead themselves. We linger — some of us for a season, some of us for a century — bound to whatever we loved too fiercely to release. A hearthstone. A ring of standing stones. A promise.

I was bound to a promise. I had made it with my whole voice, my whole name, and oaths spoken so completely do not end when the mouth that spoke them goes cold. They go on speaking. I have been speaking mine for ninety-eight years, into the dark under the mountain, to no one, until Lior came.

But I am ahead of myself. Ghosts are bad at time; we live in it like fish in deep water, all of it pressing on us at once. Let me begin where the living would begin: with the famine.

* * *

You must understand what Thornfell was before the starving year. Not the Thornfell your histories might have held, if any history still held it — not the misty borderland of ballads, the green country behind the wards. It was nine houses, and the nine houses were nine fires, and between the fires there was always war.

I was born Adaeze Oyelaran in the high keep above the Vale of Nine Banners, third daughter of a house that measured its wealth in grudges. We had warred with House Maraval over the river tolls for four generations. Four generations of sons buried under the same willow, on opposite banks. My father used to say we did not hate the Maravals; hating required thinking of them as other than ourselves, and we could not spare them the thought. We simply fought them the way rain falls — because it was the season for it, because it had always been the season for it.

And then the rains failed.

Not one season. Three. The pastures went the color of old bone. The river tolls became river funerals, barges loaded not with grain but with the bodies of those who had eaten the seed corn. I was thirty-one years old, and I remember counting — actually counting, with tally-sticks, because grief wants arithmetic — forty-one children dead in the Vale between midwinter and thaw. Forty-one. I carved the number into the doorframe of our hall and dared the gods to make me forget it.

And here is what the famine did, what only famine can do: it made the nine houses look up from their wars at the same moment. All nine of us, all at once, like animals lifting their heads when the forest goes silent.

House Maraval sent no raiders that spring. Instead old Sera Maraval herself came up the vale road under a white banner, leaning on her son's arm, and stood in my father's hall with her hollow cheeks and said the words I will carry past death:

*"There is no victory left to win. Only meals."*

That was the beginning. Not idealism — never believe the ones who tell you it began with idealism. It began with forty-one children and a bone-colored valley and an old woman too tired to hate. The nine houses met on neutral ground, at the hot springs below the mountain's root, and for eleven days we did something the nine houses had never done: we negotiated instead of maneuvered.

The problem before us was simple and monstrous. The wars had eaten everything. Even if every sword was sheathed tomorrow, even if the granaries were pooled and rationed with perfect justice, there was not enough. The land itself was wounded — three generations of burning, salting, poisoning wells, felling orchards for siege timber. Thornfell could not feed Thornfell. And the kingdoms beyond our borders, seeing us starve, were already circling. I had read the letters. Vessia offered grain in exchange for the river tolls in perpetuity — a leash disguised as charity. The Ashen Compact offered soldiers to "restore order," which is what wolves call shepherding.

We could surrender to our neighbors and be eaten slowly, or fight each other and be eaten quickly. Those were the choices the world gave us.

So we made a third choice. It came, as terrible ideas do, in the smallest hours of the eleventh day, when everyone left alive around that table was too exhausted to lie well. My cousin Tehila — brilliant, half-mad Tehila, who kept the house archives and spoke to wards the way farmers speak to weather — looked at the map of Thornfell with its nine colored territories and its scarred borders, and said:

"What if the borders were the problem? Not the people. The lines."

She meant it geographically first. Erase the borders, pool the land, farm it as one country instead of nine battlefields. But Tehila was an archivist's daughter before she was anything else, and she knew — we all knew, sitting there in the steam of the hot springs with our dead stacked unburied behind us — that lines drawn in ink can be redrawn, but lines drawn in memory cannot. As long as the nine houses remembered their banners, their claims, their grievances, the wars would grow back like grass after fire. And as long as Thornfell remembered its own strength, its own identity, the circling kingdoms would never let it heal in peace.

To save the body, we would have to cut away the remembering.

Tehila called it the Amaranthine Accord, because amaranth is the flower that does not fade, and she had a cruel sense of humor even then. Nine seats. Nine oaths. One working, woven by all nine houses together, that would fold Thornfell out of the world's memory — its name, its borders, its wars, its very existence — while the wards hid the land itself, and the people lived on inside the folded country, fed at last, forgotten by every enemy who wanted them dead.

Feed the people by erasing the wars with them.

It sounds mad. It sounded mad in the hot springs too. Old Sera Maraval laughed until she cried, and then cried until she laughed, and then said, "Yes. Obviously yes. What else is there?" Because when you have counted forty-one children, madness with bread in it looks saner than sanity with graves.

I want you to understand this, whoever you are, however you have come to read what I was not supposed to leave behind: I swore that oath with open eyes and a full heart. Not coerced. Not deceived. I stood at the ninth stone with my hand on my grandmother's signet and I gave my name to the erasure willingly, gladly, the way you give blood for a bleeding child. I thought it was the bravest thing I would ever do.

I was right about the brave part. I was wrong about everything else.

* * *

Here is what the oath cost, item by item, as I understood it at the stone and as I understand it now, with a hundred years of hindsight pressing against me like deep water:

It cost the nine houses their names in the outer world. We knew this. We accepted it. What we did not fully understand — could not, until the working took hold — was how much of a person lives in their name. When Thornfell vanished from memory, it did not vanish cleanly. Memory is not a ledger; it is a loom, and pulling out the country's thread pulled threads through everything touched by it. Songs went mute mid-verse. Letters arrived with holes in them where the place-name should have been, the ink faded to nothing, the sentences limping. Children born after the folding grew up in towns they could not name, singing anthems whose words dissolved on their tongues.

We told ourselves: they will keep what matters. Love keeps. Family keeps. Bread keeps.

Love kept. But love without a name is a strange animal. I watched my own grandchildren — the generations born inside the folds — stand in the market of Emberhold and feel, without knowing why, a homesickness for a country they were standing in. That is the wound I did not anticipate. You can erase a nation from the world, but the nation leaves ghosts in its people, and ghosts, as I have good reason to know, do not sleep either.

It cost us the Court itself. Nine seats, nine voices, one law — the thing that had held the wars in check for the last decade of their raging, imperfect and quarrelsome and ours. We did not merely dissolve it; we sealed it, buried the throne hall under the mountain's root, warded it shut, because a Court that could be reassembled could be seized, and a seized Court would drag Thornfell back into the world's memory at the worst possible moment. We buried our own sovereignty to keep it safe from everyone, including ourselves.

And it cost us — this is the part I did not understand until I was dead and could see the whole shape of it at once — it cost us the future. An oath is a loan taken against tomorrow. We borrowed from the children not yet born: their history, their anthem, their right to know what they were and where they came from. We spent their inheritance to buy their grandparents' bread. Every generation since has paid interest on our loan without ever seeing the principal.

Was it worth it?

I have had a hundred years to answer that question, and I will tell you honestly: I do not know. Some nights — if the dead have nights, and ours are long — I take out the arithmetic and turn it over. Forty-one children saved, and their children, and theirs. A century of peace inside the folds while the kingdoms outside gnawed at borders that weren't there. Against that: a people who cannot sing their own song. Grandmothers like me, dying with our true names already half-unmade. A girl in an orphanage humming a melody no one taught her, drawing nine chairs from her dreams, because the erased country will not stay erased in the blood of the young. The debt comes due. Debts always come due.

Sera Maraval, at the ninth stone, said something I did not understand then. She was the oldest of us, the most tired, and she gripped my hand hard enough to bruise and said: *"One day someone will have to unsay this. Pray they are kinder than we were."*

I thought she meant the world would need kindness. She did not. She meant us. She meant that the ones who break the oath will inherit not just our sin but our reasons, and they will need to hold both at once — the grave-guilt and the bread — or the breaking will curdle into something worse than the oath was.

* * *

Lior came to my shrine on an evening that smelled of rain and woodsmoke, and she brought the whole impossible future with her: a disgraced archivist with guilt in his pockets, a child full of stolen songs, a handful of strays and deserters who remembered too much. I felt them coming for a mile, the way you feel a storm in your teeth — the founding oath shivering in its foundations, recognizing the blood that swore it walking back toward the stone.

She knelt where the pilgrims used to kneel. She did not know she was kneeling to me. She thought she was reading an old carving.

"Grandmother," she said — to the air, to the stone, testing the word like a coin bitten for gold — "if you're there. If any of you are there. Tell me why."

Why. Of all the questions. Not *how*, though she would need that. Not *what did it cost*. Why.

Ghosts cannot speak easily. We are poured through whatever vessel the binding offers — stone, water, dream, blood. I gathered myself the way you gather wind into a sail and I pushed my hundred years down through the shrine stone and into her oath-bound blood, and I showed her.

I showed her the bone-colored valley. The forty-one tallies on the doorframe. Sera Maraval's white banner on the vale road. Eleven days of negotiation in the steam. Tehila with her map and her terrible flower-named idea. The nine stones. My hand on my grandmother's signet. The moment of swearing — not grim, as she may have imagined it, but luminous, all nine of us weeping and certain, giving away our names like bread.

And I showed her what came after. The letters with their holes. The mute verses. The children homesick in their own streets. The loan, and the interest, paid in forgetting, generation upon generation.

When I released her she stayed kneeling a long time. Rain moved through the shrine. Somewhere below, her ragged court-in-miniature waited — the archivist, the child, the strays — and I felt her weigh them against me, against the woman in the vision who had sworn away a country out of love.

"You bought us bread with ourselves," she said at last, to the air, to the stone, to me. Her voice was steady, which is how I knew how much it cost her. "I understand. I even forgive it. But the loan is spent, Grandmother. The creditors are at the gate — the Wardens, the Regent, the kingdoms circling. And I won't pay our future to protect your past."

Good, I thought, with a dead woman's fierce pride. Good. Anger with understanding underneath it. Sera's prayer, answered almost exactly: someone kind enough to grieve the oath, and hard enough to break it.

But then she said the other thing. The thing that turned my pride to fear, there in the rain, in the dark, in the hundred-year echo of my own voice still speaking the founding vow beneath the mountain.

"The Regent offered me a throne built on forgetting," Lior said. "He says the forgetting protects us. He says the world outside wants to finish what the famine started. And I've seen his face, Grandmother. He isn't lying. He believes it. Which means he's not just my enemy — he's my inheritance, wearing a crown."

Yes, I wanted to tell her. Yes. He is the ninth seat's heir, the one house that kept faith with the oath when the others' lines thinned and scattered, and he keeps it the way a jailer keeps his prisoners: faithfully, desperately, out of the same love that built the cage. You will meet him at the center of everything, and you will find him holding a mirror.

"And if breaking the oath calls the wolves," she went on, and now her voice did shake, one crack in the stone of her, "if the stars come back and the borders show and Vessia and the Compact come running — then I'll have unsaid your mercy and rebuilt your wars. Tell me, Grandmother. Tell me what the oath bought, if it bought only time."

Time. The oath bought time. A century of it, purchased with a country's name, and time is the one currency that always runs out.

I pressed the answer down through the stone, through the rain, into her blood, and I felt her receive it — not as words, but as weight, the way the dead give everything:

*The oath bought a hundred years for someone to find a better answer. If there is no better answer in you, then it bought nothing, and we were fools twice.*

She rose. She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, the way my mother used to, the gesture passing down the generations like an heirloom no one chose. And she walked back down toward her strays and her child and her archivist, toward the buried hall and the nine empty seats and the man waiting among them with his tired official's eyes.

I settled back into the shrine stone, into the dark, into the long night of the dead, and I did what I have done for ninety-eight years: I held the founding oath in my hands and turned it over, looking for the flaw, the seam, the loose thread.

And found it. There, in the ninth clause, where Sera Maraval's line was written — the clause that binds the Regent, the clause he has guarded with his whole gray life. It has a hinge in it. A release. Tehila put it there — clever, guilty Tehila — in case the day came when the oath needed unsaying. A counter-oath, anchorable only by a speaker of the founding blood, at the cost the founding blood knows best.

My blood. Lior's blood.

She is walking toward it now, and she does not yet know the price, and I cannot warn her — the binding takes my voice and gives me only visions, and visions are such blunt instruments for telling a girl you love that saving everything will cost her everything.

Unless. Unless I spend what remains of me, what the binding still holds, what ninety-eight years of lingering has left — and force my ghost through the ward-seal on the buried hall, and stand beside her at the ninth stone when the moment comes, and speak the anchor with my own unmade throat.

The dead do not sleep. But we can choose, at the end of our lingerng, what we stay awake for.

Below the mountain, faint as a heartbeat, I felt the nine seats wake.
