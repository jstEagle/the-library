## 10. Wardens at the Ford

Sefi counted the river before she saw it.

That was the trick of it now — the thing Lior had been teaching her without ever saying she was teaching it. You learned a country by its sounds before its shapes: the hush of pine over stone, the particular way wind combed through high grass, and then, faint under everything, water moving over rock. Sefi had grown up in Emberhold's orphanage with one window and no view, but she had always known there was a river somewhere near, because in her dreams it sang a song with words in it, and the words were *Thornfell*, and nobody else could hear them.

Now the river was real in front of her, wide and grey-green and loud, running fast with snowmelt out of the mountains they'd crossed. The ford was where Lior said it would be — a shallow braided place where the water spread itself thin over pale stones, calm enough to cross if you were careful and foolish enough to try if you weren't.

"Boots off," Lior said, already crouching to work her laces loose. "Trousers rolled. We cross single file, packs high, and nobody stops in the middle for poetry."

"I never stop for poetry," Wren said, with dignity, and began unlacing his boots with the fumbling care of a man who had clearly never forded anything more dangerous than a library gutter.

Sefi sat down on a flat stone and pulled her boots off. The cold coming up off the water bit at her bare ankles even from here. She liked the bite of it. Cold meant real. So much of the world had gone soft and wrong since the stars went out — since the night Lior had found her humming in the orphanage stairwell and knelt down and asked her, very quietly, *what song is that?* — that Sefi had made a private rule about trusting anything that hurt a little.

"Where are we going after?" she asked. "The other side."

Lior glanced up. In the grey light her face was all angles and patience. "There's an old shepherd's track that climbs toward the shrine passes. We'll make camp above the tree line where patrols don't like to go." A pause. "And then we keep going inward. Toward the seats."

*The nine seats.* Sefi had drawn them so many times on scraps of slate and flour-sack cloth that she could do it blind — three thrones of mountain stone, three of river stone, three of ash-dark wood, each one different, each one waiting. She'd drawn them before she knew what they were. She'd drawn them back when drawing them got her punished for lying about a country that didn't exist.

She was still thinking about that — about how strange it was that the truest things she knew were the ones everyone called dreams — when the birds stopped.

Not quieted. *Stopped.* All at once, like a door closing.

Sefi's head came up. Downriver, maybe two hundred paces, the alder thicket stood dark and still, and between one heartbeat and the next the light along the water changed — went thin and colourless, the way it did around the Wardens, as though something were drinking the warmth out of the morning.

"Lior," she whispered.

Lior was already moving. That was another thing Sefi had learned about her: Lior's body understood danger a full breath before her face did. One moment she was rolling her trousers; the next she had Sefi's wrist and was pulling her low behind the stone, one hand flat on Sefi's back to press her small against the cold granite, the other sliding her sword free of its sheath without a whisper of leather.

"How many?" Lior breathed.

Sefi closed her eyes. This was the part she couldn't explain, the part that made Wren write things down in his little book with his little worried face. When she listened past her ears — into whatever place inside her held Thornfell whole, unbroken, singing — she could feel the shape of things the erasure had tried to hollow out. And the Wardens were hollow. They moved through the world like holes cut in cloth, and she could feel the edges of the holes.

"Eight," she said. "Maybe nine. Two up on the ridge behind us — I think they were following us since the pass. The rest in the alders, spread out. They're not sneaking anymore. They're just... waiting."

"They know we have to cross," Lior murmured. "A ford is a gift to an ambush. Stupid of me. I was thinking about wet boots instead of open ground." Her voice wasn't angry at herself, exactly. It was the flat, quick voice of someone rearranging a mistake into a plan. "Wren. Get down behind the pack stones. Whatever happens, you keep hold of Sefi's shoulder and you do not let go."

"And you?" Wren's voice cracked in the middle.

"I'm going to be extremely inconvenient," Lior said, and Sefi felt, absurdly, like laughing, even with her heart hammering against her ribs like a trapped bird.

Then the alders spoke.

Not with voices — with arrows. Three of them, hissing out of the thicket in a flat grey line, and Lior hauled Sefi down so hard her chin scraped stone, and the arrows buried themselves in the pack stones above their heads with a sound like teeth. Shouting erupted on both banks. Grey shapes rose out of the alder brush and came down toward the ford at a run — six, seven, eight of them, long coats streaming, faces blank as unwritten paper beneath their hoods.

"Up!" Lior pulled Sefi to her feet and shoved her toward Wren. "To me! Both of you, to me!"

They ran. The river roared ahead of them, and behind them the Wardens' boots hit the shallows with splashes that sounded too loud, too many. An arrow skipped off a stone near Sefi's heel. Wren grabbed her arm and nearly pulled her off her feet, and then they were all three stumbling into the current together, the water seizing their legs with cold so fierce it burned—

—and the nearest Warden reached them.

He was young. Sefi saw that even under the hood, even with the blankness on him. Young and scared, holding a short spear in hands that shook, wading toward them with the desperate mechanical stride of someone being carried by orders rather than walking. Ten feet away. Eight. Six.

And Sefi, standing hip-deep in the river of her dreams, did the only thing she could think of.

She said his name.

She didn't know how she knew it. It was simply *there*, the way the anthem was there, the way the nine seats were there — folded up inside her like a letter she'd been carrying since before she could read. The name rose in her throat and came out clear across the water:

"*Dannel Marris!*" 

The word didn't travel like sound. It traveled like fire.

Sefi felt it leave her — a heat in her chest, a pulling, as though she'd torn a strip off something inside herself and thrown it. The name struck the young Warden in the face and *caught*. His hood blew back. His eyes went wide, wide, wider — the blankness cracking like ice on a spring pond — and he stopped dead in the rushing water with his spear half-raised and his mouth open, and what came out of him was not a battle cry.

It was a sob.

"*Thornfell,*" he gasped. "I — my mother — we lived below the terraces, there were plum trees, why can't I — *why couldn't I remember the plum trees—*"

The spear fell from his hands and spun away downstream.

For one heartbeat the whole ford hung still. Even the other Wardens faltered, their blank faces turning toward the sound of a man weeping in the shallows, because it was a sound none of them could account for, a sound with no orders behind it.

Then the sergeant — a broad woman with iron-grey hair and a scarred jaw — screamed "*Hold formation!*" and broke the spell, and the world crashed back into motion.

But something had changed, and everyone on that river felt it.

* * *

What happened next, Sefi would remember afterward in pieces, the way you remember a storm — flashes and noise and the taste of fear.

Lior met the second Warden in the middle of the ford, and it was like watching water decide to be a blade. She didn't fight the way the soldiers fought, all weight and fury; she flowed, turned, redirected, used the current and the slick stones and the man's own momentum until he went down gasping, disarmed, pinned under her knee with his hood fallen back and confusion written plain on his face. She wasn't killing them. Sefi realized that with a jolt. Lior was *unmaking their orders*, one body at a time.

"Dannel!" the sergeant shouted again, and Dannel Marris — still weeping, still standing in the river like a man waking from a ten-year sleep — turned and looked at her, and Sefi saw the exact moment the last of the grey fell off him. He looked at the sergeant the way you look at someone who has been lying to you your whole life.

"You told us there was nothing here," he said. His voice shook. "You told us this was empty land. I *fished* in this river. I fished here when I was nine years old."

"*Marris, stand down or I'll—*"

"*Plum trees,*" he said, and started walking toward her, unarmed, and two other Wardens turned to face him instead of Lior, and the formation — the beautiful, terrible machine of them — began to tear along its own seams.

Sefi stood in the shallows with Wren's hand clamped on her shoulder, and she understood, in the way she understood the songs and the seats, that this was hers to finish. The names were rising in her faster now, crowding up like starlings. Every Warden on that ford had been someone once. Had eaten bread in a kitchen she could almost see, had a grandmother, had a street, had a country — *her* country, stolen out from under their memories while they slept.

She lifted her voice, small and thin and absolutely certain, and she gave the names back.

"*Brey Oduya. Katrin Voss. Ilse and Marta Ferren, twins, born in Hollowbridge in the year the plums failed.*"

Each name landed like a coal on dry thatch. The tall Warden on the left bank staggered as though struck and tore his hood back himself, gasping. The twins — Sefi hadn't known they were twins until the names left her mouth, and then she knew it the way she knew her own heartbeat — the twins dropped their weapons simultaneously and caught each other's arms, staring, mouthing each other's names like a prayer.

"*Aldous Ren! Tam Broadfoot! Serah Ives, who mended sails on Lake Verre before the lake forgot its own name!*"

One by one, the grey cloaks came apart. Some of the Wardens wept. One fell to his knees in the shallows and began, horribly and wonderfully, to hum — the anthem, the erased anthem, the song Sefi had been alone with her whole life — his voice cracked and rusty and joining hers across the water without either of them deciding it.

Only the sergeant held. She stood in the middle of the ford with her sword drawn and her jaw set, and the erasure clung to her like armour, and Sefi felt the reason why before she understood it: the sergeant was *holding the others' forgetting too*. Carrying it for her squad, the way a sergeant carries everything. There was a knot of grey in her deeper than the rest, wound tight and deliberate, and Sefi's names skated off it like sparks off wet stone.

The sergeant looked at Sefi across the water. Her eyes were the worst thing Sefi had ever seen — not cruel, just *full*, stuffed to bursting with other people's emptiness.

"You should have stayed forgotten, little speaker," the sergeant said. "Do you know what you're doing? Do you know what wakes up when this country remembers itself?" And then, before anyone could move, she drove her own sword point-first into the riverbed, released it, and walked away — upstream, alone, hood up, leaving her squad standing disarmed and weeping in the shallows behind her.

Nobody followed her. Nobody had the heart, or perhaps the orders.

* * *

Afterward there was the long, shaking business of becoming people together on the far bank.

Lior made them build a fire even though the smoke might draw eyes. "They already know where we are," she said grimly. "Warmth is worth more than hiding now." She bound a gash on the tall Warden's forearm — Brey Oduya, the sail-mender's son, who kept apologizing for flinching — and she did it gently, Sefi noticed, the way she did almost nothing else gently.

Seven of them sat around the fire. Seven former Wardens, grey cloaks piled and burning at the edge of the light because Aldous Ren had insisted, saying it felt like taking off a sickness. Dannel Marris sat closest to Sefi. He hadn't stopped looking at her since the ford, with an expression she couldn't name — awe and grief and something like guilt all tangled together.

"You knew my mother's name," he said finally. "Before I did. How?"

Sefi shrugged, uncomfortable. "I don't know. It's just — in me. All of it. Like somebody put the whole country in a jar and gave it to me and forgot to tell me what it was."

Wren, hunched over his book, wrote that down. Sefi saw him do it and decided not to mind.

"The sergeant," Lior said quietly. She was cleaning her blade, not looking at anyone. "She took the squad's forgetting into herself. Deliberately. That's a discipline — an old one. Court-trained." She glanced at Wren. "You said the Wardens were recruited after. That they'd never served the Ninefold Court."

"They haven't." Wren's face was pale. "But whoever trains them learned from someone who did. Someone who survived the dissolution and went to work for the Regent." He closed the book slowly. "Which means the Regent isn't just guarding the forgetting, Lior. He's *studying* it. Refining it. Making it portable."

The fire crackled. Somewhere above them, on the ridge, the wind moved through the pines, and Sefi thought she heard — underneath it, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well — the river singing its one-word song.

Dannel cleared his throat. He looked around at the other six, and then at Lior, and then he did something that made Sefi's chest hurt: he stood up, straightened, and bowed. Not deep, not courtly — he clearly didn't know how — but real, from the waist, the way you bow to something you've decided to follow.

"We're done hunting children," he said. "If you're going inward — to the seats, to whatever's under the mountain — we're coming with you. All of us. We know the Warden roads, the signals, the patrol rotations. We know how they think, because until an hour ago we were how they think."

Lior studied him for a long moment. Sefi watched her weigh it — watched the caution and the arithmetic and, underneath, something warmer that she tried to hide and failed.

"Seven trained soldiers who remember Thornfell," Lior said at last. "Every Warden in these mountains will be hunting you by nightfall. You understand that. The moment you chose the river over your orders, you became exactly what the Regent fears most."

"What's that?" asked one of the twins — Ilse or Marta; Sefi still couldn't tell, and suspected she was the only person alive who could learn to.

Lior sheathed her sword. When she smiled, it was small and sharp and lit something in the grey morning.

"Witnesses," she said.

They broke camp as the sun finally climbed clear of the ridgeline — the first sun in days that seemed to have any conviction behind it. Sefi walked in the middle of the column, between Wren and Dannel, with seven new heartbeats around her and the river falling behind.

She was tired down to her bones. Her chest ached where the names had torn loose. But as they climbed toward the shepherd's track, she caught herself humming the anthem under her breath, and Dannel picked it up, and then Brey, raggedly, and then the twins, and Wren — toneless, embarrassed, unstoppable.

And far off, back down the valley, so faint it might have been wind, Sefi heard the answer come: voices from the direction of Emberhold, threading up through the passes. More than two. More than ten.

The song was traveling home ahead of them — and something in the mountains, something old and seated and waiting, had begun, very softly, to listen.
