## 11. The Price of Names

The rain came at dusk, thin and needling, the kind that did not fall so much as hang in the air and wait for you to walk into it. Lior stood at the mouth of the Hollow Pass with her hood up and her hand on her sword hilt, watching the road below for fires.

There were no fires. That was the problem. A hunting party lit fires; a hunting party wanted to be seen, wanted its quarry to know fear was coming. What moved along the valley floor tonight moved dark, without light or song or the clatter of ordinary soldiers making camp — six shapes on horseback, maybe seven, riding in the disciplined silence of men who had been told what they hunted and had not yet begun to question it.

"They've been following us since the ford," Wren said behind her. He crouched under the overhang where the others sheltered, his spectacles fogged, his voice pitched low. "Since Sefi burned the erasure off them. The Wardens let them live precisely so they could be used this way."

"I know how a bloodhound works, Wren."

"I wasn't explaining it to you." He pushed his glasses up. "I was explaining it to myself. There's a difference between knowing a thing and believing it, and I have spent one hundred years not believing things I knew."

Lior didn't answer. Below, the riders reached the place where the smugglers' track split — the low fork, the safe way, the route she had kept in her head through ten years of guiding caravans because it was the only road through these mountains that no Warden patrol had ever walked. Her last clean way out. Her last clean way *in*, if it came to that. Every plan she'd made since Emberhold had been built on the assumption that the Hollow Pass remained hers: unwatched, unwarded, unremembered even by the country that had forgotten itself.

The riders took the fork without hesitation. Without slowing. As though they had walked it a hundred times.

Her stomach dropped the way it had dropped the night the stars went out — that same vertigo, the ground tilting under a map that had lied.

"They can't know that road," she said. "Nobody knows that road. I barely know that road."

"Then ask yourself what else the Wardens know," Wren said quietly. "They catalogued an entire country, Lior. Do you imagine they left the smugglers' roads out of the ledger?"

* * *

She went down to them alone.

It was not bravery, whatever Dannel called it when he argued afterward. It was arithmetic. Seven armed men against eleven half-starved fugitives — two of them children, one of them an archivist who fought like a man defending a library with a quill — could only end one way unless someone changed the terms before steel came out. And the terms, Lior thought as she picked her way down the scree in the dark, had been wrong from the beginning. Wrong the night the Court dissolved. Wrong every year since. She was simply the first Oyelaran in a century to say so out loud.

She stepped onto the track ahead of them and struck flint to her lantern, letting the light find her face.

"Hold," she said, in the parade-ground cadence every soldier in Thornfell's border companies learned before they learned to shave. "Who commands?"

The lead rider reined in. He was young — younger than her, certainly, with the soft jaw of a man who had never been hungry — and his hand went to his sword and stopped there, caught between training and confusion. Behind him the others bunched, horses blowing in the cold.

"Border company," the young man said. "Third Warden Company, attached. We're — " He faltered. His eyes did something strange, flicking past her shoulder toward the mountains as though he'd heard his own words and found them hollow. "We're pursuing deserters."

"Deserters," Lior repeated. "From what army?"

"From — " His brow furrowed. She watched the furrow deepen, watched him reach for something that slid away from him like fish through water. "I don't — the orders said — "

"Brey," Lior called, without taking her eyes off him. "Come down here. Slowly. Let him see your face."

Brey came down the scree with the twins flanking him, and the young soldier's face went through a journey in the space of three heartbeats: recognition, denial, and then a kind of terrible waking, like a man surfacing from deep water into air too bright to breathe. Lior had seen that look once before, at the ford, on the faces of soldiers whose erased country had been spoken back into their skulls by a child's voice.

"You," Brey said gently. He was the oldest of the rescued soldiers, grey at the temples, and he had a grandfather's way of standing. "You drilled us at Coldford. You made us sing on the march. Don't you remember what we sang?"

The young man's lips moved. Nothing came. Then, cracked and half-ashamed, barely a sound:

"...Thornfell stands."

"Thornfell stands," Brey agreed. "And you're standing in it. And your orders come from men who want it to stay dead, because a living country has borders, and borders are inconvenient to people who've already started dividing up the corpse."

"Traitor talk," one of the rear riders snarled, but his voice shook, and his horse sidestepped, and nobody moved to back him.

Lior raised her lantern higher. "Look at each other," she said. "Not at me. At each other. You rode out together as a company. Ask yourselves why none of you can remember the name of the fort you garrison. Ask yourselves why your pay ledgers list a province that isn't on any map. Ask yourselves—" and here she leaned forward, and put all the cold fury of a woman whose homeland had been stolen twice, once by oath and once by silence, into her voice— "why the men who sent you after us didn't come themselves."

Silence, except the rain and the horses.

Then the youngest rider — a boy hardly older than Sefi would be in ten years — pulled off his glove with clumsy fingers and held up his hand, palm out, the way children in the valley towns still did without knowing why, the old ward-sign against forgetting. And the company broke, quietly, the way ice breaks: no drama, just a long crack running through the middle of a thing until it was two things, and one of them was hers.

* * *

Eleven became eighteen around a fire built small and shielded in a hollow above the pass, because eighteen mouths needed feeding and eighteen bodies needed warmth and there was no longer any point pretending to be small enough to hide.

"We can't keep them," Dannel said. He said it kindly, which was worse. He was the steadiest of her smugglers, the one who kept the accounts and the tempers both. "Seven more soldiers, Lior. They eat. They slow us. And now every Warden in the range knows our faces and our road. We should cut east while the fork's still open and be in the lowlands by week's end."

"The fork's not open," Lior said. "They took it. They knew it, Dannel. Which means the Wardens have my maps, or enough of them. There is no east anymore. There is no *out*. There's only deeper in, and whether we go there as eleven or eighteen."

"Then we leave them here with rations and a—"

"With what?" Brey asked. Not angry. Just tired, the way only old soldiers get tired. "A promise? The Wardens will find them by morning. And they won't kill us, girl—" he nodded at Lior— "that's the worst of it. Did you notice? At the ford they fought to take us, not to fell us. Dead men don't spread remembering. They'll take these seven back, wipe them again, and send them after us a third time, and a fourth, until one of us gets tired and leads them to the child."

At the word *child*, every head turned slightly toward the hollow's edge, where Sefi sat wrapped in a blanket between the twins, teaching them — of all things — the verses of the anthem that named the nine rivers. Her voice was thin with exhaustion but it did not waver. She had burned seven minds free at the ford and slept twelve hours and woken hungry for the work. Lior watched her and felt the same thing she always felt now: awe braided tight with dread, because the girl was ten years old and carrying a country in her chest, and everyone around her, Lior included, kept finding reasons to let her carry it.

"There's another option," Wren said.

Everyone looked at him. He had his ledger out — the old court ledger, the one that burned cold, the one he'd carried through a disgraced lifetime like a stone in his belly — and he was turning its pages without reading them, the way he did when he was about to confess something.

"The mine," he said. "Kettledeep. It's on no road because there is no road; the seam failed forty years ago and the company that worked it scattered. But the galleries run deep, they're dry, and there's a ventilation shaft that comes out above the tree line where no patrol walks. I catalogued it. During the erasure. It was designated a containment site."

"A containment site," Dannel repeated flatly. "For what?"

"For people," Wren said. "In the first years, when the forgetting was new, some minds wouldn't take. Miners mostly. Men who'd spent their lives underground and knew the mountain's bones too well to be told the mountain had never been anything but empty rock. The Wardens housed them in Kettledeep while they... worked on them." He closed the ledger. "I filed the requisitions. Blankets. Lamp oil. Sedatives. I was very thorough. I was promoted twice."

Nobody spoke. The fire popped. Somewhere above, the absent stars pressed down on the dark like a weight.

"So it's a prison," Dannel said finally.

"It's a hole in the ground with water and walls," Lior said. "So is every town in Thornfell, these days. So was my grandmother's house." She stood, brushing the rain off her knees, and looked around the fire at the faces turned up toward her — smugglers and soldiers, an archivist drowning in his own guilt, two children who should have been learning sums instead of survival. "Here's where we are. Our safe road is gone. Our faces are known. Every day we stay hidden, the Wardens tighten the net, and every day Sefi speaks names, more people wake up, and more people wake up needing somewhere to *be*. We cannot outrun this. I've been running since the night the stars went out, and I'm telling you the math doesn't work."

"And the alternative?" Dannel asked, though his voice had gone quiet, because he'd done the math too.

"We stop being fugitives," Lior said. "We become something else. Something the Wardens can't chase, because you can't chase a place. We take Kettledeep. We hold it. Anyone the remembering touches, anyone the Wardens hunt, anyone who wakes up in the night humming a song they've been told doesn't exist — they come to us. We shelter them. We feed them. We teach them what was taken, and we decide — all of us, together — what happens next." She paused. "I won't lie to you. This burns the last route out. After tonight there's no going back to guiding caravans, no going back to being small and quick and invisible. We will be rebels. Outlaws. Traitors to a government most of this country doesn't remember exists. Justice outside the law, Wren calls it. The law forgot us first."

Dannel looked at her for a long moment. Then he laughed — short, helpless, real. "Ten years I've followed you through passes no map admits to," he said. "Might as well follow you into treason."

One by one, the others nodded, or shrugged, or simply moved closer to the fire, which was its own kind of yes. Brey spat in his palm and offered his hand like a man sealing a bargain in the old style, and Lior took it, and his grip was warm and calloused and certain.

Only Wren hesitated. He was staring at the ledger in his lap as though it might bite.

"You understand what you're building," he said softly. "A refuge full of the remembering, in a mine the Wardens already think of as theirs. They won't send seven riders next time, Lior. They'll send seventy. They'll send what it takes."

"Then we'd better be worth what it takes," Lior said. She held out her hand for the ledger. He gave it up slowly, like a man surrendering a wound. "And Wren — you'll show me every gallery, every shaft, every wall you helped them build. We're going to know that mine better than the men who catalogued it. Your penance starts there."

He almost smiled. "My penance started a hundred years ago. But yes. There too."

* * *

They moved before dawn, eighteen shadows climbing away from the pass while the rain thickened toward snow. Lior took the rear, as she always did, and looked back exactly once.

Below, far down the valley, lights had appeared along the fork of the Hollow Pass — lanterns strung in a line, patient and orderly, marking the road she had owned for ten years the way a man marks a grave. Wardens, settling in to wait for quarry that would never come. Somewhere beyond them, further than lantern-light could reach, Emberhold lay sleeping under its forgetting, and the buried throne hall waited under the mountain with its nine empty seats, and the Regent of Ash sat somewhere in his grey offices doing sums of his own.

Let him count, Lior thought. She had numbers too now. Eighteen, and growing.

Sefi fell into step beside her in the dark, small boots crunching frost, and after a while the girl began, very quietly, to hum. Not the anthem this time. Something older, slower, a walking song for miners coming off shift, and Lior realized with a chill that ran sweeter than fear that the girl had never heard it sung — she was drawing it out of the same dream-well she drew everything, out of the country that lived in her chest.

"That's a Kettledeep song," Wren said from ahead, stopping dead. He turned, and the lantern-light caught his face, and Lior saw that his hands were shaking. "That is a Kettledeep song. How do you—"

"It was stuck in the walls," Sefi said simply, as if that explained everything, and perhaps it did. "The mountain remembers who sang to it. It's glad we're coming."

She walked on. Lior exchanged a look with Wren — dread and wonder in equal measure — and then followed the child down into the dark, toward the mine that remembered, toward the war that had finally, irrevocably, chosen them back.

Behind them, faint under the wind, the lanterns of the Hollow Pass flickered and went out one by one, as though the road itself had closed its eyes.
