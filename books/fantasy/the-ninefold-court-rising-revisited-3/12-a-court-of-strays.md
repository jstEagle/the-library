## 12. A Court of Strays

The mine had been dead longer than Wren Vale had been alive, and it smelled like it — cold iron and old water, the mineral breath of stone that had not been disturbed in a hundred years. Someone had called this place the Grieving Vein once, according to a rusted sign at the mouth whose letters had been picked away by weather until only *GRIE—* remained, and Wren thought that was fitting enough. Everything they had gathered here was grieving for something: a country, a name, a version of themselves that had never known what had been taken.

He stood at the mine's mouth as dusk came down over the valley, counting lanterns.

Forty-one. When they had arrived three days ago there had been eleven — Lior, Sefi, himself, and eight rescued soldiers who still flinched at their own reflections. Now forty-one souls sheltered in the dark, and more came every night, drawn up the smuggler roads by word passed mouth to ear in taverns where no one said the name Thornfell aloud because no one *could* say it, and yet everyone somehow knew where to walk.

That was the thing about erasure, Wren had learned. You could burn every record, dissolve every oath, rewrite every map and memory. But you could not erase the shape of a hole. People felt the absence the way a tongue finds a missing tooth — worrying at it in the dark, unable to sleep, unable to stop.

"Archivist."

One of the soldiers — Captain Brannoc Hale, though he'd asked them weeks ago to stop calling him that, since his commission belonged to an army that remembered him as a deserter now — came up beside him, lantern held low. He was a big man gone gaunt, with a scar across his jaw he could no longer explain because the battle that gave it to him had been fought against a country the world insisted did not exist.

"You should eat," Hale said.

"I will."

"You said that two hours ago." The soldier's voice wasn't unkind. It had settled into something flat and patient over the weeks on the road, the voice of a man who had learned that most catastrophes could be survived if you simply kept doing the next small necessary thing. "Lior says you forget when you're working. She says Sefi has instructions to sit on your foot until you notice."

Wren almost smiled. "She would do it, too."

"She would do it *happily*."

Below them, down the shaft where the first gallery opened into a cavern tall enough to stand a house in, lamplight moved among bedrolls and cook-fires. Someone had strung rope lines for drying clothes. Someone else had chalked a tally on the wall — the number of the remembering, updated each evening like the count of a harvest. A woman named Odile who had once, before her memories returned, believed herself a midwife in a town that wasn't hers, was teaching children their letters using names she was recovering syllable by syllable. Two of the soldiers were drilling in the lower galleries, quietly, with staves, because being hunted taught a man to keep his edge even when his hands shook.

And in the middle of it all, cross-legged on an overturned ore cart with her slate balanced on her knees, sat Sefi, drawing.

Wren knew what she was drawing without looking closely. She always drew the same nine shapes when she drew at all — nine seats arranged in a crescent, each one different, each one rendered with the fierce particularity of a child who had seen something once and refused to let go of it. The Seat of the Spear. The Seat of the Ledger. The Seat of the Bell. The Seat of Roots. She had never seen the throne hall. None of them alive had, except in dreams, and Sefi dreamed it more completely than anyone.

"She's teaching again," Hale said, nodding toward the far side of the cavern, where a knot of the newest arrivals — a family of five from the lowland farms, hollow-eyed and confused, still asking questions no one could answer gently — had gathered around the ore cart. "Old law. Your old law."

Wren watched. Sefi was holding up her slate, showing the family the crescent of seats, and her voice carried up the shaft thin and clear as a wire:

"—and the Court didn't rule *because* they were better than anyone. That's what everybody gets wrong, Wren says. They ruled because they *agreed to be responsible*. Nine seats means nobody gets to be alone with a decision. That's the whole trick of it. That's why there were nine and not one."

*Wren says.* He felt the words land somewhere beneath his ribs, in the place where his guilt lived, and found — to his continuing surprise — that they did not hurt the way guilt usually did. Somewhere along these roads the child had begun quoting him the way scholars quoted authorities, and he had not earned it, and she gave it anyway.

"I keep thinking," Hale said slowly, "about my grandfather. Or — the man I thought was my grandfather. He used to sing me a song about green valleys. I always assumed it was a lullaby from wherever we were from. Last week Odile heard me humming it and went white as bone-marrow." He turned his lantern so its light caught the lines of his face. "It's the anthem, isn't it? The one Sefi hums. My grandfather sang the anthem of a country that doesn't exist, and neither of us knew."

"It exists," Wren said. "It's just been made unspeakable. There's a difference. Erasure isn't destruction, Captain. Destruction leaves ruins, and ruins can be rebuilt. Erasure leaves *nothing* — nothing but the ache where the country used to be." He paused. "And apparently, occasionally, a girl who remembers anyway. Which is either a miracle or a flaw in the Regent's great work. I haven't decided which."

"Does it matter?"

Wren considered the question honestly, the way he tried to consider everything now, having spent the first half of his life considering things conveniently. "No," he admitted. "I don't suppose it does."

* * *

Later, when the fires had burned down to red eyes in the dark and the mine had settled into the breathing quiet of a place full of sleepers, Wren sat with his ledger — the last surviving volume of the Court archive, the one he had stolen rather than catalogued for burning — and tried to work, and failed, because Sefi had crept out of her nest of blankets and planted herself beside him exactly as promised, watching him write.

"That's the wrong seat," she said, after a while.

He looked down at the page. He had been sketching the arrangement of the Ninefold Court from memory — the archivist's memory, the official memory, the one recorded in documents he had personally shelved. "This is how the records describe it."

"The records are wrong." She tapped the page with a grimy finger. "The Seat of Roots goes on the left end, not the right. Left hand is where you hold the ground. Right hand is where you hold the sky. Everybody knows that."

"Nobody knows that," Wren said mildly. "Nobody living has seen the hall."

"I have." She said it without bravado, which was somehow worse. In her dreams she had walked those buried corridors so many times she spoke of them like a neighbor's house. "In the dreams the seats aren't empty. There are people in them, but they're all... blurry. Except one." She hesitated, and Wren saw her small shoulders draw in. "Except the one at the left end. The Seat of Roots. There's an old lady sitting there, and she's looking right at me, like she's waiting for somebody to come home. Sometimes she says things. Mostly she just looks sad." Sefi glanced up at him. "Lior says I shouldn't tell people about the dreams. She says it makes the Wardens hunt harder. But you're not people. You're Wren."

"I'm honored to be exempted from personhood," Wren said. His pen had gone still. An old lady at the Seat of Roots, waiting. He thought of Adaeze Oyelaran, the ghost bound to the shrine at the ford, the first to swear and — by her own ghostly confession — the first to regret. He thought of how Lior went silent whenever the grandmother was mentioned, carrying that inheritance like a stone sewn into her coat.

"Sefi," he said carefully. "In your dreams. The seats. Who sits in them?"

"People." She frowned, searching. "All kinds. Not fancy people. There's a man with rope burns on his hands. There's a soldier lady missing an arm. There's a kid older than me who's got ink all over his fingers like you do. And—" she pointed at him, matter-of-fact, devastating— "you're in one too. Near the middle. You look scared, but you stay anyway."

Wren set down his pen.

He looked around the cavern — at the sleeping soldiers, the chalked tally, the drying ropes, the children learning letters from recovered syllables — and something assembled itself in his chest with the slow, inexorable click of a lock finally finding its key. He had spent the evening sketching the old arrangement of the Court out of habit, out of archival instinct, and the whole time the true answer had been sleeping around him in the dark, breathing.

"Everyone in your dream," he said. "They're not the old nobility. Are they."

"No."

"They're..." He gestured, helplessly, at the mine. At all of it.

Sefi followed his hand and understood, because children understood things whole, without the intervening steps that adults required to talk themselves out of truth. "Yeah," she said simply. "I figured that out a while ago. Why do you think I'm not scared of the dreams anymore?" She hugged her knees. "The Court comes back, but it comes back like *this*. Like us. Like the road." She yawned enormously. "Can I sleep here? Lior snores."

"The Court of Thornfell," Wren said to the empty air, half to himself, "was seated by bloodline. Nine houses, nine oaths, nine inheritances. And the founding oath bought bread for the hungry and peace for the tired, and it cost a country." He looked at the child already curling up against his side, trusting, warm, asleep within minutes as only the young and the exhausted can be. "What would it cost to seat it differently? What would it buy?"

The question kept him awake long after his lamp had guttered. And somewhere in the black hours, between one breath and the next, he began to draft.

* * *

He worked for three days. He told no one at first, not even Lior — partly from the archivist's superstition that a document should be finished before it is defended, and partly because he was afraid of the shape of his own idea and wanted to live inside it a little longer before exposing it to argument. He wrote in the margins of the stolen ledger, then on loose sheets, then on the backs of supply manifests, filling the mine's quiet hours with a language he had sworn never to use again: the formal law-speech of the Ninefold Court, clause nested in clause, precision laid upon precision.

On the fourth evening, he called them together.

Not all forty-one — the newest arrivals were still too frightened, and the soldiers stood watch — but the core of them, the ones who had come up the smuggler roads together: Lior with her arms crossed and her sword never quite out of reach; Hale and six of his remembering soldiers; Odile the midwife; a wiry smuggler named Tamsin Corvane who had guided the last stretch of the journey and who looked at any mention of law the way cats looked at water; the farm family, steadier now, the father Vessel keeping the tally chalked and accurate; and Sefi, installed on her ore cart at the exact center, because everyone had tacitly agreed that meetings went better when she was where she could see.

Wren stood before them holding his papers, and found his hands were shaking, and let them.

"I was the court archivist," he began. "You know this. Some of you have been polite enough not to spit when I say it. I catalogued the erasure. I wrote the inventories of what was destroyed — every charter, every genealogy, every map. I told myself it was mercy. I told myself the oath-speakers must have known best; they had fed a starving people, after all. They had ended a century of border wars with a single act of forgetting. What was paper, compared to that?" He looked down at his pages. "I have spent a long time telling myself things. It is, I think, the family trade of bureaucrats everywhere."

"Get to it, archivist," Tamsin said, but without bite. She had a way of hurrying speeches along that he had come to find almost affectionate.

"The founding oath dissolved the Court to save the people," Wren said. "That much is true, whatever else is true. But look at what it actually built. Look at what a century of forgetting produced." He counted on his fingers, archivist's habit. "A border that dissolves in one bad night. Wardens who hunt children for humming songs. Soldiers who fight wars they cannot remember. A Regent so afraid of what the world will do to Thornfell that he keeps it erased forever — a country preserved like a body in ice, safe and dead." He lifted his head. "The old Court failed not because it was seated badly, but because it was seated narrowly. Nine houses. Nine bloodlines. Nine families who decided that their inheritance entitled them to spend a nation's memory like coin. And when the price came due, they paid it with our past and called it sacrifice."

"And your proposal?" Lior said quietly. She had been very still through all of this. Wren had learned to read her stillness; this was not the stillness of doubt. It was the stillness of someone standing at a door they had been afraid to hope was open.

"My proposal," Wren said, and unfolded the pages, "is the Second Oath."

He read to them. He read the preamble he had labored over for a full day — *We, who remember, being also the dispossessed, the scattered, and the unnamed, do hereby reseat what was broken* — and he read the clauses: the nine seats filled not by birthright but by service, open to any who had borne the cost of the erasure; the rotation of seats so that no family, no faction, no single soul could hold power long enough to repeat the first sin; the explicit dissolution of noble privilege in matters of succession; and — near the end, written in his steadiest hand — the release clause.

"When the second oath is spoken and anchored," he read, "the founding oath shall be discharged, and all obligations arising from it shall fall void — including the obligations binding the line of the oath-speakers." He looked up. "Including Lior's line. Breaking the founding oath won't just restore the country. Properly drafted, it frees the Oyelaran name from the vow their blood has carried for a hundred years. The cage opens. The inheritance ends."

Silence. In it, Wren heard the drip of water deep in the mine, and the crackle of the banked fires, and nothing else — the whole underground world holding its breath.

Then Tamsin Corvane started laughing. It wasn't cruel laughter; it had a wild, delighted edge. "You want to give the throne of a nation to smugglers. To *me*. I've got a bounty in two provinces and a warrant I never read."

"Not the throne. A *seat*. One of nine. And yes." Wren met her eyes. "Who better? The smugglers' roads were the only roads that stayed true when the wards fell. You kept the geography of this country alive in your feet when every map forgot it existed. That's not criminality, Mistress Corvane. That's stewardship."

"And me?" Hale asked roughly. "My army remembers me as a traitor."

"Your army remembers nothing. That's rather the point. You remember *for* them." Wren turned, taking in the whole circle. "Soldiers who kept faith with a country they couldn't name. Midwives who delivered the next generation of the forgotten. Farmers who worked land whose name was stolen from under their plows. Archivists who stole back what they helped bury." His gaze landed, last, on the ore cart. "And speakers. Children who carry what was erased and refuse to put it down. The First Court was nine houses. Let the Second be nine *kinds of keeping*."

Odile was crying silently, the way women cried who had done too much of it to make noise anymore. Vessel the farmer had taken off his hat, which among lowland folk, Wren knew, was what they did in chapels.

Only Lior had not moved. She stared at the pages in his hands, and when she spoke, her voice was strange — scraped thin, careful.

"You've written a release clause for my family."

"I have."

"Without asking me."

"I—" Wren stopped. He had drafted it in the black hours of the third night, thinking of the ghost at the shrine, thinking of the stone Lior carried, and it had seemed like a gift. Standing here, under her stare, he saw suddenly how it might look otherwise. "I thought it was what you wanted."

"It is what I want." She stepped forward and took the pages from his hands, and read, and the firelight moved on her face. "But you've made the same mistake the nine houses made, Wren. You've decided for someone else what their inheritance costs and what it buys. You've decided my burden ends whether I consent or not." She looked up, and her eyes were bright and hard and — he realized with a jolt — not angry. *Moved.* "So here is my amendment, and I'll accept nothing less: the release clause stays. But it binds no one alone. If the Oyelaran vow is discharged, it's discharged because a whole court chose to discharge it — because strangers took up our debt willingly and called it theirs. Nobody gets freed by decree. Not even me." She held the pages out to him. "Rewrite it so the release requires the assent of all nine seats. Then bring it back, and I'll sign."

For a moment Wren could not speak. Then he bowed — the deep, formal bow he had not given anyone since the fall of the archive, the bow of an archivist to a lawful authority — and took the pages back.

"As the Court commands," he said, and heard, saying it, that the words had stopped tasting like ash.

It was Sefi who broke the solemnity, as it was ever Sefi's office to do. She stood up on her ore cart, raised both arms, and announced to the mine at large: "Everybody has to learn the anthem properly now. Odile's teaching verses four through nine tomorrow. If you're going to be a government, you can't hum."

Laughter ran through the cavern — ragged, disbelieving, real laughter, the first Wren had heard underground — and the meeting dissolved into the ordinary business of feeding forty-one people, which was its own kind of legislation.

Wren stayed up late rewriting the release clause, Sefi asleep against his side, and it was nearly dawn when Lior appeared out of the dark gallery with a lantern and an expression he had not seen on her before. Not grief. Not resolve. Something colder and quicker than either.

"Wren," she said. "There are riders on the valley road. Grey cloaks. A dozen or more, moving slow, like they're searching." She crouched beside him, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "They're not coming for the mine. They're following the river trail — the one Sefi uses when she goes to the shrine pool to wash the slates." Her knuckles whitened on the lantern handle. "Sefi went down before first light. She isn't back yet."

Wren was on his feet before the sentence ended, the Second Oath scattering across the stones, unfinished — and already, he would think later, worth less than the child it was written to protect.
