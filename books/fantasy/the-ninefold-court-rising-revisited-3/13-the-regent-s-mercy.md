## 13. The Regent's Mercy

The message came the way winter comes to the high passes — not announced, but simply there, a cold that had been waiting at the edge of things until you turned around and found it already inside your coat.

Lior was checking the ward-lines on the mine's western gallery when Sefi's voice stopped singing.

That was how she knew. Not shouting, not alarm — silence. The girl had not gone more than an hour without humming since the night in Emberhold, since Lior first heard that thread of erased melody winding through the dark like water through stone. Wren said it was habit. Adaeze, when she chose to speak through the shrine-stones they carried now, wrapped in wool and treated with a reverence none of them quite admitted to, said it was *keeping*. A country lived as long as someone kept it in their mouth.

Now the mouth was empty, and Lior's blood went cold before her mind caught up.

She ran. Through galleries that still smelled of old copper and older grief, past the strays' cookfire where Tam the smuggler was arguing with the one-eyed soldier Veyra about whether river-trout could be smoked with pine — ordinary sounds, blessedly ordinary, and she would remember afterward how much she'd hated them in that moment for continuing without Sefi.

The eastern entrance stood open to the grey afternoon.

Sefi's boots were by the door. That detail would haunt her longest, she thought: the boots. The girl had been taken barefoot, or had walked out barefoot, or —

No. She made herself stop. Made herself look instead at what the ground offered, because the ground always offered something to a guide who knew how to read it, and reading ground was the one language no erasure had ever managed to take from her.

Small feet. Light steps. And around them, pressed into the frost-softened mud, the prints of men in formation — six, maybe seven, moving in the tight economical pattern of Wardens who had learned their trade somewhere worse than a barracks. They had not dragged her. There was no scuffing, no struggle. Sefi had walked out between them like a child going to see something interesting.

That frightened Lior more than blood would have.

"They didn't even run," Veyra said behind her, arriving with sword half-drawn and eyes already tracking the treeline. "Who walks a prisoner out? You carry a prisoner. You bind her. This is—" the soldier's jaw worked, hunting for the word, "—this is *polite*."

"Polite," Lior repeated.

"Like an invitation."

Wren came last, out of breath, his archivist's satchel banging against his hip, and when he saw the bootless tracks he went grey in a way Lior had only seen once before — the day he confessed to cataloguing the erasure, listing what had been unwritten with the same hands that wrote it down.

"The Regent," he said quietly. "This has his signature all over it. Nothing wasted. Nothing cruel for cruelty's sake. He wants us to follow."

"Then we follow," Lior said, and heard her own voice come out flat and hard, a blade laid on a table.

* * *

They followed for two days, and the trail was so clean it insulted her.

Lior Oyelaran had spent eleven years guiding contraband and fugitives through passes that did not appear on any honest map, and she knew what pursuit looked like from both ends. Pursuit left sign even when it tried not to — a snapped fern healing crooked, a fire-pit buried shallow, the particular way horses grazed when their riders were nervous. This trail left nothing. It was as if the Wardens moved through the forest leaving no more impression than light through leaves, and every time the track should have grown difficult — a river crossing, a rockfall, a fork where three valleys argued — some small courtesy marked the way. A ribbon of grey cloth tied to a branch. A cairn built where no cairn needed building.

"He's guiding *us*," Wren said on the second evening, staring at one of those cairns with the expression of a man reading a sentence he wished were in another language.

"I know."

"You understand what that means? He could lose us with a thought. Instead he's spending effort to keep us close." Wren turned, and in the failing light his face looked older than its years, worn smooth like a document handled too often. "Lior, I've studied this man's work for twenty years. Every record, every gap in the records — which is its own kind of record. He is many terrible things, but he is not careless, and he is not theatrical. If he is inviting you somewhere—"

"Then I'll hear him out," Lior said. "And then I'll take Sefi back, and we'll leave, and everything he says will be one more thing to survive."

But that night she sat awake watching the shrine-stone wrapped in its wool blanket, and when the ghost finally stirred — Adaeze's presence like the smell of rain before it comes, like pressure behind the ears — Lior asked the question she had been carrying since the boots by the door.

"If he wanted her dead, she'd be dead."

The silence from the stone was long enough to hurt. When Adaeze spoke, her voice was the voice of someone very tired and very far away, though her ghost had never once complained of distance. "Yes."

"Why take her then?"

"Because you cannot be reasoned with, child. But she can be *shown*." A pause, and when the ghost continued there was something in it Lior had never heard from her — hesitation, almost fear. "I have told you what the oath bought. What it cost. But I have not told you what my sister-oath-speakers feared most, in that famine year, when the wars burned hotter than any hunger. It was not conquest. Conquest we understood; we had survived conquerors since before the Ninefold Court had its name. What we feared was *being remembered wrongly*. Being remembered as a battlefield. Do you understand? A land can die of memory too."

"She's eight years old," Lior said. "Whatever he shows her, whatever he tells her — she hums the anthem. She draws nine seats from dreams. She's the most alive piece of Thornfell either of us has ever touched."

"Yes," Adaeze said again, softer. "Which is exactly why he took her, and exactly why I am afraid."

* * *

They found the place on the third morning: an old watchtower on the shoulder of Mount Cerenne, one of the chain that had guarded Thornfell's southern marches back when the borders had been worth guarding. It rose from the mist like a broken tooth, and around it the pines grew in the strict ranks of a planted forest, and there — absurdly, terribly — a table had been set outside the tower door.

An actual table. White cloth. A pitcher of something dark, bread still steaming, honey in a clay pot, and two chairs. One large. One small.

Sefi sat in the small chair, barefoot, her legs swinging because they didn't reach the ground, eating bread and honey with the unselfconscious appetite of a child who had been fed properly for perhaps the first time in her remembered life. Her hair had been braided — braided, in the Thornfell style, the crossing pattern Lior's own mother had used — and someone had given her a cloak lined with fox fur.

When she saw Lior, the girl's face lit up without a flicker of guilt.

"Lior! They have *real* butter here. Not the grey kind. And the Regent knows all the verses of the anthem — even the fourth one, the sad one, nobody knows the fourth one —"

"Sefi." Lior crossed the space between them in five strides and gathered the girl up, and felt her whole body shake with relief she refused to name, and held on until the child wriggled in protest. Then, and only then, did she look up at the man standing by the tower wall.

He was smaller than she had imagined. Grey-cloaked like his Wardens, but the cloak was plain where theirs were marked, and beneath it he wore the clothes of a clerk — good cloth, mended carefully, ink stains at the cuffs. His face was long and tired and ordinary, the face of a man who had done sums all his life, and his eyes — his eyes were the colour of slate after rain, and in them Lior found nothing of the monster she had carried in her head across half a country. Only attention. Terrible, patient, total attention.

"Lior Oyelaran," he said. His voice matched his clothes: quiet, precise, worn at the edges. "You have your grandmother's walk. She also arrived at every meeting ready to kill me. Sit. Eat. The bread is from Emberhold's ovens; I thought you should taste what you are fighting for while it is still warm."

"You took a child."

"I borrowed a witness," the Regent said. "There is a difference, and I think you know it, because if there weren't, you would have drawn that beautiful sword by now instead of standing there doing arithmetic." He gestured at the second chair, the large one. "Please. I have very little time, and rather more honesty than you will find comfortable."

Lior did not sit. Neither did she draw. She positioned herself between Sefi and the Regent and waited, and he accepted this with a small nod, as a colleague accepts a term in an equation.

"Do you know what happened to the Kingdom of Vessia?" he asked.

"Never heard of it."

"No," he agreed gently. "You haven't. Nobody has. Vessia was a country east of the Saltreach, prosperous and proud, whose border wards failed four hundred years ago — not dissolved, mind you, merely *failed*, the way stone fails, slowly and then suddenly. Within a decade its neighbors had taken the coast. Within fifty, the language was a scholarly curiosity. Within a century, scholars were the only ones who remembered there had ever been anything to be curious about." He folded his ink-stained hands. "Vessia died of being visible. Thornfell will not die that way. Not while I hold the forgetting in place."

"The forgetting starves towns," Lior said. "It burns records cold. It hollows people out of their own names."

"It keeps them *alive*," the Regent said, and for the first time something moved under the clerk's calm — not anger, but the ghost of an old exhaustion. "Look beyond the wards sometime, Archivist. Go on, slip over the border as you did. What do you find?"

Wren, who had come up the path behind them with Veyra and Tam and a dozen of the strays, spoke from the edge of the clearing, his voice thin. "Armies. Three kingdoms massing on our old frontiers. Maps with partition lines already drawn. Treaties of division drafted and waiting for a country to officially stop existing."

"Treaties," the Regent repeated, tasting the word. "Signed, sealed, sitting in diplomatic pouches in three capitals. The moment Thornfell becomes real again — the moment the wards fall and the world remembers us — every clause wakes at once. The war my nine oath-speakers ended with their terrible bargain resumes on the first morning, with a century of stored appetite behind it. Emberhold burns. The passes fill with soldiers. Your strays become refugees, your remembering become casualties, and your new court, Mistress Oyelaran, becomes a footnote in someone else's chronicle of the partition." He looked at her, and his tired eyes did not waver. "I am not offering you a throne today. I am telling you what your throne will cost, and asking whether you love Thornfell enough to let it sleep."

Sefi, small and warm against Lior's side, tugged her sleeve. "Lior," she whispered, "he showed me the stars. From the top of the tower. He showed me where they used to be, and he cried a little, and he knew all the words to the sad verse." Her voice dropped lower. "Is he the bad man?"

And there it was — doubt at its deepest, doubt like floodwater, closing over her head. Because Lior had built her whole road on a simple architecture: the Regent was the enemy, the enemy had taken her country, therefore taking it back meant breaking him. But the man by the tower had braided a lost child's hair in the old style and wept at vanished stars, and the treaties were real — Wren had seen them, had described them with his own guilty mouth — and Adaeze's ghost had warned her that a land can die of memory, and the bread smelled like Emberhold, like home, like everything she was supposed to be fighting for.

*What if mercy is just a slower knife?* whispered a voice in her that sounded uncomfortably like the Regent's. *What if forgetting is the only shield a small country gets?*

"I need to speak with my people," Lior said at last, and was proud of how steady it came out.

"Of course." The Regent inclined his head. "Keep the girl. Keep the bread. I ask only one thing in return." From inside his cloak he drew a folded paper — heavy stock, official seal, the kind of document Wren spent his life among — and set it on the white cloth between the honey pot and the pitcher. "The partition treaty. The original. Vessia's fate, with Thornfell's name written into the blanks. Let your archivist verify it. Let your ghost read it. Then decide what kind of inheritance you want to break." He stepped back, and the mist took him the way mist takes everything, gradually and then completely, and the Wardens melted from the treeline around him like frost giving up its grip on grass.

Nobody moved for a long time. Somewhere above them, wind worried the empty tower where the stars used to be counted.

Then Wren crossed the clearing with shaking hands and picked up the treaty, and read it, and Lior watched the last certainty drain out of his face like color from a dying leaf.

"It's real," he said hoarsely. "It's signed. All three crowns. Lior — it's dated for *next month*."

Sefi's small hand found hers in the cold. Above the watchtower, in the blank starless sky, the first thin wind of autumn began to sing — and for one terrible, vertiginous moment, it sounded almost like the fourth verse of the anthem, the sad one nobody was supposed to know.

* * *

They did not speak much on the road back to the mine. The treaty rode wrapped in oilcloth against Wren's chest like a wound, and every mile of familiar pass felt less like homecoming than retreat. It was Veyra who finally broke the silence, the one-eyed soldier speaking around a mouthful of the Regent's stolen bread.

"In the King's Third Legion we had a saying. A trap doesn't have to be shut to catch you. Sometimes it only has to be true."

"Aye," said Tam the smuggler, spitting into the bracken. "Best bait I ever used on a mark was the truth. Truth's the only bait they never check for hooks."

Lior walked at the front and said nothing, because she was doing the arithmetic the Regent had accused her of, and the sums kept coming out wrong no matter which way she set them. Break the oath, and the world remembers Thornfell — and falls on it like wolves on a lamb that has slept a hundred safe years. Keep the forgetting, and the country survives as a shadow, a hunger, a hole in its children's mouths. Mercy or memory. Sleep or slaughter. Every road she had ever guided had a far side. This one seemed to end at the cliff's edge with the map torn off beyond it.

Only Sefi seemed untroubled. The girl trotted along at Lior's elbow, fed and furred and barefoot still — she refused the spare boots Veyra offered, saying her feet remembered the ground better that way — and hummed the anthem under her breath, all four verses, including the sad one.

Lior stopped dead on the trail. "Sefi. The fourth verse. Where did you learn it?"

The girl looked up, blue-eyed and baffled. "The Regent taught me. On the tower." She frowned, trying to catch the memory. "He said his grandmother sang it to him. Before. He said—" the child's brow creased with the effort of exact recall, the gift that made her a weapon and a wonder both, "—he said it was the verse about coming home when it isn't safe anymore."

Behind them, Wren made a sound like a man being unmade.

"What is it?" Lior demanded.

"The fourth verse," Wren whispered. "In the pre-dissolution archives — the fragments I catalogued, the ones the erasure missed — the fourth verse wasn't public. It was the oath-speakers' private verse. Sung only within the Ninefold Court." His eyes were wide and wet and terrified. "Lior, only the nine houses knew that verse. Which means the Regent of Ash is not just the ninth oath-speaker's heir." He clutched the treaty to his chest like a talisman against what he was about to say.

"The Regent of Ash is an Oyelaran."

The wind came down the pass then, cold off the high snows, carrying the smell of winter and woodsmoke and the faintest thread of ember-and-ash, and far to the north, where the buried throne hall slept beneath the mountain, Lior felt — unmistakably, undeniably, through whatever oath-blood ran strange and stubborn in her veins — a stirring. As if nine empty seats had heard a family secret spoken aloud.

As if something down there in the dark had begun, very softly, to wait for her.
