## 14. The World Outside the Wards

Wren Vale had spent thirty years learning that the truth lived in the margins.

It was there in the ledgers of the Court archive, in the cramped annotations beneath the official entries: *requisitioned by decree*, *removed for review*, *burned at the Regent's instruction*. It was there in the colophons of books whose last pages had been razored away with surgical care. And it was there, most of all, in the maps — in the soft grey wash where Thornfell should have been, in the cartographer's polite fiction of *unclaimed highland waste*, in the way every atlas of the last century drew the mountains as if no one had ever built a city inside them.

So when Lior told him she needed someone to go beyond the fallen border and see what the world was doing while it pretended Thornfell had never existed, Wren did not argue. He only asked for two days' head start and the oldest map in his satchel — the one he'd stolen from the archive the night before he fled, the one with the country still inked on it, before some careful clerk had scraped it down to blank vellum and written *waste* over the wound.

"You're not a fighter," Lior said, not unkindly, tightening the strap on her bedroll by the cold fire. "If the Wardens catch you—"

"They won't." He adjusted his spectacles, which was what he did instead of believing things. "I've been overlooked my entire life, Lior. I intend to make a profession of it."

Sefi looked up from the anthem she was scratching into the dirt with a stick — nine notes, nine seats, drawn from dreams she couldn't explain — and gave him a look far too old for her face. "Bring back something true," she said.

"That," said Wren, "is the only thing I know how to carry."

* * *

He went alone, on foot, through a pass so narrow the mountain walls brushed both his shoulders, following a smuggler's thread that Lior had traced for him in charcoal on a strip of linen. The road smelled of pine resin and old snow and, faintly, of iron — the iron smell that had hung over everything since the night the stars went out, since the wards fell like a curtain cut from its rod.

The border itself was stranger than he'd imagined. He had catalogued it once, in another life: the Thornfell wards described in a twelfth-century folio as *a seam of starlight stitched into the world*, invisible from within, unmistakable from without. Crossing it now felt like walking through a doorway made of held breath. The air pressure changed. His ears popped. For three heartbeats every sound vanished — the wind, his own footsteps, the tick of his watch — and then the world resumed on the far side, subtly wrong, like a song transposed into a minor key.

He stood very still and let the silence finish passing through him.

Then he took out the stolen map and held it up against the horizon.

The horizon lied.

Where Thornfell's northern marches should have risen — the terraced vineyards of the Oyelaran hill country, the smoke of Emberhold's kilns, the white line of the Starward Road climbing toward the buried hall — there was nothing but forest gone wild, trees grown fat and lawless in a century without roads, swallowing what might once have been field walls. A hundred years of forgetting, growing like moss over stone.

And beyond the forest: torchlight. Many torches.

Wren crouched in the bracken at the treeline and did what he had always done best. He watched, and he wrote.

* * *

The camp spread across the valley below like a stain.

He counted fires first — a habit from the war-ledgers, where supply columns could be estimated by cookfire density — and stopped counting somewhere past four hundred. Tents in the livery of three different kingdoms: the black-and-gold pavilions of Vessarion to the east, the lean canvas shelters of the Marcher League, the brutal square blocks of the Karstish legions, who had not crossed their own borders in living memory and were apparently making up for lost time. Siege engines under oilcloth. Corrals of remounts. Wagons stacked with surveyors' stakes and coils of rope and the long tripod legs of instruments Wren recognized from the archive's cartography wing — theodolites, heliotropes, the cold elegant tools of people measuring land they intended to own.

Survey stakes already marched across the valley floor in neat red-tipped rows. Someone had tied little flags to them, and the flags snapped in the wind with a sound like small applause.

There were no soldiers drilling. That was the detail that turned Wren's stomach, more than the engines or the numbers. Armies preparing for war drill; he knew the rhythm of it from a hundred after-action reports. These men were *waiting*. They lounged at their fires. They gambled. They sharpened nothing. They had the loose-limbed patience of an inheritance being probated — heirs around a rich uncle's deathbed, waiting for the lawyers to finish.

Because that was what they thought had happened. Wren understood it now, watching a Vessarion officer laugh over a wine bottle with a Marcher captain, watching them gesture lazily at the dark mountains as though dividing a pie. To the world outside, Thornfell had simply *ceased*. One hundred years ago the stars above this region had dimmed, the trade roads had ended in wilderness, and the maps had quietly agreed among themselves that there was nothing here worth naming. No conquest. No treaty. Just absence, patient and total, and now — with the wards broken, with the stars gone out entirely instead of merely dimmed — absence had become opportunity.

A clerk moved between the command tents with a lantern and a ledger, and Wren, driven by an archivist's instinct older than his fear, crept close enough to read upside-down over the man's shoulder as he worked by firelight.

*Provisional Partition of the Unclaimed Highlands,* said the ledger's header, in beautiful Vessarion copperplate. Beneath it, columns. *Lot One: the western valleys, to the Marcher League.* *Lot Two: the central range and all mineral rights, to the Karstish Crown.* *Lot Three:* — and here Wren's breath stopped, because Lot Three was Emberhold, was the orphanage, was Sefi's small bed and the kiln-smoke Lior grew quiet whenever she spoke of it — *Lot Three: disputed, pending settlement.*

Pending settlement. As though a town full of living people were a clause in a contract.

And at the bottom of the page, stamped in grey wax — grey, not black, grey like the Wardens' cloaks, grey like ash — a seal Wren knew intimately. He had catalogued it ten thousand times. The Regent of Ash's mark: a circle bisected by a horizontal line. The sign of the forgetting.

The Regent had signed the partition.

Wren sat back on his heels in the freezing bracken and felt the argument he had been carrying against the man begin, very quietly, to change shape.

* * *

He should have gone back. Every instinct honed by thirty years of surviving institutions told him so: gather your evidence, withdraw, report. But the archive had also taught him that evidence without context was a weapon that cut its wielder, and so he stayed one more night, and then another, moving along the edge of the camp like a moth along the rim of a lamp, reading everything.

He read the proclamation nailed to the wellhouse door of an abandoned village just past the border — a village whose houses still stood but whose wells had silted shut, whose orchards had grown into thickets, where a hundred years of no one had left its fingerprints on everything. The proclamation offered amnesty to any persons found within the Unclaimed Highlands, provided they presented themselves for *documentation and resettlement*. Resettlement. The word was doing a great deal of work. Wren had seen it before, in the annexes of other kingdoms' histories, in the chapters that preceded the ones about refugee camps.

He listened to soldiers talk. This was easier than it should have been; nobody guarded against a stooped, grey, forgettable man gathering firewood at the camp's edge. Around one fire, Karstish legionaries complained about the wait. "Stars went out over these hills a season back," one said, spitting. "Old stories say that means the country's dying for good. My grandmother used to say there was a kingdom here once." Laughter around the fire. "Your grandmother," said another, "told you the river eels paid taxes." More laughter, but the first soldier didn't laugh. He looked north, at the mountains, with an expression Wren recognized because he saw it in his own mirror every morning: the expression of a man trying to remember a dream that mattered.

That, too, went into the notebook.

And on the third night, he found the thing he had come for, though he would have given anything not to have found it.

He had slipped into the Vessarion command tent after midnight — not bravado, never bravado, only the archivist's compulsion, the knowledge that the true record is always in the private papers — and there, on the campaign table, weighted down with a dagger and a candlestick, lay the original partition draft. With marginalia. In the Regent's own hand; Wren knew that hand the way a priest knows scripture.

Beside each lot, the same annotation, repeated in increasingly weary iterations:

*Ensure no survivors of the old administration remain in Lots One and Two.*

*Resettlement protocols per the Emberhold precedent. Nothing that remembers may stay.*

And beside Lot Three, in handwriting gone suddenly tired, suddenly human, pressed hard enough into the paper to tear it slightly:

*They fed them. God forgive us, we fed them, and it bought a hundred years, and now the wolves are at the dam, and they will call me monster for holding it. Let them. Better a monster than a gravedigger.*

Wren stood in the dark tent with the page trembling under his fingers and understood, with terrible clarity, that he was looking at the confession of a man who believed himself to be the last sane officer of a drowning ship.

The erasure was not greed. Or not only greed. It was a dam — and the flood it held back was standing in this valley, four hundred cookfires strong, with survey stakes in the ground and resettlement protocols drafted and siege engines under oilcloth. Adaeze Oyelaran and eight other frightened rulers had sworn away their country's name to buy their people food and peace, and the price had seemed monstrous and the alternative had seemed worse, and for a hundred years the arithmetic had held. The wolves had stayed outside. The people of Thornfell had lived unmolested inside a lie, poor and unwitnessed and alive.

And now Lior wanted to speak the stars back, and raise the Ninefold Court, and put Thornfell's name in the world's mouth again.

And the world's mouth was full of knives.

* * *

Wren walked back through the border-seam at dawn, and this time the silence on the far side felt less like held breath and more like a held grief. Inside the wards, the light was strange and starless but the land was *theirs* — every crooked mile of it, every shrine and smuggler's path. Outside, the sun rose honest and golden over four hundred fires and a ledger dividing a living country into lots.

Both things were true. That was the cruelty of it. He had spent his career teaching himself that every erasure had an author, and authors could be hated cleanly. But the author here had signed the partition with tears in his marginalia, and the wolves were real, and the dam was cracking, and the woman he followed was proposing to break it open on purpose.

He found the camp at midday — Lior's camp, the real one, tucked in a fold of the hills where the old Starward Road dipped beneath a ridge. Sefi saw him first. She was teaching two of the rescued soldiers the anthem's second verse, badly, joyfully, and when she spotted him stumbling up the switchback she abandoned the lesson mid-line and ran.

"You brought something true," she said, grabbing his sleeve.

"I brought too much true," Wren said. "That's rather the problem."

Lior came out of the command lean-to with her sword half-buckled, reading his face before he'd said a word, the way she read weather. Behind her, the ghost of Adaeze Oyelaran flickered at the edge of the firelight, translucent and watchful, and Wren met the dead woman's eyes and found, to his surprise, that he pitied her now. He hadn't expected that either.

"Three kingdoms," he said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "Vessarion, the Marcher League, Karst. Four hundred fires at the northern pass, siege trains, survey stakes already in the ground. They have partitioned Thornfell into lots, Lior. Emberhold is Lot Three. Disputed." He swallowed. "And the Regent has countersigned it. Not to claim the land for himself. To control who takes it. There are orders in his hand — *nothing that remembers may stay* — and beneath them—" he pulled the memory of the marginalia up like a splinter— "*better a monster than a gravedigger.*"

Silence, except the wind in the pines and the distant sound of Sefi's soldiers resuming the anthem's second verse without her.

"He was right about one thing," Lior said at last, and her voice was quiet in the way that scared Wren far more than shouting would have. "The moment our name is spoken in the world again, every knife outside these hills will remember why it was forged." She looked north, toward the border she could feel like a missing tooth, toward the fires Wren had counted. "So we don't give them a country to carve."

"No?" Wren said.

"We give them a court." Her hand closed around the hilt of her sword, and the ghost beside her nodded once, slow and sorrowful and proud. "Kingdoms can be partitioned. Courts must be seated. Get your notebook, Wren. We're going to need every name you collected — and we'll need them fast."

"Why fast?"

Lior turned back toward him, and behind her the starless sky pressed down like a lid.

"Because the Regent knows you were in that tent," she said. "And the Wardens ride at dusk."
