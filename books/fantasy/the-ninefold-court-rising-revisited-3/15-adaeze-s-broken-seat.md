## 15. Adaeze's Broken Seat

I have been a stone in the river of my family for one hundred years.

Not a stone in the sense the living mean it — not still, not patient, not worn smooth by water into something acceptable to hold in the palm. A stone in the sense that the current cannot move me and I cannot move with it. The founding oath is the riverbed. I am set into it like mortar, and every year that passes over me wears me thinner, and every year I am grateful for the wearing, because thinness is almost an ending and I have wanted an ending since the night I knelt in this hall with eight others and unmade our country.

They do not tell you what a ghost's body is made of. Not mist — the poets are wrong about mist. A ghost is made of the oath that binds her. I am made of Adaeze Oyelaran's last spoken vow: *let Thornfell be forgotten so its people may be fed.* Those words are my bones, my blood, my breath that does not breathe. When Lior speaks to me she is speaking to a sentence. When I reach for her hand and feel nothing pass between us — no warmth, no cold, only the strange pressureless nearness of one intention touching another — it is because sentences have no hands.

I did not choose to linger here. Understand that. No grandmother chooses to haunt her granddaughter through a century of winters. But the oath required a witness, and the oath chose me, and so I have stood in the dark beneath the mountains watching the nine seats gather dust and the dust gather silence, waiting for someone of my blood to come down these stairs angry enough to break me.

Lior came down the stairs angry. That was three nights ago, though time moves strangely down here where nothing rots and nothing ripens. She came with the archivist behind her and the child on her hip and a company of strays at her back — a soldier with a healing scar across his jaw, a smuggler woman who whittles when she thinks, two brothers who used to drive ore-carts before they remembered there had ever been ore to cart out of Thornfell. They sleep now in the antechamber where petitioners once waited, wrapped in whatever wool they own, and the mine above them breathes slow and cold.

And Lior — Lior sits on the floor beside my seat.

That is what I want to tell her and cannot find the shape of words for: she sits beside the Seat of the Hearth as though it were a kitchen bench and I were merely late coming in from the fields. Her granddaughter's stubbornness. Her mother's habit of making herself at home in places that should frighten her. Mine. All of it mine, passed down like eye color, like a temper, like a debt.

Tonight she has brought a candle stub and a strip of dried pear and set them on the seat's armrest — offerings, though she would never call them that. "For you," she said, as if feeding a ghost were the same as feeding a stray dog. "You probably can't eat it. But it seemed rude to just talk at you."

The pear smells like an orchard I burned rather than surrender to the Vellish army in the famine year. I could have told her that. I did not. There are confessions enough queued up between us without adding the orchard to the front of the line.

* * *

Let me begin where all honesty must begin: with the seat itself.

Nine seats stand in this hall, cut from the mountain's own bones before the mountain had a name. The Seat of the Hearth — mine — is carved from granite shot through with garnet, so that firelight catches in it like embers caught mid-flight. It was said that whoever sat the Hearth-Seat could draw warmth from stone and give it back to the cold, could make a granary yield past reason, could hold a household together with nothing but attention. Small magics. Domestic magics. The kind that win wars by keeping soldiers fed and lose them the same way, when the granaries empty.

Each seat holds a fragment of power. Wren knows this — he catalogued the hall a hundred years ago, poor man, with ink-stained fingers and a clerk's obedience, measuring each seat and writing down what each contained without ever once asking himself who would want such a catalogue or why. The Hearth fragment sleeps in my seat still. I can feel it the way an amputee feels a hand: warm, present, useless to me. It will not answer a ghost. Ghosts are made of oaths, and the fragment is made of country, and the founding oath has bound the country fast.

But an anchor does not need to be answered. An anchor needs only weight.

If Lior speaks a counter-oath in this hall — if she stands among the nine seats and swears the founding vow broken, named and renounced in the old forms — the breaking will need something to hold it true, or it will gutter out like a flame in wind, and she will have spent her fury for nothing and alerted the Regent besides. The fragments are the natural anchors. Nine fragments, nine seats, a new oath seated in the old ones. But eight of those fragments belong to houses that no longer exist, sleeping in stone that has forgotten how to be asked.

Mine is different. Mine is different because I am here, wound around it, a hundred years of regret packed against it like snow against a wall. My fragment remembers being loved. If Lior forgives me — truly forgives, not the polite absolution the living grant the dead to make themselves comfortable — the bond between my oath-self and my seat will crack, and through the crack the fragment can pour itself into her new vow and anchor it the way a keel steadies a ship.

Forgiveness as engineering. I laugh at it sometimes, alone in the dark. A hundred years of penance, and it comes down to whether a girl with her grandmother's chin can mean a kind word.

* * *

She came tonight to ask me about the swearing. Not to accuse — she is past accusation, I think, or she is saving it — but to understand, and understanding is harder to bear than blame. Blame keeps the blamer safe. Understanding asks you to stand where the sinner stood.

"Tell me again," she said. "From the beginning. You always stop before the beginning."

"The beginning is a famine," I told her. "You know this."

"I know the shape of it. Wren gave me the histories." She turned the candle stub in her fingers. "Histories aren't the same as standing in it."

So I stood her in it. This is what ghosts are for, in the end — we are memory with nowhere else to put itself.

The winter I was forty-one, the rains failed for the third year running, and Thornfell — which had been at war with Vellmark for eleven years, and skirmishing with Karsava for six, and bleeding along every border like a stuck animal — found itself unable to feed both its armies and its children. You have heard versions of this. Every country has a famine story; ours was simply the one where we chose wrongly and then chose rightly and could not live with either choice.

The war councils said: burn the eastern villages, fall back to the fortress line, let Vellmark take empty land. Save the heart of the country by cutting off the limbs. I voted for it. Write that down in whatever ledger heaven keeps — Adaeze Oyelaran voted to burn her own eastern marches, and she slept afterward, and her sleep was dreamless, and she counted that dreamlessness a mercy.

My daughter Ilana — Lior's great-great-grandmother, the one whose face you carry — voted against it. She stood up in council with her son in her arms and said, "If the country survives by forgetting its people, what exactly have we saved?" And the room hated her for it, and I hated her for it, because she was right and rightness in a famine is an obscenity.

It was Ilana who found the other road. Not conquest — the Regent lies about that part, when he tells it, and he tells it beautifully. No enemy broke us. We broke ourselves on purpose, and the weapon we chose was forgetting.

The nine houses met in this hall on the longest night of the worst winter. There was a sorcery then — the last working of the Court's combined strength, all nine fragments poured into a single vessel of law. The oath would fold Thornfell out of the world's memory. Maps would blur. Records would read as blank. Neighboring kingdoms, whose appetite for our valleys was the engine of every war, would look east and see only mountains worth ignoring. Our people would remain — alive, fed on the grain the wars had been eating — but nameless, scattered into other nations' census rolls like salt into soup. A country that could not be conquered because it could not be found.

And the price, which the oath-speakers knew and accepted: none of us would remember clearly either. Not fully. We would keep a shadow-knowledge, a guilt without a crime attached, and our descendants would inherit that shadow and call it melancholy, or temperament, or the family habit of staring out windows at nothing.

"We will feed them," I said, when it was my turn to speak my piece. First to swear. I was first to swear. "We will feed them, and we will be forgotten for it, and that is a fair trade, and I will go first so the rest of you may follow a brave woman instead of leading a frightened one."

Eight voices followed mine. Ilana refused hers until the end — she swore last, weeping, holding her son, because the oath needed unanimity or none of it would hold, and because she believed — gods forgive us all, she *believed* — that a living nameless child outweighed a dead named country.

I went first, and I regretted it before the echo left the walls.

* * *

Here is the thing I stopped telling Lior before the beginning, the thing I stopped telling her every night this week: the regret did not come later. It was not some slow dawn of conscience over years. It came *in the act*. The moment the oath left my mouth I felt my seat go cold beneath me — felt the Hearth fragment turn its face away, the way a dog turns from a master who has kicked it — and I knew with total clarity that I had done the cruelest thing a ruler can do, which is to decide for a people that they would prefer survival to themselves.

Because that is what erasure was. Not mercy. Theft dressed as mercy. We stole from every soul in Thornfell the knowledge of what they were, and we told ourselves it was kindness, because the alternative was admitting we had chosen our own guilt over their grief.

The famine ended. The wars did not come — Vellmark's generals looked at our mountains and yawned, Karsava's merchants forgot there had ever been a trade road — and the children ate, and grew, and did not know why their grandmothers hummed songs no one had written down. And I stayed. Bound as witness. Watching a century of the forgetting work its way through my family like grey dye through water.

Lior listened to all of it tonight with her hands flat on the cold floor, and when I finished she was quiet for a long time, and then she said the thing I had been dreading:

"You're still bound to it. The oath. That's why you can't leave this hall."

"Yes."

"And if it breaks—"

"I break with it." I made myself say it plainly. "A witness is released when the contract ends, or consumed by its ending. With an oath this old, consumed. I don't know what that means for a ghost. Possibly nothing. Possibly everything. I've had a century to imagine it and I still don't know, and that not-knowing is the only fear I have left, and I would like you to understand that I accept it. I am not telling you this so you'll spare me. I'm telling you because you deserve to weigh my death alongside everything else before you decide anything."

She looked at me the way her daughter looked at me once across a burning orchard — no. Wrong. Across a *saved* orchard. I get the fires confused; there were so many.

Then Lior said: "You voted to burn the east. You swore first. You built the cage and locked yourself in it and you've spent a hundred years watching your family breathe its air." Her voice didn't shake. "Why should I forgive you?"

"Because I am asking," I said. "Because no one else ever did. Because the oath took my voice along with everything else and I have stood in this hall wanting to shout *undo it, undo it* at every Oyelaran who walked these stairs, and none of them could hear me, and none of them came angry enough, and you—" and here, for the first time in a hundred years, my sentence-self frayed at the edges, "—you came angry enough. Anger is the only key that fits this lock, child. Grief slides off it. Duty slides off it. But anger wants an object, and the object is the oath, and if you aim it true you will break it, and I will break with it, gladly, and my fragment goes with you into whatever you build next."

"That's not forgiveness," she said quietly. "That's a transaction."

"It is both," I said. "Everything honest is."

* * *

She did not forgive me tonight. She stood, and picked up her candle, and said, "I need to think," which is what Oyelarans say instead of *no* when the answer might yet become *yes*, and she walked up the stairs with her shoulders square and her jaw set, carrying my dried pear with her, which I choose to interpret as a good sign, because taking the offering means she considers the relationship ongoing.

But at the top of the stairs she stopped. Turned half-back. The candlelight caught the garnet in my seat and threw little red stars across the floor between us — stars, in this starless country, the only ones anyone has seen in weeks.

"Grandmother," she said. "When the oath broke your voice — did it also break the humming? Sefi says she learned the anthem from dreams. Whose dreams?"

"Mine," I said. "A hundred years of humming it in here, where no one could hear."

"She heard." Lior's mouth did something that was almost a smile and almost grief. "A child nobody erased managed to hear you anyway. Maybe forgiveness isn't the only thing that travels through cracks."

Then she was gone, and I sat in the dark with the red stars fading, and I thought: *she is going to do it. She is going to speak the counter-oath, and I am going to spend myself anchoring it, and my last act in the world will be to help unmake the worst thing I ever made.*

Above me, faint through the mountain's stone, I heard the strangest sound — many feet, many voices, drawing closer through the deep galleries. Singing. Hundreds of them, singing the anthem I had hummed alone for a hundred years, and under the singing, boots, and under the boots, the unmistakable dry whisper of the Regent's Wardens moving to meet them.

The cracks were opening everywhere now. What fell through them was no longer only memory.

It was war.
