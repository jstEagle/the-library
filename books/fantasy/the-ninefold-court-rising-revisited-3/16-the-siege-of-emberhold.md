## 16. The Siege of Emberhold

The fire came out of the north like a second dawn, and it was wrong in every way a dawn could be wrong.

Lior stood on the roof of the Emberhold tannery with her sword already drawn, though there was nothing yet to fight—only light where no light should be at that hour, a red-orange bloom over the hills that had no sun behind it. The smoke that rose from it did not rise straight. It curled and bent toward the town, as if the burning were inhaling.

"They've fired the orchards," said Dagny beside her, one of the rescued soldiers, his voice flat the way men's voices went when they had decided not to feel something yet. "The whole eastern slope. That's not a raid. That's an erasure."

She knew. She had known since midnight, when Sefi had woken the whole mine with screaming—not nightmare-screaming but name-screaming, the girl sitting upright in her blankets reciting streets of Emberhold under her breath like a rosary: *Tallow Row, Bellmaker's Close, the Steps of Ash, the Steps of Ash, the Steps of Ash*—as if she were trying to hold each one in her mouth before someone took them.

Now someone was taking them.

Below, Emberhold was waking badly. Lanterns swung in windows. A woman's voice carried from the well square, cracked with sleep and fear. Somewhere a dog barked itself hoarse against the smell of smoke, which even at this distance tasted wrong—not woodsmoke but something mineral, scorched salt and old iron, the smell Lior had learned to associate with Warden-work. They burned with a fire that didn't just consume. It *unmade*. What it touched would not be destroyed; it would be *never-haved-been*, and the ash left behind would be the only proof anything had stood there, and even the ash would confuse people who looked at it.

"The archive first," Lior said. "They'll go for the archive."

Dagny frowned. "It's a cellar full of paper."

"It's the only building in this valley that remembers Thornfell's true borders. Wren catalogued what they kept there—the old survey rolls, the parish books, the muster lists from before the Dissolution." Her grip tightened on the sword hilt until her knuckles ached pleasantly, a small pain to hang things on. "Burn the archive and every claim we make is a story. Leave it standing and it's a record. The Regent knows the difference. He was an official once. Officials always burn the paperwork first."

She turned and went down through the tannery, taking the stairs three at a time, calling orders ahead of herself in the smuggler's shorthand that had become their army's tongue over these weeks: *two fingers to the ford, eyes on the north road, keep your names in your mouth.* Behind her, the mine company moved. Not soldiers—they would never be soldiers—but forty-odd souls who remembered, and remembering, it turned out, made people very hard to frighten into forgetting again.

Sefi met her at the street door, already dressed, hair unbraided and flying, Oren the smuggler's enormous hand resting on her shoulder like a yoke.

"You're not fighting," Lior told her.

"I'm *speaking*," Sefi said, and her chin came up with that stubborn set that Lior had long ago stopped mistaking for childishness. "That's different."

"Sefi—"

"You said my memory was a weapon. You said it at the ford. I heard you tell Wren." Her eyes were too old, the way they always were after she dreamed of the throne hall. "You don't get to say a thing like that and then hide me in a root cellar when it matters."

Lior crouched to her height. The smoke-smell was stronger now, threading in under the door. She thought about lying to the child and found, examining herself honestly, that she no longer knew how. That was what Sefi had done to her, among other things.

"Then you stay behind the line," she said. "Behind me. You speak when I tell you to speak, and you stop when I tell you to stop, and if I say run, you run and you don't argue, because arguing is how Oren got that scar and he'll show you it later and gloat. Do we understand each other?"

"We understand each other," Sefi said solemnly, and then, because she was still also a child: "Oren says he got the scar falling off a mule."

"He did," Lior said, straightening, drawing the door open onto the burning dark. "He tells a better story about it, though. Come on."

* * *

Emberhold fought the way a town fights when its people have decided the town is worth more than their fear—which is to say, messily, bravely, and with a great deal of shouting.

The Wardens came down the Tallow Row road in their grey cloaks, thirty of them in a wedge, carrying not swords primarily but lanterns—grey-flamed things that guttered without wind and threw light that fell *short* of everything it touched, so that the street seemed to shrink around them as they walked. Behind the lantern-bearers came the burners with their clay pots of the grey fire, and behind them the crossbows, and above them all rode a Warden-captain whose face Lior couldn't hold in her memory no matter how she looked at him. That was the erasure working in reverse, she realized. They had given their officers faces you weren't permitted to remember.

But the strays were already in the upper windows. And Emberhold itself—Emberhold, which had spent a century unable to say its own nation's name, whose people had wept without knowing why at certain songs—had chosen, tonight, to remember.

Lior heard it before she saw it work. From the doorway of the chandlery, old Marta Voss—who had once told Lior, kindly, that she must be confused, there had never been any Thornfell—stood with her arms raised and her voice pitched to carry, calling out house-names. Not the new ones, the blank ones the maps agreed on. The old ones. *House of the Nine Bells. House of Adaeze Oyelaran, may her rest be troubled until it is just.* And where her words struck the grey lantern-light, the light recoiled like a hand pulled back from a stove.

"Names burn it," Dagny breathed beside her. "Marta's *burning it*."

"Not Marta." Lior was watching Sefi, small and straight-backed in the lee of the well, hands cupped around her mouth. "Her. Listen."

And Sefi began to sing.

Not the anthem—that would come later, that would come at the end, when everything else had failed or succeeded and there was nothing left to lose. Tonight it was simpler and more terrible. She sang the census. Street by street, door by door, in the old cadence Wren had taught her from the salvaged rolls: *Alder Fenwick, bell-founder, and his wife Sorrel, and their daughters Ivy and Bramble, born under the ninth star of Thornfell's sky.* Each name landed in the smoky air like a hammer on a bell. Each name made the grey fire flinch.

Because that was the thing about erasure, Lior understood suddenly, watching it recoil—it was never finished. It was maintained. Every day, everywhere, by the weight of the world agreeing. And a child singing the dead census aloud in a burning street was the sound of that agreement *breaking*.

The Wardens' wedge faltered at the mouth of Tallow Row. Their lanterns guttered. One of the burners dropped his pot, and the grey fire spilled across the cobbles and *went out*, snuffed by nothing, by a girl's voice naming a bell-founder dead eighty years.

"Now," Lior said, and drew steel, and led the countercharge down the Steps of Ash with forty strays howling behind her.

* * *

Fighting, for Lior, had always been a kind of cartography. You read the ground. You found the lines of force. You guided violence along the routes it wanted to take anyway and let it exhaust itself against terrain of your choosing. It was the one skill from her old life that had transferred whole into this one, and she used all of it now.

She gave them the narrow way past the tannery vats because a wedge cannot turn in a narrow way. She let the crossbows loose their volley early, into smoke, because smoke was cheap and her people were not. She put Dagny and the ford-soldiers on the flank where their drilled discipline could do what discipline does—hold, hold, hold—while the smugglers did what smugglers do, which was appear where they should not be, behind the lantern-bearers, with knives and chain-hooks and the specific vicious creativity of people who had been lied to by respectable roads all their lives.

Lior herself fought the captain with the forgettable face.

His blade was grey steel that drank light, and every time it touched hers the contact felt less like metal than like a page being torn—a little flutter of loss, a word gone from a sentence. She understood, in the body-deep way of combat, that he was not trying to kill her quickly. He was trying to *subtract* her. Cut away the parts of her that remembered, piece by piece, until what remained was a woman standing in an ordinary street fire with no idea why.

So she stopped letting him touch her blade.

She fought close and low, inside his reach, shoulder-to-shoulder, the way her grandmother's ghost had once shown her in a shrine-yard dream—*the old style, the Court style, the style they erased from every fencing manual in nine kingdoms*—and it was like opening a door in her own muscles. Her feet knew it. Her wrists knew it. Adaeze Oyelaran's blood knew it, and sang through it, and the captain with the forgettable face found himself fighting a ghost's technique wielded by a living woman, and it undid him.

She broke his lantern-hand at the wrist. His grey light died. In the sudden ordinary darkness—firelight, honest firelight, orange and warm—he became visible for the first time: young, exhausted, terrified, a farm-boy's jaw under the grey hood.

"Say it," Lior panted, sword-point at his throat, and behind her Sefi's voice rang out clear over the battle: "*Corporal Edmun Raeth, third son of Hollowmere, sworn under the ninth star—*"

He shuddered like a man coming up from deep water. His eyes focused. He looked at the grey fire eating the orchards, at his own dropped lantern, at his own hands—and he began, quietly, horribly, to cry.

"Go home," Lior told him. "Or don't go home. But stop burning things."

* * *

They won the street.

By the time the eastern sky showed its first honest grey, the Wardens' wedge had broken, a dozen of their number standing dazed and weeping in the custody of the people they'd come to erase, and the grey fire in the orchards was dying under a bucket-line of Emberhold townsfolk who worked with the grim efficiency of people saving not trees but *proof*. The smoke thinned. Someone, incredibly, put water on for tea. Oren sat on the tannery step with Sefi asleep against his shoulder, her voice finally worn out, and Lior stood in the wreckage of Tallow Row feeling the wild animal triumph of a won fight move through her blood—

—and then Wren Vale came running out of the archive lane, and she saw his face, and the triumph curdled before it ever cooled.

"Wren." She caught his arm. He was grey-lipped, singed, shaking. "Wren, what happened. Where's the fire?"

"There was no fire." His laugh was a broken thing. "That's the point. There was never any fire, Lior, that's what they do, that's what they *did*—" He pressed his hands flat against his own temples as if holding his skull together. "I went down to pull the survey rolls, the pre-Dissolution borders, everything we need for the Second Oath's warrant. The cellar was full of ash. Floor to ceiling. Cold ash, weeks old, months. And the stairwell walls—" His voice failed and restarted. "Someone carved tally marks. Dates. Going back years. They've been *emptying it slowly*, roll by roll, book by book, and tonight they came to finish, and I—" 

He looked up at her, and Lior saw the exact moment the archivist in him died and something harder took its place.

"I catalogued that collection," he whispered. "Twenty years ago, when I still believed the forgetting was mercy. I wrote its index in my own hand. And now the index describes nothing. It's a map of a country that isn't there. Which is fitting," he added, with a bitterness like acid, "since that was rather the job description."

Lior looked down the archive lane. The building stood untouched, whitewashed, innocent. No scorch marks. No broken door. Only a thin drift of fine grey powder seeping out from under the cellar door like breath, like the building exhaling its last memory.

All those names. Alder Fenwick the bell-founder. The muster rolls, the parish books, the births and marriages and debts of a hundred years of Thornfell's small forgotten lives—all of it, reduced to cold ash while they'd been winning a street.

The fury came up through her like floodwater through a cellar stair. She let it. She stood in the lane and let it fill her to the brim, and she did not scream and she did not break anything, because somewhere in the last months she had learned the difference between fury that consumes and fury that *fuels*. This was the second kind. It burned clean.

"They think the record was our foundation," she said at last, quietly. Wren looked at her. "They think if they burn the paper, the oath has nothing to stand on. But the paper was never the foundation, Wren. It was only the *receipt*." She turned back toward the well square, where Sefi slept, where Marta Voss was telling the freed soldiers their own names, where four hundred people who remembered were beginning, in the grey morning light, to gather—drawn by smoke and rumor and the unstoppable human gravity of a story being told aloud.

"The foundation is walking around," Lior said. "It's singing the census. It's asking questions. You can't cellar-burn people." She picked up her sword from where she'd leaned it against the wall. "Get the mine company up. All of them. Everyone who can walk and everyone who can't—we carry them. We're done hiding in holes and defending what we have."

"Lior." Wren's voice was careful. "Where are we going?"

She looked north, past the burnt orchards, to where the mountains shouldered up black against the fading stars—the few stubborn stars that had bled back through since the ford, faint as breath on glass. Toward the buried hall. Toward the nine empty seats.

"We're going to stop keeping their calendar," she said. "Wake everyone. We march at dusk."

And far off, high on the northern ridge, a single grey lantern kindled in the dark—as if the Regent of Ash had seen her answer coming, and was already lighting the road to meet it.
