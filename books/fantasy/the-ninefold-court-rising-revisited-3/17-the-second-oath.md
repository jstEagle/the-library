## 17. The Second Oath

The mine had been a silver seam once, before the erasure, and the silver was still there in the walls — Wren could see it when he held his lamp close, threads of pale metal running through black stone like veins through a closed fist. The miners who remembered Thornfell had lit fires at the shaft mouths, and the smoke rose and curled along the ceiling, and the whole cavern smelled of pitch and cold iron and the particular mineral sweetness of deep rock. Somewhere below, water dripped with the patience of centuries.

Wren sat at a trestle table salvaged from the foreman's office and tried to write an oath.

He had written oaths before. That was the poison of it. In the Archive of the Grey Wardens — though it had not been called that then, when he was young and proud and believed that memory was a thing to be *managed* — he had drafted language for the Regent's clerks. Careful clauses. Elegant subordinations. He had once written four hundred words that made a village forget its own dead, and he had been commended for the economy of the phrasing.

His pen hovered over the page now, and his hand would not move.

Behind him, the mine breathed with people. They had come in ones and twos over the past three days: soldiers from the ford whose eyes still went wide sometimes when they remembered their own regiments turning on them; smugglers from Lior's scattered caravan, drawn back by word passed along roads that officially did not exist; families out of Emberhold's burning quarter, soot still in the creases of their knuckles. Forty-one souls, last count. Sefi counted every morning, standing on a crate by the first shaft, and she announced the number like a herald announcing a coronation.

Forty-one. And nine seats waiting under the mountain, empty as pulled teeth.

*Seated by service, not blood.* He had said it to Lior in the dark after the ford, and she had looked at him with an expression he was still learning to read — not forgiveness, exactly, but something adjacent to it, something that let him keep walking beside her. Now he had to make those words into law, and law, he knew better than anyone alive, was just forgetting with good posture.

He wrote: *Let the Ninefold Court be raised again.*

Crossed it out. Too much like resurrection, and resurrections always came with debts.

He wrote: *Let nine be chosen.*

Also wrong. Chosen by whom? He had spent a career watching men choose, and choice curdled into appointment, and appointment curdled into inheritance, and within three generations you were back where you started, with blood calling itself merit.

The lamp guttered. Down the tunnel, someone was singing — low, half-remembered, one of the rescued soldiers working through the anthem's second verse with Sefi correcting him in a whisper fierce as a drill sergeant's. The melody kept snagging on the places the erasure had chewed, gaps in the tune like missing teeth, and each time the singer reached a gap he hummed through it, and Sefi filled it, and slowly the verse was knitting itself whole again. Wren found he had to look down at his page until his eyes stopped stinging.

*Service,* he wrote. *Not blood. Let any soul who has carried this country — in exile, in silence, in chains of forgetting — stand for a seat. Let no name be a door, and no door be barred by birth.*

Better. He read it aloud under his breath, testing the weight of it the way a mason tests stone, and it held. He kept writing. The old Court had been nine houses because nine houses had existed, which was arithmetic pretending to be philosophy. The new Court should be nine *functions*: the road-keeper, the record-keeper, the speaker for the small, the warden of borders — no, not warden, never that word again — the keeper of thresholds. The hand that feeds. The voice that remembers. The sword that answers only to the spoken law. Nine chairs, nine duties, and any body willing to sit in the fire of them.

And then the clause he had saved for last, because he was a coward in exactly one respect and had known it since Emberhold.

*And let the founding oath be broken entire,* he wrote, *and let the House of Oyelaran be released from its burden, its name returned to it unbound, its debt paid—*

"Lior won't sign that."

Wren's pen jumped, leaving a blot like a small dark wound on the page. Sefi stood behind him — she moved quietly for a child, or perhaps he simply failed to hear children, having spent too many years among documents that never spoke — and she was looking at the page upside down with the unnerving fluency she had for written things. She was eleven. She had memorized the old Court's procedural codes from dreams. She read upside down as easily as right-side up. Some nights Wren lay awake wondering what else the erasure had left in her, intact, like a seed bank under snow.

"You can't know that," he said.

"She told me what her grandmother's ghost told her." Sefi came around the table and put one finger on the clause, on the words *released*, *unbound*, *paid*. "Adaeze said breaking the oath unmakes the Oyelaran name. Not frees it. *Unmakes.* Everyone who ever bore it stops being remembered as bearing it. Lior doesn't get to walk away clean. She gets to stop existing as herself." The finger tapped once. "You wrote it like a gift. It's a bill."

"I wrote it like mercy."

"You wrote it like a man paying off guilt," Sefi said, without cruelty, which was somehow worse, "with someone else's money."

She went back down the tunnel toward her singers, and Wren sat with the blot drying on the page and understood, with the particular clarity that only humiliation brings, that she was entirely right.

* * *

Lior came to him at the change of the watch, when the mine was quietest and the fires burned lowest. She moved like she always moved, silent on the gravel, a woman built by the high passes — economical, weathered, her sword worn at her hip the way other people wore their own arms. She sat down across the trestle without asking, which was itself a kind of answer, and looked at the pages spread before him for a long time.

"Read it to me," she said.

So he read it. All of it — the nine functions, the open seats, the bar against blood-right, the machinery of choosing by service. His voice steadied as it went, because some of it was genuinely good, and even a guilty man knows the difference between his penance and his work. When he reached the final clause, the release of the Oyelaran line, he read it evenly, without flinching, because flinching would have been its own insult.

Lior listened with her eyes on the middle distance, the way she listened to wind coming off a col, reading what it carried.

"The rest is sound," she said at last. "The seats by service. The open doors. Even the ugly parts — I'd have made them uglier. You've given the record-keeper power to compel testimony, and you've written *testimony* to include confession, which means someday a record-keeper will have to sit and listen to a man confess what you did at Emberhold's archive, or worse. Did you notice that?"

"Yes," Wren said. "I noticed."

"Good." She turned the pages toward her and read the release clause again, slowly, her lips moving slightly. Then she looked up. "But this part you wrote alone."

"I wrote it because it's true. Breaking the oath will cost your name. Adaeze told you. The ghost of your own grandmother told you what the price is, and I watched you decide to pay it anyway, alone, in a shrine in the rain, and I have thought about nothing else since. If the Second Oath can carry even a fraction of that price—" He stopped, gathered himself. "If the Court rises and owes its existence to your sacrifice, then we haven't broken the old pattern. We've just changed whose blood pays. I refuse to build the new country on the same foundation as the old one. One person's erased name holding up everyone else's sky."

"That's noble," Lior said.

"It's justice."

"It's noble," she repeated, "and it's also you deciding my sacrifice for me, which is the exact crime the founding oath committed. Nine houses swore that the people of Thornfell would forget, and none of them asked the people. You're nine — well, forty-one — deciding that I will be forgotten, and you haven't asked me either." She pushed the page back across the table. Her voice didn't rise. It never needed to. "Ask me."

The lamp flame leaned in a draft from the shaft, and Wren heard the water dripping below, counting out seconds, and understood that he had just been architected by a smuggler with more constitutional instinct than the entire College of Clerks he had served for thirty years.

"All right," he said. "Then tell me what you want the clause to say."

"I want it struck," Lior said. "Entirely. No release. No return of my name unbound. Write instead that the breaking of the founding oath is borne by all who sign — every seat, every witness, every soul in this mine who wants the stars back. If my name goes into the dark, it goes with company. If the price is forgetting, then we all agree to remember together what it cost, and to whom, and why." She leaned forward, and the firelight caught the scar along her jaw, the one she'd gotten guiding caravans through the pass the winter the wards first flickered. "You think solidarity means sharing my bill. It doesn't. It means nobody pays alone, and nobody pays *for* another. Those are different things, Wren. The first oath confused them. Don't let the second one."

He sat very still. Below them, faintly, the anthem had reached its third verse, and forty-one voices were learning it.

"There's a problem," he said finally, because there was, and she deserved the truth of it from him if from no one else. "A legal problem. The founding oath was sworn by nine. To break it cleanly — to break it so it stays broken — the counter-oath needs weight behind it. Weight of names. If you sign alone, you're one house against nine, and the oath will crack you before it cracks the vow. But if all must bear it—" He hesitated. "Then all must be *able* to bear it. Every signer takes a share of the unmaking. Every person in this mine who signs risks their own name in the world's memory. Smugglers. Soldiers. Children." His eyes went involuntarily down the tunnel, toward the sound of the singing. "Sefi is eleven."

Lior was quiet for a long moment. The water dripped. The fire settled.

"Do you know what she told me yesterday?" she said at last. "While you were drafting. She said that when the Wardens took her memories at the orphanage — the ones they managed to take — it didn't hurt the way losing things usually hurts. She said it felt like being *edited*. Like someone had decided which parts of her were allowed to exist." Lior's jaw set. "She has already paid more of this price than any of us, and she paid it without being asked, and she is down there right now teaching grown men a song the world stole from them. If she chooses to sign, Wren — chooses, with Sefi's ferocious, impossible understanding of what oaths are — then I will not insult her by signing in her place. And if she chooses not to, no one will think less of her, and the oath will hold anyway, because I'll add my weight twice if I have to." She stood, and picked up his pen, and held it out to him. "Rewrite the clause. All who sign bear it together. None bears it for another. And then bring it to the mine floor and read it aloud, and let every soul here decide — freely, with no shame attached to refusal — whether Thornfell is worth their name."

Wren took the pen. His hand was shaking, and he let it shake.

"And if they refuse?" he asked. "If they hear the price and choose their own names?"

"Then we find another way," Lior said, "or we fail honestly. An oath sworn by the coerced is just the first oath wearing new clothes." At the mouth of the tunnel she paused, silhouetted against the far firelight, and added, without turning around: "For what it's worth — the archive clerk who wrote four hundred words to erase a village, and the guide who's about to lose her name to save a country, are going to sign the same page. I find I want that. I find it matters more than I expected."

Then she was gone down the tunnel, toward the singing, and Wren Vale sat alone with his lamp and his blot-stained draft and began, carefully, line by line, to write the truest thing he had ever written.

*Let all who sign bear the price together. Let none bear it for another. Let no name be spent in secret, and no sacrifice accepted that was not freely offered.*

When he finished, near dawn, the ink still wet, the first light of a starless morning finding the shaft mouth — Sefi appeared at his elbow again, read the new clause upside down, and nodded once, gravely, like a magistrate.

"It's ready," she said. "They're waiting. Everyone's awake. Nobody slept." A pause, and then, with the terrible directness of children: "Are you scared?"

"Terrified," Wren said.

"Good," said Sefi. "Me too. Lior says that's how you know an oath is honest." She held out her hand for the pages. "Come on, then. Time to see who Thornfell is."

And down the tunnel, forty-one voices — and more arriving even now, footsteps on the gravel, faces Wren didn't yet know, drawn by rumor and anthem and the smell of woodsmoke — were rising to the third verse, and the mine that had once yielded silver was about to yield something rarer.
