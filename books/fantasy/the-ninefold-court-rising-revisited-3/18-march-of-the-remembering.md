## 18. March of the Remembering

Sefi had never seen so many people in one place who were all looking at her.

That wasn't quite true. Once, in Emberhold, before the orphanage burned its records and the Wardens came with their grey hoods and their questions that weren't really questions, there had been a naming day when the whole town square turned toward her because she'd been chosen to carry the bread. But that had been different. That had been looking *at* her, at a small girl holding bread. This was looking *through* her — looking through her at something behind her, something none of them could see yet but all of them could feel.

She stood on the lip of the old mine's mouth with the dawn coming up grey behind the mountains, and the road below was full of people.

Hundreds. Maybe more than hundreds. They had come in the night and kept coming: smugglers with their pack-ponies and their knives worn smooth from use; soldiers still in the torn uniforms of regiments that officially did not exist; farmers with dirt under their nails and their children asleep against their shoulders; miners; weavers; an old woman who claimed, loudly, to have once baked for the Ninefold Court and whom nobody contradicted because it didn't matter whether it was true. It mattered only that they had come, and that every single one of them remembered.

Not everything. Sefi had learned that about memory in the long weeks since Emberhold — that remembering wasn't a door you opened all at once but a house full of rooms you entered one at a time, some of them warm and some of them terrible. The woman who baked for the Court might be lying about the Court but telling the truth about being hungry once, so hungry she would have sworn anything. The soldier with the torn uniform remembered his regiment's name but not why he'd stopped saying it out loud.

But they all knew the song.

They knew it the way you know your own heartbeat — not because anyone taught it to you but because it was there underneath everything, waiting. Sefi had hummed it in the orphanage dormitory and alone in the dark of the mine, and now, as the light came up over the pass, she lifted her voice and gave them the first line properly, the way Lior said her grandmother's ghost had sung it, the way it was meant to go:

*Nine seats beneath the mountain sleeping,*
*Nine names the night has sworn to keep.*

The road answered her.

It came up out of them like water finding a crack — ragged at first, uncertain, voices cracking on words half-forgotten, and then firmer, and then *loud*, hundreds of voices braiding together until the sound went up off the valley walls and rolled away into the passes like weather. Sefi felt it in her teeth. She felt it in the soles of her feet, through her boots, through the stone itself, as if the mountain were listening. As if the mountain had been listening for a hundred years and had finally, finally heard its name.

Beside her, Wren Vale made a small sound. She glanced up at him. The archivist was crying without any shame about it, tears running down into his beard, and he was mouthing the second verse along with everyone else, and his hands — those careful hands that had catalogued the erasure, that had written down what was being taken away — were shaking.

"Archivist," Sefi said, because he liked it when she used his title, "you're singing wrong."

"I am not," Wren said thickly.

"You're singing it like a sad song. It's not a sad song." Sefi looked back down the road, at all those faces turned upward, at the dawn catching the dust their marching raised. "Lior says it's a promise."

* * *

They marched at first light, because Lior wanted the sun at their backs and the shadows thrown forward, and because, as Lior put it in her flat guide's voice that meant she was frightened and covering it, "If the Wardens want to stop us, let them do it looking into the sun."

Lior walked at the front. Of course Lior walked at the front; Lior always walked at the front, even when the front was the most dangerous place, which Sefi privately thought was either very brave or very stupid and probably both. She had her sword loose in its sheath and her map-case slung across her back even though no chart in the world held the roads they were walking anymore, because Thornfell's roads lived now in Lior Oyelaran's feet, and her feet had never once lied to her.

Sefi walked beside her. That had been decided last night around the fire, in the brief, fierce council of the strays — the smuggler Brannic arguing for sending scouts ahead, the rescued soldier Yewen arguing for splitting the column, and Lior standing with her arms crossed saying no, no, and no.

"They expect us to hide," Lior had said. "Everything the Regent built expects hiding. A hundred years of forgetting works because the remembering stays small, stays scattered, stays ashamed. So we don't hide. We walk up the main road to the mountain door, and we sing loud enough to wake whatever's left under it."

"And if he brings the whole garrison?" Brannic had asked.

"Then they'll hear the anthem," Lior said, "and some of them will remember their mothers singing it. And some of those will lower their spears. And some won't, and we'll deal with that too." She had looked around the fire then, at all of them — smuggler and soldier and archivist and child — and her voice had gone quieter. "Nobody has to march who doesn't want to. Nobody fights who doesn't choose it. That's the whole point of what we're building. If we win by making people do things, we've just built the same cage with new bars."

Sefi hadn't understood all of it then. Marching now, feeling the column stretch out behind her like a river deciding its course, she understood it completely. Nobody was driving these people. They were driving themselves. Their feet found the rhythm of the anthem and the anthem carried them, and when the third verse came round again — the verse about the doors of morning, the verse Sefi loved best — she heard a farmer two rows back teaching it to his daughter, syllable by syllable, patient as planting.

The mountains rose ahead of them, blue-grey and enormous, wearing their clouds like old scarves. Somewhere inside them was the buried hall. Somewhere inside them were nine empty seats that had waited a hundred years for this exact morning.

Sefi sang louder.

* * *

The first Wardens appeared an hour past noon, where the road bent around a shoulder of rock above a ravine.

Sefi saw them before the shout went up — six figures in grey, standing across the road with the particular stillness of people who have been told to stand somewhere and are afraid to move. Behind them, further up the switchbacks, more grey shapes: a patrol, maybe twenty, blocking the pass. The column rippled and slowed, hundreds of footsteps faltering on the gravel, and for a moment the anthem wavered like a candle in wind.

"Don't stop," Lior called back, clear and carrying. "Whatever happens, keep singing. Keep *walking*. You're not an army. You're a country remembering itself, and no one on this earth can stand in front of that."

The Wardens' officer stepped forward. Sefi could see him clearly as they drew near — a young man, younger than Lior, with a pale face and the grey hood pushed back off close-cropped hair. His hand rested on his baton but didn't grip it. He looked, Sefi thought, less like a soldier facing an enemy than like a clerk who had found the ledgers suddenly, horribly wrong.

"In the name of the Regency," he called, and his voice cracked on the word *Regency*, "you are to disperse. Return to your homes. There is no—"

He stopped. Because the anthem had reached him.

Sefi watched it happen. Watched the words of the fourth verse — *and though the stars forgot our burning* — wash over him, watched his mouth move silently, shaping sounds his throat had forgotten how to make. His eyes went wide and then wet and then somewhere very far away, and when he spoke again his voice was different, smaller, younger, a boy's voice from a hundred years ago or from a kitchen yesterday.

"That's—" He swallowed. "My grandmother used to... I thought I dreamed that. I thought I dreamed the whole..."

"We all thought we dreamed it," Sefi said, stepping forward before Lior could stop her, the way children step forward before adults can stop them, because that is what children are *for*. She looked up at him, at his grey cloak and his pale frightened face, and she gave him what she had given the soldiers at the ford, what only she seemed able to give: the name. Not his name — the other kind. "*You are of Thornfell,*" she said. "Your grandmother was of Thornfell, and her mother before her, back to the founding, back to the nine houses, back to the famine and the oath and the long forgetting. And the forgetting is ending today."

Something went out of him. Something grey. Sefi saw it leave — saw it the way you see heat shimmer off summer stones — a thin veil lifting off his face, off his shoulders, dissolving in the mountain air. When he looked down at her again his eyes were his own.

"Halt the line," he said, turning to his own patrol, and his voice rang off the rocks. "Halt! Stand down your weapons. Stand *down*." And then, to Lior, in a voice that shook: "There are four more checkpoints between here and the upper gate. I can't call them off. But I can..." He fumbled at his belt, drew free a slate-grey token stamped with the Regency's seal, and snapped it in half. "I can fail to report. We all can."

Behind him, one by one, twenty Warden batons sank to point at the ground.

And twenty voices, hesitant, cracked, half-remembering, joined the anthem on the next verse.

* * *

After that it was easier, and after that it was harder, and Sefi couldn't decide which was worse.

Easier, because checkpoint after checkpoint melted as they came. The Wardens were not monsters; Sefi had known that since the ford, since she'd spoken the erasure off a soldier's mind and watched him fall to his knees weeping for a country he'd helped bury. They were sons and daughters of the erased land itself, set to guard its forgetting, and every one of them carried the anthem folded up small inside them like a letter never opened. All it took was the opening. Some of them needed only the song. Some needed her voice, her names, the strange inheritance she'd been born with that let her reach into the grey places and pull. One big sergeant wept so hard he had to sit down on the roadside, and a farmer's wife sat down beside him and held his hand and sang to him until he could stand again, and the column flowed around them both without stopping, because the column had learned that grief on the march was just another kind of walking.

Harder, because near dusk, at the last bend before the upper gate, they found the ones who wouldn't melt.

These Wardens stood in a solid rank across the road, forty strong, and they did not sing and they did not weep and they did not lower their batons. Their officer was older, iron-faced, and when Sefi stepped forward and began the speaking he shouted *now*, and the grey cloaks surged forward with their batons rising, and the front of the column screamed.

Sefi was knocked down. She tasted gravel and someone's blood that wasn't hers. Through the tangle of legs and shouting she saw Lior come through the crush like something out of the old songs — sword drawn at last, moving with the flat economical grace of a woman who had fought her whole life in places where losing meant dying — and saw the smuggler Brannic take a baton strike across the shoulder meant for the old baker-woman and go down laughing, actually laughing, and saw Yewen and the remembered soldiers lock shields they'd improvised from cart-lids and hold, hold, hold.

And through it all, impossibly, the anthem did not stop. Someone was always singing. When a voice went down another took it up, and the wounded sang through gritted teeth, and the old woman who had baked for the Court stood in the middle of the melee with her arms spread wide, singing the sixth verse at full volume, untouchable, as if the song itself were armor.

It ended quickly. The iron-faced officer, seeing his line waver — seeing three of his own drop their batons mid-swing and turn to face *outward* — gave a snarling order and fell back up the road toward the gate, dragging his loyalists with him, leaving nine of his men sitting dazed among the strays, blinking, touching their own faces as if checking whether they were still theirs.

The column reformed. Bruises were bound. Brannic's shoulder was splinted with a cart-pole and he insisted on walking anyway. Lior found Sefi in the press, knelt, checked her over with rough quick hands, and for one unguarded moment pressed her forehead against Sefi's.

"You scared me," Lior said.

"You told me nobody fights who doesn't choose it."

"You're seven."

"I'm the youngest speaker of the Ninefold Court," Sefi said, "and I chose." She wiped blood off her lip — her own, this time — and grinned at Lior's expression. "Besides. You're scared too. You said that's how you know an oath is honest."

Lior made a sound that was half laugh and entirely surrender, stood, and turned to face the road ahead.

Sefi followed her gaze, and her breath caught.

The upper gate stood open against the darkening sky. Beyond it, cut deep into the heart of the mountain, stairs descended — wide, ancient, star-carved, lit by nothing and glowing faintly anyway, the way the seats in her dreams had glowed. Cold air breathed up out of the deep place, smelling of stone and silver and something older than either, something like the moment just before a name is spoken.

And on the stairs, silhouetted against that buried glow, stood a single figure in grey, waiting. Not armed. Hands empty at his sides. Even at this distance Sefi knew the tired set of those shoulders, the weight of a man who had carried a country's silence for a century.

The Regent of Ash had come down to meet them himself.

The anthem rose behind her, hundreds of voices strong, echoing into the mountain's throat. Sefi took Lior's hand, and together, at the head of all the remembering, they started down the stars.
