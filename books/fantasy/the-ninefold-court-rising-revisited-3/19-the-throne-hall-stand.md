## 19. The Throne Hall Stand

The mountain swallowed them by degrees.

First the light went — the grey morning of the high passes thinning to a blue dusk that had nothing to do with hour or weather, a dusk that came from the stone itself, from the deep of the mountain remembering an older dark. Then the sound went: Sefi's humming, Wren's footfalls on the stair, the creak of Adaeze's presence like wind through a shut house — all of it muffled, folded away, until Lior could hear only her own heart and the slow drip of water somewhere far below, counting out seconds in a language older than any of them.

Then, last of all, the way went.

Lior felt it happen beneath her boots — a seam in the rock that had been a road for a thousand years and was now, suddenly, only rock. She stopped so abruptly that Sefi walked into her back, and for one vertiginous moment she stood on solid stone with no path under her at all, the way her grandmother's ghost had once described the erasure: *not destroyed, child. Unremembered. The mountain forgot it owned a door.*

"Here," she said, and her voice came out strange in the hush, too loud and too small at once. "This is where it should be."

Wren pressed past her, hands already moving over the wall — the archivist's blind search, fingertips reading stone the way other men read paper. He found it in less than a minute. Of course he did. He had catalogued this room; he had written its description in a hand that no longer existed, in ink made from a flower that no longer bloomed anywhere but in his memory.

"The threshold answers to blood," he said quietly. "Or to grief. The old records weren't clear which."

"Both, probably." Lior set her palm against the cold granite. Beneath the skin of stone she felt it — not a mechanism, not a ward, but something more like scar tissue. The founding oath had sealed this door when it unmade the Court above it, and every year since, the seal had grown into the mountain like a root. "It doesn't want to open. It wants to stay closed the way a wound wants to stay closed."

"Then we don't ask nicely." Sefi stepped up beside her, small hand finding Lior's wrist. The girl had been quiet for the last day of marching — quieter than Lior liked — and now Lior understood why. She was listening. Her head was tilted the way it tilted when she heard the anthem no one else could remember, and her lips moved without sound, shaping words.

*The nine seats wait beneath the stone,* the girl mouthed. *The nine seats wait for their own.*

The seam split.

Not violently — that was the terrible part. The stone parted the way lips part around a name, gently, reluctantly, with a breath of air that smelled of dust and myrrh and something burnt long ago, and the darkness beyond was not empty. It was *expectant*. Lior had guided caravans through caves and crypts and the buried chapels of dead kings, and none of them had ever felt like this: a room holding its breath, waiting a hundred years for someone to walk in and be judged.

She lit no torch. There was light below — a faint, sourceless amber, the color of lamplight seen through honey, pooling somewhere deep. She drew her sword instead, out of habit rather than hope, and led them down.

* * *

The stairs were wide enough for six abreast, cut for ceremony rather than defense, and every step was carved with a word in the Old Script. Lior read them as she descended, because she could not help it; her blood translated them before her mind caught up.

*We swore. We fed them. We forgot ourselves so they would not starve.*

*We swore. We fed them. We forgot ourselves—*

Over and over, step after step, the same confession worn into the rock by the feet of nine houses and ten thousand years of courtiers, until the words were shallow as riverbed stones. Wren ran his fingers along them and did not speak. Sefi read them aloud in a whisper, learning the Old Script the way she learned everything — instantly, hungrily, as if the erased country had been waiting inside her all along for a door to open.

At the bottom, the throne hall opened like a held breath released.

It was vast — vaster than Emberhold's whole temple district, vaster than anything the valley towns built anymore, because the people who had raised this roof had believed a country should make its greatness visible so that no one would ever mistake it for something negotiable. Nine pillars rose to a ceiling painted with constellations, and though the stars outside the world had gone dark, these had not. They burned still, patient and gold, a sky in exile preserved in pigment and prayer.

And between the pillars, on a dais of black basalt, stood nine seats.

They were not identical. That was the first thing that struck Lior — she had expected symmetry, the cold geometry of power, and instead each seat was its own argument. One was carved from a single trunk of ironwood, its arms worn smooth by generations of hands. One was barely a seat at all, more a woven platform of reeds, because the second house had been river-folk before they were nobility and had refused to forget it. One was a plain wooden chair, unadorned, almost poor, and somehow it commanded the most attention of all. Each held a fragment of the old power — Lior could feel them the way you feel heat radiating off a hearthstone, nine separate warmth-signatures, banked but not dead, waiting.

Eight of them were empty.

The ninth was not.

A man sat in the plain wooden chair, and he had been sitting there long enough that the amber light seemed to have accepted him as part of the room. He was old — not ancient, but old the way a well-used tool is old, all its ornament worn down to function. Grey robes, undyed wool, the same cloth the Wardens wore but without the masks, and his face was tired in a way that no night's sleep could touch. He looked up at them as they came down the last steps, and he did not rise, and he did not reach for any weapon, and somehow both of those things were more frightening than if he had.

"You took the long road," he said. His voice was mild, almost conversational, the voice of a man discussing grain tariffs. "Through the Weeping Pass. I wondered if you would. Your grandmother always took the long road too. She said the short roads taught you nothing about the country you were crossing."

Lior stopped at the edge of the dais. Behind her she heard Wren's breath catch — he had recognized the voice, then, or the face, from some archive of the Regency — and she felt Sefi's small hand slide into hers, gripping hard.

"You know who I am," the Regent said. Not a question.

"I know what you are," Lior answered. "You're the man who burned Emberhold's archive. Who sent masked soldiers to hunt a child. Who unmade a country and called it mercy."

"Yes." He said it simply, without flinching, and that simplicity was worse than any denial. "All of that. And I am also the ninth seat's heir, and I have kept this room swept for sixty years, and I have sat in this chair every solstice so that the fragment in it would not go entirely cold. You may weigh those things however you like. I have weighed them every night for six decades, and I will tell you honestly: the scales never settle."

He rose then — slowly, joints protesting — and gestured at the hall around him with a kind of weary proprietorship.

"Do you know what this room is, Lior Oyelaran? Not what the archives say. What it *is*?"

"A throne room."

"It is a dam." He walked the length of the dais, his shadow sliding across the eight empty seats. "That is all the founding oath ever was. My grandfather — Aldric Vane, ninth speaker, keeper of the granaries — stood where you're standing during the Hunger War, with three kingdoms at our borders and our own houses at each other's throats over the last stores of grain, and he understood that Thornfell was going to die. Not lose. *Die.* Be partitioned, its people scattered, its name used as a banner for someone else's war. So the nine houses met in this hall and they chose the only weapon left to a dying country." He turned to face her fully. "They chose to die first. On their own terms. To erase themselves so thoroughly that the wolves outside would find nothing to fight over. A country no one remembers cannot be conquered. A court that never existed cannot be usurped. The forgetting wasn't cruelty, girl. It was a tourniquet."

"And the people?" Lior's voice came out low and dangerous. "The children born into a country that isn't there? Sefi, singing an anthem no one taught her? My mother, dying without a nation to be buried in?"

"The tourniquet hurts. That is how you know it's working." But something flickered across his face when he looked at Sefi — something that was not cruelty and was very close to grief. "I have watched that child since she was born. Do you imagine the Wardens hunt her because I hate her? I sent men to bring her to me, gently, and they put masks on because masks are kinder than faces when you're taking a child from her bed. She remembers what the rest of the world paid everything to forget. Every year she remembers more loudly. And every year the borders of three kingdoms draw a little closer to this mountain, because memory leaks, Lior. Erasure is not a wall. It is a debt, and the interest compounds, and someday soon the world outside comes to collect — unless someone sits in these seats and holds the dam another century."

"So hold it," Lior said. "If you believe it, sit here and keep sitting."

"I'm seventy-one years old." For the first time, the mildness cracked, and she saw the man underneath: exhausted, alone, carrying a weight he had inherited the way she had inherited hers. "And there is no one after me. The Wardens follow orders; they do not understand the oath. I needed an heir who carried oath-blood. And then—" he laughed, short and bitter, "—then the stars went out, because your caravan broke the border wards, and I understood that the dam was failing whether I willed it or not. The world is coming to collect, Lior. It is already massing at the passes. Wren Vale has seen it. Ask him."

She didn't turn to look at Wren. She didn't need to; she felt him go still behind her, guilt arriving like weather.

"So here is my offer," the Regent said, "and it is the same offer your grandmother refused, and her mother before her, and every Oyelaran for a hundred years, because your line has always been too proud to finish what it started. Take the ninth seat. Take it *with* the forgetting — inherit it, carry it, keep it alive the way your family helped build it. The Court rises again, Thornfell exists again, the stars come home — but the erasure stays, wound into the foundations, and the world stays blind, and the wolves stay hungry and ignorant, and the people live. All of them. Even the ones who grieve. Especially the ones who grieve." He spread his hands. "Or break the oath, and give the world back its map, and let three kingdoms divide the corpse of my country while its children learn the words to an anthem they'll never be safe singing."

The hall was silent. Above them, the painted stars burned on, indifferent and golden.

Lior looked at the nine seats. She looked at the plain wooden chair — the ninth, the granary-keeper's, the one that had never needed ornament because hunger needs no heraldry. She thought about the tourniquet. She thought about Emberhold burning, about the rescued soldiers hunted through the ford by their own comrades, about Adaeze's ghost bound to a vow she had regretted with her final breath and every breath since.

And she understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the Regent was not lying to her. That was the trap. He believed every word. He loved this country exactly as much as she did — loved it enough to let it be forgotten, as she loved it enough to let it burn bright and die loud. They were the same inheritance, she and this tired old man, two heirs of the same sin, standing on opposite banks of the same river.

"You're right," she said.

His eyebrows lifted.

"About all of it. The dam. The wolves. The cost." She stepped forward onto the dais, and beside her — unbidden, unafraid — Sefi stepped too. "My grandmother spent a hundred years regretting that oath, and I have spent this whole road learning why she couldn't take it back. Because taking it back meant admitting the price was wasted. And it wasn't wasted, was it? People lived. Generations of them. You're not a tyrant. You're a man guarding a grave that's also a cradle, and you can't tell anymore which one you're defending."

"Lior," Wren said softly, warningly.

"But you've made one error," she went on, "and it's the same error the nine houses made, and the same error my family made, and the same error every throne in every age makes." She laid her hand on the arm of the ironwood seat — the first seat, the smuggler-roads' seat, warm as a living thing under her palm. "You think the choice is yours alone. You think the country is a thing that gets decided *for* its people, by its speakers, its regents, its oath-bound bloodlines. One man in a chair, holding the dam, alone, for sixty years." She shook her head slowly. "That's not a country. That's a hostage situation that lasted a century."

Sefi began, very quietly, to hum.

The anthem rose through the hall — thin, clear, a child's voice in a cavern built for thousands — and the painted stars overhead seemed to lean toward it. Along the dais, the eight empty seats woke one by one, warmth bleeding up through stone and wood and woven reed, and the Regent's composure finally, fully broke. He stared at the child as if looking at a ghost, and perhaps he was; perhaps every erased voice of Thornfell was in that song.

"That's not possible," he whispered. "The anthem was the first thing taken. I took it myself. I signed the order."

"You can't erase what people carry in their bodies," Sefi said, and did not stop singing. "You should know. You've been carrying it too. I can hear it in you. You hum it when you sweep the floor."

The Regent's hand went to his chest — an involuntary motion, the gesture of a man struck — and for a long moment the only sound in the buried hall was a child's song and an old man's breathing, ragged now, uneven.

Then he straightened, and Lior saw him become what he had always been underneath the tiredness: the last loyal servant of a broken vow, and dangerous precisely because his loyalty was real.

"No," he said. "No. You don't understand what you're waking. The oath isn't a lock I keep, girl — it's a load-bearing wall. Break it and everything it holds comes down at once. The wards, the borders, the peace, the *stars* — you'll shatter the tourniquet and bleed the country dry in front of the wolves, and I will not stand aside and watch you do it. I have stopped Oyelarans before. I can stop one more."

"Grandmother," Lior said quietly, to the air beside her, "he says he's stopped you before."

The temperature of the hall changed. Not colder — *older*. The amber light bent, and in the bending stood a woman in the dress of a hundred years ago, translucent as smoke over water, and the Regent looked at Adaeze Oyelaran's ghost and aged ten years in a single breath.

"You," he breathed.

"Me," said the ghost. "Every solstice, boy, I stood behind you while you swept. You never once looked over your shoulder. That was always your trouble — you guard the past so fiercely you never check whether it's standing behind you." Her gaze moved to Lior, and it was steady, and it was sorry, and it was ready. "Now. Before he gathers himself. The counter-oath, child. Speak it, and I will hold what breaks."

Lior drew a breath that tasted of dust and myrrh and a hundred years of held grief, and reached for the words her grandmother had taught her at the wayside shrine — the words that would unmake the Oyelaran name, and the founding oath, and possibly everything else—

The Regent moved for the ninth seat.
