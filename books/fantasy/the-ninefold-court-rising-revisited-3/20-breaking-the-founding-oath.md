## 20. Breaking the Founding Oath

The Regent reached for the ninth seat the way a drowning man reaches for driftwood — not with triumph but with desperation, his grey robes catching on the shattered stone as he climbed the dais.

"No," Lior said, and it was not a command so much as a refusal to let the moment slip. She had spent her whole life learning that mountains did not move because you asked them to. But she was done asking.

The throne hall beneath the mountain breathed around her. Nine seats of black granite rose in a crescent, each one humming with the fragment of power Wren had catalogued and mourned, each one cold as an unlit hearth. Above them all — above everything — there should have been a ceiling of stone, and beyond the stone a sky, and in the sky stars. There were no stars. There had been no stars for a hundred years, and Lior could feel their absence like a hand pressed flat against her sternum, pushing down.

Sefi stood at her left shoulder, small hands balled into fists, humming under her breath — three notes of the erased anthem, over and over, a child's metronome of courage. Wren stood at her right, clutching the Second Oath against his chest like a shield made of paper, which, Lior supposed, was exactly what it was. Behind them, filling the gallery and the stairwells and every shadowed alcove of the buried hall, stood the remembering: smugglers and soldiers, orphans and archivists, hundreds of them, their breath steaming in the cold, their faces lit by lanterns and by something older than light.

And before the ninth seat, the Regent turned.

He looked older than he had at the ford, older than he had in Emberhold when he'd offered her an inheritance built on forgetting. The tired official was gone. What remained was a man standing at the grave of everything he had loved and defending it with the only weapon left to him: the oath itself.

"You don't understand what you're about to do," he said. His voice echoed strangely in the hall, as though the mountain itself were listening. "You think you're breaking a cage. You are pulling down a dam."

"I know," Lior said. "I've seen what's outside the wards. I know what comes."

"Then—"

"And I know what stays if we do nothing." She stepped forward, onto the first step of the dais. The stone was warm beneath her boot — impossibly warm, blood-warm, as though the hall remembered being alive. "A country no one can name. Children who hum an anthem they've never heard and can't explain why they're crying when they sing it. My grandmother's ghost, bound to a vow she regretted before the ink of it dried on the world." She looked past him, to the empty ninth seat. "You kept the forgetting alive out of love. So did she. Love isn't enough anymore, Regent. It hasn't been for a hundred years."

"It has to be enough." For one naked instant, grief cracked his face open. "It's all I have. It's all *any* of us ever had."

Behind him, Adaeze Oyelaran rose out of the floor like mist rising off a morning river.

Lior's grandmother's ghost had grown thinner since the wayside shrine. Ghosts were memory given shape, and memory needed tending; a century of erasure had starved her down to essentials — the line of her jaw, the iron in her spine, the eyes that had watched Lior grow from a girl scrambling through the passes into a woman who guided caravans through storms. Now those eyes fixed on the Regent with something that was not hatred and not pity but the terrible clarity of someone who had stood exactly where he stood and known, even then, that it would end here.

"Ninth seat," Adaeze said, and her voice filled the hall the way thunder fills a valley. "Your grandsire swore beside me. Your mother renewed it. You have kept the watch faithfully, and I will speak your name with honor in whatever world comes next. But the watch is ended. Stand aside."

The Regent's hand closed on the armrest of the ninth seat.

"I am the heir of the ninth oath-speaker," he said quietly. "The oath lives in my blood same as yours, Lior Oyelaran. If you speak your counter-oath while I hold this seat, the vow won't break. It will *teeter*. Half-kept, half-broken, and a teetering oath is worse than either — ask any archivist." A glance at Wren, bitter and almost pleading. "Ask him. He'll tell you."

Wren's throat worked. "He's right," he admitted. "An oath half-broken doesn't shatter. It *hangs*. And everything caught inside it hangs too." His eyes found Lior's. "She can't break it while he holds the seat. Unless—"

"Unless the seat is empty," Sefi finished.

Every head turned to the child. She wasn't looking at the Regent. She was looking at Adaeze, and her voice, when she went on, had the strange ringing steadiness it took on whenever the old country spoke through her. "Seats hold people who swear. That's how the Court worked — Wren taught me. Nine speakers, nine seats. If the ninth speaker is dead..." She swallowed. "If the ninth speaker is *spent*, then the seat is just stone until someone new swears into it. And nobody new is going to swear the founding oath."

Silence pooled in the hall like water finding its level.

The Regent understood one heartbeat after everyone else. His face went white. "No," he said, turning on Adaeze. "No. You can't. You've held on a hundred years — another hour, another day, you could—"

"A hundred years," Adaeze agreed softly, "is long enough."

* * *

Lior had imagined this moment a dozen times on the road inward: the speaking of the counter-oath, the shattering of the founding vow, the world cracking open like an egg. In her imaginings she had always been triumphant, or terrified, or both. She had never imagined it would feel like this — like standing at her grandmother's deathbed a second time, except this deathbed was a century wide and Lior herself was holding the knife.

"There is a way to make it quick," Adaeze said, drifting closer, until Lior could see the stars that should have been reflected in the ghost's eyes — the old sky, the true sky, preserved in a dead woman's remembering. "The founding oath was sworn by nine voices together. It can only be broken the same way. Eight voices are already willing — the strays, the soldiers, the child. They will lend you their breath. But the ninth voice must come from the line that swore first." Her smile was small and fierce and heartbroken. "From me, Lior. From the first to swear and the first to regret."

"If I take your voice—"

"You spend me. Yes." Adaeze said it the way she'd once said *mind the scree, girl* — briskly, as though it were nothing, as though the ground weren't falling away beneath them both. "Ghosts are only memory. Memory wants, more than anything, to be *used*. A century of being useless, Lior. Do you know what that's been? To remember everything while the world forgot? Let me be useful once. Let me be the last thing the old oath touches, and the first thing the new one stands on."

Lior's hands were shaking. She made fists of them. "Grandmother—"

"*Do not.*" The word cracked like a whip, and then Adaeze softened, and her hand — cold as riverwater, light as ash — cupped Lior's cheek. "Child. You think I don't know what it costs you? I watched your mother bury her grief in work. I watched you bury yours in the passes, in other people's roads, in anywhere but home. We are Oyelaran. We carry things alone because carrying them together would mean admitting they hurt." Her thumb traced Lior's cheekbone. "Break that too. Break it with the rest."

Above them — far above, through a mile of mountain — the darkness where the stars should have been seemed to lean closer.

"The Regent," Wren said urgently. "Lior. He still holds the seat. Once you begin, you can't stop — a half-spoken oath is worse than none, he told you true. But if Adaeze spends herself *into* the ninth seat at the same moment—" He stopped, and Lior watched the thought complete itself behind his eyes, watched the archivist understand the geometry of it. "The seat won't be empty. It'll be *hers*. Sworn to regret instead of forgetting. The counter-oath would have nine voices. All nine."

"It would work," the Regent whispered. He had gone very still, his hand still resting on the granite armrest as though it were the edge of a cliff. "God help me, it would work." He looked at Lior, and there was no fight left in him, only the exhausted wonder of a man who had guarded a prison so long he'd forgotten it was a prison. "My whole life, I thought the choice was between the forgetting and the war. Nobody ever showed me a third door."

"There's always a third door," Sefi said. "That's what the anthem says. The verse nobody remembers." She began to hum it, and this time she didn't stop at three notes. She sang the fourth line, and the fifth, and the remembering behind them picked it up — raggedly at first, then in earnest, hundreds of voices in a buried hall singing a song that officially did not exist, and the stone drank the sound and grew warmer, and warmer—

"Lior." Adaeze stood before the ninth seat now, translucent in the lantern-light, waiting. "Speak. I'll follow. And when I'm gone, grieve fast and grieve loud, and then get up in the morning and guide somebody somewhere. Promise me that much."

"I promise," Lior said, and her voice broke on the second word, and she let it break, because breaking was the whole point, wasn't it? Breaking was the door.

She raised her hands, and the hall went quiet, and she spoke.

* * *

She did not use words anyone had written down. Wren had drafted the Second Oath in careful legal script, clauses and conditions and releases, and it was good — it was *right* — but oaths, Lior understood now, were not law. They were promises made to the dark, and the dark only listened when you meant them.

So she spoke plainly, in the language of the high passes, the language her grandmother had sung her to sleep in.

*"We were promised safety, and it was bought with silence. We were promised peace, and it was bought with forgetting. The bargain was fair once, and it is fair no longer. Let the silence end. Let the wars come if they must — we will meet them awake, with our names in our mouths and our neighbors at our sides. I break the founding oath. I break it clean. And on its broken bones I build this: that Thornfell will be remembered, that her people will be counted, and that no one — no house, no bloodline, no crown — will ever again purchase peace by erasing the people it was meant to protect."*

Her voice rang off the nine seats and came back to her multiplied.

And Adaeze Oyelaran stepped backward into the ninth seat, and smiled at her granddaughter one last time, and gave the dark her hundred years of held-back words:

*"I swore to keep them safe, and I kept them so safe they vanished. I release the oath. I release my line. I release myself — oh, blessed dark, I release myself — and I witness this new vow, and I anchor it with everything I am, everything I was, everything I regret, which is to say: everything."*

She came apart like dawn mist.

Not violently — gently, terribly gently, unraveling upward in threads of silver and myrrh-smell, pouring into the ninth seat until the granite drank her and began, faintly, to glow. Lior felt her go. Everyone felt her go; a hundred years of presence lifting off the world made a silence like the silence after a bell. Sefi was crying without sound. The Regent had sunk to his knees before the seat, his forehead pressed to the stone, whispering something — a name, maybe, or thanks, or simply *finally*.

And the founding oath broke.

There was no thunder. That surprised Lior most of all, afterward, telling it and retelling it in the years when telling was all she had. There was only a sensation like a great held breath finally released — a sigh moving through the mountain, through the valley, through the world, a century of tension unwinding in a single exhalation. The border wards didn't shatter. They *dissolved*, like frost on morning glass, and Lior felt the edges of Thornfell blur back into existence, felt the map redraw itself in invisible ink across a thousand miles of passes and rivers and high green valleys.

Wren made a sound like a sob and a laugh fighting each other. "The records," he choked out. "Every archive in the world — the ink is coming back, I can *feel* it—"

But Lior wasn't listening. She was looking up.

Through the mile of stone above them, through rock and root and the bones of the mountain, something was bleeding through. Points of light, faint as breath on winter glass — one, then three, then a scatter of them, then constellations, wheeling slowly into place overhead as though the sky were a page being read aloud after a century of silence. The stars came back the way memory comes back to a soldier whose erasure has burned away: hesitantly at first, then all at once, then so bright that the lanterns guttered and died and nobody minded, because the true light was better.

Sefi pointed up at the constellation over the ninth seat — the Shepherd, the one that anchored the old calendar, the one every Thornfell child learned first. "It's really her," the girl whispered. "The sky remembers her."

Lior looked. Among the newly returned stars, one burned brighter than the rest, low over the ninth seat's crown, steady and white and unmistakably stubborn.

"She always did refuse to leave before the job was done," Lior said, and laughed, and the laugh tore open into weeping, and she let it, kneeling on the warm stone of the dais with her grandmother's star burning overhead and the anthem rising around her from three hundred throats, grief and triumph in one breath, indistinguishable, inseparable, the same thing at last.

The stars wheeled. The borders rewove. And in the ninth seat, faint and patient and waiting to be filled by whoever came next, a fragment of power pulsed like a heartbeat.

Three hundred voices turned toward it.

Then toward the other eight.
