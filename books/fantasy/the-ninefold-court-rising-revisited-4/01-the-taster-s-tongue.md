## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The poison came, as it always did, with the soup.

Freya Galloway stood at the long tasting table in the under-kitchens of Cinderfall Palace, a silver spoon in his right hand and the evening's first course cooling in its tureen like something dredged from a riverbed. Around him the kitchen roared—sixty cooks, forty scullions, a brigade of spit-boys sweating before a wall of ovens large enough to roast an ox whole—but at the tasting table there was silence, the particular reverent hush that followed him the way his shadow did. They called him *the Tongue* behind his back, though never within earshot of the Master of Ceremonies, who preferred *His Majesty's Humble Servant* and said it as though the words tasted worse than anything Freya was paid to swallow.

He dipped the spoon. He lifted it. He looked—not at the soup but past it, through it, the way a jeweler looks through a stone—and he waited for the small warm flare of awareness that lived behind his breastbone, the sense he had never been able to explain to anyone without lying about it. Most men tasted with their mouths. Freya tasted with all of himself.

Tonight the soup was venison broth, saffron, a whisper of madeira gone slightly too long in the reduction. And underneath, coiled in the fat like a snake in grass—

There.

Not poison, precisely. Not arsenic, not foxglove, not any of the eleven classics every taster learned before he was permitted to stand in this room. This was subtler: a charmwork residue, a binding-solution, the kind of thing a hedge-magician might charge three coppers to brew for a jealous wife. *Loyalty-binder*, the street called it. Drink enough and you'd love whoever fed you until your heart forgot how to do otherwise.

Someone in this kitchen wanted the King gentle tonight.

Freya set down the spoon. His hands did not shake; seven years had burned the shaking out of them. "This one's off," he said, in the flat voice the kitchen knew, the voice that meant a course would go to the hogs and no questions asked, because questions meant the Master of Ceremonies, and nobody wanted that before the second bell.

The under-cook, a vast woman named Odile with forearms like hams and eyes like gimlets, peered into the tureen as if she could see what Freya saw. She couldn't. Nobody could. That was rather the point of him.

"Off how?"

"Just off." He let his mouth curl a fraction—the smile that cost him nothing and bought him everything. "Hogs will be grateful."

Odile grunted and waved the tureen away, and the great machine of the kitchen swallowed the incident and rolled on toward the second bell, and Freya Galloway stood alone at the table for exactly four breaths, letting the flare behind his breastbone gutter back down to embers.

Four breaths. Then he picked up his napkin, wiped the spoon, and went looking for Lark.

* * *

She worked the scullery end, of course—Lark always did, small and quick and perpetually soaked to the elbow, a girl of perhaps fourteen with a burn scar crawling up her left forearm like a vine and a mother at home coughing herself into the ground. Freya had watched her for three weeks. That was rule two: *never be seen*. Rule one was *never cast*. Rule three—

But rules one and two were the ones that mattered tonight.

He found her hauling wash-water at the far sink, where the drainage grate groaned and the lamplight didn't quite reach. He leaned against the stone beside her the way a man leans when he's tired, which he was, genuinely, though not so tired he'd forget why he'd come.

"Lark."

She glanced up, wary. Everyone was wary of the Tongue. It was a strange kind of fame, being the man whose job was to die first. "Master Galloway."

"That cough your mother has." He kept his voice low, pitched beneath the clatter of pots. "The wet kind. Worse at night. Blood sometimes, in winter."

Her wariness sharpened into something rawer. "How'd you—"

"I taste things," Freya said. "Everything. It gets everywhere after a while." He let that sit. Then his hand moved—a small motion, nothing, the sort of fidget a tired man makes against a countertop—and a folded square of grey cloth passed from his sleeve into hers, weightless as a moth. "For the washing water. Steep it. Don't tell anyone where it came from, because I'll deny it, and I'm very good at denying things."

Lark stared at the cloth in her palm as though it might bite. "What is it?"

"Cheap," he said. "That's what it is. It's also a lung-ease, brewed proper, none of your alchemist's chalk-and-water. Three nights and she'll sleep through. A month and the blood stops." He straightened. "It won't cure her. Nothing cures the wet lung. But it buys time, and time is the only coin poor people get to spend."

"You're a magician." The word came out half-whispered, half-accusation, and Freya felt the old familiar cold slide down his spine, because that word, spoken aloud, in this building, was worth a cell in the Undercroft or a rope on Warden's Hill depending on the mood of the magistrate.

"I'm a food-taster," he said mildly. "I count calories and check for arsenic. If I were a magician, girl, do you think I'd be standing in your dishwater?" He pushed off the counter. "Three nights. Steep it dark. And Lark—" he paused, and made himself say it, because saying it was part of the price, "—if anyone ever asks, you found it in a gutter and you're a fool for taking gutter-charms. Swear it."

"I swear." She was clutching the cloth now like it was made of gold leaf, and her eyes had gone bright in a way that made something in Freya's chest ache dully, an old ache, a familiar tenant. "Why? Why would you—"

"Because once," Freya said, "someone gave me something in a gutter, and I've been paying it back ever since, and I'm nowhere near done." He tipped two fingers to his brow—a taster's salute, mocking and sincere at once—and walked back into the light before she could ask anything else, because asking was dangerous, and answering was worse.

Rule three, he reminded himself, was *never hope*. He'd added it later. Rules one and two kept the body alive. Rule three kept the rest of him from following Lark home to her coughing mother and doing something stupid and warm and fatal, like caring where it showed.

* * *

At midnight the last course was cleared, the King had eaten and lived, and the kitchen exhaled. Freya took his pay-chit from the Master of Ceremonies' clerk—a slip of stamped lead, good only at the palace comptoir, because the Crown trusted its servants with nothing so liquid as coin—and climbed the narrow stair to the room above the coal-store that the household granted him in lieu of wages it would have had to admit paying.

It was a monk's cell, six feet by nine, with a cot, a stool, a candle-end, and a floorboard he'd loosened himself in his first week. He lifted the board. Beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth against the coal-damp, lay his life's accounting: eleven silver marks and four coppers, saved across seven years at the rate of whatever survived the bribes, the ingredients, the quiet purchases from the night-market under Tanner's Bridge. Eleven marks. A decent horse cost thirty. A cottage in the hill-country, somewhere green and far from Cinderfall's smoke, cost two hundred at least, and that was before you counted the price of a new name, which was steep, because names had to be bought from people who sold them well.

Seven years of tasting poison for a court that would hang him if it knew what he was, and his entire fortune wouldn't buy him out of sight of its walls.

Freya sat on the cot and counted it anyway. He counted it every night. Not because the number changed—it almost never changed—but because counting was a ritual, and rituals were the only magic the law allowed him. Coin by coin, mark by mark, he laid the future out on the oilcloth and looked at it honestly, the way he looked at soup: *what's in you? What are you hiding? Will you kill me?*

Eleven marks and four coppers. A plan that needed two hundred. A gap between them wide enough to bury a man in, and men got buried in it every year—tasters died young, everyone knew that, of accidents and bad shellfish and the occasional genuinely clever poisoner, and the Crown simply hired the next orphan with a steady hand.

He should leave, he thought, as he thought every night. Take the eleven marks, take the charms sewn into his coat lining—three lung-eases, a fever-break, a sleep-draught, and one thing he had never used and told himself he never would, a little woven knot of intent that could, briefly, turn a lock's opinion of itself around—take all of it and walk north through the Wardgate before dawn and never taste another man's supper as long as he lived.

He should. Every night he thought it, and every morning he rose and washed his face in the basin and went down to the kitchens, because—

Because of rule three. Because hoping was the one sin he couldn't stop committing, no matter how many times he executed it. Because somewhere under the weariness there was still a boy of twelve who had sold his first spell—a luck-knot, three coppers—to a carter with a lame horse, and had walked home through the rain feeling ten feet tall, feeling *chosen*, feeling like the world had finally handed him the one thing orphans weren't supposed to have: a use. The boy had believed the talent meant something. The man knew better. The man knew the talent was just a tool for surviving, the way a rat's teeth were a tool for surviving, and nobody wrote songs about rats.

And yet.

And yet Lark's face tonight, going bright over a scrap of grey cloth. Eleven marks and four coppers, and every copper had a story attached that wasn't about survival at all. The widow on Rope Lane whose fever had broken. The stable-boy whose nightmares the sleep-draught had eaten. Small sins, the hedge-witch who raised him had called them—she'd meant it fondly, mostly—and they piled up inside him like coins in the oilcloth, worthless by any honest measure, and he could not stop collecting them.

Freya blew out the candle and lay back on the cot and listened to the palace breathe above him: the settling of stones, the distant footfall of the night watch, the enormous sleeping weight of a building full of people who would burn him alive if they knew him. Seven years he'd been invisible here. Seven years of swallowing danger and keeping his head down and telling himself the story that got him through: *one more year, one more mark, one more rung.*

He was nearly asleep when the first wrongness touched him.

It came the way wrongness always came to Freya—not through the ears but through that place behind his breastbone, the deep-taste, the sense that never slept. A tremor, far off and far *down*, the flavor of a vast thing shifting in its sleep. Like the moment before an earthquake, when dogs begin to howl and the wine in a cup shivers into rings.

He sat up in the dark. His skin prickled. Along his forearms, beneath the sleeves, the faint woven lines of his hidden charms—dormant for months, obedient, *quiet*—began to glow.

A soft blue-white, thread by thread, like veins filling with light.

Freya stared at his own arms in the blackness of his cell and felt, for the first time in seven years, entirely naked.

"What," he whispered to the empty room, "is happening?"

Above him, through the thick palace stone, faint as memory, came a sound like glass breaking somewhere impossibly far away—and every star outside his high slit of a window went out at once.
