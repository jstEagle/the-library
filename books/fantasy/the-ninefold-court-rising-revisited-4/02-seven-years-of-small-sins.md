## 2. Seven Years of Small Sins

The coin went under the loose hearthstone the way all his coins did: with a small apology.

Freya Galloway knelt in the dark of his rented room above the tallow-chandler's shop and counted what the stone had been hiding. Fourteen silver bits. Three coppers worn smooth as river pebbles. A half-mark with a clipped edge that no moneylender would take but a charm-seller might. Seven years of small sins, stacked in a hole in the floor of a room that wasn't his, in a city that would hang him if it knew one word of what he was.

He counted twice, because counting was a kind of prayer, and because he had promised himself long ago that he would always know exactly how much freedom cost.

*Enough,* he thought, though it never quite was. *Almost enough.*

He set the stone back down, swept the dust across the seam with the side of his palm until the hearth looked innocent again, and lay on his narrow bed to let tomorrow arrive. Sleep came slowly. It always came slowly on tasting days, when his tongue still carried the ghost-memory of everything he'd put in his mouth for the crown — the bitter almond shadow beneath the spiced pears that had meant nothing, or had meant everything, depending on how the night turned out.

He lay there listening to the chandler's dog shift in its kennel below, and — because he could not help himself, because he never could help himself — he remembered.

* * *

He was nine years old, and the orphanage at Cinderfall's edge smelled like boiled turnips and wet wool, and he was hungry in the way that hollows you out rather than sharpens you. The matron called him *the quiet one*. There were forty children and she had stopped learning names after twenty, so *the quiet one* was as close to a self as anyone had bothered to give him.

Except her. Except once.

She came in autumn, the hedge-witch, wrapped in a coat that had been dyed too many colors and now resembled a storm about to happen. She bought nothing from the orphanage and sold nothing either. She only stood at the gate while the matron shouted at her — *devil's work, devil's woman, get off consecrated ground* — and over the matron's shoulder she looked straight at the small boy hauling water, and winked.

Freya dropped the bucket. It seemed important, later, that he remember this: that before anything else, before any spell or lesson or bargain, she had seen him, and he had made a fool of himself about it.

Her name was Odile. She lived in a leaning hut past the tannery ditches where the city gave up pretending to be respectable. He found it by following the smell of woodsmoke that didn't behave like ordinary smoke — it curled against the wind, which should have frightened him and instead felt like an invitation written in a language he hadn't learned yet.

"You're the quiet one," she said, opening the door before he knocked.

"Yes."

"What do you want?"

"Nothing," he said, which was a lie so old it fit him like skin.

Odile looked at him for a long moment. Then she stepped aside. "Come in, Nothing. Mind the cat. He bites liars."

The cat bit him that first day. And the second. By winter they had reached an understanding, mostly.

She taught him the way water teaches stone — slowly, patiently, without any sign of effort. Not spells, not at first. She taught him that magic was mostly attention. "Any fool can shout a fireball," she said, feeding nettle tea to a sick goat. "The craft is in noticing. What's wrong with this goat? Look. *Look.* Don't tell me what you see. Tell me what you almost missed."

What Freya almost missed, that day and every day, was everything. He had spent nine years learning not to be noticed, and it turned out that not-being-noticed was the opposite of seeing. So he unlearned. He learned to read the flicker of a candle flame as weather-report. He learned that iron left a cold taste in the air near it, and that grief pooled in corners like spilled milk, and that a lie had weight if you held your hand flat above the speaker's words — not much weight, a moth's weight, but there.

The first true charm she taught him was a warming knot. Three loops of red thread, tied in a particular order, spoken over with four words in a language older than the city's charter. It kept a beggar's hands from freezing. That was all. It was nothing. It was the most beautiful thing he had ever done.

"Why teach me?" he asked her once, twelve years old and suspicious the way orphans are suspicious, which is thoroughly and forever. "You don't get paid. The matron says you'll burn."

"The matron says a great deal for someone who sells orphan labor to the wool-merchants." Odile trimmed the goat's hoof, unbothered. "I teach you because you asked without asking. Because you look at things. And because—" she paused, and for once the pause wasn't performance— "because somebody taught me, when I was nothing too. Debt travels forward, boy. That's the whole of the ethics."

*Debt travels forward.* He would carry those three words through fire later. But he didn't know that yet.

At twelve, he sold his first spell. A fishwife wanted luck for her husband's nets; she had two coppers and a bad attitude. Freya wove a finding-charm into a scrap of blue cloth — nothing more than Odile's warming knot bent toward lost things, clumsy, overcharged — and took the coins with hands that shook.

It worked. Of course it worked; that was the terrible part. The husband came home heavy with herring and the fishwife told everyone, and everyone included people who shouldn't have known, and within a season there was a whisper through Cinderfall's poor quarters: *there's a boy who does charms, cheap, discreet, the quiet one.*

Odile boxed his ears when she found out. Actually boxed them, hard enough to ring.

"You sold *craft* for *coins*?" she said. "Do you know what happens to hedge-witches who become known? I'll tell you. The ward-keepers come. They read you with their little brass instruments, they write your name in their little gray ledgers, and then one morning there's a bonfire in Charter Square and the city feels *safer*, and your students spend the rest of their lives burying what you gave them." She gripped his shoulders. Her eyes were the color of pond ice. "Never cast, boy. Never be seen. Never hope. Say it."

"Never cast. Never be seen. Never hope."

"Again."

"Never cast. Never be seen. Never hope."

"And mean it," she said, "or the rules are just rope to hang yourself prettier."

He meant it. He meant it for seven years, in the way that a man means a vow he is breaking daily in small, survivable pieces.

* * *

Present-day, Freya turned over in his bed and pressed his face into the pillow that smelled of tallow and other people's cooking, and let the memory keep coming, because it was nearly dawn and dawn was when he allowed himself this.

* * *

He was fifteen when he understood that Odile was teaching him toward something specific, and sixteen when she finally said it aloud.

They were mending charms on the roof of her hut — replacing the salt line that kept the rain from remembering to fall indoors — when she sat back on her heels and studied him the way she studied goats: thoroughly, without sentiment.

"Do you know why the court beneath the mountain hates us?" she asked.

"The priests say it hates magic."

"The priests say whatever the charter pays them to say." She handed him the salt. "The court doesn't hate magic, boy. Magic is its native tongue. It hates *unlicensed* magic. It hates anything it hasn't priced. Everything under that mountain is owned, catalogued, owed. A free spell is the only truly free thing left in this world, and free things offend owners." She tapped the roof beam between them. "And we live above an owner, whether we acknowledge our rent or not."

"What does it own?" He'd asked it lightly. He was sixteen; lightness was his armor.

"That depends," said Odile, "on what your parents sold."

He laughed. Then he saw her face, and stopped laughing, and the silence between them grew teeth.

"I'm an orphan," he said. "Nobody sold me. Nobody wanted me enough to sell."

"Mm," said Odile, and said nothing more, which from her was a shout.

He thought about that conversation for two years. He took the taster's post at seventeen, and told himself it was practical — steady wage, palace bread, access to the kitchens where charm-components moved through like rumors. But underneath the practicality, honest as a scar, was the thing Odile had planted: *what did they sell?*

A food-taster's value was his tongue, and his tongue's value was his death, deferred. Every poison he survived, every toxin his body learned to shrug off, made him worth more and killed him slower. The palace physician said he was a marvel. The captain of the guard said he was lucky. Seven years in, Freya had drunk enough slow arsenic, distilled ergot, and powdered wyrm-scale to kill a plow-horse, and his body processed it all like well-water.

Lucky. Yes. Like being born rich is lucky.

Because here was the truth he kept in the same locked room as the hearth-coins: the immunity hadn't come gradually. It had arrived whole, in his first month of service, the way a debt arrives — *interest on principal*, some cold clerkish part of his mind whispered whenever he swallowed something lethal and lived. As if somewhere a ledger already held his name, and someone below the mountain was very punctual about protecting their investment.

He never said this aloud. He barely said it to himself. But he built his life around the shape of it, the way a tree builds around a fence wire.

So: the rules. Never cast — except small knots, warming threads, a finding-spell palmed to a scullery maid whose sweetheart was lost on the border roads. Never be seen — except his charms had stayed invisible so long he'd begun to believe they were. Never hope — except fourteen silver bits and three smooth coppers buried under a hearthstone, accumulating toward something, some door, some elsewhere, some life where Freya Galloway was nobody's asset and nobody's interest payment.

Small sins. Small savings. Small hopes, kept small on purpose, the way you keep a fire small so the smoke won't show.

* * *

On the morning the stars went out, he woke before the bell, as tasting-days required, and performed his ablutions in the yard basin where the water had a skin of ice on it. He dressed in the palace gray that made servants furniture. He ate a heel of bread standing up, tasted nothing wrong in it, and felt the small daily grief of that — food that was only food, safety that was only safety, a tongue with nothing to say.

He walked to the palace through streets still blue with night. The chandler's dog barked once at him, then thought better of it. Above the rooftops, the last stars were going out one by one, giving way to dawn.

Or so he assumed they were giving way to dawn.

Later, he would understand that he had walked to work on the last ordinary morning of his life, carrying fourteen silver bits' worth of hope in a hole in his floor and a sister-shaped absence in his chest that he had spent nineteen years refusing to look at directly. Later, he would understand that Odile's third rule had been breaking down for months, grain by grain, the way all his rules broke — not with defiance but with longing.

But that morning, he only climbed the kitchen stair, tied on his apron, and took up his spoon.

The head cook thrust the first dish at him — honeyed quince, for the regent's table — and said, "Well, Galloway? Do we live?"

Freya tasted. Quince, honey, a breath of clove, and beneath it—

Beneath it, something vast shifted underground, like a mountain rolling over in its sleep, and every star in the sky went out at once, and the wards along Cinderfall's borders fell with a sound like a wineglass the size of heaven being struck by a hammer.

The spoon rang against the floor. Somewhere in his chest, seven years of carefully buried charms flared alight, visible through his shirt like embers through ash — glowing, unmistakable, public — for the first time in his careful, criminal, hidden life.

"Oh," Freya said, to no one, to the dark, to whatever ledger had his name in it. "*Oh no.*"
