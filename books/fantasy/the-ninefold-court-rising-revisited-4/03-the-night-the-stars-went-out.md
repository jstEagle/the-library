## 3. The Night the Stars Went Out

The soup arrived at the ninth hour of the evening watch, as it always did on Thursdays, carried up from the under-kitchens in a lidded silver tureen by a boy whose hands shook too much to be trusted with anything sharper than a spoon.

Freya Galloway stood at his station beside the regent's table and watched the tureen the way other men watched card sharps.

It was barley soup tonight — he could smell it from six feet away, mutton fat and leeks and something sweeter underneath that didn't belong. He let the boy set it down, let the under-steward lift the lid with the ceremonial flourish that everyone had stopped finding impressive twenty years ago, and stepped forward with his tasting spoon already warm in his palm.

The sweetness was honey. That was the first thing wrong. Barley soup took no honey; honey was for the second course, the glazed quail, which would not arrive for another half hour. The second thing wrong was where it sat in the steam — not distributed through the broth but pooled low, near the bottom of the tureen, which meant it had been added after cooking, added cold, added *late*.

Someone had dosed the pot at the pass.

Freya dipped his spoon, brought it to his lips, and tasted death the way an old friend knocks: familiar, almost polite. Bitterroot, cut with honey to hide the bite, and beneath both a mineral tang he couldn't name — something new, something someone had paid good coin for. Not kitchen poison. This came from an apothecary with ambitions.

He did not spit. Spitting was theater, and theater drew eyes. Instead he swallowed, set down his spoon with a small precise click, and said, in the bored voice seven years of practice had honed to perfection:

"This one's been touched. I'd send it back."

The under-steward's face went through three colors in the space of a breath. The regent's chamberlain was already shouting for the kitchens to be sealed, and Freya stepped back into the shadow of the serving alcove and let the machinery of panic run without him, because that was the third rule, the rule above all rules:

*Never be seen.*

He had rules the way other men had religion. Never cast what could be bought. Never be seen doing either. Never hope — hope was the poison no immunity could touch. Seven years he had served the palace of Cinderfall as its royal food-taster, seven years of swallowing small deaths until his blood had grown a coat of armor around them, and in all that time he had broken his rules exactly often enough to stay alive: rarely, carefully, and only when someone desperate enough was willing to pay.

Tonight there was such a someone. He felt her before he saw her — the scullery maid Tansy, sixteen and hollow-eyed, working the far end of the alcove with a rag she hadn't actually moved in a minute. Her brother owed money to people who collected in fingers. She had come to Freya nine days ago with the look of a girl deciding which sin she could live inside, and he had told her to wait, because charmwork sold cheap and cost dear, and he never sold to anyone who hadn't yet learned what it cost.

Nine days was long enough. He palmed the charm from the seam of his sleeve — a knucklebone wrapped in red thread, worked over three nights with hedge-magic so old it predated the city's bans — and let it fall into the fold of her apron as he passed, a touch so light it might have been accident, might have been nothing at all.

"For your brother," he murmured. "Carry it on the left side. Don't tell him where it came from."

Tansy's breath caught. Her hand closed over the little weight of it, and for one moment she looked at him with an expression he recognized from mirrors — gratitude and grief sharing a face — and then the chamberlain's voice cracked across the hall like a whip, and she vanished into the noise, and Freya returned to being furniture.

That should have been the whole of the night. A poisoned soup caught, a poisoner to be found, a charm delivered, a few coins counted into a tin behind a loose brick in his room above the stables. Ordinary. Survivable.

Then the stars went out.

* * *

Freya noticed it the way one notices silence — absence arriving before understanding. The great hall's high windows faced east, and beyond their leaded panes the night had been thick with stars, the kind of cold autumn clarity that made even palace-dwellers pause and look up. He had glanced at them a hundred times during the tasting, out of habit more than wonder. Orphans learned early that the sky was free entertainment.

So when he looked up between courses and found black glass where the sky should be — not clouded, not dimmed, but *empty*, every star gone as if snuffed by a vast wet thumb — his body knew before his mind did. His skin prickled. His hidden charms, sewn into hems and bootlinings and the lining of his jaw where no search would think to look, woke like cats scenting smoke.

Around him, the hall noticed too. Conversation died in patches, like candles going out one by one. A server dropped a tray of wine glasses and nobody scolded her. The regent's chancellor rose from his seat and stood with his mouth open like a landed fish.

And then the wards fell.

Later, scholars and survivors would argue about how to describe the sound. Some said thunder. Some said a bell the size of the world. But Freya heard it plain, and what he heard was glass — all the glass in creation breaking at once, a high crystalline shriek that came not through the ears but through the bones, through the teeth, through the water standing in cups on the tables, which shivered into rings though no hand had touched them. The border wards of Cinderfall. The invisible lattice that had stood since the founding, that every child learned about in school as casually as they learned the city's name. Falling. All of it, all at once.

Every light in the hall guttered. Every flame leaned east, toward the mountains, as if bowing.

And Freya's charms flared.

He felt it happen and could do nothing — *nothing* — to stop it. The knucklebone in Tansy's apron lit up like an ember. The thread-charms along his own seams kindled, soft gold light bleeding through wool and linen, tracing his sleeves, his collar, the seams of his doublet, until he stood in the middle of the panicked hall outlined in gentle fire like a saint in a window, or a criminal in a confession.

Seven years of never being seen, undone in a single breath.

A woman screamed. It was not about the sky. It was about *him*. "Marked — the taster's marked — look at his *hands*—"

Freya looked down. His palms glowed faintly through the skin, the old scar-tissue of swallowed poisons lighting up like veins of ore, and beneath the glow he could feel something else, something he had never once felt in seven years of careful, buried, deniable life: the *attention* of magic, vast and slow and deep below, turning toward him like a whale turning in dark water.

The hall erupted. Guards poured in from the corridor doors, half-drawn swords, faces tilted upward toward a sky with nothing in it. The chancellor was shouting orders that contradicted each other. Somewhere a dog was barking itself hoarse. The regent herself appeared at the head of the stairs, gray-faced, gripping the rail, staring first at the empty heavens and then — Freya watched it happen — at him, at the softly burning outline of her food-taster, and her expression was one he would carry to his grave: not fear, not anger, but the terrible blankness of a woman revising everything she believed about her own household.

"Seal the gates," she said. Her voice was very quiet and somehow carried to every corner. "Seal the gates and bring me Captain Vane."

Freya did the arithmetic of escape in about a second and a half. Three exits. Two guarded, one blocked by falling masonry from the tremor that followed the ward-fall — a deep groan through the palace stones, as if the mountain itself had cleared its throat. Even if he reached a door, the streets beyond would be chaos, checkpoints by morning, and a man glowing like a lantern could hide in exactly nowhere.

So he stood still. He folded his hands. He let the guards take his arms, and he kept his face arranged into the mild, stupid honesty that had served him through a thousand tastings, while inside him two feelings warred for ownership of his chest.

The first was terror, clean and animal, rising up his throat like bile. They burned charmworkers in Cinderfall. Slowly, publicly, on Warden's Green, and the crowds brought their children, because it was educational. Seven years he had built a fortress of ordinariness around himself, brick by invisible brick, and now every brick was lit up like festival glass and the whole city could read him at a glance.

The second feeling was worse. The second feeling was exhilaration.

Because the fire in his seams didn't hurt. It *sang*. For the first time in his adult life the magic in him was awake and unhidden and answering something — answering whatever had woken beneath the mountain, whatever had eaten the stars, whatever had just torn down the oldest wall in the world — and some buried orphan-boy part of him, the part that had loved the hedge-witch's kitchen and the smell of drying herbs and the first impossible warmth of a spell held cupped in small hands, that part was *rejoicing*.

*There you are,* said the fire in his blood. *We waited so long.*

"Hold him," said the guard-captain, though Freya hadn't moved. "Don't let him near anyone. And somebody get those lights off him."

"You can't," Freya said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "They're not lamps. They'll burn till they burn out."

"And how would you know, taster?"

Freya looked at the man — young, frightened, gripping his sword like a rosary — and gave him the truth, because tonight was apparently the night for it:

"Because I've been carrying them for seven years. Tonight's just the first time they've answered back."

Above them, through the high windows, the starless dark pressed down like a lid. Below them, through the flagstones, through the mountain's roots, Freya felt that slow vast attention still turned his way, patient as debt.

In his sleeve, against his wrist, the last unlit charm — a scrap of hedge-work he'd carried since he was twelve and had never had the heart to use — began, very softly, to glow.

It was shaped like a summons. And something, somewhere far below, was pulling the thread.
