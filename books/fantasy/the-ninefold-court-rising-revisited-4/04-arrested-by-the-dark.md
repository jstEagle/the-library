## 4. Arrested by the Dark

Captain Ilsa Vane had been awake for nineteen hours when the stars went out, and she would remember afterward that the last thing she felt before the dark was irritation—at a requisition form, of all things, ink smudged where some clerk's heavy thumb had dragged across the column for wardstone reserves. She had been sitting in her office above the Third Wardhouse with the lamp turned low, because the lamp hurt her eyes these days, and because there was a particular kind of honesty that came only in dim rooms. Numbers didn't lie in dim rooms. They simply sat there on the page, patient as corpses, telling you what everyone preferred not to know.

The reserves were short. The wards along Cinderfall's border had been drawing harder every month for three years—she had the consumption ledgers to prove it—and no one above her rank wanted to hear why. *Wardstone fatigue,* the engineers called it, writing it down in their neat hands as though naming a thing explained it. Ilsa had walked the border line herself, twice in the last year, and had pressed her palm to the standing stones and felt what the ledgers couldn't say: they weren't tired. They were *afraid.* Wardstone was dead rock taught to hold a shape of refusal, and hers had begun, in the small hours before dawn, to shiver.

Then the sky over Cinderfall had gone blank.

She had been looking out her window when it happened—or rather, she had been looking at the reflection of the window in the dark glass of her cabinet, which is where she did most of her looking, because a woman who spent twenty years watching for things coming out of the dark learned to watch reflections too. The stars vanished all at once, not fading but *cut*, as if a hand had passed between the world and its lights. And beneath her feet, through the floor, through the foundation stones, through the old bones of the city itself, came a sound like breaking glass—but vast, so vast it wasn't a sound so much as a pressure, a single long note of something enormous ending.

Every ward-keeper in the city felt it. She knew this because within minutes the bells began, and because her own instruments—the brass and bone array on her desk, calibrated by her own hand—had all swung hard left and gone still, like birds dropping off a wire.

Ilsa Vane stood in her office holding a requisition form, in a city whose sky had been emptied, and discovered that twenty years of discipline amounted to this: she counted to ten, put on her coat, and went to work.

* * *

By the second hour of the dark, Cinderfall had done what Cinderfall always did with catastrophe: it had made it into noise. Bells clanging from the citadel, dogs howling in the Warrens, drunks singing in the streets because if the world was ending there was no reason left to be sober. The palace had lit every lamp it owned, and from the Third Wardhouse roof Ilsa could see the whole terraced sprawl of it burning yellow against the starless black—a city trying to pretend, with candlelight, that nothing fundamental had changed.

Her investigators were assembled in the wardroom below: six of them, grey-cloaked, carrying the portable instrument cases that marked them as Ward Authority rather than city watch. Ordinary constables handled cutpurses and wife-beaters and the small brutal arithmetic of street crime. The Ward Authority handled the other category—the crimes that shouldn't be possible, the smuggling of charms, the trafficking in bound spirits, the slow illegal commerce between Cinderfall and whatever lived under the mountain that polite society had agreed, two centuries ago, to stop discussing.

"Three names," Ilsa said. She stood at the head of the table with the night's first reports spread before her, and her voice did what it always did in briefing, which was go flat and precise, each word set down like a stone in a wall. "The wards didn't fail. They were *fed upon*. Something drew the stored power out of the border line faster than it could replenish, and the resonance signature matches documented court-work." Nobody flinched at the word *court*. That was one thing about her people; they might be frightened, but they were professionals first. "Which means someone in this city has been running traffic to the Ninefold Court recently enough and heavily enough to leave a channel open. We find the channel, we find the channel-makers. Three names."

She laid them down. A hedge-charmer from the Saltmarket who had been flagged twice for unlicensed work. A midwife in the Tanner's Row district whose name appeared in an old seizure ledger for possessing a soul-lure. And the third—

"The food-taster," said Investigator Corren, frowning at the sheet. "Freya Galloway? Captain, he's palace staff. Taster's Tongue, seven years' service, commendations. What's he got to do with wards?"

"He has charms on him," Ilsa said. "Working ones. My roof-sensor caught the flare when the wards fell—every hidden charm in the city lit up for four seconds like festival lanterns, and his were the brightest signature inside the palace walls. Brighter than hedge-witch work. Brighter than anything licensed." She let that sit. "A man can be guilty and useful, Corren. Both at once. It's the most common condition there is."

The truth—which she gave no one, because it wasn't theirs yet—was more complicated. The flare had shown her hundreds of charm-signatures across the city, little warm embers of illegality winking out again as their owners smothered them. But Galloway's hadn't winked out. His had *settled*, the way a fire settles into coals, deliberate and banked and ready. Seven years of commendations, she thought, and a man who kept working charms woven so deep into his clothing they read like skin. That wasn't a dabbler. That was someone who had decided, long ago, exactly how much lawbreaking he could carry and had carried it flawlessly ever since.

She almost respected it. That was the trouble with this job; it kept showing you the exact weight of a person.

"Bring all three," she said. "Gently. If half the city saw what my sensors saw, then half the city knows the taster lit up like a beacon tonight. I want him behind a door before a mob decides what he is."

* * *

They brought the midwife first, weeping and denying everything in the same breath, her instrument readings muddy and human—fear-spikes, guilt-echoes, nothing structured. Then the Saltmarket charmer, an old man who came in with the dignity of someone who had expected this arrest for thirty years and had rehearsed his silence accordingly. His readings showed craft, real craft, faded and careful.

Then they brought Freya Galloway, and Ilsa understood within ten seconds that she had misjudged the room she'd need.

He was not frightened. That was the first thing. Everyone was frightened tonight—the whole city wore fear like ash wears a burnt house—but the taster came into the interrogation room with the unhurried gait of a man arriving at his own workplace, looked at the instruments on the table with mild professional interest, and said, "Should I sit on that side or yours? The chair on your side has a loose leg. I noticed when they brought me past."

"Yours is fine," Ilsa said.

"I assumed it would be. You strike me as someone who checks the furniture." He sat. He was a narrow man, ordinary-looking in the way that servants learn to be ordinary on purpose, brown-haired, somewhere past thirty, with the pale, faintly bruised complexion of someone who worked nights and ate carefully. His hands rested on the table—clean hands, cook's scars on the forearms visible below the cuffs—and they did not move at all.

"Freya Galloway," she said. "Food-taster, Second Kitchen, palace service, seven years."

"You have my file in front of you, Captain, so I'll spare us both the recitation."

"Your file says you're clean. No record, no flags, no associations of interest."

"My file is accurate. Files usually are, if nobody important is lying."

"And yet." She opened the instrument case. Her array was older than most—brass housing, bone resonators, a soul-glass lens ground by her own predecessor—and she trusted it the way she trusted few living things. "When the wards fell tonight, every latent charm in Cinderfall flared for roughly four seconds. Yours didn't just flare, Mr. Galloway. Your signature was the strongest reading inside the palace grounds. Would you like to explain that?"

"Would you believe kitchen magic?" He said it lightly, but his eyes stayed on her, level and dark and patient. "Charms to keep the milk sweet. A warming cantrip on the plating shelf. Cook swears by me."

"I'd believe it if your readings matched it. Milk-charms are shallow work. Shallow signatures decay fast." She set her hand on the array's activation stud. "This will take a moment, and it may feel strange. Most people describe it as being looked at."

"I'm used to being looked at," Galloway said. "It's most of my job."

She activated the array.

The resonators began their low hum, and the soul-glass lens filled with soft grey light, and Ilsa watched the needle-traces begin to draw themselves across the smoked paper drum: charm-signatures first, and yes, there they were, dozens of them, layered through the man like veins in marble—tiny, exquisite, interlocking pieces of illegal workmanship, each one smaller and subtler than the last, a cathedral built out of things no bigger than sins. Seven years of them. Hundreds. The work of someone who had practiced a forbidden art every single day in the most watched building in the city and had never once been caught.

And beneath the charm-signatures, where the lens should have shown the warm bright core of a living soul—

Ilsa stopped breathing.

The paper drum turned. The traces drew themselves. And the place where Freya Galloway's soul should have registered showed *nothing*. Not dimness. Not damage. Not the grey smudge of a drained victim or the ragged hole of a spirit-burn. Nothing, a clean absence, as though the lens were pointed through an empty chair at an empty wall—as though the man sitting across from her were a body walking around with the interior already removed.

She had seen this once before. Once, eleven years ago, as a junior investigator on a case that had ended her faith in tidy endings: a foundling child pulled from a drain culvert near the mountain road, half-starved, mute, brought in because the watch couldn't find any record of her. Ilsa had run the standard intake scan. The lens had shown her that same clean nothing, and her instructor—an old man named Dossel, since dead—had taken one look at the trace and gone white, and had burned the paper in the stove, and told her: *That mark means the Court has already had them. Whatever's sitting in front of you is what's left after. There's no procedure for it. Lose the paperwork.*

She had lost the paperwork. She had thought about that child perhaps twice a year ever since, always at night, always with the same question she had never let herself finish asking: *left after what?*

And here it was again, drawing itself in fine grey absence across the drum, inside the most trusted servant of the royal household.

"Captain?" Galloway's voice was quiet. "You've gone very still."

"Be silent, please." Her own voice came out lower than she intended. She checked the calibration. Checked the lens seating. Ran the trace a second time, and the second trace matched the first, absence answering absence, and beside her Investigator Corren shifted uncomfortably and said, "Captain, is the instrument—" and she said, "The instrument is fine," and heard her own certainty crack down the middle like river ice.

Because here was the arithmetic she could not make come out even. The court took children—Ilsa knew this the way ward-keepers know things, in the ledger-sense, in the rumor-sense, in the sense of old files sealed by men now dead. The court-marked were hollowed, emptied, *spent*. They were not functional. The child in the culvert had barely been a person anymore, just breath and reflex wearing a face. And yet Freya Galloway sat across from her fully alive, fully present, sharp-eyed and sardonic and *competent*, running charmwork of a sophistication her instruments could barely resolve, and underneath it all, where his soul should burn: nothing at all. A man-shaped room with the fire gone out of it, and the room still furnished, still swept, still occupied.

Either her instruments were wrong—and they were not wrong; she had calibrated them against grief itself and they had never lied—or the Ninefold Court had found a way to take a soul and leave the person working. Leave the person *serving*. Leave the person tasting the king's food, year after year, commendation after commendation, closer to the crown than any spy the Ward Authority had ever planted or caught.

Or—her mind offered the third possibility quietly, the way unpleasant thoughts introduce themselves—either possibility was wrong, and something else entirely was true, something with no file and no procedure, something Dossel would have burned the paperwork for.

"Mr. Galloway." She folded her hands on the table so they wouldn't do anything else. "Where were you born?"

He raised his eyebrows slightly. "Is that on the charge sheet?"

"Answer the question."

"Gallow's Foundling Home, Saltmarket parish. As far as anyone knows. I was five when the records start." A pause, fractional, controlled. "Why does a ward investigation care where I was born?"

Five. The number landed in her chest like a dropped coin. The culvert child had been five, or near it. The old sealed files—all of them, every case she'd ever heard whispered about—five, six, seven. Young enough that the world hadn't finished writing them yet.

"No reason," Ilsa said, and stood, and gathered the trace papers, and heard how thin the words came out of her. "You're remanded to secure custody pending formal charges. You'll be held here overnight."

"Of course." He rose without protest, without fear, and paused at the door, and looked back at her with those dark patient eyes. "Captain. For what it's worth—your instrument isn't broken. It just doesn't have a category for me yet." A beat. "Neither, I think, do you."

The door closed behind him. Corren was looking at her, waiting, and the trace papers were trembling very slightly in her hand—not from fear, she told herself, from fatigue, nineteen hours, anyone's hand would shake—and outside the window the city lay under a sky with no stars in it, and beneath the city, beneath everything, the mountain sat in the dark like a held breath.

"Corren," she said. "Pull every sealed file from before the Charter era. Court-taken children. All of them."

"All of them? Captain, that's—"

"That's an order." She smoothed the trace flat on the desk and looked at the clean grey nothing where a soul should have been, and finished, at last, the question she had been asking for eleven years: *what did they take, and what did they leave, and why does what they left look like a man doing his job better than anyone else in the palace?*

In the cell below, she knew without checking, Freya Galloway was not sleeping either. And somewhere in the small hours, a summons was going to arrive through a pocket no search had ever found—and though she could not have named how she knew it, Ilsa Vane went down the stairs toward the cells with her hand resting, without quite deciding to, on the hilt of her warrant-knife.
