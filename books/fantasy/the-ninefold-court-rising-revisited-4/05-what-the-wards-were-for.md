## 5. What the Wards Were For

The interrogation rooms beneath the Wardhouse had no windows, which Ilsa Vane had always considered a mercy. Windows let in weather, and weather made people sentimental, and sentimentality was the enemy of clean testimony. Tonight, though, with the stars gone and the wards dead above her like a roof pulled off the world, she found herself missing even a slit of sky. The dark outside felt less like an absence now and more like a presence—something leaning its weight against Cinderfall's walls to listen.

She sat on one side of a scarred oak table. The taster sat on the other.

Freya Galloway's wrists were bound—not chained; she'd refused the chains—with cord woven through with null-thread that should have kept any charmwork inert against his skin. It was doing nothing at all. She could see it plainly: the faint gold shimmer crawling along his forearms like sunlight trapped under ice, pulsing gently in time with his breathing. His hidden charms, flaring visible since midnight. Every instrument in her office had screamed about him for hours.

"Comfortable?" she asked.

"No," he said. "But then, neither are you."

He wasn't wrong. Her left hand still ached from where her resonance gauge had cracked in its cradle when she'd first brought him in—the needle swinging past every calibrated mark, past *living*, past *dead*, and pinning itself against the stop labeled, in her grandfather's handwriting, **NOT APPLICABLE**.

"You're going to tell me what you are," she said. "Slowly. From the beginning. And Galloway—if you lie to me, I'll know. I've spent twenty years reading ward-signatures. People leak when they lie. Their charms flicker out of rhythm."

"My charms," he said mildly, "have been out of rhythm since the stars went out. Everything has." He looked down at his glowing arms with an expression she couldn't parse—wonder, or grief, or both wearing each other's clothes. "Ask your questions, Captain."

So she did. She asked them the way she'd been trained, in the old order, before the Wardhouse became a place where ambitious men filed paperwork instead of working wards. She asked about the charm he'd passed to the scullery maid—a warming sigil, he admitted, trivial, worth two coppers on any black-market corner. She asked how long he'd been practicing. Seven years, he said. Since he was fifteen. He named the hedge-witch who taught him—a woman called Odile Marsh, dead three winters—and gave an address, a description, details that checked against records she didn't yet have but would by morning. He lied about almost nothing. That frightened her more than lying would have. Honest criminals were either stupid or certain of something bigger than arrest, and Freya Galloway did not look stupid.

It was only when she laid out the resonance readings—her own instruments' verdicts, transcribed in her own careful hand—that the room changed temperature.

"Your soul-mark reads as voided," she said. "Do you understand what that means?"

"I can't say I do."

"It means the instrument finds no living signature. No unspent years, no natural decay curve, none of the ordinary noise a person makes just by being alive. The last time this notation appears anywhere in our archives—" she turned the page toward him, and watched him read it, and watched the color leave his face with something like professional satisfaction and something much worse, like pity— "is forty-one years ago. A case file from the founding quarter. Nine children, all marked the same way. All under the age of six. All listed as having died of winter fever within the same month."

"That's not me," he said quietly. "I'm twenty-two."

"No," she agreed. "You're not nine children from forty-one years ago. But you carry the same mark they did. *Voided.* As if something already owns you, and the ownership is so complete there's nothing left over to measure." She folded her hands. "In twenty years of ward-keeping I have seen that mark exactly once, Galloway. On you. So you're going to help me understand it, because my instruments can't."

For a long moment he said nothing. The gold light under his skin dimmed, brightened, dimmed—out of rhythm, like a heart trying to remember its own tempo.

"I was an orphan," he said finally. "Foundling house off Tanner's Row. There were nine of us in my intake year who never got fostered out." His voice stayed level, but his charms had gone very still. "I don't remember much before six. Fever, mostly. Everyone remembers fever."

*Everyone remembers fever,* she thought, because that was what the old file said too, in the same clerk's hand, the same formula copied nine times. Winter fever. Winter fever. Winter fever. Forty-one years ago, and again—she checked her notes, hating what she was checking—again seven years ago, the year Galloway would have been fifteen, another cluster of orphan deaths in the founding quarter, all marked *voided* posthumously by a junior examiner who'd been reprimanded for clerical error and reassigned.

Reassigned to where? She'd find out. She was beginning to suspect the answer was *nowhere*, because the man had retired to the coast and died comfortable, and comfortable men don't get reassigned for seeing things correctly.

* * *

She gave him water. She told herself it was procedure—hydration improved testimony—but the truth was that watching him sit in the lamplight with his dead-inside soul-mark and his gentle, exhausted face had started to feel like interrogating a ghost, and ghosts deserved water if anything did.

"You said the wards fell with a sound like breaking glass," he said, cradling the cup. "I heard it. Half the palace heard it. What were we hearing, Captain? Because the way people are telling it in the corridors, the wards were a shield, and the shield broke, and now magic will pour in and eat us all."

"That's the story." She leaned back. "That's the story I was raised on too. Wards keep things out. Walls keep things out. Doors lock the bad weather on the other side." She paused. "I'm going to show you something now, Galloway, and I want you to understand that showing you is itself a crime. Section Fourteen. Disclosure of foundational instruments to an unauthorized civilian. It carries a ten-year sentence."

"Then why show me?"

"Because in twelve hours none of it may matter, and because everyone else I could show is either panicking, lying, or both." She rose, went to the iron cabinet in the corner, and spoke the release-phrase her grandmother had drilled into her before she could write: *the mountain keeps its own accounts.*

The cabinet opened on a single object: a disc of grey slate the size of a dinner plate, banded in nine rings of metal she could not name, older than any charter in the city archive. The First Gauge. Every ward-keeper in Cinderfall's history had sworn their oath with their palm flat on its surface. It hadn't spoken in living memory—or rather, it had always said the same thing, the single word it was built to say, etched in light across its face whenever a keeper touched it: **CONTAINED**.

She set it on the table between them. The null-cord around Freya's wrists hummed. His charms flared.

"Touch it," she said.

"If it's an instrument, shouldn't you—"

"It won't answer me anymore. It stopped saying *contained* at midnight. Now look."

She flattened her palm on the slate. The old word tried to rise—she saw the ghost of it forming, the familiar pale letters—and then it broke apart, unraveled, and reformed into something else. Two words now, burning slow and cold:

**DEBT UNPAID**

Beside her, Freya Galloway made a small sound, involuntary, like a man stepping into a hole he hadn't seen.

"What does it mean," he whispered, "by debt?"

And here was the vertigo, the moment she had been circling all night the way a tongue circles a broken tooth. Because she knew. She had known since she was eleven years old and found her grandmother's private papers sewn into the lining of a coat, and she had spent her whole life deciding not to know it, filing it away under *old stories* the way the whole city filed it away, because a story that terrible stops being a story and starts being a foundation stone, and you cannot live in a building while questioning its foundations.

"The wards don't keep magic out," she said. "That's the children's version. Here is the ward-keeper's version."

She sat back down. She made herself meet his eyes.

"Cinderfall was founded four hundred years ago by nine families with nine fortunes and not enough land above ground worth having. What they had instead was a mountain, and under the mountain something vast and old and *hungry*—for warmth, for light, for life, no one wrote down precisely what. The founders could have sealed it. They had the craft; the wards they built prove they had the craft, and those wards are the finest work this country has ever produced. Instead, they built the wards *inward*. Not a wall to keep the thing below from getting out. A channel. A throat. A way to feed it."

"Feed it what?"

"A price. Negotiated once, in the founding year, and renewed ever since. The wards aren't armor, Galloway—they're plumbing. They direct the flow. And what flows downward is—" her voice caught, and she hated it, hated the crack in twenty years of cold duty— "*payment*. Warmth drawn from hearths. Light drawn from lamps. Years drawn from the sick. And sometimes—" 

"Souls," Freya said. Very quietly. His glow had gone white.

"The founders called it *tithing*. The archive calls it *maintenance of the covenant*. My grandmother called it what it was." She looked at the slate, at the words burning there. "She was the last keeper who read the original contract. She told me the mountain doesn't distinguish between coin and children. To it, value is value. And every few generations, when the tithe runs thin, it reaches up and takes what it was promised—and the wards, which were built to *facilitate*, stand aside and let it."

The lamp guttered. Neither of them moved.

"Forty-one years ago," she continued, because stopping was unthinkable now that she'd begun, "there was a thin year. Bad harvests, plague in the low quarters, the tithe short by whatever measure the mountain uses. Nine children from the founding quarter were recorded dead of winter fever. Their soul-marks were voided. And that same night, Captain—my predecessor noted it in the log, in his tidy hand—the dragon-veins beneath the palace burned brighter than they had in a century. Enough surplus fire that the court below paid the city back. A new charter. New privileges. The founding families' descendants still live on the interest."

Freya had gone very still. Stillness, in a man whose skin glowed, was louder than screaming.

"And seven years ago," she said, "another thin year."

"Don't," he said.

"Another cluster of orphan deaths—"

"*Don't.*"

"—and a boy of fifteen who walked out of a fever ward that killed everyone else in it, carrying a soul-mark like a receipt, and took a job in the one building in Cinderfall that sits directly above the oldest vein." She exhaled slowly. "You're not the thief here, Galloway. You're the *collateral*. You've been collateral since you were five years old. Whatever immunity you've built, whatever charm-work you think you learned—you didn't build it. It was *left in you*. Like a deposit. Like a marker saying *this one is claimed, this one is coming due.*"

He stood up so fast the chair went over. The null-cord snapped—not broke, *unmade*, threads unraveling into dust—and for one suspended second his whole body blazed, gold-white, casting his shadow huge and shaking on the wall, and Ilsa reached for her knife and knew it would be useless and reached anyway.

But he didn't attack. He stood there trembling, blazing, staring at his own hands as though they belonged to a stranger—which, she understood suddenly, they always had.

"There's someone," he said. The words came out broken. "There was—my intake year—there were eight others, but there was one who—" He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "Bryn. My sister. We weren't blood. None of us were. But she was mine. When I was fifteen they told me she died of winter fever, and I believed it, I *built my whole life* on believing it, because believing it meant the world made sense—"

"Bryn," Ilsa repeated. The name snagged on something. She flipped back through her notes, through the transcription of the resonance archive, through the forty-one-year-old file and the seven-year-old file, and there it was, in the junior examiner's neat condemned hand, the ninth name in the newer cluster:

*Bryn — female, approx. 5 yrs — mark voided — winter fever.*

Except the examiner had added one line the reprimand had singled out as fantasy, one line Ilsa had read an hour ago without understanding, and understood now with a vertigo so complete it felt like falling:

*Subject's mark not merely voided but ACTIVE. Signature consistent with combustion. Recommend immediate inquiry.*

They had recommended inquiry. The city had responded by losing the file in a drawer for seven years.

"She's alive," Freya said. He had gone gray under the glow. "Below. Isn't she. That's what *active* means. That's what the interest payments are." His voice climbed. "Captain. *Isn't she.*"

Ilsa Vane had spent her whole career believing that duty meant holding the line between the city and the dark. Standing in this windowless room, with the First Gauge burning *DEBT UNPAID* between them and a foundling taster shaking apart in front of her, she finally understood the shape of the lie she'd inherited. The line had never held anything back. It had only ever measured what flowed down—and decided, four hundred years ago, by men whose names were on half the streets above, that some lives were currency.

"Yes," she said. "I believe she is."

He came at her then—not violence, just momentum, grief with nowhere to go—and she let him grip the front of her coat and shake her once, twice, before his strength failed.

"Take me down," he said. "Whatever the procedure is, whatever the law says—take me down there, or I'll go alone, and I promise you, Captain, I have survived poison enough times to survive anything else."

Before she could answer—before she could decide, though she suspected, with a strange calm, that she had decided the moment the slate changed its word—light bloomed inside his ruined sleeve. Not the diffuse gold of his charms. A shape. Deliberate, addressed, *delivered*: a small paper seal unfolding in midair like a moth, inked in a hand she didn't recognize, bearing a sigil she did—one from the oldest plates in the archive, the steward's mark of the court below.

It hung between them and spoke in a dry, patient voice:

*"Freya Galloway. You have questions. I have answers, and fewer of them than you fear. Come down by the old vein-road, tonight, before the regent seals the mountain and makes us all into a closed account. Come willingly, and bring the ward-keeper if she'll come—she reads true instruments, and true instruments are rare up there now."*

The seal burned itself to ash.

In the silence after, Ilsa looked at the prisoner she was legally obligated to keep, and the door she was legally obligated to guard, and the mountain that had just, for the first time in four hundred years, asked the city *upstairs* for a meeting.

"Well," she said, reaching for her coat. "I suppose Section Fourteen is the least of it now."
