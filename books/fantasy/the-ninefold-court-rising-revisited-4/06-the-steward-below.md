## 6. The Steward Below

They had put him in a cell with no stars in it.

Freya understood the logic even as he resented it. After last night—after every window in Cinderfall had shown nothing but a black and starless sky where the constellations used to be—the interrogators were taking no chances on what a prisoner might draw strength from. So the cell was deep in the palace underbelly, a room of dressed granite and a single lamp that never flickered, and Freya Galloway sat on the edge of a cot too short for him and counted the things he still owned.

One knife, taken from him at arrest. One set of lockpicks disguised as a fish-gutter's kit, also gone. Seven coins sewn into his left boot cuff, undiscovered because Captain Ilsa Vane had searched him like a soldier and not like a thief, and soldiers looked for weapons before they looked for wealth. And in his right boot, flat against his ankle where it had lived for eleven years, a pocket-charm no bigger than a thumbnail: a disc of horn engraved with a spiral, warm to the touch, humming faintly when he pressed his thumb to its center.

It was humming now.

That was impossible, or near enough. The charm was a listening-thing, one-way, a hedge-witch's trick he'd bought at fifteen for the price of two good meals. It received. It did not speak. In seven years of wearing it against his skin through baths and searches and one memorable night in the wine cellar of the Amberlark Tavern, it had never once done anything but hum when someone on the other end thought of him—and the only person who had ever held its twin was dead these four years, buried outside the walls in an unmarked grave because hedge-witches didn't get headstones.

Freya pressed his thumb to the spiral.

The hum resolved into a voice, thin as thread pulled through cloth, speaking directly into the bone behind his ear. He flinched. No one else could hear it—he checked anyway, glancing at the door, at the lamp, at the guard-shaped shadow beyond the grate—but the intimacy of it made his neck prickle. A voice inside his skull was a violation even by the standards of his life.

*Freya Galloway,* said the voice. It was a woman's voice, dry and unhurried, with the particular flatness of someone reading from a list she had memorized long ago. *You are sitting in a cell beneath the Hall of Records. The cell is warded against egress but not against ingress; the distinction matters more than you know. My name is Maren. I keep accounts for the Ninefold Court. I am the only keeper they have ever had who kept them honestly.*

Freya said nothing. His heart was going hard enough that he half-feared the charm would transmit it.

*I watched you taste for the regent's table for three years before the wards fell,* Maren continued. *You have a gift for survival, taster. I respect gifts. But your survival is running out. At dawn the regent signs the order to seal the mountain approaches and burn the lower galleries. You are scheduled to be questioned again first, and the men who will question you do not use questions. I can offer you the truth instead. All of it. What the wards were, what the court is, why the stars went out, and—* here the voice paused, and something almost human moved beneath the dryness— *why you of all people can walk into the fire without burning.*

The hum faded. Then returned, fainter, like a speaker leaning closer to a door.

*There is a drain in the floor of your cell, third stone from the corner. It is not a drain. Come down willingly, and I will meet you at the first gate. Refuse, and I will not be able to help you after dawn. Choose quickly. The court does not wait, and neither does the regent.*

Silence. The charm cooled against his ankle, going inert, going back to being a dead woman's gift.

Freya sat very still for a long time.

He ran through it the way he ran through everything: twice, slowly, looking for the poison. A voice out of a charm that couldn't speak. A drain that wasn't a drain. An offer of truth from a steward of a court that had spent seven years being the thing his whole life was built to avoid. Every instinct he owned said *trap*. And underneath the instinct, quieter, older, was the other thing—the thing he had felt when the stars went out and the hidden charms under his skin had lit up like festival lanterns, visible to everyone in the great hall while the wine turned to smoke on his tongue. Terror, yes. But also exhilaration. Also relief. Seven years of hiding, ending whether he wanted it to or not.

And there was this: the captain had told him, in her flat way, between questions, that the wards hadn't been built to keep magic out. They'd been built to keep something *down*. The city's founders had sold a price. She hadn't said what price. He suspected she didn't fully know herself.

But he knew—or his body knew, some animal ledger deeper than thought—that the price had his name in it somewhere. When Maren said *why you of all people*, something behind his sternum had turned over like a page.

Freya took off his right boot and worked the charm free. It sat in his palm, small and warm and absurd, the spiral worn nearly smooth by eleven years of sweat. He thought about the hedge-witch who'd sold it to him, old Sef, who had smelled of woodsmoke and comfrey and had taught him that the difference between a trick and a crime was mostly who caught you doing it. *Never cast where they can see,* she'd said. *Never be seen casting. And never, ever hope, boy. Hope's the most expensive spell there is, and nobody sells it cheap.*

Well. He was already arrested, already marked, already scheduled for questioners who didn't use questions. Hope was expensive, but the down payment had apparently been made without him.

"Third stone from the corner," he said aloud, to hear how stupid it sounded.

It sounded exactly as stupid as it was. He got up and knelt by the drain anyway.

* * *

The stone came up easier than any stone has a right to come up in a palace cell, which should have frightened him more than it did. Beneath it was not filth and pipe but a shaft, circular, lined with smooth dark rock that gave back the lamplight like water gives back a face. Iron rungs descended into warmth. Not the damp cold of cellars—warmth, dry and mineral, with a smell underneath it like hot iron and, distantly, like bread. That smell unsettled him worse than everything else. He knew ovens. He knew kitchens. Something below the mountain was *cooking*, and he was going to climb down toward it.

He considered leaving a note. He considered, briefly and seriously, simply waiting for dawn and taking his chances with honest cruelty rather than dishonest kindness. But then he thought of the questioners' reputation—Captain Vane's face when she'd mentioned them, the first crack he'd seen in all that granite—and he swung himself onto the rungs.

Halfway down, he heard the door above unlock.

He froze against the shaft wall, breath shallow. Lamplight spilled into the opening, and a silhouette leaned over the hole: broad-shouldered, close-cropped hair, the unmistakable posture of a woman who stood the way walls stand. Captain Ilsa Vane stared down into the darkness. Freya pressed himself flat and did not breathe.

She stared for a count of ten. Fifteen. Long enough that he began to hope she'd seen nothing.

"I can hear your boots creaking, Galloway," she said.

Freya exhaled. "Captain."

"You're escaping." It wasn't quite a question. It wasn't quite an accusation either. Her voice had the quality of a ledger line—recording, not judging. Yet.

"I'm descending," Freya said. "It's different. Escaping goes sideways or up."

"Where does it go?"

"Down. Toward answers. A steward of whatever's under the mountain offered me truth if I came willing." He paused. "I'd have told you, Captain, but you'd have locked me in irons and reported it, and then we'd both be standing in the same place with less time."

Above him, silence. He could picture her face without seeing it: the narrow eyes, the scar bisecting her left brow, the expression of a woman perpetually auditing the world and finding discrepancies. Three days of interrogation had taught him her rhythms. She hated him—or hated what he represented, the smuggled magic, the seven years of small crimes—but she hated being lied to by her own institution more, and something in her had been quietly breaking since the wards fell. He had watched it break in increments. He was gambling now, with his life, on the shape of the wreckage.

"The regent's order burns the galleries at dawn," Vane said. "If you're in them—"

"I know."

"If you die down there, the record will say you escaped custody and perished. I won't lie for you twice."

"You haven't lied for me once, Captain."

Another silence, longer. Then, to his genuine astonishment, her silhouette shifted, and she stepped onto the top rung. "I'm coming with you."

Freya blinked up at her. "Why?"

"Because my instruments read you as a dead man, Galloway, and I've seen that reading once before, twenty years ago, on a case file I was ordered to burn. Because the regent wants you questioned by men who don't use questions, which means the regent is afraid of what you know without knowing you know it." Her voice dropped into the shaft, echoing slightly, stripped of its official cadence until it sounded almost young. "And because I burned my own career getting those instruments calibrated, and I'll be damned if I let the answers rot in a hole without me. Move down. You climb slower than a man carrying guilt, which is saying something."

He moved down. Behind and above him, the captain descended with the deliberate economy of someone who had climbed things in worse places, and the lamplight from the cell shrank to a coin, a spark, a memory. The dark closed in around them both, warm and breathing.

* * *

The shaft ended in a gallery that took Freya's breath and held it hostage.

It was a road—no, a *street*, wide enough for six carts abreast, carved through living rock and lit along both sides by veins of light that ran in the stone itself like ore that had learned to glow. The light was orange-gold and unsteady, guttering in slow pulses, brightening and dimming with a rhythm so regular that Freya's kitchen-trained mind supplied the word before he wanted it: *heartbeat.* The veins pulsed like a heartbeat slowing. Like a heart that had been beating a very long time and was tired.

Dragon-fire, said the oldest stories, the ones told in kitchens over the heads of children. The founders had found a dragon sleeping under the mountain, or killed one, or bargained with one—the story changed depending on how much wine was poured—and bound its fire into the earth to hold back the dark. The veins were its arteries. And they were dying.

"How long?" Freya asked. His voice came out hushed, though there was no one to disturb.

Vane crouched beside a vein, holding her instrument case open. The brass gauges within spun lazily, uselessly. "The ward-masters' logs say the fire was steady for three hundred years. Decline measurable in the last forty. Accelerating in the last—" she stopped. Recalculated. "Days. Maybe hours. Whatever fed it stopped feeding it, or started failing."

"Or was eaten," Freya said, before he could stop himself.

She looked at him sharply. "What?"

"Nothing." Everything. The word *eaten* had arrived in his mouth fully formed, with a taste attached—copper and ash, the taste of the wine in the hall the night the stars went out. He filed it away next to the other things he refused yet to think about.

They walked. The street descended in long switchbacks, and the architecture around them changed as they went, the way sediment changes color in a cut hillside. Near the top, the carvings were human: doorways sized for men, lintels bearing names in Old Charter script, niches that might once have held household gods. Deeper, the proportions drifted. Doors grew taller than any man. Steps lengthened until climbing them was a stride and a half. And the ornamentation changed character entirely—not cruder, Freya noted with professional discomfort, but *different*, spirals and interlocking knots that his eyes kept trying to resolve into faces and failing, the way a tongue keeps probing a missing tooth.

"They lived here," Vane said quietly. "Before the charter. Before the city. We built our walls on their roof."

"You sound like you've revised a report."

"I've revised several." She said nothing more, and Freya let it lie. There was a particular silence a person made when they were reorganizing their entire understanding of the world, and he knew better than anyone not to interrupt it.

The pulse of the veins slowed further as they went down. Between pulses, the darkness was total, and in the total darkness Freya heard things: a vast distant slowness that might have been breathing; a skittering far overhead that might have been rats and might have been nothing with fur; and once, unmistakably, a voice singing in a register below hearing, felt in the teeth rather than the ears. Vane heard it too. Her hand went to the warrant-case at her belt and stayed there, gripping, the way another woman might grip a rosary.

The first gate announced itself by temperature. The air grew hotter by degrees, then suddenly, and the street opened into a cavern where the ceiling rose beyond sight and the vein-light converged into a pool of gold on the far wall. Set into the wall was a doorway—human-sized, mercifully, though flanked by pillars carved as intertwined figures whose anatomy Freya declined to study—and before the doorway, hands folded, stood a woman.

She was grey all over: grey robe, grey braid coiled at her neck, grey eyes that had the specific patience of someone who had waited out centuries. She was not old, particularly. Or perhaps she was extremely old in a way that had given up on showing it. She looked at Freya the way he looked at a dish he was about to taste: completely, cataloguing, missing nothing.

"Taster Galloway," she said. Her voice was the voice from the charm, dry and unhurried. "You came willingly. Do you understand how rare that makes you? Nine hundred years of charter, and you are the first thing this court has received freely given."

"I received a threat and an offer," Freya said. "That's not freedom. That's a menu."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile had been excavated. "Good. You argue. The last ones they took down here didn't argue. Arguing means you still think you have standing." She turned her gaze to Vane, and something complicated crossed her face. "Ward-keeper. Investigator. Ilsa Vane. Your grandmother signed the second amendment to the founding charter. Did you know that?"

"My grandmother was a laundress," Vane said flatly.

"She laundered for the signatories. She witnessed the amendment. Everyone who touched that document is in it, Captain. That is what a contract of this kind *is*." Maren folded her hands again. "You may follow. You may speak when spoken to. You may turn back, and the mountain will forget you kindly. Those are the terms, and I have no authority to amend them, and neither, tonight, does anyone else."

"And if we refuse the terms?" Freya asked.

"Then the gate doesn't open, the fire dies by morning, and everything your city is—including the two of you, standing here arguing—goes out with it." She said it without drama, the way one reads a shipping manifest. "I don't like the terms either. I've disliked them for longer than your language has had a word for it. But I am the steward, taster. I keep the accounts. And the account is overdue."

Beyond her, past the doorway, the gold light breathed. In. Out. Slower each time.

Maren stepped aside and gestured through, and Freya saw, down a corridor lined with nine empty alcoves—nine thrones, he would learn, though from here they looked like tombs—a tenth light at the corridor's end that was not gold at all but white, cold, and steady, burning like a star that had fallen indoors.

"That's where your answers live," Maren said. "Both of yours." Her grey eyes settled on Freya, and for one moment the steward's mask slipped, and what showed underneath was grief so old it had fossilized into procedure. "Walk carefully, taster. Some truths are heavier than others, and yours weighs more than most. I've read the ledger. I know exactly what your name costs."

Freya looked at the white light. His immunity—seven years of swallowed poisons, of the court's strange investment in his body—hummed under his skin like the charm had hummed, like something waking up.

He walked through the gate. Behind him, after a moment's weighted silence, came the measured tread of Captain Ilsa Vane, following him into the dark, her warrant-case clutched like a shield against everything she was about to stop believing.
