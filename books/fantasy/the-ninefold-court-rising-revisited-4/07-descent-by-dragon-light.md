## 7. Descent by Dragon-Light

The door in the cellar wall had not been a door when Freya was a boy.

He knew this the way he knew the taste of arsenic—certainly, and with a small cold horror. He had grown up three streets from Cinderfall's undercity, had crawled through every culvert and coal-chute a hungry child could squeeze into, and there had never been a door here, only damp stone furred with old mortar and the smell of things that preferred the dark. Now it stood open on a stair that went down farther than lamplight cared to follow, and the air that rose out of it was warm.

Not hearth-warm. Blood-warm. The warmth of something enormous and patient, breathing beneath the world.

"You first," Ilsa Vane said behind him, and her hand was nowhere near her sword hilt, which meant she wanted him to know it *could* be.

Freya looked at Maren. The steward of the Ninefold Court stood in the doorway she had summoned out of solid rock, and up close she was not what he had expected of anything courtly. She wore grey—the grey of ash, of dove's wing, of a thing that has decided not to be seen—and her face had the particular stillness of someone who had spent so long among powers that she had stopped performing for them. Her eyes were the color of the guttering light below: amber going to ember, ember going to ash.

"You're afraid," she said to Freya. It was not an accusation. It sounded almost like approval.

"Good," Freya said. "I'd hate to be the only one."

"I'm not afraid." Maren stepped back into the dark, onto the second stair, and the light from below moved across her face like water. "I'm past it. Fear is for people who still believe they'll be spared. Come down or don't, taster. But come down deciding, not dragged."

Freya had rules. Seven years of them, carved small and daily, like the charms he stitched into his cuffs where no instrument of Ilsa Vane's making had thought to look until two nights ago. *Never cast. Never be seen. Never hope.* All three were already broken or breaking; the stars were gone, the wards were down, his hidden work had flared visible in front of half the palace guard, and here he was, following a voice out of his own pocket-charm into the belly of the mountain his city had been built on top of like a lid on a pot.

So much for rules. What remained was the fourth thing, the one he had never written down because writing it would have made it real: he had been called by name. Not *taster*, not *Galloway*, not *the suspect in cell three*. His name—his whole name, the one the orphanage ledger had shortened, the one no one living used—and the voice that said it had known things about him that Freya himself had spent seven years burying.

A person did not walk away from that. A person walked *toward* it, carefully, with his hands where everyone could see them and his tongue ready to tell true from false, because that was the one skill he owned outright.

"Captain Vane," he said, without turning around, "if I die down there, my ghost is going to haunt your paperwork specifically."

"Noted," Ilsa said. "Move."

* * *

The stairs went down for an hour, maybe more. Time went strange under the mountain; Freya counted it in his body instead, in the ache of his knees and the way his breath began to taste faintly metallic, like licking an old coin.

The vein-light came first as a rumor—a red glow seeping up through cracks in the steps, painting each of their shadows upward against the walls so that they climbed down through a corridor of their own inverted ghosts. Then the walls themselves opened, the stair giving way to a road, and Freya stopped counting his aches and forgot them entirely.

It was a road. That was the thing his mind kept snagging on. Not a tunnel, not a worm-hole, but a *road*—broad enough for eight abreast, paved in slabs of black stone fitted so precisely that no seam caught his boot-sole, running straight through the mountain's heart toward a glow that was not daylight and did not pretend to be. And along both walls, following the road like veins following the length of an arm, ran the dragon-fire.

That was what Maren called it, anyway, when Ilsa asked in a voice carefully sanded flat of wonder. Veins of it, Freya thought, was exactly right: branching channels in the living rock, filled with light that moved the way blood moves, slow and pressurized and *alive*. It was gold at the centers and red at the edges and somewhere deep in each pulse was a flicker of white, like a word about to be spoken. Where the veins thinned, the light guttered—not flickered, *guttered*, the way a candle gutters when the tallow runs low—and in those places the dark came right up to the edge of the stone and leaned there, waiting.

"The founders' charter," Ilsa said. She had been quiet for a long time. "The histories say the city bought light from the mountain. Literal light. I always thought it was poetry."

"It was a receipt," Maren said.

Freya reached out—he couldn't help it—and let his fingertips hover a hair's breadth above the nearest vein. The warmth off it was tremendous. His skin prickled with the near-touch, some animal instinct in him screaming *not food, do not put that in your mouth*, which was, he reflected, the closest thing to professional courtesy the fire had shown anyone yet.

"What does it feel like?" Ilsa asked him. She had watched his hand. She missed very little, this woman; it was going to be a problem.

"Like standing too close to an oven," Freya said. Which was true, and also a lie, because it felt like standing too close to an oven *that was humming a tune he almost remembered*. But that part went in the drawer with everything else, locked, key swallowed.

They walked. The road bent once, gently, around some vast obstruction the mountain had grown rather than been carved past, and in the bend's shelter the veins had pooled into a wide, shallow lake of light, and there Maren stopped and let them rest, and eat, and ask nothing, while the fire pulsed its slow tide beside them.

It was Ilsa who finally broke, because Ilsa's silences were load-bearing and even hers had limits.

"How long has it been dying?"

Maren turned a piece of road-bread over in her fingers. Under the vein-light her face looked carved from something older than wood.

"When I was sworn in," she said, "the veins ran white-gold, bank to bank, and the throne room was bright enough to read fine print in. That was ninety years ago." She bit the bread, chewed, swallowed. "My mother served before me. She said the dimming began in her grandmother's day. The fire eats, captain. It has always eaten. The only question—the only question there has ever been—is what."

"And now?"

"Now it is nearly done eating what it was fed at the founding, and it has begun—" Maren's voice did not change at all, which was how Freya knew the next words cost her more than anything else she'd said— "it has begun to draw on its reserves."

"Which are?"

Maren looked at Freya. Just at him. Directly, steadily, with the patience of someone setting a trap she wished she didn't have to set.

"That," she said, "is what the court will want to explain to you itself."

* * *

The last league of the road climbed, which Freya had not expected and found somehow worse. Things beneath mountains should go down. This went *in*, deeper than depth, the way a wound goes in, and the veins thickened around them as they walked until the light was constant and the shadows had nowhere left to hide, and Freya understood with a lurch that they were walking inside the last strong artery of a dying heart.

Then the road ended, and the throne room began, and awe curdled in Freya Galloway's chest like milk in sun.

The chamber was vast beyond sense—vast the way the sky is vast, a size the eye refuses and then surrenders to. Nine daises stepped up in rings toward the center of the mountain's hollow crown, and on each dais stood a throne, and the nine thrones together formed a constellation, a pattern, a *sentence* in some grammar of power that made Freya's teeth ache to look at, though look he could not stop doing.

Eight of the thrones were empty.

They were not merely vacant. They were *ended*—each one draped in its own particular ruin, as if emptiness were a thing that happened differently to different kings. One had cracked clean through, halves leaning apart like a book abandoned open. One had sunk into its dais as though the stone had digested it halfway. One was whole and pristine and wrong in a way Freya's tongue could find no word for, except that looking at it tasted of copper and apology. Dust lay on all nine equally, undisturbed, in layers deep enough to date the abandonment of this place to longer than Cinderfall had existed.

And the ninth throne was occupied.

It sat at the center of the constellation, and the light of the great veins converged upon it from every direction, and on it sat a king.

Freya's mind reached for the shape the way his tongue reached for an unknown poison: testing, cataloguing, refusing to commit. The figure wore the form of a man—or the memory of one, worn smooth, the way a coin worn smooth still says *king* on it long after the face is gone. Robes of a darkness that drank the vein-light. A crown that might have been metal or might have been flame given permanence. A posture of such absolute, ancient repose that the stillness itself became a kind of motion, the way a mountain's stillness is motion slowed past seeing.

It did not breathe. Freya checked. Seven years at the palace tables had taught him to read chests across a banquet hall, to mark the rise and fall that separated the living from the merely seated, and the figure on the ninth throne did not do it. Nothing about it did anything. And yet the whole room oriented around it—the light, the dust, the silence—all of it arranged like courtiers around a presence, and Freya felt his skin crawl with the understanding that he was not looking at a statue or a corpse or a costume.

He was looking at something wearing a king the way Maren wore grey.

"There you are."

The voice did not come from the throne. It came from everywhere the light was—from the veins, from the walls, from the floor underfoot and the air in his lungs—and it was warm, it was *terribly* warm, warm the way the road had been warm, blood-warm, familiar-warm, and Freya's whole body went rigid with a recognition he had no memory to attach to it.

"You came down willingly," the king said, and the words moved through Freya like heat through a held cup. "Do you know how long it has been since anyone came down willingly? They send us thieves and wardens and frightened clerks with warrants." A pause, and in the pause something that was almost—almost—humor. "But you. You followed your own pocket home. Good child. We did tell them you'd be clever."

*We,* Freya thought, and his mouth was dry as ash. "*We told them.*"

Ilsa's hand was on her sword now after all, and Maren had drifted a careful half-step away from them both, positioning herself—Freya noted distantly—in the exact spot where neither the throne nor the captain could reach her first. Traitors all, then. Traitors all, including him.

"We have watched you a long time, Freya Galloway," the king said, gentle as a nurse, vast as a landslide. "Every charm you ever palmed. Every poison you ever survived. Every year of that immunity we built into you, coin by coin, like interest on a debt." The light in the veins pulsed once, all of them together, like a single heartbeat, and in the pulse Freya felt something turn over in his chest, something that had been asleep a long time, something with his sister's laugh attached to it—

No. Not his sister. He didn't—there was no—

"Come closer, child," said the king on the ninth throne, in the voice of something that remembered him fondly, and had the power to make that the most frightening sentence in the world. "We have been keeping something of yours. And you have been keeping something of ours."

The veins guttered, all down the endless road behind them, all at once.

And in the half-dark, Freya heard—faint, far off, rising up through the mountain's bones like a song through a wall—someone humming a tune he almost remembered.
