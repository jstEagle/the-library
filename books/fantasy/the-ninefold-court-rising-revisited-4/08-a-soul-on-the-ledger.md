## 8. A Soul on the Ledger

The ledger was not a book.

Freya had expected a book. Everything in the world above kept its accounts in books—kitchen inventories, the palace paymaster's columns, the hedge-witch's grimoire with its grease-stained corners and its price lists scratched in the margins. He had spent seven years among paper and ink, and he trusted paper the way other men trusted iron: it could be burned, torn, forged, but at least it pretended to be honest about what it was.

Maren led him instead to a chamber where a slab of grey stone stood upright against the wall, tall as two men, its surface moving. Not with light—with *writing*. Lines of script surfaced and sank like fish beneath ice, silver on slate, appearing at the top of the slab and sliding down into some depth he could not see, as though the stone were an endless throat swallowing words forever.

"The Ledger," Maren said. She said it the way a priest names an altar, or a jailer names a door. "It records what the Court is owed. It has recorded it since before Cinderfall had a name."

Freya stood a careful distance from it. His hands had gone cold in his pockets, around the little charms there—the coin that warmed, the knot that unlocked, the scrap of red thread that had once been his whole fortune and was now merely habit. The veins of dragon-fire along the chamber walls guttered as they passed, dimming toward him and then flaring back, and he had stopped pretending he didn't notice. Things down here noticed him. That was the problem with being property: everything with a claim came sniffing.

"You said truth if I came willingly." His voice came out steadier than he felt. "I'm willing. So say it. Why did the wards fall? Why did the stars go out? Why can I walk through this mountain when every soul in Cinderfall would burn?"

Maren turned. In the firelight her face was all planes and patience, a woman carved out of long service and longer regret. She had been beautiful once; now she was something better than beautiful, which was legible. Every line meant something.

"Because you are not a guest here, Freya Galloway," she said. "You never were. You are an account come home."

* * *

She made him sit first. There was a table in the chamber, black wood worn pale at the edges by centuries of elbows, and a stool, and she set a cup before him without asking whether he wanted one. Steam rose off it—something bitter and herbal, smelling faintly of the same hedge-gardens he'd robbed as a boy. He looked at the cup and then at her, and something in his chest twisted, because this was his trade, his art, his seven-year discipline: look at every cup. Ask who poured it. Ask why.

"You're testing me," he said.

"I'm respecting you." She sat across from him, folding her hands. "In the Court, a drink offered unasked is either hospitality or a snare. Only someone who knows that distinction should be told what I'm about to tell you. Drink or don't. It's only willowbark. Your nerves have been loud since you entered my hall."

He drank. It was only willowbark. It settled nothing.

"Start at the beginning," he said. "Not mine. Yours."

So Maren began where all the city's histories refused to begin: with hunger.

"Cinderfall was not founded," she said. "Cinderfall was *purchased*. Two hundred years ago, the people who would become your founders were starving on the plain above—a drought, a war behind them, winter ahead. They came to the mountain because the mountain was warm. And inside the mountain they found us. Or what was left of us. Eight thrones empty, one occupied, a great fire burning low, and a court too old and too proud to beg." Her mouth curved, without humor. "So we didn't beg. We negotiated."

"With starving refugees."

"With desperate ones. Desperation makes the best counterpart to any contract—it signs quickly and reads slowly. The founders wanted warmth, water, stone to build on, and wards to keep the wild magics off their backs while they raised a city. We wanted—" she paused, and Freya watched her choose the word with the care of a woman laying down a card she'd held for two hundred years, "—*sustenance*. Dragon-fire does not burn wood, Freya. It burns memory. It burns soul-stuff. Every flame you've walked past on your way down here is fed, and has always been fed, on lives given over by contract. The founding charter of Cinderfall specified the payment schedule."

"Payment schedule." He heard his own voice go flat. "You mean tithes."

"Matters of the soul are best discussed in the language of accounts. It keeps everyone honest about what's actually happening." Maren's eyes did not waver. "One soul per generation was the original term. Later charters added more. Later still, the founders' descendants discovered they could sell not only their own line but anyone the law declared ownerless. Foundlings. Orphans. Debtors' children. The city called it wardship. The Ledger calls it what it is."

The fire in the walls popped softly, like a log settling. Somewhere far above, he supposed, Cinderfall was lighting lamps against a starless night, and every one of those flames drew breath from down here, from this slow digestion of the sold.

"My parents," Freya said quietly. "The people who founded it. They knew."

"They wrote the price into the deed of their own houses. It's in the charter's appendix. Clause after clause. Warm hearths, lit streets, warded borders—in exchange for—" 

"Don't." He put up a hand. He was aware, distantly, that a royal food-taster stopping the steward of the Ninefold Court mid-sentence was possibly the most absurd thing he'd ever done, and also that he had no choice. "Don't soften it yet. Show me."

Maren studied him for a moment. Whatever she saw seemed to satisfy her. She rose, went to the slab of living stone, and laid both palms flat against its face.

"Ledger," she said, in a language that wasn't Common—older, rounder, each syllable shaped like something set down gently. "Open the account of Galloway."

The writing on the surface stilled. Then it began to move differently—not sinking, but gathering, lines converging like streams finding a riverbed, until a single column of text stood centered on the stone, bright enough to hurt.

Maren stepped aside.

"Read it yourself," she said. "I won't read it to you. Some things must be taken, not given."

Freya got up. His legs worked, which surprised him. He crossed the chamber, and the heat of the wall-veins pressed against his left side like a crowd leaning in, and he read:

**ACCOUNT: GALLOWAY, FREYA.**

**STATUS: SOLD.**

**SELLER: THE FOUNDLING HOUSE OF SAINT EDEL, CINDERFALL, BY CHARTER AUTHORITY.**

**DATE OF SALE: THE FIFTH YEAR OF THE ACCOUNT'S LIFE.**

**CONSIDERATION RECEIVED: THREE MARKS, PAID TO THE HOUSE. ONE HEARTH-BLESSING, PERPETUAL, GRANTED TO THE SELLER INSTITUTION.**

**PURPOSE OF PURCHASE: FUEL, CENTRAL FLAME, SCHEDULED.**

**AMENDMENT:** **THE ACCOUNT HAS NOT BEEN BURNED. DEFERRED AT THE DIRECTION OF THE THRONE. INTEREST ACCRUES.**

**NATURE OF INTEREST: IMMUNITY.**

The last word swam. He blinked and it did not resolve; it simply stayed there, patient as stone, and the meaning of it arrived in his body before his mind—arrived as a ringing in his ears, a loosening in his knees, the specific vertigo of a floor that turns out to be a lid.

Three marks. He had counted coins his whole life. He knew exactly what three marks bought in the Saint Edel foundling house: three months of gruel for forty children. One child, traded for one season of thin soup and a blessing nobody present could feel.

And the amendment. *Deferred at the direction of the throne.* Something wearing a king's shape, eight empty chairs behind it, had looked at the account named Freya Galloway five years old, scheduled for the fire—and had said *not yet.*

"Why," he said. His voice cracked on it. He hated that. He fixed it. "Why defer me? What am I worth whole that I'm not worth burned?"

"That," Maren said, "is the question, isn't it. You've been asking it your whole life without knowing. Why do poisons slide off you? Why did the hedge-charms take to you like rain on plowed ground when other children's spells curdled? Why did the Court's summons find the pocket-charm you stitched yourself, out of all the charms in Cinderfall?" She came to stand beside him, close to the ledger, and her reflection moved in the silver script. "You thought you built your immunity. Seven years of small doses, yes? A discipline. A craft."

"It *is* a craft."

"It is a *dividend*," she said. "The Court pays interest on what it holds. It always has—that's how it bound the founders so thoroughly. Every blessing in Cinderfall, every warded border, every warm street: those aren't gifts. Those are returns on capital. And you, Freya Galloway, are capital. The immunity you're so proud of earning—it isn't resistance to poison. It's the Court protecting its own asset. Nothing may harm you that would diminish what the throne intends to collect."

The chamber tilted. He gripped the edge of the table—he was sitting again, he realized, though he had no memory of sitting—and stared at his own hands. Taster's hands. Steady hands, hands he had trained, hands he had believed were *his*, the one inheritance no orphanage could sell.

Every cup he had ever drained safely. Every poison he'd survived while grown men died. The night he'd drunk the arsenated wine meant for the regent and tasted only, faintly, of almonds and triumph. Seven years of building himself into armor, plate by plate, dose by dose—and the armor had been fitted before he could walk. By them. For them.

"Then the charmwork," he said. His tongue felt thick. "The magic I taught myself—"

"Also interest. The Court's fire runs on soul, and soul is what magic is made of. A purchased soul held unburned leaks power back into its vessel. You never learned charmwork, boy. You *remembered* it—from wherever souls go before they're sold, whatever they carry of the hands that made them." Maren's voice gentled, which was somehow worse. "You are the most valuable object in Cinderfall, and you have spent your life hiding in its kitchens."

* * *

For a long time there was only the sound of the ledger, that soft perpetual swallow of script sliding down into the stone's dark throat.

Freya had a rule for moments like this—or rather, he had a rule for everything, and it applied here too: *when the floor goes, count.* Count the exits. Count the weapons. Count the lies. It was how he'd survived the tasting of Lord Aldery's funeral feast, how he'd survived the hedge-witch's deathbed, how he'd survived every year of being small and saleable in a city that ate its small and saleable things politely, clause by clause.

He counted.

Exits: the corridor behind Maren, the vein-lit road upward, the long dark. Weapons: six charms, none of them useful against a steward or a ledger or a fact. Lies: he searched for hers and could not find one, and that frightened him more than anything else, because a lie he could fight. A true thing just sat there, like the ledger, swallowing.

"Say it plainly," he said at last. "All of it. No accounting language. I've read contracts my whole life; I know how they hide the knife in the footnotes. Say it like a person."

Maren was quiet a moment. When she spoke, the accounting cadence was gone from her voice, and what remained was tired and old and human, and he understood suddenly that she had been waiting two hundred years to say these words to someone who could bear them.

"You were a child," she said. "They took you from a room full of children because the charter allowed it and three marks was cheap. You were carried down here—or you were meant to be; some accounts are collected at once, some are left to ripen—and something on the throne looked at you and chose not to feed you to the fire. Not mercy. The throne doesn't have mercy. Strategy. And ever since, everything you are has been growing in soil it owns. Your body, your craft, your cleverness, your luck—all of it is the Court's investment appreciating. You are not a man who survived the Ninefold Court, Freya Galloway. You are a vintage it is saving for a particular occasion."

"What occasion?"

"That, the Ledger does not say." Her jaw tightened. "The throne keeps its intentions off the page. But I have served this court longer than your city has existed, and I have watched nine kings wear that shape and eight of them die of it, and I will tell you what I believe. The fire is dying. The souls it burns give less each century—the founders' stock is spent, the later sales are thin gruel. Something on that throne believes a soul like yours—one steeped in immunity, marinated in the Court's own interest, fed on seven years of deliberate small poisonings until your blood itself became a distillation—might burn brighter than any fuel the Charter has produced. Might relight the flame entire. You weren't deferred, taster. You were *cured*. Like meat. Kept sweet for the feast that matters."

The willowbark came back up his throat and he mastered it, barely, sweat standing cold on his lip. Seven years. Seven years of doses measured by his own hand, proud of his discipline, proud of the callus his courage had built—and every dose had been seasoning. Every small sin had been marinade. Somewhere below, in the dark under his fear, a colder thought formed, precise as a scalpel:

*I did it to myself. They only held the cup. I drank.*

That was the worst of it. Not that he was owned. That he had been complicit in his own keeping, diligently, daily, with pride.

"The stars," he whispered. "Two nights ago. The wards falling. Was that—"

"A collection notice," Maren said. "The first open demand the Court has made of Cinderfall in a hundred years. The wards were the founders' collateral—now forfeit. The stars are the Court showing what it holds in escrow. And the summons that reached your pocket-charm was the delivery date." She met his eyes. "You were called home, account Galloway. The only question left—the question I have treasonously brought you down here to ask—is whether you intend to be *paid out* like the rest, or whether you intend to make a withdrawal."

He laughed. It came out wrong, jagged, half sob, and he let it, because there was no one down here to perform steadiness for except a woman who had just confessed to two centuries of complicity. "A withdrawal. From a bank that owns my soul. With what collateral?"

"With knowledge," Maren said. "With access. With the fact that you, uniquely, can stand in the flame-chamber without burning—which means you can reach what others cannot." She paused, and for the first time since he'd met her, the steward of the Ninefold Court looked afraid. "There is another account. Opened the same night as yours. Same seller. Same house of Saint Edel. Three marks."

The chamber went very quiet. Even the wall-veins of fire seemed to hold their breath.

He was five years old in Saint Edel's dormitory, and there had been forty children, and one of them had slept beside him and stolen his blanket and given it back warmed, and she had—

"Bryn," he said. The name came out of him like a tooth coming out, root and all. He hadn't spoken it aloud in twenty years; he'd buried it with the rest, never cast, never seen, never hoped, and certainly never *grieved*, because grief was a luxury item and he'd been born insolvent. "Her account. Where is her account. Is she—"

Maren turned toward the ledger, palms rising.

"Read it," Freya said, and his voice was not a taster's voice anymore, controlled and cool. It was a five-year-old's, flat on his back in the dark, listening to the wind off the plain. "*Read it.*"

Maren hesitated—hesitated, this woman who had faced down nine reigns—and then touched the stone.

"Ledger," she said. "Open the account of Galloway, Bryn."

The script gathered. Converged. Stood bright and terrible in the center of the slab, and Freya read the first line, and the second, and on the third line the ledger told him where his sister had been for twenty years, and what she had been *for*, and the word it used was **BURNING**, and beneath it, in the Court's merciless accounting hand: **REMAINING: LITTLE.**

Far down the corridor, deep in the mountain's heart, the central flame flickered—as if, somewhere inside all that fire, something small had heard her name.
