## 9. The Girl Who Was Also Sold

The stair down did not behave like a stair.

Ilsa Vane had spent eleven years learning how things behaved—how wards hummed at the frequency of a struck tuning fork, how iron filings trembled before a breach, how liars' hands went still while their mouths kept moving—and the stair beneath Cinderfall violated every category she owned. It went down for an hour and then, by every measure of her aching knees, another hour, and yet the air stayed warm and faintly sweet, like bread left to cool in a shuttered room. The walls were not cut stone. They were grown stone, ribbed and veined, and through those veins ran light: thin rivers of orange-gold that pulsed too slowly, like something breathing in its sleep.

Dragon-fire. The word arrived in her mind unbidden and unwelcome, from the oldest stratum of her training—the lessons given to ward-keepers before they were trusted with instruments. *Beneath everything,* old Master Corvel had said, tapping the mountain's shape on the survey map, *there is a furnace. That is all you need know. That is all anyone needs know.*

*That is all you were permitted to know*, she thought now, and hated how easily the correction came.

Ahead of her, Freya Galloway climbed down without looking back. He moved like a man descending into his own house in the dark—not confidently, exactly, but with the terrible familiarity of someone who has just learned the house was never his and cannot stop touching the walls anyway. His charms still glowed faintly at his wrists and throat, little constellations of borrowed light he no longer bothered to hide. Since the stars had gone out, since the wards had fallen with that sound like a cathedral of glass giving way, hiding had become a luxury of a vanished world.

"You're limping," she said, because saying nothing felt worse.

"I'm walking," he said. "It's different."

"Your left knee drags on the fourth step of every stride. You've been favoring it since the third landing." She heard her own voice and recognized it: the investigator's voice, flat and precise, deployed against fear the way sand was thrown against grease-fires. "I catalogued it. I catalogue things when I don't understand them."

He stopped on a step and half-turned. In the vein-light his face looked older than twenty-eight or whatever age he claimed; the shadows pooled in the hollows under his cheekbones like water finding low ground. "And what have you catalogued about me so far, Captain Vane?"

*A soul-mark reading as already dead inside.* A ledger-line where a heartbeat should be. A man whose file said *orphan, no kin, no history before age twelve* written in the same clerk's hand as forty other files she'd pulled last night from the archive, forty other children entered into the city's records on the same founding-day ritual, each one marked with the same small sigil she'd once believed was a blessing.

"A great deal," she said. "None of it fits together yet."

"That's the honest answer." He resumed climbing down. "I'll take honest answers where I can get them. Down here they seem to be the only currency that holds its value."

She followed. Her warrant burned against her ribs—or rather, the memory of it did; she had not yet burned it, not truly, only folded it smaller and smaller until it fit inside her breast pocket like a coal she was carrying toward water. Captain Ilsa Vane, Warden-Investigator Third Rank, was pursuing a fleeing suspect into the Ninefold Court beneath the mountain. Or: Captain Ilsa Vane had abandoned her post, her regent's sealed orders, and the entire scaffolding of law that had held her upright for thirty-four years, and was following a food-taster into the dark because he knew something about her city that she did not, and she could not bear the shape of not-knowing anymore.

Both sentences were true. She carried them both, the way one carries two buckets and walks level or spills everything.

* * *

The archive room announced itself before they reached it—a change in the air, dryness after sweetness, the smell of vellum and old tallow and something mineral, like the inside of a geode. Maren, the court's steward, stood waiting in a doorway of black wood carved with nine interlocking crowns, eight of them defaced, one polished bright.

"You brought your shadow," Maren said. She was tall in the way of people who had stopped caring whether they intimidated, silver-streaked hair bound back with plain cord, wearing the grey livery of a servant of a house that no longer existed. Her eyes went over Ilsa's uniform with something between pity and recognition. "A ward-keeper. Good. You'll want to see this more than he will. He thinks he already knows what he is. You don't yet know what your city is."

"I know what my city is," Ilsa said.

"Do you." Maren stepped aside from the doorway. "Then come tell me what this is."

The room beyond was round, and its walls were shelves from floor to unseen ceiling, and the shelves were full—not of books, though there were books, but of ledgers. Thousands of them. Each spine bore a year. Some of the years predated the city itself, which should have been impossible, and Ilsa felt the floor tilt gently under her certainties, the way a ship's deck tilts when you stop trusting your legs.

"The founding records," Freya said quietly. He had gone still in the way of a man hearing his own name spoken in a distant room. "You said—last night, through the charm—you said there were papers with my name."

"There are papers with everyone's name." Maren crossed to a lectern of dragon-bone, yellowed and dense as teeth, and opened a ledger across it. "Every charter, every covenant, every bargain the founders struck has its price recorded here. Your regents know of this room. They have always known. There is a reason the ward-keepers' first oath includes a clause about silence regarding 'the accounting below.' Did you never wonder why that phrase was left undefined?"

Ilsa had wondered. She had wondered for years and been told, each time, that wondering was the first symptom of corruption. Wonder less, Corvel had advised. Wonder is a door left open on a cold night.

Maren turned the ledger toward them. "Founding Day, Year One. The Charter of Cinderfall. Here is the grant of the mountain's shelter, the river's course, the fire-rights. And here—" her finger, blunt and ink-stained, stopped on a line, "—is the price."

Ilsa read it. Then she read it again, because the first reading refused to be true.

*Received in payment, per annum of the covenant: nine children, unclaimed, their names surrendered entire. First installment rendered this night.*

Nine children. Every year. For three hundred years.

"They weren't sold by slavers," Maren said, and her voice had gone quiet in the particular way voices go quiet in this room, hushed by the weight of the shelves. "They were sold by the city. By the founders, then by the regents, then by the orphanage masters who were paid a fee per head and told it was an apprenticeship placement. Unclaimed children—orphans, bastards, foundlings. Children no one would come looking for." Her eyes lifted to Freya. "Children like you."

Freya's hand had gone to the edge of the lectern. He held it the way a man holds a railing on a ship in weather. "My name," he said. "Show me my name."

Maren turned pages backward through years—Year Two, Year One—and there it was, in copperplate so fine it might have been engraved:

*Galloway, Freya. Age five years. No kin recorded. Name surrendered entire. Received by the Ninth Flame, account of the Founding Charter, final installment.*

"Final," Ilsa said, before she understood why the word snagged. "Why final?"

"Because the covenant was paid off that night," Maren said. "The city's debt was settled. After that year, no more children were required. The founders got their bargain complete, and the ledger closed." She paused, and Ilsa watched something move behind her face—regret, or its well-practiced imitation. "Except that the entry for that same night lists two children. Not nine. Two."

"Two," Freya repeated.

Maren turned the page.

Ilsa saw the second name before Freya did—or perhaps he saw it first and simply stopped functioning, which amounted to the same thing. The line sat there in its immaculate copperplate, patient as a knife on a table:

*Bryn Galloway. Age four years. Kin: sister to the above. Name surrendered entire. Received by the Central Flame, account of the Founding Charter, final installment.*

The room made a sound. It took Ilsa a moment to realize the sound was coming from Freya—a single exhale, long and controlled, the exhale of a man who had trained himself for seven years to show nothing to the poisoners of kings, and who was now showing everything to an empty archive because there was no one left to perform for. His knuckles had gone white on the lectern's edge.

"Bryn," he said. The name came out broken in the middle, like a stick stepped on. "Her name was Bryn. She used to—I had a—" He stopped. Started again, lower. "I thought I imagined her. I thought I'd invented a sister out of loneliness, the way children do. There was no record. The orphanage said there was no record. I was five. What was I going to do, sue them?" A laugh came out of him with nothing in it. "They erased her. They didn't even need magic. Paper was enough."

*I have seen this mark before,* Ilsa thought, and the memory rose with the taste of ash. The instrument readings from the interrogation cell, the soul-gauge that had swung past *diminished*, past *severed*, into a region at the bottom of the dial labeled only with the old ward-keeper's glyph for *rendered*. She had read that gauge as evidence of guilt. She had written in her report: *Subject displays post-mortem soul-signature; method unknown; recommend continued detention.* She had looked at a stolen child and filed him as an anomaly.

"How many," she said aloud, and her voice sounded like someone else's. "How many entries like hers. Central Flame."

Maren walked the shelf-wall with her lamp, counting spines, and Ilsa realized the ledgers were not organized by year alone. Whole sections bore the same heading stamped in gold leaf worn to a ghost: *CENTRAL FLAME — SUSTENANCE ACCOUNT*. Hundreds of volumes. Thousands of names.

"Three hundred years," Maren said. "At first only the founding installments. But the fire is hungry, Captain. Dragon-fire does not burn wood or coal. It burns what remembers. A name surrendered entire is fuel that burns slowly—a century or more, if the flame is fed regularly. So the payments never truly ended. They changed shape. Accidents. Fever-deaths in the foundling wing. Children who 'ran away.' Your city has been feeding its light with its unwanted for three centuries, and calling the missing entries clerical errors."

"And the flame," Ilsa said. Each word cost her something and she paid anyway. "Where is this central flame."

"Below. At the root of the mountain, where the veins all run together." Maren closed the sustenance ledger with a soft, terrible gentleness. "You asked me once, through your instruments, whether the wards kept magic out of Cinderfall. Now you can ask the better question. The wards kept the fire's reach contained. Kept it from rising up through the roots of the city and taking names wherever it pleased. Your founders didn't build a wall against enemies, Captain. They built a dam around a debt. And dams," she added, "require maintenance, and maintenance requires belief, and your regents have let the belief lapse for want of anyone willing to say aloud what the dam holds back."

Ilsa looked down at Bryn Galloway's line. *Kin: sister to the above.* Four years old. Someone had written *sister* in the kinship column, which meant someone, at some point, had known the two children belonged together, and had sold them anyway, separately, into two different fires.

Grief arrived ahead of understanding. That was the only way she could ever afterward describe it: grief came first, whole and physical, a fist closing around her sternum—for the girl she had never met, for the boy in front of her who had spent seven years believing himself invented, for the hundreds of thousands of names on these shelves burning one by one in the dark so that Cinderfall could have streetlamps—and understanding trailed after it, slow and unwilling, assembling itself out of the pieces: *this is what the wards were for, this is what the silence was for, this is what I was for.* She had spent her life maintaining the dam. She had arrested a man for the crime of being stolen.

"Freya," she said.

He wasn't listening. He had both palms flat on the open ledger now, framing his sister's name, and the charmwork at his wrists had flared bright enough to cast his shadow huge against the shelved years. When he spoke, his voice was the voice from the interrogation cell—the flat one, the dangerous one, the one that had recited his rules like scripture. *Never cast. Never be seen. Never hope.*

"She's not dead," he said. "The steward said the fire doesn't kill what it takes. It spends it. Slowly. Which means some of her is still—"

"An ember," Maren said. "Small, and growing smaller. The Central Flame is guttering—that is why your stars went out, why the wards fell. The fire is dying, and it is consuming what remains of its fuel faster, trying to keep itself lit. If it gutters out entirely, every name it holds is released—but into darkness, and perhaps into nothing. If it is fed, the names burn to the end." She met Ilsa's eyes. "Your regent's order, the one you carry folded in your pocket like a stone you haven't decided to throw—seal the mountain, starve the flame, let the court die in the dark. Have you read what that means for the souls inside it?"

Isla had not. She had read the order eleven times and each time stopped at the word *containment*. "Tell me."

"Maren." Freya's voice cracked across the room like a whip of light. "Take me to the flame. Now. Tonight. Whatever's left of her, I want to see it with my own eyes."

"You are not ready for what you'll see," Maren said. "No one is. I wasn't, and I've served here thirty years."

"I tasted poison for kings for seven years," Freya said, and there was nothing of the charming black-market boy left in him at all; he had burned that identity away somewhere on the stairs, and what stood in the archive was older and harder and five years old all at once. "I have been swallowing what kills me my whole life. Take me down."

Maren looked at Ilsa. It was, Ilsa understood dimly, a question—the last one, perhaps, that would ever be put to Warden-Investigator Third Rank Vane by anyone holding authority over her. *Your prisoner is asking to go deeper. Your orders say seal the mountain. Your city says these children were fuel. Choose.*

Her hand went to her breast pocket. Through the fabric she could feel the warrant's edges, small now, folded to the size of a coal.

"Captain," Freya said, softer. "You don't owe me anything. You could walk back up. Report that I descended alone. Keep your rank, your law, your—" He gestured vaguely at her, at the uniform, at the whole architecture of duty. "Whatever it is that holds you up."

"That's just it," Ilsa said. She unfolded the warrant. She read it once more—*by order of the Lord Regent, containment of the subterranean anomaly supersedes recovery of personnel or assets*—and heard, finally, the word *assets*, and understood that the regent who signed it had read these same shelves, or enough of them, and had chosen the word *assets* anyway.

*Assets*, she thought. *Bryn Galloway, aged four, asset.*

She held the warrant's corner to Maren's lamp until it caught.

The paper burned quickly—it was good vellum, city-made, meant to last—and the light of it lit her face for three seconds, and then the ash dropped from her fingers onto three hundred years of ledgers, and Captain Ilsa Vane stood in the Ninefold Court's archive with no rank, no orders, and no law between herself and the truth except the truth itself.

"Take us both down," she said.

Maren studied the ash a moment. Something in her face loosened—not warmth, exactly, but the relaxation of a woman setting down a weight she'd carried alone for decades. "Then you should know what you're walking toward," she said, lifting the lamp. "The thing on the throne wears a king's shape, and it has been waiting a very long time for a Galloway to come home. Both of you. It keeps accounts, and it considers your family"—her eyes touched the open ledger, the two lines, brother and sister, sold the same night—"an outstanding balance."

Freya was already moving toward the door, toward the downward dark, toward the ember that had been his sister. Ilsa followed him, ash still on her fingertips, grief arriving everywhere ahead of understanding, and far below them—so deep it was less a sound than a pulse in the bones of the world—the central flame breathed in, and held, and began, very slowly, to burn a little faster.
