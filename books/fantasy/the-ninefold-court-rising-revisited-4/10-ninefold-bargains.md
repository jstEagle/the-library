## 10. Ninefold Bargains

They came for him at what the deep roads called morning, though no sun had ever reached beneath the mountain, and the hour was marked only by the way the dragon-fire veins in the walls dimmed and brightened, like the slow breathing of something enormous asleep. Freya had not slept. He had spent the dark hours in a stone cell that was not quite a cell—there was no door, only an archway hung with curtains of woven silver that hummed when touched—and he had passed the time the way he passed all waiting: by counting.

Seven years of small sins had taught him that counting kept fear at a distance. Count the exits: one archway, one seam in the floor where water had once run, one high vent breathing cold air that smelled of iron and old snow. Count your assets: a tongue trained to detect ninety-one poisons and forty-odd venoms, a body so steeped in slow toxin that most magic slid off it like rain off oiled leather, a pocket-charm gone silent since Maren's summons, and a conscience he had buried so long ago he sometimes forgot where. Count your debts. That list was shorter and much heavier. One name on a ledger, sold at age five by the people who fed him thin soup and called it charity. One sister whose existence he had recovered only hours ago, her soul burning somewhere ahead of him in the dark.

The curtains parted without being drawn.

The thing that stood there wore the shape of a court herald—or perhaps it *was* a court herald, for Freya had learned in his short descent that beneath the mountain the distinction between a creature and its office was often meaningless. It was tall and grey-robed, and its face was smooth as an egg with features pressed into it afterward: eyes too far apart, a mouth placed slightly off-center, as if the maker had worked from memory rather than life.

"Freya Galloway," it said, and his name sounded wrong in its mouth, syllables handled like unfamiliar coins. "The Ninth Seat is prepared. You will walk before us."

"And if I don't?"

The herald considered this with what seemed genuine curiosity. "Then we will carry you. But you would prefer to walk. Everyone prefers to walk. It is one of the few things they all have in common."

It was such an honest answer that Freya almost smiled. He rose, straightened his tunic—he was still wearing the palace kitchen whites, absurdly, a food-taster's uniform in the hall of the Ninefold Court—and walked.

* * *

The throne room had been built by something that understood scale but not comfort.

The chamber opened around him the way a cathedral opens around a candle-flame: suddenly, vertiginously, so that his eyes needed several breaths to find the walls. Nine thrones rose on nine tiers of black stone, arranged in a crescent like a jaw of teeth. Eight were empty—not merely unoccupied but *unoccupied*, vacant in a way that made the air around them seem thinner, as though the seats themselves were holding their breaths. Veins of dragon-fire ran up the tiers between them, guttering low, casting shadows that moved wrong, lagging behind the bodies that cast them.

On the ninth tier, alone, sat the king.

He looked, from a distance, like a man—a tall man in robes of ash-grey silk, crowned with a circlet of dull iron. But distance was kind to him. As Freya approached, walking the long aisle between pillars carved with names he could not read, the king's edges began to misbehave. The light did not settle on him properly. His hands, folded on the armrests, were elegant and terrible, fingers too long by half, each fingertip faintly translucent, glowing from within like paper held before a flame.

Behind the throne, Freya saw it—the source of everything, the wound in the world. A shaft of fire rose through the chamber's heart, wide as a well, climbing toward some distant chimney in the mountain's crown. The central flame. Bryn's flame. Even from here he could feel it pulling at him, a hunger so old and patient it had become indistinguishable from gravity.

Maren waited at the foot of the ninth tier. She looked smaller here, the renegade steward in her patched steward's coat, her hands clasped to keep them from shaking. Her eyes flicked to Freya with something that was not quite apology.

"You came," she murmured. "I told them you'd come willingly. They didn't believe me. Willing things are rare down here."

"They should believe you," Freya said quietly. "Willingness is about all I've got left."

The king spoke, and his voice was the strangest thing yet—not heard so much as *remembered*, arriving inside the skull like a recollection of a voice Freya had never known.

*You are the one on the ledger.*

"I'm told I am." He kept his eyes on a point just left of the king's crown. In the palace kitchens he had learned never to meet a dangerous man's gaze directly; you watched the hands, the posture, the angle of the shoulders. Here there were no hands to watch. There was only the fire behind the throne, and the hollow eight seats, and the sense of standing inside a mouth.

*Eight of us were,* said the king. *Once. Now I keep the fire alone, and the fire keeps me, and we are both nearly finished.* The translucent fingers flexed. *You want something. All of you surface-thieves want something when you finally crawl down to look at what your grandfathers bought with our lives. Speak it.*

"Bryn," Freya said. "Bryn Galloway. Sold the same night as me. Her soul is in that fire. I want her back."

Silence, then—not empty silence but weighted silence, the silence of a ledger being opened somewhere very far away. When the king spoke again, there was something new in the remembered voice. Interest. Appetite.

*A bargain, then. Good. We have not bargained honestly in three hundred years. Everything since has been collection.* The king raised one hand, and the fire behind him flared. *But we do not give gifts to property. And you—we hold your deed. Your soul bears our mark; your body carries our investment. Seven years of poison, taster. Do you think that was your cleverness? We fed you immunity the way a farmer feeds a calf, and now the calf walks into the house on its own legs. There is poetry in it.*

The words landed harder than Freya expected. He had suspected—Ilsa had shown him enough—but suspicion was a coat you could wear; hearing it spoken aloud in the voice of your owner was another thing entirely. He let the blow land and did not stagger. Seven years of tasting had taught him that too.

"Then let's talk terms," he said.

*Terms.* The king inclined his head, and the gesture was almost human, almost courteous, which made it worse. *Very well. The old form. Nine cups will be brought. Eight bear false oaths—words that bind nothing, promises that dissolve like smoke. One bears the true oath of the Ninefold Court, sworn as it was sworn at the founding, binding on both parties equally, breakable by neither. Find the true cup, drink from it, and we will negotiate the girl's ransom in good faith. Choose wrongly, and you drink a lie—and a lie, once swallowed, becomes yours to speak forever. You will leave this mountain carrying our words in your mouth, and you will say what we wish, when we wish, to whom we wish.*

Freya's stomach went cold. He thought of Captain Vane, waiting somewhere above or below with her burned warrant and her terrible honesty. A puppet's tongue. That was the trap, then—not death, worse than death: *usefulness*, the one thing he had spent seven years pretending to be on his own terms, turned inside out.

"And if I refuse the test altogether?"

*Then you are property attempting to negotiate with its holder, and we will discuss the matter with your soul directly. It is more pliant than you.* A pause, and something that might, in a man, have been a smile. *But you will not refuse. You are a taster. Tasting is what you are for.*

That was the deepest cut of all, delivered with the flat precision of someone reading from a ledger. Freya felt the old loneliness rise in him—the orphan-boy loneliness, the one he'd papered over with charmwork and competence—and he let it rise and pass through him and out, the way he'd been taught by a hedge-witch named Odell when he was nine years old and crying over his first stolen spell.

*Breathe through it, boy. Fear's just another flavor. Learn it, and it can't surprise you.*

"Bring the cups," he said.

* * *

They brought them on a tray of black glass, borne by two grey-robed figures whose faces were smooth as the herald's, and set it upon a low table before the throne's dais.

Nine cups.

No two alike, and that, Freya understood immediately, was the first cruelty of it. There was no pattern to reject. One was horn, one clay, one silver gone black with tarnish, one of clear crystal, one of wood dark with age, one of iron, one of green jade, one of plain glazed earthenware chipped at the rim, and one of bone. They stood in a row, and the dragon-fire light moved across them, and every one of them looked exactly as honest and exactly as false as its neighbors.

Above the table, letters appeared in the air, written in fire-script that hurt to read directly:

*NINE CUPS. ONE TRUE OATH. CHOOSE WITH YOUR FAMOUS TONGUE.*

"They're laughing," Maren breathed beside him. "Freya, listen to me—" Her hand found his sleeve. "There's no way to taste an oath. An oath isn't a substance. I've seen scholars try to codify this ritual for centuries and the texts disagree even on whether the true cup changes position. Some say the court chooses after you choose. Some say—"

"Some say it can't be won," Freya finished.

"Some say it was never meant to be won. Only survived."

*I will remind the steward,* the king's voice said, mild as milk, *that she is present at our sufferance, and that her own ledger entry remains open.*

Maren went pale and silent.

Freya stepped forward to the table alone.

And here is what he knew, what no scholar under three hundred years of failed scholarship had known, because none of them had stood where he stood: they had all tried to solve this as a puzzle of *magic*. But Freya Galloway had solved a thousand small cruelties as a problem of *people*. Poisoners were predictable. Kings were predictable. Even immortal hungry things wearing kings' shapes were predictable, because pride was the one flavor that never varied.

The court wanted him to fail. More than that—it wanted to *watch* him fail. It had staged this theater, nine cups and fire-script and a wager of his tongue itself, because it believed its own legend: that no surface-dweller could tell truth from falsehood in the house of lies. Every detail of the scene was designed to overwhelm—to make him count cups instead of reading the room.

So he stopped counting the cups and started counting everything else.

The tray of black glass: cold, except—there, at the second position, where the crystal cup stood—the faintest warmth blooming under his palm when he held his hand near the tray's underside. Not much. A hearthstone holds heat longer than a wall. Something *had* rested in or near the crystal cup longer than the others.

No. Too easy. That was either the answer or bait laid for a taster clever enough to check the tray. He filed it away and kept reading.

The grey-robed cup-bearers: their smooth faces gave nothing, but their *hands* did. The right-hand bearer had set down cups one through five; the left-hand bearer, six through nine. The right bearer's fingertips were smudged—faintly, invisibly to any eye but a taster's, trained to spot a grain of powder on a palace spoon—with residue at positions one, four, and five. Handling-residue. Wax, or something like wax. Sealant. Three cups sealed, six open? Or three cups touched twice?

And the fire-script overhead: it had said *choose with your famous tongue*, and everyone in the room assumed the test was the choosing. But Freya had spent seven years learning how instructions were weapons. The script was part of the trap. It steered him toward tasting the *contents*—and whatever was in those cups was not wine. He bent close, carefully, breathing shallow over the row, and caught it: a scent thread-fine beneath the mineral smell of old stone, sweetish, green, like sap from a cut stem.

*Emberwort.* Odell had ground it for him once, to show him why hedge-witches died young. Emberwort masked everything else. Under it, a good nose could find almost anything—and emberwort itself was the classic vehicle for oath-binding draughts, because it burned away clean in the body and left no trace for later diviners to find. Every cup probably held it. Which meant scent would tell him nothing, and the court knew it, and the invitation to *taste* was the cruelest joke of all: the moment he sipped the wrong cup, the lie was in him.

Nine cups. No safe sip. The whole design said so.

Which meant the true cup was not the one to be drunk safely. It was the one to be *chosen*—and choosing was not done with the tongue.

"The rules," Freya said aloud, his voice steady in the vast hush, "as written above us, say I must choose with my tongue. Very well." He lifted his head and addressed the king directly, risking the gaze at last. "But your founding charter was drafted by my city's lawyers as much as by your court, and I've tasted contracts before—metaphorically, at least. A charter binds *both* parties. So I claim the taster's oldest right: the right of the assay. I will not swallow. I will assay each cup as a taster assays—by craft, not by swallowing poison on faith. Unless your true oath requires blindness. Does it? Does the Ninefold Court swear honestly only to liars?"

He felt the question land. Somewhere among the empty thrones, a sound like wind through a keyhole.

*Clever calf,* the king said, and the remembered voice had sharpened. *Assay, then. But know this: the assay itself is witnessed. Whatever method you use, use it before us all. No hidden charms. No whispered counsel.* The translucent fingers gestured at Maren. *Step back, steward.*

Maren withdrew to the foot of the dais, her eyes full of warnings she couldn't speak.

Freya stood alone before nine cups, before a hungry god and its hollow court, and did the only thing he had ever been truly great at.

He worked.

First, the tray again, openly, tilting it slowly toward the firelight so the whole court could see: condensation patterns. Cups recently filled showed a fine mist on their inner walls; older fillings had dried to a ring. Five cups bore fresh mist. Four were dry-ringed. Of the dry ones—the horn, the iron, the jade, and the bone—one had a ring pattern that repeated, a tide-mark of many fillings and emptyings, as if it had been used before, rehearsed, *practiced with*. Rituals got rehearsed. Honest ones got performed once.

He said none of this aloud. Saying it aloud would teach the court his methods, and a taster who reveals his methods tastes his last meal.

Second: he asked for water. This caused a stir he found deeply informative. *Why would a taster need water?* the hesitation among the bearers asked. Because a palate cleanses between samples—that was plausible cover. But also because he wanted to see which cup they'd pour the water *from*, and whether any vessel in this room was trusted enough for drinking. The bearers fetched water in a plain ewer, unremarkable, unmarked—and poured it without hesitation. Nothing in it. Fine. But the ease of their pouring told him these creatures did not fear accidental contamination. They feared only *detection*. Which meant the true oath-cup would not be poisoned at all. It would be *protected*—warded, sealed, made untouchable by anything less than willing consent.

Which brought him back to the wax-smudged fingertips. Positions one, four, and five. He walked the row slowly, openly, a craftsman inspecting tools, and at the fourth cup—the dark wood, plain and unlovely among its flashier neighbors—he saw it. Not wax on the cup. Wax *in* the cup's rim, worked into the grain, ancient, colorless: a seal that had been broken and remade so many times the wood had learned it. The other eight cups were props, replaced per ceremony, their surfaces too clean, too new in their dishonesty. The wooden cup was old in the way only a working tool gets old.

Everything said the wooden cup. The warmth on the tray said the crystal. The rehearsal-marks said the horn. Three answers, mutually exclusive, all plausible, all laid out like a spread of poisons—which meant at least two were bait, and possibly all three, and the real answer was the one nobody's evidence pointed to, because the court had assumed he'd follow evidence.

The court had assumed he'd think like a magician.

Freya closed his eyes and thought like a scullery maid instead. Think of the person who has to *carry* the tray. Who has to lift it, walk it across a throne room, set it down before a king, and stand there through the whole long ceremony. What does the carrier avoid? Spills. What spills? What sloshes when a tray tips—and trays always tip, always, ask any servant alive. A carrier trusts the heavy cup, the wide-based cup, the cup that won't turn over when her elbow jars against a doorframe.

He looked at the row again, forgetting everything arcane, seeing only a serving problem.

The bone cup would tip in a strong draft. The crystal was stable but slippery. The horn was light. The clay was chipped and sat unevenly—he checked, pressing gently near its base: it rocked. A carrier hates a rocking cup.

The iron cup sat solid as a hammerhead. Heavy, ugly, base wide, center of gravity low. If Freya had been handed this tray in the Cinderfall kitchens and told to cross a crowded hall, his hand would have gone to the iron cup first, to anchor it.

And the iron cup was the one the fire-script's glow fell on *least*—he verified it now, watching how the light moved: the script above shed its brightness evenly across the row except for a small, deliberate penumbra over the iron, a shadow no one would notice unless they'd spent seven years noticing how light behaved around things people wanted overlooked. Courtiers hid jewels in plain sight. Liars hid truths the same way: not concealed, merely *unglamorous*.

Heavy, stable, ignored. The cup a servant would trust and a king would overlook.

Or it was the finest bait yet, and he was about to swear his tongue to a hungry god on the strength of a scullerymaid's instincts.

Freya laughed—short, quiet, mostly at himself. Then he picked up the iron cup, and the court went still as held breath.

"Not drinking," he said clearly, for the record, for the witnesses, for the ledger. "Assaying."

He tipped a single drop onto his palm—the taster's assay, the drop-test Odell had taught him on his twelfth birthday, the one skill that had kept him alive through three assassination attempts and a hundred palace dinners. A true oath, freely offered and binding both ways, was contract-ink: it would *take* to living skin, bite in, stain. A false oath was performance, smoke and emberwort: it would sit on the skin like oil, pretty and uncommitted.

The drop hit his callused palm.

It bit. Cold, then hot, then a fine bright sting like nettle-silk, sinking past the layers of scar and slow-poison toughness that seven years had built, finding skin that had never been armored because no one had ever thought to poison him *there*. Where the drop sank, a mark surfaced—brief, temporary, a line of script in a language of interlocking knots, legible for three heartbeats and no more.

Three heartbeats was enough. Freya could not read old court-script. But he could read *structure*, the grammar of bindings, and what lay in his palm had the structure of a mutual clause—two columns of knot-work, mirrored, equal-weight, the signature architecture of a covenant that bound both signatories alike. He had seen its like once before, in miniature, in the founding charter fragment Ilsa had shown him in her office by lamplight, the night his whole life had come apart.

The iron cup held the true oath.

He set it down. His palm throbbed where the drop had bitten, a small red knot already fading. Around the chamber, the eight empty thrones creaked—actually creaked, like old ships—and the fire behind the king's shoulders stood up taller, as if listening.

"Well?" Maren whispered.

"The iron cup," Freya said. "Your oath is in the ugliest cup on the tray. You hid it the way honest things always get hidden—by being no fun to look at."

For a long moment the king did not move. Then the remembered voice filled the chamber, and it was different now—not milder, never milder, but *engaged*, the way a master chess-player leans forward when the game stops being a lesson.

*Three hundred years,* the king said, *since anyone chose rightly. And you chose as a servant chooses. Of course. We forgot that servants see the room while lords see the stage.* The translucent fingers unfolded, one by one, a gesture of formal attention. *Very well, taster. The true oath stands open. Name your audience, and we will bargain in good faith for the soul called Bryn. Audience rights are granted: you may speak, propose, and withdraw thrice. And in exchange—for the oath must bind both ways, as you yourself insisted—*

Here the king paused, and Freya felt the trap close around his ankle, gentle as a manacle lined with velvet.

*—you owe the Ninefold Court one favor. Unnamed. Uncalled until we name it. Sworn under the same true oath you have just assayed. Refuse, and there is no bargain, no audience, no ransom. The fire is patient. Are you?*

The chamber waited. Maren's face was white. Far above, through leagues of stone, Freya imagined the starless sky, and Ilsa somewhere under it with her burned warrant, and the city that had sold him before he could walk.

One favor, unnamed. Every instinct he owned screamed the arithmetic of it: an unnamed favor owed to an immortal creditor was not a debt, it was a hook. And every instinct also told him the alternative: Bryn, nearly spent, a candle-flame in a well of fire, her name already thinning from the world's memory.

Victory that feels like a trap is still victory, he told himself. Take the win. Survive the hook. That was rule number one of the kitchens: get through tonight; deal with tomorrow's poison tomorrow.

"I swear it," Freya said, and took up the iron cup, and drank.

The oath went down like winter. It did not burn—it *witnessed*, settling into him with a cold thoroughness, taking his measure, writing his name into a column beside the court's. For one dizzy instant he felt the entire shape of the bargain: himself, the king, the fire, the ledger, all of it one machine of exchange older than the city, and him a new gear cut into it, small and load-bearing.

Then it was done. The cup was empty. The fire-script overhead dissolved into sparks.

*Audience granted,* said the king, and leaned forward on the ninth throne, and the light behind him gathered like a drawn breath. *Now, taster. Let us speak of your sister.*

And as the bargaining began—as the king named his first price, and it was not a price of gold or souls but something far stranger, something that made Maren inhale sharply and step forward despite herself—Freya understood, with the particular clarity of a man who has just tasted something he cannot spit out, that the favor he had sworn would be called sooner than any of them dreamed.

Perhaps even before he left the mountain.
