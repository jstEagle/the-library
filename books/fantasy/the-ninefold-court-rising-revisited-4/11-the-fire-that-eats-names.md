## 11. The Fire That Eats Names

Maren had kept the ledger room clean for three hundred years, and in all that time she had never once opened the great book for a living guest.

She did so now by lamplight, because the Ninefold Court had no other light to give. The veins of dragon-fire that threaded the mountain's deep roads burned low and amber in their channels, and where they passed beneath the floor of the ledger room their glow came up through the seams of the stone like heat through the skin of a fevered sleeper. The lamplight was hers — a small, deliberate rebellion, oil carried down in her own hands, wick trimmed with her own scissors, because a steward who could not choose how her workroom was lit was no steward at all but a hand moving at another's command.

The taster sat across the table from her, his wrists still ringed faintly with the glow of his own charms, watching the book the way men watch a snake.

"You may touch it," Maren said. "It has already read you."

Freya Galloway did not touch it. He had not touched much of anything since she had turned the pages back to the year of the city's founding — back to the column where a child's name stood written in ink that had gone brown as old blood: *Galloway, Freya, aged five years, sold by the Sisters of the Foundling Hearth, price one founding charter, paid in full.* His face had gone very still when he first saw it, stiller than grief, stiller than shock. It was the stillness of a man doing arithmetic.

"Interest," he said now. His voice came out level, which cost him something; Maren could see it costing him, see it in the small muscles around his jaw. "Seven years of immunity. You've been paying interest on me."

"The court does not think of it as generosity." Maren folded her hands on the table. She had learned, over centuries, to keep her hands still when what moved beneath them wanted to shake. "Property that is well-kept appreciates. A soul held in reserve must be maintained against the day it is called due. Your body was fed, sheltered, hardened against every poison that might have taken you before your value matured. Every meal you ever tasted without dying was the court keeping its collateral sound."

"And my sister."

The name he did not say. Maren respected that. Names were currency here, and he was learning quickly not to spend them aloud in this place.

"She is further along," Maren said. "In the flame."

* * *

She took him to see it, because she had decided — three hundred years ago, or perhaps only last night; time ran strangely down here — that a man who was going to make a choice ought to know what he was choosing between. She led him along the fire-road to the chamber the court called the Vigil Hall, though no one had held vigil in it for an age except the thing upon the throne, and what it held vigil over could not be called holy by any honest mouth.

The central flame rose from a basin of black iron wide as a millpond. It did not leap like any fire Freya would have known from hearths above. It breathed. It swelled and drew in slow, patient rhythm, the way a sleeping animal breathes, and its color was wrong for warmth — a deep gold shading into white at the heart, and at the edges, where the light thinned, a color like the last moment of dusk.

"That is dragon-fire," Maren said. "Or what is left of it. The dragons are long dead. This is the last of the last clutch, burned down to its essence and fed ever since, generation after generation, so that it would not go out."

"Fed what?"

Maren looked at the flame for a long moment before answering. It was not reluctance, exactly. It was the pause of a woman measuring out a dose she knew would burn.

"Souls," she said.

The taster said nothing. He had gone into that particular stillness again, the one she suspected he wore above like armor.

"Not bodies. Not lives taken violently — the court has never been crude. Souls given over willingly by contract, or sold by others on the signer's behalf, as yours was sold. They come down the same road you walked, though they do not walk it. And the flame takes them one at a time, very slowly, over decades, over centuries. A soul burns the way a candle burns. And while it burns—" she turned to look at him directly, because this next part mattered more than everything else she would say, and she needed to see whether he understood it "—the world forgets."

"Forgets what?"

"The name. Everything the name touched. A soul spent in the flame is erased backward through memory. Not killed — nothing so kind. Unremembered. The mother who bore such a soul wakes some morning unable to say why the second cradle makes her weep. The friend who loved such a soul feels a hole shaped like a person and calls it melancholy. Songs lose verses. Ledgers lose lines. The world heals over each absence like flesh over a splinter, smooth and unscarred, and no one above ever knows what was taken, because the taking includes the knowing."

Freya's hand had risen, without his seeming to notice, to the pocket where he kept his charms. Maren watched him find it empty — watched him remember that his own name stood written in a book downstairs, and that a book could be amended.

"Bryn," he said, and broke his own rule, and spoke the name into the burning air. "If she's nearly spent—"

"Then there are people who loved her who cannot tell you why their kitchens feel too large," Maren said gently. "There may be a grave above with her name worn off the stone, and no one able to say why the wearing-off troubled them. There may be songs in Cinderfall missing their middle verses. The flame has been eating her for twenty years, taster. What remains of her is small. But it remains. I have checked."

"How?" The word cracked out of him harder than he meant it, and she let it lie where it fell. "How do you check? How do you even measure—"

"Because keeping count is the only treason available to a steward." Maren turned back to the flame. Its breathing filled the hall, vast and slow, and she hated it with a hatred so old it had gone smooth as river stone. "I was appointed to keep the ledgers. So I kept them. All of them. Including the ones the court believes exist nowhere but in its own memory. I have recorded every soul that entered this flame for three hundred years. Their names, so far as names could be recovered. Their ages. The terms of their sale. And the date their ember gutters out — which I mark in advance, because the arithmetic of souls is exact, and the court's own accountants are lazy."

She drew a slim black notebook from her coat — paper, real paper, hoarded against the court's preference for vellum and memory — and opened it to a page marked with a slip of ribbon faded almost white.

"Galloway, Bryn," she read. "Sold the same night as her brother, by the same signers, under the same charter clause. Entered the flame at age five. Present remainder: less than a tenth part. At current rate of consumption, her ember goes dark within the year. Perhaps within two seasons."

The silence that followed belonged to the fire. It breathed in, and the whole hall leaned toward it, and breathed out, and the shadows relaxed.

"There is a way to take a soul back," Maren said. "Out of turn. Before its term. The vessel would have to be made — charmwork of a delicacy I have seen attempted twice in three centuries, and both attempts are recorded in my ledger as deaths. But it can be done. In theory."

"In theory," Freya repeated. He was looking at the flame now, and Maren saw in his face the beginning of the thing she had brought him here to see: not horror yet, but the ground sloping toward it. "And if it's done. If her soul comes out whole. What happens to the fire?"

"Ah," Maren said. "Now we arrive at it."

She walked the hall's circumference as she spoke, her footsteps precise on the stone, because movement helped her say the unsayable things.

"The dragon-fire does not warm this court alone. Follow the veins upward and you will find they branch, and the branches rise, and somewhere under your city they thin to threads no wider than a hair — and those threads hold up the border wards. The wards did not fall because they failed, taster. They flickered because the flame guttered. The night the stars went out, the fire drew breath deeper than usual, and the wards starved for a moment, and everything your founders built to hide this place went briefly transparent. The court is starving. The flame is dying. And everything above — your sun, such as it is, your stars, your seasons, the walls that keep the wild dark off your city — all of it runs, at root, on this one fire, and this one fire runs on souls."

"So the choices are," Freya said slowly, "keep feeding it, or watch it die."

"Yes."

"And if it dies?"

Maren stopped walking. She faced the flame full-on, letting its dying gold wash over her, because she had earned the right to look at what she intended to kill.

"If it dies, the wards go with it. The stars stay dark — the stars are already mostly memory, did you know that? The sky above your city is a ward-projection, and it runs on this. Whatever lives in the wild dark beyond the borders will no longer be kept off. The cold will come down out of the mountains that has no name in any language still spoken. Cities will suffer. People will die. Possibly many. Possibly, in the long centuries after, nearly all."

"And that is the choice the court offers everyone," Freya said. "Feed us, or the world ends."

"The court would put it exactly so." Maren smiled, and it was not a kind expression; she had not practiced kindness in a very long time. "Feed us, or the world ends. It is a good bargain, as bargains go. It has held for a thousand years. It is holding right now, with your sister as fuel and your name as collateral, and every soul in my ledger as kindling, and the whole bright fragile surface of the world balanced on a single guttering flame that must be fed forever."

"But you don't believe it should be fed."

"I believe," Maren said, "that a fire built on stolen children should be allowed to go out."

She said it quietly. The flame did not react; it was beyond hearing anything so small. But Freya looked at her as though she had struck a bell.

"You want it dead."

"I want the debt ended. There is a difference, and it matters." She closed her notebook. "I have served this court three hundred years, taster. I have entered ten thousand names into ledgers, and I have watched perhaps four hundred embers gutter out, and I have told myself each time that the alternative was darkness, was the end of cities, was the death of everything warm. And then one century it occurred to me to ask: who taught us that the only fuel for light is what we steal? Who wrote that law, and why were they never questioned, and what kind of god demands children and calls it arithmetic?" Her voice stayed level. It always stayed level. That was the discipline of her life. "A fire that must eat souls to burn is not a hearth. It is an appetite. And appetites that are fed do not stop growing. The court's demands have doubled in my tenure. Did you know that? Twice the souls per century, for the same light. We are feeding it faster to get less, and calling it salvation."

"And your answer?"

"To smother it." She met his eyes. "Deliberately. Soon. While I still can. There are dampers in the old veins — the founders built them, in the days when they still imagined they might someday want to stop. I have spent sixty years restoring them. I can close the flame in an hour. The souls still burning within it would be freed — released whole, unburned, returned to whatever waiting exists for such things. Every name in my ledger restored to the world's memory in a single night. And the wards would fail, and the stars would stay dark, and the cold would come, and the world above would learn in one terrible season what its comfort had been built on."

Freya was silent for a long time. The fire breathed. Somewhere far off in the mountain, a vein of it pulsed once, amber, like a heartbeat skipping.

"People will die," he said at last.

"Yes."

"Children."

"Yes."

"And you'd do it anyway."

"I have done the arithmetic," Maren said. "That is what a steward does. Three hundred years of stolen souls against one hard winter — no. Not one winter. Generations, perhaps. Against the certain continuation of an appetite that ends only when everything is consumed." She spread her hands, and for the first time he could see how old they were, older than her face, older than her voice. "I am not asking you to agree. I am telling you what I intend, because you are the first living soul in three hundred years to stand in this hall with a reason of his own to act, and I am tired of conspiring with no one."

"You're telling me," Freya said, "because you want something from me."

"I am a steward. Everything I do, I do in trade." Almost, she smiled. "I want your help opening the damper. And I want it soon — sooner than your sister's term, if her ember is to be saved. Because once I close the flame, taster, there is no reopening it. No negotiation, no bargain, no ninth cup offered and won. The court will fight. The thing on the throne will fight. And the world will be darker than it has ever been, and freer than it has ever been, and I will not pretend to know whether that trade is just. I only know that someone must finally make it, and I am the only one positioned to make it, and I have been making ready for longer than your city has had its current name."

He looked at the flame a long while. Maren let him look. She knew what he was seeing — she had stood where he stood, in her first century, and looked, and wept, and gone back to her ledgers. He was seeing his sister in it. He was seeing the small gold remainder of a girl sold at five, burning down toward nothing in a hall where nobody had ever wept for her until possibly this minute, in the person of a brother who had not known she existed until yesterday.

And she saw him arrive, slowly, at the thought she had arrived at herself three hundred years ago, and she watched it land on his face like frost:

*Both choices are ruin. Feed it, and the ruin is slow and named. Starve it, and the ruin is sudden and shared.*

"There's a third way," Freya said at last. It was barely a whisper. "There's always a third way. There has to be."

"Three hundred years," Maren said, "and I have never found it. But then, I have never been a taster. I have never been property. I have never been the thing the fire was built expecting." She snapped the notebook shut, and the sound rang in the great hall like a small door closing. "Find your third way if you can, Freya Galloway. But find it fast. Because I have waited three centuries to open that damper, and I am done waiting past the season — and the thing on the throne has begun to suspect me. Two nights from now, when the court sleeps its long vigil, I move. With you or without you."

She left him standing before the flame, and climbed back down the fire-road alone, and did not look back — because stewards do not look back, and because she did not want to see whether hope had entered his face or despair.

Either one, she thought, climbing through the amber dark, might ruin everything.

Behind her, the fire breathed on, patient as debt, eating the last of a girl's name one slow golden mouthful at a time.
