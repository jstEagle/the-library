## 12. Charmwork in Earnest

The workshop Maren gave him had been a counting-room once, back when the Ninefold Court still counted anything—souls, years, debts owed to the mountain. The shelves were empty now except for dust that glittered faintly, like ash that hadn't decided whether to settle. A single vein of dragon-fire ran along one wall behind cracked glass, guttering so low that its light came and went like a sleeper's breath. Freya set his tools out on the long scarred table and tried not to think of it as an altar.

He failed at that too.

* * *

For seven years his magic had been a matter of inches. A charm to turn one coin into the look of two, held for the length of a market stall. A warmth-knot sewn into a scullery maid's apron string against the winter pipes. The little taste-trick he'd invented himself, a whisper of craft on the tongue that could tell him arsenic from nightshade before the poison ever reached the part of him that bled. Each spell small enough to deny. Each one cast with his shoulders hunched and one ear cocked for boots in the corridor. Never cast where wards could see. Never twice the same way. Never, never hope—that was the hedge-witch's third rule, and she'd made him repeat it until he hated the word.

Old Nan Sallow had taught him letters and charms both, in a room above her tincture shop that smelled of camphor and burnt sugar. *Magic is a trade like any other,* she used to say, tapping his knuckles with a spoon the way other tutors rapped them with rulers. *A joiner doesn't build a cathedral first. He builds a box. He builds ten thousand boxes, and if he's honest with the grain, someday the boxes become a door.* Freya had built ten thousand boxes. Coin-charms and warming-threads, taste-wards and pocket-lures, seven years of small sins committed in kitchens and alleys, each one folded up small as a stolen plum and swallowed whole.

Now Nan Sallow was eleven years dead, and Freya stood alone in a dead court's counting-room, about to build a door.

On the table before him lay the materials Maren had scoured from nine empty treasuries: a lens of true crystal no bigger than his thumbnail; a coil of wire drawn from star-iron, which the mountain had mined in the days when it still had stars to draw from; a scrap of the founding charter's vellum, blank where the contract's price had been paid and the ink had fled; and a thimble-sized cup of unglazed clay, plain as porridge, which Maren swore had been fired in the last of the old dragon-heat and would hold nothing but what it was given.

"A vessel," Maren had called it, setting these things down with the reverence of a woman handing over relics. "The court burns souls because it has never learned to carry them. Fire is easy. Keeping is hard." Her eyes had gone to the flame-vein behind the glass, then away. "You want to keep."

Freya turned the clay cup in his fingers. It was warm, faintly, the way a stone stays warm after a hand leaves it. Somewhere below, past nine floors of empty throne-halls and corridors hung with the portraits of kings who had never been born, Bryn's ember burned in the central flame, small now as a candle-flame, smaller every hour. He did not let himself do that arithmetic. He had done it once already, lying awake in the guest-cell Maren pretended was hospitality, and the numbers had nearly unmanned him.

Instead he thought about boxes. About grain and joinery. About the fact that for the first time in seven years, nobody was coming down the corridor.

His hands, he noticed, were steady. That surprised him more than fear would have.

* * *

The first attempt took four hours and failed in eleven seconds.

He began with the wire, because wire was honest work. Star-iron fought him—it remembered being a star the way old soldiers remember being young, stubbornly and without useful detail—but his pliers were good and his patience was the kind you bought with seven years of standing at a duke's elbow waiting to sip his soup. He drew the wire fine as hair and wove it into a cage the size of a walnut, a sphere of intersecting rings, each joint bound with a knot of charmwork so small he had to hold his breath to tie it. Nine rings for the Ninefold Court, though he told himself that was engineering, not sentiment. Nine points of balance. A soul, if Maren's ledgers could be believed, was not a thing but a tension—a pattern that insisted on itself—and the vessel's whole art would be to insist back, gently, the way a mother's arms insist on a child: *you are still you, you are still here.*

Into the heart of the cage he set the crystal lens, charmed clear. Around the lens, the scrap of charter-vellum, rolled tight as a scroll in a locket. The vellum was the idea of it: paper that had once held a soul's price might be taught to hold a soul instead. He spoke the binding words over it—not Nan Sallow's hedge-cant, but words of his own, worked out syllable by syllable across three sleepless nights, because this spell had no ancestor. No one had ever needed it before. Every magic he had ever learned assumed the soul was beyond reach, beyond price, beyond keeping. The court's whole architecture said otherwise.

When the cage was finished it looked like nothing—a walnut of wire, a toy, a trinket from a fair. Freya held it in his cupped palms and felt the charms wake one by one under his skin, tiny warm lights rising in him like bubbles in dark water.

"Small sins," he murmured, "become something," and fed the vessel a thread of his own craft to wake it.

The backlash came up through his hands like a bell being rung inside his bones.

He had time to think, absurdly, *the joints—the third ring's knot slipped—* and then the vessel's nine rings seized against each other, tensions crossing instead of holding, and everything he had poured in came home to him at once with interest. The world went white at the edges. He was aware of the table hitting his hip, of the wire cage skittering away ringing on stone, of himself on his knees with his teeth locked and light leaking out of his mouth. Seven years of immunity did what it was made to do: it drank. The poison-analog of raw craft-backlash poured into the part of him that had been marinating in slow toxins since he was nineteen, and his body, that patient ledger of absorbed harm, took the debt without breaking.

But it *hurt*. Gods, it hurt, in a way no poison had ever hurt—poison was always someone else's malice arriving in his mouth, and this was his own work turning on him, intimate as a bitten tongue. When the white receded he was flat on his back on the counting-room floor staring at the guttering fire-vein, and his hands were shaking, and the wire cage lay six feet away with its third ring snapped clean through.

He laughed. It came out wrong, half sob, and he let it, because there was no one to hear.

"Eleven seconds," he told the ceiling. "Nan would've charged me double for that."

* * *

He rebuilt the cage twice as carefully and it failed anyway, the second time slower and worse.

The problem, he worked out through a night of pacing and cold tea, was not the knots. It was the insistence. His vessel insisted *you are still you* with all the desperate force of a man who had spent seven years insisting *I am no one, I am a taster, I am nothing worth noticing.* The vessel learned its lesson from its maker. It gripped. And a soul-tension, Maren had warned him, gripping only teaches to fight. You cannot hold a soul the way a fist holds a coin. You have to hold it the way—

He sat down abruptly on the stool.

The way a kitchen holds a smell. The way a broth holds a flavor without clutching at it. Open. Permeable. A shape, not a seal. Of course. Of course it was cooking; it had been cooking all along; every charm he'd ever cast had been a recipe written in a language he'd been told was forbidden. The great magicians built fortresses and the fortresses fell, and some orphan taster was going to build a stockpot.

He remade the vessel a third time, and this time he left gaps—deliberate, measured openings in the ninth ring, places where the tension could breathe in and out, where the world could touch the soul inside without either one bleeding. He lined the clay cup with the charter-vellum and set the crystal lens not as a lock but as a window. And for the binding words, instead of commands, he wrote invitations. *You are welcome here. Nothing will burn you. Stay as long as you like, leave whenever you must.* Words you'd say to a tired person at your door. He almost wept writing them, and did not examine why.

The test, when he finally dared it, could not be done with nothing. A vessel for souls needed a soul-shaped thing to prove itself on, and the only such thing he could safely spend was craft itself—his own, spun dense. So he packed seven years of small spells into the vessel's heart: the coin-glamour and the warming-thread, the taste-whisper and the pocket-lure, every trick folded down to its essence, a distillate of his whole hidden life. If the vessel could carry that without burning or spilling it, it could carry Bryn.

He woke it gently. The rings rose, balanced, breathed. The lens took the light of his spent charms and held it—held it, steady as a lamp, no seizure, no slip. For one full minute the vessel sat on the table glowing softly, and Freya stood over it hardly daring to blink, watching his seven years of sin shine like a captured star.

Then the fire-vein behind the glass flickered.

It was nothing—a gutter, a draft, the mountain's dying pulse skipping a beat. But the vessel heard it. Souls know their kin. The glow inside bent toward the wall, yearning, and the ninth ring's careful gaps—the breathing-gaps, the open door—let the yearning *out*. The vessel strained toward the flame like a plant toward sun, and Freya saw in one horrible flash of understanding exactly what he'd built: not a prison, not a lamp, but a moth-lantern. An open door with a light in it. He'd built the thing the court itself was—something that invited souls in and could not let them go.

"No—" He grabbed for it. "No, stay, stay *here*—"

The vessel broke its own ninth ring reaching for the fire, and everything inside it tore free at once.

This time the backlash did not ring him like a bell; it *emptied* him. He felt his own craft ripped out along the channel he'd opened, sucked toward the flame-vein, toward the great central fire below where all spent things went to feed the light—and then his immunity rose up like a dam, the deep seven-year reservoir of it, and slammed against the drain. The tug-of-war lasted perhaps two seconds. It went through every bone he owned. He came back to himself face-down on the floorboards with blood in his mouth—he'd bitten his tongue properly this time, and possibly the inside of his cheek, and possibly something further down that would explain the taste of copper and ozone—and the vessel lay in pieces around him, and the flame-vein behind the glass was burning, very slightly, brighter than before.

Fed. By him. By the wreckage of his work.

Freya lay there a long moment, cheek against cold wood, listening to his heart bang like a shutter in wind. Then, slowly, he started to laugh again, and this time it was the real thing, ragged and helpless and alive.

"Of course," he wheezed at the floor. "*Of course.* It wants to burn me. It's been wanting to burn me since I was five years old. My whole life is a deposit." He pushed himself up on shaking arms. "Fine. Fine! Then I'll stop building locks and start building *bargains.*"

Because that was the lesson, and it had cost him two vessels and most of a night and blood he could ill afford: the fire took. That was all it knew how to do. But *he* was the one thing in the world the fire couldn't take by force—seven years of the court's own investment, sunk into his skin, compounding quietly while he tasted dukes' soup. Any vessel he built from craft alone would always strain toward the flame, because craft belonged to the same economy. The vessel had to be built from something outside the ledger.

Something freely given. Something the court had never priced.

He didn't have the word for it yet. But he had a sister whose name the world was forgetting, and a steward downstairs who kept treasonous notes, and a ward-keeper somewhere above ground burning her own certainties, and a thimble of clay that had been fired in the last of the old heat and would hold nothing but what it was given.

Freya gathered the broken wire from the floor, ring by ring, and began sorting the pieces into piles: what could be mended, what could be rethought, what had to be abandoned entirely. Outside the cracked glass, the fire-vein guttered low again. Down below, in the dark at the mountain's heart, a candle-flame of a girl was running out of hours—and far above, muffled by nine floors of stone, he heard—or dreamed he heard—boots. Deliberate ones. A soldier's pace, descending the stair, stopping at his door.

A knock. Two knocks, precise as a signature.

Freya froze, wire in hand, and knew before he opened it that whatever Ilsa Vane had come down the mountain carrying, it wasn't a warrant anymore.
