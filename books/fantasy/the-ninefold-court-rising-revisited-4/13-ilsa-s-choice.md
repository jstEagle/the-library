## 13. Ilsa's Choice

The regent's seal was warm when Ilsa Vane pressed her thumb into it, and that warmth felt like a lie.

She stood in the map room of Cinderfall Keep, where the great round table had been cleared of everything but a single document and a single candle. The regent—Lord Aldous Crane, a man whose authority lived entirely in other people's fear of it—did not look up as she read. He never did when he expected obedience. He saved his eyes for the ones who made him use them.

*By order of the Regency Council of Cinderfall,* the warrant began, in the dry ink of men who believed paperwork could survive the end of the world. *All approaches to the mountain known as the Ninefold Court are to be sealed with wardstone and mortar. No provision shall be made for persons within. The court, being sustained by means inimical to the city's welfare, is to be permitted to starve.*

*Permitted to starve.* She read the phrase twice. It was such a gentle way to say it. Such a tidy way. Somewhere beneath three hundred feet of granite, there was a flame fed by stolen souls, and among those souls—she had seen the ledger herself, had watched Maren's finger stop on the entry—a girl named Bryn Galloway, sold at five years old by the same orphanage that had sold her brother. And somewhere on the road down, walking toward that flame with nothing but a taster's tongue and seven years of borrowed immunity, was Freya Galloway.

The candle guttered. The wax smelled of tallow and something faintly sweet, like the incense they burned at funerals for people too poor to afford real grief.

"You've read it," Crane said. Not a question.

"I've read it."

"Then you understand your orders."

Ilsa set the warrant down on the table and smoothed it flat with both hands, the way she'd once smoothed bandages over burns in her ward-keeping days, back when the work was about protecting things instead of documenting their loss. Her instruments—the brass rings, the resonance plates, the little glass vials of suspended light that trembled near magic—all of them hung silent at her belt. Down here in the keep, far from the mountain, they read nothing at all. She missed them reading something. Anything. Silence had become its own kind of scream since the night the stars went out.

"The mountain has been sealed before," she said. "Hasn't it? That's what the wards were. That's what my whole office was. A seal."

Crane's quill paused over his own papers. "Your office," he said, "was to maintain the wards. The wards have failed. We do not rebuild failed walls while the enemy is still inside them. We let the siege do its work."

"There are two of our people inside."

"There were *nine hundred* of our people inside when the founders signed the charter." Now he looked up, and his eyes were pale and patient, the eyes of a man who had rehearsed this conversation in front of a mirror until every objection died on arrival. "The city was built on a bargain, Captain. A foul one. I won't pretend otherwise—I've read the same ledger you have. But the bargain is dying, and when it dies, either we die with it or we stand clear and let it. There is no third option. There is no clever rescue. There is only arithmetic."

"Freya Galloway isn't arithmetic."

"No," Crane agreed mildly. "He's an asset. And assets are precisely the sort of thing one does not spend on sentiment." He rose, gathered his papers, and paused at the door without turning around. "You held the north wall during the Ember Riots. You burned your own supply wagons rather than let the mob take them. I chose you for this commission because you understand that some things must burn so that more may live." The door opened onto torchlight. "Seal the mountain, Captain. Be the wall. It is the only shape in which this city will remember you kindly."

Then he was gone, and Ilsa stood alone with the candle and the warm wax seal and the arithmetic.

She thought about the north wall. She thought about the wagons. Everyone always thought she'd burned them out of coldness, and she had let them think it, because the truth was worse: she had burned them because the grain inside was already spoiled, and hungry men fighting over rotten bread would have killed each other by the dozen. The fire hadn't been cruelty. It had been honesty. She had simply been the only one willing to look closely enough at the grain to know what it was.

She looked now at the warrant. At the phrase *permitted to starve.* At the small neat letters that contained, folded inside them like a knife in cloth, a girl named Bryn who had been five years old when the city sold her, and who had spent nineteen years burning so that Cinderfall could have streetlamps and charter-law and the comfortable fiction that its light came from nowhere, from no one, from nothing at all.

Her instruments hung silent. But her hands, she noticed, were not. They were trembling, very slightly, the way the resonance plates trembled when something enormous passed nearby in the dark.

* * *

She went down to the evidence vault first, because defiance, done properly, begins with paperwork.

The vault sat three levels below the keep, behind a door of iron-banded oak that required her captain's key, the duty sergeant's key, and a spoken phrase she had never once said aloud in eleven years of service. Tonight she said it. *Ash remembers.* The lock turned with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

Inside, the city kept the things it preferred not to know it owned. Confiscated charms in lead-lined drawers, each labeled with the crime of its making. Ward-plates from the old border stones, cracked and dark since the night the stars went out. And in the deepest cabinet, under a shelf label reading *FOUNDERING DOCUMENTS — DO NOT WITHDRAW WITHOUT REGENT'S COUNTERSIGN*, the founding charter itself, and beside it the ledger Maren had copied, and beside that—

Ilsa took down the box marked *GALLOWAY, F. & B. — EVIDENCE, CASE 9-VANE.*

It was thin. That was the first thing that struck her. Eleven years of her career reduced to a single shallow box: the intake record from St. Verity's Orphanage, dated twenty-four years gone, listing two admissions, no surname parents, ages five and three. A scrap of linen embroidered with a crooked sun—a child's charmwork, technically contraband, catalogued and forgotten. And a page torn from a payment register, which she had seized in her first year as an investigator and never managed to get entered into any official finding, because every superior who read it found a reason to lose it again.

The page recorded a disbursement. Two entries, the same night. Two children's names. Two sums paid to St. Verity's by an account whose origin line read, in the beautiful evasive hand of the founders' clerks, simply: *the Charter.*

Two names. *Bryn.* And above it, in the same tired clerk's script, *Frey.*

They had even misspelled him. They had taken his name and his sister and written them down wrong, and the wrongness had entered the legal memory of the city, and no one had ever corrected it because correcting it would have meant asking what the payment was *for.*

Ilsa sat down on the vault's single stool, in the cold air that smelled of lead and dust and old iron, and held the page, and understood—at last, fully, in the place where understanding does its damage—that she had known for eleven years. She had carried this page through promotions and purges and the quiet erosion of everything she'd believed about her office, and she had told herself she was keeping it safe, keeping it *for later*, and later had never come, because later was a country you needed courage to emigrate to.

The stars went out, and later arrived all at once.

She thought of Freya in the interrogation cell, sitting very straight with his cuffed hands folded like a man at prayer, answering her questions with the terrible patience of someone who had expected to be hated his whole life. She remembered her instruments reading him—*already dead inside*, the soul-mark blank and hollow where a living soul should ring—and how she had written in her report, in language that would pass review: *subject presents anomalous readings consistent with prolonged exposure to court-taken individuals.* As if he were a survivor of a plague. As if the deadness were something that had happened *to* him, rather than something the city had *done* and then billed him for.

His immunity wasn't talent. His emptiness wasn't nature. The court had bought him at five years old and had been paying interest into his body ever since—feeding its property, keeping it fresh—and every meal the palace kitchens had ever tested through his tongue was a withdrawal slip. Seven years of service. Seven years of the city using, daily, the machinery of its own original sin, and calling it employment.

And she was ordered to pour mortar over the door while he walked deeper into the dark to fetch back what the city stole.

"No," Ilsa said aloud, to the vault, to the boxes, to the eleven years.

It echoed. Vaults echo. It sounded smaller than she meant it and truer than anything she'd said in a decade.

* * *

The sealing detail met her at the mountain road an hour past midnight: twelve masons, four ward-engineers, a wagon of quicklime and cut wardstone, and a junior officer named Pell who saluted her with the crisp terror of a man who had heard what happened to officers who displeased Captain Vane.

The mountain loomed against the starless sky, blacker than the sky itself, a hole in the world shaped like a mountain. Where the border wards had stood, there was nothing now—not rubble, not residue, just absence, the way a pulled tooth leaves absence. Her instruments, strapped to her belt, gave off the faintest hum she'd felt since the collapse: the mountain's breath, deep and slow, the breathing of something vast that was, by every honest measure, starving.

"Positions," she said, and the masons moved.

She watched them lay the first stone. Watched the ward-engineers chalk the binding-runes, the ones designed not to keep the court in but to keep rescuers out—to make sure that whatever happened below stayed below, that no one came back up with inconvenient truths stuck to their boots. The runes were elegant. She recognized the hand of their design. They were descended from the same tradition as her own instruments; somewhere back through the generations, the people who built cages and the people who measured them had all learned from the same teachers.

Pell appeared at her elbow with the ignition taper for the ward-bonding, held ready, waiting for her word.

"Captain?" he said. "Shall we begin the bonding sequence?"

The mountain breathed. Below it, a flame the size of a cathedral burned a girl who had owned one crooked-sun embroidery and nothing else in the world. Above it, a city slept under a sky with no stars, trusting its captains to be walls.

Eleven years ago, she had seized a page of evidence because it was her job. She had told herself *later*. Later had a price, and the price had compounded quietly, all this time, exactly the way the court's interest had compounded in a food-taster's veins.

Ilsa took out the warrant.

She did not do it dramatically. There was no audience worth performing for—only masons and engineers and young Pell with his taper, and the dark, and the mountain. She unfolded the document with the regent's warm wax seal already broken by her own thumb, held it up to the engineer's lantern, and read it once more, all of it, every clause, because if she was going to commit the act she was about to commit, she was going to commit it *informed*, with full knowledge, like an adult, like an officer, like a woman who had finally stopped confusing obedience with honor.

*No provision shall be made for persons within.*

"Captain Vane?" Pell's voice had gone careful. "Is there a problem with the orders?"

"Yes," Ilsa said. "There's a problem with the orders."

She turned to the brazier at the roadside—the one the detail used to warm bonding-pitch—and she put the warrant into it.

Paper takes flame quickly when the paper has decided. The wax seal caught last, bubbling, releasing a smell like burnt honey, like funeral incense, like the sweet fat of candles lit over cheap coffins. Ilsa watched Crane's authority curl and blacken and lift away on the mountain wind in flakes of ash, and she felt—she searched herself carefully for horror, for panic, for the vertigo of the lawless, and found none of them. What she found instead was the specific, exhausted calm of a wall that has finally been allowed to fall.

"Pell," she said.

He was staring at the burning warrant. His taper hung forgotten at his side. "Captain, that's—you can't—that's high treason. That's the regent's own seal, that's—"

"That's the regent ordering us to brick up a living child because she's cheaper dead than returned." She kept her voice level, the voice she'd used on the north wall, the voice that didn't shake because it couldn't afford the luxury. "You have three choices, Lieutenant. One: arrest me, and explain to the council why their sealing detail came back with its captain in irons and its orders unexecuted, along with everything I'm about to show them. Two: proceed without me, bond those runes yourself, and carry the rest of your life knowing what's under the mortar. Three—" she held out the evidence box, the thin shallow box, GALLOWAY, F. & B., eleven years of cowardice and custody— "you open that, read the second page, and then decide which side of the wall you're standing on."

Pell looked at the box. He looked at the brazier, where the last of the seal was going out like a tiny dying star—one small light returning to a starless sky, though neither of them knew yet how literal that image would prove.

Slowly, with the taper still in his hand, he took the box.

He read. She watched his face do what hers had done in the vault—the flicker, the denial, the arithmetic starting and then failing, because there was no arithmetic in which two sold children balanced against streetlamps. When he looked up, his eyes were wet and furious, and she understood that fury, having been that fury, and she waited out of it with him, giving him the silence she'd never been given.

"The bonding-runes," he said at last, hoarsely. "Should we—should I erase them?"

"Chisel them off," Ilsa said. "Every one. Then break the wagon-axles. If anyone asks, the detail was delayed by rockfall." She turned to face the mountain, its slow breath humming against her instruments, its darkness holding two people she had sent down and one child she had failed for eleven years. "We were ordered to be a wall tonight, Lieutenant. Instead we're going to be a door. Someone has to hold it open until they come back up."

"And if they don't come back?"

Ilsa watched the ash of the warrant settle on the cold road, fine and grey, already indistinguishable from the mountain's dust.

"Then we leave the door open anyway," she said, "and we tell the city exactly what it was built on. All of it. Even the parts that burn going down."

Behind them, chisels began to ring against stone—not building a wall, but taking one apart—and the sound carried down the mountainside, faint and stubborn, like knocking. Like someone, far below, might hear it and know:

the way out was being kept open.
