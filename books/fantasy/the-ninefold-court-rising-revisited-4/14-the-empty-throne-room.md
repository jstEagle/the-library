## 14. The Empty Throne Room

The vigil began at the ninth bell, and Freya learned its shape the way he had once learned the rhythms of the palace kitchens: by listening.

Maren had told him that during the long vigil the court slept—or did whatever passed for sleep in things shaped like courtiers—and that even the thing on the throne withdrew into itself, folding its attention inward like a hand closing around a coal. For three hours, she said, the flame-chamber stood unguarded. Not empty. Never empty. But unwatched in any way that mattered to living flesh.

"You'll have one chance," she said, pressing a sliver of pale metal into his palm. It was warm, and it hummed faintly against his skin like a trapped wasp. "Soul-iron. It remembers what it held. Hold it near her ember and it will call to whatever of Bryn is left. But Freya—" she caught his wrist, and her grip was cold as the stone they stood on, "—if the vigil ends while you are still in that chamber, no bargain we have made will matter. The court will simply take you. There will be no negotiation. There is never negotiation with hunger."

"I've spent seven years being tasted," Freya said. "I know how it goes."

She didn't laugh. He hadn't expected her to.

* * *

The way down from Maren's alcove was a throat of black stone, and the dragon-fire ran through its walls in slow veins, pulsing like something breathing in its sleep. Freya walked with his charms banked low—seven years of practice at making himself small, at making magic so quiet it barely qualified as existing—and even so the veins brightened as he passed, leaning toward him like flowers toward sun.

*It knows me,* he thought, and the thought sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. *It has always known me. Every cup I drank for the regent, every poison my blood shrugged off—that wasn't luck. That was the ledger keeping its accounts current.*

His immunity wasn't talent. He had learned that in the ledger-room, reading his own name in a dead man's handwriting: *Freya Galloway, aged five, sold by the Foundling House of Cinderfall, payment rendered against the founding charter.* The court had been feeding interest on him ever since. Every year of health, every resistance to venom, every charm that slid off him harmlessly—all of it was dividends paid to an asset the court intended eventually to collect.

He was walking toward the collection office now. The thought should have terrified him. What frightened him more was how familiar the road felt—as if his feet remembered it from before his mind did, from some night twenty years ago when a smaller boy was carried down this same throat, sleeping, sold, and returned without the piece of himself that made the transaction complete.

The vein-light dimmed ahead, and the walls opened out, and there it was.

* * *

The flame-chamber was a hollowed mountain-heart vast enough to hold the whole palace of Cinderfall inside it with room left over, and Freya stopped on the threshold because his body refused to take another step until it understood what it was seeing.

The Ninefold Court's chamber was not lit by torches. It was lit by the fire itself—a single flame standing in a great basin of black iron at the center of the floor, rising perhaps the height of two men, burning with no fuel, giving off heat but no smoke. Around it, at intervals, stood nine thrones of veined silver, arranged in a crescent facing the flame. Eight were empty. Their emptiness had weight; looking at them felt like looking at open graves.

The ninth throne, set apart at the crescent's center and closest to the fire, was occupied.

Freya had seen the king-shape twice before, always at distance, always flanked by the court. Now he saw it alone, and alone it looked less like a king and more like a costume worn by something that had studied kings from very far away. The proportions were nearly right. The hands folded on the armrests were nearly right. The face—handsome in the way old coins were handsome, all profile and erosion—was nearly right. But it did not breathe. In a chamber where the great flame rose and fell in slow tidal breaths, the figure on the throne was the only still thing, stillness itself given a silhouette.

And below it, below everything, woven through the black floor so that the whole chamber seemed to grow up out of it, ran the roots of the fire: channels of ember-light converging from nine directions into the basin, each channel fed from somewhere deep beneath the world. Souls, Maren had said. The fire ate souls the way a lamp ate oil, and with each soul burned, a name went out of the world's memory—not forgotten exactly, never missed exactly, just smoothly erased, a stitch unpicked so cleanly the cloth looked as if it had never been sewn.

Somewhere under his feet, one of those channels carried his sister.

Freya moved along the crescent wall, keeping the empty thrones between himself and the flame, and the soul-iron in his pocket grew warmer with every step, humming louder, tugging gently toward the fire like a compass needle finding north—or like a dog straining toward a door behind which someone it loved was waiting.

The nearest channel passed beneath a seam in the floor, and there—Maren had marked it for him, a chalk-faint sigil only visible to charmwork—he knelt.

The heat came up through the stone and pressed against his knees like a living thing investigating him. He drew out the soul-iron. It flared white at the edges. And through the seam in the floor, small as a coin seen at the bottom of a well, an ember glimmered.

It was the size of a candle-flame. That was all. Twenty years of burning, and what remained of Bryn Galloway was a candle-flame's worth of light, guttering in its channel, thin as breath on a winter morning.

"Bryn." He said it aloud, barely voiced, and hated how loud it sounded in the vast dark. The soul-iron trembled against his fingers. The ember flickered—once, twice—not rhythmically, not like fire. Like recognition.

Something in his chest tore along an old seam. He was five years old again, standing in the Foundling House yard, watching a cart take his sister away to be fostered in the country—the lie the orphanage had told him, the lie he had believed for thirty years, the kind lie that let him survive believing she was somewhere warm and fed. She had waved at him from the cart. He could still see her hand, small, chapped from cold, moving back and forth until the corner turned.

She hadn't gone to the country. She had come down here. They had both come down here—one sold, one kept, two entries in the same night's ledger, the city's founding sin paid in children.

"I'm here," he whispered. "I found a way to carry you out. I need time. Can you hold on? Bryn, can you—"

The ember flared. Just slightly. Just enough.

And then the light in the chamber changed.

* * *

Freya had been trained his whole life to notice what changed in a room. A new dish, an unfamiliar spice, a guest who sat too near the regent's cup—it was noticing that had kept him alive. So he noticed immediately that the great flame had gone quiet, drawn down into itself, burning low and close like a fire holding its breath.

He noticed that the veins in the walls had stilled.

And he noticed that he was no longer kneeling at the channel. He was standing in the open floor, halfway across the chamber, though he had no memory of crossing it—and the thing on the ninth throne was looking at him.

Its eyes were open. He would have sworn they had not been open a moment ago. They were the color of the flame, gold shading into ember-red at the center, and they did not blink, and they did not reflect the light so much as produce the impression of having already absorbed it.

"Freya Galloway," it said.

The voice was wrong in a way he couldn't immediately name, and then he could: it was warm. Everything else about the creature was cold and carved and still, but the voice came out warm as a kitchen, warm as bread, warm as the hedge-witch's cottage where a boy had first learned that magic could be small and kind. It used warmth the way a lure-fisherman used a painted fly.

"Do you know," it said, and the head tilted, the movement smooth as poured honey and utterly unlike anything alive, "you have grown into your bones. I wondered, sometimes, whether you would. The last time I saw you, you were such a small parcel of a boy. All elbows and appetite."

Freya's mouth had gone dry. Seven years of tasting poisons and he had never once faced anything like this—not fear of death, which was simple, but fear of *this*: the horror wearing intimacy like a mask fitted carefully over a void.

"That's not possible," he said. His voice came out steady. He was almost proud of that. "I've never been down here. Not since—"

"Not since you were carried down and carried up again, no. You slept through the interesting part." The king-shape unfolded from the throne—rose, rather, the way smoke rises, without quite moving limbs—and descended the three shallow steps of the dais. It did not walk closer. It was simply nearer, the way things in dreams are nearer. "But I remember all my property. Do you imagine I forget? Names burn, Freya, but names are only what the world calls things. I keep what the world misplaces."

It stopped an arm's length away—close enough that he could see that the handsome eroded face had pores, had fine lines, had everything except life—and it regarded him with the patient tenderness of a merchant examining stock.

"You bit the taster's thumb when they carried you past the kitchens. Did you know that? Such a small thing, and so furious about it." Something happened at the corners of its mouth that a generous observer might have called a smile. "I have thought of it fondly for twenty years. Fury is rare in what comes down here. Most of them arrive already resigned. Children learn resignation so quickly—it is the first thing we teach them, really, the city teaches them for us. But you bit."

Freya's hands were shaking. He let them. He had long ago learned that fighting a tremor cost more than enduring it, and he needed every scrap of himself for what came next.

"You're lying," he said. "Souls don't remember. You burn them. Maren told me—you erase the names. You're trying to make me feel known because known things trust."

"Maren," the creature said, tasting the name. "Yes. My steward. My traitor. You may tell her, when you next conspire, that I have known about her treachery for eleven years and permitted it, because a court that cannot be betrayed forgets it can be refused, and refusal is delicious. We have had so little of it lately." The flame-colored eyes moved over him, unhurried. "As for lying—I never lie to what belongs to me. Lying devalues the asset. Ask your question, taster. You have one. I can see it sitting behind your teeth like a pit in fruit."

Every instinct Freya owned screamed at him to ask nothing, to run, to wake Ilsa, to do literally anything other than stand in the open floor of a hungry court conversing with the thing that had eaten his sister by installments.

He asked anyway.

"Does she remember me?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere far above, impossibly far above, he imagined he heard the sound of a city holding its breath beneath a starless sky.

"She did," it said softly. "For a long time, she did. Your name was among the last to go—she held it the way your kind holds warmth in cupped hands. She spoke it into the fire, oh, hundreds of times, in the early years. *Freya. Freya. Freya.* It made the flame burn brighter, actually. Love is rich fuel. We prize it." It leaned closer, and the warmth of the voice wrapped around him like a blanket, and underneath the blanket was nothing at all. "So you see, Freya Galloway, I am not your enemy in the way you suppose. I am the only creature left in either world that heard your sister say your name. When everything else has forgotten there was ever a girl called Bryn, I will still carry her voice saying it. Is that not a kind of love?"

The soul-iron in Freya's pocket was burning hot now, hotter than metal had any right to be, and through the floor beneath his feet the candle-flame ember pulsed—once, twice—faint, frantic, like a heart trying to be heard through a wall.

Or like warning.

"No," Freya said quietly. "That's not love. That's inventory."

The smile on the king-shape's face did not falter. But the temperature of the chamber dropped, sudden and total, and every vein of dragon-fire in the walls went dark at once, and in the black the creature's eyes remained—two coals in the dark, regarding him with what might have been disappointment or might have been delight.

"And yet," it said, as the great flame in the basin guttered down to almost nothing, "you came all this way to take inventory of your own. So let us discuss terms, taster. I have been thinking about what to charge you for her. And I find—" the darkness leaned in, intimate as a shared secret, "—that I have finally decided."
