## 15. What the Court Wants Back

The throne room had a way of making time feel negotiable.

Freya had noticed it on his first audience and noticed it again now, standing in the long shadow of the ninth throne while the thing that wore a king's shape regarded him with what he could only describe as patience—patience like deep water, patient the way stone is patient, the way debt is patient. The nine thrones rose behind it in a crescent of black glass, eight of them empty, their surfaces dull as old coins. Only the ninth held light: the seated figure, and beneath its feet the slow pulse of veins in the floor, carrying dragon-fire up from the chamber below in colors that had no honest names. Not orange. Not gold. Something older than both, the color of the first fire anyone ever stole.

He had come back to this room because there was nowhere else to go with what he knew. Bryn's ember burned small as a candle-flame in the chamber beneath his feet—he could feel her absence of presence through the soles of his boots, a wrongness in the mountain's hum, a note missing from a chord. And the court knew he knew. The court had known everything before he did. That was, he was learning, rather the point of courts.

"You have been busy," said the king-shaped thing.

Its voice was the worst part. It was not booming, not cold, none of the register a nightmare would choose. It sounded like a tired magistrate at the end of a long assizes, a man who had heard every plea and believed none of them and still got up each morning to hear them again. It sounded almost kind. Kindness from something like this was worse than cruelty; cruelty you could brace against.

"I've been looking for my sister," Freya said.

"Looking." The shape turned the word over like a coin of doubtful mint. Its face was nearly a face—a king's face, carved by someone who had seen kings but perhaps never loved one, all the planes correct and the eyes slightly too far apart. "You found a ledger instead. You found a name. You found an ember." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table. "Do you know how long she has burned?"

Freya's mouth was dry. Seven years of tasting had taught him every species of fear the body could brew, and he catalogued this one automatically, the way he catalogued everything: bitter almond, no. Hemlock sweetness, no. This was simpler. This was just a boy in a big room asking after his sister.

"Tell me," he said.

"Nineteen years." The shape's hands rested on the arms of the throne—hands, again, nearly hands, the fingers a little too many-jointed, folded with a courtesy that made Freya's skin crawl precisely because it was practiced. "Nineteen years of flame. She was five when she came down. Do you remember her at five?"

"I remember everything."

"That," said the thing on the throne, "is unusual. In my experience, most people remember nothing. That is rather the design." It inclined its head, and the gesture was so human, so courteously human, that for half a heartbeat Freya forgot what sat inside the shape. Then the light under its skin moved—the fire-veins showing through, faint as veins in an egg—and the forgetting broke like thin ice. "But you are not most people. You were never meant to be most people. We made sure of that."

"We," Freya said carefully, "being the court."

"The court is nine seats and one occupant, these days." Something flickered across the almost-face—not quite grief, not quite hunger, but the shape grief makes when it has been hungry for a very long time. "Once we were nine. Once the fire ran bright enough to be seen from the surface, and your founders called it a miracle and knelt in the snow to bargain with us. They wanted light that would never gutter. Walls that would never fall. A city where the wards sang so sweetly the dark itself kept its distance." The too-many fingers drummed once on the armrest, a sound like knuckles on coffin-lid. "We asked our price. They paid it. They always pay it. They simply prefer not to remember paying."

"Children," Freya said. The word came out flatter than he intended. He let it stay flat. Flat words cut deeper in rooms like this. "You took children."

"We took what was sold to us. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole of the law, mortal and otherwise." The shape leaned forward, and the throne-room temperature dropped by a degree Freya felt in his teeth. "Your orphanage sold. Your charter signed. Your first regents countersigned, and their successors renewed the contract every fifty years without reading past the first clause, because the first clause promised them light. You have read the ledger now. You know the accounting better than any living soul above ground. So let us not waste each other's patience pretending either of us believes this began yesterday."

Freya breathed. Counted. One, two, three, four—the old kitchen discipline, the taster's trick, steady the hand before the spoon reaches the lips.

"What do you want," he said, "for Bryn?"

* * *

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight, like wet wool. Somewhere high above, in the vaulted dark of the throne room ceiling, water gathered and fell from a stalactite into a pool, and the drop echoed nine times before it died—nine, always nine, the number worked into the mountain's bones like a signature.

The shape stood.

It was taller standing than sitting, which should not have been possible given the throne's proportions, and Freya filed that impossibility away with the others: the too-far-apart eyes, the extra joints, the way its shadow fell toward the fire-veins instead of away from them. Everything about it was correct except the direction of its wanting.

"Come," it said. "Walk with me. Bargains are poorly struck standing in judgment's shadow, and I am—" the pause was brief but genuine, Freya thought, or performed so well the distinction stopped mattering, "—tired of judging."

It led him along the crescent of thrones. Up close, each empty seat told its story in erosion. The second throne's armrests were worn smooth as riverstone, as if gripped by desperate hands over centuries. The fifth was cracked clean through the crown of its back, a wound filled with cooled glass. The seventh was the strangest: it looked *new*, unblemished, its black glass still holding a faint polish, and Freya understood without being told that whatever had sat there had left recently, or been removed, or removed itself. Maren's old post, he thought. The steward's vacancy. He filed that away too.

"My steward tells me you craft charms," the shape said, conversational, terrible. "Small ones. Illegal ones. A warming cantrip sewn into a scullery maid's apron hem. A mending charm braided into a bootlace. Seven years of tiny sins, each one invisible, each one deniable." It glanced sideways at him, and the almost-smile on the almost-face was devastatingly familiar—Freya had worn that exact expression in palace kitchens for seven years, the smile of someone holding a secret lightly enough to survive it. "I confess I find it charming. The irony. Our own investment, working against us in the dark, one stolen warmth at a time."

"Investment," Freya repeated.

"You have read the ledger entry for yourself. Or Maren has read it to you, which amounts to the same, since Maren reads selectively." They reached the end of the crescent, where the ninth throne's dais dropped away into the descending vein-road, and the heat came up to meet them like breath. "Payment rendered at founding: two souls, juvenile, unencumbered. Interest accruing thereafter, per annum, in the form of—" the shape paused, savoring, and Freya realized with a lurch that it was quoting, reciting contract language it clearly relished, "—*preservation of the asset against all harm, poison included.*"

The words landed in him like stones into well-water, dropping through layer after layer of everything he had built himself out of.

His immunity. His famous, precious, hard-won immunity—the thing that had gotten him the taster's post, the thing that had saved his life four times by his count, the competence he had spent seven years believing was *his*, earned dose by careful dose, arsenic and nightshade and foxglove taken in measured sips until his blood learned to shrug. He had told himself a story about that boy: the clever orphan who out-thought poison through sheer stubbornness. And underneath the story, the truth the ledger had already shown him, and which hearing it aloud made real in a way ink never could:

He had never out-drunk anything. He had been *kept*. Preserved. Maintained like a cellar of good wine against the day the owner came to collect.

"You've been protecting me," he said. His voice came out steady. He was distantly proud of that. "All this time. Every poison I survived—"

"Was survivable because we willed it so," the shape agreed pleasantly. "Though I will grant you this: you learned faster than the contract required. There is a difference between preserved meat and a good hound, taster. You trained yourself on top of our keeping. We found it... promising."

Promising. For what? The question formed itself before he could stop it, and the shape heard it anyway—it heard everything, that was becoming clear, it heard the questions he didn't ask louder than the ones he did—and its smile widened by one careful degree.

* * *

They descended.

The vein-road spiraled down into the fire-chamber's antechamber, and with every turn the heat thickened and the light changed, until Freya walked inside the color he had only glimpsed from above. The walls here were veined through with living flame, slow rivers of it moving like blood behind skin, and the air tasted of hot iron and something sweeter underneath—burning sugar, almost, the smell of a name being consumed. He had smelled it in the ledger room too. He was beginning to understand it was the smell of the entire economy down here.

Bryn was close now. He couldn't explain how he knew—no sense he owned reported it—but some part of him that predated the orphanage, that predated language, pulled toward a point beyond the eastern wall of the antechamber like a needle finding north. *She's in there.* Nineteen years burning, and the ledger said names eroded as souls fed the fire. Did she remember anything? Did she remember a boy named Ferren, before the orphanage renamed him Freya because Bryn couldn't pronounce the first syllable? Did she remember—

He shut the door on that thought. Tasters learned to shut doors. You could not retch and keep the job.

The shape stopped at a balustrade overlooking the flame-chamber proper, and gestured downward with those courteous, many-jointed fingers.

Below, the great flame burned—or rather, Freya corrected himself, the great flame *starved*. He had expected an inferno. What he saw was a bonfire three days dead: a broad bed of embers the size of a village square, glowing deep red shot through with dying gold, and rising from it a single tongue of true flame no taller than a man. Around the ember-bed, at nine points, stood pillars of fused glass, and Freya counted the souls visible in them the way Maren had taught him to count—one flicker here, a dim pulse there, most of the pillars dark as smoke. Eight occupied once. Now perhaps three still held light, and even those guttered.

And at the center of it all, apart from the pillars, in a vessel of clear crystal barely larger than a lantern: a flame the size of a candle.

Small. Steady. Stubborn.

"Bryn," Freya said. It wasn't a question. It wasn't even a word, really. It was a hand reaching through nineteen years and six hundred feet of rock.

"She burns well," the shape said, and the pride in its voice was the proudest, cruelest thing Freya had ever heard, because it was genuine. "Of the two of you, she has lasted longer than any soul we have housed in four centuries. Most gutter in ten years. Twelve, if they fought us. She has fought us the whole time, and still—" the almost-face tilted, admiring, "—still she holds a core. Do you know why?"

Freya's jaw ached. He realized he was clenching it and made himself stop. Made himself do the other thing instead, the professional thing: listen. Watch. Catalogue. The shape wanted something—that was why it was giving him this tour, this intimacy, this horrible guided visit to his sister's cage. Everything down here was transactional. Which meant everything down here was *negotiable*. Maren had said that. Maren, who wanted the whole fire dead, who saw darkness as mercy. Ilsa, somewhere above, holding a warrant she might burn or obey. And him, standing between all of it, holding the only currency he'd ever had: a body the court had spent nineteen years preserving.

"No," he said quietly. "Why does she last?"

"Because she was loved before we took her." The shape said it without mockery. Somehow that was worse. "Souls that arrive loved burn slower. The love is a fat the fire must render first. Yours arrived equally loved—we checked, we always check—and yet you were never placed in a pillar. Why do you suppose that was?"

"Because you found a better use for me."

"Because," the shape said, "we found a *price* for you. There is a difference, and the difference, taster, is the whole of this conversation."

* * *

It turned to face him fully then, and up close, lit from beneath by the ember-light, the king-shape was less convincing than at distance. Freya could see now what the nearness revealed: that the face was a mask fitted over something, that the eyes' odd spacing marked the seams, that what looked out through it was not a man and had never bothered to fully become one. But it wore the mask the way a good liar wears the truth—carefully, and with obvious long practice.

"Here is our situation," it said. "The fire starves. When it dies, the wards die, the stars stay gone, and the dark comes up the vein-roads into a city that has forgotten what dark is. Your founders bought light. Their heirs will inherit the invoice. That is one future, and I do not favor it, despite what my traitorous steward may have whispered to you. A dead court serves no one. Not the city. Not me. And not—" one finger indicated, delicately, the crystal lantern below, "—her. A soul released from a dead fire returns to the world scattered, taster. Unremembered. Nameless forever. Is that the rescue Maren promised you? To free your sister into oblivion?"

Freya kept his face still. Inside, something lurched—because that was exactly what Maren had promised, more or less, freedom as extinguishment, mercy as darkness, and he had carried that promise for days like a coal in his chest, and now the enemy had picked it up and shown him the crack in it in four sentences. *Don't let it see that land,* he told himself. *Tasters don't flinch at the table.*

"So the fire must live," he said neutrally.

"The fire must be *fed*," the shape corrected. "There is a difference, and—" the smile again, self-aware this time, almost playful, "—yes. You see it. Everything, with me, is differences. Very well. The fire needs fuel. Souls are the traditional fuel. But souls are finite, and the supply above ground has grown cautious, and your regents renew our contract with fewer signatures each century. We have diversified. We have experimented. Nothing burns like a loved soul, but a loved soul takes decades to render and yields diminishing returns. Unless—" and here the shape paused, letting the heat rise between them, letting the moment stretch the way a good interrogator stretches a silence, "—unless the soul is renewed."

"Renewed," Freya said. "How?"

"A soul in a body renews itself. That is the engine of you people. Eat, sleep, love, grieve, wake, and the soul mends. A soul alone in a pillar cannot mend. It can only spend." The too-many-jointed hands spread, presenting the arithmetic like a merchant presenting scales. "So. We require a keeper. A servant of the flame. Someone to walk among the pillars, tend the fire, gather what drifts loose, and—" the pause was surgical, "—renew what can be renewed. By touch. By presence. By the ordinary machinery of a living person spending their life beside the dying. It is not cruel work, taster. It is hospice work. Someone must do it. No one ever has, because no one could survive the chamber. Fire kills fire-tenders. Except."

"Except," Freya said, "someone immune to fire."

"Someone immune," the shape agreed softly, "to *everything*. Poison. Flame. Age, within reason. Sorrow, within limits. Nineteen years of preservation, taster. Nineteen years of interest, compounding. We did not maintain you out of sentiment." The mask leaned close, and beneath it, for one unguarded instant, Freya saw the actual occupant—something vast and patient and terribly, terribly tired, older than the city, older than the bargain, watching him with what might have been hope if hope could fossilize. "We maintained you because you are the answer to a question we have been asking since the eighth throne went empty. Serve the flame. Keep your sister alive by your own warmth. Renew her daily, as a body renews a soul, and she will never finish burning. Never scatter. Never be forgotten. She will persist, diminished but present, for as long as you serve."

"And how long is that?"

The shape smiled its magistrate's smile.

"Forever," it said. "That is the price. Not your soul—we have souls, they are worth less every year. Your *life*. Your living, breathing, unaging, unpoisonable life, spent in the dark, at her side, until the mountains wear down. That is what the court wants back. Everything we invested. With interest." It straightened. Behind it, the candle-flame in the crystal lantern below flickered, and Freya would have sworn—would have staked his palate on it—that the flicker bent toward him. Toward the sound of his voice. "Take a day. Take two. Consider it honestly; I will know if you merely perform consideration. You are, after all, the finest performer I have ever purchased."

* * *

They gave him a cell that was not a cell—a warm room off the antechamber, veined with gentle firelight, stocked with bread and cheese and a jug of water that tasted of minerals and deep places. A room designed, he understood, to make captivity feel like hospitality. The court had been doing this a long time.

Freya ate nothing. Old habit: never eat what your host provides when the negotiation is open.

He sat on the edge of the cot with his elbows on his knees and considered it honestly, because the thing had asked him to and because lying to himself had never been among his talents—deceiving others, yes, exquisitely, for seven years, but the mirror was the one audience that always caught him.

Consider it honestly. A lifetime below. Warmth, purpose, his sister alive at his elbow instead of ash in the world's memory. Against: the sun. Rain. Ilsa Vane's crooked scowl. Maren's treason, whatever shape it finally took. Bread with butter. The smell of a city morning. Everything he had crawled through darkness to reclaim, traded away in exchange for a smaller, kinder version of the same cage the court had built around him at age five.

*Preserved meat or a good hound,* it had said. And the trap was perfect, that was the awful thing. It wasn't even cruel. It was *reasonable*. That was what made it a court's offer and not a monster's: it was the only reasonable offer in the room, and reason was the strongest chain ever forged.

Freya looked down at his hands—taster's hands, scarred faintly at the fingertips from a childhood of hedge-witch needles, steady as bedrock—and slowly, deliberately, he began to build the performance the thing had demanded.

It would believe consideration. Good. Let it watch him consider. Let it see him pace, sit, stand at the balustrade staring down at the crystal lantern, press his palm to the warm stone above where Bryn burned. Let it read longing there, because the longing was real, and the best lies are load-bearing truths with a false roof.

Let it believe he was weighing forever against freedom.

Because here was what the court's arithmetic had missed, what nineteen years of ledgers could not price, what no contract clause had ever anticipated: Freya Galloway had spent his whole life learning to taste what was hidden in a dish. And he had tasted this offer to its depths, and beneath the warmth and the reasonableness and the genuine fossilized weariness of the thing that wore the king, he had found the flaw.

*Forever* was a word for creatures that expected to endure.

And the eighth throne was cracked, and the seventh was polished new, and the fire was starving, and somewhere above him a ward-keeper was holding a warrant she had not yet burned.

Two days, he had been given. Freya lay back on the cot, closed his eyes, and began to count the hours—not as a prisoner counting sentence, but as a cook counting down to the moment the pot came off the fire.

He just needed the court to keep believing the meal was still cooking.
