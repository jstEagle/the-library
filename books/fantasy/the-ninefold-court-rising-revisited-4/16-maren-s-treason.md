## 16. Maren's Treason

Maren had counted the flame's breaths for two hundred years, and tonight it was breathing wrong.

She stood in the gallery above the fire-chamber, hands folded in the sleeves of her steward's grey, watching the great vein of dragon-fire pulse in its basin of black glass. Once—before the thrones emptied, before the ledger grew fat—it had roared. Now it sighed. Each exhale was a little shorter than the last. Each inhale drew something out of the world that could not be put back: a name here, a face there, a song whose melody everyone had forgotten and no one could grieve for, because grief requires remembering.

Two hundred years of service. Two hundred years of carrying trays to the thing on the throne, of keeping the nine candles trimmed, of writing down in her small neat hand exactly how much of each soul had been spent and on what. The court believed she kept the ledger out of loyalty.

The court had never been good at reading its own servants.

She turned from the gallery and walked the long corridor toward the counting room, her footsteps silent on stone worn smooth by centuries of feet. The court was asleep—or whatever the court did instead of sleep, all those hollow courtiers folded into their alcoves like moths in winter. Even the throne-thing had gone still during the vigil hours, wearing its king's shape the way a coat hangs on a hook, empty and patient until morning.

That was when it must be done. Not at the appointed hour. Not after the bargains were settled, not after the boy from above had finished his clever negotiations and the ward-keeper had finished her slow thaw. Now, while everything in the mountain held its breath.

Maren let herself into the counting room and barred the door behind her—not with iron; the court heard iron—but with an old word, one of the first words she had ever learned, back when she had been a girl with a name of her own and not a function. Then she knelt and pried up the third flagstone, the loose one, the one no ledger recorded, and lifted out what lay beneath.

A shroud of woven ash. She had made it herself over ninety years, thread by thread, from the residue of souls already burned past recovery—from cinders so spent they carried nothing left to lose. Wrapped around a flame, it did not extinguish. It *unfed*. It separated the fire from everything that fed it and let the fire consume only itself, cleanly, completely, like a lamp running dry.

It would free every soul still held in the dragon-fire. Bryn Galloway's ember among them, if there was enough of her left to free. Every name would come home.

And both worlds would go dark.

Maren sat back on her heels and looked at the shroud for a long time. She was not a cruel woman. That was the thing none of them understood—the boy with his immunity, the ward-keeper with her burned warrant, even the throne-thing with its borrowed memory of fondness. She was not cruel. She was *arithmetic*. She had done the sums so many times they had worn grooves into her like water wears stone:

The fire would gutter within the year regardless. Perhaps within the season. There were not enough souls left worth taking, and the ones above had finally learned what the price had been, and no city sells its children twice without flinching. When the fire died on its own, it would die hungry—half-consumed souls trapped mid-burn, names torn in half, the dark coming anyway but arriving filthy, with wreckage.

Smothered now, deliberately, by a hand that knew how: clean. Whole. The souls released intact. The dark arriving honest.

She had chosen the honest dark two hundred years ago, in this room, kneeling where she knelt now. She had simply needed time to make the instrument of it.

Tonight she had time.

* * *

She almost didn't hear them coming, which frightened her more than anything else that night. Two hundred years of hearing everything in this mountain—the court's whispers, the flame's breathing, the ledger pages turning in their sleep—and she had let her attention narrow to a single flagstone and a single task, and so it was Freya Galloway's voice, low and unhurried, that pulled her back into the world.

"You're doing it tonight, aren't you."

He stood in the doorway of the counting room. The bar-word she'd set should have turned him away; instead it hung around him like smoke around a candle, thinning, confused. His immunity again. The court's investment, walking through her locks as if they were courtesy.

Behind him, filling the corridor with the smell of cold iron and rain-soaked wool, came Ilsa Vane. The ward-keeper's instruments were strapped across her chest, dead and dark—they always were down here—but her eyes worked fine, and they went straight from Maren's face to the ash-woven shroud in her hands.

"Ah," Ilsa said quietly. "So that's what treachery looks like."

"It looks like arithmetic," Maren said. She rose, slowly, the shroud cradled against her breast like an infant. She would not hide it. She had hidden it for ninety years and her arms were tired. "Sit, if you like. I'll explain the sum. You'll want to check my figures anyway."

"We don't have time—" Freya began.

"We have less time than you think," Maren said, "which is precisely why it must be tonight. Sit." And because she was still, in the eyes of the court, the steward—because her voice still carried the weight of two hundred years of being obeyed—they sat, the taster and the ward-keeper, two intruders perched on counting stools in the heart of the Ninefold Court, while their ally showed them the knife she meant to use.

She laid the shroud open between them. Up close it looked like nothing—a grey weave, soft as moth-wing, faintly warm. Freya reached toward it and stopped himself, fingers curling back. Good instincts. It wasn't dangerous to touch. But it *wanted* fire the way a dry field wants lightning, and things that want are never entirely safe.

"Ninety years," Maren said. "One thread for every soul burned past saving. Do you understand what that means? Everything this holds has already been lost. Already erased. This can't hurt anyone who isn't already beyond hurting." She met Freya's eyes. "Your sister is not on it yet. Her ember is still bright enough to save. That is why tonight, and not tomorrow. Tomorrow she may be thread."

"And if we wrap the flame," Ilsa said. Her voice was flat, careful, the voice of a woman reading an instrument she doesn't trust. "Every soul comes free. And then?"

"Then the dark," Maren said simply. "Both worlds. For a season. For a year. However long it takes to find another way to keep a sky lit. People will die in the dark—I won't pretend otherwise. The old, the sick, the ones who panic. Cities will do ugly things to each other by torchlight." She folded her hands. "But the alternative is that we feed it. Forever. A little longer each generation, a few more names each decade, until there is nothing anywhere that remembers being anyone. I have read the founding charter, Captain Vane. Have you? It doesn't say *forever*. But it doesn't say *stop*, either, and contracts that don't say stop are the most dangerous kind."

Freya was staring at the shroud, and his face—the face of a man trained since childhood to show nothing—was showing everything. Hope and horror in equal measure, fighting for the same ground.

"You said the fire would gutter within the year," he said. "You told us that. Why build all this if the problem solves itself?"

"Because of *how* it solves itself," Maren said. "A starved fire doesn't release what it holds. It digests. The last thing the dragon-fire does, as it dies, is burn what remains—quickly, greedily, to warm itself through the ending. Every soul still in that basin gets consumed whole in the final hour. Your sister doesn't drift out into the dark, boy. She goes *down*." She let that sit. "I built the shroud so that when the end came, someone could choose the manner of it. So that the ending could be a door instead of a stomach."

"Someone," Ilsa repeated. "Not you."

Maren was quiet a moment. "A steward cannot unmake the flame. The office forbids it. The moment I touch the fire with intent to end it, the court wakes—all of it, every hollow courtier, and the throne-thing most of all. It would take the shroud from me and me besides." She shrugged, grey and small and utterly calm. "I had intended to teach the working to someone the court cannot bind. Someone it has no contract on anymore. Someone whose whole existence is, as of a week ago, an unpaid debt the court is desperate to collect." She looked at Freya. "I had intended you."

"And instead," Ilsa said, "you decided to do it yourself, tonight, while we slept."

"Instead I decided to stop waiting for permission," Maren said. "Yes."

* * *

They argued for an hour. Maren let them argue, because arguing was honest, and honesty had been so rare in this mountain that she hoarded it like a miser.

Freya argued first with fury—she had not expected fury from him; he wore weariness the way other men wore armor—and then, when the fury spent itself, with craft. He knew fires, he said. He had tended kitchen hearths since he was six; he knew what happened when you smothered a blaze too fast, how the heat went sideways, how a choked chimney breathed back into the room. The dragon-veins ran through the whole mountain, through the border wards' roots, through—his voice caught—through whatever thin filament still connected this place to the sky above. Choke the heart suddenly and every vein might burst at once, or every vein might go dark *at once*, and either way—

"And your sister?" Maren asked gently, when he ran down. "If we wait for your careful method?"

"I'm not saying wait forever. I'm saying—" He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "I've been down here a week. A week ago I didn't know she existed. Give me a day to build the vessel right. Give me one day."

"One day is four hundred thousand breaths of that flame," Maren said. "I have watched it breathe for two hundred years. I know what four hundred thousand sounds like." But something in her had wavered, and she hated that it had, and she examined the wavering honestly, because honesty was the only coin she had never debased. Was it mercy for him? Or was it, at the bottom, the oldest cowardice of all—wanting one more night of a world that still had light in it?

Before she could answer herself, Ilsa Vane spoke, and she spoke like a woman laying cards face-up.

"If you smother it tonight, you kill the wards' last anchor."

Maren turned. "The wards are dead. I felt them fall."

"The *border* wards fell. The deep lattice didn't—it's been running on residual charge, on whatever the founders banked before they died. My instruments have been reading it since we descended. Faint. Fainter every hour. But present." Ilsa tapped the dead instruments on her chest. "And the deep lattice isn't a wall. It's a *handrail*. It's the only structure in either world that maps where the freed souls are supposed to *go*. Burn it out with the flame and you don't just bring the dark—you release ten thousand names into a house with no rooms. They won't come home, Steward. They'll come apart."

The silence that followed was very long. In it, Maren heard the flame breathe, and heard her own two hundred years of certainty develop, for the first time, a hairline crack.

She had planned for the court. She had planned for the throne-thing, for the regent above, for the charter, for the fire's own hunger. She had not planned for a disgraced ward-keeper who read dead instruments the way other people read scripture.

"You could be lying," Maren said at last.

"I could," Ilsa agreed. "I burned my warrant rather than seal this mountain. Ask yourself why a woman who does that would lie about a handrail."

Freya was looking between them, and Maren saw him do the thing that tasters do—weigh, balance, detect the poison in a cup that smells of honey. When he spoke, his voice was quiet.

"She's not lying. And neither are you. That's the worst part." He stood, and for a moment the chamber seemed smaller with him upright in it, as though he carried some of the sky down with him. "So here's the bargain, Steward. The only kind I know how to make. You give me until the flame reaches the second mark—that groove on the basin's rim, the one it passes twice a night. When it hits that mark, we wrap it together. All three of us. Your shroud, my vessel, her lattice held open with whatever she's got left. We don't choose between your honest dark and her mapped dark. We build a door with hinges."

"The vessel fails, you die," Maren said. "It nearly killed you twice."

"I'm aware."

"And if the court wakes before the mark—"

"Then I suppose," Freya said, and there was something in his face she recognized suddenly, something she had seen once in a mirror two centuries ago and never since, "we'll all find out what we're owed."

Maren looked at the shroud in her hands. Ninety years of threads. Ninety years of patience, undone in an evening by arithmetic she hadn't included in her sums: that allies are variables, and variables move.

"The second mark," she said. "Not one breath later."

Below them, through the floor, through the black glass, the dragon-fire exhaled—and the exhale stuttered, caught, shortened. The flame sank visibly, a tide going out, and somewhere far above them both worlds tilted a degree deeper toward evening.

The second mark was coming sooner than any of them had promised.

Maren rose, and took up the shroud, and led them down toward the fire-chamber at a pace that was, for the first time in two hundred years, a run.
