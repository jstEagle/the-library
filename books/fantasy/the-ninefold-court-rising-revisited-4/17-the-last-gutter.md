## 17. The Last Gutter

The flame chamber had no ceiling that Freya could see. The walls rose and kept rising until they became suggestion, then darkness, then nothing at all, and the only light came from the fire itself—a column of white-gold flame standing in a bowl of black stone the size of a village square. It had been tall when he first saw it, from Maren's gallery: a pillar of light like the memory of a sun. Now it barely reached his waist.

It guttered as he watched. Not the flicker of a candle in a draft but something deeper, a stutter in its existence, as if the fire were forgetting itself between one heartbeat and the next. Each time it dimmed, the dark pressed closer, and Freya felt it against his skin like cold water—felt it *weigh* on him, the way the court's presence always weighed, except now the weight was failing too, thinning, becoming hungry.

*It's dying,* he thought, with the strange calm of a man who has already made his decision and is only waiting for the world to catch up. *The whole rotten magnificent thing is dying, and everything it holds up is going down with it.*

Behind him, steel rang on stone.

He turned. In the doorway to the flame-chamber—tall enough to drive a cart and eight horses through, carved with nine thrones in relief along its lintel—Ilsa Vane stood with her sword drawn and her warrant-hand bleeding. She had burned the paper an hour ago; he could still smell it on her, ink and ash and stubbornness. Beyond her, coming up the stair in their terrible silence, were the court's vigil-keepers: nine of them, robed in grey that drank the light, moving without hurry because they had never once needed to hurry in four hundred years.

And past them all, at the far end of the gallery above, small and sharp-eyed against the dark, stood Maren with a clay jar cradled in both arms like a sleeping child.

Freya knew what was in the jar. He'd watched her make it. Sand from the river under the mountain—the dead river, the one whose water remembered being fire—and powdered bone-char, and three words of unmaking she had never taught him. A smothering-jar. Pour it into the bowl and the flame would go out like a pinched wick, instantly, mercifully, and every soul still burning inside it would be freed into the dark with nothing left to hold them together.

Free. And dissolved. Both at once.

"No," Freya said. He didn't raise his voice. Seven years of tasting had taught him that quiet carried further than shouting, in the right room. "Maren. Whatever you're about to do—you put that jar down."

"Whatever I'm about to do?" Maren's voice came down thin and clear as a wire. "I've been doing it for thirty years, taster. Slowly. Carefully. Every soul I've smuggled out, every name I've kept off the ledger—that's been my whole life's work, so that when this night came, I would be ready to end it." She lifted the jar slightly. "You've seen what's left in the flame. You've seen *her*. There's not enough of Bryn Galloway left to fill a thimble. Another hour of burning and there won't be anything at all—not even enough to free."

"She's in the vessel." His hand went to the charm at his chest, the one he'd built across two failures that had nearly cooked him alive, his immunity drinking the backlash each time like a man swallowing poison for another's sake. "I made it work. Third time. It carries a soul without burning it. I can lift her out whole—"

"You can lift out *one*." For the first time Maren's voice cracked, and beneath the crack was something old and exhausted and grieving. "There are nine hundred souls in that fire, boy. Nine hundred names nobody remembers. You want to save your sister and walk away while the rest of them burn down to smoke? Then let me finish it. Let me end the machine. That's mercy too. That's the only mercy big enough."

"The city goes dark," Ilsa said. Her sword-point didn't waver, but her voice did, just barely, and Freya heard in it the ward-keeper's arithmetic—the same arithmetic that had kept her awake in her cell of a office for twenty years. "No dragon-fire, no reflected heat, no light under the mountain or over it. Cinderfall freezes in a winter that won't end. You know the charts, Maren. You showed them to me yourself."

"I know," Maren said softly. "I've known for thirty years. I decided whose lives to spend a long time ago. Have you?"

The vigil-keepers reached the threshold. They did not draw weapons. They simply stood, nine grey shapes filling the doorway like a held breath, and the temperature of the room dropped, and the flame behind Freya shrank another inch, and he understood suddenly that they weren't here for Maren or for Ilsa.

They were here for the vessel on his chest. For the one soul that had ever been taken *out* of the fire.

"The court is owed," said the foremost keeper, in a voice like ash settling.

"The court is *owed*," Freya repeated. Something hot climbed his throat that wasn't fear. He'd tasted fear for seven years; he knew all its flavors, almond and bitterroot and copper, and this wasn't any of them. This was anger, plain and mortal and wonderful. "That's all any of you ever say. The court is owed. Well, I've read the ledger. I know exactly what this court is owed, because I know exactly what it paid." He stepped forward, putting himself between the keepers and the guttering fire. "It paid nothing. My father-city sold me for a founding charter and called it a price. Five years old. Two names on one page. And you—" he pointed at the lead keeper, and his finger shook, and he let it shake, "—you've spent four centuries collecting interest on stolen goods and calling yourselves creditors."

The keepers did not move. But the fire behind him flared—one sudden gasp of height, as if it had heard him and agreed.

And in that flare of light, Freya saw something he hadn't seen before. Saw it with the taster's eye, the eye that read a broth in one sip and knew which of nine cups held the true oath.

The flame wasn't just dying. It was *changing flavor*.

He crossed to the bowl's rim before the keepers could stop him—they didn't try; perhaps they couldn't imagine anyone wanting to stand that close—and looked down into the heart of the last fire in the world.

What burned there now was mostly ember: a bed of coals like a city seen from very high up at night, and threading through it, faint as breath on glass, the lights of souls. Hundreds of them. Most were down to sparks. Some were already gone—he could see the gaps, little holes in the pattern where a name had burned clean through—and the sight of them hit him like a fist, because he understood, finally and completely, what those gaps meant. Somewhere above, right now, people who had once loved those souls were finding they couldn't quite recall a face. Letters in old drawers were becoming addressed to no one. Whole lives were finishing the long work of never having mattered.

And near the center, one light still struggled. Small. Stubborn. Flickering in a rhythm he recognized without knowing how—which wasn't magic, he realized, it was just being someone's brother, some things you know in the marrow before you know them in the head.

*Bryn.*

"It doesn't have to be souls," Freya said aloud. He hadn't planned to say it. The words arrived the way the best dishes arrived, all at once, fully formed. "That's the lie at the bottom of everything. The founders assumed it, the court assumed it, even you assumed it, Maren—it burns souls or it dies. But I've spent seven years learning what else burns. What else feeds a fire meant to hold back the dark." He turned. The light of the failing flame caught his face, and he knew—he knew the way he knew a finished sauce—that every person in the chamber could see what he was about to do written there. "Seven years ago I walked into the palace kitchens and drank poison on purpose, a little at a time, to build myself into something the court couldn't take back easily. Do you know why the immunity works? Why I can stand next to this fire and not char, why the backlash breaks against me like weather against a sea-wall?"

Maren had gone very still. "Because it's theirs," she whispered. "Their investment. They've been paying interest on you since you were five."

"Exactly." He laughed, and it came out ragged. "They grew me like a crop. A body that could serve forever, proof against their own fire. Fourteen years of slow cultivation, seven of mine added on top. Do you know how much power that is? Do you know what it's worth?" He put his hand flat against his own chest, over the sternum, where the immunity lived—not in the blood, not in the charms, but somewhere deeper, a second self laid over the first like gilt over wood. "It's fuel. It's the finest fuel this court has ever refined, and it's sitting right here wearing an apron and counting coppers."

"Don't." Ilsa's voice, cracking all the way through now, duty and doubt and something rawer underneath. "Freya. There are other ways. We haven't tried—"

"There aren't." He smiled at her, and it cost him more than the smile had ever cost anyone. "You're the honest one, Captain. Don't make me lie to you. Nine hundred souls, one working vessel, and a fire that needs feeding tonight or everyone in it becomes a hole in the world. There's exactly one substance in this chamber that can substitute, and it took fourteen years to make." He looked at Maren. "Put the jar down. Please. I'm about to give your machine something better than souls, and if you smother it now you'll have murdered us all for nothing."

Maren's arms trembled. Thirty years of treason balanced in a clay pot. Then, slowly, she knelt and set it on the gallery floor, and sat back on her heels, and covered her face with her hands.

The vigil-keepers parted. Freya noted distantly that they parted *for* him, making a corridor to the bowl, and understood: the court wanted this. Had maybe always wanted this. A debt repaid in full by the debtor himself—clean, legal, final. Even now, at the end, everything here was bargains.

Fine, he thought. Let it be a bargain. Bargains were the one language he'd spoken fluently all his life.

He climbed the three steps to the bowl's rim. Up close, the fire was almost nothing—a hand's height of flame shivering over its embers, throwing heat that licked at him and found nothing to burn. The immunity rose to meet it, the way it always did, wrapping him in that familiar numb competence, and Freya thought: *seven years of never feeling anything. Seven years of tasting poison and feeling nothing. Time to feel it all at once.*

"I don't know the words," he said to the fire, conversationally, because talking had always steadied him. "Never learned the high liturgy. Never got the court's schooling. But I know contracts. I know price and payment and fair exchange, and here's mine." He spread his arms over the flame. "Take it back. All of it. The interest, the principal, the fourteen years of cultivation and the seven of service. Every drop of what you built me to be. I pour it back freely—not sold, not taken. *Given.* And in exchange: the souls stop being fuel. All of them. Tonight. That's the new contract, and consent freely given is the one thing you never priced, so you'll honor it whether you want to or not."

The flame went utterly still.

Then it leaned toward him like a listener.

He began to pull it out. There was no gesture for it, no spell—he simply did what he had done ten thousand times at the tasting table, reversed it: instead of taking a sip and keeping himself safe, he opened himself and let the safety drain away. And oh, it hurt. Oh, it *hurt*, the way thawing hurts, the way feeling begins. He felt the first year go—his twelfth, the hedge-witch's kitchen, the smell of burnt honey—and the second, and the taste of every poison he had ever swallowed came back all at once with nothing between him and it, arsenic and nightshade and the green bite of hemlock, seven years of small deliberate deaths all arriving together, and he poured them into the fire like wine into a wedding cup.

The flame rose.

It rose like a thing waking, like dawn happening underground, gold climbing to white climbing to a brightness that filled the whole impossible chamber and threw the nine keepers' shadows huge and wavering up the unseen walls. Through the roar of it—through the agony of it, his hands on the black stone rim, his whole borrowed armor of numbness streaming out of him—he heard the embers below begin to sing, hundreds of tiny voices, sparks lifting free of the coal-bed and hanging in the updraft like a reversal of snowfall.

Souls, rising. Fed, not consumed. Carried on fuel that had never belonged to them.

His knees went. Someone caught him—Ilsa, her armored arm hard across his chest, lowering him down while the fire thundered, and her face above him was wet and furious and shining.

"You idiot," she was saying. "You brave, stupid, magnificent idiot—"

"Maren," he managed. The word cost him most of what he had left. "The vessel. While the fire's high. Get her out. Get my sister—"

Above, on the gallery, Maren was already moving, jar abandoned, hurrying down with her sleeves pinned up like a scullery maid, like the woman she'd been before the court bought her too. The flame blazed steady now, fed on seven years of a food-taster's purchased invulnerability, and it would blaze a long time yet—months, maybe years, enough time, enough time for whatever came next.

Freya lay on the warm stone and felt the shape of himself, the way you press your tongue into the gap where a tooth was. Nothing there. No wall between him and the world. He could feel everything now—the heat, the grief, the terror, the wild unbearable hope—and every feeling went through him undefended, all the way to the bottom.

It was, he thought hazily, the worst and finest thing he had ever tasted.

Then, through the blaze, through the rising sparks, he heard it: a sound from the throne-side door, slow and deliberate, the scrape of something vast settling forward onto a dais that had stood empty all night.

Footsteps. Coming down.

The thing that wore a king's shape had come to see what had become of its investment—and it did not look pleased, and it did not look like a king anymore, and it stopped at the edge of the light with the ledger open in its hands.

"Payment received," it said. "*In part.*"
