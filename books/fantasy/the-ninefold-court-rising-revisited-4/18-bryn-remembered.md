## 18. Bryn, Remembered

The vessel held.

Freya had built it twice and failed twice—once in Maren's workroom with the walls weeping condensation from the mountain's breath, once in the antechamber with his hands still shaking from a backlash that should have stopped his heart. Both times the charmwork had come apart like wet bread. Both times his immunity had swallowed what should have killed him, the court's old investment in him drinking down its own poison, and he had lain on cold stone thinking: *this is what I am for. This is all I was ever for.*

But the third vessel—the one he shaped not from craft alone but from everything he'd hoarded like a miser across seven years of small sins, every half-remembered lesson from the hedge-witch Sable's kitchen, every stolen gesture of charmwork performed under tables and behind steam, every night he had whispered spells to himself just to remember he existed—it held.

It looked like nothing. That was the point. A taster learns that poison hides best in plain dishes, so he had made the soul-vessel plain: a shallow bowl of fired clay glazed the grey of dishwater, no sigils visible, no light leaking from its seams. Inside it, folded small as a moth at rest, he carried what he had lifted from the flame-chamber's central fire while the thing on the throne watched him with something that might have been fondness.

Now he knelt before it in the long gallery beneath the mountain, where nine thrones stood in their crescent and eight were empty and the ninth was occupied by a king-shape that did not breathe. The dragon-fire veins along the walls had dimmed to a sullen orange, as if the mountain itself were holding its breath.

"Say when," Maren said softly. She stood at his shoulder, her steward's chain hanging loose and tarnished, her hands clasped tight enough that her knuckles had gone white. She had wanted this fire dead entirely; she had nearly smothered it two nights past, and Freya and Ilsa together had dragged her back from that brink. She had not forgiven them. She had agreed to help anyway, which Freya suspected was the closest thing to forgiveness she knew how to give.

Behind them, near the gallery's mouth where the descent road climbed toward the world of stars—or the world that used to have stars—Ilsa Vane kept watch, her burned warrant's ash still ground into the creases of her palms. She had refused to stand closer. "I don't want to see her wake up looking at a stranger," she'd said. But she hadn't left either. She stood like a ward-keeper at a door that had no more wards, guarding nothing, guarding everything.

"When," Freya said.

Maren unbound the last knot of the working, and the vessel opened.

There was no thunderclap. No burst of light. What came out of the clay bowl was quieter than that and infinitely larger: a warmth, first, like an oven door opening in winter, like bread, like a kitchen—and Freya would understand later why his soul chose that shape of memory, but in the moment he only felt it wash over him and thought absurdly, *someone is baking.*

Then the warmth gathered itself. It drew upward out of the bowl the way smoke draws upward, except that smoke has no intention, and this did. It turned in the air above the vessel, slowly, the way a person turns when they wake and don't yet know where they are. It had no face. It had no hands. And it was the most familiar thing Freya Galloway had ever seen.

Because he knew it. Not her name—he didn't have her name yet, not truly—but the *shape* of her presence, the particular weight of attention that some souls carry. He had been five years old when they took her. Thirty years of forgetting could erase a face, a voice, a name. It could not erase the way it felt to be known by someone before you knew yourself.

"Bryn," he said. His voice cracked on it. He hadn't said the name aloud since he was five. He wasn't sure he'd ever said it aloud; maybe he'd only thought it, in the orphanage cot, in the dark. "Bryn. It's me."

The warmth stilled.

And then, in a voice that was not a voice—that arrived inside him rather than through his ears, the way charmwork arrives, the way meaning arrives in dreams—a child spoke his childhood name.

"*Fen.*"

The word went through him like a blade going through water: no resistance, total depth. He had not heard that name in thirty years. He had buried it so deep that even he had trouble finding it; he had been Freya Galloway for so long that Fen was a coat he'd outgrown and given away and forgotten he'd ever owned. But she said it—*Fen*—with the exact cadence a small girl uses for her brother's name when she wants him to come look at something, come quick, Fen, come see—and thirty years fell off him like a burnt skin.

"Yes," he said. He was crying. He noted it distantly, the way he noted flavors: salt, heat, something bitter underneath. "Yes. I'm here. I came for you. I'm sorry it took—" His voice failed. "I'm sorry it took so long."

"*You got big,*" she said, wonderingly. "*Fen, you got big. You're all tall and sad.*"

A laugh tore out of him, ugly and wet and real. "That's me. Tall and sad."

"*Did you bring sweets?*"

"I—" He fumbled at his coat like a fool, patting pockets that contained charmwork components and a stolen ledger-page and nothing sweet at all, and then stopped, because she was laughing too, or doing whatever laughter was for a soul without lungs, a bright ripple in the warmth. She remembered teasing him. She remembered *him*. Somewhere inside the flame that ate names, she had kept one name safe after all, and it was his.

"The only proof I ever had a family," he said to Maren, not caring that his voice shook, "is a dead girl calling me by a name nobody living remembers." He wiped his face with his sleeve. "Worth it. Whatever it costs. Worth it."

He should have known better than to say *whatever it costs* in this place. Words were contracts here. He was learning that the hard way.

The warmth drifted lower, circling the vessel, examining her own deliverance with a child's practical curiosity. "*Is this mine? Can I keep it?*" She meant the body-shaped hollow of the charmwork, the vessel he'd built to hold a soul without burning it. Twice it had failed. Third time, it held.

"It's yours," Freya said. "It's yours forever. Nothing here burns you anymore. I promise."

"*You promised last time you wouldn't tell Cook I fed the gulls the fish.*"

"You fed the gulls the fish?"

"*They looked hungry, Fen.*"

And there it was again, that lurching vertigo of tenderness, grief arriving years late and all at once. He had spent seven years building himself into a man with no attachments, no hopes, no history—a man who was only a tongue and a set of careful hands—and in three minutes his sister had dismantled all of it by remembering that he'd once ratted her out to the kitchens.

"Ilsa," he called, without turning around. "Come here."

"I don't think—"

"She won't bite. She doesn't have teeth. Come meet my sister."

Boots on stone. Then Ilsa Vane, disgraced keeper of fallen wards, stood at the edge of the lamplight with her hand resting on a sword hilt she had sworn never to draw in this place, and Bryn's warmth turned toward her, and Bryn said, "*Oh. She's sad too. Why is everyone down here so sad?*"

"They're working on it," Freya said.

* * *

For perhaps a hundred heartbeats—for perhaps an hour; time ran strangely in the Ninefold Court, stretched thin as pulled sugar—the gallery held a peace it had likely never known. A freed soul turning slowly above a dishwater-grey bowl. A renegade steward watching with an expression Freya couldn't read and suspected was hope, which frightened her worse than treason had. An exiled ward-keeper standing awkward guard over a ghost-child. And Freya kneeling in the middle of it, letting himself feel the thing he'd forbidden himself for thirty years, which was simply *having.*

Then the throne moved.

Not the king-shape upon it—though that stirred too, unfolding by degrees like a spider uncurling, and Freya felt its attention swing toward them with the pressure of a falling building—but the throne itself, the seat of black stone that had stood empty and waiting through everything, began to sound. A low tone, subterranean, felt in the teeth and the sternum rather than heard. Around the crescent, one by one, the eight empty thrones answered it, each with its own pitch, until the nine notes wove together into a chord that was unmistakably, appallingly, a demand.

"Oh no," Maren breathed. "No, no—you *fool*, you beautiful fool, do you know what you've done?"

"What?" Freya didn't take his eyes from Bryn. The warmth had begun to shiver, drawn thin between his sister's presence and the pull of that chord. "What have I done?"

"You've taken delivery." Maren's voice was flat with dread. "That's what the contract calls it. Delivery of goods. For thirty years your soul sat in their ledger as an asset—unclaimed, dormant, accruing. Tonight you walked in and exercised a claim against the estate. You withdrew property. Property that was theirs." She pointed one trembling finger at the vessel, at the warmth, at the whole impossible miracle of her. "That's not theft to them. Theft they can punish. This is *breach*. And the founding charter has remedies for breach."

The chord deepened. Along the walls, the dragon-fire veins flared—not the guttering orange of a dying flame but a hard, hungry white, the color the fire must have been when the world was young and the dragons still lived. Heat pressed against Freya's face. His immunity, that vast reservoir he had already begun pouring out to fuel the vessel, stirred uneasily, like a dog that smells its own blood.

From the ninth throne, the king-shape spoke. It had worn a courtier's courtesy in their earlier meetings; it wore none now. What remained was older and vaster and very tired, the way a debt is tired, the way a hunger is tired.

"*THE ACCOUNT IS OPEN,*" it said, and the words arrived everywhere at once, in the stone, in the fire, in the marrow of his bones. "*ONE SOUL WITHDRAWN WITHOUT PAYMENT. THE LEDGER DEMANDS RESTORATION.*"

"Take it up with the founders," Ilsa snapped, stepping forward despite herself, despite everything. "Take it up with the city that sold these children to you. They wrote your precious charter. They signed. Not him—he was five years old. You can't hold a five-year-old to a contract."

"*WE HAVE HELD FIVE-YEAR-OLDS TO CONTRACTS FOR THREE THOUSAND YEARS,*" the court said, without malice, which was somehow worse. "*IT IS THE ONLY KIND OF CONTRACT WE HAVE EVER BEEN OFFERED.*"

"That ends tonight," Freya said. He rose. His knees ached; he was thirty-five and felt ninety and expected to die young, and none of it mattered. "You want repayment? Fine. Name it. But Bryn goes free regardless. She's not collateral. She's not interest. She's *out*."

Bryn's warmth pressed against his shoulder—against the place a small sister would stand, tucked under an arm—and said, in the smallest voice yet, "*Fen? Are we in trouble?*"

"No," he lied, gently, the way you lie to children about storms. "We're negotiating."

Above them, the nine thrones rang their terrible chord again, and beneath the chord, rising through the mountain like water through a drowning ship, Freya heard something else: the sound of a flame dropping low. The central fire—the great engine that lit both worlds, that burned souls into light, that had eaten his sister down to a candle-flame—was guttering. Starved by Bryn's removal, starved by the eight souls he and Maren had already freed into vessels, starved by three thousand years of spending principal instead of interest, it was going out.

The court convulsed around its own hunger. And the king-shape leaned forward on its throne, and named its price.

"*RESTORATION,*" it said. "*OR COLLAPSE. CHOOSE QUICKLY, TASTER. YOU HAVE ALWAYS BEEN QUICK WITH YOUR TONGUE.*"
