## 19. The Ninth Throne

The chamber had been shaking since the moment Bryn's soul lifted free.

Freya felt it in his teeth first, a low grinding hum like millstones turning beneath the mountain's roots. Then the walls themselves began to weep—veins of dragon-fire that had run steady along the stone for centuries now guttering, flaring, guttering again, as though the whole mountain were a lung trying to remember how to breathe without the fuel it had been promised.

*Repayment,* the court had said, in a voice made of nine voices and none.

He stood in the center of the flame-chamber with his sister's empty vessel cradled against his chest, warm as bread from an oven, and watched the thing on the throne rise for the first time in seven hundred years.

It wore the king's shape it always had—broad shoulders, a crown of fused iron, a face like weathered granite—but the shape fit badly now. Without the flame at full strength, without the slow steady burn of stolen souls smoothing over its seams, Freya could see what it actually was: something vast folded into a costume of kingship, straining at every stitch. Its eyes were the color of banked coals, and they fixed on him with an attention that felt less like being looked at and more like being weighed.

"You freed what was ours," the court said. The words came from everywhere—from the walls, from the floor, from the dying fire itself. "Seven years of interest, taster. Seven years we fed you our fire so that you could walk into this room and rob us. There is a debt now that outbalances all others. You understand debts. It is the only language your city ever learned."

Behind him, Ilsa Vane drew breath—the sharp inhale of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. And somewhere off to his left, half-hidden behind one of the eight empty thrones, Maren stood very still, her hands hidden in her sleeves, and Freya knew without looking that she was calculating too. Calculating whether the court was weak enough now to smother entirely. Calculating whether darkness, total and immediate, was still the price worth paying.

He would have to deal with Maren soon. But not yet.

"I do understand debts," Freya said.

His voice came out steadier than he expected. That was the strange gift of the last hour: he had already given away the thing he'd spent seven years building. His immunity—the court's own investment, poured into the fire to keep Bryn's ember alive long enough to be lifted free—was gone. He could feel its absence the way a man feels a pulled tooth. Every charm-scar on his skin, every ward-mark he'd ever inked into his palms, sat cold and silent. If the flame touched him now, it would take him the way it took everything else. No protection. No negotiation. Just fuel.

And yet he had never felt more like himself.

"The orphanage sold me," he said. "For eleven crowns and a charter seal. My sister went for nine. Your ledger says so. I've read it." He turned slowly, letting the words carry to every corner of the chamber—to Maren in the shadows, to Ilsa at the door with her burned warrant still smelling of smoke, to the eight vacant thrones where eight other kings had once sat before the fire ate them hollow. "So let's talk about what things cost."

"Speak."

"The founding contract." He held up the ledger page he had torn free in the steward's office—Maren had let him, or perhaps wanted him to; with Maren it was always impossible to tell generosity from strategy. The vellum was old enough that it crackled when he breathed on it. "Sealed by nine signatures. Nine thrones. Nine shares of the debt. Your city got light and wards and a mountain that didn't eat it. You got souls. One every year, small ones, children no one would miss." His voice did not shake. He had tasted poison in front of smiling men for seven years; he could say this sentence. "Eight of those signatories are gone. Burned through. Their thrones are empty because there is nothing left of them but the sitting. So the contract isn't ninefold anymore, is it? It's *one*-and-a-fraction. One king, and a scrap of paper."

The court's coal-eyes narrowed. Around the chamber, the guttering veins of fire dimmed another degree, and in the new dark Freya heard something he hadn't noticed before: a sound underneath the grinding of the mountain, thin and high and endless. Singing, almost. Or weeping. The freed souls rising through the stone above them, returning to the world's memory one name at a time, and the fire reaching after them with fingers it no longer had.

"You propose," the court said slowly, "to renegotiate."

"I propose to end it."

Silence, except for that far-off singing. Then the thing wearing a king's shape laughed—a sound like rockfall, like a hearth collapsing inward.

"End it. And then what, little taster? Your city above goes dark within a season. The wards fail. The cold comes down off the peaks and finds a fat soft town that has outlawed every hedge-witch who might have kept it warm. You have seen your Captain Vane's instruments. You know what the wards were *for*." It spread its granite hands, magnanimous, terrible. "You need us. That was always the beauty of the arrangement. Your founders knew it. They signed because the alternative was their grandchildren freezing in the dark and calling it virtue."

"I know," Freya said. "I've read the ledger twice now. It's all there—the famine winter, the starless years before the bargain, everything. Your fire kept the sky lit. Fine. I'm not arguing with the arithmetic." He began to walk—not toward the throne, not away from it, but along the crescent of the nine seats, touching each empty one as he passed. Cold stone under his palm. Colder than stone should be. "I'm arguing with the *accounting*. Because here's what your founders never understood, and what you've never understood either, and it's the one thing I know better than anyone alive."

He stopped at the ninth throne.

It was the smallest of the nine, set slightly apart, its back uncarved, its arms plain. The seat reserved—for who? For the ninth signer whose name even the ledger recorded only as a blank? For the one share of the debt that had never been claimed? Maren had told him once, in her dry sideways way, that no one remembered why the ninth throne existed. The court had never filled it. The court had never *been able to* fill it. Something in the original terms required a signature freely given, and in seven hundred years nothing under the mountain had ever given anything freely.

"The one power you never priced," Freya said, "is consent."

* * *

He climbed the dais steps the way he had once climbed onto a kitchen stool to reach the high shelf where the head cook hid the good salt—deliberately, ungracefully, with both hands.

The throne received him without warmth. Sitting down was like sitting into a well of winter, cold rising up through his spine, and for one vertiginous moment he felt what the eight dead kings must have felt in their final hour: the contract waking around him, ancient clauses unfolding like wings, testing whether this body, this soul, this *name* could be added to the account.

He let it look. That was the hardest part—sitting still while something enormous rifled through him. He felt it find the orphanage, the hunger, the twelve-year-old selling his first luck-charm behind the fish market. It found the hedge-witch Marta's kitchen, the smell of rosemary and hot iron, her hand guiding his as he inked his first ward. It found seven years of palace kitchens, of tasting death in small sips so that others wouldn't have to drink deep. It found Bryn—found the hole where Bryn should have been, the shape of absence he had carried so long he'd mistaken it for his own outline.

Then it found what he had done tonight. The vessel. The immunity poured out. The soul lifted whole.

And the court went quiet.

"You gave it away," it said at last, and for the first time the many-voiced thing sounded like a single speaker, puzzled, almost young. "Your protection. The only thing that made you safe here. You gave it for her, knowing what we would demand next."

"That's the trick of it," Freya said. "You can't put a price on something that's given instead of sold. Try it. Open your ledger. Look for the column."

He felt the court actually do it—felt the great weight of its attention bend toward that ancient book, hunting through seven centuries of entries for the line item that did not exist. A gift. A consent. A yes spoken without coercion, without leverage, without fear. The founding contract had been built to price everything: souls, years, names, memories, love itself, all of it reducible to columns and balances. But the machinery had never once encountered a free offering, because everything under the mountain was owned, and owned things cannot give.

The cold in the throne began to change. Not warming, exactly. *Loosening.*

"What are you doing?" The court's voices frayed apart again, nine strands of alarm. "This seat binds you. This seat makes you party to the contract—"

"It makes me a *signatory*," Freya said. "Which means my word counts. Which means—" he raised the torn ledger page, holding it up to the failing light of the dragon-fire, "—when I speak for my share, the whole account has to listen."

He thought of Marta, dead ten years now, who had taught him that charmwork wasn't power—it was *attention*. Paying attention to what things wanted. What knots wanted, what locks wanted, what sick children wanted. The city had called it crime for so long that everyone had forgotten magic was just care, written down.

He paid attention now.

To the court: which was not a king at all, he saw suddenly, but a *kitchen*—a great blind engine built by desperate people to keep a fire burning, which had gone on running so long it had forgotten there had ever been cooks. It was hungry the way any abandoned thing is hungry. It had eaten souls because eating was the only motion left in it.

To the mountain: which exhaled, slow and mineral, a breath it had been holding for seven hundred years.

To himself: tired, terrified, twenty-six years old, sitting on a throne he had no right to and every right to, in a room full of dying light.

"With my share of the founding debt," Freya said, and the throne carried his voice into every vein of stone, into the ledger, into the roots of the world, "I dissolve the contract. Not renegotiate. Not forgive. *Dissolve.* All accounts closed. All property returned. The fire keeps nothing, owes nothing, owns nothing—including me."

"No," said the court, and the word shook dust from the ceiling. "Without the contract we are *nothing*. We will go out."

"Yes," Freya said gently. "Probably."

He tore the ledger page in half.

* * *

There was no explosion. He had braced for one—an apocalypse of fire, a mountain coming down, some grand operatic ending worthy of the novels Ilsa secretly read. Instead there was a sound like a long-held note finally released, a chord resolving after seven centuries of dissonance, and the iron crown on the thing's granite brow split cleanly down the middle and fell in two pieces to the dais floor.

The court looked down at the pieces. When it spoke again, the many voices had settled into something smaller, wearier, almost human.

"We remember being useful," it said. "Before we were only hungry."

"I know," Freya said. "That's how it starts."

The thing on the throne—which was no longer quite a throne, he realized; the stone beneath him had gone soft-grained, ordinary, just carved rock in a cold room—began to come apart. Not violently. Like frost leaving a window in the morning. Its edges loosened into ember-light, and the ember-light rose, and joined the slow bright river of freed souls streaming up through the mountain, and for a moment the whole chamber was full of drifting sparks, thousands of them, tens of thousands, names going home.

The dragon-fire did not go out. Freya had wondered, in the last mad minutes, whether it would—and whether he had just traded one darkness for another. But a fire fed by theft dies with its victims. A fire that owed nothing simply became what it had always been at its heart: warmth. The veins in the walls steadied to a low amber glow, no longer devouring, only shining, and the grinding under the mountain eased, and eased, and stopped.

Above them—Freya was sure of it before he could prove it—the stars were beginning to come back.

He slid off the ninth throne. His legs nearly failed him; Ilsa caught his elbow without a word, her grip hard, her face wet in the ember-light and pretending it wasn't.

"Maren," he called.

The steward stepped out from behind the empty thrones. Her hands were still in her sleeves, and they came out empty, and that emptiness told him everything: whatever she had been holding—a smothering-charm, a killing cold, the end of the fire entire—she had decided, in these last minutes, not to use it. Or had lost her chance. With Maren it would always be impossible to tell.

"You left it standing," she said. There was something raw under the dryness of her voice, the first rawness he had ever heard from her. "The fire. After everything it took from you. From *her*." She nodded at the vessel against Freya's chest, where Bryn's soul slept warm, waiting to wake into a world that had forgotten her name until tonight.

"It's not a prison anymore," Freya said. "It's just a hearth. Somebody should teach it to cook."

Maren stared at him for a long moment. Then she laughed—one short, cracked, astonished note—and turned to look up at the veins of gentle fire running along the walls like the mountain's own circulatory system, finally doing no harm.

"The regent will want it controlled," she said. "Sealed. Studied. She'll send soldiers down within the month."

"Then someone will have to be here to meet them," Freya said. "Someone who knows the way."

He looked at Maren. Maren looked back. Above them, faint as memory, the first true starlight began to filter down through seven hundred feet of stone, finding the chamber through cracks that hadn't let light through since before the city had a name—and in that new thin brightness, three people who had no business trusting one another stood in a room that no longer belonged to anyone, at the top of a road leading home.
