## 20. Ascension Without Stars

The stairs went on longer than any staircase had a right to.

Ilsa Vane counted them at first—ward-keeper's habit, counting was a way of staying sane in the dark—but she lost the count somewhere past four thousand and let it go. The mountain did not want to be counted. The mountain wanted to be climbed, and that was all it would permit.

Freya climbed ahead of her, one hand trailing the wall. He moved like a man who had been emptied and refilled with something unfamiliar, the way a jug tastes of whatever was poured into it last. His immunity was gone. She had watched him pour it out, seven years of slow poison and stubborn flesh, into a fire that ate names, and what remained was thinner than the man who had sat in her interrogation cell two weeks ago, smiling his market-stall smile, lying to her with every tooth he owned.

He wasn't lying now. That was the strange part. He hadn't said more than forty words since the throne room, and not one of them had been a lie.

"Rest," she said, and her voice came out sanded flat by the climb.

He stopped without argument. That too was new. In the cells below the Wardhouse, Freya Galloway had argued about everything—the temperature of the room, the legality of his arrest, the quality of the bread. Now he lowered himself onto the step like an old man and put his back against the warm stone, and Ilsa realized the stone *was* warm, faintly, all through the mountain's bones, the way a hearthstone stays warm long after the fire dies.

She wondered how long it would stay warm now. She wondered if anyone knew.

"Your lamp's guttering," Freya said.

"I know."

"You've been saying you know for six hours."

"I've also been right for six hours." She unhooked the lamp from her belt and turned the wick down anyway, hoarding light the way she'd learned to hoard everything in twenty years of service—a grain of salt, a scrap of warrant-paper, a truth kept in a drawer where it couldn't hurt anyone. "There's enough to reach the top."

"And if there isn't?"

"Then we'll have a very quiet conversation in the dark, and I'll finally ask you all the questions I didn't get to ask you in the cells."

His mouth twitched. It wasn't quite a smile. It was the memory of one, worn smooth. "You'd waste a dying lamp on questions?"

"I'd waste anything on answers." She said it before she could decide whether to say it, and then, because there was no taking it back and no reason to take it back: "I spent my whole career believing the wards were a shield. You were under the mountain two days and you learned they were a leash. Do you understand what that makes me? Twenty years of guarding a kennel door and calling it a city gate."

"It makes you someone who opened the door," Freya said.

"That's not absolution."

"No," he agreed. "But it's not nothing."

Above them, far above, where the shaft of the old vent-road narrowed toward the mouth of the mountain, there was a darkness that was not the ordinary darkness of stone. Ilsa felt it the way you feel weather coming in your teeth. The sky. The starless sky that had swallowed Cinderfall nineteen nights ago, when the wards fell with a sound like a wineglass breaking in a god's fist, and the stars went with them.

Except—

She frowned. She had been frowning at it for the last hour without letting herself name it, the way you don't name a sound in an empty house until you absolutely must. But it was unmistakable now, up here where the dark was thinnest.

The sky was not entirely black anymore.

"Freya."

"I see it." He was already standing, hand pressed to the stone, head tipped back. "I've been seeing it since midnight. I thought I was imagining it."

"What is it?"

He was quiet a moment. When he spoke, his voice had the careful weight of a man handling something breakable. "When the fire ate a soul, it ate the name too. Everything that soul ever touched got a little dimmer. Every person who remembered them remembered less. Maren said—" he stopped, steadied himself. "Maren said some of the stars were souls. Old ones. Court-taken children from the first centuries, burned so long ago their names had gone out of the sky entirely. And the ones we freed last night—"

"The ledger," Ilsa said slowly. "Every name in that ledger."

"Goes home," Freya finished. "Wherever home is. For some of them, I think home is up there."

They climbed faster after that. Neither of them said so; neither needed to. It was the first time in nineteen days that Ilsa had climbed toward something instead of away from it, and her body understood the difference before her mind consented to it, legs burning, lungs raw, the lamp swinging its small defiant circle of light against the dark.

* * *

They came out of the mountain at dawn—or at what would have been dawn, once, when dawn had a sun to recommend it.

The mouth of the vent-road opened onto the shoulder of the mountain itself, a shelf of black granite furred with frost, and beyond it the world fell away in terraces toward Cinderfall, far below and impossibly small, a scatter of lights that should not have existed and yet did—candlelight, lamplight, the orange bloom of the great beacon-fires the regent had ordered lit along the walls. A city refusing to go dark. Ilsa looked at those lights and felt something in her chest turn over, painful and tender, like a bruise being pressed.

And above it all, the sky.

It was not the sky she had grown up under. That sky had been a certainty, a map, a clock—she had taken her bearings by it for twenty years, had taught recruits to find the Warden's Star and never think twice about the fact that the Warden's Star had a name at all, because names were just things the sky had always had.

Now the sky was mostly empty, a vast ash-grey vault with no moon and no sun and only the palest suggestion of light pooling along one edge of the horizon, light with no source, light that simply *was*, the way warmth lingered in a hearthstone. But scattered across that emptiness, sparse as survivors after a plague, stars were coming back.

Not all at once. Not gently, either.

Ilsa watched one of them return and it was not like watching a candle being lit. It was like watching a wound close. The dark above the eastern ridgeline shivered—and within the shiver something gathered, coalesced, fought its way back into being the way a drowning woman fights toward the surface, and then flared, sudden and hard and white, and Ilsa heard herself make a sound she had not made since she was a child.

Because she knew it. She knew that star. The Shepherd's Eye, second from the left hand of the winter constellation, the one her father had pointed out to her from the rooftops of the Tanner's Quarter before the ward-service took her at nine years old. *That one's yours, little hawk. Born under it, watchful, stubborn.* She had forgotten that. She had genuinely, completely forgotten it, the memory filed away somewhere so deep she'd have sworn under oath it had never existed—and now it came back whole, her father's smoke-rough voice, the cold roof tiles, the exact smell of tallow and wool, and she understood, standing on the frozen shoulder of the world with her heart slamming against her ribs:

That was what it meant, the returning. Every star was a soul, or carried one, or was stitched through with one, and every soul that came back brought its name with it, and every name dragged its memories behind it like a bride's train—and the memories were landing on people. On everyone. All across the darkened city below, all across the world, people were waking up this morning remembering things they had lost without knowing they'd lost them, dead siblings, drowned friends, faces of grandparents, entire childhoods reassembling themselves out of nothing—

And it hurt. That was the thing no one would say in the histories, if there were histories. It hurt. The star flared white and Ilsa's eyes streamed and her skull felt split open, and beside her Freya had dropped to one knee with both hands over his face, and when she reached for him his shoulders were shaking.

"Whose?" she asked. Her voice cracked on the single syllable.

He didn't answer for a long time. The wind came off the empty sky and pushed at them, smelling of frost and far-away snow and, faintly, unbelievably, of woodsmoke from the city below, from breakfast fires, from morning, from life going stubbornly on.

"Bryn," he said finally. "Her star's not up yet. Hers was the newest name. It takes—I don't know. Longer." He lowered his hands. His eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "But I can feel her. Somewhere between the mountain and the sky. Coming home the long way." He laughed, a broken scrap of a sound. "Seven years I built myself into a man with no family, no name worth keeping, no hope. Rules instead of a sister. And it turns out the whole time there was a piece of me walking around inside a fire, waiting for me to come get it."

"She waited," Ilsa said.

"She waited." He said it like a man testing whether the words would hold his weight. They held.

Ilsa turned back to the sky. Another star kindled, far to the south, and the pain of it rolled over the mountain in a silent pressure-wave, and somewhere below in Cinderfall she knew a thousand people had just clutched their heads and then each other. More were coming. The ledger had held thousands of names. Nine centuries of names. The sky would be relearning itself for years.

And beneath her feet, the mountain breathed. There was no other word for it. The stone expanded and contracted with a rhythm slow as tide, and the dragon-fire veins that laced the vent-road behind them glowed steady and low—not the ravening hunger-fire of the court, but something banked, something *fed freely*, something that would burn now on consent instead of theft, thin and honest, enough to keep the wards' distant grandchildren alive, enough to keep the border stones warm. Freya's gift, still paying interest. It would for a long time. Maybe forever.

Maybe forever, she thought, and found she could bear the size of it.

"We should go down," Freya said. "Before the regent decides the mountain's a threat again and sends the garrison up to seal us in a tomb."

"He already has." Ilsa pulled the folded papers from inside her coat, and even now, frost-rimed and travel-worn, she could feel the weight of them—the seal of the regency, the wax stamped with the nine-pointed ward-star, the orders written in a clerk's careful hand: *Captain Ilsa Vane is hereby instructed to collapse the vent-road, seal the mountain entire, and report the destruction of all persons and evidence within.*

She had carried those orders down the mountain and up again. Through the flame-chamber. Past the throne. She had read them by dragon-light while a steward committed treason ten feet away and a food-taster poured seven years of himself into a dying fire.

She unfolded them now, and the wind took at the edges, and Freya watched her with the particular stillness of a man who has learned what paper can cost.

"They sent me to bury it," Ilsa said. "Whatever happened down there. Seal it, collapse it, and report that the court destroyed itself and no witnesses survived. A tidy lie. Clean borders, clean story." She looked at the papers a long moment. Then she walked to the lip of the vent-mouth, where the old ward-anchor stone jutted from the granite like a broken tooth, and she struck flint to her lamp, and she set the regent's orders on fire.

The wax bubbled. The nine-pointed star curled black and let go of itself. The wind peeled the burning pages off the stone one by one and carried them out over the void, sparks streaming, and Ilsa stood at attention while they died, because some part of her insisted that even treason deserved a salute.

"Vane." Freya's voice was very quiet. "You understand what that costs you. Your commission. Your pension. Probably your neck."

"My neck," Ilsa said, "has been property of the Crown since I was nine years old. I'm told it's possible to buy things back." She brushed the ash from her gloves. "I spent twenty years keeping a kennel and calling it a gate. I'm done reporting tidy lies. When we get down that mountain, I'm going to walk into the regent's hall, still in uniform, and I am going to tell the truth. All of it. The charter. The orphanage. The ledger. The founding sin, in front of the whole court, in front of the city if they'll have me. Let them hang me for it. At least they'll hang me for something true."

Freya looked at her for a long moment, this man who had lied professionally for seven years, who had trusted no one and nothing, and there was something in his face she had never seen there—not gratitude exactly, and not surprise, but the expression of a man discovering that the world contained one more honest thing than he had budgeted for.

"They won't believe you," he said. "A disgraced ward-keeper and a convicted charm-thief walk out of a sealed mountain claiming the sky used to be full of stolen children. Half the city will call it treason. The other half will call it madness."

"Probably." Above them, another star caught fire, and the pain of its naming rolled down through Ilsa's teeth, and this time she didn't flinch. She let it hurt. It seemed important, suddenly, to stop flinching. "But look up, Galloway. Look at the sky. By tomorrow night there will be fifty more. By next month, hundreds. Every one of them proof. I don't have to convince anyone of anything." She allowed herself—one degree, no more—a smile. "The truth is going to keep arriving overhead, all night, every night, whether the regent likes it or not. I'm just the first witness."

Freya turned his face up to the rekindling dark. And though his own star was not yet risen—though Bryn was still traveling the long road home—he stood there in the wind on the shoulder of the mountain, and Ilsa saw him do the hardest thing she had seen anyone do in twenty years of service, harder than burning warrants, harder than pouring out one's own soul:

He hoped. Openly. With nothing to protect himself with. No immunity, no charms, no rules, no lies. Just a man in a burnt coat looking up at a sky that was learning his sister's name.

"Come on," he said at last, voice rough. "It's a long way down."

"And every step of it true," Ilsa said, and shouldered her pack, and led the way.

Behind them the mountain exhaled, warm and slow, and above them the stars came home one aching name at a time, and far below, in the grey half-light of a wounded city, the regent's hall was already filling with people who had woken that morning remembering things—and asking, loudly, dangerously, whose fault it was.
