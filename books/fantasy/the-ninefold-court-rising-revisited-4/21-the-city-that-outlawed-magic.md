## 21. The City That Outlawed Magic

The mountain let him go the way a fist lets go of something it has held too long—slowly, finger by finger, with a reluctance that left marks.

Freya climbed the last of the switchback road in daylight, which felt like an obscenity. He had gone down into dark and fire and the smell of old gold; he had sat on a throne older than the city's name; he had poured seven years of himself into a flame and watched it take. And now the sun lay warm on the scree, ordinary as bread, and Cinderfall's smoke rose against a sky still scrubbed bare of stars. The wards' collapse had not been repaired. Nothing had been repaired. The city above had spent two months learning what it felt like to live under an open wound.

He was thin. He noticed it the way you notice someone else's coat: distantly, without claim. The immunity that had armored him since he was eighteen—the slow accumulation of poisons survived, the court's interest paid out in flesh that refused to fail—was gone. The first blistering wind off the mountain had chapped his lips raw. The first bad smell from the tanneries had turned his stomach. His body had become merely a body again, and every step down the road reminded him of the price of tickets he no longer held.

Ilsa walked half a pace behind him, as she had for most of the descent. She had burned her warrant in the flame-chamber, and the ash of it was still somewhere in the seams of her gloves. She hadn't spoken in an hour. Neither had he. There was a particular silence between people who have watched each other nearly die and are not yet sure they're glad to have survived.

Bryn walked ahead of them both, stopping every few steps to touch things. Fence posts. Lichen. A dead beetle she turned over twice before setting back down with the gravity of a scholar returning a borrowed book. Her soul had been out of the world for twenty-two years, and the world was new to her at every inch. When she found a wild strawberry gone overripe in the ditch grass, she ate it and then stood very still, eyes closed, and Freya had to look away because watching her taste sweetness was worse than watching the flame gutter.

"You'll want to know how this goes," Ilsa said finally, as the city walls came up gray through the haze. "When we get there."

"I know how it goes," Freya said. "I've been on the other side of it."

"Then say it anyway."

"They'll arrest me at the gate. Or they'll try." He flexed his hands, empty now of every charm but the memory of charmwork. His fingers still moved through the shapes sometimes, unbidden, the way a taster's tongue still reaches for flavors after the meal is done. "A man walks out of the sealed mountain with a ward-keeper who burned her commission and a woman who's been legally dead since before the regent was born. They won't arrest me at the gate. They'll arrest me in the square, in front of everyone, so it looks orderly."

Ilsa said nothing, which was agreement.

"And then?" Bryn asked, turning. Her voice was still finding its weight; souls returned from fire spoke softly at first, Maren had told them, like instruments retuned.

"And then," Freya said, "they'll find out they can't hold me, because there's nothing left to hold. No magic. No immunity worth pricing. Just a food-taster with a story nobody wants to be true." He smiled, and it cost him. "They wanted my soul once. Now all I've got is testimony."

* * *

The gates of Cinderfall were open, which was itself a confession. For two hundred years the city had shut its gates at dusk; now they stood wide day and night, because the night no longer had stars in it to fear, only absence, and absence could not be barred against. The ward-towers flanking the gate were dark. Their keepers had struck the sigils rather than maintain wards that guarded nothing anyone could name.

The crowd at the gate was not a mob. It was worse: it was a queue. Hundreds of people waiting to file past a table where clerks of the provost's office took down names, trades, and—one question written on a slate beside the inkpot—*any dealings, past or present, with charmwork or the court below.*

Freya read the slate twice.

"They're taking a census of sin," Ilsa murmured.

"They're taking a census of *neighbors*," Freya said. "Look closer. Nobody's being arrested at the table. They write the name and wave them through." He watched a fishwife admit, red-faced and defiant, that her mother had been a hedge-charmer, and watched the clerk write it down without looking up, and watched the fishwife walk through the gate carrying her baskets and her confession both. "Somebody decided that if the whole city is guilty, arresting one person at a time is arithmetic, not justice."

"Somebody," Ilsa said, "is going to be very interested in us."

Somebody was. They had barely passed the table when a runner peeled off from the gatehouse and went up the hill toward the palace at a pace that made his opinion plain.

* * *

They did not arrest him in the square. They arrested him in a parlor.

The regent's men came for him at the inn near the Tanner's Bridge where he'd slept his first night back—a clean room, a soft bed, the first mattress he'd touched in two months—and they were polite about it, which was the most frightening thing. Six of them, in the provost's colors, and a clerk with a portable desk, and a chair set facing the window so that the light would fall properly on his face.

"Master Galloway," the senior officer said. "You're not under arrest. You're *invited* to give account."

"In a locked room?"

"The door isn't locked." It wasn't. Freya tested it with his eyes and knew it wasn't, and understood that the unlocked door was the message: *we don't need locks anymore; we need you willing.* The city had learned, in two months of starless nights, that compulsion was a currency in freefall. Everything now had to be asked for.

So he gave account. Three days of it, in that parlor, with the clerk's pen scratching like a mouse in a wall. He told them about the ledger. He told them about the founding charter and the orphanage that had sold two children to pay for it—their names read aloud in a government room for the first time in twenty-two years, *Freya Galloway, aged five; Bryn Galloway, aged four*, entered in the provost's record now beside the older entry in the court's black book. He told them about the dragon-fire and what it ate. He told them about the nine thrones and the eight empty ones and the ninth, which he had sat, and what it had cost, and what it had unmade.

The clerk wrote everything. The officers listened with faces like closed shops. And on the third day, when he finished, the senior officer asked the question Freya had been waiting for since the mountain.

"Why should we believe you?"

And Freya, who had spent seven years making himself invisible, who had built a life on never casting, never being seen, never hoping—who had sat a throne and given away his armor and come back with nothing but a tongue that remembered every flavor of every lie he'd ever swallowed—looked at the man and said:

"You shouldn't. Belief is charmwork. I'm not asking for it. I'm evidence. Go to the orphanage records. Go to the charter vault. Go count the names in your own founders' hands. I'm not a witness. I'm a document."

The pen scratched. Somewhere outside, a bell rang the hour, and the sound went out over a city with no stars, and no one flinched anymore, which was its own kind of scar tissue.

* * *

The public testimony happened because the regent's council could not agree on what else to do with him.

He learned this later, pieced together from Ilsa's reports and Maren's letters (the steward had come up out of the mountain in the first week of the armistice, blinking like a mole, and had taken a house near the archive and begun, methodically, to make herself unbearable to every committee in the city). The council had wanted him silenced—some for treason, some for mercy, all for quiet. But the census at the gate kept growing. Half the city had a grandmother who charmed warts, a brother who sold luck-knots, a debt owed to somebody in the licensed quarter that didn't exist yet. You could not try one magician while the whole city queued up to confess smaller ones. The arithmetic problem again.

So they gave him the Assize Hall. Open doors, they said. Public record. Let him say it all where anyone could hear, and let the city decide what it had bought with its charter.

The hall was full. It was fuller than full—people standing in the aisles, hanging out the windows, perched on the roof tiles, and the noise of them went through the floor and up through the soles of his feet like a second heartbeat. Freya stood at the witness table in a borrowed coat that fit badly and felt, for one vertiginous moment, exactly like a boy of twelve selling his first spell in an alley: small, illegal, and certain.

The advocate for the crown was a careful woman named Doss who had clearly been instructed to discredit him gently. She began with his crimes.

"Master Galloway, you admit to seven years of unlawful charmwork within the palace itself."

"I do."

"You admit to trafficking in charms. To forging a taster's credentials. To escaping lawful custody."

"All true."

"You admit to sitting the throne of the Ninefold Court."

A ripple went through the hall at that—fear, mostly, the old animal fear of the mountain—and Freya waited it out the way he'd wait out a poison's first bite, letting it move through him.

"I sat it," he said. "It's empty now. I broke it. You can send a delegation to see the rubble."

Doss turned a page. "Then help the court understand. You say the founders of this city purchased their charter with two children. Sold, in your word. Yet the charter stands. The city stands. Every institution of law in this hall descends from that bargain. If we accept your testimony, we accept that our law is the proceeds of a crime. What would you have us do? Unmake ourselves?"

It was a good question. It was the question. Freya had turned it over every night on the mountain road, and every night it had come back to the same place, the place where all his rules had lived.

"No," he said. "Unmaking is the court's trade. It ate names. It erased things until nothing was left but fire. I'm not asking you to erase the charter." He looked out at the hall—at the fishwife from the gate, he realized, sitting three rows back with her baskets on her knees, come to hear what happened to confessions. "I'm asking you to enter it into the record. All of it. The sale, the price, the children's names. A debt doesn't stop being real because the debtor died. It passes. Your law already knows this—you collect a father's debts from his estate every day. Well. This is the estate."

"And the payment?" Doss said. "What does the city owe?"

"That's not mine to set. I'm not the creditor." He spread his hands, and the hall went quiet enough that he could hear the pigeons on the roof. "I'm the receipt."

* * *

They deliberated for nine days. Of course they did. Nine, someone remarked bitterly, as though the number itself had opinions.

Freya spent them in a kind of freedom he had never practiced: the freedom of having nothing left to hide. He cooked, at first in the inn kitchen and then, when the innkeeper realized whose hands they were, in half the kitchens of the lower city, because a man who had fed a court needed feeding work and the work asked no questions his answers couldn't survive. He taught Bryn the names of vegetables. She learned them the way she learned everything now—voraciously, greedily, tasting the words as much as the food—and some evenings she would stand in the doorway of whatever kitchen had adopted them and simply watch him work, and he would feel her attention on his back like sunlight, and understand that this was the payment. Not the council's. Hers. The chance to watch her brother be ordinary.

Ilsa spent the nine days being tried in absentia for burning her warrant, and spent them also refusing to defend herself. "Everything I did wrong," she told the tribunal, "I did by the book. The book is the problem. Ask the taster." They stripped her rank. They could not strip her the truth, and everyone in that room knew it, and the knowing sat in the air like weather.

On the tenth day the council delivered its findings, read aloud in the Assize Hall to another packed house.

The charter was entered into the record, complete, with the court-ledger's entries appended. The sale of Freya and Bryn Galloway was declared, in the council's own cautious language, "a debt of origin, acknowledged and unresolved." The outlawry of charmwork was—not repealed, the council was not brave enough for repeal—but suspended pending "the establishment of a licensed quarter wherein such arts may be practiced under inspection." The fishwife's grandmother became, retroactively, a registrable profession. The queue at the gate became a queue at a licensing office. It was a half-measure, a coward's measure, a measure stitched from compromise and fear of the next election—and it was also, Freya thought, watching the clerk read it out, the first honest sentence the city had ever spoken about magic: *it lives here, and always has.*

His own crimes were addressed last. Seven years of unlawful charmwork, escaped custody, contempt of the regency, usurpation of a foreign throne. The sentence, when it came, was a fine of one mark, payable immediately, and a formal note that "the offender's cooperation materially enabled the resolution of the starless crisis." One mark. He paid it from the innkeeper's till, laughing, and the laughter went through the hall like a spark through dry grass, because everyone understood what the fine meant: the law had looked at him and found nothing it could reach. He had already paid everything.

Justice, he thought, walking out into the street afterward through a crowd that parted and then, tentatively, began to applaud—not for a hero, he could hear it in the sound, they weren't sure what they were applauding, maybe the idea that a person could be evidence—justice had begun. Begun. The charter was in the record; the quarter was a line on a map; the debt was acknowledged and unresolved. Unresolved. He carried those two words out of the hall like a stone in his shoe, and he suspected he would carry them for years.

That evening, Maren came to the inn. She brought a letter, sealed with wax the color of ember-light, and set it on the table between the soup bowls without ceremony.

"What is it?" Bryn asked.

"A summons," Maren said. Her face, which had grown rounder and more human in the sun, was carefully blank. "From the other eight thrones. The ones I told you were empty."

Freya put down his spoon. "They're not empty."

"No," Maren said. "They never were. They've been watching the trial. All of them. And they've read the council's findings, the way they read everything that happens under an open sky." She tapped the letter once, and left it. "They wish to negotiate the debt, Master Galloway. Directly. With the man who broke the ninth throne. They ask—" and here, for the first time, the old steward's composure slipped, just slightly, showing the vertigo underneath— "they ask whether the city will send its *receipt* to speak for it. Tomorrow. At the mountain's mouth. Where the fire used to be."

Freya looked at the letter, and at his sister, and at the bowl of soup going cold between them—ordinary soup, onion and barley, the best thing he had ever tasted—and understood that the unfinished part of justice had just sent him its address.
