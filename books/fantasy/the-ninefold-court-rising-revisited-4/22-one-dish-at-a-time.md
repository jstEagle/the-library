## 22. One Dish at a Time

The kitchen was small enough that Freya could stand in the middle of it and touch both walls if he stretched. He had measured it once, out of an old habit of knowing his exits, and then laughed at himself alone in the dark with flour on his hands. There were no exits to know anymore. There was a door, and beyond the door a street called Tallow Row, and beyond the street the licensed quarter's crooked boundary stones, and beyond those a city learning—slowly, badly, argumentatively—to live with what it had done.

He had hung the copper pots himself. That mattered to him more than he could explain to anyone who asked. For seven years he had owned almost nothing: a taster's knife, three hidden charms sewn into his coat lining, and a body that belonged on a ledger in a court beneath the mountain. Now there were pots, and a shelf of jars labeled in Bryn's careful hand, and a window over the sink where the morning came in gray and gold and ordinary.

The stove was lit. The bread was proving under a cloth. And on the cutting board before him lay an onion, which was, as far as Freya Galloway could tell, the most dangerous thing in the room.

He picked up his knife and did not taste the air first. That was the strangest part, still, after all these months—the not-tasting. Seven years of habit did not die easily. Every meal of his old life had begun with a small ritual no one saw: tongue against the roof of the mouth, a breath drawn slow through the nose, the whole animal attention of a man listening for the whisper of nightshade or the almond ghost of bitter cyanide. He had done it ten thousand times. He had done it so often that the tasting had stopped being a skill and become a self.

Now he cut the onion, and his eyes watered, and he let them.

* * *

Bryn came down the stairs at half past seven, as she always did, barefoot on the cold boards because shoes still confused her. She stood in the doorway for a moment the way she always stood in doorways—like someone checking whether the room would hold—and then crossed to the window and looked out at the street with the particular hunger she brought to everything now. Streets. Weather. Dogs. Old women arguing about fish prices. The entire unremarkable machinery of being alive.

"Is that bread?" she said.

"It's bread."

"It smells like bread." She said it with the gravity of a scholar confirming a theorem. "I remember bread."

"You remember bread," Freya agreed, and turned back to the onions so she wouldn't see his face do the thing it did whenever she remembered something. It happened more now. In the first weeks she had known nothing—not her name until he gave it back to her, not food, not sleep, not the difference between warm and cold except as raw sensation. A soul spends decades as fuel and comes back thin. But the ember they had lifted from the flame-chamber had been whole—that was the mercy Maren had never quite believed in, the thing Freya had staked everything on—and wholeness meant it could grow again. She relearned the world the way a plant relearns light: patiently, from every direction, without shame.

She had taken to the kitchen first of all things. Food, she said, was memory you could put in your mouth. She chopped herbs with fierce concentration, tasted everything twice, kept a slate on the wall where she wrote down flavors she wanted to try again. *Honey and salt together,* the slate said. *Rain on hot stone. The inside of a plum.*

"The quarter bell rang in the night," she said now, watching him. "Did you hear it?"

"I heard it."

"Was it the ward-charms again?"

Freya scraped the onions into the pan and listened to them hiss. Twice since midwinter the boundary stones along the licensed quarter's edge had flared in the dark—old hedge-magic meeting new civic law, sparks arcing off the charter-lines like static off wool. Both times the city council had convened an emergency session, both times the newspapers had used the word *incident*, and both times nothing had actually happened except that everyone remembered, briefly and uncomfortably, that magic was real and lived next door.

"Probably just the frost settling," he said, which was what he always said, and which they both knew was probably not true.

* * *

The shop opened at eight. It wasn't really a shop—it was a kitchen with a counter and four stools, a place where the people of Tallow Row could buy a bowl of whatever Freya had made that morning, and sit, and eat it warm, and talk. He had not planned it. It had grown the way Bryn grew, out of sheer stubborn aliveness. First the neighbors had smelled the bread. Then Ilsa Vane had come by on her rounds—she walked the quarter now, having traded her captain's insignia for something with less rank and more honesty—and eaten a bowl of barley soup in silence and said, "You should sell this," and left coins on the counter that Freya had spent three days deciding whether to return.

He sold it. That was the whole of his ambition these days, and it astonished him how much ambition it took.

The morning crowd came the way it always came: the rope-maker's widow first, because she liked the corner stool and the first pour of tea; then the two dyers' apprentices, who argued constantly and tipped badly; then Master Corvin from the licensing office, who wore his little brass badge of office like a man wearing a borrowed coat, and who ordered the same egg dish every day and never once looked at Freya's hands.

That last part was new, and Freya had learned to be grateful for it. In the first months, people had stared. They had stared at his hands, at his mouth, at the space around him where his charms used to hang invisible and then, on one terrible bright night, visible. *The Taster Who Walked Into the Mountain*, the broadsheets had called him, before the story got too big and too strange and the city did what cities do with things too big and too strange—it turned them into testimony. He had sat in the Council Hall for six days in the spring and told them everything. The ledger. The founding charter. The orphanage that had sold two children for the price of a city's safety. His own name, read aloud into the record, itemized like cargo.

They had not thanked him. He hadn't expected them to. Testimony is not gratitude; testimony is evidence, and evidence is uncomfortable, and a city that must choose between admitting its sin and burning its witnesses will usually find a third door. The third door, in Cinderfall's case, was the licensed quarter: magic legal here, in this narrow grid of streets between the old tannery and the river wall, taxed and registered and bounded by charter-stones, illegal everywhere else. It was cowardice dressed as compromise. Freya had testified anyway, and then gone home and cooked, because someone had to feed the city while it decided what it believed.

"Master Galloway." Corvin set down his spoon. "The office sent me with a question. Nothing official. Just—" he turned his badge around on its chain, a nervous habit, "—the boundary stones flared again last night. Third time this month. And there's talk of expanding the quarter's charter-line east, toward Tanner's Walk. Some of the households there have petitioned. Others have petitioned against. Loudly."

"What's the question?"

Corvin hesitated. "Whether you'd speak to the assembly again. About living alongside it. You're—" he gestured vaguely at the kitchen, the pots, the steam, the sister writing flavor-notes on her slate, "—you're proof of concept, I suppose."

"I'm a cook," Freya said.

"Yes," Corvin agreed, with unexpected gentleness. "That's rather the point."

* * *

Midmorning, when the stools emptied, Bryn took down the slate and studied it, and Freya watched her the way he caught himself watching her too often—hungry, greedy, unable to stop. She was twenty-six years old in a body that had never learned anything past five, and she carried it lightly in a way that broke his heart daily. She had decided, somewhere in the winter, that fear was a waste of appetite. She talked to strangers. She touched everything. Last week she had held a stranger's baby and pronounced, with total authority, that the child tasted like milk and thunderstorms, and the mother had laughed instead of being frightened, and Freya had understood in that moment that his sister might be the best ambassador the quarter would ever have.

"Bryn," he said. "Do you remember the court? At all?"

She didn't look up from the slate. "Warm," she said. "That's mostly what I remember. Warm, and loud, like standing next to a fire the size of a cathedral. And sometimes—" the chalk paused, "—sometimes I remember being *useful*. Which is the worst one." She turned and looked at him, and her eyes were steady. "Why?"

"There's talk of sending an embassy down. Officially. To settle the contract question—the ninth throne, the dissolved kingship, all of it. The court still exists, even if it isn't a court anymore. Something lives down there in the quiet. And the council wants to know what it wants." He wiped his hands on his apron. "They asked me to go."

"And you said no."

"I said I'd think about it."

Bryn set down the chalk. She crossed the kitchen and took his face in both her hands, the way she had learned to do from watching other families on the street, from studying the grammar of affection she'd been robbed of. Her palms were floury and warm.

"You already went down once and gave it everything you had," she said. "Your protection. Your name. Seven years of being poisoned slowly so you could survive exactly one walk into the dark. Nobody gets to ask for it twice." She kissed his forehead, solemn as a priest. "Besides. If they need someone to talk to the mountain, send me. I remember warm better than anyone alive."

He laughed—a real laugh, from the belly, rare these months and precious—and she grinned and went back to her slate, and the kettle sang, and outside on Tallow Row a cart went by selling oysters, crying its wares in a voice like a rusty hinge, and Freya thought: *this*. This was what he had bought with everything he'd spent. Not glory, not restoration, not even safety. A Tuesday. An onion. A sister who remembered bread.

* * *

In the afternoon he did the thing he still did every day, though he told no one: he tasted.

Not for poison—he couldn't anymore; that sense had gone with his immunity, poured into the fire like wine into sand, and what remained was only an ordinary tongue in an ordinary mouth. No, what he tasted was the soup, and the bread, and the tea, and the rain coming in off the river, and each of them separately and completely, with the full attention he had once spent hunting death in a king's cup. It had taken him months to understand that the skill hadn't died. It had just changed employers. Seven years of tasting for what could kill, and now he tasted for what could nourish, and it turned out to be the same discipline, the same devotion, pointed the other way.

The soup needed salt. He added it. Outside, the light shifted toward evening, and the boundary stones at the end of the row glowed their faint lawful amber, marking the edge of the world's newest idea: that magic could be a neighbor.

And then, as he was ladling out the last bowls for the supper crowd, he felt it.

Not tasted—felt. A pressure behind the sternum, faint as a far-off bell, familiar in a way that made the hair rise on his arms. Once, that feeling had meant a charm waking. He had no charms now. He checked anyway, hands moving over his chest out of seven years of reflex, and found nothing but his own heartbeat, and underneath the heartbeat—

Underneath the heartbeat, deep and patient and unmistakably *below*, something in the mountain had turned its attention toward Tallow Row. Toward the kitchen. Toward him.

The soup steamed. The stools waited. On her slate, in fresh chalk, Bryn had written a new line without either of them noticing:

*It remembers your name.*
