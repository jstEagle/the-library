## 1. The Date in the Margin

The library of the Hollow Vale had been dying for longer than Xanthe Marchetti had been alive, and it intended to go on dying at its own pace, thank you very much. He catalogued it the way one tends a beloved invalid: gently, daily, without any real hope. Damp crept up from the stone floor and bloomed in foxed pages; silverfish made lace of the older folios; and every spring a fine grey dust settled over everything like ash from a fire no one remembered.

He worked by lamplight in the western alcove, where the shelves leaned together like conspirators, copying titles into his ledger in a hand so small and neat it looked apologetic. *Herbal of the Nine Winters. On the Taming of Rivers (water-damaged). Prayers Against the Court.* That last one he'd copied three times now, finding new copies tucked into odd places — pressed behind a treatise on beekeeping, sewn into the binding of a child's primer. The vale's founders had written about the Court beneath the mountain the way people write about plague: quickly, fearfully, with the ink still shaking.

Outside, the evening was doing what autumn evenings did in the Vale — going gold, then copper, then that bruised violet that meant Tam would come stomping in demanding supper. Xanthe smiled at the thought despite himself. Seventeen years old and still ate like a threshing crew. Still tracked mud through three rooms as if mud were tribute owed to him personally.

Xanthe himself had never left the Vale. Not once in thirty-one years. He was the gatekeeper; the gate did not travel. He told visitors — the rare tinker, the rarer pilgrim — that he simply hadn't found reason to go. He did not tell them the truth, which was that the first time he'd stood within ten feet of the gate itself, something vast and cold on the other side had turned its attention toward him like an eye opening, and Xanthe had understood with total certainty that if he ever walked through, he would be seen. Truly seen. And whatever saw him would know exactly what he was.

So he stayed. He kept the door shut, kept the wards fed with salt and spoken names, kept the ledger current, and told himself the story of contentment until the telling wore smooth.

It was while reshelving the beekeeping book that he noticed the loose back board.

He might have missed it entirely — the lamplight caught at just the wrong angle, or the right one — but there was a sliver of parchment visible in the gap, thin as a moth wing. He pried the board free with a thumbnail, heart ticking faster than such things warranted.

A single folded sheet. Old. The paper had gone the color of old honey, soft as cloth along the folds. No title. Just a hand — cramped, urgent, the writing of someone who expected to be interrupted — marching down the page in a language Xanthe knew only because he'd spent his life reading the vale's founders: formal Old Common, the kind used for wills and wards.

He read it once without understanding. Then again, and understanding arrived all at once, the way cold water arrives.

*In the year the vale turns two hundred, when the last leaf falls on the eve of the Saining Moon, the debt comes due. The Court beneath shall collect the soul held in trust, and none may bar the way.*

And beneath that, smaller, underlined twice:

*The gatekeeper's own name is writ in the bargain. Xanthe Marchetti. He who keeps the door must open it, for the debt walks through no other way.*

Xanthe read his name a fourth time. A fifth. His own handwriting, when he finally looked down at his ledger, seemed to belong to someone else — someone safe, someone whose name appeared nowhere in old books.

Tonight was the eve of the Saining Moon. He had swept the last of the leaves off the library steps that very morning.

"No," he said aloud, to the empty room, and the room said nothing back, which felt like agreement and also like mockery.

*The soul held in trust.* There was only one soul in the Hollow Vale that anyone could have held in trust. Only one soul that had arrived here not born but *left* — set down on the gatehouse step nineteen years ago in a basket lined with wool gone grey with age, with no note but a token: a small coin of pale metal stamped with nine interlocking rings. Xanthe's mother had kept that coin on the mantel her whole life. *Payment received*, she'd said once, when Xanthe asked, and then she'd laughed in a way that wasn't laughing, and never explained.

He was moving before he decided to move, ledger abandoned, lamp guttering behind him. Through the archive hall, past the cases of founder's relics, out the door and down the path toward the gatehouse where Tam should have been splitting wood for the evening fire — Tam, who was always there, who had been there since before Xanthe's grief had words for itself, loud and freckled and stubbornly, infuriatingly *alive*.

"Tam!"

The valley gave back his voice and nothing else.

The woodpile stood half-split, axe buried in the block mid-swing. The evening meal sat covered on the hearthstone, untouched, still warm. And in the middle of the flagstone floor — the exact center, though neither of them had ever been able to say why the house's center mattered —

— there was a mark.

It was shaped like nothing Xanthe had words for: a ring of frost-blackened stone, perhaps two feet across, and within it the floor was cold in a way that had nothing to do with weather. He knelt and laid his palm against it and snatched it back. Not cold like winter. Cold like *depth*. Cold like the space between stars, like water at the bottom of a well so deep the light has never visited. The kind of cold that means something enormous is paying attention.

Nine interlocking circles were etched faintly in the frost-scorch, and at their heart, pressed into the dust as neatly as a seal into wax, lay the coin. Their coin. The mantel's coin. It had been upstairs on the shelf beside his mother's portrait not two hours ago; Xanthe was certain of it the way you are certain of your own heartbeat.

"Tam," he said again, quieter, and this time it wasn't a call. It was a confession to an empty room. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I'm sorry —"

His hands were shaking too badly to pick up the coin properly; it took him three tries. It was warm. Everything else in that circle was cold as the grave's front porch, and the coin was warm as a living hand.

He understood then, standing in his brother's empty kitchen with the light failing, that the invisibility had been a lie all along — *his* lie, the one he'd told himself for thirty-one years. He had thought keeping the gate closed meant keeping the world out. He had thought the Court beneath the mountain didn't know him, couldn't see him, had no use for a quiet man in a quiet valley. But his name was in their books. Had been in their books since before he could walk. Every year he'd tended the wards, every year he'd kept the door shut, he hadn't been guarding the vale from the Court.

He'd been holding the door *for* them.

The prophecy sat in his memory like a swallowed stone: *He who keeps the door must open it, for the debt walks through no other way.*

There was no rescue coming. There were no soldiers in the Vale, no wizards, no allies beyond a valley of farmers who slept soundly precisely because men like Xanthe kept silent watch. If he wanted Tam back — and there was no *if*, there had never been an *if*, not since a basket on a doorstep and a laugh that filled a gatehouse — then he would have to do the one thing his whole life had been built to prevent.

Open the door.

Xanthe put the coin in his pocket, took down his coat, and wrote a note he doubted anyone would find in time: *Gone after him. Keep the hearth lit. I mean to come home.* Then he climbed the path to the gate as the Saining Moon rose fat and white over the eastern crags, and the great door of the Hollow Vale waited for him, silent, patient, and — for the first time in his life — looking less like a wall and more like a mouth.

His hand shook as he laid it against the iron.

On the far side, something exhaled.
