## 2. Down Where the Lanterns Lie

The door had never opened for Xanthe Marchetti. In thirty-one years of tending it, he had polished its iron hinges, swept the frost from its threshold, and pressed his palm to its cold face each night as his father had taught him, whispering the words that kept it shut. *Stay closed. Stay quiet. Keep them out.* He had believed, all his life, that the door was a wall between two worlds — that on one side was the vale and everything soft and human, and on the other was nothing he would ever need to understand.

Now Tam's name was written in a dead woman's ink, dated to this very night, and the mark on the library floor had already gone cold.

He stood before the gate with a lantern in one hand and the prophecy-book in the other, and the door did not feel like a wall anymore. It felt like a mouth.

"They took him through here," he said aloud, though there was no one to hear. His voice sounded thin against the mountain's silence. "They took my brother through *my* door."

The iron did not answer. But when he set his palm against it, the metal was warm — the first warmth it had ever shown him — and beneath his fingers he felt something like a pulse, slow and vast, like a heart buried deep in the rock. The Court beneath the mountain, awake. Collecting.

Xanthe thought of Tam at six years old, foundling-warm and furious, fists balled because the village children said foundlings had no souls to save. *I have a soul,* Tam had shouted, *it just hasn't decided where it lives yet.* And Xanthe, fifteen and awkward, had put a hand on that wild hair and said, *Then it can live with me until it does.*

They had never told him. Their parents had sold their brother before they were born, and let him grow up laughing in a valley bought with the price of himself.

The rage came first. Then, underneath it, worse: the vertigo of realizing that every year of safety had been a lie he'd told himself. He had not been invisible to the Court. He had been *irrelevant* — a caretaker of a door, a line in an old ledger, a man whose whole life fit inside someone else's bargain. Tonight the ledger had come due, and it had reached past him as if he were furniture.

"I'm coming," he told the door. "Do you hear me? I'm coming, and I'm bringing him home."

He whispered no keeping-words. He spoke the opening ones instead — words he had only ever seen, never said, inked in his grandmother's hand in the margin of a book about debts. The hinges groaned like waking cattle. Cold air poured out, smelling of wet stone and lantern oil and something sweeter underneath, like rotting fruit and candle smoke.

Xanthe Marchetti stepped over his own threshold for the first time, and the dark closed behind him like water.

* * *

Tam woke on marble.

That was the first wrong thing — the cold of it, seeping up through his clothes, because the vale floor was always wood and always warm. He lay still and counted the wrong things. Marble. Lanterns hanging from chains so long they vanished into darkness above, hundreds of them, burning without flicker. And voices — beautiful voices, everywhere, speaking a language that made his teeth ache, except every third word arrived in his head already translated, which was somehow more frightening than not understanding at all.

"Up," said someone. "Debts stand upright."

A servant girl hauled him by the arm. She was small and sharp-faced, with ink-stained fingers and eyes the grey of old dishwater, and she moved like someone who had learned that slowness was punished. She shoved a tray into his hands — bread, wine, salt.

"Eat," she said. "Not too much. They like you hungry but standing."

"Where am I? Who are—"

"The Ninefold Court." She said it the way people in the vale said *weather*. "And I'm nobody, so don't waste questions on me. Walk. The Ninth is waiting."

"Ninth what?"

She didn't answer. Ahead, doors of black wood swung open onto light, and Tam walked toward it because the alternative was being dragged, and his legs remembered they were proud.

* * *

The Ninefold Court did not look like anything Xanthe had imagined, because he had never dared imagine it. He had crossed a stair of living stone that spiraled down for what felt like a day and a night, past galleries where faces watched him without eyes, and emerged into a hall so vast that the ceiling was a rumor. Lanterns by the thousand hung in slow constellations. Beneath them, nine thrones of glass and bone arranged in a crescent, and upon them nine figures robed in colors that hurt to look at directly — not bright, exactly, but *insistent*, colors that seemed to be arguing with each other.

Each wore a mask. Eight of the masks were beautiful — porcelain faces smiling serene smiles, gilded, jeweled, perfect.

The ninth mask was blank. Plain white. No smile at all. It sat on the center throne, and Xanthe could not stop looking at it.

"Mortal," said the leftmost mask, a woman's voice like struck silver. "You are not expected. That is either an insult or an opportunity. We have not yet decided which."

"I've come for my brother," Xanthe said. His voice cracked on *brother*, and he hated it, and kept going anyway. "Tam. The foundling. Your ledger says he belongs to you tonight. It's wrong. Whatever bargain was made, it wasn't made by him."

Laughter rippled across the eight beautiful masks, sweet and terrible, like bells rung at a funeral.

"A debt does not care who made it," said another noble — male, bored, warm as bathwater. "Only who owes it. Your parents signed in blood and marrow. The vale stands unburned; the boy stands delivered. A fair trade, fairly paid."

"You call that fair?"

"We call it *paid*," said the silver voice, gently, the way one corrects a child. "Fairness is a currency here, mortal. You may purchase some, if you have anything left to spend."

And Xanthe understood, then, the shape of the trap — understood why the air itself seemed to lean in and listen. Every word he spoke might be a signature. Every kindness offered might carry a price hidden in its folds. In the vale, a gift was a gift. Here, a gift was a hook, and generosity was simply cruelty wearing its best coat.

"Then I'll buy fairness," he said. "Whatever it costs."

"Ohh." The warm voice sighed with pleasure. "They always say that early. Where is your advocate, mortal? No one speaks for themselves in the Ninefold Court. Even we speak through masks."

"He has none," said a voice behind him. Small. Steady. Familiar. "So I will."

Xanthe turned. The sharp-faced servant girl from the stair — the one who had fed Tam, who had told him *walk* — stood at the edge of the light with her dishwater eyes lifted to the thrones, and her chin up, and her hands shaking where she thought no one could see them.

"Wren," said the silver mask, and something in the sweetness curdled. "You owe this Court everything."

"Yes, lady. Everything." Wren stepped forward, into the lantern-light, and Xanthe saw that her wrists bore faint silver rings, like scars, like shackles grown into skin. "Everything. Which is why I know precisely how much everything costs. And I know the Court keeps honest ledgers — it must, or none of us would keep serving. So write me down as this mortal's advocate, and charge my account."

Silence, vast as the hall itself.

Then the blank ninth mask tilted, very slowly, and for the first time all nine nobles leaned forward on their thrones at once, and the lanterns above dimmed as if the hall itself were narrowing its eyes.

*Interesting,* said a voice that came from nowhere — from everywhere — from the stone under Xanthe's feet, older than any of the nine voices, and it did not sound like kindness at all. It sounded like arithmetic.

* * *

They put Tam in a cell of glass, because the Court liked to watch its debts breathe. He sat with his back to the wall and his knees up, and tried to be small in a way the vale had never asked of him.

Footsteps. Then the servant girl crouched outside the glass, close enough to touch if the glass weren't there.

"You're the brother," she said. Not a question.

"Where's Xanthe? What did they do to him?"

"Nothing yet. Nothing's free here — remember that, whatever happens. If they're kind, count the cost twice." She glanced down the corridor, then pressed her ink-stained fingers flat against the glass. "My name's Wren. I was taken at six years old. Foundling. Same as you."

Tam looked at her hands. At the silver rings around her wrists. "You've been here a long time."

"Long enough to forget my other name." Her voice stayed flat, careful, but her fingers didn't move from the glass. "Long enough to learn that owing everything is its own kind of belonging. That's what they tell you, anyway." A pause. Something flickered under the flatness — quick, fierce, half-drowned. "Don't believe it. Whatever they offer you, whatever they make you feel — don't believe it."

"Then what do I believe?"

Wren looked at him a long moment, this wild-haired boy behind the glass, and felt something shift in her chest that she had thought the Court had taken years ago — some stubborn, unledgered thing.

"That someone came down here for you," she said. "Nobody ever came down for me. So believe that."

She stood to go. Behind her, far off in the glittering dark, a bell began to ring — once, twice, nine times — and the last ring did not fade, but hung in the air like a held breath.

Wren went still. She knew that bell. Everyone in the Court knew it, and everyone feared it, and in three hundred years no one had heard its ninth toll finish ringing.

"The Court convenes," she whispered. "Trial at dawn. They mean to judge the debt tomorrow."

"And?" Tam pressed his palms to the glass.

"And," Wren said slowly, turning back to him with her dishwater eyes gone wide, "they've summoned an advocate of record. Boy — they've summoned *me*."
