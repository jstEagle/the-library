## 3. The Ninth Mask

They brought Tam out at the ninth bell, when the lanterns of the Court burned lowest and their light went strange—amber going to green, green going to a color Xanthe had no name for. The boy walked between two guards whose faces were smooth as river stones, and he did not look afraid. That was the worst part. He looked *polite*, hands folded, chin lifted, as though this were simply another evening in the vale and someone had asked him to carry water.

Xanthe stood at the edge of the dais steps with Wren's hand locked around his wrist like a shackle of her own choosing. Above them, nine thrones curved in a crescent beneath the mountain's root, and in each throne sat a masked figure. Eight masks were beautiful: silver, jade, mother-of-pearl, lacquer black as spilled ink. The ninth throne stood empty, draped in grey cloth gone to cobwebs, and no one looked at it, which meant everyone was looking at it.

"Tam of Nowhere," said the noble in silver, voice like coins poured into a bowl, "is called to account."

"For what?" The words tore out of Xanthe before he could weigh them. Wren's grip tightened hard enough to bruise. "He owes nothing. He's a child."

The silver mask tilted. "All souls in the Court owe something. It is only a question of arithmetic." A ledger appeared in the noble's lap—Xanthe had not seen anyone carry it in—and it fell open to a page that made his stomach drop through the floor of the world. His parents' names were written there, in ink that still glistened wet after twenty years. Beneath them, one line: *One soul, held in trust.*

"Held in trust," Xanthe repeated. "You make it sound like a kindness."

"We are very kind," said the jade mask, "when it is paid for."

* * *

*They put me on a scale,* Tam thought, from wherever they kept him between the bells, which had walls of glass and no doors. *Not a real scale. A scale where they weighed the things I remember. My first snow. Freya's bread. Xanthe teaching me letters on the gatehouse floor, his finger tracing big crooked shapes in the dust.*

*The scale tipped toward Xanthe every time.*

*I tried not to let my face show how much that frightened me. In the Court, wanting something is how they get their hooks in you. So I folded my hands and thought about the door of the vale, and how Xanthe used to say it must never open, and how tonight I understood—I finally understood—that he'd been standing guard over me all along without ever saying so.*

*They read out the debt. Twenty years' interest. Protection rendered, protection owed. It sounded fair, the way a spider's web sounds like architecture if you've never watched it being spun.*

*"Do you contest the account?" the stone-faced ones asked me.*

*"I don't understand it," I said honestly, because lying has a smell here, and honest boys last slightly longer.*

*"That is not the same as contesting."*

*"Then yes," I said. "I contest it. I don't know how yet. But I contest it."*

*Somewhere far above—or below; directions were a rumor here—a boy who shared no blood with me was doing the same thing. I could feel it, the way you feel weather coming.*

*Contesting together.*

* * *

"The bargain stands," the pearl mask pronounced. "Unless—" and here every masked head turned, slow as weathervanes, toward Xanthe, "—a party wishes to assume it."

"There it is," Wren breathed against his ear. "Don't. Xanthe. Whatever they offer, the price will be your name, or your years, or your soul, and none of those come back."

But the ledger was already turning pages, and there was his own name, waiting, blank beside it, patient as an open grave. "A soul for a soul," the silver mask said gently. "The vale keeps its gatekeeper. The Court keeps its due. Everyone is satisfied."

Everyone except the two people who would be gone.

Xanthe looked at the page. His own handwriting, almost—the same slant, the same loops—because bargains written by the Court learned your hand. *Xanthe Marchetti, one soul, freely given.* All he had to do was speak it aloud. All he had to do was stop being the person who came home, and become the person who stayed, forever, in a hall where kindness was currency and he'd just been counted rich.

Tam was watching from the scale-room now; Xanthe didn't know how he knew, but he knew. And Tam was shaking his head, small and fierce, mouthing *don't you dare* in the silent way of brothers.

"No," Xanthe said.

The hall went very quiet.

"No," he said again, louder, and stepped forward off the dais steps onto the trial-floor, alone. "Because your arithmetic is wrong. You've been counting wrong for twenty years, and I found the error, and I'm going to make you look at it."

Wren made a sound behind him—not a word, more like a prayer bitten in half.

"The debt was paid," Xanthe said. "The night you took my parents. That wasn't a tragedy. That was a *transaction*. You wrote it down yourselves." He pointed at the ledger, at the glistening names. "Protection purchased. Price rendered. Two lives, paid in full, on the night of the frost. And your own oldest law—" his heart slammed against his ribs; he was making this up as he went, building a bridge out of nothing over an abyss, "—your own law says what? Say it. Say what the old books say."

Silence. Then, from the empty ninth throne, from under the grey cloth, a voice—not a voice, a resonance, the mountain itself clearing its throat:

"*No soul may be held for a debt paid in full.*"

The eight masks turned toward the ninth throne as one, and for the first time, Xanthe saw them afraid.

"That's not—" began the silver mask.

"You wrote it," Xanthe said, walking toward the draped throne, each step ringing on the stone like a hammer on an anvil. "Before you were nine. When you were only the Court, and the Court was only a promise. There's a book in the vale library older than any of your ledgers, and I've spent my whole life reading it because I thought I was nobody, and nobody reads, and it turns out nobody was exactly who you should have been afraid of." He pulled back the grey cloth.

Beneath it sat a mask of plain unglazed clay, featureless but for two eyeholes, and it was warm.

"The Ninth Mask," Wren whispered from the steps. "It's real. We thought—it was only a story servants tell—"

"It's the story the others are built on," Xanthe said. He picked it up. It weighed nothing and everything. "And I invoke it. Before the assembled Court, by the law older than your thrones: the soul of Tam of Nowhere was bought and paid for by the deaths of Rowan and Isolde Marchetti, rendered in full on the night of the frost. Hold him, and you hold a soul that isn't yours—and a Court that breaks its own foundation holds nothing at all."

The clay mask grew warm in his hands. Warm as bread. Warm as a gatehouse hearth.

The eight masks said nothing, and their silence was the loudest sound in the world.

Then the ledger in the silver one's lap closed itself, and every candle in the hall bent sideways as though bowing, and the ninth throne's empty shadow stretched long across the floor—long enough to touch Tam's bare feet on the trial-stone.

"Debt discharged," said the mountain, through the mask, through Xanthe, through everything. "*Release the boy.*"

And somewhere above—or below—the glass walls of the scale-room cracked clean through, and Tam Okonkwo-Marchetti stepped out into the light of nine dying lanterns, free, and running, and calling his brother's name in a voice that shook the Court to its glittering bones.

Behind them, soft as a moth landing, Wren descended the dais steps to stand at Xanthe's shoulder—no longer a servant, she realized, the moment she chose it. The eight masks watched her go, and did not stop her, because stopping her would have required admitting that anything in the Court could simply *leave*.

"They'll want a reckoning for this," she murmured.

"Then they can find us," said Xanthe, catching Tam up as he crashed into him, all elbows and tears and laughter, "behind a door that finally opens both ways."

But high above the crescent of thrones, in the dark where the lantern-light gave out, something ancient turned its attention downward—and began, slowly, to climb.
