## 4. What the Valley Keeps

The silence after the law was spoken was not like other silences. It had weight, and texture, and a slow pulse, like the heart of something very old waking beneath the floor of the world.

Xanthe stood in the center of the Court with Tam's name still burning on his tongue and the Ninth Mask's verdict hanging over them all: *no soul may be held for a debt paid in full.* His parents' lives—spent crossing into the vale's protection, spent birthing him inside its borders where magic could not follow—had been the payment. All of it. Every coin of it. The bargain was ash.

The nine nobles sat upon their thrones of glass and bone, and for the first time since he had descended into their glittering dark, they did not know what to do. He could see it in the way their masks tilted, in the small restless movements of jeweled fingers. They were creatures of contract, of ledger and clause, and the oldest law of all had just been read aloud against them by a gatekeeper in a coat that smelled of woodsmoke and valley rain.

"The soul," said the First Mask at last, her voice like frost forming on a window, "is released."

Tam made a sound beside him—a small, broken sound, the sound of a boy remembering he had lungs—and Xanthe caught his arm as his knees buckled. Up close, in this light that came from nowhere, Xanthe could see the change happening: color flooding back into Tam's cheeks like water returning to a dry riverbed, the gray veil lifting from his eyes. The part of him the Court had held for twenty years was pouring home.

"Hey," Xanthe whispered. "Hey. I've got you."

"You came down here." Tam's voice was raw. "You actually—you hate doors, Xanthe. You hate *leaving*."

"I hate losing you more." He laughed, and it cracked in the middle, and he didn't care. "Apparently I'd walk through every door in creation first."

Above them, the lanterns flickered—not dying, but dimming, as though the Court itself were drawing breath. And then Xanthe saw Wren.

She stood at the edge of the dais steps, half-forgotten servant, half-remembered ghost, her livery worn to threads at the cuffs. She owed the Court everything—that was what she had told him once, in a corridor smelling of candle smoke, when kindness had felt like a trap she couldn't afford to spring. She owed it everything, and it owned her in return, and now she was looking up at the nine masked faces with an expression Xanthe recognized from his own mirror: the terror of discovering that invisibility was a lie you told yourself.

"Wren," he said.

"Don't," she said quickly. "Don't ask me anything. Not yet."

But the First Mask had heard. Masks turned toward her like flowers finding a cruel sun.

"The servant," said the Third Mask, silk and venom. "She aided the claimant. She revealed the archives. The Court's debts are not discharged so easily as mortal ones."

"Then let her debt be weighed," Xanthe said, before he could stop himself, before wisdom could catch up with his mouth. "By your own law. Openly. You just taught me how it's done."

He expected the floor to open. He expected thorns. Instead, the First Mask rose, and her voice, when it came, was almost gentle, which was somehow worse.

"Her debt stands at one hundred years of service, less forty-three served. She may pay the remainder"—the mask inclined toward Xanthe—"or she may claim exile. Exile cancels all debts. That is also old law. Older than us, if we are honest, which tonight we are forced to be."

The hall waited. Nine masks. A thousand lesser faces in the galleries, souls bought and traded, watching the way starving people watch bread.

Wren looked at Xanthe. Then at Tam, standing shaky and alive and *whole* beside him. Then at the door behind the throne—the one through which runaways were never permitted to run.

"I choose exile," she said.

And the strangest thing happened: the Court bowed. All nine masks, all at once, low and formal, honoring a choice rather than punishing it. Because that was the secret at the bottom of the Ninefold Court, Xanthe realized—they were bound far more tightly than anyone they held. Justice was their currency because it was their cage. They could no more break their own oldest law than he could unlove his brother.

"Then go," said the First Mask, "and take nothing but what is yours."

"That's easy," Wren said, and her voice shook only a little. "I never owned anything here anyway. Except myself. And I'm taking that."

* * *

The road back up through the mountain was longer than the road down, or perhaps it only felt that way because hope makes a heavier burden than fear. It sloshed and shifted and demanded carrying with both hands.

They walked through corridors of lantern-light thinning to ordinary gold, past doors that whispered their old bargains as the three of them passed, bargains going quiet one by one, like birds settling at dusk. Tam slept against Xanthe's shoulder in the last stretch, truly slept, the sleep of someone whose dreams belonged to himself again. Wren walked a half-step apart, cataloguing everything—the moss, the dripping stone, the smell of living earth—with the wide-eyed hunger of someone seeing the world without a mask between herself and it for the first time in fifty-seven years.

"How does it feel?" Xanthe asked quietly. "Being free?"

"Like falling," she said. "But upward."

The gate appeared at last, and Xanthe stopped before it, and put his hand flat against the cold stone, and understood it fully for the first time.

All his life he had believed the vale's magiclessness was a wall. A defense. Magic cannot enter; therefore the door must never open. But standing here, with his brother's soul warm beside him and a freed woman waiting behind him, he finally saw the truth his parents had died to build: the vale wasn't a fortress. It was a hearth. Magic could not enter because magic *bargained*, and bargains devoured families, and his parents had wanted one place in the world where a family could simply *be*, whole, unowned. The gate had never been meant to keep magic out.

It had been meant to keep family in.

Which meant, he thought, hand still on the stone, feeling the old prohibition hum beneath his palm like a sleeping animal—that it had been built by people who understood that family is not what you're born into. It is what you refuse to abandon. And a thing built on that foundation could bear more weight than one family. It could bear many.

"Xanthe?" Tam murmured. "What are you thinking? You get that look."

"I'm thinking," Xanthe said slowly, "that the gate opens both ways."

He pushed.

The stone swung inward on hinges that had not moved in a century, and morning poured through—real morning, valley morning, wet grass and woodsmoke and the particular green-gold light of the Hollow Vale at dawn. No magic followed them through. No magic ever would. But three people did, walking out of a mountain and into a life.

* * *

**Tam**

The vale looked different to Tam now. Smaller, maybe—or maybe he was simply bigger, having been to the bottom of the world and come back with all his pieces.

He watched Xanthe work on the gatehouse, hauling the old barricades down board by board, humming the off-key tune he always hummed when he was nervous about a decision he'd already made. Wren was in the garden, kneeling in actual dirt, laughing at something—the feel of rain, probably. It rained yesterday and she had stood out in it for an hour, arms out, like a woman greeting an old friend.

People were coming. That was the strange part. Word traveled strangely in the deep places, and already there were signs: a silver button on the doorstep that hadn't been there last week. A scrap of ribbon tied to the gatepost, the kind servants wore in the Court. Last night, a knock—three soft knocks—and when Tam opened the door, no one was there, only a small carved bird left on the threshold, still warm.

"They're asking," Tam said, coming to stand beside his brother at the half-dismantled gate. "The ones who want out. They're finding the way."

"I know." Xanthe wiped his hands on his coat. "I keep expecting to feel afraid. I spent my whole life terrified of opening this door. And now—" He shrugged, and smiled, and it was the realest smile Tam had ever seen on him. "Now it's just a door. Doors are for letting people home."

Below the gatehouse, the valley lay green and quiet and utterly magicless, which was to say: utterly safe. Above, the mountain kept its glittering, cruel court, bound forever by its own oldest law. And between the two, a gate stood open, tended now not by a keeper who feared it, but by a family that had chosen itself—one foundling, one gatekeeper, one exile—and was learning, day by day, to leave the lamp lit for whoever came next.

That evening, the knock came again. Three soft knocks.

This time, when Tam opened the door, someone was standing there.
