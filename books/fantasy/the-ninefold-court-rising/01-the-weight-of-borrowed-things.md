## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Things

The trick to stealing a memory, Theo Dunmore had learned before he was old enough to shave, was never to take the whole of it.

A whole memory was a wound. It left people blinking at their own hands like they'd woken in a stranger's body. They wept in the street without knowing why. They went home and stared at their supper until it went cold, and sometimes they didn't stop staring, ever. The valley had a word for those ones—*hollowed*—and Hollowmere did not forgive you for making more of them.

But an edge of a memory, a sliver shaved off clean? That was different. A man who'd lost the smell of his wife's bread still remembered his wife. He just felt a strange ache when he passed the bakery on Tallow Row, and he paid good copper to anyone who could put the ache back.

Theo sold ache-putting-back at a markup.

He stood now beneath the leaning awning of Doss's dry goods stall, ostensibly sorting bolts of grey wool, actually watching the morning traffic come down into Hollowmere from the pass. Market day. Three carts, a mule train, and—his pulse quickened—a pilgrim in a faded blue coat with a staff wound in ribbons, walking with the particular lightness of someone carrying something precious.

Pilgrims were the best. They came over the mountains with heads full of shrines and miracles, and they always wanted to remember them perfectly, which meant they noticed when pieces went missing, and they always, always paid.

"Morning, Theo." Doss's wife, Petta, thumped a crate of turnips onto the counter beside him. "You're doing that thing again."

"What thing."

"The thing where your hands are busy but your eyes are somewhere else. Like a cat watching a bird through glass." She wiped her palms on her apron. "My mother says your father was the same. Eyes like a pickpocket's conscience."

"My father," Theo said mildly, "was a man nobody remembers, which is either very sad or very convenient depending on the day." It was a joke he'd made so many times it had worn smooth as river stone, and like all worn stones it hid whatever was underneath. Nobody in Hollowmere remembered his father because there was nothing to remember; Theo had arrived at the foundling door eleven days after the winter solstice some nineteen years ago, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of woodsmoke and something sweeter he could never name. His earliest memories were not his own. That was the first thing you learned about yourself, growing up a thief of other people's pasts: eventually you couldn't tell which recollections were yours and which you'd lifted and kept.

He kept almost none of them anymore. That was the discipline. Take, sell, return, forget. A memory held too long grew roots, and rooted memories were harder to pull, and pulled roots bled.

The pilgrim in blue stopped at the well in the square's center, drank, and fumbled for his purse. Theo drifted out from behind the wool.

* * *

The touch itself took less than a breath. That was the part no one believed until it happened: no pain, no flash, nothing so dramatic. Just Theo's fingertips resting on the back of a hand, or brushing a wrist as if steadying a drunk, or—in crowded markets—the innocent collision of two bodies passing. And then he *listened*, the way you might press your ear to a door, and the memory came away like ripe fruit off the stem.

This one was warm. Incense smoke. A mountain shrine with bells made of green glass, and a woman's voice singing a hymn Theo didn't know in a language he half-recognized, and the pilgrim's own young face lit gold by candleflame, weeping without shame because everyone wept at the shrine, that was what the shrine was *for*. Underneath it, threaded through it like wire through wax: the reason he'd walked three weeks over the pass. Someone's name. Someone dead or dying. *Let me remember her right. Let me keep it whole.*

Theo drew the sliver out gently—the incense, the bells, the golden light—and left everything else. The name stayed. The grief stayed. Only the color of it went, the vividness, the way the sun through glass-bells had looked.

The pilgrim blinked once, frowned faintly at the well water as though it had gone stale, and moved on toward the chapel row, already forgetting to be troubled.

By evening, Theo would find him outside the Gilded Ewe, looking lost in a way that men only got when something sweet had been scooped out of them, and say the words he'd said a thousand times:

"You've lost something. I can help you find it. It'll cost you."

* * *

Hollowmere sat in its valley the way a coin sits in a closed fist, and Theo had never questioned the fist.

That was the honest shape of his life, nineteen years deep: the valley walls rose gray-green on every side, forested to the crags, and the single road in through Coldpass was the single road out. Travelers came—peddlers, pilgrims, the occasional surveyor who always left looking vaguely disappointed—and travelers went. But nobody from Hollowmere left. Not because they couldn't. Because by the time you thought about leaving, you'd usually found a reason not to, and the reason always felt like your own.

Theo had tried once, at sixteen. He'd gotten as far as the pass road's first bend, where the old boundary stone stood with its weathered carving nobody could read, and he'd stood there for the better part of an hour with his pack on and his heart hammering, and then he'd thought of Petta needing help with the turnip crates, and Maren's roof leaking, and the fact that he hadn't finished repairing the shutters on his loft, and he'd turned around. It had seemed entirely sensible at the time. Walking back down into the valley had felt like exhaling.

He thought about that hour sometimes, lately more than usual, the way you probe a tooth you suspect is bad.

But today was market day, and market day meant coin, and coin meant the sack of barley he needed, and the barley meant another winter survived, and surviving winters was the whole architecture of a life if you built it carefully enough.

He made four lifts before noon. A drover's memory of a horse fair—he'd pay two bits to have the colors back. A tinker's recollection of a girl in Lowmarsh, just the moment she'd laughed at his terrible joke; Theo took only the laugh, and knew the tinker would be back within the month. An old woman's image of her childhood dog, which Theo felt genuinely bad about and priced accordingly.

And one from a merchant's guard who shouldn't have been carrying anything worth taking, but whose mind glittered like a magpie's nest: a city of white towers, a hall with nine thrones, nine seats, and on each throne—

Theo flinched so hard he nearly dropped the apples he was holding as cover.

Nine thrones. Nine figures seated, robed in colors he had no names for, and the guard-memory held them with the flat reverence of a man who'd seen them once, at a distance, during a parade he'd been paid to stand watch at. The Court. The word arrived in Theo's head attached to the memory like a label tied to a jar: *the Ninefold Court*, and under it, older, half-scraped: *the covenant binds the realms, the regalia bind the court, and blessed are the quiet years—*

Then the memory-edge was in his palm, cold and humming faintly, and the guard was walking on unaware, and Theo stood in the market with an apple in each hand and his heart going wrong.

He'd stolen tens of thousands of memories. They were grain and gossip and grief and small joys. None of them had ever hummed.

* * *

He sold the ordinary ones at dusk, working the tavern steps in his usual rhythm—half fortune-teller, half pawnbroker, a boyish smile and a voice pitched low. "You've lost something. Something small. Something sweet." People never asked how he knew. That was the strangest mercy of Hollowmere: everyone had gaps, and everyone had learned not to look at the gaps too closely, because the valley was full of gaps the way a wall is full of mortar.

The drover paid. The tinker paid, and cried a little, which Theo pretended not to see. By the time the lamps were lit along the square, the humming memory sat in his pocket like a coal wrapped in cloth, and he'd decided—not for the first time—that some things were better returned unsold.

There was no one to return it to yet. The guard would leave tomorrow with the merchant's caravan. Fine. Tomorrow.

Except the stranger found him first.

* * *

She was standing at the mouth of Cooper's Lane when he cut through after closing up, and everything about her said *not from here*: a traveling cloak scorched along one hem, boots ruined to the ankle, hair cropped short and practical and silver-grey though her face was young—no, not young either, he corrected himself, thirty maybe, weathered early, with eyes that caught the lamplight strangely. Pale. Silver-pale, like coins at the bottom of a well.

She wasn't begging. She was watching him with the absolute stillness of a hawk that has already chosen its branch.

"You take edges," she said. "Not whole things. Edges."

Theo kept walking. "Don't know what you mean, mistress. Good evening."

"You touched the guard with the pike scar near the fruit cart. Two fingers, here—" she tapped her own wrist, "—and he walked on smiling at nothing. I watched you do it. I've watched six of you do it, in six valleys, and this is the first time one of them has done it *well*."

Every instinct Theo owned, instincts honed across nineteen years of being unremarkable, invisible, *safe*, told him to keep walking, to laugh, to become boring. But his feet had stopped. That was the trouble with being good at something: when someone named it aloud, in the open air, under the lamps of the only town you'd ever known, your body betrayed you by listening.

"I don't know what you think you saw," he said quietly, "but this is Hollowmere, mistress, and nothing happens here but turnips."

"Hollowmere." She said the name the way a physician reads a symptom. Her gaze went past him, up the black shoulder of the valley wall, to the crags where the unreadable boundary stone sat in its bend of road. "Wards this strong. Memory-dead ground. Do you know what this place is, boy?"

"It's my home."

"It's a *bandage*." Her voice cracked on the word, and for the first time he saw how badly she was hurt—the scorched cloak wasn't fashion, and she was standing the way men stood when something inside them was bleeding. "Someone wrapped this whole valley around a wound and told it to sleep. And it worked. It worked for a long time. Long enough that children grew up here thinking sleep was the world." She swayed, caught herself against the lane wall. "I need—I'm looking for someone. A courier. Came through Coldpass yesterday or today. Tall, one eye clouded, carries a satchel chained to his—"

"There was no courier through Coldpass," Theo said. "I watch the pass road. It's a habit."

Something moved through her face then, fast and terrible, and Theo recognized it because he wore it himself most nights: the look of a person hearing a door close on the last room they had left.

"No," she said. "No, he was ahead of me, he *had* to be ahead of me—"

"Mistress—" Theo started, and then she folded.

Not dramatically. She simply stopped being able to stand, sliding down the lane wall with a sound like a sack of grain, and when Theo lunged to catch her the lamplight fell full across her face and he saw the blood. It had soaked through her side and her cloak's scorched hem was soaked too, and there was so much of it that his hands came away red from a single touch.

"Help," he called, uselessly, into the empty lane. Hollowmere's streets emptied at dusk the way they always did, the way they *always* did, and no lamp in any window flickered, and no door opened, and the silence of the valley lay over Cooper's Lane thick as snowfall.

"Listen." She had his collar in both fists, and her strength was astonishing and fading at once, like a fire seen through closing doors. "You're a thief of the in-between. I can see it—you're *made* of the in-between, born on the seam, that's why the wards let you be awake while the rest of them dream. So listen to me. There were nine. Nine seats, nine regents, nine regalia, one law, and the law is *breaking*, do you understand me, the seams are coming apart, and the Regent—"

"The Ashen Regent," Theo heard himself say, and had no idea where the words came from. They surfaced out of the humming thing in his pocket, out of the guard's parade-ground memory, out of somewhere older than that. His mouth had known the name before his mind did.

Her silver eyes fixed on him with something between terror and hope. "*You know.* Then you know what's coming. Thirteen turns of the moon, thief. When the thirteenth moon rises full, the boundary goes, and everything on the far side of the dark comes through, and no bandaged little valley will save anyone. The courier has the map. Find the courier. Find the map. Or we are all—"

She coughed, and the cough was wet and final-sounding, and Theo, kneeling in the lane he'd walked ten thousand times, holding a dying stranger who knew words like *regalia* and *seam* and *ward*, understood with sudden total clarity that his invisibility had just ended. Whatever happened next—whether she lived or died, whether he helped her or ran—the valley he'd spent his whole life disappearing into had just looked back at him.

"Stay awake," he said stupidly. "Stay awake, I'll get help, there's a physicker on—"

"No time for physickers." She was smiling, which was worse than the blood. "Funny. All that way. Wrong valley after all." Her grip shifted, caught his hand, and pressed something into it—a fold of stiff leather, oilskin-wrapped, cold as a winter stream even through his sleeve. Cold. Colder than any object had a right to be. "If not him... then you. You'll do. Thief of the in-between." Her lips were going grey. "You'll do."

"Who are you?" Theo asked. "What's your *name*?"

She looked at him with those coin-bottom eyes, and the last thing she said was not her name. It was:

"Thirteen turns. Count them."

* * *

The lamps along Cooper's Lane burned steady. Somewhere a dog barked twice and stopped, as if remembering it wasn't allowed. The valley held its silence around Theo like a hand closing.

In his grip, the oilskin bundle pulsed with cold, patient weight, and did not leave his skin.

Above the eastern crags, thin as a paring, the first moon of thirteen was already rising.
