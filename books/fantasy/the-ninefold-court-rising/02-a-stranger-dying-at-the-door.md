## 2. A Stranger Dying at the Door

The knock came at the worst possible hour, which was to say any hour at all, because Theo Dunmore had spent the day being recognized.

He'd felt it first at the tanner's stall—a prickle between his shoulders like a splinter of ice working under the skin. Old Hollis had looked at him a half-second too long while counting out coppers for the memory Theo had sold him three weeks past. Not suspicion, exactly. Recognition of a kind people didn't have words for in Hollowmere, where everyone's memories arrived secondhand and nobody asked too carefully whose they'd been before. But it was enough to make Theo alter his route home, cut through the alder row, double back along the millrace with his collar up and his heart doing arithmetic against his ribs.

So when someone hammered on his door at midnight—hammered like the door owed them money—he stood very still in the loft above his shop and considered not answering.

Hollowmere did not have visitors at midnight. Hollowmere barely had visitors at all; that was rather the point of it. The valley sat in its bowl of grey hills like water sitting in cupped hands, quiet as held breath, and travelers who wandered in by mistake always seemed to forget why they'd come long before they remembered how they'd left. The wards did their work without anyone tending them, the way a hearth keeps embers without being watched. In living memory—and Hollowmere's living memory went back a very long time, because old Maren could still recite her grandmother's stories—the wards had never failed.

Which meant the person hammering on his door had either been invited, or had broken through something that did not break.

Theo took down the knife he kept behind the ledger shelf—not for cutting, but for what it was made of, cold iron from outside the valley, the only metal in Hollowmere that hummed if you held it wrong—and went down the stairs two at a time, keeping his weight off the fourth step, which announced visitors to the whole street.

He opened the door with the knife low and ready.

The stranger fell into him.

* * *

She was heavy the way dying things are heavy—all her strength gone somewhere else, leaving only mass. He caught her under the arms and dragged her backward over the threshold, kicking the door shut behind them, because whatever she was, whoever might be following her, the street outside was not where he wanted this conversation to happen.

"Who—" he started.

She coughed. It was a terrible sound, wet and rattling, and in the dim light of the single lamp he kept burning over his workbench he saw why: there was blood on her lips, black in the lamplight, and more of it soaking through the left side of a courier's coat—good leather, travel-worn, the kind stitched with the sigil of some postal service Theo had never seen. Three slashes across the chest, neat as tally marks. A blade had done that, or claws pretending to be one.

And her eyes—

Her eyes were silver. Not grey, not the pale wash of an old woman's cataracts. Silver, bright and liquid, catching the lamp flame like coins at the bottom of a well. They fixed on him with an intensity that made him want to step back even while he was holding her up.

"You," she rasped. "The thief."

Every muscle in Theo's body went cold. "I don't know what you mean."

"I walked past your stall today." She laughed, and the laugh turned into another cough, and she spat red onto his floorboards. "Watched you work. You're good. Better than good. You take and give back so smooth they never feel the seam." Her hand found the front of his shirt and gripped it with a strength that surprised them both. "But I felt it. When you brushed me. Felt my own memories shift in my skull like furniture."

"I think you're confused," Theo said, which was the speech he gave, the practiced one: *you've been traveling, the valley air does strange things, sit down, let me get you water.* He'd said versions of it a hundred times. It had never once failed him.

It failed now. She pulled him closer instead, close enough that he could see the fine network of burst vessels in those silver eyes, close enough that he could smell blood and horse sweat and something underneath both—a smell like struck flint, like the air after lightning, a smell that did not belong in Hollowmere at all.

"The map," she whispered. "Take it. Take it or we've both wasted our deaths."

"I don't want anything you have."

"Everyone wants something." Her mouth curved, and there was something almost fond in it, almost pitying. "You most of all, thief. You just pretend yours is nothing."

Then her legs went, and Theo caught her again, lowered her onto the floorboards because the cot was too far and the moment was too short. He knelt beside her, pressing his palms against the wounds the way his hands remembered from a lifetime of small kindnesses performed for profit—staunching a traveler's nosebleed before lifting a memory, steadying a drunk before selling him back his own wedding day. The blood came through his fingers anyway, warm and relentless.

"Press here," she managed. "No. Don't bother. It's past bothering." She drew a breath that whistled. "Listen. Thirteen turns. Do you understand? Thirteen moons and the boundary—"

"The boundary of what?"

"Everything." Her eyes were losing focus, sliding toward the ceiling beams, then snapping back to him with visible effort, dragging themselves to his face like a swimmer fighting a current. "You live in the wound and you don't even know it. All of you. Sleeping in the scar tissue and calling it peace."

"You're raving."

"I'm dying. There's a difference." Another laugh, thinner than the last. "Raving is easier. Raving doesn't cost anything." Her hand crawled along the floor, found his wrist, closed around it. Her grip was ice. "I carried this from the Ashen Court. Through six hundred miles of everything she owns. They took—" a shudder ran through her, "—they took pieces of me to make me talk. I bit my tongue bloody so I wouldn't. So it would arrive whole. So it would arrive with *you*."

"With me?" The word came out smaller than he wanted. "Why me? You don't know me. Nobody knows me. That's—that's the entire point of me."

"I know what you are." She swallowed, and Theo watched the effort of it move down her throat like something being buried alive. "Memory-thief. The gift runs in lines. In families. There's one line left unbroken, one line the Regent hasn't touched, and it ends in a valley full of people who traded remembering for safety." Her silver eyes burned into his. "It ends with you."

"That's not true. That's not—I stole this gift. Bought it cheap off a drunk peddler fifteen years ago, it's a trick, it's a *trade*—"

"It's a key," she said. "And she knows it exists. And when she finds you, she'll hollow you out to the walls and wear your gift like a glove." Her breath hitched. "Unless you finish what I started."

"I haven't started anything."

"You have now." And she pressed the folded paper into his hand.

* * *

Later—for years afterward, though he didn't know it yet—Theo would try to describe the moment of contact, and fail every time.

Cold. That was the only word that ever came close. Not the cold of winter or iron or fear, but the cold of deep places, of wells and cellars and the far side of the moon. The folded map hit his palm and the cold went *through* him, past skin and muscle, into whatever part of a man holds the shape of himself. He cried out. He tried to drop it. His fingers would not open.

"No," the stranger breathed. "Good. It's chosen. It knows—" A cough tore through her, worse than the others, and this time the blood came freely, running from the corner of her mouth in a thin dark line. "Thirteen turns," she whispered. "Tell no one who can be made to forget. Find the nine. Reforge—"

"What nine? What am I supposed to—"

But the light in her eyes guttered the way a lamp gutters when the oil runs dry, and then she was looking at something beyond the ceiling beams, beyond the roof, beyond perhaps the sky itself, and then she wasn't looking at anything at all.

The silence afterward was enormous.

Theo knelt in it, in his loft above his little shop in the safest place in the world, holding a piece of paper he could not put down, beside a dead woman whose eyes were slowly fading from silver to the ordinary grey of any corpse. Outside, the night pressed against the windows. Inside, the lamp flame stood perfectly still, as if it too were afraid to move.

He counted to a hundred. Then he counted again. Then, with the deliberate calm of a man who has spent fifteen years practicing the appearance of innocence, he set about trying to open his hand.

It wouldn't.

He pried at his own fingers with the other hand. Nothing. He wrapped his fist in cloth and pulled against the workbench edge until his shoulder screamed. The map stayed. He thought, wildly, absurdly, of the cleaver in the kitchen below—thought of it clearly enough to make himself ill—and did not go downstairs, because some animal part of him understood that it would not matter, that the cold thing in his palm was not held there by fingers at all.

"All right," he said aloud, to the dead woman, to the room, to whatever had chosen him without asking. "All right. Fine."

He unfolded the map.

* * *

It should have been paper. It looked like paper—cream-colored, soft at the folds, marked in ink the color of dried violets. But when Theo bent over it by lamplight, the lines moved.

Not obviously. Not the vulgar writhing of enchanted novelties sold at fairs. The roads simply were slightly different each time he blinked, the mountains breathing at the pace of geology, rivers rearranging themselves like sleeping men turning over. And in nine places across that shifting geography, small symbols glowed with a light that came from nowhere, faint as foxfire: a lantern. A ring. A crown. Six others he couldn't read, drawn in a script that hurt to look at directly, the way the sun hurts.

At the center of it all, where a capital should have been, there was a hole in the map. Not blankness—an actual absence, a place the ink refused to describe, ringed with nine converging roads like spokes around a missing hub. Theo stared at it and felt the same vertigo he'd felt watching the stranger die: the sensation of standing at the edge of a truth much larger than the floor beneath him.

And threaded through everything—the roads, the mountains, the nine small fires—ran a single line of text in that violet-dark ink, written so finely he had to hold the map inches from his face:

*Nine seats. Nine regalia. One law. While all are held, the boundary stands.*

Below it, smaller, in a different hand, hurried and human:

*Thirteen turns from the breaking. Twelve are gone. She has six. We have none. Run.*

Theo sat back on his heels.

His hand was shaking. The map was not. It lay across his palm like a sleeping bird, cold as a stone pulled from a riverbed, and it did not care what he believed or didn't believe, what he wanted or feared. It had chosen him the way a hook chooses a fish, and it was not going back in the water.

"Thirteen moons," he said quietly. "Twelve gone." He did the arithmetic because he couldn't stop himself, because his mind had always been a ledger and ledgers must balance. "One left."

One moon. One turn of the sky between now and whatever happened when the last of thirteen waned. Between now and the boundary failing—whatever boundary meant, whether it was the edge of the valley or the seam of the world itself.

He looked down at the dead woman. Up close, in the honest light, she was younger than he'd first thought. Maybe thirty. There was a faded scar on her jaw, old and careless, the kind you got falling out of a tree as a child. Somebody had loved her once. Somebody, somewhere out in the wide broken world, was probably waiting for letters that would never arrive, and would grieve, and would eventually pay someone like Theo to remember her—and there was no one like Theo out there, not anymore, according to her. According to her there was exactly one, asleep in a valley of forgetting, and the most dangerous woman alive wanted him hollowed out and worn.

"Why me?" he asked her again, uselessly. The dead keep their reasons.

The lamp flickered. And in the flicker, Theo saw something that turned his blood to well-water.

On the floorboards where the stranger's blood had pooled—her blood, spilled inside his walls, inside the valley's silence—the boards were *glowing*. Faintly. A thin tracery of light rising through the wood grain in slow, spreading veins, like frost blooming across a window in reverse. The wards, he realized. The wards that had stood unbroken since before Maren's grandmother's grandmother. They weren't just failing.

They were healing over the breach she'd made—and in healing, they were announcing it. Somewhere, in whatever deep place the valley's protections were anchored, a bell he'd never heard was ringing, and everything with ears to hear it would know: something had entered. Something had died. Something remained.

Something remained.

Theo got up on legs that felt borrowed, dragged the stranger's body onto the cot—he would bury her properly, at dawn, before anyone woke, because that was the only decent plan available—and stood a long moment looking at his own right hand, at the folded map fused to his palm, glowing faintly through his skin now, its nine small lights shining up through his flesh like stars through deep water.

He had spent fifteen years making sure no one saw him. No debts, no ties, no history heavier than coin. Invisible as fog. And in one night, without his consent, he had become the single most findable object in the world.

Outside, far off in the hills, a dog began to bark. Then another. Not at a fox. Not at wind.

At something coming down the mountain road.

Theo snuffed the lamp and stood in the dark, holding a dead woman's secret against his heartbeat, and waited for morning—which he already understood, with perfect clarity, would change everything.
