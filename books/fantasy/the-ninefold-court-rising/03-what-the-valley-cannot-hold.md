## 3. What the Valley Cannot Hold

The map burned.

Not with heat—Theo had expected that, had braced for it in the way you brace for a scalding kettle—but with cold, the kind of cold that lived in the deep of winter wells, that crept up through boot soles and settled in the marrow. He'd wrapped it in oilcloth and buried it beneath the floorboards of his loft, under the loose plank where he kept the strongbox and the few things worth stealing from himself: a brass button, a river stone shaped like a tooth, a lock of hair whose owner he could no longer name. The courier's blood had dried to rust-brown on one corner, and even folded small it seemed too large for the space it occupied, as though the floorboards bent around it rather than the other way round.

He hadn't slept. That was the first thing. He'd sat cross-legged on his cot through the whole grey length of the night, listening to Hollowmere breathe—the creak of the millwheel turning in its sleep, a dog complaining somewhere near the tannery, old Hobbes coughing three houses down the way—and waiting for something to come through the door after him. Nothing did. The wards held. Or they seemed to hold. But the stranger had gotten *in*, silver-eyed and dying, past whatever kept the valley safe, and Theo could not stop doing the arithmetic of that. If a wounded man could slip the net, what else might follow him?

At dawn he took the map out and carried it to the forge.

* * *

Bram Tuller was the only man in Hollowmere who worked fire for a living, and he asked no questions about anything so long as the questions weren't his own. Theo had sold him back his dead wife's laughter twice—once when grief made him forget how it sounded, once when the memory wore thin—and Bram repaid such services with discretion. When Theo arrived before the morning bell and asked to use the coals alone, Bram looked at him for a long moment with eyes gone soft as slag, then picked up his bellows strap, hung it on its hook, and walked out into the yard without a word.

Theo fed the coals until they glowed orange, until the heat rolled off them in visible waves and the iron smell of the place thickened in his throat. Then he unfolded the map.

Up close, in good light, he could finally see what the darkness had hidden. It wasn't ink. The lines were drawn in something finer than any pen could manage—hair-thin threads of silver that shifted when he wasn't looking directly at them, like fish seen through moving water. Nine circles were marked across it at intervals, each ringed with script he couldn't read, and at their center lay a shape that made his stomach drop before he understood why: a valley, small and closed as a cupped hand. Hollowmere. Drawn there as if the valley itself were one of the nine—or the wound where a tenth should have been.

He didn't hesitate. Hesitation was for people who had something to lose by being wrong. He dropped the map onto the coals.

It screamed.

There was no other word for it. No flame caught, but the air above the coals shivered and produced a sound Theo felt in his teeth more than heard—a high, thin keening like wet glass rubbed wrong, like the last note of a song cut short. The courier's face flashed behind his eyes unbidden: the silver eyes going dull, the mouth shaping those two words one final time. *Thirteen turns.* Then the sound stopped, and the smell of scorched paper rose, and Theo allowed himself one breath of relief before he looked down.

The coals were untouched. The map lay on top of them, pale and pristine, the silver lines brighter than before, as if the fire had been a meal it accepted politely.

Theo snatched it up with a poker and threw it into the quench trough. Water boiled around it. Steam sheeted up, and when it cleared, the map floated on the surface like a lily pad, patient, dry, faintly luminous in the smithy's gloom.

"Right," Theo said aloud, because saying it aloud kept the panic on the far side of his teeth. "Right."

He tried the whetstone next. He tried tearing it—he braced a foot against the anvil and pulled until his shoulders screamed, and the map stretched like living skin and snapped back without so much as a wrinkle. He tried burying it in the ash pit. He tried leaving it on Bram's counter while he walked to the well and back, telling himself that if it stayed put, it wasn't his problem, it belonged to whoever found it. When he returned, breath fogging, heart hammering a stupid hopeful rhythm, the map was in his hand. He looked down at it and it was simply *there*, warm-cold against his palm, as though it had always been there, as though every other location had been a brief hallucination.

That was when the vertigo started. Not in his body—in his certainty. Because the map wouldn't leave him, and the courier had died to bring it, and neither of those facts cared what Theo Dunmore wanted. For eleven years he had been the invisible man of Hollowmere, the one who touched everything and left no mark, and now something had chosen him, had fastened to him like a burr, and no amount of cleverness or quiet would shake it loose.

He wrapped the map back in the oilcloth, mostly out of superstition, and went looking for Maren Ostwick.

* * *

Maren lived at the far end of Lantern Row, in a house older than every other house on the street, which was saying something in a town that measured age in generations of the same six families. She was ninety-one by her own count and ninety-four by everyone else's, having added years the way other people lost them. Her memory was the opposite of Theo's trade: she forgot nothing. The children said she remembered the valley being carved. The adults said the children exaggerated. Everyone brought her their disputes anyway, because Maren's recollection was the closest thing Hollowmere had to law.

She was on her porch shelling beans when he came up the path, and she didn't look up, which meant she'd already known he was coming. Maren had a way of knowing things that had nothing to do with magic and everything to do with ninety-odd years of watching people.

"You've got your grandfather's walk this morning," she said, thumbing a bean from its pod. "Quick and guilty."

"I need to ask you some things."

"People usually do, eventually." She gestured at the second chair with the bean pod. "Sit. And take that thing out of your coat before you sit down. It's making my teeth ache."

Theo stopped mid-step. "What thing?"

"The thing you're trying very hard to pretend isn't there." She looked at him then, and her eyes were pale as skimmed milk and just as clear. "I'm old, boy, not blind. There's a weight to certain objects. Your grandmother carried one just like it the day she arrived."

The words landed wrong, tilted, and Theo heard the tilt. "Arrived. Grandmother arrived?"

"Mm." More beans. "Everyone in this valley arrived. Nobody here is *from* here, not truly. Surely you've noticed. Six families, all of them founded within the same twenty years, all of them refusing to speak of anywhere else. You steal memories for a living, Theo Dunmore. Has it never struck you as strange that a town full of people would choose to live in a place where memories don't stick?"

He sat down slowly. The oilcloth bundle pressed against his ribs like a second heartbeat. "I thought it was a curse. I thought—we all thought the valley eats memories. That's just what Hollowmere is."

"That's what it was built to be." Maren set down the basket and folded her hands, and for the first time since he'd known her—which was all his life—she looked tired. "You want answers, so I'll give you the ones I have, and you'll understand they're secondhand. My mother told me, and her mother told her, and the woman who told *her* was there. The valley wasn't cursed. It was *made*. Deliberately. By people running from something so large they couldn't outrun it—they had to hide inside a hole in the world instead."

"Running from what?"

"The Court." She said the word carefully, the way you handle something that might still be sharp. "The Ninefold Court. Before your time, before mine, before my grandmother's grandmother. There were nine regents who ruled everything—not lands, understand, but *laws*. The law that fire burns. The law that the dead stay dead. The law that names have weight. They bound it all together with regalia—crowned things, terrible things—and the binding held the worlds apart from one another. Kept the doors shut between what is and what else is."

"And the exiles fled it?"

"Some fled its wars. Some fled its justice." Her mouth thinned. "My grandmother used to say the covenant was a cage with nine locks and no key on the inside. But she said it quietly, and only after wine." Maren turned to look at him fully, and Theo had the peculiar sensation of being read like a ledger. "You're holding a piece of that cage, boy. I can feel it from here."

"The courier—" His voice cracked; he steadied it. "A man came over the pass two nights ago. Wounded. Silver eyes. He gave me this map and said *thirteen turns*, and then he died in my loft, and I can't burn the damn thing, drown it, tear it, or lose it. It comes back. It's *mine* now, apparently, and I never agreed."

Maren was quiet for a long moment. Down the lane, someone was beating a rug, the flat percussion of ordinary life continuing on schedule. A cart passed, wheels groaning. The world insisting it was still the world.

"Silver eyes," she said at last. "Then the Court's messengers still run. Then it's begun again." She pushed herself up from the chair with the effort of a woman hauling an anchor, crossed to the door, and disappeared inside. Theo heard drawers opening, the scrape of wood on wood. When she returned she carried a tin box, dented, older than the house around it, and opened it with hands that shook only slightly.

Inside lay a scrap of fabric, ash-grey, embroidered with a sigil Theo had never seen and yet recognized instantly, the way you recognize a face from a nightmare: nine interlocked rings, broken—one, two, three—clean through.

"My grandmother tore it from the cloak of a man who came hunting exiles," Maren said. "Forty years after the founding. He stood at the valley's edge for three days, and he could not come in, because the founders had paid a price to make this place a hole in remembering. Nothing of the Court holds here. No magic. No memory. No reach." Her milk-pale eyes fixed on him. "Until now. Until a dying man crossed our wards carrying that. Which means either the price has failed, or someone has stopped paying it, and both of those mean the same thing."

"What thing?"

"It means, child, that the hiding is over." She closed the tin box. "And it means you should decide very quickly whether you're a man who hides, or a man who was only ever hiding because he'd never been given a reason to leave."

The words went through him sideways, finding the bruise he kept hidden even from himself. Eleven years of careful, competent, profitable invisibility. Eleven years of touching other people's pasts because he had none of his own worth keeping. He thought of the map burning cold against his skin and understood, with a swimmy lurch, that it had not chosen him despite his emptiness. It had chosen him *because* of it. A vessel holds best when it's empty.

"Maren," he said. "The founders. Did they ever say what happens if the Court falls? If the binding breaks?"

She looked out past him, down the lane, toward the hills that cupped the valley, and her face did something he had never seen it do. It went young. Frightened, and young.

"They said the realms are held apart the way a roof is held up," she said quietly. "You don't notice the beams until they come down. Thirteen turns, he told you?" She nodded before Theo could answer, answering herself. "Then count the moons, boy. Count them fast. And whatever you decide—" she pressed the tin box into his hands, sigil-side up, "—decide it soon. Because my grandmother's hunter waited three days at the border. The ones who come next won't wait at all."

* * *

Theo walked home the long way, along the millrace, because the long way was empty and he needed empty. The water ran brown-green and slow, carrying leaves that had no business falling yet, and above the eastern ridge the moon hung pale in the afternoon sky like a coin someone had forgotten to pocket. He counted without wanting to. One moon up. Twelve to go.

Twelve turns left. Maybe fewer.

The map pulsed against his chest, cold as a swallowed stone, and for the first time in eleven years Theo Dunmore felt the true shape of the valley around him—not a haven, not a home, but a hiding place, walls built by frightened people, and walls, he knew now, only work until someone decides to knock.

Down in the market square, three riders were dismounting at the well. Their cloaks were ash-grey, and the sigils on their shoulders caught the light.

They were asking after a courier.
