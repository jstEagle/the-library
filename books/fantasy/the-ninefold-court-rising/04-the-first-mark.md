## 4. The First Mark

They came on the third day after the stranger died in my loft, riding down the valley road with the particular stillness of men who have never once been surprised.

I saw them from the market first. Three riders on grey horses that moved wrong for Hollowmere—too smooth, too quiet, as if the valley's hush had gotten into their hooves and they'd learned to use it. They wore travelling cloaks of undyed wool, plain enough to pass for merchants or pilgrims, but at their collars each bore a sigil stitched in thread the colour of old fire: a grey flame, inverted, its point aimed downward like a knife going into something soft.

Ash-grey. I didn't know what it meant yet. I only knew that my hands went cold around the memory-vial I was holding—a widow's recollection of her wedding day, warm amber light caught in glass—and that every instinct I had honed in nineteen years of being invisible screamed at me to put it away and become furniture.

"Morning, Tam." I set the vial back in its velvet slot and smiled at the cooper's son, who was hovering at my stall pretending he hadn't already spent his coin this month. "Come back Thursday."

"You always say Thursday."

"And you're always broke by Tuesday. We've made peace with it." I closed the case, latched it, and let my eyes drift past his shoulder to the riders dismounting at the well.

Hollowmere's market ran the way it had run since before Maren was born—fishwives shouting prices down, the baker's boy weaving between carts, Old Pell arguing with a tinker about a kettle. Nobody else had stopped. That was the thing about my valley: we were so used to forgetting that we'd forgotten how to notice. The strangers might have been anyone. Travellers came through twice a season, bought what they needed, left.

But travellers looked *at* things. These three looked *through* them, sweeping the square the way I swept a crowded room before a job—cataloguing exits, marking faces, deciding who mattered and who was scenery.

The tall one spoke to the well-keeper, and even across the square I caught the shape of the question, because some words carry:

"—courier. Silver-eyed. Would have passed through within the week."

My blood turned to well-water.

*Silver-eyed.* The man who had died on my floor with a map burning cold against my skin, folded now into the lining of my coat where it had refused to stay anywhere else. The map I had burned. The map that had come back. The map I had finally buried under the hearthstone of an empty house three streets from mine, because throwing it in the river felt too much like admitting I couldn't destroy it, and burying it at least let me sleep.

Three days of sleep. Not much. Enough to convince myself the riders were coincidence.

The well-keeper shrugged at them, open-faced and empty-handed, because that was all she had. Whatever memory she might have held of a dying man carried through her town on someone else's shoulder—it had been taken from her the moment it formed, the way everything true was taken in Hollowmere. We lived inside a silence so complete we mistook it for peace.

The tall one thanked her. He smiled while he did it, and the smile was the worst thing I'd seen all year, because it was *kind*. It was the smile of a man doing necessary work and regretting the mess of it.

Then the second rider—the woman, sharp-faced, with the sigil at her throat and a thin silver rod strapped to her forearm like a surgeon's tool—turned her head, slowly, and looked directly at my stall.

Not at me. At the stall. At the little velvet case, the rows of vials, the sign I kept deliberately faded: *KEEPSAKES RESTORED. HONEST WORK.*

She said something to the tall one. He looked too.

I sold the widow's wedding day to Tam at half price just to have a reason to close up, and walked home the long way, taking alleys I could lose a shadow in, telling myself all the while that traders asked about couriers all the time. Roads were dangerous. Couriers went missing constantly. There was no reason—

There was every reason. The wards had failed. For the first time in living memory, something had come *in* that the valley should have turned away, and it had come in looking for exactly what I was hiding.

Maren had told me, two nights ago, sitting in her kitchen with her hands wrapped around a cup of tea gone cold because her grip had forgotten how to lift it: *"The founders didn't build Hollowmere to be a home, boy. They built it to be a hole. A place the Court's eye would slide off of. You don't live in a hole. You hide in one. And when the thing you're hiding from finds the hole—"*

She hadn't finished. Her mind had wandered, the way it did now, and she'd started talking about her sister instead, and I'd sat there with the vertigo of a man learning his floor is a trapdoor.

*When the thing you're hiding from finds the hole.*

It had found it.

* * *

I spent the afternoon doing the stupidest thing I have ever done, which is to say I spent it trying to act normal.

I checked the buried map—still there, cold through the hearthstone's dust when I pressed my palm flat, patient as winter. I packed nothing, because packing is how people get caught. I ate dinner at the common table with fourteen neighbours and laughed at Joss Wren's story about the goat in the chapel and thought, the whole time, *these are the faces I'm leaving*, and hated myself for how easily the thought arrived, and hated myself more for noticing that it arrived without any grief attached at all. Nineteen years of keeping everyone at arm's length, and here was my reward: I could contemplate abandoning them with the same efficiency I brought to picking a lock.

Except.

Except that night, near midnight, the screaming started.

I was awake—I'd been awake for three days, more or less, sleeping in snatches against the wall nearest the door—and the scream cut through Hollowmere's silence like a blade through cloth. It came from the direction of the Tallow Row houses. Then it stopped. Not faded. *Stopped*, mid-note, the way a candle stops when pinched.

I was out the window before I decided to be.

Tallow Row was lit by lanterns I didn't recognize—three of them, hung on hooks the riders must have brought, burning a white flame that gave off no warmth and cast shadows that fell the wrong way, toward the light instead of away. The tall man stood in the doorway of the Hedley house. The woman stood over Bramble Hedley, our farrier, who knelt in the street in his nightshirt with his wife gripping his shoulders, both of them wearing the expression of people watching a flood come for their home.

"We've been very clear," the tall man was saying, in the tone of a schoolmaster grown tired of repeating himself. "A courier passed through your town. Someone sheltered him. We only want the name."

"I don't know any courier," Bramble said, and it was true, and it was useless, and everyone standing in that street knew it was useless except Bramble.

"There was a man," the woman said softly. "Wounded. Carrying a leather satchel. Someone took him in." She crouched, bringing her face level with his, and I saw the silver rod slide free of her forearm strap into her hand, thin as a knitting needle, catching the white light. "You're not in trouble, farrier. But someone in this town knows, and until they tell us, we'll keep asking. House by house. And asking leaves marks."

"I don't—"

"Your daughter," the woman said. "Liese. She has her mother's laugh. Do you remember the first time you heard it?"

Bramble's face changed. Of course it changed. Every parent carries that sound like a coal banked in the chest, and the woman had reached into his shirt and touched it barehanded.

"Don't," Bramble whispered.

"It can be the last thing you ever lose," she said. "Or the first. Your choice, really, comes down to whether your neighbour talks faster than I do."

I was on the roof of the chandlery across the lane. I don't remember climbing it. I remember the slate cold under my palms, and my heart slamming, and the thought arriving with terrible clarity: *they'll do it anyway. She's not offering a choice. She's teaching the town what she is.*

And then, because Bramble Hedley was brave in the way of stubborn men and foolish ones, he said: "Get out of my town."

The woman sighed. Genuinely sighed, like a laundress facing another basket.

She touched the silver rod to his temple.

I have stolen thousands of memories. I know the mechanics of it better than any thief alive—the little tug at the root of a thing, the way a recollection lifts whole if you find its seam. What she did was nothing like that. There was no seam. There was no care. She put the rod against his head and *poured*, and Bramble Hedley's face emptied like a basin knocked over.

He didn't scream. That was the worst part. The scream had happened earlier; this was quieter than screaming. His eyes stayed open and his mouth stayed shut and behind them, visibly, like frost retreating across a window, forty years went out. His wife was shaking him and saying his name and he looked at her with the mild, faintly apologetic attention of a man regarding a stranger who has mistaken him for someone else.

"Liese?" his wife was saying. "Bramble, say Liese. Say her name. Bramble—"

"Who," said Bramble Hedley pleasantly, "are you?"

His wife made a sound I will hear until I die. It wasn't a word. It was the sound the valley itself must have made, centuries ago, when the founders sealed it—a small creature realizing the cage door has closed.

"Ma'am." The tall man touched the widow's elbow with something that in a kinder world might have been gentleness. "We regret the necessity. Your husband will be cared for. The Crown—"

"Not your crown," she spat. "Whatever crown you serve."

"The crown that keeps the walls of the world standing," he said, and for one unguarded moment something flickered behind his kindness—not doubt, exactly. Fatigue. A workman's fatigue. "Someone sheltered a traitor here. Treason doesn't stay in one house, ma'am. It gets into the water. We're draining the well."

The woman straightened, rod retracted, and scanned the street—and I flattened myself against the chandlery roof, pulse roaring, certain she'd look up, certain the white lanterns would find me—

But her gaze slid past. Slid *off*, the way eyes slid off Hollowmere, off me, off everything in this valley that had learned to be unremarkable. For one heartbeat the old warding worked, the founders' hole doing what holes do.

Then she frowned, very slightly, like a woman who has miscounted coins, and looked again.

I rolled off the far side of the roof and dropped into the alley and ran.

* * *

I didn't go home. Home was the first place they'd look, if they looked. I went to Maren's, in through the kitchen window she never latched because locks are for people with something worth locking, and found her sitting up in her chair in the dark, fully dressed, waiting.

"They took Bramble's mind," I said. No preamble. My voice sounded like someone else's.

"I know." She didn't ask how I knew. Nineteen years, and she had never once asked how I knew things. "Saw the lanterns from my window. White fire. Haven't seen white fire since I was a girl, and I only saw it once, the night the last warden left." Her hands were shaking around her cold tea. "That's Regent's work, Theo. That's the Ashen court's own instrument. They haven't come past the valley walls in eighty years."

"They're asking about the courier."

"Are you surprised?"

"No." I crouched in front of her chair, and the map against my ribs went cold, colder, answering the white fire outside like iron answers a lodestone. "They'll go house to house. She said so. Asking leaves marks, Maren. By tomorrow noon half the town will be smiling at their own children like strangers, and they won't even know what they lost."

"Yes." She said it simply. That was Maren—she had survived the valley's founding truth, the one she'd finally given me: *this is a hole, not a home.* She'd known longer than anyone that peace built on forgetting is a debt, and debts get collected. "So. What are you going to do?"

"What can I do? I'm a pickpocket." I heard the bitterness come out of me sideways. "I steal afternoons and sell them back at a markup. Against those—" I gestured at the window, at the white glow on the rooftops. "Against that, I'm nothing."

"Mm." She set down the cup. When she looked at me, her milky eyes had gone sharp, and I remembered that Maren had not always been old, and had not always been safe, and had crossed whatever distance lies between the Court's war and this kitchen with her mind intact by choosing, every single day, what she would refuse to forget. "Boy. You stole a dead man's errand off his chest and you've spent three days calling it a burden. Say what it actually is."

"A map."

"To what?"

I hesitated. Outside, somewhere down Tallow Row, a door opened and closed, and voices murmured—the hunters moving to the next house, methodical as tide.

"To the Ninefold Court," I said. "To the regalia. Thirteen turns, he said, and twelve are gone. One moon left to gather nine relics and reforge a covenant I barely believe existed, hunted by a Regent whose soldiers unmake people like Bramble Hedley before breakfast." I laughed, and it came out cracked. "I'm a memory-thief from a valley that isn't real. I'm the least qualified person alive."

"Qualified." Maren snorted. "The Court wasn't reforged by qualified people last time either, if the songs are honest. It was reforged by frightened ones who went anyway." She leaned forward and gripped my wrist, her fingers bird-light and immovable. "Here's what I know, and then I'll stop talking, because you need to walk and I need to sit. Those three will finish Tallow Row tonight and start on Chapel Street tomorrow, and they will keep starting until someone gives them a name or there's no one left to ask. You can't fight them—you're right about that. But you can take the thing they're hunting and carry it out through the valley's teeth, and every hour you run is an hour they chase *you* instead of hollowing *us*."

"That's not heroism, Maren. That's arithmetic."

"It's both. Most important things are." She released my wrist. "Go at dawn. The east road, past the standing stone—there's a gap in the old wardline, the founders' back door, and the hunters don't know it because the wardens forgot to write it down before they forgot everything else. Take the coat with the deep pockets." A ghost of her old dry humour. "You'll want to steal things out there. Steal *well*."

I stood. My legs felt borrowed. At the window I paused, looking north across the sleeping rooftops toward the well and the market and the loft above the tannery where I'd slept my whole careful life, and I understood—with the strange clarity of a man watching his own house from the outside—that I was saying goodbye to the only version of myself I'd ever been. The invisible one. The competent one. The one who kept everyone at arm's length so that losing them would cost nothing.

It was going to cost everything. I knew that even then.

"One more thing," Maren said behind me. "The woman with the rod. When she looked at you tonight—did her eyes find you, or slide off?"

"...Slid off. Then she frowned. Like she'd dropped a stitch."

Maren nodded slowly. "Then she'll remember the stitch. They always do. Which means you have until dawn, and not one hour more." She waved me toward the window with trembling, imperious fingers. "Go. Sleep two hours if you can. Wake up angry. Anger keeps better than fear on a long road."

* * *

Dawn in Hollowmere comes up gold through the valley mist, and I left in the grey part before it, when the town was still the colour of ash.

I went by way of Tallow Row. I tell myself it was to check the hunters were gone. I tell myself a lot of things.

Their lanterns were dark and their horses were stabled at the inn—they meant to stay, then, to keep asking, house by house, exactly as promised—and the Hedleys' door stood open to the morning. Bramble sat on his own doorstep in the cold, wrapped in a blanket his wife had put on him, watching the sunrise with the placid delight of a child. His wife knelt beside him, feeding him bread soaked in honey, bite by bite, the way you feed someone you love who has forgotten how to be loved back.

"Beautiful morning," Bramble said to me as I passed, cheerful, courteous, utterly vacant. "Do I know you, friend?"

I stopped. Everything in me that was trained to vanish said *keep walking*. Instead I crouched down to his eye level, and Bramble Hedley—who had shod my boots, who had shouted at me for running through his forge yard, who had once, when I was nine and fatherless, taught me to hold a hammer without breaking my thumb—looked back at me with nothing behind his eyes but morning.

"My name's Theo," I said.

"Theo." He tried it out, tasting it. "Good name. Solid." He smiled. "Is it a good day?"

I thought about the map cold against my ribs. About thirteen turns and twelve gone. About a woman with a silver needle and a tall man with a kind smile and a crown that kept the world's walls standing by hollowing out anyone who noticed them fall.

"It's going to be," I said. "Ask me again sometime."

I stood, and walked east through the mist, past the well, past the market stalls shuttered and sleeping, past the standing stone where the founders' back door gaped in the wardline like a missing tooth. Behind me Hollowmere lay quiet in its valley, and for the first time in my life the silence didn't feel like safety. It felt like a wound I was finally walking out of—and carrying, hidden in my coat, the one thing that might teach the world to heal it.

The road beyond the stone climbed into hills no one from Hollowmere had names for.

I took a breath, stepped through, and heard—faint, behind me, already fading—the white lanterns kindling again on Chapel Street.
