## 5. Crossing the Threshold

Dawn in Hollowmere came the color of old milk, and Theo watched it from the ridge above the eastern pass with a pack on his back and a dead man's map burning cold against his chest.

He had left before full light, which was its own kind of confession. Honest travelers waited for the sun. He had also taken three things that were not his: a coil of rope from the ferry shed, half a wheel of Maren's smoked cheese—she would notice, and she would say nothing—and a memory of his own choosing, pulled carefully from his own head two nights ago and pressed into a glass vial like everything else he'd ever stolen.

It was the memory of the stranger's face. Silver eyes, blood on his teeth, the word *thirteen* shaped more than spoken. Theo had decided he did not want to carry it every waking hour. Not yet. The vial sat in his inner pocket beside the map, and when he held it up to the grey morning it caught the light the way frost catches light, all edges and no warmth.

The town lay below him, small as a spilled handful of coins. Chimney smoke rising straight in the windless air. The market square where he'd spent ten years making himself unremarkable, lifting hours out of other people's heads and selling them back at a markup. Somewhere down there, ash-grey riders were asking questions, and a neighbor named Odell Brase no longer knew his wife's name, or his own, or why he woke at night weeping without knowing what for.

Theo turned his back on it before he could change his mind.

The pass was narrow, hemmed in by slopes of grey scree and the black skeletons of pines that never quite grew. Hollowmere's silence went with him—that felt wrongness in the air, the sense of a room where music had stopped. He had lived inside that silence for twenty-six years. He had thought it was the world.

The map shifted against his sternum with every step, cold as a coin left overnight in snow. He had tried to be rid of it twice already. Burned once—it had reappeared folded in his boot. Buried once beneath a cairn—he'd walked fifty paces before the cold bloomed against his skin again, and when he looked back the cairn stood untouched and the map was in his hand. It did not want to leave him. That was the part that frightened him most. Things in Hollowmere could be dropped. Things in Hollowmere stayed dropped.

An hour past the ridge, the valley's edge announced itself.

He had never seen it. No one spoke of it, because no one crossed it, because crossing it was not a thing anyone did, the way leaving one's own body was not a thing anyone did. But there it was: a line in the landscape so subtle that if he hadn't been watching for it he'd have missed it. A place where the scree changed color. Where the pines, dead on the southern slope, stood green and heavy-needled on the northern one. Where the wind—

Where the wind *moved*.

Hollowmere had no weather worth the name. Still air, grey light, seasons you tracked by the market calendar rather than by anything the sky did. But here, ahead of him, the wind combed through the trees in long silver waves, and birdsong threaded down from somewhere above him, actual birds, three and four voices braided together, and Theo stood at the border of it with his heart going hard against the map's cold weight like a fist knocking on a door.

"Right," he said aloud, to no one, because saying things aloud was how he kept himself from thinking them. "Right."

He stepped across.

---

The world hit him like deep water.

Not sound—sound came later, roaring, enormous, the rush of a river and the creak of living wood and the far-off cry of something that was not any bird he knew. What hit him first was *color*, violent and excessive, greens so green they hurt, sunlight falling through the canopy in golden blades instead of diffusing into grey. And underneath the color, underneath the sound, something else poured into him, something with no name he possessed, a pressure and a singing and a vast attention turning toward him the way a crowd turns toward a stage.

Magic. He knew the word; everyone in Hollowmere knew the word, the way prisoners know the word *sky*. He had never expected it to feel like this—like being *recognized*. Like the world had been squinting at him his whole life and suddenly saw him clearly and found him interesting.

The map woke.

Cold flared to heat—not burning, but alive, a hearth-warmth spreading through the cloth and through his chest and down his arms until his fingers tingled. He fumbled it out of his shirt and it unfolded itself in his hands, creases moving like breathing, ink crawling. Lines he'd studied for three days in the valley—blank parchment, mostly, with a few stubborn markings that refused to fade—now blazed. Rivers ran. Roads drew themselves in fine silver thread. And at nine points across the living geography, marks surfaced like fish rising to feed: nine sigils, nine shapes, each distinct, each thrumming faintly against his palms like held breath.

One of them pulsed brighter than the rest, close by—a day's walk, maybe less. A shape like a lantern, drawn in gold, hanging over a mark on the map that read, in letters that assembled themselves while he watched:

*Vespergate Chapel, drowned.*

"Drowned chapel," Theo repeated. His voice sounded thin under the roar of the river. "Of course. Why would it be anywhere sensible."

A bird shrieked directly overhead and he nearly lost his footing. Everything here was too loud, too vivid, too *much*—he had grown up in a valley that muffled the world like wool wrapped around a bell, and now the bell was ringing inside his skull. He laughed, actually laughed, giddy and terrified, and the sound startled him because he couldn't remember the last time he'd heard himself do it.

Then someone put a knife to his throat, and the laughter died where it stood.

---

"Don't move." A woman's voice, low and roughened, close behind his ear. "Hands where I can see them. Slowly."

He raised his hands. The map hung open between his fingers, still glowing, still showing its nine bright sigils to anyone who cared to look—which she plainly did, because her breath stopped.

"What," she said, very quietly, in the tone people used for things that frightened them, "is that."

"It's a map."

"I can see it's a map." The blade pressed harder. A sliver of warmth ran down his neck; his own blood, he realized distantly, first blood he'd ever shed outside the valley. "Maps don't breathe. Maps don't *shine*. Where did you get it?"

"A dying man gave it to me."

That earned him a pause. "What dying man? Describe him."

"Tall. Grey coat, court-cut, though he'd dressed it down. Wounded through the side—he'd been riding hard for days. Silver eyes." Theo chose his next words with the care of a man defusing something. "He said thirteen turns. Then he said—" he cast back to the vial in his pocket, to the face he'd locked away, "—then he died."

The silence behind him stretched so long he counted his own heartbeats. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen, and something in him flinched at the number.

"You're lying," the woman said, but her voice had changed—the certainty gone out of it, replaced by something rawer. "No one crosses out of Hollowmere. Nothing crosses out of Hollowmere. It's a hole in the world. So either you're lying about where you came from, or—"

She came around into his view, and he got his first look at her. Tall, broad through the shoulders, maybe forty, with brown skin going leathery at the corners of her eyes and a scar that split one eyebrow cleanly in two. She wore caravan leathers over mail, road-worn and well-kept, and at her hip, beside the knife she'd already shown him, hung a sword in a plain scabbard—and on the scabbard's throat, a badge of dark metal shaped like an eight-pointed star with one point broken off.

Theo had seen that shape only once before: scratched hastily into the margin of a book in Maren's house, next to a sentence in a language neither of them read. *The Court's last war*, Maren had said. *They fled it. That's why the valley is empty of magic. They cut it out like a rotten tooth.*

"You're Court," he said. "Or you were."

The knife moved faster than he could track. It didn't cut him—it hooked the map's edge and tore it out of his grip, and she backed away three quick steps with the parchment snapping taut between them, alive and glowing in her hands, and her face above it was terrible to see. Not anger. Something older than anger.

"Where," she said, and now her voice shook, "did he die. The courier. Where exactly."

"My loft. In Hollowmere. Two nights past."

"And you took his map off his corpse and just—walked out. With this." She turned the map over, hunting its edges, and Theo watched her realize, as he had, that it would not burn, would not tear, would not leave. Her jaw tightened. "Do you have any idea what this is?"

"I know it doesn't stay lost. I know it burns cold. I know it shows nine places, and I know one of them is a drowned chapel a day from here, and I know a man bled out on my floor telling me I had thirteen turns to—" He stopped, because the next part was the part he didn't believe yet. "To find whatever's at those nine places. Before the boundary comes down."

"The Ninefold regalia," she said flatly.

"If that's what they're called."

"That's what they're called." She stared at him, and he watched her doing arithmetic—his accent, his ignorance, his obvious terror, the impossible fact of a Hollowmere-born walking and talking and carrying a covenant-map. Whatever sum she reached, she didn't like it. "You're telling me a courier made it *into* the valley. Through wards that have held since before your grandmother was born. And then died in your house. And you're telling me you've never heard of the Ninefold Court."

"I'm telling you the valley doesn't have a *Court*, madam. The valley doesn't have much of anything. We trade, we farm, we keep our heads down. Nobody remembers why we're like that. We just are."

Something flickered across her face at that—pity, maybe, or recognition. She lowered the knife an inch. It was not, he noted, lowered far.

"Sefa Vane," she said abruptly. "Caravan guard, out of Marrow's Crossing, currently employed by the Tallow Road company, currently supposed to be watching six wagons of dyewool that are at this moment rolling north without me. And you are a dead man, valley-boy, because that map has a scent on it, and everything that hunts by that scent is going to find *you* before it finds me."

"The Regent's men." He said it carefully, watching her. "Grey sigils. Ash on their collars."

Her knuckles whitened on the knife. "You've seen them."

"They're in my valley. They wiped a neighbor of mine down to nothing looking for the courier. I watched it happen. So I left." He heard his own voice go quiet and hard and was surprised by it. "I'd like it noted that I didn't want any of this. I sold memories at a market stall. I was *good* at it. I had a system."

For the first time, something almost like humor touched Sefa Vane's scarred face and did not survive the journey. "You stole memories. For coin."

"Sold them back to their owners, mostly. At a markup. It's not—" He stopped. Reconsidered. "It's exactly as bad as it sounds. But it's honest theft, at least. Everyone knows what I am. They just don't mind, because grief is expensive."

She looked at him for a long moment then, really looked, the way he'd seen traders appraise goods—and he hated it, and understood it, and stood straighter under it anyway. Beyond them the river roared and the strange green light moved through the leaves and the map glowed in her fist like a coal that refused to die.

"There's a chapel," she said finally. "Half a day east, sunk in the flood-meadows since the Sundering. Fishermen won't go near it. They say the bells ring underwater." She jerked her chin at the lantern sigil, still pulsing gold. "If your map's pointing where I think it's pointing, then the first regalia isn't a rumor anymore, and if the first regalia isn't a rumor, the thirteenth moon is closer than anyone wants to say."

"So you'll take me."

"I'll *walk in the same direction as you*," she said, "because whoever killed your courier will come for that map, and I'd rather be standing next to it than wondering where it is. That's not trust, valley-boy. That's arithmetic."

"I wouldn't dream of calling it trust." He held out his hand. "Theo Dunmore. Memory-thief, recently unemployed."

She looked at his hand and did not take it. She did, however, turn east and start walking, boots finding the deer trail along the waterline without hesitation, and after a moment the map drifted back across the space between them—floating, unhurried, insolent—and settled into Theo's hands again, warm as a sleeping animal.

Sefa glanced sideways at it, then at him.

"It likes you," she said, in the tone of a woman delivering a diagnosis she hoped was fatal. "In my experience, that's worse."

They walked east along the roaring river, thief and deserter, toward a chapel that rang its bells underwater—and behind them, far back along the trail, something small and grey lifted off the ridge of Hollowmere's pass and followed.
