## 6. The Bridge of Small Debts

Sefa had killed men for looking at her the way the boy was looking at her now.

Not exactly that way. The boy—Theo, he'd said, in a voice still rough with valley dust—looked at her with something worse than fear or challenge. He looked at her like she was a door he intended to walk through, and doors could be broken. She'd been a knight of the Ninefold Court for eleven years. She knew what it meant when someone decided you were an obstacle instead of a person.

So she kept her sword drawn and let him see her do the arithmetic.

He sat across the dead campfire where she'd dragged him by his collar after nearly opening his throat, hands raised, palms out, the way you showed a stranger you carried no blade. Except this one did carry a blade—a small one, a thief's knife—and more dangerous than that, he carried a map folded against his chest like a second heart. She had felt it through his shirt when she'd pinned him. Cold. Wrong. The kind of cold that didn't come from weather.

"Ask me again," she said, "why I shouldn't take it from your corpse."

"Because you've had three chances since the road forked," Theo said. His voice didn't shake. That annoyed her. "And each time, you waited to see if I'd run first."

"I was being thorough."

"You were being curious." He tilted his head, and there it was again—that thief's steadiness, the calm of someone who had spent his whole life reading strangers because his survival depended on it. "You saw the map and you didn't call for anyone. You didn't turn me over to the magistrates in Corrow's Ford, and they pay for things like this. You bought me dinner instead. A man doesn't feed the thing he means to destroy."

"A man does feed a prisoner before interrogating him," Sefa said. "It's traditional."

The boy almost smiled. Then he looked down at his hands, and whatever humor he'd found went out of him like a snuffed lamp.

"They came through my valley," he said quietly. "Two days after the courier died in my loft. Men in ash-grey, wearing a sigil like a handprint made of cinders. They took a neighbor of mine—her name is Wren, she sells dyed wool—and they *emptied* her. Not killed. Emptied. She sat in the square afterward smiling at nothing, and everyone who loved her had to decide whether that counted as losing her." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious and trying to be neither. "That's what follows this map. So either you tell me what it actually is, or you kill me, but I'm done being led around by someone who knows more than me and pretends otherwise."

The fire crackled. Somewhere off toward the tree line, a nightjar called its two-note question and got no answer.

Sefa sheathed her sword. It wasn't mercy. It was that her arm was tired, and because some part of her—the part she hated most, the part that had once knelt on marble floors and sworn oaths—wanted to say the words out loud to someone who would understand what they cost.

"All right," she said. "You want to know what the Covenant is. I'll tell you. But understand that I'm not telling you as a kindness. I'm telling you because you're going to walk into this whether I help you or not, and I'd rather you die knowing the shape of the thing that kills you."

"That's very comforting."

"It's honest. Take notes."

* * *

"The Ninefold Court ruled everything," Sefa said. "Before your time, before mine. Nine regents, nine seats, nine regalia—the physical anchors of the binding law. Crown, lantern, ring, chalice, spear, veil, coin, bell, and the heart-stone. Each regalia held one clause of the Covenant, and each regent bore one regalia, and together they held the boundary between worlds shut. Do you know what's on the other side of the boundary?"

"No."

"Neither do I. Neither did any of us. That was the point. The Court sealed it so long ago that even the regents forgot what they were keeping out. All they remembered was the weight." She turned her hands over, studying the calluses. "Nine locks on one door. And the door only stays shut while all nine agree to hold their keys."

"And the Ashen Regent broke that agreement."

"She took three seats." Sefa heard her own voice go flat, the way it always did when she reached this part, flattening itself against her will like a hand pressed over a wound. "Crown, spear, and bell. She didn't steal them. Stealing implies the holders fought back. She arrived at each court with her erasure-hunters and offered every regent's household the same choice: surrender the regalia and keep your memories, or keep your regalia and become a stranger to your own children. Two regents chose their families. One chose pride, and his whole line forgot his name within the hour." She paused. "Three regalia in a single season. No war. Just arithmetic."

Theo was silent for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was careful. "And the other six?"

"Scattered. Hidden by regents who saw which way the wind blew. The ring went east into the mountains. The chalice sank with its bearer rather than surface. The lantern—" Sefa stopped. Her jaw tightened. "The lantern drowned with a chapel in the fenlands south of here. If your map shows what I think it shows, that's your first stop, and you should know the chapel didn't drown by accident. Its keeper flooded it herself to keep the lantern from the Regent's reach. Forty-one people were inside when she pulled the levee."

The boy flinched. Good, Sefa thought, and then hated herself for thinking it. Let him flinch. Let him understand early that this road was paved with people who had made hard choices and paid for them in full, so he'd stop treating this like a job of work, another mark, another lift.

"Why do you know all this?" Theo asked. "Really. A caravan guard doesn't recite the structure of the Ninefold Court like scripture."

Because I helped write it, she thought. Because I was there.

What she said was: "I told you. I'm settling a debt."

"To whom?"

She stood, because it was easier to say standing, and walked to the edge of the firelight where the dark began. Behind her she heard him shift, wary, one hand finding his knife. Smart boy.

"My name isn't Sefa Vane," she said. "That's the name I bought in Corrow's Ford six years ago, along with the guard's papers and the scar story about the bandit raid. My name is Sefa Anharis. Third lance of the Court's honor guard. Sworn shield to the seat of the Bell." She turned her head just enough to catch his expression in the firelight: the slow widening, the recalculation, the thief doing sums. "The night the Regent came for the bell, I was stationed at the palace gate. My orders were to hold it. To hold it with my life if necessary. That was the oath. Every guard in that courtyard swore the same oath on the same stone."

"What happened?"

"I looked at the hunters coming up the avenue—two hundred of them, grey cloaks, and behind them a woman on a white horse with eyes like banked coals—and I did the same arithmetic everyone else did. I thought: my death buys nothing. The bell falls whether I stand or run. And I ran." The word came out of her clean, like a tooth pulled whole. "By morning the bell was hers, and the regent who owned it was sitting in a chair smiling at his daughters, and he didn't know them, and I was three counties away learning to answer to a different name."

The silence stretched. Theo didn't offer comfort. She noticed that. Most people rushed to fill silences like this one, pouring out absolution like it cost them anything. The boy just watched her with those careful eyes and let the guilt sit where it fell, which was either cruelty or respect, and she found she couldn't tell which, and found she didn't entirely mind.

"So the debt," he said finally.

"When the Court fell, the old law didn't vanish. It frayed. Every oath sworn under the Covenant left a residue—you could feel it, in the years after, like a draft under a door. Oaths don't stop existing because the thing they bound is broken. They go... unkept. And unkept oaths have weight. They follow you." She tapped her sternum, twice, where the armor used to sit. "Mine has followed me for six years. Every caravan I've guarded, every drunk I've pulled out of a gutter, every mile I've walked without turning myself in—I've been paying interest on a debt I can never settle in full. Because the debt isn't to the Court. The Court's gone. The debt is to the oath itself."

"And the map?"

"Is an oath given flesh, if half the stories are true. A covenant-heart's summons. It doesn't choose bearers randomly, boy. It chooses people who owe something." She let that land, watched him look down at the fold of leather against his chest as though it might bite. "Which tells me the dying courier saw something in you worth saddling. I haven't decided yet if I pity him."

"You said you'd guide me," Theo said. "In the ford. Before you knew what I carried."

"I said I'd guide you to the crossroads. You've passed the crossroads."

"Then why are we still talking?"

Sefa laughed despite herself—a short, rusty sound, like a hinge unused for years. She came back into the firelight and crouched across from him, forearms on knees, close enough that he could see the faded brand on her inner wrist: nine interlocked rings, half-scarred over, a deserter's shame she'd never bothered to remove. Removing it would have been one more forgetting.

"Here are my terms," she said. "One. We find the lantern, because if the Regent gets even one more regalia, the boundary thins past saving, and I'd rather the world didn't end on my watch twice. Two. You don't lie to me. Not about the map, not about what you are—and yes, I know what you are, valley-born, memory-thief, I felt your gift brush my mind on the road and I let you live anyway, so don't bother denying it. Three." She held up a third finger, and hesitated, and hated the hesitation. "Three. When this is done—if we live—there may come a moment when I ask you for something. A theft. Of a particular memory. Mine."

His eyebrows rose. "Which memory?"

"The one I told you about tonight. I want it taken out. Cleanly. I want to wake up owing nothing." Her voice stayed level through all of it, which she counted as a victory. "That's my price. That's the debt I'm settling—not to you, not to the Court. To myself. Guide you, protect you, get you to the regalia, and in exchange you cut the rot out of my head. Do we have terms?"

Theo studied her for a long moment. Firelight moved in his eyes, and behind the wariness she saw the thing she'd seen in the market at the ford, the thing that had made her spare him: not greed, exactly. Hunger. The hunger of a man looking at a door he'd never known existed.

"You want me to steal your desertion," he said slowly. "To make you forget you deserted."

"I want you to make me stop carrying it. There's a difference."

"Is there?" He asked it gently, which was somehow worse. "I've sold memories back to grieving people for most of my life. I know what forgetting costs. Wren's family couldn't look at her after. Whatever you're running from, cutting it out won't make it unhappen. It'll just mean there's no one left in your head who remembers what you owe."

"And that's bad?"

"That's lonely," Theo said simply. "Believe me. I'm the loneliest expert on loneliness in nine kingdoms."

Something in Sefa's chest shifted, small and unwelcome, like a stone rolling in a streambed. She smothered it.

"Terms," she repeated. "Or I take the map and you can walk home to your valley and explain to the ash-grey men why you smell of covenant-magic."

"We have terms," Theo said. "But I'm not taking anything until you're sure. And Sefa—" He paused, and for a moment the market-thief vanished and someone younger looked out of his face, someone who had watched a neighbor smile at nothing. "If the day comes and you still want it, I'll do it free. But I hope you don't."

She stood, abruptly, needing to be moving, needing distance from the strange pressure of being pitied by a boy twenty years her junior. She kicked dirt over the fire and shouldered her pack.

"Dawn in four hours," she said. "Sleep. Tomorrow we reach the fenlands, and after the fenlands nothing between us and the drowned chapel but water and the ghosts of forty-one people who died keeping that lantern safe." She glanced back at him, a silhouette against the last ember-glow. "Try to sleep lightly, thief. I still haven't decided if I trust you."

"Same," Theo said into the dark, and she heard the ghost of a smile in it.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat with her back against a birch trunk and watched the boy breathe and did what she always did on the worst nights, which was rehearse the gate.

The courtyard. The torchlight. The two hundred grey cloaks coming up the avenue like a tide of ash. Her hand on her sword hilt, the oath-stone warm beneath her palm, the old words rising in her throat—*hold, hold, hold*—and then the arithmetic, always the arithmetic, always the reasonable, survivable, damnable arithmetic, and her feet already turning.

Six years of that dream. Six years of waking with her hand closed around a sword hilt that wasn't there.

Except tonight, for the first time, the dream changed. Tonight, in the rehearsal, when the grey tide came up the avenue, she saw a thin figure step out of the palace shadows—a boy with a thief's knife and a dead man's map pressed to his chest—and he looked at her across the courtyard and asked, gently, terribly: *is there?*

Is there a difference between cutting out the rot and being freed of it?

Sefa opened her eyes. The stars wheeled overhead, indifferent, ancient, the same stars that had watched the Court rise and fall and hadn't lifted a finger either way. Against her ribs, she felt her own heartbeat, steady, stubborn, still keeping its count.

*Hold,* said the oath, faintly, from wherever unkept oaths wait.

"Not yet," she murmured. "But maybe soon. Maybe this time."

Across the dead fire, the boy slept with one hand curled around the map even in dreams, as if afraid it would leave him, or as if—she considered the possibility with something approaching wonder—as if he'd already begun to understand that he couldn't put it down.

Tomorrow, the fens. Tomorrow, the lantern of unkept promises, full of every vow the world had ever failed to keep—including, she suspected, one made long ago by a woman whose voice the boy didn't know he remembered.

Sefa closed her eyes and, for the first time in six years, let herself hope that dawn would come before her dreams did.
