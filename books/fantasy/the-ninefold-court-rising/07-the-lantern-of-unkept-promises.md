## 7. The Lantern of Unkept Promises

The drowned chapel had been a chapel once, before the river took it. Now it was only a suggestion of one—a bell tower rising from the shallows like a finger pointing at nothing, its spire furred with moss, and beneath the green water the shapes of pews still ranked in their rows, patient as congregations that had waited three hundred years for a sermon.

Theo stood at the edge of the reed bank with the map pressed against his chest and felt it pulling.

It wasn't heat. He'd expected heat. The thing had burned cold since the dying stranger had folded it into his hands, and cold it remained, but here, at the water's edge, the cold had direction. It tugged at his sternum like a fish on a line. Behind him, Sefa crouched to check the girth strap on her horse, and her silence had a quality he'd learned to read over three days of road: she wanted him to say something about what he could feel, and she would die before asking.

"It's down there," Theo said. "Under the water."

"Obviously." She cinched the strap. "The Lantern of Unkept Promises, sunk in a flooded chapel in the middle of nowhere. Someone went to a great deal of trouble to make sure no one would ever come looking."

"Someone failed."

"No." Sefa straightened and looked at the tower with an expression Theo hadn't seen on her before—something older than her scarred pragmatism, something almost like mourning. "No one came looking because everyone who knew it was here is dead or scattered, and everyone who didn't know has no reason to wade into a haunted marsh. That's not failure, Dunmore. That's the most successful hiding I've ever seen."

"You've seen other regalia hidden?"

"I've seen where they *were* hidden." She swung up into the saddle without offering to help him onto the packhorse, as usual. "Empty places. Empty like robbed graves. Come on. We'll lose the light if we stand here admiring it."

* * *

The water came up to Theo's ribs by the time they reached the chapel doors, and it was the wrong temperature—not cold, exactly, but thin, as though it had forgotten how to be properly wet. His boots found stone underfoot. The doors themselves had rotted to lace, so they simply walked through them, and inside, the nave opened around them: a vast drowned hall lit from above by holes in the roof, shafts of afternoon light slanting through green water like pillars of glass.

Fish scattered from his footsteps. That much was ordinary. Everything else was not.

"Theo." Sefa's voice came strange and flattened, as if the chapel were swallowing the edges of it. "Look at the windows."

He turned. Where stained glass should have been there were only empty frames—but the frames were full of something. Not light. Not color. Shapes moved behind the leadwork, slow and formless, like smoke trapped in amber, like figures seen through frosted ice. And when Theo let his gift open—the sense he usually kept leashed so tight it ached—he heard them.

Whispers. Hundreds of them. Thousands. A congregation of voices all speaking at once, none of them words, all of them *unfinished*.

"What is this place?" His voice cracked on the last word.

"I told you. A chapel." Sefa waded past him toward the altar, water swirling around her waist. Her hand rested on her sword hilt, which was how she said *I'm frightened* without saying it. "The Court built these. Way-stations, sanctuaries—places where people could swear oaths that mattered. Before your valley existed. Before the Covenant broke." She stopped at the altar steps and stared down through the water. "There. Do you see it?"

Theo saw it.

At the foot of the altar, half-buried in silt, lay a lantern. It was smaller than he expected—no bigger than a loaf of bread—with a frame of dark metal and panes that glowed faintly from within, though there was no flame. The glow shifted as he watched, brightening and dimming like breathing. Like waiting.

"That's it? That's the first regalia?"

"That's the Lantern of Unkept Promises." Sefa did not move toward it. "One of nine. Each one holds one of the binding laws of the old Covenant. This one held the law of oaths—that promises made before the Court could not be broken lightly, and could never be made falsely." Her jaw worked. "When the Court fell, everything the Lantern had ever witnessed spilled back out. Every vow sworn in every chapel across the realms, all the ones people broke, all the ones they never got the chance to keep. They say the weeping could be heard for forty miles."

"And now?"

"Now it just... collects." She finally looked at him, and her silver eye caught the underwater light. "That's why the chapel is drowned, Dunmore. It's not hiding the Lantern. It's *muffling* it. Water between the world and all that grief." She gestured at the shadowed windows. "The oaths got into the glass. Into the stones. This whole place is soaked in things people meant to do and never did."

Theo stood very still in the thin water, listening to the whispers, and thought of Hollowmere—thought of the valley where memory pooled like rainwater and he had spent his life skimming it off the top. He had thought he understood what it was to be surrounded by other people's pasts.

He had been playing in puddles.

"So we take it," he said. "And then what happens to all this?" He nodded at the windows, the whispering walls.

Sefa was quiet for a moment. "I don't know," she admitted. "The books say the regalia have to be borne. Carried by someone willing. Maybe carrying it means carrying *this*." She shrugged, armor clanking softly. "Or maybe it means setting it free. No one's ever gotten far enough to find out. Everyone who tried to take one before either died or—" She stopped.

"Or what?"

"Or gave up halfway. Walked away weeping and wouldn't say why." She climbed the altar steps, water streaming off her, and stood above the lantern with her fists on her hips like a woman arguing with it. "Well. I deserted my post the night the Court fell, so I suppose I'm the right person to carry a box of broken promises. Poetic justice." She reached down—

—and Theo's hand shot out and closed on her wrist.

She spun, sword half-drawn, but he was already shaking his head, already hearing himself say: "Wait. Wait—it wants—"

The map against his chest had gone from cold to *singing*, a frequency he felt in his teeth, and the lantern's glow had steadied, fixed, focused—on him.

"It knows you," Sefa breathed.

"It knows the map." But even as he said it, Theo knew he was lying, because the pull wasn't coming from the folded paper anymore. It was coming from the lantern itself, and it wasn't pulling at his chest. It was pulling at something deeper. At the hoard.

Every thief has a vault. Theo's was behind his eyes—in the stolen memories he'd carried for eleven years, the ones too precious or too dangerous to sell, the ones he told himself he kept for emergencies. A sunset over mountains he'd never climbed. A wedding he'd attended invisible. Three seconds of laughter from a child whose face he'd taken by accident from a dying traveler and had never been able to put down.

The lantern wanted all of them. Not to take—to *read*. To weigh them. To know whether he was a man who kept promises or a man who made them cheaply.

"It's deciding," Theo whispered.

"Then let it decide fast," Sefa said, "because if it doesn't like you, the books are very clear about what happens next, and I don't want to watch."

* * *

Later, Theo would try to describe what touching the Lantern felt like, and he would fail, because language was built by people who had never drowned in someone else's heart.

His fingers met the metal frame and the whispers stopped.

All of them. At once. The chapel went silent as a held breath, the fish hung motionless in the light-shafts, and then the flood came.

Not images, at first. *Intentions*. Raw and unarmored. *I will build her a house with blue shutters.* *I will come home by harvest.* *I will tell my brother the truth before winter.* *I will stop drinking after tonight—I mean it this time—I mean it—* *I will love her better than my father loved my mother.*

Ten thousand intentions, layered like sediment, each one followed by its own absence. The house never built. The harvest missed. The truth died with the brother. The bottle won. The love ran dry. Theo felt every failure land in his chest like a stone dropped down a well, and the well was bottomless, and the stones did not stop falling.

He was on his knees in the water. He didn't remember kneeling. Sefa was shouting his name somewhere far away, and the lantern was drinking him—reading the hoard behind his eyes, weighing his small treasury of stolen moments against its ocean of abandoned vows—and he understood suddenly, with terrible clarity, why the chapel was drowned, why the water was thin, why everything here was muffled.

Because this was what the world sounded like when the muffling was removed. This was the true weight of human wanting. And no unguarded mind could bear it.

*Let go,* he told himself. *Drop it. Take your hands off—*

But he couldn't. Because underneath the sediment, underneath ten thousand strangers' failures, something else was surfacing. Something the lantern had saved for last, the way a current returns what sinks.

A vow.

*I will find him. Whatever it costs, whatever I have to become—I will find my son, and I will tell him why I left, and I will pray to every dead god that he can forgive me.*

A woman's voice.

Theo's mother's voice.

He had never heard it. He had been four years old when she left Hollowmere and never returned; whatever memory he might have had of her was lost with the rest of his childhood, in the accident, in the sickness, in whatever hole lived at the center of his past that Maren would never talk about. He had built his whole life in the space where her voice should have been. He had sold other people's mothers' voices in the market of Hollowmere for copper coins, and gone home to a loft with no one in it, and told himself he didn't need what he couldn't have.

Now he had it. Three seconds of it, wrapped around a promise.

Her voice was low and roughened, the voice of a woman who had been crying or shouting or both. She spoke the way people speak when they are already running. And the vow was *broken*—he could feel its shape, feel the lantern holding it the way a hand holds a snapped thread—she had never found him, or she had died trying, or she had become whatever she had to become and lost herself along the way. The promise sat in the lantern's keeping like an unpaid debt, accruing interest for twenty years, and now its creditor was kneeling in a flooded chapel with salt water on his face that had nothing to do with the marsh.

"*Theo.*" Sefa's hand on his shoulder, real and hard. "*Theo, breathe.*"

"She's in there." His voice came out wrecked, unrecognizable. "My mother. Her voice. She made a vow—to find me—and it's—it's in the lantern, it's one of the unkept ones, she never—"

He couldn't finish. He didn't need to. He saw Sefa understand, saw it hit her in the face like a slap: the orphan boy, the memory-thief, the map that had chosen him. The odds were impossible. The odds were also irrelevant. The lantern did not deal in probability. It dealt in truth.

"Is she alive?" Sefa asked quietly. "Can you tell?"

Theo reached into himself, into the place where the lantern's touch still lingered like frost, and searched the vow the way he'd searched a thousand travelers' minds—gently, thoroughly, looking for the pulse underneath the words. Looking for the timestamp every memory carries, the scent of the air, the age of the hands.

The vow was old. Twenty years old, give or take—the same age as the hole in his life.

But it was not the newest thing in the lantern.

Somewhere in that drowned cathedral of broken promises, more recent than his mother's voice, there was another vow in another voice—one he hadn't touched yet, one the lantern was holding back, saving, the way he himself saved the best goods for buyers who could pay.

And it was addressed to him by name.

* * *

He surfaced gasping, and the chapel's whispers rushed back in around him like water into a footprint. The lantern sat in his hands—when had he picked it up?—light and cold and humming faintly, its panes dimmed now, sated or sleeping.

Sefa hauled him upright by the collar. "You were under for a minute. You weren't breathing. I thought—"

"I'm fine." He wasn't. His legs shook. The lantern's weight in his palms was nothing—two pounds of dark metal—and everything, an anchor chained to his soul. He could still hear the sediment settling in him, all those broken vows finding places to live, and threaded through them, bright as wire: *I will find my son.*

Grief, yes. Grief like he'd never known, grief for a woman he couldn't verify, a voice he couldn't date, a promise he couldn't collect on. She might be alive. She might be twenty years dead. She might have stopped searching a week after she started. The lantern held only what was sworn, not what was done, and the difference between them was the whole width of Theo's hollow life.

But underneath the grief, quieter and uglier, something else was waking up.

Hunger.

He knew this hunger. He'd fed it carefully his whole existence, rationing it, keeping it civilized. It was the hunger that made a good thief and a bad neighbor: the need to reach into the next mind, and the next, and the next, to keep pulling threads until the whole garment unraveled and he finally, finally knew.

The lantern had shown him three seconds of his mother. Somewhere in the world—or somewhere in the *past*, which amounted to the same thing—there was the rest. Her face. Her name. The reason she left. And Theo had spent eleven years selling memories back to the grieving, and he knew exactly what a person will pay for the voice of the dead.

"Dunmore." Sefa's voice cut through it, sharp. "Whatever you're thinking—stop. I've seen that look before. On men standing over treasure. It doesn't end well."

"I'm not thinking anything."

"Liar. You're a thief; I've watched you lie to innkeeps for three days. You're a bad liar when it matters." She nodded at the lantern. "What did it show you? All of it. Don't ration me."

So he told her. The vow. The voice. The way the promise had frayed and never been honored, and the newer whisper underneath, the one that spoke his name. When he finished, Sefa was silent for a long moment, staring at the lightless windows where the trapped oaths drifted like weather.

"There's a story," she said finally, "that the ninth seat of the old Court was the Keeper of Oaths. That the Regent—whoever she was, whoever she is—began as the one who tended the Lantern. Who heard every vow and judged which ones deserved enforcement." Her silver eye fixed on him. "If that's true, then that lantern isn't just a regalia, Theo. It's a record. Everything anyone ever promised the Court. Including—" she hesitated, and he watched her decide to say it— "including whatever your mother promised, and to whom, and why a vow to find her son ended up filed among the failures instead of the fulfilled."

The implication landed slowly, the way heavy things land.

"If I learn to read it," Theo said, "really read it—not just drown in it, but *search* it—then somewhere in there is the rest of her. What happened to her. Whether she's alive."

"Yes."

"And the newer vow, the one that used my name—someone swore something to me. Recently. Someone who knew I'd end up here."

"Yes." Sefa climbed the altar steps and stood over him, water dripping, sword still drawn, and for the first time since he'd met her she looked less like a mercenary and more like what she claimed she'd once been: a knight of the Ninefold Court, standing witness. "Which means the person who sent you that map—the one who died in your loft—didn't just choose you at random. They made you a promise, and they filed it here so you'd find it. So think very carefully, Dunmore, before you start digging through that thing looking for your mother. Because promises in that lantern get kept the hard way, or not at all."

Theo looked down at the Lantern of Unkept Promises, cradled in his arms like a sleeping animal, and felt the hunger sharpen itself against his ribs.

Somewhere in the drowned dark, a voice that was not his mother's whispered his name again—closer this time, and clearer, as if it had been waiting twenty years for him to wade in and listen.
