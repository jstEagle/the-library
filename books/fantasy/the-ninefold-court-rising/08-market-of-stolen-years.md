## 8. Market of Stolen Years

Vell announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke and brine and something sweeter underneath, like fruit left too long in the sun. Theo crested the last hill on the caravan road at dusk, and the city unfolded below him in tiers of lamplit stone, spilling down toward a harbor where a hundred ships rode at anchor with their lanterns lit like fallen constellations.

He had never seen so many lights in his life. Hollowmere kept its lamps few and its nights dark; light drew eyes, and eyes drew questions, and questions were dangerous when your whole town was built on pretending to be nothing. But Vell burned like it wanted to be seen from the moon.

"First time?" Sefa reined her horse beside his mule. She'd barely spoken in three days—not since the drowned chapel, not since he'd woken screaming from the flood of broken vows, not since she'd watched him press his palms against the lantern's glass and weep for a woman whose voice he couldn't place. "You look like a man seeing the sea for the first time."

"I've seen the sea," Theo said. "In borrowed memories. A fisherman's, once. It was cold and it smelled like death and he loved it anyway."

"That's the sea." She nudged her horse forward. "The city's worse. Keep your hood up, keep your hands in your pockets, and don't touch anyone's skin unless you mean to."

"I know how to be careful."

"You know how to be careful in a village of four hundred sheep-farmers." Her silver-grey eyes—Court-trained, he was learning, everything about her was Court-trained—swept the road behind them out of habit. "Vell is the largest free city on the coast. No regent holds it, no law does either, which means everyone here is running from something or selling something, and usually both. Including people who can do what you do."

That landed harder than he let it show. In Hollowmere he had been singular, a secret even from himself until recently—a boy who could lift a traveler's wedding day off their tongue like a thorn and sell it back to the widow who needed it more. Here, apparently, he might be one of dozens.

Or he might be prey.

* * *

They entered through the Salt Gate as the bells rang evening, and Vell swallowed them whole.

Theo had thought he understood crowds. He did not. The streets moved like rivers in flood—fishwives shouting prices, a funeral procession threading between fruit carts, children selling roasted chestnuts from trays strapped to their chests, a woman arguing theology with a statue. Noise stacked on noise until it became a kind of silence, and Theo walked inside it feeling both terrified and unbearably awake. Every brush of a stranger's shoulder carried an echo. Not enough to take—he kept his hands closed, kept his gift leashed—but enough to feel, the way you feel heat from a fire across a room. Grief here. Longing there. A man passing with forty years of a miller's life sloshing around inside him, and beside him a girl of maybe twelve carrying nothing at all, hollowed clean, her eyes flat as coins.

Sefa followed his gaze and grimaced. "Tithe-takers work these streets. The Regent's people, before she fell out with the merchant guilds and got herself banned. They buy wholesale now instead of taking." She said it lightly. It didn't land lightly. "Come on. You wanted the market. It's under the customs house."

The market proper didn't advertise itself. They descended a stair behind a chandler's shop, past a door guarded by a one-armed woman who took Sefa's coin without looking up, and down again, and down again, until the sounds of the city faded above them like weather. Torches guttered in iron brackets along a vaulted corridor, and then the corridor opened—

—and Theo stopped breathing.

It was a bazaar built inside an old cistern, ringed galleries overlooking a flooded central pool where gondolas drifted between stalls. And every stall sold what he had spent his life believing only he could touch.

Memories. Bottled, vialled, stoppered in wax, strung like beads, laid out in velvet trays like jewelry. A vendor with a forked beard held a green phial to the torchlight: *"Summer storm, first kiss, genuine, uncut."* Another hawked childhoods by the decade. A woman whispered over a tray of griefs arranged darkest to palest, and a rich man in fur turned one over in his fingers the way Theo's customers used to turn over a jar of honey, weighing whether they could afford to feel someone else's loss.

"They're not all stolen," Sefa murmured, reading his face. "Some sell their own. Widows sell the good years to buy bread for the bad ones. Some are cut from the dead—it's legal here, technically, inheritance laws cover it." She paused. "And some are taken. Same as you'd take them. There's a whole guild."

"A guild," Theo repeated. His mouth had gone dry. His whole life he had thought of his gift as an affliction, a secret, a sin committed carefully in the dark. Here it was commerce. Industry. *Currency.*

"Careful," Sefa said quietly, because he had started walking toward the nearest gallery without deciding to.

He wasn't shopping. He told himself that. He was gathering intelligence—the map under his shirt had been cold and quiet since the chapel, but the lantern-vision still haunted him: a woman with his mother's voice making a vow she broke, and beneath the vow, half-buried, a name that might have been his own. If memories were traded in Vell like spices, then somewhere in this drowned market there might be a trace of her. A crumb. A single grain.

He just hadn't expected to find the trail so fast.

* * *

The stall was tucked into the second gallery's shadowed end, apart from the shouting vendors, marked by nothing but a single candle and a hand-lettered sign: *RARE RECOLLECTIONS. DISCREET INQUIRIES.* The keeper was old, thin as a heron, with ink-stained fingers and spectacles thick as bottle-bottoms, and he was sorting phials with the tenderness of a man handling eggs.

"Looking or selling?" he asked without glancing up.

"Looking."

"Everyone says that first." He set down a phial and looked at Theo properly, and something in the old man's face shifted—wariness layered over curiosity layered over, unmistakably, fear. "You've got the look. Hands like yours don't come to browse."

"The look?"

"Thief's hands." The keeper said it flatly, not accusatory. Professional. "Loose wrists. Eyes that weigh people. I dealt in your trade thirty years, boy, before my hands shook too much for the fine work. Sit, if you're sitting. Standing buyers make me nervous."

Theo sat on the offered stool. Behind him he felt Sefa drift to a watching distance, arms crossed, radiating *I will intervene if needed* with the subtlety of a drawn blade.

"What kind of rare?" Theo asked.

The keeper studied him a long moment. Then, slowly, he reached beneath his counter and brought up a ledger bound in grey leather, older than any book Theo had ever seen in Hollowmere, and laid it open.

"I keep a catalogue of provenance," the old man said. "Where each piece came from, who held it, how it changed hands. Collectors care about such things. And some pieces—" he tapped the page, "—some pieces have been coming through my market for years. Same source. Same vintage. Very high quality. And always, always the same subject."

He turned the ledger around.

The entry was written in a careful clerk's hand. *Lot 114. Fragment. Female, approx. 30 years of age at time of capture. Subject designation: LIRIEL DUNMORE.*

The world went very quiet, the way it did underwater.

Theo read the name four times. His lips shaped it without sound. *Dunmore.* His name. His actual, private, never-told-anyone-in-Vell name, sitting in a stranger's ledger in a stranger's handwriting, attached to a woman designated with a word that made his stomach turn: *capture.*

"How," he said, and his voice came out sanded raw. "How long."

"Fifteen years, give or take. Fragments arrive two, three times a year. Always fresh-cut—well, fresh fifteen years ago, but you understand, cut from the living mind, not lifted posthumously. Whoever harvested her was skilled. Surgical. They took slivers, not swaths. Kept her alive and whole enough to harvest again." The keeper removed his spectacles and polished them, not meeting Theo's eyes. "There are forty-one lots in the catalogue. I hold nine. The rest scattered to collectors across the coast. One went east, I heard. One went to a regent's court, though which regent changes depending on who's telling it."

"Who sells them?"

"A courier." The old man said it simply, and the floor tilted beneath Theo's stool. "Silver-eyed fellow, came twice a year like clockwork. Never gave a name. Paid his market fees in old coin. Kept to himself." He paused, and his voice gentled in a way that made Theo want to break something. "Stopped coming about a season back."

*A season.* The dying stranger in his loft. The map pressed into his hands. *Thirteen turns.*

"He's dead," Theo said.

"I'd guessed as much." The keeper's eyes flicked up, sharp. "You knew him."

"I found him dying on my doorstep three valleys east of nowhere. He put a map in my hands and died before I could ask him a single question." Theo leaned forward, and let a little of his gift rise into his voice, the seller's cadence, the market patter he'd used since he was fourteen. "And now I'm going to ask you instead. How much for Lot 114?"

The keeper hesitated. "That lot isn't for general sale. There's interest in it. A buyer in the capital has standing offers on anything Dunmore-related—"

"*How much?*"

"Forty crowns."

Theo had eleven crowns and Sefa's loan of twenty, promised against future payment and grumbled about extensively. He put all thirty-one on the counter, and then, after a breath, added the only other thing of value he carried: a small tin of Hollowmere honey-mint sweets, the kind travelers paid real money for because the recipe existed nowhere else.

"And that," he said. "My mother grew the mint. It seemed right."

Something flickered in the old man's face—pity, maybe, or recognition—and he swept the coins into a drawer and pushed the phial across the counter without another word.

It was smaller than Theo had imagined. A thumbnail of glass, stoppered with black wax, holding a curl of pale-gold light that moved like smoke underwater. Inside it, faintly, he could already hear it: a hum. A voice. Warmth leaking through the wax like sunlight through a curtain.

His hands shook. Forty-one lots. Nine here. Thirty-two scattered across the world, in collectors' cabinets and regents' courts, and somewhere a buyer in the capital hoarding every scrap of a woman named Liriel Dunmore like a dragon on a hoard. Fifteen years of fragments, cut from a living mind by someone surgical and patient, ferried twice a year by a silver-eyed courier who had died pressing a map into her son's hands.

None of it made sense. All of it fit together perfectly. That was the worst part.

"You can play it here, if you like," the keeper said quietly. "Most do. First ones are hard alone."

Theo almost said yes. Then he looked at the crowded gallery—at the tithe-takers' old colleagues, at the guild eyes, at the hundred strangers who would see a thief weep over a vial and remember his face forever.

"No," he said, and pocketed the phial like a coal. "Somewhere private."

* * *

Sefa found them a room above a chandlery near the harbor wall, paying double for discretion and triple for a door that locked. The room was small and smelled of tallow and rope, and Theo sat on the bed with the phial in his cupped hands for a long time before he worked the wax loose.

"You don't have to do this tonight," Sefa said from the doorway. She'd said little since the market; she'd gone pale at the ledger, paler than at any ambush. "Sleep on it. Things read different at dawn."

"If I sleep on it, I'll dream about it. I'd rather have the truth than the dream." He laughed, short and unsteady. "That's a joke, sort of. Truth is literally what I'm holding."

She crossed the room and sat beside him on the narrow bed, close enough that their shoulders touched, and said nothing, and stayed. That was Sefa's way of saying things. He was grateful.

He pulled the stopper.

The memory rose out of the vial like breath in winter, gold and slow, and settled over him not as a vision but as a *wearing*—he was suddenly behind someone else's eyes, in someone else's body, in a kitchen with copper pots and a window full of rain.

A woman stood at the stove with her back half-turned. Dark hair pinned up carelessly, escaping in curls at her neck. Sleeves rolled. Flour on her forearms. She was singing something low and tuneless under her breath, and the body Theo wore—small hands, a child's hands, flour-dusted to match hers—knew this kitchen, knew the exact loose third step of the floorboard, knew that if he ran at her now she'd catch him without turning around, because she always caught him, because she had eyes in the back of her heart.

So he ran.

And she caught him—of course she caught him, laughing, swinging him up onto her hip with the practiced grace of ten thousand repetitions, and she turned her face to his and Theo saw her fully for the first time in his entire remembered life and the sight of her went through him like a spear.

He had no face of his own to compare her to. He had never had one. But he knew her instantly, completely, the way you know your own heartbeat: the crooked smile, the scar through one eyebrow, the deep-set eyes that were gray-green and laughing and suddenly, abruptly serious as she looked at her child and asked:

*"What's my clever boy's name?"*

And the child answered, delighted, showing off, and the answer rose in Theo's chest from thirty years away and a stolen throat:

*"Theo. Theo Dunmore."*

"*Theo,*" she repeated—and oh, the way she said it, the way she *spent* it, like the word was made of honey and held all of him inside it. "*Theo Dunmore. My whole heart, in three syllables.*" She set him down and tapped his nose with a floured finger. "Now wash your hands, love. We're making bread for the festival, and if you eat all the currants again, Maren will tell everyone I raised a thief."

*Maren.* The name struck like a bell. Old Maren, oldest resident of Hollowmere, who had told him fragments of exiles and wars and hiding places. Maren, who knew his mother's name was in that memory. Maren, who had never once said so.

The memory folded gently, the way a hand closes, and was gone. The vial lay empty and dark in Theo's palms. He was crying without any memory of starting.

"She knew Maren," he managed. "She was *in* Hollowmere. Making bread for a festival. She—" His voice cracked. He tried again. "Fifteen years of fragments, cut from a living mind, and the earliest ones start in a valley that doesn't exist on maps, in a town founded by exiles fleeing the Court. Sefa. Someone knew exactly where she was. Someone harvested her in her own kitchen, in the safest place in the world, slowly, for years, and shipped the pieces here through one courier." He looked up, and his tears had gone cold and hard on the way down. "And that same courier handed me a map and died telling me to save the world. What are the odds those are two different stories?"

"Low," Sefa said quietly. She was staring at the empty vial with an expression Theo couldn't read—horror, yes, but under it something older and more personal, like a debt being called. "Theo. The buyer in the capital. With standing offers on Dunmore material. There aren't many people with coin like that and patience like that."

"Say it."

"The Ashen Regent collects." Sefa's jaw tightened. "Before her coup, she spent decades buying provenance—lineages, bloodlines, histories of the old seats. Everyone assumed it was scholarship." She met his eyes. "Your mother's name. Liriel. Say it around any of the neutral regents and watch their faces. If I'm right—if the Regent has been collecting your mother for fifteen years—then she hasn't just been building a hoard. She's been keeping a leash on something. Or waiting for something to grow up."

The phial felt suddenly heavy as an anvil. Outside the window, Vell glittered and roared, oblivious. Somewhere in that glittering sprawl—or in the capital beyond it, or in an ash-grey citadel—someone had spent fifteen years and a fortune acquiring the scattered pieces of a baker-woman's mind, and had sent a silver-eyed courier to find her son the moment the moons began their final turn.

Theo looked down at his empty hands, where his mother's voice still echoed, warm as bread: *my whole heart, in three syllables.*

"Maren lied to me," he said softly. "My whole life, she lied to me. And tomorrow I'm going to make whoever's been buying my mother tell me why."

Far to the north, unseen, the map beneath his shirt woke from its long cold sleep—and began, for the first time, to burn hot.
