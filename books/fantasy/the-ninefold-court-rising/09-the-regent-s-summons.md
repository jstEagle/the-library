## 9. The Regent's Summons

The Ashen Regent did not sleep in palaces.

This was a lesson she had taught herself over twelve long years, and like most of her lessons, it had been paid for in blood—though not, mercifully, her own. She kept instead a tower at the roof of the world, a needle of grey stone above the cloud line where nothing grew and nothing rotted and nothing *changed*, and from its highest chamber she could watch the sun come up over nine horizons at once, if she cared to turn in a circle. She rarely cared to. Sunrises were for people who still believed the world would be there tomorrow.

She was awake before dawn, as always, standing at the window with her hands folded behind her back, watching the last stars burn down over the Ashen Reach—the lands that had once been the Ninth Seat's demesne, before the ninth seat had become the only seat that mattered.

Her name had been Vessa Corvane once. The Court had called her the Regent of Thresholds, keeper of doors and crossings and all the small permissions by which one life touched another. It had been, she thought now, an almost perfect irony: the seat of doorways, given to the woman who would one day lock every door in the nine realms and swallow the key.

"Mother."

The voice came from behind her, soft as ash falling. She did not turn. Only three people in the world were permitted to use that word in her presence, and two of them were dead by their own choices, which left Wren, her warden of summons, standing in the doorway with a lacquered tray and the particular stillness of someone carrying bad news they hoped would evaporate if held quietly enough.

"You may speak," Vessa said.

"The courier reached Hollowmere." A pause, measured to the half-heartbeat. "He did not survive his wounds. But he delivered."

Vessa watched a star go out above the eastern ridge, extinguished not by dawn but by the slow grey creep of her own reach, the boundary of quiet she had spent a decade drawing around that miserable valley. "He delivered," she repeated. "To whom?"

"We don't know. The town has no memory of him arriving. That's the trouble with Hollowmere, my lady—it eats our eyes along with everyone else's." Wren set the tray on the table without being asked. On it lay a single object: a scrap of grey cloth, torn from a hunter's cloak, stained dark at one corner. "Third patrol we've lost at that border in two years. They walk in remembering everything and walk out as strangers to themselves. Whatever wards the founders raised there, they don't just hide the valley. They *unmake* what enters it. Slowly."

"And yet a dying man crossed it."

"Yes."

"And pressed something into someone's hands."

Wren's silence was answer enough. Vessa finally turned from the window. In the candlelight her face was famous across six realms—the face on the tithe-notices, the face children were taught to fear and adults pretended not to remember—and it was a handsome face, deliberately so, carved by years of discipline into an expression of patient, reasonable sorrow. Grief, worn well, was more frightening than rage. She had learned that from the Court itself.

"Show me what the patrols recovered from the last one," she said.

Wren hesitated—a flicker, quickly mastered, but Vessa saw it the way she saw everything, because seeing everything had been her office and her curse since the day she took her Seat. He drew a second item from his cloak: a hunter's memory, decanted into a vial of smoked glass, its contents swirling a sullen silver.

"He was found at the border-stone," Wren said. "Mind intact. Mostly. We couldn't question him—he'd wiped himself rather than be taken back for questioning. Left this behind."

"A suicide note in liquid form."

"Of a kind."

Vessa took the vial. This, too, was part of her creed, though she doubted any of her hunters understood it: she never asked her people to carry a memory she would not carry herself. She unstopped the glass and let the contents pour into her palm, cold as meltwater, and closed her hand, and *drank*.

* * *

A border-stone in the rain. Grey grass, grey sky, the valley below wrapped in its perpetual hush like a sleeping thing under a blanket. The hunter—young, ambitious, terrified in the way young ambitious men are terrified, which is to say only of failure—stood at the marker with his orders in his head and the taste of the crossing already souring his thoughts.

*Find the courier. Recover the map. Burn the body if burned it must be, but the map comes home.*

And beneath the orders, the thing the hunter had been told and had not believed until now: *the valley forgets you as you forget it.* He had been inside for eleven days. He knew this because his journal said so. He did not know why he had come. He read his own handwriting—*find the courier, recover the map*—and felt the words slide off his mind like rain off slate. Somewhere in the fog of him was a name, his own name, and it would not come when called.

So he sat down at the border-stone, in the rain, and did the only disciplined thing left to him. He took out the extraction kit. He drew out the eleven days entire—all of it, the fog and the orders and the shame—and sealed them in smoked glass, because the Regent must have them, because the Regent must understand that Hollowmere was not hiding a courier, Hollowmere was hiding *itself*, and no army she sent would ever come home whole.

And then, with the last of his trained mind, he thought—not in panic but in a strange, clear professional calm: *she will want to know what I felt at the end.*

What he felt at the end was relief.

That was what Vessa carried out of the memory, standing in her high chamber with her hand slowly closing on nothing: the taste of a young man's relief as his self drained away into glass. Not horror. Relief. As if forgetting were a warm bath, as if the mind were a knot finally allowed to loosen.

She opened her eyes. Wren had stepped back; he always did, when she drank. Superstition. Half her court believed the Regent ate memories and grew fat on them. Let them believe it. Fear kept better order than love ever had—she had that from the old covenant too, though the old covenant had never admitted it.

"Eleven days," she said. "Inside the ward-line, a hunter retains nothing. Outside it, he keeps himself. Which means the valley's silence isn't passive anymore." She turned the empty vial in her fingers. "It's *hungry*. The founder-exiles built it as a blindfold and it has become a mouth. And a dying man ran straight through its teeth carrying—" she stopped.

Carrying the map. Carrying the one object in the nine realms that could undo everything she had built, everything she had suffered, everything she had *become*. Twelve years since the Breaking Night. Twelve years since she had stood in the Court hall with blood on her hands and three Seats vacant and the remaining regents scattered like startled birds, and told herself the lie she had long since refined into truth: *this is mercy. This is surgery. Someone had to cut the covenant out of the world before it killed us all.*

"My lady," Wren said carefully, "the patrols are asking for new orders. Whether to press the search into the valley itself. Some of them are saying—" he chose the word with visible care— "that no one comes back from Hollowmere. That it's cursed."

"It is not cursed. It is *protected*, which is worse." Vessa moved to the great table at the center of the room, where a map of the nine realms lay pinned beneath weights of black iron—nine realms, and on the table's edge, nine empty brackets of tarnished silver where the regalia markers used to sit. Six of those brackets she had filled with captured relics; the lantern of oaths had been reported sighted near a drowned chapel in the Marches, the ring of the Second Seat had gone silent in the mountains, and now this. A courier. A map. A thief in a forgotten valley who had no idea what he was holding.

Or—her mind turned the thought over, examining its underside, as she examined everything—an idea *exactly* what he was holding, and exactly what it cost.

"Recall the patrols," she said. "All of them. Every hunter within three hundred leagues of Hollowmere."

Wren blinked. "My lady?"

"I do not send more men into that valley to be eaten. I've fed enough of the faithful to its silence to prove what I already knew." She laid one finger on the map, on the small grey smudge where Hollowmere sat like a bruise. "You cannot take a fortress by assault when the fortress unmakes attackers. You take it by *summons*. You make the enemy come to you."

"He won't come," Wren said. "Whoever holds the map will run. They always run."

"They run toward the regalia. That's the nature of the thing—the map is a leash and the regalia are the collar, and the covenant made sure of it when the Nine bound themselves. Whoever gathers the pieces must gather them *in order*, at the sites where the Seats fell. Which means I know where he's going before he does." Her finger traced the route east, through the Marches, toward the drowned chapel. "Post watchers at every site. Do not engage. Do not reveal. I want to know what he looks like, who travels with him, and whether he flinches when he touches the relics. Then, when he has gathered enough to make the journey worthwhile—when the leash has played out to its full length—"

She lifted her gaze from the map, and Wren, veteran of a dozen erasure campaigns, took an involuntary step backward.

"—then you will send him my invitation."

* * *

Afterward, alone, Vessa did what she did every morning at first light. She went down the spiral stair to the vault beneath the tower—not the relic vaults; those were elsewhere, guarded by her wardens and her warden's sister and walls of poured silence—but the other vault, the one that existed on no plan and in no record. Her private archive.

One shelf. One bottle upon it, older than all the others, its glass cloudy with age.

The covenant's defenders had called the Court's law *justice*: nine seats, nine regalia, nine regents binding the realms together in one great web of obligation. Every crossing taxed. Every threshold witnessed. Every birth, death, bargain, and border registered in the great ledger of the Seats, so that no deed anywhere went unwitnessed, ungoverned, unforgiven. They had dressed it in ceremony and called it peace.

She had sat the Ninth Seat for thirty years. She had read the ledger entire.

She had seen the farmer fined a year of his remembered harvest because he moved his fence-stone without filing notice. She had seen lovers severed by decree because their match crossed two jurisdictions. She had seen a village erased—not by her erasure-hunters, but by the Court's own tidy justice—for the crime of harboring a renegade regent, its name struck from every record, its people scattered with their pasts confiscated as surety. She had seen all of it, and she had signed her assent at the bottom of each page, because that was the Seat, because refusal was abdication, because the web did not care what it caught so long as it caught *everything*.

Slavery dressed as justice. That was the phrase she had used on the Breaking Night, standing over the bodies—or what remained of bodies—of the three regents who refused to yield. She had said it then as accusation. She said it now as scripture.

The bottle on the shelf held a memory: her own, drawn out and kept apart, the way a physician keeps a sample of the disease. Inside it was the sound of the Court hall in session, the great wheel of the covenant turning, and her own younger voice rising in objection—*this is not justice, this cannot be justice*—and the eight other voices answering her, gentle and terrible in their unanimity: *it is the price of order. There is no other way. There has never been another way.*

Twelve years ago she had decided they were wrong. There was another way. It required fire.

Now, standing before the bottle, Vessa performed the ritual she performed every dawn, the one no warden saw: she recited her ledger of costs. The three regents dead by her hand. The dissenters unmade by her hunters—she did not call it killing; killing left corpses, and hers left only absences, gaps in the world shaped like people, and somehow that was worse and she knew it was worse and she did it anyway. The villages emptied. The tithes of memory levied across six realms, the confiscated pasts stacked in her citadel like grain against a famine that would never come.

Every empire keeps such a ledger. Most emperors burn theirs. She kept hers because she believed a cure should know exactly how much poison it contained.

*When it is finished,* she reminded herself, as she reminded herself each morning, *there will be no more ledgers. No tithes. No thresholds. No web. A world where no one owns your past but you. Where memory is not currency, not tax, not chain. They will curse me for a generation. Then they will live free for all the rest, and never know whose hand broke their shackles.*

It was a good creed. It was, in fact, the only creed she had ever found that survived contact with her own conscience—which was why she had to keep the conscience so carefully rationed, like the last lamp-oil in a siege.

The flaw, of course, was the map.

The covenant had been cruel, but it had been thorough, and thoroughness meant redundancy: a reforging rite, a thirteenth moon, a heart of the Court that beat dormant beneath the ruins waiting for someone foolish enough to wake it. She had spent twelve years making certain the regalia could never be gathered. Six sat in her vaults. Two more were hunted. And the map that revealed them all—the map she had believed destroyed with the courier network, with the loyalists, with the whole underground lattice of remembrance that had taken her a decade to strangle—was now in the hands of some nameless creature in a hole in the world that even her power could not enter.

A valley that unmakes memory. And a thief, according to the last reports out of that region, who *traded in stolen recollections*. Who hoarded minds the way misers hoard coin.

Vessa stood a long moment in the dark of her private vault, and something moved across her face that was not quite a smile and not quite grief.

"So," she said softly, to the bottle, to the dead, to the boy she had been on the Ninth Seat. "The world sends me a memory-thief to undo the woman who outlawed memory. Of course it does. Of course."

She climbed back up into the grey light, and gave the order that would set the next moon turning.

"Wake the warden of the citadel. Double the vault-guard. And send riders to every site on the old circuit—I want eyes on the drowned chapel within the week." At the door, she paused, and added, in the tone she reserved for the few orders that truly mattered:

"And find out everything that breathes about whoever took up that map. Name, face, family, fears. When I summon him, I intend to offer him precisely what he wants most." Her reflection looked back at her from the dark window, faint and patient and ash-grey. "Everyone wants something. The covenant taught me that much, before I taught the covenant anything."

Far to the east, past the Marches, past the drowned chapel where a lantern full of broken promises waited in the flooded dark, a thief who did not yet know he was being summoned was learning what the first regalia sounded like when it sang.

The thirteenth moon had begun to wax.
