## 10. Ash on the Road

Sefa Vane had fought in the Court's vanguard for eleven years, and in all that time she had learned one truth above every drill and doctrine: ambushes were not surprises. They were appointments. Somewhere, days or weeks before the blades came out, someone had made a decision, marked a road, set a price. The violence was only the invoice arriving.

So when the third crow crossed their path in an hour—each one flying against the wind, each one silent—she did not need omen-craft to read it.

"Off the road," she said quietly.

Theo looked up from the map he was pretending was a scrap of linen. "We're two miles from the ferry."

"And they'll expect us at the ferry." She tilted her chin toward the treeline on the eastern slope. "There's a drover's path through the pines. Ugly walking. We take it anyway."

He didn't argue. That was one of the boy's better qualities, she'd decided somewhere on the fourth day out of Hollowmere—he argued like a merchant, haggling for what he wanted, but when her voice took a certain edge, he obeyed it without pride getting in the way. She had known knights with twenty years' service who couldn't manage that.

They cut upslope through wet bracken. The morning fog still clung to the hollows, and Sefa kept them along its upper seam where the ground showed pale and the trees stood in grey ranks. The map rode inside Theo's coat, and she could feel it from three paces away—a cold spot in the world, like standing near a cellar door in summer. The first regalia, the lantern, hung wrapped in oilcloth from his belt, and even swaddled it hummed faintly with all the promises it had swallowed. Oaths never fulfilled. She tried not to look at it. Looking at it made her think of things she preferred not to think of.

The crows stopped following at the ridge line. That was the worst sign of all. Crows that stop are crows that have reported.

"They know," Theo said. Not a question.

"They've known since the bridge at Marrowford, I'd guess. Maybe since Vell." She checked the angle of the sun, the lie of the land. "Question is whether they're behind us or ahead of us, and how many."

"How many would there be?"

"A retrieval detail? Four. Six if they think you're dangerous." She glanced at him—at the thin shoulders, the market-thief hands, the eyes that had gone strange and old since the lantern touched him. "If they think you're *important*, eight, with a reader."

"A reader?"

"You'll know if you meet one. Everyone does, once."

They came down through the pines toward the river road again because the drover's path dead-ended at a washout, and Sefa hated being funneled more than she hated being exposed. The road here ran between the river and a steep bank of gorse and broken stone. Good ground for her. Bad ground for anyone who wanted to take something without breaking it.

She was halfway through explaining this to Theo when the world went quiet.

Not silent—the river still roared, a cart could be heard groaning somewhere far off—but *quiet* the way a room goes quiet when a door opens. The birdsong cut off in a clean line, as if scissored. The insects stopped. Even the water seemed to flatten.

Sefa had heard that silence exactly once before, eleven years ago, in the throne hall of the Ninefold Court, on the night everything ended.

"*Down*," she said, and shoved Theo into the gorse, and drew steel.

They came out of the stones like ash off a fire. Four of them in grey, moving with the terrible unhurried economy of people who have never once needed to hurry. Their faces were smooth—not blank, nothing so honest as blankness, but *smoothed*, features softened as though someone had pressed thumbs into wax. Ash-grey sigils glinted at their collars: nine bars, eight struck through.

Erasure-hunters. The Regent's own.

"Hold," said the one in front, and its voice was pleasant, reasonable, the voice of a clerk reading terms. "Theo Dunmore of Hollowmere. You carry property of the Court. Come freely and no harm will come to your companion."

*Your companion.* They hadn't named her. That meant either they didn't know her—which was possible; she'd been nobody for eleven years—or they knew her very well indeed, and were saving the name for leverage.

Neither possibility improved her mood.

"Sefa," Theo breathed beside her. His hand had found hers in the gorse, grip slick with sweat. "What do they do, if we don't?"

"You've seen it," she said. "In your town. You told me."

The neighbor. He'd told her about it on the second night, in a flat voice that shook only at the edges: the hunters standing in a kitchen, the neighbor smiling afterward, empty as a scraped bowl, thanking them for visiting.

"I'll handle this," Sefa said, and stepped out onto the road.

She put herself between them and Theo and let eleven years of muscle memory arrange her body: weight low, blade angled to invite rather than threaten, left foot back. The hunters watched her with those smoothed faces and made no move to draw. Why would they? They didn't fight. Fighting left bruises, and bruises were evidence, and evidence was memory, and memory was the whole of their trade.

"The last man who pointed steel at me," she said loudly, "is buried under a cairn outside Vell. I built the cairn myself. It was a long afternoon and I resented every stone. Walk away."

"Serah Vane." The lead hunter said her name gently, the way one reminds a child of a chore. "The Court remembers you."

And there it was.

Her blood went cold and then hot, in that order, fast. Eleven years of being nobody, of guarding grain caravans and merchant sons, of drinking alone in caravan camps and never giving a surname—and one word from a grey mouth undid all of it.

"I don't know what you mean," she said.

"You deserted your post at the Seating of the Ninth," the hunter continued, mild as milk. "You were sworn to the Third Seat, Serah Vane, Knight-Errant of the Inner Ring, and when the Ashen Regent rose you turned your back on your oath and walked out through the servants' stair. There is a warrant. There has always been a warrant. The Regent is merciful. She does not wish to erase you. She wishes to *retrieve* you."

Behind her, Theo made a small sound.

Sefa did not turn around. Turning around would have been worse than anything the hunters could do. She kept her eyes on the smoothed face and her blade steady and her voice level, and inside she was falling down a stair that had no bottom.

*Eleven years,* she thought. *Eleven years of carrying it, and now it walks up a road and speaks.*

"We're taking him," the hunter said. "You may go. Your desertion will be forgiven. All records restored. You could kneel in the Inner Ring again by winter. Isn't that what you've wanted? To have it un-done?"

And gods help her, some rotten corner of her heart leaned toward it like a plant toward light. *Forgiven. Restored.* To stand in the white hall again with her oath whole and her name clean and the whole shaming night of it erased from the record—

That was the trick of the erasure-hunters. Everyone thought they stole memories. They didn't, mostly. Mostly they *sold* them. A future without consequence. A past without shame. Everyone had a room in themselves they'd pay anything to have emptied.

Safa had spent eleven years trying to empty that room by hand, one drink at a time, and here were professionals offering the service free.

"No," she said.

"Serah—"

"I said no." Her voice cracked on it, which she hated. "You want the boy, you come through me, and I'm not the woman who ran down a servants' stair. I've been paying for her ever since, and the payment's made me someone else. Someone you should worry about."

The four hunters looked at one another. Something passed between them—she saw it in the slight tilt of heads, the way a flock turns—and then the leader sighed, genuinely regretful, and raised one grey-gloved hand with two fingers extended, and spoke a word that had no vowels.

The air split.

It came as a ribbon of pale light, thin as a paper knife, and it did not fly at Sefa at all. It flew past her shoulder, straight and patient, at the boy crouched in the gorse.

Later—much later—she would understand why she moved. Not tactics. Not training. Some arithmetic the heart does faster than the head: *not him, not this one, not another thing I fail to protect.* She threw herself sideways into the ribbon's path with her sword arm outstretched as if steel could argue with light.

Steel could not argue with light.

The ribbon touched her forearm above the bracer and slid *in*, the way cold slides in, the way water finds a crack, and Sefa Vane felt eleven years begin to pour out of her.

* * *

Pain would have been kinder. What came instead was absence—a cool, surgical unmaking, starting at the newest memories and working backward like frost creeping across a window. She felt it take the road behind them, the bridge at Marrowford, the drowned chapel and the lantern's humming weight. Felt it close around the night in Vell when the seller had whispered about a woman's memories. Felt it reach for—

No.

She knew what was coming. She could feel the shape of the thing it hunted: a particular memory, load-bearing, the keystone of her entire ruined self. The servants' stair. The torchlight on the steps. The sound the Court made when it broke—nine bells and one of them cracking.

She dug her heels into it the way you dig heels into a door in a flood.

*I deserted. I ran. I left them—*

But the flood was stronger than her heels, and the horrible thing, the thing she would never be able to explain afterward, was that losing it felt like relief. Like setting down a pack carried so long the straps had grown into the flesh. The guilt lifted. The shame lifted. The reason she drank alone in caravan camps lifted, and underneath it was—nothing. Clean, white, merciful nothing.

Somewhere very far away, a boy was screaming her name.

* * *

Theo had never stolen from anyone who didn't have it coming.

That was his rule, the rule he'd built his whole gray little life on: travelers passing through, memories they'd forget they missed, sold back to the grieving at a markup. He'd told himself a hundred stories about the fairness of it. He was telling himself none of them now.

Because Sefa had taken the wipe meant for him—had thrown her body into a ribbon of pale light like a woman stepping in front of a carriage—and now she knelt on the road with her sword fallen from her hand and her face going smooth, going *soft*, going the way the neighbor's face had gone in that kitchen in Hollowmere, and Theo Dunmore discovered that he was furious.

Furious in a way he had never been in his life. Furious past fear.

The lead hunter was already turning back to him, two fingers rising again, that vowelless word gathering behind its teeth.

It never got the word out.

Theo had spent his life learning the geography of other people's heads—the way you learn the alleys of a town you rob. He knew where orders lived. Orders weren't deep; they sat right at the surface, bright and brittle, the first thing in any mind because they were the last thing put there. He didn't need to touch the hunters. He didn't need to be close. He needed only the moment when the leader *reached for the order again*—needed the attention to snag on it, the way a pickpocket needs his mark to pat the purse.

The leader reached for its next command, and Theo followed the reach home.

It was like slipping through a door left open. For half a heartbeat he stood inside a smoothed mind, and it was worse than he'd imagined—not evil, just *tidied*, rooms swept bare and labeled, a house where someone had burned all the furniture and called it peace. And pinned to the front wall, glowing, urgent:

*Retrieve the courier-bearer alive. The map must not be damaged. Deliver to the Citadel by the waning of the twelfth moon. Secondary directive: the deserter Vane is to be recovered intact—she knows the location of the Sixth Seat's refuge. Tertiary: if resistance, partial erasure authorized. Full erasure requires a Reader. Reader Corvess is two days behind you.*

Two days. A Reader was coming.

Theo ripped himself free, gasping, nose bleeding freely down his lip, and the world snapped back—the river, the gorse, the four grey shapes staggering as their minds hiccupped around a theft they had no practice feeling. Stealing from erasure-hunters was like stealing from blind men; they had surrendered the very sense that would have caught him.

The lead hunter raised its fingers again, and Theo did the only thing left.

He shouted the orders back at them.

"The map must not be damaged!" His voice came out cracked and doubled, still wearing the cadence of the mind he'd robbed. "Deliver by the waning of the twelfth moon! Vane is to be recovered *intact*—full erasure requires a Reader, and your Reader is two days behind you, so unless you want to explain to the Citadel why you burned their best witness—" he wiped the blood from his lip and grinned, and it wasn't a nice grin, "—you can't touch either of us. Can you?"

Silence. Real silence, this time, the tactical kind.

The hunters looked at one another. Theo watched the arithmetic happen behind those smoothed faces: orders conflicting, directives jamming against one another like gears packed with sand. Recover Vane intact. Partial erasure only. No Reader for two days. And the bearer somehow knowing things he should not know, which meant he might already have done to them what they did to everyone else.

They were, he realized with a lurch, *afraid*. These creatures who unmade people for a living were afraid of him, and the fear was the most human thing on the whole road.

"Fall back," the lead hunter said at last, and its pleasant voice had developed a hairline fracture. "To the crossroads. Wait for Corvess."

They withdrew up the bank the way they'd come, dissolving into the grey stone, and the birdsong resumed all at once, absurd and ordinary, as though the world had merely cleared its throat.

Theo got to Sefa.

* * *

She was sitting where she'd fallen, sword in the dirt beside her, staring at her own hands.

"Sefa." He crouched, took her wrist—her pulse was galloping. "Look at me. Talk to me."

"I was going to say something clever," she said slowly. "About the cairn outside Vell. About burying them." Her brow furrowed, a small, lost motion. "Wasn't I? I remember wanting to say it. I remember..." She trailed off. Started again. "Theo. Why am I traveling with you?"

His stomach dropped through the road, through the riverbed, into the dark.

"What do you remember?"

"I remember Vell. The market of memories—I remember buying... no. You bought something. Someone's voice." She frowned harder, groping. "I remember the chapel. The lantern. I remember the map burning cold and you crying about it, and me saying something hard to you because that's—" her jaw worked, "—that's what I do, apparently. But *why*, Theo? Why am I here? A caravan guard doesn't walk a thief across the realms out of charity. There was a reason. There was something I was—" She pressed both fists against her temples. "*There was a debt.* I keep reaching for it and it's smoke. Why does the word 'desertion' make my hands shake when I don't know what it means?"

Theo knelt in the road with blood drying on his lip and understood exactly what had been taken, because he had read the orders himself: *the deserter Vane.* They hadn't wiped her whole past. They'd taken the wound precisely—one memory, the keystone, the night on the servants' stair and everything it meant. They had left her the shape of the hole, so she'd spend her life falling into it.

He could fix it. Probably. He'd pulled memories out of travelers for years; he could find the ribbon's trail, follow it back to wherever the hunters stored what they took—

Except the orders said *Corvess is two days behind*, and Corvess would be carrying what they took, and following that road led to a fight he couldn't win yet.

Or—

"There's a hospice," he heard himself say. "In the mountains east of here. An old regent. Senile, the villagers say—someone stole her decades." He met Sefa's eyes, and saw a stranger in them, a capable, wary stranger who had every reason to leave him on this road and no debt binding her to stay. "I think what they did to you, someone did to her. Worse. I think there's a ring there that answers to witnessed truth. And I think—" his voice steadied, surprised by its own conviction, "—I think if we get there, I can give you back what they took. Both of you. That's the deal. That's the new debt. You guard me to the hospice, and I steal your night back from the people who sold it."

Sefa looked at him for a long moment. Wind moved the gorse. Far off, the crows had begun again, ordinary crows, cawing about nothing.

"You stole from them," she said finally. "Their orders. I saw your face change." Something kindled in the stranger's eyes, slow and assessing and—there, faintly, familiar. Respect. "You could have run. The moment they turned to me, you could have run."

"I considered it."

"You didn't."

"No."

She picked up her sword. She sheathed it. Then she held out her forearm—the place where the ribbon had entered, unmarked, unnaturally smooth-skinned, like wax pressed flat—and said, "Then we move. Your Reader is two days out, and I find I don't much like the idea of meeting anyone whose job title is *Reader*."

As they turned east, away from the river and the ferry they'd never reach, Theo walked with the lantern knocking softly against his hip and the map cold against his chest, and he caught himself thinking a thought that would have been unthinkable a month ago, back in the market stalls of Hollowmere where every theft had a price tag:

*What else can this power do, if I point it at the people who deserve it?*

Behind them, at the crossroads, unseen, a fifth figure in ash-grey stepped out of the treeline—too late for the fight, early for the schedule—and turned south, toward the mountains, carrying word of a thief who could reach into the minds of the Regent's own.

Reader Corvess smiled without warmth, and began to walk faster.
