## 11. The Second Seat Remembers

The mountain had a name once. The map told us that much—*Vael's Shoulder*, inked in the courier's cramped hand—but the name meant nothing to anyone we asked on the road up, and by the third switchback I stopped asking. The hospice sat where the treeline gave up: a long, low building of grey stone with blue shutters gone silver with weather, smoke rising thin and patient from its chimneys like the mountain itself was breathing.

"They send people here to be forgotten," Sefa said.

She hadn't spoken in an hour. She'd barely spoken since Vell, since the memory-seller's stall and the voice that named me from a bottle of someone else's past. She walked with her hand resting on the pommel of her sword out of habit rather than intent; she'd been doing it less since Ash on the Road, since I pulled the hunters' orders out of their heads instead of leaving them hollow. I think she was still deciding what that made me.

"Forgotten," I repeated. "Or kept somewhere forgetting can't reach anyone else."

"Is there a difference?"

I looked at the shutters, the smoke, the narrow windows glowing amber against the dusk coming down off the icefields. "My whole valley is a place for keeping things away from the world," I said. "The people inside it don't call that cruelty."

Sefa grunted—the sound she made when she thought I was being naive but couldn't find the argument yet—and pushed open the gate.

* * *

The inside smelled of lavender and lamp oil and something older underneath, something mineral and cold, like water running through stone. A woman met us in the entry hall: broad-shouldered, white-haired, wearing an apron stained with tallow and broth. She looked at Sefa's sword first, then at my face, and whatever she saw there made her sigh.

"Pilgrims," she said, not as a question. "We get pilgrims. They come up the mountain hoping she'll know them." Her eyes were kind and very tired. "You'll want to see her anyway. Everyone does. Come. But I'll tell you now what I tell them all—she won't know you. Some days she doesn't know herself."

"Who do they hope she is?" Sefa asked.

The woman paused with her hand on the stair rail. "They hope she's the Lady of the Second Seat," she said quietly. "And some days, near dawn, I almost believe she remembers being one."

* * *

Her room was at the top of the house, under the eaves, with a window that framed nothing but sky and the far ridge of the shoulder. She was small in the bed, small the way old trees are small after lightning takes their crown—still enormous, somehow, in what remained. Her hair was braided in nine thin plaits gone white as frost, and her hands lay folded on the coverlet, and around the fourth finger of her right hand was a band of plain iron so old its edges had worn soft.

She was talking when we came in. Talking steadily, to no one.

"—and the toll was three questions, always three, and he paid them wrong every time because he never learned to listen, and I told him, I said, Wren, the bridge will have your boots if you keep—" She stopped. Turned her head. Her eyes found us, pale grey, unfocused, then focusing with a snap that made the hairs on my arms rise.

"You're carrying something," she said.

"Lady—" the nurse began.

"No, no, hush, I'm not confused, look at him, look—" The old woman's hand rose, trembling, pointing at my chest. "There. Under his ribs. Something that isn't his. Or something that is his but wasn't, once, or—" Her face crumpled with frustration, and for a moment she was only an old woman losing her words, and it was unbearable to watch. Then she rallied, fierce. "What did you bring me?"

"We brought nothing," Sefa said carefully. "We're looking for the ring."

"The ring." The old woman laughed—a dry, rusted sound. "Everyone's looking for the ring. Half the mountain's dug itself silly over sixty years looking for the ring. It's on my finger, fools. It's been on my finger the whole time, and it's worth nothing, because it won't wake for me anymore. Won't wake for blood, won't wake for tears. I've bled on it and wept on it and buried it and dug it up and it just lies there like any other iron." Her voice cracked. "Like me."

The nurse touched my arm. "She has days like this. Perhaps you should—"

"What wakes it?" I asked.

The old woman went very still. When she spoke again her voice had dropped to almost nothing, and every word cost her something, and she spent each one anyway.

"Witnessed truth," she said. "That's what the ring eats. Not blood. Truth—spoken true, seen true, held true in front of it. That was the Second Seat's charge, in the old days. We were the record-keepers. We were the ones who watched. Nine regents, nine charges, and mine was to witness what was done, so that no throne could ever claim it hadn't happened." Her fingers curled around the iron band. "And now I can't hold onto my own mornings, and the ring sleeps, and everything I witnessed is going into the dark with me."

The room was quiet except for the wind testing the shutters.

"I can help," I said. "Maybe. I don't know how, exactly. But I've been learning what I am, these last months, and—" I swallowed. "May I take your hand?"

* * *

Here is what I knew about myself by then, though I would not have phrased it this way until later:

I could steal memories. That was the trade I'd learned in Hollowmere, lifting recollections from travelers like coins from a careless purse. But on the road, with Sefa bleeding beside me and erasure-hunters closing in, I'd learned the other half of the craft—that what could be taken could also be given back. That theft and restoration were the same door, opened from different sides.

The old woman's mind was a burned library. That was my first impression, sinking into the shallows of her: shelves upon shelves, and most of them charred, empty, the spines crumbled to ash at the touch. Sixty years of senility, or something worse than senility—something that ate outward from certain dates, from certain rooms, gnawing the middle of her life into tunnels and voids. And in the deepest shelf, behind everything, sealed and dark, a presence that was not hers. Cold. Patient. Shaped like a lock.

But the ash wasn't all ash. Underneath, pressed flat the way flowers are pressed in books, things survived. Fragments. Whole hours, even—sealed off from the rest, unreachable by her, but intact. I understood then what had happened, or began to: her mind hadn't destroyed those years. Something had *hidden* them from her. Locked them behind that cold shape, and let time and confusion bury the vault.

I didn't force anything. I did what felt right, which was the thing I'd been made—or cursed—to do. I gathered the fragments the way I'd gather spilled seed, gently, both hands, and carried them back to the light, and gave them home.

When I opened my eyes, the old woman was sitting bolt upright.

* * *

"Sixty years," she said. Her voice had changed. There was weight in it now, ballast, a keel. "Sixty years I have been a stranger in my own skull, and you—you're not even trained. Who taught you to do that?"

"No one," I said. My hands were shaking. Returning stolen goods, it turned out, cost more than stealing them; I felt scoured, wrung out, like I'd run a mile at altitude. "It's just—what I am. Apparently."

"She's awake," the nurse whispered from the doorway. She had both hands over her mouth. "Mother Aldith, you're—you said my name. You haven't said my name in four years—"

"Brida. Of course it's Brida. You burn the lamps too low and you cry when you think no one sees, and you've been crying since before I lost myself, girl, because you knew what was coming for me before I did." The old woman—Aldith—swung her legs off the bed. She was steady. Terrifyingly steady, for a person who'd been a husk ten minutes ago. "How long do I have? Be honest. Whatever he did, it's a loan, not a gift."

"I don't know," I admitted.

"Then we spend it well." She stood, wavered, gripped the bedpost, and looked down at the iron ring on her finger. "Well," she said softly, to it, "you waited long enough."

Nothing happened.

Aldith frowned. "Witnessed truth," she murmured. "It needs truth. Spoken true, in front of it. Sixty years ago I would have known the rite." She closed her eyes, and I watched her dig—not vague and drifting now but precise, a scholar retrieving a citation. "Yes. Yes, it was this: the claimant speaks what they know to be true, and the ring weighs whether they *witnessed* it—whether they carry it as fact or only as hope. It cannot be flattered. It cannot be bargained with. It knows the difference between believing and knowing." Her eyes opened, and fixed on me. "Which is why they hid my years, boy. Because I *witnessed* too much."

"Who did?" Sefa asked. Her hand was back on her sword. "The Regent?"

"The Regent." Aldith said the word the way you'd spit out a bad tooth. "Sit down, both of you. If I have an hour of myself, I will not waste it standing."

* * *

She told it walking, eventually—she refused to stay abed, and Brida gave up arguing, and so we moved slowly along the gallery under the eaves while the last light failed outside and the lamps came up one by one.

"You've heard her creed, I imagine. Everyone has. The covenant was slavery dressed as justice, she says. Nine thrones binding the realms with law none of us chose, nine regents lording over the wild places in the name of balance. She calls it the Great Unbinding. Freedom." Aldith's lip curled. "I believed some of it, once. That's the horror of her—that the lie is built on truths. The covenant *was* cruel in places. The Ninth Seat *was* given the dirtiest work and the least thanks. I voted against measures she now cites as her grievances. I remember voting. I remember her face across the council floor." She stopped at a window, and her reflection ghosted faint against the glass and the darkening mountainside beyond. "But listen to me, and listen carefully, because I am the last living witness of the Court's final session, and I have carried this locked in my own head for sixty years without knowing I carried it:

"She does not want freedom. Freedom is the *sales pitch*. What she wants is a single throne."

The words landed in my chest like stones into still water.

"The nine seats exist to check one another. That was the covenant's entire architecture—nine powers, none sufficient alone, each able to veto the worst ambitions of the others. It was clumsy. It was slow. It was often unjust. And it made tyranny structurally impossible." Aldith turned from the window. "Break the Court, and you don't free the realms. You free whoever broke it. Every law the Court enforced dissolves; every ward it maintained fails; every boundary it held goes soft—and into that softness walks the one power left standing. She shattered the covenant to clear the ground. The erasures, the hunters, the unmaking of dissenters—that's not policy. That's foundation-laying. You cannot build one throne on top of nine living memories of another way. So she is deleting the other way, town by town, mind by mind, starting with anyone who might testify otherwise."

"Starting with you," I said.

"I was the Second Seat. Witnessing was my charge. Do you understand? She couldn't kill me—killing a regent leaves a mark the world notices, and besides, in the first chaos after the shattering there were still eyes everywhere. So instead she sent someone into my sleep, and that someone locked my witnessing away behind a seal I could feel but never open, and left me to rot politely in the mountains while the world forgot there had ever been nine." Aldith smiled, and it was the coldest expression I have ever seen on a warm face. "Sixty years. She counted on me dying with it. And then a thief walks up my stairs smelling of stolen light and gives me back my own testimony."

In the lamplight, the iron ring on her finger caught fire.

Not literally—though my heart stuttered as if it had. It *woke*. I felt it the way I'd felt the map against my skin, the way I'd felt the lantern in the drowned chapel: a pressure change, a note struck below hearing. The worn metal brightened. Fine lines surfaced across its band, script so old it predated the script I'd been taught, circling the finger like a sentence that had been waiting sixty years for its verb.

Aldith stared at it. For a moment, the formidable old woman vanished, and what was left was someone holding proof, after sixty years, that she had not dreamed her own life.

"It woke for you," she said to me. Her voice shook. "It shouldn't have. The rite needs witnessed truth spoken before it—and you spoke nothing. Unless—" She looked at me differently then. The way I imagine you'd look at a lock that turns out to be a key. "Unless giving a witness back her testimony counts. Unless the act itself was the truth."

"Does it matter?" Sefa asked. "It's awake. Take it and go."

"No." Aldith closed her fist around the ring, and the light leaked between her fingers. "No, knight. It matters enormously. The regalia aren't tools. They're *offices*. Each one answers to its charge—the lantern to oaths, the ring to truth. It woke because its charge was honored, not because its bearer was strong." She looked at me. "Do you know what that means? It means the covenant can be reforged. It means she hasn't won as thoroughly as she thinks. It means—" She swayed.

Brida caught her. "Enough. Enough, my lady, you've spent—"

"I have spent exactly what I intended to spend," Aldith snapped, but she let herself be eased into a chair by the window. Her color was high and her breathing fast; whatever I'd returned was already slipping, the loan being called. She seemed to know it. She gripped my wrist with surprising strength. "Listen. The crown is missing—I witnessed its hiding, though the seal ate the where before I could ever speak it. And the covenant-heart, the ninth piece, the piece that makes the reforging possible—she has been hunting it for sixty years, and she is close, closer than you know, because she has something now she didn't have before." Her grip tightened. "She has a map."

The cold went through me like well water.

"How do you know that?" Sefa demanded.

"Because the seal on my mind was cut with the same craft as the wards on Hollowmere," Aldith said, "and the same hand that made that courier's map made both. He was mine, boy. The courier who reached you. My apprentice, sixty years ago, before either of us lost our years. He spent his whole life leading the Regent's hunt astray, and at the end he put the true map in the hands of the one person alive who could do what I've just watched you do." Her eyes were dimming even as she spoke, the keel going out of her voice, the tunnels closing back in. "He chose you. So whatever you're planning to do with that paper under your ribs—do it faster."

"Mother Aldith—" Brida was crying again.

The old woman's gaze drifted toward the window, toward the first stars over Vael's Shoulder, and her voice softened into the distance. "Three questions," she murmured. "The toll was always three questions, and Wren never listened..."

And she was gone again, somewhere deep in her beautiful ruined library, and the ring on her finger dimmed to ordinary iron—but it did not go out entirely. A thread of light remained in the script, faint as an ember under ash, pulsing slow and steady, like something waiting.

Brida looked at us over her lady's bowed head, tear-streaked and furious and awed all at once. "Whatever you two are carrying down this mountain," she said, "carry it faster. She said faster. She's never wrong about the important things."

Outside, above the ridge, the moon was rising—thinner now than it had been in Vell. Counting what remained, I found I couldn't stop my lips moving.

Two regalia. Two moons.

The map stirred against my ribs like something waking, and pointed south, toward the sea.
