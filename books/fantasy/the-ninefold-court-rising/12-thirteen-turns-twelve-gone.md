## 12. Thirteen Turns, Twelve Gone

The map changed on the night the twelfth moon waned.

Theo felt it before he saw it—a cold pulse against his sternum where the parchment had lived for months now, folded into a shape that fit no pocket and obeyed no reason. He'd stopped trying to leave it behind in Vell. It always came back to him, warm as a stolen coin, patient as debt.

He unrolled it by lantern light in the room above the Wayhouse at Grayford's Crossing, where they'd stopped to rest the horses. The ink had moved. The careful lines of roads and rivers—the geography of their long, slow hunt—had rearranged themselves into something new: a single dark shape at the map's heart, drawn in strokes like ash smeared on vellum. A citadel. And around it, seven small burning points of light, arranged like a crown of coals.

"Seven," Theo said aloud, because saying it made it worse and he needed it to be worse, needed the fear sharp enough to cut through his exhaustion.

Sefa looked up from whetting her sword. She'd been doing that for an hour without once testing the edge—her hands wanted work her mind refused to give them. "Seven what?"

"Regalia." He turned the map so she could see. "All the rest of them. Lantern, ring, and five more we haven't even named. They're not scattered. They're not hidden. She *has* them."

Sefa set down the whetstone very carefully, the way people do when they're afraid of what their hands might do otherwise. "Then she's been collecting since the night she took the three seats. Eleven months of gathering while we spent two moons finding two."

"Not gathering." Theo traced the citadel's walls with one finger; the ink was cold enough to sting. "*Keeping.* There's a difference. You gather things you mean to use."

"And you keep things you're afraid of," Sefa said quietly.

Outside, the twelfth moon hung low and swollen over the crossing, one night from dying. Thirteen turns, the courier had whispered into Theo's hands with his last breath. Twelve gone. Somewhere beyond the window, the boundary between worlds was thinning like ice in spring, and every hour of sleep they stole from the road was an hour the world came closer to tearing along its seams.

* * *

*The Ashen Regent had watched the twelfth moon rise from the highest balcony of her citadel, and she had smiled.*

She stood now in the vault corridor where her regalia slept behind glass and warding, seven pieces arranged in a row like accused prisoners awaiting sentence. The Crown of the First Seat. The Scales. The Bell that had once rung across nine realms to call the Court to order. She did not touch them. Touching them would be admitting they mattered.

"They have two," said Warden Ilse behind her, voice flat as slate. "The lantern and the ring. My brother's map led them to both."

"Your brother's map led them to both," the Regent agreed, "because your brother believed the old lie right up until it killed him." She studied the Bell through the glass. Inside its bronze throat, faint as breath on a mirror, something stirred. "Do you know what I felt when I heard they'd taken the lantern? Relief. Do you understand that, Ilse? For eleven months I have been the only person in these realms willing to hold this burden, and now there are others carrying it too. Let them feel its weight. Let them learn what it costs."

"The hunters report the boy steals memories. That he returned decades to the second seat. That he gave my brother's last moments to me freely." Ilse's jaw tightened around the words. "Freely. He asked nothing."

"Yes. That is the dangerous part." The Regent turned from the glass at last, and in the vault-light her eyes were the grey of long-dead coals. "I can fight a thief. I can fight a knight, a king, a prophet. But a boy who gives things back—" she shook her head slowly, "—the covenant was built on giving. On sacrifice dressed up as virtue. If he rekindles it, everything I've burned will have been for nothing. Everything I've *unmade* will have been for nothing."

"You could kill him."

"I could." She walked the length of the vaults, past the bottled memories shelved in their thousands—confiscated pasts, dissenters' histories, whole towns' worth of yesterdays sealed in glass. "But killing him makes him a martyr, and martyrs are just memories that learned to walk. No. When the thirteenth moon rises, I will offer him exactly what he wants. And I will offer it honestly, which is more than the old Court ever did." Her hand rested briefly on the glass above the Crown. "That is how I will win. Not with lies. With truth, offered at the moment truth is most unbearable."

Ilse said nothing. But she thought of her brother's face in the memory the boy had given her—thought of how it had broken her open and rebuilt her in the same breath—and for the first time in eleven years of service, she wondered whether the Regent's arithmetic accounted for everything.

* * *

They broke camp before dawn, and it was Sefa who finally said it.

They were three miles out from Grayford's Crossing, the road climbing toward the hill country, when she reined in and didn't start again. Theo pulled up beside her. Behind them, Brannic—the ex-soldier who'd joined at Vell, all shoulders and suspicion—kept riding a few paces before realizing and circling back, scowling.

"Sefa?"

"I need to say something before we get closer to that citadel." She was looking at the horizon, not at him. Her hands were steady on the reins, but Theo had spent a lifetime reading people's small betrayals, and hers were everywhere: the set of her shoulders, the breath she took like a woman stepping off a ledge. "And you're going to want to ride away from me after. I'd deserve it. But you need to hear it here, on open ground, where I can't make it sound better than it is."

Theo's stomach dropped. He thought of the market in Vell, the seller who'd known the dying stranger. He thought of the way Sefa had recognized the map on sight, that first day on the caravan road, and nearly killed him for it. He thought: *I knew there was a door in her I couldn't open.*

"Say it."

"The night the Ashen Regent seized the three seats—" Sefa's voice was level, trained, terrible in its control, "—I wasn't absent. I wasn't deceived. I helped her."

Brannic's horse stamped. Nobody spoke.

"I was the Court's youngest knight. Sworn to the ninth seat—sworn to *her*, before she was the Regent, when she was only the one who asked the questions nobody else would ask. Is the covenant just? Is binding nine realms to nine thrones freedom or slavery? And I sat in the hall and listened to her, night after night, and every word rang true, because some of it *was* true." Now she looked at Theo, and her silver-grey eyes held nothing back, gave him nothing soft to hold onto. "The covenant was cruel, Theo. You've touched the lantern—you've heard the oaths it holds. People bound by vows they never chose, generations paying debts their ancestors signed. She showed me the original terms. A child given to the Second Seat as a living surety. A realm that lost its name for a hundred years as penalty for rebellion. I read those documents with my own eyes, and I helped her take the seats because I believed breaking the cage mattered more than the noise the birds would make."

"And then?" Theo's voice came out strange and quiet.

"And then I watched what she did with the broken cage." Sefa's composure cracked—not into tears but into something rawer, a flinch, as if the memory itself struck her. "The erasure-hunters. The town that stopped existing. I went to her and said *this isn't what we swore*, and she looked at me with such kindness and said, 'Sefa, freedom requires forgetting. You cannot build a new world on top of the old one's grave-markers.' And I understood that I hadn't joined a revolution. I'd joined a fire, and fires don't keep promises." She swallowed. "So I deserted. I ran. I let her take everything and told myself a lone knight with a guilty conscience was worth nothing to anyone. Until a valley-rat with a thief's gift handed me a dead man's map and I saw a chance to pay down the debt."

The silence stretched. Somewhere downslope, a lark started up, absurdly cheerful.

"So," Sefa finished. "Now you know. I didn't fail the Court. I betrayed it first. Everything the Regent has, she has partly because I opened doors for her. Every town she erased, I made easier to erase." She gathered the reins. "Ride ahead if you want. I'll point you to the citadel road and you'll never see me again. I'll even understand it. That's the worst part—I'll *understand* it."

* * *

Brannic was the first to move, and Theo braced himself for the soldier's contempt—but Brannic only swung down from his horse and stood in the road, working his jaw like a man chewing on something too big to swallow.

"My village paid tithe to a lord who burned it," he said at last. "For taxes. I was twelve. I spent twenty years telling myself I'd have fought him if I'd been older, been stronger, been anything." He spat into the dust. "You know what I actually would have done? What I did do? Nothing. Kept my head down. So don't stand there acting like helping a monster once makes you the monster, knight. Some of us never even got the chance to be wrong that usefully."

"That's not—" Sefa began.

"It is, though." Brannic remounted, abrupt. "It's exactly that. Ride or don't. I'm following the map."

And then they were both looking at Theo, and Theo understood that whatever happened next would happen because of him, and hated him for it, and rode forward anyway.

He stopped his horse beside Sefa's. Close enough that their stirrups almost touched.

"When I worked the Hollowmere market," he said, "I had rules. Never take more than a traveler could spare. Never sell a memory twice. Never take from someone who looked like they were already empty." He stared at the road ahead, at the hills hiding the citadel somewhere beyond them. "Good rules. And every rule existed so I wouldn't have to ask myself the real question, which was whether taking memories from strangers to survive made me a thief or something worse. I never answered it. I just kept moving." He turned to look at her. "You answered yours. Wrong, and late, and at a cost you'll carry forever—but you answered it. I still haven't answered mine."

"Theo—"

"Two regalia found. One moon left. Seven sitting in her vaults." He nudged his horse forward. "We settle our accounts after the world's saved. Not before. That's not forgiveness, Sefa. It's a truce. Don't confuse them."

Something shifted in her face—relief and grief tangled together, neither winning. "A truce," she repeated. "I can work with a truce."

"Don't make me regret it."

"No promises," she said, and for the first time in days, something almost like her old dry humor flickered through. "I'm told I have a history with those."

* * *

They rode hard for six days, and on the sixth night the map woke again.

Theo was on watch when it happened—the parchment heating against his chest like a coal wrapped in cloth, and he pulled it out expecting pain and got instead a flood of image so vivid it knocked the breath from him. The citadel rendered in lines of light, every wall, every gate, every vault corridor drawn in burning detail—and threaded through it, seven points of fire, and beneath them, deeper than the foundations, a darkness shaped like a hollow heart.

"The covenant-heart," Sefa breathed, awake instantly, sword half-drawn before her eyes caught up with her mind. "It's showing us the Court's heart. Under the citadel." Her finger hovered over the dark shape without touching it. "The old hall was built above it. When the Regent took the seats, she built her fortress on top of the one thing in the world that can reforge what she broke."

"Why show us?" Brannic growled. "Maps don't get generous."

"Because it's not showing us a way in." Theo's mouth had gone dry. He looked at the seven burning points, then at the dark heart below them, and a memory rose unbidden—the drowned chapel, the lantern's flood of broken vows, a woman's voice with an echo of something he'd spent his whole life missing. "It's showing us what we'll have to do when we get there. Look at the heart. Really look."

They looked. And slowly, as their eyes adjusted to the map's strange grammar, they saw what the darkness was made of: not emptiness, but *absence*. An outline waiting to be filled. A vessel shaped like a sacrifice.

"It wants something given," Sefa said softly. "Not taken. The covenant-heart doesn't respond to force—that's why the Regent can't use it, why she keeps it buried under her fortress like a corpse she can't bury deeper. It needs a willing offering. Something irreplaceable." Her eyes came up to meet Theo's, and he saw the realization land in her the same moment it landed in him, saw her go pale in the moonlight. "Theo. What did the lantern show you? In the chapel. What did you hear?"

He didn't answer. He rolled the map closed, and the cold of it sank through his fingers into his bones, and far to the east, over the hills that hid the Ashen Citadel, the sky was beginning to bruise toward dawn.

"One moon left," he said. "We ride."

Behind them, the twelfth moon set for the last time, and the world did not notice, and the world was wrong.
