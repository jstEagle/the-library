## 13. The City That Forgot Its Name

The road told them before the map did.

For three days the caravan track had wound through hill country gone strangely empty—no waystones, no shrine-posts, not even the crude boundary cairns farmers stack to mark a neighbor's field. Sefa rode with her hand resting on her sword hilt the whole time, which was how Theo had learned to read her: she touched steel when she was remembering, and she was remembering constantly now.

"It should be here," she said on the third morning, reining up on a ridge. "Vessarin. It was a river city. Forty thousand souls, maybe more. I escorted grain barges past it twice a year." She pointed at the valley below, and Theo followed her finger and saw—nothing.

Not ruins. Not burned foundations or broken walls. Nothing.

A river ran through the basin, silver and indifferent, and fields lay green to its banks, and there was not one stone stacked upon another in the whole of it. No city had ever stood there. The land itself seemed to agree. The grass grew straight through where streets should have been, unbroken, as if no cart wheel had ever pressed it.

"The Regent did this?" Theo asked, though he already knew.

"Her erasure-hunters, working for a season." Sefa's voice was flat in the way he'd learned meant she was holding something down hard. "They don't burn cities. Burning leaves bones. They *unmake* them. Every record, every grave-marker, every mind that held the city's name. The people who lived here were scattered to other towns and left with holes where their lives used to be."

Theo sat his borrowed horse and looked down at the green nothing, and something in him went cold and then hot, the way it always did now when he thought of Hollowmere's wards, of ash-grey sigils, of Maren's face going smooth and blank under a hunter's palm.

He could feel it before they descended. Even from the ridge, faint as a smell of smoke on far wind—the stones were still there. Under the grass. Under the river mud. The city hadn't been destroyed; it had been *forgotten*, and forgetting left residue the way a fire left ash. And Theo Dunmore, who had spent nineteen years picking memories out of travelers' heads like coins from careless pockets, was the only soul alive who could feel that ash still warm.

* * *

The survivors were camped outside the basin, three hundred or so in tents and lean-tos along the north road, because none of them could explain why they wouldn't go home. Most didn't know they had a home to refuse. They spoke of a pull they couldn't name, a grief without an object, and they had drifted here over months like water finding its old level.

Theo walked among them on the first evening and felt like a man wading into a flooded cellar.

Their wounds were everywhere. A woman kneading bread with hands that moved in patterns her mind had lost—a baker once, certainly, but she wept without knowing why whenever she finished a loaf. An old man who had carved the same unfinished toy boat forty times, unable to remember for whom. Children who woke screaming at a sound—the river's voice—that no longer meant anything to them.

And underneath all of it, soaked into the ground itself, lay Vessarin. Theo closed his eyes and the city rose around him whole: market bells, the blue tiles of the bathhouse, a ferryman's song, ten thousand small lives stacked like sediment. The hunters had taken it from every living mind, but memory clings to places. Stone remembers longer than flesh. He was standing on a grave that didn't know it was one.

"You feel it too," Sefa said quietly beside him. She was watching his face. "I can tell. You've got the look you had at the lantern chapel."

"It's all still here," Theo said. His voice came out rough. "Every bit of it. They took it out of the people but they couldn't take it out of the earth. It's just—" he groped for the word, "—waiting."

Sefa said nothing for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had the careful flatness again. "Theo. We have five regalia and one moon. We can't stay."

"I know."

"We have maybe two days of margin if we push hard after."

"I know." He looked at her. "Sefa, these people are going to spend their whole lives grieving for something they can't name. Some of them will die like that. Like animals that lost their den. I can give it back."

She studied him, and he watched her do the arithmetic he hated: what he was, what it would cost him, what it might make him. She had seen him steal from a hunter's mind on the road. She knew the shape of his gift better than anyone living.

"And what will it cost you?" she asked.

"Nothing I haven't given before."

"That's a lie," she said, not unkindly, "and you've gotten worse at telling those since the hospice."

* * *

He started with the baker.

It was easier than theft, which shamed him a little—he had spent years perfecting theft, and giving turned out to require only willingness. He knelt by her fire, took her flour-dusted hands in his, and reached down into the soil beneath the camp the way he'd learned to reach into minds. The city's memory rose to meet him like water rising in a well shaft, and he cupped what he could carry and poured it gently into her.

Her name came back first. Odile. Then the shop—her father's shop before hers, on Cordwainer Row, with the crooked door. Then the crooked door itself, and the smell of the ovens at dawn, and the day her daughter was born in the room above the shop while the whole street sent up food—

Odile made a sound like a hinge and folded forward over her hands, sobbing, laughing, saying her daughter's name over and over. *Mariel. Mariel, she'll be at her aunt's in Dolefield, oh gods, my girl.* Around the fire, other survivors had gathered to watch, and Theo felt their hope hit him like heat from an open oven door.

He gave until his nose bled that first night. Fourteen people. A ferryman who remembered his river. Two sisters who remembered each other. A boy of nine who got back his dog's name and wept as only children weep, totally and without shame.

By dawn he understood the arithmetic that would rule the rest of his life.

Three hundred survivors. He could restore perhaps twenty a day before the strain began tearing at him—and each restoration cost more than the last, because he wasn't drawing from a person this time but from a whole buried city, sifting ten thousand lives for the fragments that belonged to one grieving stranger. Two days became four. Four became six. And every hour he spent kneeling in the dirt pouring a grandmother's lullaby back into some hollowed-out man was an hour not spent riding toward the Ashen Citadel, toward the crown, toward the thirteenth moon that would not wait for anyone's grief, least of all his.

* * *

On the fourth day, the choosing began.

He didn't decide to choose. He decided, fiercely, that he wouldn't—but the gift made liars of everyone. Twenty restorations a day, and thirty people waiting, and he caught himself ranking them. The old man with the toy boats had less life ahead than the young widow; the boy with the dog would suffer longest if unfixed. He heard his own thoughts arranging strangers like inventory, like stock in his old market stall in Hollowmere, and he stopped in the middle of the camp, sickened, staring at his own hands.

This was what power did. This was what the Regent had learned, maybe—no. He pushed that thought away, because the Regent had chosen erasure, and erasure was the opposite of what he held in his hands. But the shape of the choice was the same. Someone always had to be ranked. Someone always waited while someone else was served.

That night Sefa found him sitting alone on the riverbank, in the exact place where the ferry steps had been, though to any eye but his it was just mud and grass.

"How many left?" she asked.

"Ninety-one."

"And the moon?"

"Five turns gone of thirteen. I can hear it ticking in my sleep." He threw a pebble into the river. "There's a woman here, Sefa. Third row of tents. Her husband was the one who built half these boats the old man keeps carving. She doesn't know she's a widow. She keeps setting out two bowls at meals and then staring at the second one like it belongs to a ghost." He swallowed. "If I don't reach her, she spends her life mourning a man whose face she can't picture. If I do reach her, she gets the whole weight of it at once—every year of marriage, and the fact that he's gone, all in one breath. Sometimes I think leaving her hollow is a mercy. Sometimes I think mercy is just what cowards call cowardice."

Sefa sat down beside him on the cold stone that wasn't stone anymore. For a while neither spoke, and the river made its soundless-remembered noise that only Theo could hear.

"When I deserted," she said finally, "there was a gate captain at Thornhold who covered my retreat. I never learned her name. Never went back." She turned the ring on her sword-hand—the plain band she wore, the knight's vow-ring of a Court that no longer existed. "I've told myself for eight years that finding her would be too dangerous. That it would compromise the mission. Do you want to know the truth I finally admitted on this road?"

"That you were afraid of what she'd say to you."

"I was afraid of what I'd have to feel." She looked at him. "Give the widow her husband back. All of it. The grief included. People aren't vessels to be kept from overflowing, Theo. They're fires. Let them burn."

So he did. He knelt before the widow at her fire, took her hands, and let Vessarin pour through him into her—the wedding on the bridge, the boats, twenty-two years, the fever last winter, the burial she had slept through inside her own erased mind. She screamed. She held onto him so hard he thought his ribs would crack. And when it passed, she looked at him with red-rimmed eyes that were, for the first time, entirely her own, and said, "Thank you," and he carried the sound of that thank-you away like a coal in his chest, because it was the most terrible and beautiful thing anyone had ever handed him.

* * *

He restored sixty-three more over the next two days. He learned to work faster and paid for it in nosebleeds and nights where he dreamed in other people's childhoods. He learned that some fragments wouldn't come—the hunters had been thorough with certain districts, burning the residue itself, and there were gaps in the city he fell into like potholes. One family he simply could not find anywhere in the stone. He searched for hours and came up with nothing, and had to stand before a gray-haired man and say, *I'm sorry. Whoever took this from you was very good at their work,* and watch the man nod and turn away, and understand that some debts could never be repaid, only witnessed.

On the sixth morning, the map burned cold against his chest and refused to be ignored.

He pulled it out at the edge of camp. The ink had shifted—the route south to the Ashen Citadel now pulsed with urgent light, and above it, in script that hadn't been there before, new words had surfaced:

*SEVEN TURNS REMAIN. THE CROWN DOES NOT WAIT.*

"They know we're coming," Sefa said behind him. She had her pack on. So did the others—their company had swelled by two, Odile's grown daughter Mariel among them, armed and grim, who had kissed her mother's forehead and then asked Theo, in a voice like drawn steel, to point her at whoever had done this. "Scouts reported ash-grey riders on the south road yesterday. The Regent knows exactly where we are."

Theo folded the map. Around him the camp was striking tents, three hundred people preparing to scatter to whatever futures they could now remember wanting. Odile pressed a loaf of bread into his hands—her bread, Cordwainer Row bread, the taste of a resurrected street—and couldn't speak, and didn't need to.

Ninety-one minus sixty-three. Twenty-eight remained. He felt each absence like a tooth pulled.

"There's a girl," he said suddenly. "Sixteen. She lost her mother and her little brother both. I found the brother's fragments yesterday—I could restore him alone, it would take an hour. But if I do, we ride late, and if we ride late—"

Sefa put her hand on his shoulder. "Then we ride late."

"No." He shook his head, and to his own surprise he meant it. "No—we go now. Because if the citadel falls to the Regent, if the covenant dies, there won't be one city forgotten. There'll be a thousand. Twenty-eight people is the price of the rest of the world remembering anything at all." His voice cracked on the last word, and he hated it, and let it crack anyway. "Write their names down, Sefa. All twenty-eight. And the ones I couldn't find. Swear to me that when this is done—if we live—someone comes back here and finishes it."

Sefa looked at him for a long moment. Then she drew her knife, cut a strip from her cloak, and began writing names in charcoal, her vow-ring hand steady.

"I swear it on the Ninefold Court," she said. "Old law. New hands."

They rode south at midmorning, five regalia strong, the erased city silent at their backs. Theo did not look at the camp. He looked at the road ahead, and at the horizon where a smudge of grey smoke marked the direction of the Ashen Citadel, and he made himself hold the arithmetic of it in his mind without flinching: twenty-eight names unwritten back, seven moons turning, one crown.

And beneath his ribs, colder than the map ever burned, the thought he had been carrying since the lantern chapel, since the drowned vows and the voice like his mother's voice:

*Whatever the covenant-heart asks for at the end—it will ask me for everything. And I am starting to learn exactly how much that is.*

The road bent south. The thirteenth moon was coming.
