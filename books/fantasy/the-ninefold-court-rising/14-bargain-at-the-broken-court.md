## 14. Bargain at the Broken Court

The Court hall had been dead longer than it had ever lived. That was Sefa's first thought as they crested the ridge and saw it below: a crown of broken spires sunk in a marsh of grey reeds, its nine towers snapped off at different heights like teeth in an old man's jaw. She had knelt in this place once, twenty years ago, with her sword across her knees and her oaths still warm in her mouth. Now the banners were gone, the gutters ran with rain instead of light, and the only sound was wind moving through hollow stone like breath through a corpse.

"You've been here before," Theo said. Not a question. He was getting better at that—reading her silences, pulling the shape of things from what she didn't say.

"I was knighted here." Sefa kept her eyes on the road down. "Seat of the Third, back when there were nine seats and all of them filled. You'd have seen it whole if you'd come then. The regalia sang when the Court convened. You could feel it in your back teeth."

"And now?"

"Now it's a ruin where three old powers hide from the fourth." She said it flatly, because saying it any other way would have meant feeling it. "Veyran holds the western wing. Ondrel the eastern vaults. And Lady Iseult keeps the great hall itself, because she never could resist sitting where the throne used to be."

Theo walked beside her in silence for a while. The lantern hung at his hip under a travel cloak, wrapped in three layers of wool, though Sefa suspected wrapping did little good. Since the chapel he had been changed by it—not visibly, not dramatically, but the way a man is changed after holding something hot. He carried his hands carefully. He listened to strangers' conversations as though they might contain him.

"The neutral seats," he said finally. "Will they fight for us?"

"They'll do worse than fight. They'll bargain." Safa adjusted her sword belt out of habit; there was nothing on the road worth drawing for, but her hands wanted work. "Veyran will want guarantees. Ondrel will want leverage. And Iseult—" She stopped herself.

"Iseult will want what?"

Sefa looked at him. Younger than her by fifteen years, thinner than any courier had a right to be, carrying five hopes in a bag that weighed nothing and everything. He had given a grieving woman her brother's last moments without asking a coin for it. He had watched Sefa lose her own reasons twice over and had never once suggested leaving her behind.

"Iseult will want proof," Sefa said. "That's all you need to know until we're standing in front of her. After that, you'll know more than you want to."

* * *

The hall swallowed them by degrees. First the outer colonnade, its columns cracked but still bearing faded sigils of the nine—Sefa counted them without meaning to, the way a widow counts chairs. Then the antechamber, where the floor mosaic showed the covenant itself: nine interlocking rings around a central flame, half of it shattered, someone having taken a hammer to the Third Ring specifically. Sefa stepped over the damage. She always stepped over the damage.

Three figures waited beneath the broken dome, seated on plain wooden chairs arranged where the nine thrones had once stood in crescent. It was deliberately humble and therefore deliberately insulting—a message carved into furniture: *we keep no thrones because we claim none*, which every person present understood to mean *we keep thrones elsewhere*.

Regent Veyran was a broad, grey-bearded man in working leathers, a smith's scars down both forearms, the ring of his office worn openly on his hand. Regent Ondrel was a narrow woman with ink-stained fingers and eyes like ledger lines, who looked at Theo the way merchants look at unweighed goods. And Lady Iseult—

Lady Iseult sat perfectly still, hands folded, silver hair bound in a single braid, and looked at Sefa with an expression that had not aged a single day since the night Sefa deserted. It was the expression of a woman who has already decided everything and is merely waiting for the world to catch up.

"Serapha Vane." Iseult's voice filled the ruined hall effortlessly, as though the acoustics remembered her. "Deserter of the Third Seat. They told me you died at the fall of Callow Ford."

"Reports of my death were premature," Sefa said. "And my desertion was timely. Both facts are available for purchase, Lady Iseult, but neither is why we've come."

"No," Iseult agreed. Her gaze slid past Sefa and settled on Theo with the weight of a falling beam. "It's why he's come that interests me. The memory-thief of Hollowmere. The one who walks out of a wound in the world carrying a living map and a stolen lantern. Word travels faster than you'd think, boy, even through places like this."

"He's called Theo," Sefa said.

"He's called many things, I imagine. Names are cheap. Proof is dear." Iseult unfolded her hands. "You come to ask three regents to spend their strength—the last unspent strength in the realms—against the Ashen Regent. You come with two regalia recovered and six held in her citadel. You come with no army, no seat, and no covenant to swear upon, because the covenant-heart is ash and the throne is occupied. So tell me, thief: why should we believe you are anything more than her newest instrument? Sent soft-spoken into our hall to learn where we sleep?"

The silence stretched. Sefa's hand found her sword hilt out of pure reflex and she forced it away again. Beside her, she felt Theo take a breath.

"I can't prove what I am," Theo said. "I don't fully know what I am. But I can show you what I carry."

He unwrapped the lantern.

Even guttered, even dormant, it changed the air of the hall. Sefa felt it against her skin like the first warmth after a long fever—the sense of promises hovering just outside hearing. Veyran leaned forward despite himself. Ondrel's ledger-eyes went wide and hungry. And Iseult closed her eyes for one long moment, and when she opened them they were wet, which Sefa suspected no one in that hall had seen in thirty years.

"The Lantern of Unkept Promises," Iseult murmured. "I heard its maker swear she'd never finish it. Apparently she kept that oath the only way left to her." She rose from her chair, descended the shallow dais, and stood before Theo—close enough that Sefa's hand did go to her sword this time. "A beautiful thing. A terrible thing. And entirely insufficient."

"It's one of nine," Theo said.

"One of nine, held by a boy with no lineage we can trace, guided by a deserter, opposed to a woman who has spent twenty years building an engine of erasure while you two have spent—" she glanced at the map-case at Theo's belt— "what, four months?" She shook her head slowly. "I have watched oaths fail, Serapha. I have watched the covenant itself fail. Before I commit my people to die for a reforging, I will know that the one who carries it understands what sacrifice is. Not in theory. In the flesh."

"Name your proof," Theo said quietly.

Sefa heard it and her stomach dropped, because she knew—Iseult's eyes had been on Theo since the moment they entered, but they flicked once, briefly, to the small leather case at his hip where he kept the memory-vials he still hadn't surrendered from his market days. The hoard. The hoard he'd shown Sefa once, on the road, in a rare unguarded hour: dozens of tiny glass phials, each holding someone's wedding day, someone's childhood dog, someone's mother singing in a kitchen. Including—

"No," Sefa said aloud.

Iseult turned to her, mild as milk. "You have a better suggestion, deserter?"

"She doesn't know what she's asking," Theo said.

"Oh, I suspect she knows exactly." Iseult circled him slowly, and Sefa recognized the technique—an old interrogation habit, making the subject turn, keeping them off-balance. "You carry memories that aren't yours. Bought ones. Stolen ones. Among them, if my information serves—and my information is exquisite—are recollections of a woman named Lira Dunmore. Your mother. Whom you never met. Whose voice you have never heard except secondhand, bottled, purchased in the market of Vell from a seller who has been trading in her for years." She stopped in front of him. "Give me that memory. The voice, the naming, the whole of it. Give it freely, here, before witnesses, and the Third Seat marches with you."

The hall went very quiet. Even the wind seemed to hold itself.

"You can't," Sefa said again, stepping forward. "Iseult, you sanctimonious—she's dead. That memory is all he has of her. It isn't yours to take and it isn't his to give away like a—"

"Like a toll?" Iseult raised an eyebrow. "Yes. Precisely like a toll. Every regent who ever swore to the covenant paid one. That is what made it binding—consent that costs. The Ashen Regent broke the covenant because she believed cost was cruelty. I will not rebuild the Ninefold on the belief that cost is optional." She looked back at Theo. "But hear me, boy, and hear me well: I don't want it for cruelty either. I want it because in four months you have given away other people's memories—freely, generously, beautifully—and never once paid anything of your own. Mercy spent from another man's purse is easy. I need to see what you do when the coin is yours."

Theo didn't answer immediately. He looked down at the case at his hip, and Sefa watched his thumb move over the clasp, back and forth, back and forth, a market-street habit she'd seen a hundred times without understanding until now that it was the motion of a man counting.

"There's a version of me that says no," Theo said at last. "That walks out of here with the lantern and finds some other way."

"There is," Iseult agreed. "And that version loses."

He opened the case. The vials caught the grey light, each one glowing faintly with borrowed color—gold, blue, the warm amber of kitchens. He lifted one out: small, unremarkable, the liquid inside barely luminous at all. Ordinary-looking. Sefa knew better.

"The seller in Vell," Theo said, his voice very steady, which Sefa recognized as the most dangerous kind of steady, "called it the naming. She's young in it—younger than me, maybe. A kitchen. Morning light. She says my name twice. Once like she's calling me, once like she's telling the world." His thumb stopped moving. "I've played it eleven times."

"Twelve," Sefa said before she could stop herself.

His mouth twitched—almost a smile, nothing like one. "Twelve."

"Theo." Sefa took his shoulder and turned him, and for once she didn't care that three regents were watching, or that Iseult was watching most of all. "Listen to me. We can find another way. There's always another way. I told you I'd get you to the Court and I meant all of it, but not like this—not trading away the only—"

"Sefa." He said her name gently, the way you speak to someone standing too close to a drop. "She's right. Not about everything—I think she enjoys being right too much—but about this part. I've spent my whole life handling other people's griefs for money. Learning to hold them lightly so I could put them down again. If I won't pay when it counts, then everything I gave Ilse was performance. Everything." He exhaled. "Besides. The thirteenth moon is coming whether I'm ready or not. Consider this rehearsal."

The word landed in Sefa's chest like a thrown knife. Rehearsal. Because they both knew—had known since the drowned chapel, had never said—that the covenant-heart would demand exactly this, only larger. That somewhere ahead of him stood a moment when he would have to give up not one memory but all of them. This was Iseult forcing him to practice dying in miniature, and the cruelty of it was that it was also kindness, and the kindness of it was also cruel.

"Do it properly, then," Iseult said. She drew a slim ceremonial knife—not for blood, Sefa knew, but for witness; old custom required a blade to mark the moment an oath was cut free. "Kneel at the center ring. Speak the surrender aloud. And understand that once spoken, it cannot be reclaimed. What leaves you becomes mine to keep—or mine to return, should the renewed Court judge it fitting. I make no promises beyond that."

Theo crossed the hall to the shattered mosaic. At its center, where the flame of the covenant was carved, half the stone was missing—he knelt in the gap, in the actual absence, which Sefa thought was either accident or Iseult's design and probably both.

He held the vial up to the light one last time.

"Her name was Lira Dunmore," he said, and his voice only broke a little, on the second word. "In the memory, it's morning. There's flour on her hands. She's talking to someone out of frame—laughing—and then she turns toward the door, toward whoever's coming in, and she says my name. Theo. Twice. Once like she's calling me. Once like she's telling the world." He swallowed. "I never heard her say it any other way. I never got the chance. I'm giving this up freely, before these witnesses, as proof that I understand what the covenant costs—and as payment toward seeing it remade."

He pressed the vial to the flat of the knife.

There was no flash, no thunder. That was somehow the worst of it. The glow simply drained upward along the blade into Iseult's hand, and then into her, and the vial in Theo's fingers went clear and empty as a winter window. He knelt there a moment longer, staring at it, and Sefa saw him reach for something—reach the way you reach for a word on the tip of your tongue—and find nothing.

"That's how it works," he said softly, mostly to himself. "That's what it's like for them. Everyone I ever took from." He looked up at Iseult, and there was something new in his face—not anger, exactly, and not forgiveness. Recognition. "Now I know what I've been doing to people for ten years."

"Yes," Iseult said. She sealed the emptied light into a locket at her throat, and her expression did not soften, because Sefa doubted it was built to. "Now you do. Rise, Theo Dunmore. The Third Seat marches with you. My levies muster at Greywater within the week, and my word goes tonight to Veyran and Ondrel—they will follow, grudgingly, because they follow whoever moves first." She paused at the edge of the dais and looked back. "One more thing. The seller in Vell who traded in your mother's memories. You never asked yourself the question you should have asked. Where does a black-market broker get a decade of a dead woman's mornings? Someone fed her. Someone close to the source. When this is done, find out who—and ask yourself why they sold her rather than kept her."

She swept out through the eastern arch without waiting for an answer, and Veyran and Ondrel followed like weather fronts.

* * *

They camped that night in the colonnade, because Sefa refused to sleep under Iseult's roof even if Iseult had technically offered it. A small fire. Hard bread. The broken towers against a sky where twelve moons were already climbing, pale and patient, and the thirteenth—barely a sliver yet, dark-rimmed and wrong-colored—rising behind them like a bruise coming up through skin.

Theo sat with his back against a column, turning the empty vial over and over in his fingers. He hadn't eaten. Sefa dropped down beside him and let the silence sit between them for a long time, because some silences you fill and some you guard.

"I hate how easily I did that," Theo said finally.

"I know."

"No, I mean—" He stopped. Started again. "I stood up and walked over there and I did it in under a hundred breaths. No haggling. No looking for another way. Ten years ago I would have negotiated that woman into the ground. Tonight I just... paid." He stared at the vial. "And the worst part isn't that I miss it. The worst part is I can't remember what I'm missing. There's a hole shaped like a kitchen and I can feel the edges of it and there's nothing inside. Is that what it's like? For everyone? Forever?"

Sefa thought about the desertion she couldn't remember. The hole where her reasons used to live. She'd been walking beside that hole for weeks now, feeling its edges, and she had learned that the body remembers the shape of a loss long after the mind lets go of the content.

"Yes," she said. "That's what it's like."

He nodded slowly. "Then I'm done pretending it's a fair price. Whatever comes at the Court's heart—if the covenant really demands what we think it demands—I'm not going to stand there and call it noble. I'll pay it. But I'm going to hate it honestly."

"Good," Sefa said. "Hate it honestly. That's more than most heroes manage."

He almost laughed. Then he went still, the way he did when the map woke—it happened sometimes at night, a cold bloom against his hip like ice forming. He pulled it out, and Sefa leaned close as the ink shifted in the moonlight, lines crawling like veins, and new writing surfaced across the lower corner in a hand neither of them had seen before:

*Six remain in the citadel. The seventh was never lost.*

Below it, a single mark: a circle bisected by a line—the sigil of the Ninth Seat.

The seat the Ashen Regent had held before she took the others.

Sefa read it three times. "The crown," she breathed. "Everyone assumed the crown fell in the war. Everyone assumed it was destroyed or hidden. If it was never lost—" She looked up and found Theo already looking at her, the firelight and the moonlight fighting across his face.

"If it was never lost," he said quietly, "then she's been wearing it the whole time. And whatever the covenant-heart asks of me at the end—" he closed his fist around the empty vial— "it just became the least expensive thing anyone will pay this month."

Above the broken towers, the thirteenth moon climbed another degree, and somewhere far to the north, in a citadel full of bottled pasts, lights began to kindle—as if something there had felt them reading.
