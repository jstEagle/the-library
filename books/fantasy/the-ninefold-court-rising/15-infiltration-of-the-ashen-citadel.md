## 15. Infiltration of the Ashen Citadel

The tithe-collector's coat fit Theo the way a lie fits a practiced mouth: snugly, and with a certain shameful comfort.

It had belonged to a man named Ovist Brann, who until three days ago had collected memories for the Regent along the river road — tithes of grief from villages too frightened to refuse, bottled in grey glass and carted to the citadel each season. Sefa had found him drunk in a waystation outside Vell, and Theo had done what he did best. He had taken. Not everything — he'd learned that much restraint across a lifetime of small thefts — but enough. The man's route codes, his countersign phrases, the particular stoop of his shoulders when he spoke to soldiers. Enough that Theo could wear him like a coat.

He told himself it was mercy. He'd left Ovist Brann sitting by a warm fire with coin in his purse and a fog where the last three days should have been, a fog that would resolve into nothing worse than a lost evening. He told himself that everyone who worked for the Ashen Regent deserved a little hollowing.

He told himself these things because the alternative was to admit how easily the words came now. How fluently he lied. How little the lying cost.

*You're getting good at this,* Sefa had said on the last night before the citadel walls rose out of the ash-fields, and there had been no compliment in it at all.

* * *

The Ashen Citadel did not look like a fortress. That was the first horror of it.

Theo had expected spires, black iron, banners. Instead the citadel was low and wide and pale, built of a stone so smooth it seemed poured rather than cut, its walls curving inward like the inside of a cupped hand. No battlements. No visible gate guards beyond a token pair at the archway. It didn't need defenses. It was the most secure place in nine realms for the same reason Hollowmere had been safe for a century: nothing inside wanted to be found, and nothing outside remembered why it should care.

The road up to it was lined with poplars gone white as bone, and farmers' carts trundled along it without fear, carrying grain and cloth and, in sealed wagons with grey sigils on their doors, the pasts of the realm.

"Collector Brann." The gate clerk didn't look up from her ledger. She was young, with ink-stained fingers and the flat competence of someone who processed horrors as line items. "Season's tithe came through two days ago. You're late."

"River bridge was washed out at Marlow's Ford." The lie arrived fully dressed, wearing Ovist Brann's voice, wearing Theo's mouth. He felt it leave him without friction and hated how good it felt. "Took the ferry at Cael Row."

"Mm." She made a note. "Vault intake closes at dusk. If you've personal collections for storage, declare them now."

*Personal collections.* The phrase slid over Theo's skin like cold water. Somewhere beneath this pale stone were shelves upon shelves upon shelves of lives — confiscated, seized, tithed, erased. Whole towns reduced to inventory.

"Just the route records," he said, and patted the satchel at his hip, where six days of forged ledgers lay wrapped around the folded map, which had grown warm against his ribs the moment he passed the outermost ward-stone. Warm, and pulling. Like a compass needle finding north. Or like a dog straining toward a door it knew.

The clerk stamped his papers. "Warden Ilse will want your manifest verified before you draw stores. East colonnade, third stair down. And Collector—" she finally looked up, and her eyes were careful, "—the Warden counts everything twice. Don't be clever."

"I'm never clever," Theo said, and smiled Ovist Brann's smile, and walked into the citadel that held six of the nine keys to the world.

* * *

Inside, the citadel smelled of lye and lamp oil and something older underneath — a smell Theo recognized from his own work, from cracked memory-vials handled too often. Cold sweetness. The scent of other people's lives leaking.

The corridors were quiet in the wrong way. Servants moved through them like fish in deep water, silent, quick, eyes down. Soldiers stood at intervals, ash-grey sigils stitched on their breasts, and none of them spoke unless spoken to. Theo passed a courtyard where children played — actual children, laughing — and it took him four steps to understand why the sound made his skin crawl: they were laughing exactly alike. Same pitch, same rhythm, same pauses. Trained joy, or manufactured. He didn't stay to find out which.

The map burned against his ribs with every step deeper. It had been guiding him since the gate — not visibly, never visibly, but he could feel the pull sharpening and bending, left here, down here, *deeper*. Six regalia, Sefa had said, according to every scrap of intelligence the neutral regents possessed. Held somewhere in the vault levels. Guarded by someone the Regent trusted absolutely, which meant someone broken in precisely the right shape.

The third stair down was guarded by a single soldier who examined Theo's papers with more attention than the clerk had, then stepped aside without a word.

And then Theo was in the vaults.

They were not dungeons. They were almost beautiful. Long galleries of pale stone, lit by lamps that gave off a light like late afternoon, lined floor to ceiling with niches, and in the niches, vials. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Grey glass, blue glass, a few in amber that glowed faintly when Theo passed, as if recognizing kinship. Each niche bore a small brass plate with a number and a region code. No names. Names would have been cruel, and whatever else the Regent was, cruelty for its own sake seemed foreign to her machinery. This was cruelty as administration. Cruelty filed under category and date.

Theo stopped walking. His hands had begun to shake, and he let them, briefly, in the empty gallery, because no one was watching and because if he didn't let them shake now they'd shake later, in front of someone.

He could hear them. That was the thing he hadn't been prepared for. With his gift — his curse, his trade, the only inheritance Hollowmere had ever given him — he could *hear* the vials, faintly, the way you can hear a seashell's ocean. Murmurs. A woman singing to a child in a language Theo didn't know. An old man arguing with a dead friend about the price of apples. A boy, over and over, running through a summer field, and every time he reached the far hedge the murmur looped back and he ran it again, and again, preserved mid-flight forever.

*This is what she does,* Theo thought. *This is what we're going to unmake.*

The map pulled harder. Down another stair, into a gallery that was different.

* * *

The inner vault announced itself by silence.

Not quiet — the galleries above had been quiet. This was silence with weight, silence like held breath. The air itself felt curated. Theo's skin prickled with the absence of ambient memory: even stones remember footsteps, usually, a faint psychic patina that his gift read like warmth from a stove. Here there was nothing. The walls had been scoured, or built from stone that had never known a footfall, and the effect was like walking through a blind spot in the world.

At the center of the circular chamber stood a pedestal of black wood, and on the pedestal lay six objects, and Theo forgot, for one full heartbeat, how to breathe.

A lantern of greenish bronze, dark and patient. He knew it instantly, painfully — the Lantern of Unkept Promises, the first regalia they'd ever held, the one that had slipped from his grasp in the flight through Sefa's burning stand at the gate. Seeing it here was like seeing a friend's body.

Beside it, a ring of braided metal that seemed to turn slowly even when still. A crown of nine thin bands, unwelded, waiting. A mirror the size of a coin. A bell no larger than a thimble that nevertheless made the floor hum. And a knife whose blade reflected not the room but, Theo thought, a room that hadn't happened yet.

Six regalia. Five of them ones the map had sung about across a thousand miles of road. All of them here, all of them *real*, close enough to touch.

All of them guarded by a woman standing between Theo and the pedestal, who had been there the whole time and whom he had somehow not seen until now.

She wore the ash-grey uniform without rank marks, and her hair was bound back severely, and she was tall in the way of people who have decided to take up space deliberately. Her eyes were silver.

Silver. Full silver, iris and all, catching the lamplight like coins at the bottom of a well.

Theo's whole body went cold. He knew those eyes. He had watched them dim by inches in his loft while their owner bled out on his floorboards and pressed a folded map into his hands with the last of his strength. *Thirteen turns,* the courier had whispered. *Tell them the covenant remembers.*

The courier had never given a name. But he'd had these eyes, this face, this exact severe set of the jaw —

"You're Brann," the woman said. Her voice was flat, unhurried. "Route collector. You're late."

"Bridge washout at Marlow's Ford," Theo said. "Ferry at Cael Row."

She studied him. She had a way of studying that felt less like suspicion than like measurement — as though she were comparing him against some standard only she kept. "The Warden counts everything twice," the clerk had warned. This, then. Warden Ilse. Keeper of the six. Sister to the dead.

"Your manifest," she said.

He handed it over. She read it standing, unhurried, while Theo performed the single hardest acting job of his life: being bored. Being a tired functionary with sore feet who wanted his stores drawn and his bunk assigned. Inside, he was screaming. *That's him. That's her brother's face on a stranger's body, and I am standing six feet from five keys to creation wearing a stolen man's name.*

"The numbers are clean," Ilse said at last, handing the manifest back. Too clean? Had he overdone it? "Brann."

"Warden?"

"Why do you do this work?"

The question landed like a dropped blade. Every rehearsed answer — the pay, the stability, the Regent's peace — queued up behind his teeth. And then, because he was fluent now, because lying had become a language he thought in, he heard himself say, in Ovist Brann's shrug of a voice:

"It's honest work. People carry too much. We help them set some of it down."

Something moved behind the silver eyes. Something that was not agreement. "Do you believe that?"

"I believe people forget whether they wanted to or not," Theo said. "I just make it tidy."

Ilse looked at him for a long moment. Then she turned to the pedestal, and Theo's heart climbed into his throat — but she only adjusted the lantern's angle by a fraction, an unconscious tenderness, the gesture of someone who tended these things the way a sexton tends graves.

"You may draw your stores," she said. "Third gallery, aisle nine. And Brann —"

"Warden?"

"When you're finished at the counter, come back. There's a collection due from the eastern provinces next week. The villages there have become... difficult." She paused, and her hand rested on the pedestal's edge, near the ring of braided metal, and her voice, for the first time, was not flat at all. "Difficult villages require firm collectors. I need to know which of my collectors are firm."

"Yes, Warden," Theo said.

He walked to the counter, drew his fraudulent stores, signed his fraudulent name, and walked back out through ten thousand shelved lives with his pulse hammering and his face arranged into obedient blankness. It wasn't until he reached the upper corridors, until he was safely one more anonymous grey coat among dozens, that he allowed himself to understand what had happened in that chamber.

She hadn't recognized him. Of course she hadn't. But she had looked at him — at his face, the courier's face, the twin's face — and measured him, and asked him what he believed, and touched the pedestal like a mourner.

Sefa would tell him to use it. The neutral regents would tell him to use it. Somewhere in that silver-eyed woman was grief the size of a vault, and grief, Theo knew better than anyone alive, was a door that opened from the inside.

He had the courier's final memory folded away in the deepest pocket of himself — had carried it since the loft, unable to sell it, unable to spend it, unable to look at it directly. A brother's face. A promise kept badly. Everything Ilse had been denied.

All he had to do was lie to her one more time. Open the door gently. Give her the memory freely, ask nothing — and walk out with the world in a satchel.

Theo stood in the lamplit corridor, surrounded by the confiscated past of nine realms, and realized he couldn't feel the difference anymore between the thief who'd started this journey and the man planning his next move. Both of them used people's grief. One had charged a markup. The other called it strategy.

Dusk bells rang above. Vault intake closed. And from the east colonnade, boots approached — too many, moving fast — as a horn sounded once from the walls, and a soldier's voice carried down the stairwell, sharp with alarm:

*"The Warden's brother's seal — someone's forged courier's papers at the south post — seal the vaults!"*

Theo's hand closed around the satchel. Above him, already, he could hear her coming down the stairs.
