## 16. The Warden's Grief

The vaults of the Ashen Citadel did not sleep, and so neither did Warden Ilse Varn.

She had walked these corridors for eleven years, and in all that time she had never once heard silence the way ordinary people meant it. The vaults hummed. Ten thousand bottles of confiscated past lined the shelves from floor to unseen ceiling—memories decanted like wine, each one murmuring its grief or joy or terror through the glass in a language below hearing. New wardens went mad in their first month unless they learned to stop listening. Old wardens, like Ilse, learned something worse: to listen always, and to feel nothing about it.

Tonight the humming was louder than usual. It had been for three days.

Ilse stood at her post beneath the iron arch of the Ninth Gallery, hands clasped behind her back, boots planted at the precise angle prescribed by the Wardens' Codex. Above her, the ash-grey sigil of the Regent hung on a banner gone stiff with old smoke. Below her, shelf upon shelf of bottled lives gleamed dully under witchlight.

Her brother had died for these shelves. That was what the record said. *Corren Varn, courier of the Court, executed for treason against the Regent's order.* The record said it plainly, and Ilse had read it a hundred times, and every time she read it she felt the same cold thing move through her chest—not grief exactly. Grief would have been a mercy. This was more like frost: a hardening, a closing, a promise made to a dead man that his death would mean something.

He had carried stolen memories out of the citadel, the record said. He had sold them back to the grieving at a markup, the record said. He had died on the road with his theft still in his hands.

*You fool,* she thought, the way she thought it every night. *You beautiful, stupid fool.*

The humming swelled.

* * *

She noticed the collector on his second circuit of the gallery.

That alone was wrong. Collectors came to the vaults twice a year at most, when tithe wagons arrived from the provinces, and they moved through the galleries like men wading through cold water—quickly, heads down, eager to be done. This one lingered. He stopped at shelf after shelf, and though he kept his hood up and his posture servile, there was something in the way he tilted his head at the bottles. Not the tilt of a man appraising inventory.

The tilt of a man listening.

Ilse drifted along the gallery's edge, unhurried, a warden making her rounds. Up close the collector was young—younger than she'd expected—with ink-stained fingers and the kind of face that forgot itself easily, forgettable as weather. His tithe-satchel hung open at his hip, half full of small sealed jars.

"You're a long way from the receiving hall," she said.

He flinched. A good sign; an honest man's flinch. "Forgive me, Warden." His voice had a valley-flat accent, the kind you heard from places so far off the roads the maps didn't bother with them. "First time in the capital vaults. I've only ever worked provincial tithes. I didn't know there were so many."

"No one knows," Ilse said. "That is rather the point of them."

He looked up at the shelves then—really looked—and Ilse watched his face do something complicated. Horror, she thought at first. She had seen horror often enough. But this was not quite horror. It was closer to recognition, the look of a man seeing a room he had dreamed about.

"Whose are they?" he asked quietly. "All of them."

"Confiscated pasts." She recited the words the way the Codex required, the way she had recited them for eleven years without tasting them. "Sedition is stored, not punished. The Regent is merciful."

"The Regent is merciful," the collector repeated, and there was something in his voice that made Ilse's hand drift toward the baton at her belt.

But then he turned, and she saw his eyes in the witchlight—pale grey-green, steady despite everything—and whatever she had expected to find in them wasn't there. She had expected greed. She had expected fear. What she found instead was a question he hadn't asked yet, hanging in him like a held breath.

"What's your name, collector?"

"Theo." No surname. Valley people rarely gave surnames; everyone in those little buried places was somebody's cousin, and surnames were for strangers and lawsuits.

"Theo of where?"

"Hollowmere."

The name snagged somewhere deep, some thread she couldn't follow. Corren had mapped the delivery routes once, before his treason; Hollowmere had been on them. A dead valley, Corren used to call it, fondly. *A place where even memory goes to hibernate.*

"You'll return to the receiving hall," Ilse said. "Collectors do not browse."

"Yes, Warden." He bowed—the bow of a man who had practiced being small—and walked away down the gallery, footsteps soft on stone.

Ilse watched him go, and the frost in her chest stirred, and she could not have said why.

* * *

He was at the Ninth Gallery again before dawn shift change, and this time he was standing in front of Shelf 4412.

Ilse knew the shelf number the way she knew her own heartbeat, because 4412 held the last tithe Corren's route had ever delivered—a crate of village memories from the hill country, ordinary things, harvest songs and funeral feasts and a wedding where the bride laughed so hard she fell into the well. She had catalogued them herself. She had listened to none of them. Wardens who listened to the inventory did not last.

The collector stood before the shelf with one bottle cradled in both hands, and he was crying.

Not loudly. Not dramatically. Just tears sliding down an otherwise composed face while his lips moved around some wordless accounting, and Ilse understood with sudden, sickening certainty that this man was not a collector, had perhaps never been a collector, and was standing in front of her brother's last delivery weeping over a stranger's wedding.

She should have called the alarm then. Her hand was on the cord. One pull and the galleries would fill with grey-cloaked hunters, and whatever this boy was—thief, spy, mourner—he would end up in a bottle himself, shelved between the seditionists and the dreamers.

Instead she said, "Step away from the shelf."

He didn't startle this time. He set the bottle down with exaggerated care, the way you'd set down a sleeping child, and turned. "Warden."

"You've been crying over inventory."

"Yes."

"That's a flogging offense."

"I know."

"Then give me one reason I don't pull this cord."

Theo of Hollowmere looked at her for a long moment, and Ilse watched him decide something—watched the decision move through him like weather crossing a field. When he spoke, his voice was low enough that even the humming vaults might have missed it.

"Because I have something for you. And if you pull the cord, you'll never get it."

"I don't take bribes."

"It isn't a bribe. There's no price on it. There's no trade in it. That's—" His voice caught, and he steadied it with visible effort. "That's rather the point of it too."

* * *

They stood in the warden's alcove off the Ninth Gallery, the little stone niche where Ilse took her meals and logged her hours and had spent eleven years learning not to listen. Theo sat on the bench with his hands flat on his knees, and Ilse stood by the door with her baton drawn, because she was not a fool even if she was beginning to suspect she was something worse.

"You have until the bell," she said. "Talk."

"Do you know how I take a memory?" he said. "I don't cut it out. I don't break anything. I just—ask. The mind wants to be held. Most people are so tired of carrying themselves that they hand it over gladly and never know they're lighter." He looked down at his ink-stained fingers. "I spent years selling people back their own worst days. Grieving widows. Guilty fathers. People who'd lost a name or a face or a whole summer and paid everything they had to hold it again." He swallowed. "I told myself it was honest work. Renting back what was already theirs."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're going to think I'm lying, and I want you to hear the truth first so you can compare."

He reached into his coat and brought out a small vial, no bigger than a thumb, sealed with grey wax. Inside it, a memory coiled like smoke—old smoke, road-worn, carrying a faint smell of horses and rain that shouldn't have been possible through glass.

"This is the final memory of a courier named Corren Varn," Theo said.

The frost in Ilse's chest cracked clean through.

She did not move. Eleven years of discipline held her body still while everything inside it tore loose and stampeded. Her brother's name in a thief's mouth. Her brother's death in a thief's hands.

"That memory is evidence," she heard herself say, from very far away. "It belongs to the Regent's archive."

"He gave it to me," Theo said. "With his dying breath. He was wounded—hunters caught him on the valley road, and he crawled to my door because someone told him a memory-thief lived there, and he pressed this into my hands and said *thirteen turns*, and then he died on my floor." His own eyes were wet again. "I didn't steal it, Warden. I wish I had. Stealing would have been easier. He chose me because I was nobody, because no one hunts nobodies, because a thief keeps things safe better than an honest man does. He trusted me with the last thing he had, and I have carried it through everything since—the regalia, the road, the hunters, all of it—because it was his, and because someday it was supposed to be yours."

"How do you know my name?"

"Your brother described you." Theo's voice gentled. "In the memory. He talks about you, near the end. He says—" Theo stopped, and started again. "I won't tell you what he says. It's not mine to tell. It's not anyone's to tell but yours."

Ilse looked at the vial in his open palm, and the vaults hummed around her, ten thousand bottled lives murmuring their low chorus, and for the first time in eleven years she heard them clearly. They sounded like Corren. They sounded like everyone she had shelved and catalogued and refused to mourn. They sounded like a woman laughing herself into a well on her wedding day, and a courier dying in the rain with his sister's name in his mouth, and all the vast unbearable weight of what the Regent called mercy.

"If this is a trick," Ilse said, "if the hunters put you up to this—"

"They tried to kill me four times on the road here. Ask them when you catch them."

"If you ask me for anything after—any key, any favor, any opening—"

"I'm asking for nothing." Theo rose, slow, keeping his hands visible, and held the vial out across the space between them. "That's the whole point of it. Your brother's last gift, delivered free. Take it or leave it. If you leave it, I walk out of here and keep carrying it, and neither of us has to know whether I'm a liar."

The bell rang the quarter hour. Neither of them moved.

Then Ilse crossed the alcove and took the vial, and the glass was warm, which memories in bottles never were, and she understood that warmth was what a thing felt like when it was given instead of taken.

* * *

She drank the memory alone, in the dark of her cell, sitting on the narrow bunk with the door barred and the sigil-banner folded out of sight, because some things deserved privacy even from the Regent's woven eyes.

Corren's last hours opened around her like a door into rain.

He was on the valley road and he was bleeding, and he was thinking, absurdly, about the map sewn into his coat lining and whether wax would hold against sweat. The pain came in waves and between the waves he thought about Ilse. Not the way the record had taught her to remember him—not the traitor, not the cautionary tale—but her brother, whole and stubborn and afraid. He remembered her teaching him to swim in the millpond. He remembered the fight, the real fight, the night before his arrest, when he'd shouted at her that the Regent's mercy was a knife with a pretty handle and she'd shouted back that loyalty was the only thing keeping their family alive, and he remembered—this was the part that broke her—that he had never believed she was wrong. He'd believed she was early. That someday she would stand in the middle of all those shelves and finally hear them, and understand why he'd run.

*I'm sorry I made you choose,* he thought, in the memory, in the rain, watching the lit window of a valley loft come closer through the dark. *I'm sorry the world made choosing cost this much.*

And then, because dying men are honest: *If she ever hears this—Ilse. Little fish. You were right about the pond. I was right about everything else.*

The memory ended where he ended, on a stranger's floor, in a stranger's hands, saying *thirteen turns* to a boy whose face he never learned.

Ilse sat in the dark for a long time. Somewhere above, the citadel turned through its night watches. Somewhere below, ten thousand stolen lives hummed their patient chorus. And in her chest, the frost that had held her upright for eleven years melted all at once, and what rushed in behind it was not relief but grief—true grief, late grief, the grief she should have wept at his execution and had not been permitted—and she let it come, silently, thoroughly, the way water fills a cellar.

When it was done, she was not the same woman who had barred the door. The warden of the Ninth Gallery was still sitting on the bunk. But Ilse, little fish, Corren's sister, had come back from the dead to sit beside her, and she had brought a question with her:

*What have I been guarding?*

* * *

She found Theo at the fourth bell, waiting where she'd left him, in the shadow of the archive stair. He looked up when she came, and whatever he saw in her face made him go very still.

"You knew," Ilse said. "Before you gave it to me. You knew what it would do."

"I hoped."

"And now you want the vaults."

Theo didn't insult her by denying it. "Six regalia. In the deep galleries. My friends are coming for them whether I ask you or not, and if it happens badly, hunters die, wardens die, and the alarms bring half the citadel down on all of us. Or—" He spread his hands. "Or a warden who outranks every guard on these floors opens certain doors, and no one dies tonight, and the Regent loses six treasures without ever hearing a shout."

"And what do I get? Besides a dead brother's love and a lifetime of treason."

"A life," Ilse answered herself, because he was wise enough not to. She almost laughed. "That's what he'd say. *You get a life, little fish. The one you postponed.*"

"Is that a yes?"

Ilse looked down the long throat of the Ninth Gallery, at the witchlit shelves receding into darkness, at the ten thousand murmuring bottles holding every unapproved feeling in nine realms. For eleven years she had called this guardianship. Corren had known better. She had known better too, down in the place where knowing doesn't count.

"It's a yes," she said. "Half of it. The regalia, yes—I'll open those vaults myself. But the memory shelves stay sealed. Those people aren't cargo to be looted. If their pasts come out of here, they come out by warrant, or by law, or by a court that has earned the right to hold them." She fixed him with the look that had cowed collectors for a decade. "Do you understand me, thief? I am not trading one master for another."

For a moment Theo just looked at her, and then he smiled—tired, crooked, real. "A court renewed by consent," he said softly, as if quoting something. "Each piece borne by choice. That's the whole idea, Warden. You just invented it faster than we did."

"I invented nothing. I read a letter." But her voice wasn't steady, and she let it not be.

They went down together, warden and thief, past shelf after humming shelf. Ilse unlocked the first deep gate with her own seal-ring, and the second, and the third, and in the innermost vault, behind wards older than her post, the six regalia waited in their cases like held breath—crownless, scattered pieces of a broken wholeness, gleaming faintly as if they knew.

Theo lifted the first case free.

That was when the bell began to ring.

Not the quarter-hour. Not the watch-change. The great alarm, the one that had not sounded in eleven years, rolling down from the upper citadel in bronze waves, and beneath it, faint but rising, voices—many voices, and running feet, and the shriek of gates being sealed on floors they had already passed through.

Someone had counted the regalia. Someone had counted them days ago.

Ilse slammed the vault shut and turned to Theo, and in the red wash of the alarm-lamps she saw her brother's expression looking back at her from a stranger's face: the particular calm of people who have stopped pretending the odds are good.

"Stairwell's sealed," she said. "There's one other way out. It runs through the memory shelves."

Theo stared at her. "You said those stay shut."

"They stay unlooted." She was already moving, keys singing off her ring. "Nobody said anything about walking through them. Come on, thief—if we're fast, we can be out before the hunters finish sealing the galleries. And Theo—" she threw the word back over her shoulder like a coin tossed to a beggar, except it was worth more than coin, "—when we reach my brother's shelf, we're taking it with us. All of them. Every bottle on that row."

The alarms climbed. The gates boomed. And Warden Ilse Varn, traitor at last and only eleven years late, led the way into the dark.
