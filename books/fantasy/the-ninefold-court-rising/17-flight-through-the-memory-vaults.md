## 17. Flight Through the Memory Vaults

The alarm did not sound like a bell. It sounded like a memory of a bell—a half-remembered chime that rang inside my skull rather than in the air, and I knew at once that it was not calling soldiers to arms. It was calling them to *me*. Somewhere above us, in whatever cold heart the Ashen Citadel kept, someone had just felt four vaults open and had felt, too, the shape of the hand that opened them.

"Run," Ilse said, and there was blood on her lip where she'd struck herself—she had torn her own sigil from her collar, and the wound it left was bleeding light.

We ran.

The vault corridors of the Ashen Citadel were built like a throat: narrow, ribbed with iron, descending in slow spirals into dark. On either side, shelves of bottled pasts rose floor to ceiling—jars of smoked glass, phials of amber and black, stoppered hearts of memories still faintly warm. As we passed they woke. A thousand small voices murmured behind the glass. *I promised her I'd come back. I never told him he was mine. The day the river froze, the day the river froze, the day—* The whole citadel was a city of the stolen dead-alive, and every one of them turned toward me as I passed, because they could feel what I was, too.

"Theo." Sefa's voice came flat and hard from behind me. "Four regalia. Where are they?"

"In here." I patted the satchel across my chest. The Crown was not among them—I'd known that before we went down; Ilse had shaken her head when I asked—but five others had lain in the deep vaults, sleeping under wards that should have burned me to ash. Ilse's blood on the locks had changed that arithmetic. Five had been within reach. Four were now against my spine, humming together like struck bells.

And one was gone.

*The lantern.* Even thinking its name hurt. The Lantern of Unkept Promises, first-found at the drowned chapel, first-lost here—the thing I had touched once and heard my mother's voice through, or something wearing her voice, and I would not leave this place without it even if the Regent herself stood between us and the door.

"It slipped," I said. "When the alarms started. Back toward the third tier."

Sefa closed her eyes for exactly one heartbeat—the length of a curse—and then she was moving. "Then we go back for it."

"We don't have time—"

"We don't have a *choice*." She grabbed my shoulder and spun me around, and her silver-grey eyes, the eyes of a Court knight, were bright with something worse than fear. "That lantern is an oath-vessel. In the Regent's hands it doesn't hold broken promises—it *enforces* them. You've seen what her erasure-hunters do. Now imagine that power bound to every vow anyone ever failed to keep. Imagine the whole realm made liable." She pushed me ahead of her. "Move."

* * *

We went back up through the tiers while the citadel screamed silently around us.

Ilse stayed at the junction of the second stair, her back to the wall, one hand raised—not in farewell but in warning, palm out, reading the air the way she must once have read it as warden. Her brother's face had been the last thing I gave her, his last memory poured freely into her hands, and she had opened the vaults for grief's sake as much as strategy's. Now she stood in the corridor of bottled lives like a woman standing in the wreckage of her own house.

"They're coming down the west shaft," she said. "Six of them. Not hunters—*wardens*. Mine. They'll follow orders before they follow their hearts, so don't give them a chance to choose." Her eyes found mine, and in them I saw the twin of the dying stranger who had bled out in my loft a lifetime and twelve moons ago. "Third tier, east alcove. The lantern will be screaming. Listen for it."

"Screaming?"

"It holds unkept promises, thief. What do you think those sound like?"

I heard it before I reached the alcove. Not a scream, exactly—a keening, thin as wire, the sound of a hundred voices all saying *I meant to, I meant to, I meant to* in a key no throat could reach. The lantern sat on a recovery-sled where a warden had dropped it mid-crisis, its glass fogged with trapped breath, its light guttering the color of old regret.

I reached for it and the moment my fingers closed on the handle, it *knew* me.

Flooded again—the chapel again, the water-stained altar, the woman's voice rising through the oaths like a thread pulled from a hem—*and I swear it by whatever's left of the Ninefold Court, Theo, my Theo, if you ever hear this—* and then the vision tore sideways because Sefa hit the ground beside me hard enough to crack stone, and the lantern's light snapped shut like a door.

"You heard her again," Sefa said. It wasn't a question.

"Not now."

"Theo—"

"*Not now.*"

She let it go. That was the kind of person Sefa was underneath the guilt and the armor: she knew which fights were for later. We took the stairs two at a time, the satchel heavy with four sleeping regalia and the lantern shrieking softly against my hip, and below us, at the junction, Ilse's voice rose to meet six sets of armored feet.

* * *

I will not pretend I understood everything that happened at that junction. Some things you only understand afterward, when you take them out and look at them, and some things you never look at at all.

What I saw, in glimpses, over Sefa's shoulder as we pounded up the final stair toward the gate: Ilse stepping into the center of the corridor, arms wide, warden's sigil burning on her bare chest where she'd torn her uniform open. Six of her own wardens halting, confused, their erasure-tools glowing uncertain grey. Her voice carrying up the throat of the citadel, steady as bedrock: *"Stand down. These vaults were opened by right of grief, and I will answer for it."*

What I heard next was the sound the erasure-tools make when they are used on a willing target.

Because that was it—that was the whole of it, and I understood it even as it happened, even as Sefa dragged me through the gate-arch with my lungs full of broken glass. Ilse knew the wardens' orders. Knew that whoever opened the vaults would be wiped to nothing, hollowed into a docile shell, and that if she fought them they would wipe her anyway and call it justice. So she chose the only version of obedience left to her. She stood down into the grey. She erased herself on her own terms rather than let them do it badly.

The last thing I saw of Warden Ilse was her face going smooth and calm as a frozen lake, and the last thing I heard was her saying, in a voice already forgetting why it spoke: *"Go. He would have wanted—"*

And then nothing. A woman with no reason left to help us, standing in a corridor of other people's stolen years, holding none of her own.

"Ilse—" I started to turn.

"Don't." Sefa's grip on my arm was iron. "If you go back, she erased herself for nothing. *Move.*"

So I moved. I want to be honest about this chapter of my life, and honesty requires me to say: I moved, and part of me was relieved to be made to move, and I have carried that relief like a stone in my boot ever since.

* * *

The gate was a slab of black iron at the top of the great stair, barred from our side by a mechanism older than the citadel itself—Court-work, pre-Ashen, a relic the Regent had never bothered to replace because no one had ever needed to flee her fast enough.

No one had ever been me.

"Can you bar it?" I gasped.

"No." Sefa looked at the mechanism, and I watched her do the same arithmetic I was doing, and arrive at the same terrible sum. "It needs a hand on each lever and time. Time we won't have. They're coming up behind us, and there'll be more at the gatehouse beyond." She stepped onto the mechanism's platform and set her hands on both levers, and the old Court-script flared silver under her palms—recognizing her, recognizing what she had been. "But it will hold if someone stays."

"No." The word came out of me before thought. "No, absolutely not, there has to be—"

"Theo." She said it quietly, and somehow quiet was worse than shouting. "Listen to me. When the Regent took the three seats, I ran. That's the truth of me. I deserted, I survived, I spent eleven years guarding caravans and telling myself the Court deserved to fall because I couldn't bear to admit I'd been a coward. And then I met a thief carrying a map he didn't understand, and I thought—here, finally, here is a debt I can pay. Here is the thing that makes the running mean something." She pulled one lever. The gate groaned, began to descend. "But debts get paid. And when they're paid, what's left is what you were before. I know what's left of me. I'd rather spend what remains of this on the gate than carry it home."

"That's not how debts work," I said stupidly. "You pay them and you're *free*—"

"I'm a knight of the Ninefold Court, boy. Nothing about us was ever free."

Below us, boots on the stair. Many. Fast.

She pulled the second lever, and the gate came down between us like a verdict—iron slats descending, the gap shrinking, her face framed in the narrowing space—and I did the only thing I could think of, the thing I am, the thing I had spent my whole life pretending was only a trick for market-days.

I stole from her.

My gift leapt across the closing gap and I took the desertion-memory entire—the night of the three seats, the throne room, the choice she'd made and the choice she hadn't, eleven years of shame compressed into a single burning instant—and I ripped it out of her and held it, held it like a coal in bare hands, because I could not let her lose it. Because it was hers. Because a person is their worst night as much as their best, and I refused—

The gap was a hand's width. Her eyes widened. She understood.

And she *pushed back*.

I felt it happen—felt her seize the memory in the space between us, felt her pour herself into it, not fighting my theft but completing it, giving it away deliberately, shoving the whole of her shame through the closing gate and into my keeping as the iron met stone with a boom that shook dust from the citadel's bones.

The last I saw of Sefa was her smiling.

The last I heard was: *"Keep it safe. Bring it back when it's over. That's an order, thief."*

* * *

On the far side of the gate, alone in the gatehouse corridor, I fell to my knees with a stranger's worst night burning in my chest.

I could feel it there, distinct from my own memories the way a coin from another country is distinct—same metal, different stamp. *The throne room. Three seats empty and one filling with ash. Sefa, sword drawn, and the moment—the single endless moment—when drawing it would have mattered, and her arm not moving, and the door closing, and eleven years of telling herself there had been no moment at all.* I held it carefully. I wrapped it in the same silence I used for the memories I sold back to the grieving, and I swore an oath over it, an actual oath, the first I'd ever sworn that wasn't a lie:

*I will give this back. Whatever it costs. Whatever I become.*

Behind the iron gate, muffled but unmistakable, came the sound of erasure-tools being discharged—once, twice, a soft grey sighing, like snowfall. Then silence. Then, distantly, a voice with no memory in it at all, asking politely whether anyone had seen a thief.

* * *

I ran.

The gatehouse spat me out onto the citadel's outer ramparts, and the night received me like cold water—the thirteenth moon nearly full overhead, huge and white and wrong, hanging over the ash-grey landscape like an eye that had seen too much. Below the walls, the memory-tithe wagons stood in rows, and beyond them, the dark fields, the road, the miles between me and anywhere.

Four regalia against my spine. One lantern shrieking softly at my hip. Two memories that were not mine—one a dying stranger's map-knowledge, one a knight's worst night—pressed against my heart like coals I had no right to carry.

Five regalia. Eight seats to fill, a crown still missing, a covenant-heart still demanding a price I had known about since the drowned chapel and had not yet said aloud to anyone, including myself.

And somewhere behind me, in a corridor of bottled pasts, two women who had chosen to forget themselves so that I could run.

I went over the wall into the dark, and the thirteenth moon watched me go, and victory tasted in my mouth exactly like ash.
