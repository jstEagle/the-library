## 18. What the Regent Offered Me

The girl who brought the news did not knock. No one knocked in the Ashen Citadel; knocking was a courtesy, and courtesies had been the first thing Vessa Moraine burned when she took her throne.

"Your prisoner," the girl said, "is awake."

*Prisoner.* Vessa turned the word over like a coin she suspected of being clipped. The thief from Hollowmere was not a prisoner in any way that mattered. He was a question walking around in borrowed chains, and questions, in her experience, were more dangerous loose than caged.

She rose from the map table where six regalia lay arranged in a fan of dull light—the lantern with its guttering oath-flame, the ring that drank truth, the mirror, the bell, the chalice, the key. Two empty spaces remained at the fan's edge, shaped like absences. She had looked at those absences for eleven years the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth.

"Has he eaten?" she asked.

The girl blinked. "He— I don't know, Radiance."

"Then find out what he eats, and bring him twice as much." Vessa pulled her grey cloak from its hook. "And send for no one else. This audience is mine alone."

---

They kept him in the north tower, which was not a cell so much as an argument about what a cell should be. A bed with clean linens. A window of thick glass looking out over the ash-fields, where the wind moved the grey drifts in slow tides. A table, a chair, a carafe of water, a book of local histories chosen at random. The door locked, of course. But Vessa had learned long ago that the surest prisons were made of kindness, because kindness made people stay even after the lock rusted open.

He was standing at the window when she entered. Younger than the reports suggested. Thinner. His wrists bore the red rings of manacles he had apparently stopped trying to escape within the first hour—a fact that interested her more than any interrogation could have. A thief who surrendered quickly was either broken or clever, and the eyes that turned to hers were not broken eyes. They were wary, tired, and unbearably awake.

"You're shorter than your statues," he said.

"I never commissioned statues."

"No," he agreed. "But someone did. Three cities' worth. That's the thing about taking a throne—the flattery arrives whether you order it or not."

Vessa closed the door behind her and did not sit. Sitting put her at eye level, and she had found that most people told the truth faster when they had to look up.

"Theo Dunmore," she said. "Of Hollowmere. Memory-thief. Son of—" She paused deliberately, letting the silence do the work. "Well. You'd know better than I would."

Something flickered across his face, quick as a fish under ice. There it was. The wound she had come to press.

"You've read my file," he said.

"There is no file on you. Files are for people whose histories survive contact with my archivists. You are an anomaly, Master Dunmore, and anomalies interest me." She crossed to the table and poured two cups from the carafe, sliding one toward him. "Drink. If I wanted you dead you'd have died in the vaults, along with Warden Ilse's career."

He didn't touch the cup. He also didn't look away from her, which was rarer than courage. Most people who met her gaze were performing bravery. He was simply measuring her, the way he must measure everything—a market stall, a traveler's pocket, a room full of bottled pasts.

"You killed Sefa's memory," he said. "Twice now."

"I erased her *desertion*, once, partially. Your friend keeps choosing to stand between my hunters and other people's minds. I can't be blamed for the geometry." Vessa let a beat pass. "Though I'll admit the second time was wasteful. She was a better knight than anyone I have left."

"She held your gate open so we could run."

"Yes. Ilse reported it with something like admiration. My warden has been difficult since you gave her her brother's last moments. Do you know what she does now, in the evenings? She sits in the vault corridor and talks to a memory-bottle that contains nothing but a dying boy saying his sister's name." Vessa's voice stayed level. "You did that. One gift, freely given, and you unmade eleven years of discipline. I mention this not as accusation but as evidence. You should understand what your talent actually is before this conversation ends."

"And what is it?"

"Not theft." She shook her head slowly. "Anyone can take a memory. Erasure-hunters take them by the thousand. What you do—the way you did it to my hunters on the road, lifting their orders without touching their minds—that isn't theft. It's *return*. You handle memories like they're alive, like they belong somewhere, and you're just the courier between where they are and where they ache to be." She smiled, thin and genuine. "I've spent eleven years among people who treat memory as inventory. It is refreshing, briefly, to speak with a craftsman."

"Why am I still alive?"

"Because I'm going to make you an offer, and I want you alive to refuse it honestly." She watched him absorb that. *Refuse*, she had said, and watched him decide not to pretend he might accept. Good. Honest refusals taught her more than dishonest acceptances ever could.

"But first," Vessa said, "I'm going to show you why the offer is real."

---

She took him down through the citadel's spine, unmanacled, guarded only by her own presence—which was, in fairness, guard enough—and into the deep archive beneath the throne hall. Here the shelves ran farther than lamplight, ranks of glass and lead and bone bottles receding into dark, each one humming faintly with the pressure of a life pressed into storage. Confiscated pasts. Tithe-taken childhoods. Eleven years of the Regent's peace, catalogued and stoppered.

Theo stopped walking three steps in. She heard his breath change.

"How many?" he asked quietly.

"One million, four hundred and six thousand, give or take the month's collections." Vessa walked ahead of him, her boots loud on stone. "Every one taken from a person who signed nothing, consented to nothing, and mostly never learned what they lost. They wake some mornings with a smooth place where something used to be, and their neighbors tell them it was always smooth, because I erased that too."

"Why are you showing me this?"

"Because everyone who opposes me believes I hide this place. Kings hid their dungeons. Priests hid their relics. I show mine." She turned to face him between the shelves, and the bottle-light made her shadow enormous on the far wall. "Because I am going to tell you the truth about the covenant, and you deserve to weigh it against *this*. Not against stories. Against shelves."

She reached out and drew one bottle from its cradle—a small thing, green glass, holding a curl of light like smoke. "This belonged to a cooper's daughter in Marrow Ford. Age nine. It contains the day her father taught her to raise a barrel stave. Her father was conscripted into the Court's ninth levy during the last war and died at a river crossing no chronicle names. Under the covenant, grief of that magnitude was *unstable*. Grief fed resentment; resentment fed oaths; oaths bound power the nine regents had already claimed. So the covenant's law required that traumatic memories exceeding a certain weight be surrendered to the nearest seat—for safekeeping, they said. For harmony." She turned the bottle in her fingers. "For *order*, Master Dunmore. That is the word the old regents used. Order."

"That's not the covenant I've seen," Theo said. "The lantern holds broken promises. The ring answers witnessed truth. Whatever the Ninefold Court was, it ran on memory being *kept*, not caged."

"It ran on both, and that was the cruelty." Vessa set the bottle back with a click that echoed down the aisles. "The covenant demanded that every soul in nine realms carry their whole life, every wound, every betrayal, forever, because the regents needed the harvest of human feeling to feed the binding law. Do you know what the First Accord actually said? Not the version in your valley's half-rotted chronicles. The original, which I read with my own eyes in the Court's heart before I burned the reading room. It said: *the people are the field, and the regents are the reapers, and the harvest shall not fail.*" Her voice did not rise. That was the trick of it; she had learned to deliver horror in the tone of a weather report. "Nine thrones, fed by everyone. And when a village's sorrow grew too rich—when a plague or a war or a bad winter threatened to overflow the binding—it wasn't eased. It was *collected*. Whole towns walked around with holes where their worst days had been, calling it peace, because the alternative was to feel it, and feeling it might crack the law."

"You're lying."

"I have never once lied to an enemy," Vessa said, and meant it, and watched him register that she meant it. "Lying is inefficient. Ask your knight. Ask her what she saw the night I took three seats. She'll tell you I stood in the Court hall and read the First Accord aloud to the assembled regents before I fought them. Sefa deserted that night—not because of what I *did*, but because of what I *said*. She couldn't refute it. She could only refuse to help me, which she did, badly, and has been paying for since."

Theo was silent for a long moment. In the dark between the shelves his face was unreadable, and Vessa reminded herself that unreadable was his native language.

"If all that's true," he said finally, "then what you're building is worse. You didn't end the harvest. You became the only reaper."

"Yes," she said simply. "I did."

No defense. No justification dressed in silk. She let the admission hang there, and saw it land harder than any argument could have. People expected tyrants to prevaricate. The absence of prevarication unsettled them like a stair that isn't there.

"Here is what I learned in eleven years of holding this alone," Vessa continued. "The covenant could not be reformed. I tried—I spent my first three years as the ninth seat petitioning the other eight to soften the tithe-laws, to return the oldest collections, to let the realms grieve openly. Eight regents voted no, eight times, because their power was built on the harvest and mercy was, to them, a structural flaw." She began walking again, deeper into the archive, and he followed, because she had known he would. Curiosity was the strongest chain ever forged. "So I broke it. I took three seats in one night. I have spent eleven years collecting what the other seats scattered, hoarding the regalia, erasing the dissenters—not because I love erasure, Master Dunmore, but because a realm cannot argue its way through a collapse. When the boundary between worlds fails—and it will fail, thirteen turns, twelve gone, your little map has told you as much—everything that lives in the near worlds pours through the wound. Every hungry god. Every unshaped thing. Every nightmare the old courts ever banished. The covenant was the dam. I am trying to build a new one before the water arrives, and a new dam requires *one architect*, because nine architects built us a dam with sluices for their own gardens."

"And the people whose memories fill these shelves? Are they part of the architecture?"

"They are the price." She said it flatly, without flinching, because flinching would have been a lie. "I will return every bottle when the new binding holds. Every one. That is a written decree, posted in this citadel, dated eleven years ago. But I cannot return them *now*, while the wound is open, because returned grief feeds the very chaos that will drown us. Peace first. Restoration after. In that order, or not at all."

Theo stopped walking. He was looking at the shelves—not at her—with an expression she recognized from mirrors: the arithmetic of the impossible, where every column sums to blood.

"You believe all of it," he said at last. "That's the horrible part. You're not lying about anything."

"I am the most honest ruler these realms have had in a thousand years. It has cost me everything, including the right to be loved." Vessa turned to face him fully. "Which brings me to my offer."

---

She led him up out of the archive, past the throne hall with its cold hearth-sized emptiness where the covenant-heart had once burned, and into a small chamber off the map room that few living people had entered. She lit no lamps. She didn't need to. On the chamber's central table, under a dome of grey glass, lay a single memory-bottle unlike all the others—older, warmer, holding light the color of late afternoon through kitchen windows.

"This came to me eight years ago," Vessa said, "from a tithe-collection in the western marches. A woman named Lira Dunmore, recorded in the last year of the old covenant, when the ninth seat's collectors swept the borderlands for unstable griefs. She was carrying a child out of a burning waystation. The child lived. The record shows she gave the collectors the only thing they demanded—her memory of the child's naming—because she believed surrendering it would buy his safety from the hunt that followed. It didn't. The hunters took her anyway, three days later. But the bottle survived, and it passed through four seats' worth of chaos before it reached my archive, where I recognized the surname the moment your courier's map surfaced in Hollowmere."

His breath had gone very careful. She watched his hands—thief's hands, quick hands—go absolutely still at his sides, the way a man goes still when the ground might be trapped.

"Play it," he said. His voice came out sanded down to almost nothing.

"No." Vessa stepped back from the table. "Not yet. Listen first."

She told him. All of it, plainly, in the tone she reserved for truths that needed no ornament: his mother restored whole—not the fragment, the entire woman, reconstructed from this bottle and three others in her collection that touched her life, enough thread to weave back the whole cloth. Returned to him, living memory, her voice saying his name whenever he wished. Hollowmere warded under her personal seal for as long as her line held—no erasure-hunters, no tithe-collectors, the valley free to remember itself forever, Maren and the market and every grieving customer at his stall left untouched. And the map, handed over, intact, so that the last two regalia and the crown could be gathered by the only authority positioned to use them before the thirteenth moon.

"In exchange," Vessa finished, "you walk away. You live in your valley with your mother's voice in your ears, and I save nine realms alone, as I have done for eleven years, and history will call whichever of us it remembers a monster, and it won't matter, because we'll both be dust and the world will still be standing."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, wind moved the ash-fields with its slow tide-sound.

"You said twelve turns gone," Theo said finally. "One moon left. And you're spending the hour bargaining with me instead of gathering what's yours."

"Because the covenant-heart will not burn for coercion. It demands willing sacrifice—you knew that, or your knight did, or the lantern showed you. I cannot force the final binding. I can only hold the pieces and wait for the moon." Her eyes didn't leave his face. "Which means I have exactly enough time for one honest conversation with the one person alive whose sacrifice the heart would accept. Yours. And I would rather convince you than kill you, Master Dunmore, not because I need your permission—but because if you do this freely, the binding lasts. If I take it, it cracks in a century, and we begin again with worse odds."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I keep the bottles. All of them. Including hers." Vessa said it gently, and hated how gently, and said it anyway. "I will not destroy them. I promise you that much, thief to thief. But I will not return them to a drowning world, either."

He looked at the bottle for a long time. She watched him do the thing he evidently always did—measure, weigh, price the goods—and she watched the moment the arithmetic broke. His composure didn't shatter; it *leaked*, quietly, at the corners of his eyes, and he turned his head slightly so she wouldn't see, and failed.

"You're telling the truth," he said hoarsely. "About all of it. The accord, the harvest, her. That's what makes this evil. Anyone can refuse a lie."

"Then refuse me for the right reason," Vessa said. "Tell me what it is. I genuinely want to know. I have offered kings this bargain and they took it. I have offered saints, and they took it too—more quickly than the kings, if you want the ugly truth about sanctity."

He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, and when he looked up, the wariness had burned away into something harder and cleaner.

"Because your peace needs these shelves," he said. "A million bottles. And the day after the binding holds, there'll be a new threat, and a new reason the restoration waits, and new shelves. I've worked markets my whole life, Regent. I know what a debt looks like when the lender never intends to forgive it." He nodded at the dome, at the warm light of his mother's stolen afternoon. "And I know that if I take her back this way—bought with everyone else's smooth places—then every time she says my name, it'll sound like a price tag. I'd rather have one true memory given back to me by friends than a whole woman sold to me by you."

Vessa studied him for a long, quiet moment. Then she inclined her head, fractionally, the acknowledgment one professional gives another across a table.

"So noted," she said. "Guards will return you to the tower. You'll be treated well until the moon turns. After that—" she allowed herself the ghost of a smile, "—after that, neither of us gets to choose anymore."

They took him away. Vessa stood alone in the lampless chamber, looking down through the grey glass at the warm little light that had once been a mother's voice, and permitted herself ten seconds of something she refused to name.

Then she straightened, and rang for her captains, and began issuing orders for the march to the Court's heart—because if the thief would not sell her his sacrifice, she would simply have to arrange circumstances in which he had nothing left to lose but the giving.

Far to the south, over the drowned chapel and the hospice mountain and the city with no name, the twelfth moon was setting. Behind it, thin as a knife's edge, pale as an opening eye, the thirteenth was already climbing.
