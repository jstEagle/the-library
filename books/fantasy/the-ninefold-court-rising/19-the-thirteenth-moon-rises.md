## 19. The Thirteenth Moon Rises

The road to the Court's heart had been a river once. Sefa could still see it in the land's bones—the wide stone channel running dry between its banks, the ferry-stones drowned in silt, the way the whole country sloped gently toward a center it no longer remembered having. They followed the dead river through the last hours of the twelfth moon's dying light, five regalia wrapped in oiled cloth and strapped to three backs, and behind them the horizon burned with the color of pursuit.

"They'll come up the eastern spur," she said, not slowing. "Ilse will slow them at the vault gates, but not for long. Not against her."

"The sister," Theo said. He'd gone quiet since the citadel, quieter even than his usual silence, which Sefa had learned was never empty. "You think she'll fight us?"

"I think grief makes strange soldiers." Sefa adjusted the strap across her shoulder where the ring sat against her sternum, cold as a held breath. "I fought for the Ashen Regent for six years because I believed I deserved to lose something. Ilse just learned what she lost. That's worse."

Ahead of them, the dry riverbed curved north around a ridge of black granite, and there—Sefa stopped walking without deciding to—the Court rose out of the evening.

She had seen it whole, once. She had been nineteen and newly sworn, standing on the western terrace while the nine banners snapped in a wind that smelled of every season at once, and the Court had hummed beneath her boots like a struck bell that never finished ringing. What stood there now was the bell's ghost. The great hall still held its shape—nine walls meeting under a crown of open sky—but the walls were ash-grey where they should have rung with color, one for each seat, and the space where the tenth wall should have risen, the wall of the covenant-heart itself, was simply absence. A doorway of nothing. You could see stars through it.

"Gods," Theo breathed beside her.

"No," Sefa said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

Theo felt the regalia before he saw the Court—a pull in his blood like the moment before a memory comes loose, that sweet sick lurch when someone else's past rises in your hands. Five of them now: the lantern riding against his hip with its cargo of broken vows, the ring, the chalice Sefa carried, the mirror and the blade wrapped together because they refused to be separated. Each one tugged at him differently, the way different voices tug in a crowded market, and all five tugged toward the grey hall on the ridge.

And the hall tugged back.

He understood, walking the dead riverbed with the thirteenth moon swelling fat and wrong above the eastern hills, why Hollowmere had been silent. The valley wasn't magic-dead. It was *amputated*. This was the wound, and the valley was scar tissue grown over it, and everything he'd ever stolen or sold or hoarded had been echoes bleeding off the cut.

He had known since the chapel. He hadn't let himself finish knowing.

The lantern had shown him, that first night among the drowned candles—a woman's voice, warm and cracked at the edges, making an oath he'd never heard the end of. *I will give him back everything I took from the world, if it asks. Everything. Even the part of me that—* And then the vow broke off, unfinished, the way most vows break off, and Theo had spent four moons telling himself the missing words didn't matter.

They mattered. The covenant-heart demanded a willing sacrifice of memory. He'd read it in the map's cold script, heard it in Maren's frightened fragments, watched it confirmed in the second regent's clouded eyes. Not blood. Not death. Memory—freely given, wholly given, the self poured out into the binding so the law would have something human to hold onto.

*Everything I took from the world.*

His mother had known. His mother had maybe done it already, once, for a covenant that failed anyway.

"You've gone somewhere," Sefa said beside him.

"Nowhere new." He made himself smile. It came out crooked. "Just ahead."

Sefa looked at him for a long moment with those desert-grey eyes, and he saw her do the arithmetic—he'd gotten good enough at people to watch it happen—and decide not to say what she'd concluded. She put her hand briefly on his shoulder instead, gauntleted, heavy, almost gentle.

"When we get there," she said, "whatever you're planning to carry alone—you won't have to."

* * *

They crested the ridge as the moon broke free of the hills.

It was wrong. Everyone felt it. Not larger than a full moon should be, exactly, but *closer*, hanging low and white-gold over the grey Court like an eye opening after a long illness, and beneath it the absent tenth wall threw no shadow because absence casts nothing. The air tasted of iron and rain that hadn't fallen yet.

"We need the crown," Sefa said, scanning the dark approaches. "And we need—"

"The heart," Theo said. "I know."

"The Regent holds the crown herself. She'll be there. She'll have brought everything she has, and Theo—" Sefa turned to face him fully. "There are three of us. There are nine seats' worth of power in that hall, and most of it is hers."

"Not most." The voice came from the darkness below the ridge, level and old and familiar. "Some."

Maren climbed the slope toward them with a staff she had absolutely not needed to walk with forty years ago, and behind her, emerging from the shadow of the dead riverbank like figures stepping out of a story, came seven others. Old women and old men, mostly. One child. A smith Theo recognized from two valleys over. They wore traveling clothes and no sigils at all, and they moved like people who had spent their whole lives being careful and had finally, collectively, decided to stop.

"Maren?" Theo's voice cracked on the name. "What are you—how did you—"

"Hollowmere remembers more than it says." The old woman reached them, breathless, triumphant. "We were founded by exiles, boy. Do you think we forgot how to leave? We've been walking since you did." She looked past him at the Court and her jaw tightened. "Nine families founded this valley. Nine lines that fled the last war. Every one of us carries a piece of the old covenant in our blood, and every one of us is tired of hiding in a scar."

"The neutral regents," Sefa said slowly. "The ones who wouldn't answer our petition."

"Answered it in person." Maren smiled, sharp as a market-day bargain. "Took us a while. We're old."

Seven of them. Three regents—Sefa named them under her breath, the Lady of the Western Fords, the Keeper of Roads, the Tallyman—with their heirs and witnesses. Four seats remained empty. Eight regalia present, counting the crown wherever it lay in the Regent's keeping. One absent entirely: the covenant-heart, which was not a thing you carried but a thing you became.

And above them all, the thirteenth moon climbed toward its height.

* * *

The Regent was waiting in the hall, of course. She had always been waiting; that was her nature now, the way roots are waiting, the way winter waits.

Theo walked into the Court of Nine Walls with the lantern in his hand and felt the building recognize the regalia one by one, a shiver passing through the ash-grey stones like feeling returning to a numb limb. The five pieces sang against each other in frequencies he couldn't hear but could feel in his teeth. Ahead, beneath the open crown of sky and directly before the doorway of nothing that had been the tenth wall, stood the Ashen Regent.

She looked smaller than her legend. Grey-haired, plain-robed, her hands folded like a schoolteacher's. Only her eyes burned—not silver like the dying courier's, but banked-coal orange, embers that had never been allowed to go out.

"Five regalia," she said. Her voice filled the hall effortlessly. "Three defectors. Seven exiles. And the thief." Her gaze settled on Theo with terrible gentleness. "Do you know what I offered you? Do you know what I'm prepared to offer again?"

"I know," Theo said. "And I know it's true. That's the worst part. Everything you promised me was true."

"Then—"

"But it required erasing everyone else." He lifted the lantern. Light spilled from it, unkept promises burning gold. "Your peace only works if some people don't get to exist. That's not peace. That's just a smaller world, and you holding the door."

Something moved behind the Regent's calm—grief, maybe, or its fossil. Behind her, at the hall's far entrance, ash-grey soldiers filed in with the warden Ilse at their head, and Ilse's face was wet and set and pointed, unmistakably, at Theo.

"Ilse," he said clearly. "Your brother's memory is yours whether I live or die. I gave it freely. It stays given."

Ilse faltered. One step. Two. The soldiers behind her looked at each other.

Above them, the thirteenth moon touched the crown of open sky, and every stone in the Court began, very softly, to ring.

"The heart demands a sacrifice," the Regent said, and for the first time her composure bent, and Theo heard underneath it not malice but pleading—the plea of someone who had built a cathedral out of a mistake and could no longer see the door. "It has always demanded it. Give me the map, give me your hoard, and no one else ever has to pay. Isn't that mercy?"

"It's a bill sent to one person forever." Theo stepped forward, toward the doorway of nothing, and felt all eight regalia turn toward him like flowers toward sun. "You taught me something without meaning to. In your citadel, in your vaults. Memories aren't currency. They're not weight. They're *belonging*—and belonging can't be taken, only given."

He turned to look at Sefa. At Maren. At the exiles of Hollowmere standing in the Court their ancestors fled.

"I've known since the chapel," he said quietly. "Since her voice. Since *everything I took from the world*."

Sefa's hand flew to her mouth.

"So here's my counteroffer," Theo said, and walked toward the tenth wall, and the moon leaned down to listen.
