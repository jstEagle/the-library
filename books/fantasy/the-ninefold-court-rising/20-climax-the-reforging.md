## 20. Climax: The Reforging

The Court's heart had no roof.

Theo stood in the open circle of the ruined hall and looked up at the thirteenth moon hanging low and swollen above the shattered dome, so close it seemed he could have reached up and pressed his palm against its pocked silver face. Around him, the Ninefold Court lay broken exactly as the map had promised—nine thrones arranged in a ring of black stone, eight of them cracked or toppled or half-swallowed by creeping moss, and one at the northern arc still whole. Still waiting. The Ashen Regent's throne, raised from the ruin like a splinter driven into a wound.

Five regalia hung against his chest, wrapped in oilcloth and bound with Sefa's belt. He could feel them through the fabric—not warm, not cold, but *aware*, the way a sleeping animal is aware of the hand that might wake it. The lantern. The ring. The chalice they'd pulled from the flooded crypt beneath Vell. The mirror that showed only what had been forgotten. The small iron bell that Ilse had pressed into his hands as the alarms howled through the citadel corridors, her brother's last memory still shining wet on her face.

Behind him, boots on stone. Sefa, limping, one arm cradled against her ribs where the gate mechanism had caught her. Behind her, the three neutral regents—the old river-queen Ysolde with her crown of woven reeds, the twin stewards Brann and Corrin moving in their eerie unison, and the hedge-witch Maren had spoken of once, decades ago, as if telling a ghost story: the Lady of Small Hours, who smelled of woodsmoke and rain.

And across the ring of thrones, alone, the Ashen Regent.

She was smaller than Theo had imagined. That was the first obscenity of her—that she should be small, grey-cloaked, her face lined and human and tired, when everything she had unmade was so vast. Her erasure-hunters were nowhere to be seen. She had dismissed them, he realized. Or they had simply become unnecessary.

"The courier's boy," she said. Her voice carried without effort, the way a bell's note carries. "The memory-thief from the dead valley. Do you know what I felt when my hunters reported you? Not fear." She tilted her head, considering him the way a scholar considers a footnote. "Recognition."

"I'm not like you," Theo said.

"No. You're exactly like me. You take what others cannot bear to lose, and you decide what it is worth." She gestured, and the air between them shivered; for a heartbeat Theo saw the hall as it must have been nine hundred years ago—nine regents seated, banners of nine colors, the covenant-heart burning in a brazier at the center like a captive star—and then it was ruin again. "I have watched you since Hollowmere, Dunmore. Every step you've taken has been a step toward me. You steal memories. You sell them back. You hoard them. You are the tithe-collector of your own small empire." A pause, almost gentle. "I am simply honest about it."

"You erased a city," Theo said. His voice came out flat, which surprised him. He'd expected rage. What he felt instead was something colder and more durable. "There were children there. You didn't just kill them—you made it so no one would ever grieve them."

"I freed them," the Regent said, and here it was, the terrible thing he had heard about her even before Sefa spoke her name with loathing: *she believes*. "Grief is a chain, boy. Memory is the lock. The Ninefold Covenant bound every soul in every realm to nine masters who decided what oaths were sacred, what debts were owed, what truths must be remembered unto the seventh generation. I broke that. Yes, there was damage. Surgery damages. But I am cutting out the infection, and when the bleeding stops, the realms will be *free*—free of inherited feuds, free of sworn servitude, free of every promise some dying king chained his grandchildren to." She spread her hands. "A world without memory is a world without masters. Isn't that what a thief would want?"

Theo thought of Hollowmere. Of travelers walking out of the valley lighter than they came, smiling vaguely, not knowing what they'd lost. Of Maren, who kept her stories alive by telling them aloud every night whether anyone listened or not. Of the woman in the drowned chapel whose broken vows had poured through him like ice water—one vow made in a voice he knew better than his own heartbeat.

*A world without memory isn't freedom,* he thought. *It's just a bigger valley.*

"No," he said aloud.

He unwrapped the regalia.

They came free of the oilcloth slowly, reluctantly, five objects that did not belong together and yet hummed toward one another like separated notes of a single chord. The lantern's flame burned green and oath-shaped inside its glass. The ring turned on his finger of its own accord, seeking truths. He laid them on the cracked stone before the covenant-heart's empty brazier, one by one, and each time he set one down the moonlight thickened, the air grew heavier, and somewhere beneath the flagstones something vast turned over in its sleep.

"Four seats remain empty," Ysolde said behind him, her reed-crown whispering against itself. "Six regalia found, but the crown and the heart are the Regent's keeping. Boy, if you mean to bargain, know that she holds two pieces and we hold five. The arithmetic favors us."

"It doesn't," Theo said. "Because the heart doesn't want to be *held*."

He looked at the Regent. She had gone very still, and very watchful, and he understood suddenly that this was the moment she had been waiting for—not the fight, not the siege, but this: someone standing in the Court's heart holding all the pieces, about to say aloud what they intended to do with them. Whatever he said next, she would answer with nine hundred years of conviction.

"So here's my offer," Theo said. "Not to you. To the Court."

He knelt and pressed both palms flat against the stones.

"I know what you are," he said to the sleeping thing beneath the ruin. "Sefa told me. The covenant-heart isn't a jewel or a sword. It's an agreement that learned to be alive. It wants what it always wanted—a binding. Nine regents, nine regalia, one law. And the price of waking it is a willing sacrifice of memory. Someone gives it *everything they carry*, freely, and the law burns new."

"Which is why it will never wake again," the Regent said softly. "No one is fool enough—"

"I am."

The word fell into the silence like a stone into a well, and Theo heard it echo all the way down.

Sefa moved. He heard her armor scrape as she started forward and then stopped herself, fist clenched around nothing, because she knew—gods, she'd known since the lantern, since he'd come out of that chapel white-faced and shaking and unable to explain—he'd known too, since chapter seven of his own life, since a woman with his mother's voice swore an oath she never kept and the lantern drank it down. The covenant demanded a willing sacrifice of memory. Not a piece. Not a tithe. Everything.

"Theo." Sefa's voice cracked on his name. "You don't have to. We can find another way. We can—"

"There isn't another way," he said, and smiled at her, and hated how much it cost to make the smile work. "You held the gate for me. You lost—" He stopped. "This is mine to hold."

He turned back to the Regent, because there was one more thing, and it mattered more than anything else he would say tonight.

"And here's what you never considered," he said. "You think the choice is yours or theirs. One throne or nine masters. Erase everyone or bind everyone. But the covenant wasn't cruel because it *bound*, Regent. It was cruel because it bound people who never consented—children inheriting their grandfathers' oaths, peasants born into a king's sworn debt. So we renew it differently." He lifted the ring from the stones; it warmed against his skin like a living thing. "Each regalia borne by choice. Each seat filled by someone who *wants* it, who can set it down whenever the burden breaks them, and pick it up again only if they choose to return. No inheritance. No compulsion. A covenant renewed by consent—or it doesn't renew at all."

For the first time, the Ashen Regent looked uncertain.

"That's not binding," she said. "That's—volunteering. Laws that can be refused are no laws at all. The realms will tear themselves apart within a generation."

"Maybe," Theo said. "Or maybe people keep promises better when they're allowed to stop keeping them. You never tested it. You saw cruelty in the old covenant—which was real, I believe you—and instead of healing it you burned the whole body." He shook his head. "You're not the cure, Regent. You're the fever."

Her face went grey as her cloak. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped its bell-note and become simply a tired woman's voice, and somehow that was worse. "If you wake it on your terms, I lose everything. My hunters. My order. My *purpose*."

"Yes," Theo said gently. "That's what losing looks like. Most of us don't get to do it to other people instead."

She said nothing more. Perhaps she could not.

The moon stood directly overhead now. Thirteen turns, twelve gone. Below the flagstones, the covenant-heart stirred like a great animal smelling rain, and Theo felt it reaching for him—not greedily, not hungrily, but the way a drowning man reaches, the way something that has waited nine hundred years in the dark reaches for the first hand offered.

He looked at the regalia one last time. Then he closed his eyes and opened himself the way he had opened himself ten thousand times over ten thousand strangers in Hollowmere's market—except this time he was not taking.

He was giving.

It began with the small things, and he was grateful for that. The taste of stolen apples behind the tannery. The weight of his first purse of coin, heavy and shameful and thrilling. The sound of Maren's door creaking at midnight when he came to ask his questions. The market square in rain, awnings drumming, a traveler's laughter slipping loose from his mind like a fish off a line. Each memory left him cleanly, without pain, the way a bird leaves an open cage, and each one landed somewhere below the stones and glowed.

Then the larger things. Sefa's blade at his throat on the road east, and the strange honor in her eyes when she chose not to kill him. Ilse's face when her brother's last moments returned to her. The city that forgot its name, and the survivors outside its walls receiving their fragments back one by one, weeping over shards. The hospice in the mountains, and the second regent's eyes going clear as winter water as she said, *not freedom—a single throne.* The bridge of small debts. The drowned chapel. The map burning cold against his skin the night a stranger died in his loft whispering *thirteen turns.*

Deeper. His mother.

He tried to hold that one. Just for a moment longer. Just to feel it once more—the voice, the name she'd given him, the warmth of a memory he'd bought in a black market stall and treasured like contraband ever since. He felt it rise in him, golden and small, and he thought: *this is the price. This is the whole price. Everything else was practice for this.*

*I never knew you,* he told the memory silently. *But you named me. That's enough. That was always enough.*

He let it go.

The grief hit him a half-second after the loss, which was a strange mercy—he felt the shape of the wound without remembering what had been cut away. He was crying and did not know why. His hands were still flat on the stones. Somewhere far away, Sefa was shouting his name, and he loved the sound of it without knowing entirely who she was.

And then the Court woke.

It did not blaze so much as *breathe*. Light came up through the flagstones like dawn coming up through the floor of the world, nine-colored, ancient, gentle. The cracked thrones knit themselves whole with sounds like distant bells. Moss retreated. The ruined dome rebuilt itself overhead out of moonlight and law, arch upon luminous arch, until the hall stood complete for the first time in nine centuries—and at its center, where the brazier had stood empty, the covenant-heart rose: not a jewel, not a fire, but a figure of light shaped like nine hands joined at the wrist, turning slowly, radiant, awake.

The regalia lifted from the stones on their own. Six of them—no, seven, because the crown tore itself free of the Regent's keeping with a sound like a scream of released iron and flew across the hall to join the others, and she cried out as it went, and Theo understood that her power had been in that crown the way a parasite lives in a host, and it was leaving her now, unraveling thread by thread. The eighth regalia, the covenant-heart itself, blazed at the center of the joined hands. The ninth—

The ninth was the memory he had given. It burned in the heart's core, brighter than all the rest, and Theo felt it there—felt *himself* there, every stolen apple and every broken vow and a woman's voice saying a name—and the Court accepted it, and the light steadied, and the law of the world quietly, irrevocably changed.

Nine seats. Nine bearers, by choice. One binding law, written fresh: *no oath shall bind the unwilling, and none shall be unmade from memory.*

Across the hall, the Ashen Regent collapsed to her knees. The grey drained out of her cloak, out of her skin, out of the air around her, and what remained was an old woman in a plain dress, hollowed and mortal and staring at her own empty hands. The erasure she had spent on thousands of souls came home to roost in her alone—not wiped away, Theo sensed, but *remembered*, every act she'd committed rising in her mind at once, hers to carry now with no power to burn it off.

"Oh," she whispered. "Oh, it's so *heavy*."

"I know," Theo said. He was still crying. He still didn't know why, and the not-knowing was the heaviest thing he owned. "Now you do too."

Ysolde's reed-crown had become a crown of living green. She descended the steps of her restored dais and looked down at the woman who had broken the world, and her face held neither mercy nor vengeance but something older than either. "The Court will judge you," she said. "Not erase you. *Remember* you. Every realm will know your name and what you did with it, unto the seventh generation. That is the new law's first sentence."

The Regent bowed her head. It was, Theo thought, the first honest thing he had ever seen her do.

The light-figure at the center turned its nine joined hands toward him then—all of them, at once, the way a sunflower turns—and though it had no face he felt it looking at him with something like gratitude, and something like apology, and something like a question he was too emptied-out to hear.

Sefa reached him first. She knelt in the blazing light, heedless of it, and took his face in both scarred hands, and her eyes were bright and furious and afraid.

"What did you give it?" she demanded. "Theo. *What did it take?*"

He searched himself and found—rooms. Whole floors of himself standing open and bare, dustless, swept clean. He knew her name was Sefa. He knew she had held a gate for him. He knew she was crying, and that he wanted, with a ferocity that needed no memory to sustain it, to make her stop.

"I don't remember," he said honestly. "Tell me who I was?"

She laughed, or sobbed, or both, and pressed her forehead against his.

Above them the thirteenth moon began, at last, to wane.

* * *

Later—hours later, or minutes; time had gone soft at the edges—the three neutral regents convened in the ring of thrones to begin the long work of choosing bearers, and the Court's light settled into a steady glow like a hearthfire, and Theo sat on the steps of the southern dais with Sefa's cloak around his shoulders, watching the covenant-heart turn.

It was still looking at him.

As the others argued quietly about seats and succession, the nine joined hands rotated, slow as a compass needle, until they pointed at him again. And in the deepest chamber of his emptied self, in the place where his mother's memory had burned brightest before he gave it away, Theo felt something being *kept*—held safe, held close, not taken but tended, the way Maren tended her stories by telling them aloud every night.

The heart pulsed once, gently, like a lantern relit.

And somewhere inside it, patient as dawn, a woman's voice waited to say his name.
