## 21. After the Light

The Court did not burn her. That was the first thing Sefa understood about the world they had made.

She stood in the gallery of the renewed hall—rebuilt in a single night by nine regalia's consent, its pillars grown from light the way crystal grows from water—and watched them bring Vessa Ashen to the floor of judgment. Not in chains. The Regent walked freely between two honor guards who could not have stopped her if she'd wished it, and everyone in the hall knew it, and that was part of the sentence too: to walk unarmed among people who remembered everything you had done.

Sefa's hands ached. They had ached since the gate at the citadel, since the moment she'd planted her feet in the doorway and let the erasure-hunters' art pour into her like cold lead. The desertion-memory was gone again—the night she'd turned her back on her oath, the faces of the three regents whose seats fell while she rode away. Twice taken now. She kept reaching for it out of habit, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth, and kept finding smooth nothing where the wound used to live.

Maybe that was mercy. She didn't trust it.

* * *

They gave Vessa Ashen a chair. Sefa almost laughed when she saw it—a plain wooden chair, set in the center of the hall where the covenant-heart had blazed, and the Regent sat down in it like a woman taking her place at a table she still believed was hers.

Nine regents faced her. Seven of them were new—chosen in the weeks since the reforging, each bearing one of the regalia not by blood or conquest but because their realms had named them and the regalia had agreed. Sefa recognized two of them. One had been a ferry-woman on the Greywater who'd spent thirty years carrying refugees past the Regent's checkpoints without ever being caught. Another was barely older than Theo, a shepherd from the high passes who had touched the ring of witnessed truth during the choosing and wept for an hour before accepting it.

The ninth seat remained empty. It would remain empty until someone the Court trusted enough to hold the Regent's own burden stepped forward. No one had. Sefa suspected no one would for a long time.

The eldest of the seven, the ferry-woman—Regent Amara now, though she flinched at the title—rose and spoke the charge.

"Vessa of the Ashen Seat. You are accused not of breaking the Ninefold Covenant." A murmur ran through the hall; Sefa felt it pass through her like wind through wheat. "Breaking the Covenant was within your right. Every seat holds the right of refusal—that law predates all others. You are accused of what you did after. Of unmaking the memories of cities. Of hollowing dissenters rather than answering them. Of building peace on the graves of recollection."

"I freed them," Vessa said. Her voice had not lost its terrible calm. Sefa remembered it from the war years, from councils where the ninth seat had spoken last and softest and been obeyed. "I showed you the Covenant's teeth. I showed you what your beloved binding law did to the small folk of the realms—the tithe-memories, the conscripted oaths, the children bound to regalia before they could walk. I broke a slavery."

"You replaced it with another," Amara said. "Yours."

"Yes," Vessa said simply. And there it was—the thing Sefa had never been able to hate cleanly about her, the thing that had made desertion feel like betrayal even as it felt like survival. The Regent did not lie. She believed. She stood in the wreckage of everything she'd unmade and believed it had been rescue. "And mine was kinder. Ask the cities I spared. Ask anyone who remembers—which is, admittedly, fewer than it should be."

A ripple of anger moved through the hall. Someone shouted for erasure—give her what she gave, let her feel the void. Others called for death. The old grammar of justice, Sefa thought. Blood and forgetting, the only two sentences the old Court had ever known how to pronounce.

Amara raised her hand, and the hall quieted, because the lantern of unkept promises hung above her shoulder on a chain of light, and its glow made every voice sound like an oath.

"The renewed Court pronounces neither sentence," Amara said. "We do not erase. We do not execute. We do something older than both, and harder."

She looked at Vessa Ashen for a long moment.

"We remember you. Wholly. Publicly. Forever."

* * *

Sefa hadn't understood, at first, what that meant. None of them had, until they began.

There was no dungeon. There was instead a hall annexed to the Court's eastern wing, open to any traveler who wished to enter, and in it the full record of Vessa Ashen's reign was woven into the walls—not as text, but as memory. The covenant-heart's residue, the regalia's witness, the testimony of thousands who came forward once it was safe to remember aloud. Visitors walked the hall and lived her reign from inside it. They saw the first city she erased through the eyes of a woman who died in it holding her daughter's hand. They saw the erasure-hunters' training yards through the eyes of a boy who'd volunteered because his family had starved under the old tithe-laws and the Regent promised him purpose. They saw her genuine grief at the Covenant's cruelties, her real conviction, the moments she'd wept alone over the cost—all of it, the whole architecture of a soul that had decided the ends devoured the means and called it mercy.

Not erased. Remembered. Available forever, to anyone, including herself.

They brought Vessa to the hall on the first day and let her walk it. Sefa went to watch, and hated herself a little for it, and went anyway.

The Regent moved slowly down the corridor of her own crimes. At the third panel—the drowned chapel country, the village of Lowmark, four hundred souls returned to silence—she stopped. Sefa saw her raise a hand toward the memory of a farmer she'd ordered hollowed for organizing resistance, a man named Oskell whom Sefa had once drunk with in a border tavern, back when Sefa was still a knight and still lying to herself about what the Court was becoming.

"He wrote letters," Vessa said quietly. To no one. To the wall. "Eleven of them. I read every one. He was funnier than my ministers. I told myself his humor proved he was being manipulated." Her hand lowered. "It proved he was human. I knew that. I knew it, and I—"

She didn't finish. She didn't need to. The walls finished it for her, replaying the order she'd given, in her own voice, calm as ever: *Hollow him. Leave the hands. He can still carry stones.*

Vessa Ashen sank to her knees in the hall of her remembering, and no guard moved to help her up, and none was required to leave her down. That was the sentence. She would kneel there or stand there or walk there, every day, for as long as she lived, in a house built entirely of what she had done, with every door open, forever.

Death would have been kinder. Erasure would have been far kinder. The renewed Court offered neither, because Theo Dunmore had stood at the covenant-heart with his mother's memory burning in his hands and told them, in a voice that shook and did not stop, that a peace requiring forgetting was just a slower way of killing everyone.

Sefa left before the Regent wept. Some things a person should not watch, even when the person has earned the watching.

* * *

They came for her three days later.

She was in the courtyard behind the travelers' hospice, running forms with a practice blade because her body didn't know what to do with peace and she didn't trust it to learn. Dawn light on grey stone. Somewhere beyond the walls, the city that had forgotten its name was remembering itself street by street—Theo had spent weeks there before the final march, pressing fragments back into survivors, and now those survivors' children were chalking names on doorposts, guessing, arguing, building a history out of secondhand scraps and stubbornness. It sounded like a market. It sounded like a life.

"Sera of the Ninth Company." The voice belonged to Regent Amara, and Sefa noted, with distant irritation, that she'd stopped mid-form to face it. Old habits. Knights straightened when power entered the room; that reflex had been trained into her bones by the very institution she'd deserted. "Or should I say Knight-Captain Sefa Vaile, restored to the rolls?"

"Don't," Sefa said.

Amara held out a scroll sealed with nine colors. Sefa looked at it the way she'd once looked at a snake in the road: with respect, and no intention of touching it.

"You know what this is."

"A summons to investiture." Sefa wiped the practice blade and slid it home. "The Ninth Seat."

"The Court voted unanimously. The regalia concurred—the sword itself has asked for you. It remembers you carried it honorably for eleven years." Amara's voice was gentle, which made it worse. "You deserted in the dark hour, yes. But you came back in a darker one. You held a gate against hunters so a thief could save the world, and it cost you the memory of why you left, and you've never once asked us to restore it, because you think you deserve the loss." She paused. "That is either penance or pathology, Knight-Captain, and either way, the Court believes you belong in the ninth chair. Fill the seat the Regent poisoned. Redeem it."

The scroll hung in the air between them. Sefa found she was gripping the hilt of her practice blade hard enough to whiten her knuckles, and made herself let go.

"May I tell you what I remember?" she said.

Amara waited.

"I remember fragments around the hole. The night before I deserted, I ate supper with a young knight named Pell, who thought the Covenant was sacred and would have died for it. Two days later his seat's regent was ash and Pell was reassigned to the hunters, and I never learned what happened to him, because I was already gone." Sefa's voice stayed level. It took work. "I don't remember leaving. I don't remember the moment I chose. But I remember that everything before the hole felt like standing in a burning house insisting the smoke was weather. Whatever I forgot, I suspect I forgot it because some part of me understood that if I remembered deserting clearly—if I remembered it as *right*—I'd never be able to sit in any chair again and call it service."

"And now?"

"Now I've seen what chairs do." She nodded eastward, toward the hall of remembering, where a woman knelt daily in the museum of her own soul. "You want me to redeem the ninth seat. Maybe it can be redeemed. But not by me, and not yet, and not by anyone who takes it because a unanimous vote made it easy." She met Amara's eyes. "Theo taught me something. The fool. The thief. He stood in front of the covenant-heart and gave away every stolen thing he owned—including the only memory of his mother he had—and do you know what he said afterward? He could hardly speak, the price had just torn through him, and he said, *it should cost like this. It should always cost like this, or people will spend other people instead.*"

Amara was quiet a long time. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled—a ferry-woman's smile, tired and river-worn.

"He said that, did he? Before or after he vomited?"

"After." Sefa found herself smiling back despite everything. "During, actually. Between waves."

"That," Amara said, "sounds like a man who should advise the Court."

"He'd hate it. Ask him anyway." She hesitated, then took the scroll—not accepting, just taking—and broke the seal, and read the formal words of offer, and then held it over the brazier by the courtyard wall and let it catch. The nine-colored wax bubbled and went black. "Tell the Court the ninth seat stays empty until someone comes to it hungry and uncertain and a little afraid. That's the only kind of person I'd trust with it now. Tell them a knight who served eleven years and deserted once recommends doubt as a qualification."

"They will call this arrogance."

"They will call it worse." Sesa shrugged. "Let them remember that too."

* * *

She found Theo on the ramparts at dusk, sitting with his legs dangling over the drop, watching the road south. He looked smaller without his hoard. Everyone said that—said it carefully, the way people speak near a convalescent—but it was true. The boy who'd worked Hollowmere's market had been all edges, competence sharpened over emptiness, quick eyes cataloguing everything because he couldn't afford to be catalogued. This one sat loose-jawed and quiet, watching the light go gold on the hills, and when Sefa sat beside him he leaned sideways until his shoulder rested against hers, casual as breathing, as if belonging to people were a thing he'd always known how to do.

"How's the head?" he asked.

"Holed. Peaceful. Suspicious of both." She looked at him. "How's yours?"

"Empty." He said it easily, and then, because he was honest now in a way he hadn't had memories enough to afford before: "It's strange. I keep waiting to grieve her properly and it won't come. You can't mourn someone you don't remember mourning. It's like—" he gestured vaguely at the horizon— "like a room in a house I know is there because the floor plan says so, but the door opens onto wall." He was quiet a moment. "Maren wrote. From Hollowmere. Did I show you?"

"She said the valley's wards are down," Sefa said. "First time in living memory. Half the town wants to rebuild them and half wants to tear the gates open and see what's been passing them by for two hundred years."

"Maren wants to tear them open." His mouth curved. "She also wrote—" he stopped, and started again, and Sefa understood suddenly that this was hard for him, that honesty about hope was the last muscle he'd built. "She wrote that the town talked. About me. About what I did out here. And they decided—" his voice did something complicated— "they decided that since I gave my past away to save theirs, the least they could do is give it back. Piece by piece. Stories. Letters. Things people remember about a boy who picked pockets in the market and always, always gave too much change to the widow Ostrey and pretended it was an accident." He laughed, wet-eyed. "There's apparently a lot. I was a menace. I was also apparently loved, which—I didn't know that. Couldn't have known that. Nobody tells a thief they're loved; it ruins the trade."

"So you're going."

"I'm going." He looked south, down the long road toward the valley that had hidden him and shaped him and never once let him see the sky beyond it. "Sefa says the fool who saved the Ninefold Court is walking home to a town of farmers who'll feed him stew and correct his biography. It'll be the hardest thing I've ever done."

"It's a good plan," Sefa said. "Terrible name, though. 'Sefa says.' People will think you're hearing voices."

He elbowed her. She let him.

They sat until the sun went down and the first stars came out over a world that remembered itself, and below them the road stretched south, long and ordinary and open, waiting for a boy with an empty head and a full heart to start walking.
