## 22. Hollowmere, Remembered

The road down into Hollowmere was longer than I remembered.

That was the joke of it—I didn't remember it at all. I knew it the way a man knows a word in a language he's forgotten he speaks: my feet found the ruts, my shoulders turned at the bend where the birches crowded close, and none of it meant anything to me. I was a stranger wearing my own body down a road my bones had walked ten thousand times.

Sefa rode half a length behind me. She had been riding half a length behind me for eleven days, ever since we left the Court's heart and its white scar of new light, and she hadn't said more than forty words the whole way. She wasn't a talker on her best day, and this was not her best day. The gate she'd held in the Regent's citadel had taken more from her than she'd admitted—she still reached up sometimes to touch the place behind her ear where a whole decade of desertion and defiance had been scraped out like frost from a window. Twice now I'd caught her mouthing names as if testing whether they fit. *Veyra. Corrin. Sefa.* Herself, last of all.

"You could stop," she said now, as the valley mouth opened ahead of us, grey stone walls rising on either side like cupped hands. "At the ridge. There's an inn. Decent ale."

"There's no inn at the ridge."

"No." A pause. "There isn't. Sorry. Habit." She rubbed the back of her neck. "I keep reaching for things that aren't there anymore. You'd know about that."

I did not answer. The valley walls slid past, close enough to touch, and something in my chest pulled toward them like a tide going out—the body's grief for a home the mind couldn't claim. I had been warned about this. I had chosen it with my eyes open, standing in the blazing heart of the Ninefold Court with every memory I'd ever hoarded burning in my hands like a nest of embers, and I had poured them all into the covenant-heart anyway. My mother's voice. The first coin I ever stole. Maren's kitchen, the smell of burnt barley bread, the exact grey of the morning I watched a courier die on my floor. All of it, spent.

The Court stood reforged. Nine seats, nine regalia borne by choice, nine regents bound by law renewed by consent—and me, empty as a swept room, walking home to a valley that had never held any magic worth the name.

"It's not too late to turn around," Sefa said quietly. "The Court would find you a place. Ilse asked after you specifically. She owes you—" She stopped herself. "She'd say she doesn't owe anyone. But she does. To you."

"Ilse has a vault full of her brother's last hour and a seat at the table of the most powerful institution in the world. She doesn't need me haunting her halls."

"That's not what I asked."

I looked back at her. In the slanting afternoon light her silver eye caught fire and her dark one stayed shadowed, and I thought—not for the first time—that she was two people stitched along a seam the hunters had made, and she was learning to live in both of them at once. Braver than anything I'd done. I'd given up my memories all at once, in one bright terrible moment. She had to wake up every morning inside her loss.

"I need to see it," I said. "The valley. I need to know what I bought with all of it. Whether it was worth—" My voice did something strange. I pressed on. "Whether it holds."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then I'll stand in the market square until it does."

* * *

Hollowmere smelled the same, which nearly undid me before I got ten steps past the gate.

Woodsmoke and wet wool and the green rot of the millpond, and under it all that particular silence—the magic-dead hush that had been a wound in the world once and was now just quiet, honest quiet, the silence of a place where nothing was hidden. I stopped in the lane and breathed it in like a man surfacing, and I understood that my body remembered even if I didn't, that some part of me had been cataloguing this smell since before I could walk, and that part of me was weeping even though my eyes were dry.

"Theo Dunmore."

Maren stood in her doorway the way she'd probably stood in doorways since before the Court fell—as if the doorway belonged to her and the world was merely visiting. She was older than I remembered, but I didn't remember her, so that meant nothing. Her hair was fully white now, cropped short, and there were new lines around her mouth, and when she saw my face she went very still in the way of women who have buried enough people to recognize the ones who've come back changed.

"You're supposed to be dead," she said. "Or famous. One of the two. The stories can't decide."

"Maren." Her name came out of me like water finding a channel. I didn't remember her, and I loved her, and both of those things were true at once, and I didn't have anywhere to put that. "I don't—"

"I know what you don't." She came down the step and took my face in her hands, rough palms, flour in the creases, and studied me the way she must once have studied feverish children. "The whole valley knows. Tam's boy came through Vell three weeks ago with the tale—the thief who emptied himself to save the world. They've been telling it wrong, mind. Making you seven feet tall. Giving you a sword." Her thumb traced the line of my cheekbone, and her eyes went wet and furious at the same time. "You look terrible."

"I feel—" I started, and stopped, because the true answer was too large. *I feel like a house after the fire, walls standing, everything that mattered gone.*

"You feel hollow," Maren said. "I know. That's why I've been baking."

Behind her, the street had begun to fill. Doors opening, shutters folding back, faces I didn't know gathering in knots and then dissolving and reforming closer, the way crowds do around something half-feared and wholly wanted. An old man with a smith's shoulders. A woman holding a child who stared at me with frank, unafraid eyes. Two boys on a wall. Tam, presumably—Tam whose boy had carried the tale—standing with his arms crossed and his jaw working like he was chewing on words he couldn't swallow.

They were looking at me the way I had once looked at travelers coming over the pass: as if I carried something they needed.

"We voted," Maren announced, in the tone of a woman announcing the weather. "Before you got here. While you were still being legendary somewhere north of here. We voted on what to do when you came home, and it was unanimous, which has happened twice in the history of this valley, the other time being the decision to stop paying the toll at Miller's Ford." She jerked her chin at the crowd. "You gave away everything you had. Every scrap. So we're giving it back. Not the way you'd do it—" her mouth twisted, and I understood that she had known exactly what I used to do in this market, the lifting and the selling-back, had perhaps always known, and had loved me anyway, or because of it, "—not with magic. With stories. It'll be slow. It'll be lopsided. Some of it will contradict, because that's how remembering works when more than one person does it. But it's yours. All of it. And nobody in this valley takes payment for family."

"I'm not—"

"You are." No hesitation at all. "Now get inside. The bread's getting cold, and the first story needs the whole town listening or Doss will sulk for a week."

* * *

They began that evening, in Maren's kitchen, because of course they did—kitchens are where the truth of any town is kept.

Doss was the smith, the old man with the shoulders, and he told the first story with the gravity of a man laying a foundation stone. He told me about a boy of six who had followed him to his forge every morning for a summer and asked questions until Doss threatened to charge him by the word. About the day the boy had picked Doss's pocket—badly, obviously, a six-year-old's fingers—and instead of thrashing him, Doss had sat him down and taught him how locks worked, on the theory that a thief who understood the mechanism might one day understand the cost.

"Do you remember why?" I asked. "Why teach me instead of—"

"Because your mother had died that spring," Doss said, without softening it, "and everyone else in this valley was tiptoeing around you like you were made of glass, and you needed someone to treat you like you were made of trouble. Trouble can be shaped. Glass only breaks." He took a long pull of cider. "Your mother. Wren Dunmore. Came through the pass with a caravan and stayed a season, and stayed another, and then you arrived, and she never talked much about before. Sang, though. Constantly. Off-key. The whole valley could set its clocks by how badly she sang."

Laughter moved around the kitchen, warm and easy, and I sat in the middle of it feeling each fact land like a stone dropped into deep water—no splash I could see, but ripples spreading somewhere down in the dark, touching things I couldn't name. That was how it worked, I learned. The stories didn't return anything. They just kept dropping stones, and trusting the water.

Tam told about the winter I was twelve and stole back a grandmother's memories from a trader who'd taken too much—a traveler, grief-drunk, who'd paid for a dead wife's face and gotten her whole last decade, leaving the old woman staring at him blank across the market. Tam had seen me do it. Seen me press the excess back into her hands in the alley behind the tannery and tell her, in a whisper, *you don't pay for all of it; you only ever needed her laugh.* Tam had never mentioned it. Twenty years of silence, kept like a debt.

"I told myself it was none of my business," he said, staring into the fire. "Then the hunters came, and I watched them take Hal's whole life out of him in the square, wholesale, like clearing a shelf. And I thought: the boy who gave back the laugh would never have let that stand. And I was right, wasn't I? You didn't let it stand."

"Hal," I said carefully. The name rang faintly, a bell in an unfurnished room. "Is he—"

"Half-mended." Tam's jaw tightened. "The Court sent someone. A restorer. Got back maybe a third of him. He gardens now. Doesn't remember why he likes it, but he likes it, and his wife says that's enough." He looked at me. "His wife says thank you, by the way. Says you could have run and you didn't."

* * *

It went on for days. Weeks, maybe—I lost track, which felt fitting.

Each morning the town fed me myself in pieces. The baker's daughter described the exact scowl I wore when someone tried to haggle a memory's price upward. The ferryman swore I once paid his toll with a memory of sunlight on water I'd lifted off a dying pilgrim, and that he'd kept it in a locket ever after because it was the kindest thing anyone had ever handed him. Children who hadn't been born when I left reenacted the legend of me in the lanes, swordless and seven feet tall, and their mothers shooed them in, laughing, and corrected the details.

And every evening Maren sat beside me and did the slow work. She didn't perform. She remembered *at* me, quietly, the way you'd feed a fire one stick at a time—what I liked to eat, how I took my tea, the year the roof leaked and I complained so consistently that she'd fixed it just for the silence. Small things. Load-bearing things. Once she paused in the middle of a sentence about a festival, years ago, and looked at me with an expression I was beginning to learn how to read.

"What is it?" I asked.

"You used to watch the travelers," she said. "Coming over the pass. Every single one. Like you were waiting for one of them to be somebody." She turned her cup in her hands. "Waiting for her, maybe. Some version of her. I used to think it was the saddest thing I ever saw, and the stubbornest."

"It sounds like me."

"It was you." She set the cup down. "And here's the thing, boy. You found her. Not the way you were watching for—but you found her. Voice and all. You had to give her up to save everything, and you did it, and I have never in my life been prouder of anyone, and I include the time my own daughter pulled two drowning men out of the millpond." Her voice cracked and she let it, unashamed. "So don't sit there looking like the price was too high. It was high. It was also the finest thing this valley ever produced, and we produced you."

I didn't cry. Mostly. Maren pretended not to notice, which was its own kind of mercy.

* * *

Sefa left on the nineteenth day.

She came to Maren's door at dawn with her horse saddled and her pack already tied, and she stood there working her jaw like a woman rehearsing lines she intended to abandon.

"The Court wants an answer by the turning," she said. "About the seat. Ninth chair's empty. Ilse says they won't fill it without asking me first, which—" a short breath of a laugh, "—which is either respect or punishment, and with Ilse it's usually both."

"Are you going to take it?"

"No." She said it fast, then slower, testing it. "No. I spent twenty years thinking the Court was a cage and ten minutes helping burn it down, and I'm not ready to sit in one yet. Maybe never. There's a caravan heading east that needs a guard who's good at losing fights slowly, and I'm very good at that." She shifted. "But that's not—look. Before I go. There's something I've been carrying."

She reached into her coat and brought out a small glass vial, no longer than a finger, stoppered with wax. Inside it swam a thread of light, silver-grey, moving like smoke underwater. My chest tightened around nothing. I knew what bottled memory looked like. I had spent a lifetime handling them, and my hands remembered the weight even if I didn't.

"When the covenant-heart took everything," Sefa said, "it took what you were holding. What you'd surrendered." Her silver eye caught the early light. "It didn't take what other people held. I've kept this since the drowned chapel. Through Vell, through the citadel, through the flight, through—" she touched the spot behind her ear, briefly, "—through everything, including the parts I don't remember going through. I kept it because it seemed like the one thing in the world that should survive whatever we were doing. And because I'm a selfish coward and couldn't bear to hand it into that fire."

"Sefa—"

"Don't thank me. If you thank me I'll throw it in the river out of spite." She pushed it into my palm and closed my fingers over it, hard, her grip a knight's grip, no argument permitted. "It's hers. You'll know it when you hear it. And Theo—" she hesitated, and for a moment I saw both the deserter and the knight, guilt and honor sharing one face, "—the town's been feeding you yourself for weeks. Good. That's the way it should work. Stories, not magic. But this one isn't a story. This one's a voice, and voices are different, and you've earned the difference."

Then she mounted, and rode out through the gate, and did not look back, because knights never do, and I stood in the lane holding a vial of my mother's voice while the valley woke up around me.

* * *

I waited until evening. I told myself it was out of respect for the ritual of it—Maren's kitchen, the fire, the town gathered—but the truth was simpler and less noble. I was afraid. I had faced the Ashen Regent in her own citadel with steadier hands than I brought to that small vial. Fear of erasure-hunters is a clean fear. Fear that a voice will mean nothing to you—that you'll hold your mother's name in your ears and feel only a stranger's curiosity—that is a fear with teeth.

Maren lit the lamps. The others settled. Nobody spoke, because somehow the whole town knew, because towns always know.

I broke the wax.

The memory rose out of the vial like warmth from a hearth, and it settled over me, and—

A kitchen. Not Maren's. Smaller, poorer, a window with a crack mended by careful hands. A woman at the basin with her sleeves rolled up, dark hair escaping its braid, singing—off-key, gloriously off-key, some wandering tune about a fox and a winter moon. And she turns, because she hears the floorboard, because she always hears the floorboard, and her face does the thing that faces do, the whole sky opening—

"Theo," she says. "There's my Theo. Come here, love. Look what the light's doing on the water—it looks like coins, doesn't it? Like someone spilled a treasure and forgot to come back for it."

And small hands—my hands, six years old, chapped at the knuckles—reach up, and she lifts me onto her hip like it's the easiest thing in the world, and outside the cracked window the light does indeed lie on the millpond like spilled treasure, and she hums her broken song against my hair, and I am held. I am utterly, unbearably held.

The memory ended. The kitchen was Maren's again, and the fire had burned low, and I was crying the way I hadn't cried in the Court's heart or the citadel's corridors or anywhere in all the long war between—one great unguarded sob after another, thirty years of grief arriving all at once in a borrowed chair.

Maren's arm came around me. Around us all, really; half the town had drifted close, and no one pretended otherwise.

"She called you that," Maren murmured into my hair. "*My Theo.* Every time. Like she was marking you hers in front of witnesses."

"It's mine," I said. My voice was wrecked and I didn't care. "It's actually mine."

"All of it is," she said. "That's what we've been trying to tell you for a month. Now blow your nose and eat something. There's pie."

* * *

Spring came to the valley the way it always had, indifferent to the affairs of courts.

I stayed. There was never really a question. The Court sends letters sometimes—Ilse writes in a hand like a blade, all economy and edge, reporting on the nine regalia and the regents who bear them and the slow, stubborn work of a covenant learning to be kind. Sefa writes from the east, mostly jokes and one recurring postscript: *Still haven't taken the seat. Still not sorry. Keep the valley loud; I want to hear it from here.*

And I have a trade again, though it's a gentler one. Travelers still come over the pass, and some of them carry griefs too heavy to bear alone, and I sit with them in Maren's kitchen—or the market square, when the weather's fine—and I listen, and when they leave, they leave lighter. No magic. No lifting. Just the old craft underneath the craft: knowing what a person has lost, and what they need back, and that most of what we carry for each other is simply the willingness to remember out loud.

This morning a caravan came down through the gate, dusty and ringing with bells, and a girl of maybe eight peeled off from it and stood in the square staring up at me with frank, unafraid eyes.

"You're him," she said. "The one in the stories."

"Probably not," I said. "In the stories I'm seven feet tall."

"How tall are you?"

"Exactly tall enough." I crouched to her level. "What's your name?"

"Tally." She glanced back at the caravan, then leaned close, confiding. "My mama says you used to steal memories. Is that true?"

"It's true."

"Did it work?"

I thought about a vial of light. A cracked window. Spilled treasure on the water. A town that had spent a month handing me back to myself, story by story, stone by stone into the deep water, until the ripples reached everything.

"Sometimes," I said. "But it turns out the better trick is giving them back."

She considered this with the seriousness of the very young. Then she held out her hand, palm up, in the universal gesture of children and merchants everywhere, and I laughed—really laughed, the sound surprising me with its own weight—and shook it.

Behind her, the pass stood open, and the light came down the mountain like something returning home, and the whole valley held it, and did not let go.
