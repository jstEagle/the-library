## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The stew was wrong before Ivo ever lifted the spoon.

He could not have said how he knew — not at first, in words. It was something older than words, older than thought: a prickle at the back of his throat, a faint sweetness beneath the rosemary that had no business being there. Ten years of swallowing death by increments had tuned him like an instrument, and the instrument was singing.

He stood at the king's left hand, as he always stood, in a doublet of grey silk that made him invisible against the stone. That was the point of the grey. A taster who drew the eye was a taster who died young, and Ivo Ravensworth had no intention of dying at all — not quickly, anyway. His body was a locked house with a very careful doorman, and nothing crossed that threshold without his leave.

"Your Grace." He spoke quietly, to Duke Aldous of Marrowdale, whose place lay two seats down and whose hand was already drifting toward his spoon. "A moment."

The duke looked up, annoyed. The great hall of Ashvale Keep roared around them — forty nobles, a hundred candles, the king laughing too loudly at some joke from the Ambassador of Vell, whose smile did not reach anywhere near his eyes. In all that noise, Ivo's voice was a thread, but dukes are trained to hear threads.

"The lamb stew," Ivo said. "Don't."

"I beg your pardon?"

Ivo took the duke's bowl himself, lifted it, and set it on the salt cellar where everyone could see it. Then he took the silver spoon, dipped it, raised it to his lips — and drank.

The hall noticed. It always noticed when the taster worked; there was a hush that spread outward from the high table like frost across a windowpane. Ivo let the mouthful sit on his tongue and read it the way a priest reads scripture, line by line.

Lamb, slow-cooked, good stock, marrow bones — the kitchen knew its work. Leeks from the river gardens. Thyme. A whisper of honey, which was new, and would earn the cook a word of praise if the evening went well. And underneath, curled into the fat like a serpent into warm grass—

Henbane.

Not enough to kill outright. Not enough for a man to taste and cry poison. Enough to make a duke ill within the hour, violently, publicly, humiliatingly — or enough to finish him over three days of such meals, if the cook was bribed and the physician was blind. Someone wanted Aldous of Marrowdale sick. Or dead slowly. Or merely frightened, which was often the point. Poison was rarely about the dying; it was about what the living did afterward.

Ivo swallowed.

It went down the way all poisons went down in him: coldly, politely, like a guest shown to the wrong room. His tongue catalogued it even as his blood ignored it — *henbane, second pressing, cut with wine vinegar to mask the bite, administered post-cooking so the heat wouldn't break it down* — and somewhere behind his eyes, in the ledger he kept that no one else would ever see, a new entry wrote itself.

*Entry four thousand one hundred and twelve,* he thought. *Henbane, in lamb, at the king's own table. Sloppy work. Someone is getting lazy.*

Or someone wanted to be caught.

"Your Majesty." He turned and bowed, shallow and precise. "There is henbane in the stew. The duke's bowl, at least. I've taken the liberty of tasting it myself to confirm."

The hall went silent the way a forest goes silent — not all at once, but bird by bird.

King Osric of Ashvale set down his goblet. He was not an old man yet, but the crown had aged him unevenly, the way damp ages stone — soft here, brittle there. His eyes found Ivo's, and there was nothing in them. There never was. That was the first thing Ivo had learned about his king, back when he'd still been learning things about his king: Osric's face was a door painted on a wall.

"Henbane," the king repeated. "At my table."

"At your table, Majesty. In quantity sufficient to sicken, perhaps to kill, if served over several days."

"Then someone in my kitchens has committed treason." The king said it lightly, almost conversationally, the way a man might remark on rain. But his gaze swept the hall once, slow as a lighthouse beam, and every noble in it sat a little straighter and thought hard about their last conversation with their steward. "Master Ravensworth. How certain are you?"

"As certain as my tongue allows, sire. Which is entirely."

"And the duke lives."

"The duke will live. He hasn't eaten any. I have."

That earned him a ripple — laughter in some corners, something colder in others. Duke Aldous himself had gone pale under his ruddy cheeks, staring at the abandoned bowl as though it might grow teeth. "You — you drank it," the duke said. "Knowing what it was."

"It's what I'm for, Your Grace."

He said it without bitterness, because bitterness was a luxury he'd spent years training out of his voice. But he felt it settle in him anyway, familiar as an old coat: *it's what I'm for.* Four words that contained his whole life. Not *who* he was for. *What.*

The king gestured, and guards came for the stew — for the pot, for the servers, for the boy who had carried it up from the kitchens, a child of maybe fourteen who wept without sound as they led him away. Ivo watched him go and said nothing. He had learned that saying nothing was also a skill, and possibly the most valuable one he owned. The boy would be questioned. The boy might even be innocent. It didn't matter what Ivo believed; it mattered what the court needed to believe, and tonight the court needed a culprit more than it needed truth.

*Entry four thousand one hundred and thirteen,* the ledger wrote. *One boy, unnamed, taken for questioning. Flavor: guilt, faint, persistent.*

"You have my gratitude, Ravensworth," the king said, and it was the tone of a man thanking a servant for mopping a floor. "Duke Aldous. Raise a cup to the man who saved your life."

They raised cups to him. Forty faces, forty cups, and not one pair of eyes that met his with anything warmer than calculation. To be thanked at that table was to be marked: *this one is useful.* Useful things were kept. Useful things were never free.

Ivo bowed again and drank the toast — water, always water at the table; wine was for men whose tongues hadn't been ruined for everything but poison — and the feast resumed around him like water closing over a dropped coin.

* * *

Later, much later, when the candles had burned to stubs and the last drunk lord had been carried snoring from the hall, Ivo climbed the narrow stair to his rooms above the tasting chamber and lit his lamp and opened his book.

It was not, strictly speaking, a book anyone had given him. It had begun as a taster's logbook — the Office of the King's Palate issued them standard, leather-bound, ruled in red — but ten years of nights had overflowed the official pages long ago. Now it held three separate texts interleaved: the official record of poisons detected (dry, precise, written for the records-keepers who never read it); the private catalogue of flavors (a taxonomy no scholar alive could match, built dish by dish, poison by poison, year by year); and a third thing, unwritten but present on every page like watermark — the ledger of debts. Who owed whom. What had been swallowed and by whom and why. What it had cost.

Tonight's entries, in his small, upright hand:

*4112. Henbane, hyoscyamus niger, second pressing, masked with wine vinegar. Introduced after cooking. Detected at first spoonful. Consumed: approx. half ounce. Effect on subject: none. Effect on subject's peace of mind: negligible. Effect on subject's opinion of the palace kitchens: significantly diminished.*

*4113. One kitchen boy, name unknown, age ~14, taken by the guard. No evidence presented. No trial announced. Subject notes: the vinegar masking suggests a practiced hand. A child of fourteen does not have a practiced hand. Subject notes further: no one asked him.*

He sat back and rubbed his eyes. The lamp flame bent in a draft from the window, and beyond the glass Thornfell mountain stood black against the blacker sky, vast and patient, wearing its collar of border wards like a crown of faint blue fire. He could see them from here on clear nights — or he could have, once. Lately the fires along the ridgeline had seemed... thinner. Dimmer. He had told himself it was weather, or age, or his own tired eyes.

He told himself a lot of things. It was practically a hobby.

His tongue still tingled faintly from the henbane. It always did, a little — the ghost of each poison lingering an hour or a day, a souvenir of survival. Most men feared poison the way they feared drowning: totally, superstitiously, as a horror beyond understanding. Ivo had made his living inside that fear for so long that he sometimes forgot other people felt it at all. To him, henbane was a flavor. Wolfsbane was a flavor. The tears of the gray amanita, distilled by moonlight according to recipes the under-courts were supposed to have forgotten — flavor. He had swallowed enough death to fill a graveyard and his body had shrugged it all off, every dose, every time, since the fever at nine years old that should have killed him and instead had remade him into this: a locked house, a careful door, a cage of competence.

That was the joke nobody laughed at, least of all him. They called it a gift. The king called it a gift, when he called it anything at all. *Ravensworth's palate is worth a regiment,* the old spymaster used to say, back when there'd been a spymaster, before the spymaster had retired rather suddenly to a monastery no letters ever left. A regiment. And what did regiments get? Pay, praise, pensions, parades. What did Ivo get? A grey doublet. A room above his work. A table full of people who would forget his name the moment he stopped being useful, and a king whose gratitude was exactly as deep as the moment required and not one drop deeper.

He was thirty-four years old and he had never once been kissed without calculating the risk. He had never eaten a meal purely for pleasure, because pleasure was a door left open. He had friends the way a fortress has allies — formally, distantly, on paper. The closest thing he had to intimacy was a book of flavors only he could read, and a memory of a woman in a wine cellar that he took out at night the way other men took out whiskey: sparingly, and always with a price in the morning.

*Wren.*

He didn't write her name in the book. Some entries belonged to the third text, the watermark one, and stayed unwritten on principle.

He closed the ledger and trimmed the lamp, and that was when he heard the footsteps on the stair — quick, light, wrong for this hour. Servants didn't come to the taster's floor at midnight unless someone was dying. Guards didn't come unless someone was arrested. He put his hand on the knife he kept by the lamp — habit, not fear; fear had been bred out of him along with everything else — and waited.

The knock came, twice, soft. Then a voice he knew, low and roughened by road-dust and years, speaking through the wood in the old cadence smugglers used on the passes, the call-and-answer that meant *friend, open fast, I shouldn't be seen:*

*"Gray goose flying low. Open the door, Ivo, before I lose my nerve — and before someone sees me standing in your hallway like a fool."*

Ivo went very still.

Ten years, that voice had been gone. Ten years since the Thornfell roads, since the cellar door only tasters knew, since the night he had traded a name to keep his post and hated himself in a way that had never once dulled. He knew — had always known, in the place where the ledger kept its unwritten pages — that she would come back someday. Debts came back. That was practically the law of the mountains.

But knowing a thing and hearing it knocking at your door at midnight were different countries entirely.

He crossed the room, unbarred the door, and pulled it open.

* * *

She stood in the lamplight with snowmelt dripping off her cloak and a contraband cask of Velli red slung across her back like a sleeping child, and she looked at him the way you look at a grave you're not sure is occupied.

"Hello, Ivo," said Wren. "You look terrible."

"Wren," he said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him; inside, something ten years buried had just turned over in its sleep. "You look—"

"Like a woman who just climbed three hundred miles of frozen pass with a felony on her back? Yes. Are you going to invite me in, or does the king's taster make his oldest friend stand in the hall?"

He stepped aside. She slipped past him — close, closer than she needed to, close enough that he caught woodsmoke and pine and something underneath that was only her — and set the cask down on his floor with a thump, and looked around at the bare stone room, the ledger on the desk, the single lamp, and then back at him with an expression that was half the old mockery and half something he had no entry for.

"Still living like a monk," she said. "Still keeping score." She nodded at the book. "Some things don't change."

"No," Ivo said quietly, closing the door, turning the bar. "Some things don't."

And far above them both, past stone and starlight, on the black shoulders of Thornfell, the first ward-fire guttered — thin, blue, and dimmer than it had been the night before — though neither of them saw it go.
