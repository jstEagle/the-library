## 2. Wren in the Wine Cellar

The cellar door had no handle on the outside.

That was the point of it. Only a taster would know to press the third stone from the left, below the rack of Ashvale reds that nobody drank because the king preferred southern vintages and the king's preferences were law. The stone gave under Ivo's palm with the small reluctant click of a mechanism older than the dynasty, and the door swung inward on hinges that had been oiled — recently, and by someone who knew what she was doing.

He stepped through into darkness that smelled of dust and candle tallow and, beneath both, of her.

"You're late," said the dark.

"The duke wanted a second tasting of the quail course." He let the door close behind him. "Henbane in the stew tonight. Subtle work — someone steeped the seeds in oil first, which kills most of the bitterness. If I hadn't been looking for it, I'd have missed it."

"And did you miss any of it?"

"None of it."

"Then you're still the best tongue in Thornfell." A match scraped; candlelight bloomed, and Wren Ashvale stood at the far end of the cellar aisle with her hood pushed back and her arms crossed, and ten years fell away from him like a coat he'd been carrying too long.

She had not changed as much as he'd feared and more than he'd hoped. The smuggler's leathers were newer, better fitted, dyed the grey-green of the high passes. Her hair was shorter — practical, wind-tangled at the ends. But her eyes were the same: dark, quick, always slightly amused, always slightly ahead of whatever you were about to say. Eyes that had once looked at him across a campfire in the Thornfell passes and made him believe, for one whole season, that he could be a different man than the one the palace was making of him.

"You shouldn't be here," he said.

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "I really shouldn't. And yet."

* * *

Three years ago — no. He counted honestly, the way he counted everything now, in flavors and doses. Four years ago, the cellar door had opened onto a different Wren, one who laughed easily and stole his wine and called him *taster-boy* in a voice like a knife being drawn slowly from a sheath.

Four years ago, she had come down the hidden stair from the roof-garden with two bottles of Vellish blackwine tucked under one arm like orphaned kittens, and she had set them on the tasting bench among the crystal goblets and the little silver spoons, and she had said, "Your king taxes my roads. So I tax his cellar. Fair trade."

"It's theft," Ivo had said, without moving to stop her.

"It's *commerce*," she'd corrected. "Theft is when you take something and give nothing back. I'm giving your cellar excitement. Look at it. It was dying of boredom before I came along."

The cellar had indeed been dying of boredom. That was the truth he had never told anyone: the palace cellars were a mausoleum of vintages laid down for occasions that never came, guarded by men who tasted nothing but fear, and Wren Ashvale had walked into that mausoleum with wine stolen off her own smuggling routes and made it feel like a place where living people went. She'd sit on the barrels with her boots swinging, and she'd pour, and she'd talk — about the passes, about the ward-lights burning green over Thornfell at night, about the villages where they still left out bowls of cream for the mountain's old spirits because it cost nothing and might matter. And Ivo, who spent his days putting death in his mouth on behalf of a man he'd never exchanged ten honest words with, would listen the way a starving man watches bread.

"Why do you bring it here?" he'd asked her once, in the third month. "You could sell Vellish blackwine in Lowmarket for triple its weight in silver. Instead you carry it up a mountain to feed it to a taster who can't even get properly drunk anymore."

She'd turned the bottle in her hands, considering. The candlelight moved in her eyes.

"Because everything I carry is for someone," she'd said finally. "Silk for lords. Iron for soldiers. Poppy for the sick and letters for lovers and knives for people whose names I don't ask. Everything has an owner before it ever reaches me, and after me, and I'm just the road between them." She'd looked up. "This is the only cargo I've ever chosen myself. I pick who drinks it. That's all. That's the whole grand romance of it, taster-boy. Don't make it into a song."

But he had made it into a song. Silently, in the ledger-keeping part of his mind where he catalogued flavors the way other men catalogued insults and debts, he had given her a page of her own. *Wren:* woodsmoke and wet slate, the bite of high-altitude air, something sweet underneath that she would deny to her grave. He had never written it down. Some entries you keep only in the body.

* * *

In the present, she uncorked one of the bottles she'd brought — not Vellish this time, something older, its seal cracked and crumbling — and poured two measures into the pewter cups she'd brought for exactly this purpose, because she knew he wouldn't drink from crystal that had passed through the kitchens.

"Henbane in the oil," she mused, handing him his cup. "That's kitchen work, though. Someone inside."

"Someone inside," Ivo agreed. "Which is why I have perhaps twenty minutes before someone notices the taster has gone missing between courses."

"Twenty minutes." She raised her cup. "Then we'll be efficient. To commerce."

"To commerce." The wine was extraordinary — dark cherry and iron and something resinous, like the memory of a pine forest — and he told her so, because that was their ritual, the tasting-report, the one honest transaction in either of their lives. "Cherry, iron, pine-pitch. A northern vintage. Valley of the Nine Fords?"

Something flickered across her face, there and gone, quick as a fish turning in shallow water.

"Close," she said. "Ten Fords. They renamed it after the flood."

"I'm sorry. I didn't—"

"You didn't know. Of course you didn't know. You know every poison that's ever been brewed in three kingdoms and you don't know that a valley drowned two winters ago." She said it lightly. She said everything lightly; that was the blade under the tenderness, that she could make even accusation sound like banter. "It's fine, Ivo. It's why I'm here, actually. Partly."

"Partly."

"Partly for the wine trade. Partly because I need a route through the palace that doesn't touch the main gates. And partly—" she turned her cup slowly on the barrel-head, watching the wine climb the sides, "—partly because I heard a thing on my last run that I thought you should hear, since you're the only person in this rockpile who ever listened to me about anything."

The cellar was very quiet. Somewhere above them, faint through forty feet of stone, the orchestra was tuning for the evening's dancing. Ivo became aware of his own heartbeat, steady and slow, the heartbeat of a man whose blood had been schooled out of hurrying.

"Tell me," he said.

* * *

But first — because memory is a cellar too, and things stored in the dark keep their flavor longer — his mind went where it always went when she stood too close. Back four years. Back to the last night.

The last night, she had brought him word instead of wine. She'd come down the hidden stair white-faced, which he had never seen her be, and she'd gripped his wrist hard enough to leave marks.

"They know," she'd said. "Ivo, they *know*. There's a new captain on the gate, and he had my description, and the only reason I'm standing here is that his clerk is in my debt. They're going to sweep the cellars within the week. Every hidden door, every cache. They'll find the routes, and then they'll find the couriers, and then they'll find—" she'd stopped, and swallowed, and finished anyway, because Wren never flinched from a sentence once started, "—they'll find me."

And Ivo had stood there in the candlelight with her hand on his wrist, and he had known — with the same cold clarity with which he identified poison, that terrible gift of seeing exactly what a thing was — that he had maybe three days to choose.

Report her, and be rewarded. The crown paid well for smuggler-routes; the border wards were the king's obsession, and a courier network threading them was worth a fortune in favor. Say nothing, and wait for the sweep to find traces of her everywhere he had helped her put them — his fingerprints on every crate, his knowledge in every route-map, his complicity written in his own careful hand in the cellar ledgers he kept for her sake. When they found her, they would find him. Tasters who trafficked contraband through the royal cellars did not get trials. They got a quiet cup of something, and a new taster by morning.

Or — third option, the one that had seemed so clean, so clever, so much the work of a man who believed himself capable of managing everything — go to the Master of the Household first. Voluntarily. Give them the routes, the caches, the network — everything except her name. Trade the map for the woman. He'd reasoned it out like a recipe: the crown cared about the roads, not the runners; give them the roads and they'd have no need to hunt the girl; and Ivo Ravensworth would stand before them as the loyal servant who'd uncovered the whole operation, untouchable, promoted, safe — and Wren would slip away over the passes before the sweeps began, warned by him, free.

He had even drafted the warning letter. He remembered writing it, the candle guttering, choosing words that only she would read rightly, words that sounded like a merchant's note but meant *run north, take the Grey Stair, trust no gate.*

He had never sent it. Because the next morning, before dawn, the Master of the Household had come to *his* rooms — not summoned him, come to him, which was itself a message — and had laid a single sheet of paper on his table. A list. Seven names. Wren Ashvale's name third from the top, in a clerk's neat hand. And beneath the list, a question that was not a question: *Which of these have you entertained in the royal cellars, taster?*

They had known already. They had known before he ever began his clever maneuvering, and his visit to the Master of the Household — which he had made that same day, sweating, rehearsed, offering the routes in exchange for silence about his own part — had been received not as loyalty but as confirmation. He had handed them the map. He had handed them, in his panic to save his own skin, the precise shape of everything, and the only thing he had withheld — her name — they had possessed all along.

The sweeps came within the week. Her network burned. Couriers taken on the roads, caches cracked open, the Grey Stair sealed with new ward-stones. And Wren — Wren alone, because some smuggler instinct or some warning he never learned the source of had reached her in time — vanished into the high passes, exiled from the low country forever, her name entered in the border rolls as a proscribed trader.

He had told himself for four years that he had tried. That the letter proved it. That the difference between what he intended and what happened was the difference between a man and a coward, and that he was mostly the former.

He had never believed it. Not once. Because he knew, the way he knew poisons, that the letter had gone unwritten-in-sending for one reason only: sending it would have required him to confess to her, and confession risked the one thing he could not survive losing, which was not his post but her good opinion of him. He had protected his skin twice over — once from the crown, once from her judgment — and she had paid for both protections.

* * *

"—Ivo." Her voice pulled him back. "Where did you go just now? You did the thing with your eyes."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you look at me like I'm a dish you're deciding whether to send back." She was watching him with her head tilted, and the candle flame doubled in her dark eyes. "I said I heard something on my last run. Do you want to hear it or do you want to keep doing inventory of your guilt? Because you can't do both, and I've only got twenty minutes of you."

"I want to hear it."

"Good." She set down her cup. "There are new tolls on the mountain roads. Not the king's tolls — those I pay, grudgingly, because I'm a businesswoman. These are different. Little shrines going up at the pass-mouths, spiral-marked stones, and the villagers won't go near them. And the ward-lights—" she paused, and for the first time since he'd known her, Wren Ashvale looked uncertain, "—the ward-lights over Thornfell have been going out. One by one. Green to grey to nothing. My grandmother used to say the lights were the mountain counting its sheep. Counting us. Keeping us."

"Keeping us from what?"

"That's the thing." She leaned closer, and her voice dropped to almost nothing, and the cellar's darkness pressed in around the candle like a listening animal. "An old woman in Ten Fords — the flood valley, the one you've never heard of — told me the wards were never built against armies. Armies, she said, the mountain eats for breakfast. The wards were built against the *mountain's own court.* The gentry under the stone. And she said the reason the lights are going out is that somebody above has stopped paying what's owed."

Ivo felt the words land somewhere below thought, in the body, where he registered danger before he understood it — the same place henbane announced itself as a sweetness just wrong enough.

"Somebody above," he repeated. "Somebody in the palace."

"Somebody with a seal." Wren held his gaze. "And Ivo — the reason I'm telling you, the real reason, is that the old woman showed me a copy of one of the old contracts. Hundred years old, kept in her family because her family were ward-tenders once. And the seal on it was the royal seal. The crown has been selling something to the under-court for a century, and the price has come due, and the lights are going out like coins being counted away."

The orchestra above them had begun to play. Through the stone, the music arrived thin and distant, a court dancing on top of a debt.

"Wren." His mouth was dry. "What does the crown sell to a court like that?"

She looked at him for a long moment, and he watched her decide — watched the calculation move behind her eyes, the same instinct that weighed every cargo, every road, every stranger: *what can this one be trusted with.*

"Not gold," she said quietly. "Not silver. Nothing you can weigh, except on scales I don't want to think about. And Ivo — if you ever find proof of it, if it ever touches you, walk away. Whatever they offer you. Whatever they promise. Walk away and don't look back, because I have seen what the mountain leaves behind when it collects, and it isn't pretty, and it isn't reversible, and it isn't—"

She stopped. Her hand had gone, without her seeming to notice, to the collar of her leathers, to the hollow of her throat, an unconscious gesture, protective, the way a person touches an old scar.

"It isn't what?" Ivo asked.

"Nothing." She smiled, and it was the old smile, bright and crooked, and it convinced him exactly as much as it was designed to: not at all. "Old women's stories. Drink your wine, taster-boy. Tell me what else is in it."

"There's almond," he said, because his hands needed something to do and his voice needed somewhere to go. "Bitter almond, very faint, at the finish. And underneath that — I don't know. Something I've never tasted before. Something that tastes like—" he searched the ledger of his tongue, that vast cold library, and found the entry missing, "—like waiting."

"Like waiting," Wren repeated softly.

And that was when the bell above began to ring — not the dinner bell, not the dance bell, but the deep alarm tone that Ivo had heard only twice in ten years, the one that meant the palace gates were closing — and the candle flame bent sideways as though something enormous had exhaled through the cellar, and every bottle on the racks shivered in its cradle at once.

Wren was on her feet before the second toll. "That's not for me," she said. "Smugglers don't get bells. What closes the gates of the royal palace at sundown?"

Ivo stood slowly, and the taste of bitter almond was still on his tongue, and the wine in his cup had gone perfectly still, mirror-flat, reflecting the candle like a small dark eye.

"I don't know," he said. "But the stars are going to be involved. I can feel it in my teeth."

Wren stared at him. "In your—"

"Taster's superstition." He was already moving toward the door, pressing the third stone, hearing the lock click open onto the dark passage beyond. "Go north, Wren. Take the Grey Stair. Trust no gate."

Her hand caught his arm at the threshold — brief, hard, four years compressed into a grip — and then she was gone up the smuggler's stair with her hood up and her cup abandoned, and Ivo Ravensworth stood alone in the shaking cellar, holding the taste of a wine he could not name, as the second bell tolled and the first star over Thornfell quietly went out.
