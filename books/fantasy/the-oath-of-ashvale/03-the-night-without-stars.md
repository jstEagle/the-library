## 3. The Night Without Stars

The stars went out all at once, and Ivo Ravensworth was the first man in the palace to notice, because Ivo Ravensworth was always the first to notice the things no one else thought mattered.

He was on the roof of the tasters' wing with a cup of watered wine he had already tasted for himself — a habit his colleagues found morbidly funny — when the sky above Thornfell emptied. There was no flicker, no dimming, no polite fade. One heartbeat the stars were there, the old familiar scatter of them that smugglers navigated by and poets lied about, and the next they were gone, as if a hand had passed over the mountains and closed its fingers.

Not clouded. Clouds had texture, movement, a smell of rain or snow on their approach. The sky over Thornfell was simply *blank*, black as the inside of a locked box, and it stretched from the western peaks of the Ashvale range to the eastern shoulders where the border stones stood.

Ivo stood very still.

In ten years of tasting for the crown he had learned that catastrophe rarely announced itself with trumpets. Catastrophe arrived the way poison did: quietly, in small doses, in flavors that seemed almost right until they weren't. A stew that tasted faintly of hay when it should have tasted of thyme. A wine whose sweetness sat a half-second too long on the tongue. The world gave warnings, but only to those who made a discipline of noticing, and so Ivo had spent a decade becoming a discipline.

The sky had just given him one.

Below him, the palace went on burning its hundred lamps, unaware. Somewhere in the kitchens the night bakers were arguing about saffron. Somewhere in the west wing a drunk courtier was singing something about spring. The ordinary machinery of a court that believed itself eternal, grinding on.

Then the bells began.

They started in the ward-keepers' tower — three strokes, silence, three strokes — the old alarm pattern that hadn't been rung in living memory, the one drilled into every child of the palace guard until it had become a bedtime rhythm rather than a warning. *Three and three and three.* The wards are falling. The wards are falling.

Ivo set down his cup with great care, the way he did everything now, and went inside to dress.

* * *

By the time he reached the lower courtyard, the palace had remembered what fear felt like.

Servants ran with armfuls of candles as if wax could answer darkness. Guards were doubled at gates that had not been fully barred since the last king's coronation. A chamberlain stood in the middle of the chaos shouting names at no one, his voice cracking like green wood. And through all of it moved the quiet men and women of the Warders' Order in their grey cloaks, faces lit from below by the pale glow of their testing-lanterns, saying nothing, which frightened everyone more than shouting would have.

"Master Ravensworth." A young warder caught his sleeve — he recognized her vaguely, Serah Vail, the one they said would rise high before she burned out or burned up; her lantern threw shadows like cracks across her face. "You're wanted. The Lord Marshal's summons."

"I'm a taster," Ivo said. "Poison is my jurisdiction. Poison and nothing else."

"The Lord Marshal says otherwise." She hesitated, and something in her face shifted — not sympathy, exactly, but the recognition of one professional of unpleasantness meeting another. "He says you know the roads."

There it was. The sentence he had been waiting ten years for, arriving precisely on schedule, the way dread does when you've built your life on a foundation you know will crack.

Because it was true. It was the other thing Ivo was, the thing beneath the thing. The king's taster kept more than a ledger of flavors. He kept a ledger of roads — the smuggler paths through Thornfell that no map showed, learned in another life, in another name, from people who had trusted him. Every hidden pass, every warden's blind spot, every goat-track that crossed the border where the wards stood weakest because the smugglers had paid to keep them weak.

He knew those roads the way he knew poisons: completely, intimately, and with a cost he preferred not to count.

"I'll come," he said, because refusal had never actually been on offer, and followed her through the courtyard into the keep.

* * *

The war room smelled of cold iron and colder men.

Maps covered the long table, and around it stood the Lord Marshal, two chancellors, a woman from the treasury whose title Ivo didn't know, and the king's own spymaster, a soft-spoken horror named Aldous Crane who collected secrets the way Ivo collected antidotes. Candles guttered in brackets, but the room's real light came from a single object at the table's center: a border-stone shard, brought down from Thornfell at ruinous speed by relay riders, still wrapped in grey ward-cloth.

They unwrapped it while Ivo watched.

The stone should have glowed. Every child knew that border-stones glowed faintly blue, the visible breath of the wards that had held Thornfell's marches safe for three hundred years. This one was dark. Not cracked, not drained — dark, in the way a lamp is dark when the flame has been *taken* rather than blown out. And across its face, drawn in something that might have been ash and might have been dried blood, curled a spiral sigil Ivo had never seen and could not stop looking at.

"When did this reach us?" the Lord Marshal asked.

"Two hours past," said a courier, grey-faced with road dust. "The garrison at Thornwatch reports the same at every stone along forty miles. All at once, my lord. Not failing. Gone. And the sky—"

"We know about the sky."

"There are villages," the courier pressed, as if he'd been ordered to say it and hated it, "that report no answer at their doors. Whole hamlets, dark, and when the patrol rode in—" He stopped.

"When the patrol rode in," Crane finished softly, "they found chalk marks on every door. That will be all."

The courier left. The silence he left behind was worse than the news.

The Chancellor of the Left Hand cleared her throat. "Superstition. Chalk and fright. What matters is the military situation. If the wards are down, what stands between us and whatever did this?"

"Nothing," said the ward-keeper's representative, an old man whose hands shook. "That is what the wards *were*, Chancellor. There is no army behind them. There is no siege to lift. There is only—" he gestured at the sigil on the stone, and his shaking worsened, "—only that."

"Which is?"

No one answered. Which told Ivo more than any answer could have: they knew, some of them, or suspected, and the knowing was unspeakable in this room.

Crane turned then, unhurried, and looked directly at Ivo for the first time. His eyes were the color of dishwater and just as hard to read anything clean out of.

"Master Ravensworth. You served, before your appointment. In the Thornfell country."

"I was younger then," Ivo said. "And less employed."

"You traveled it. Extensively. Off the record." Crane smiled without warmth. "We have records anyway. You know the passes. The ways across the border that don't rely on the king's roads, the king's forts, or the king's wards."

"The smugglers' roads." Ivo kept his voice level. "Yes."

"And the smugglers," Crane continued, folding his hands, "paid tolls. To whom? For what? You would know better than anyone in this kingdom."

The question underneath the question unfolded itself in Ivo's mind like a flower of bad design. They didn't want a guide. Guides were cheap; guides were soldiers with good boots. They wanted someone who understood what the smugglers' tolls had been *buying*, and why the wards had died the moment the stars did, and whether the two facts were the same fact.

He thought of Wren, somewhere beyond that blank sky, on those same roads. He thought of the last thing she had said to him, and the thing he had done after, the ledger-entry he could never burn out of his memory no matter how many flavors he catalogued over it.

"My lord Marshal," Ivo said, "what is it you want me to do?"

"Ride for Thornfell," the Lord Marshal said. "Tonight. Take the smuggler roads. Find what killed the wards, find what happened to the villages, and report back through the relay at Thornwatch. You will travel quietly and alone."

"Alone," Ivo repeated.

"A man known to the smugglers travels best alone," Crane said gently. "And Master Ravensworth, let us be honest with one another, since honesty costs so little tonight. If the roads are watched, a famous taster is expendable. If they are not, he is essential. Either way, the crown thanks you in advance."

There it was again, that word. *Expendable.* Spoken aloud, politely, in a candlelit room, as though it were a rank he'd earned rather than a grave awaiting its date.

Ivo looked down at the dark border-stone and its spiral sigil, and felt the old sensation rise in him — the taster's certainty, the palate's verdict rendered before the mind consents. Something was wrong here, wrong in the deep structure of the evening, wrong the way a broth is wrong before the tongue can name the herb. The stars had not fallen. They had been *collected*. Someone had called in a debt written against the sky itself, and the crown's men were sending a single disposable servant to walk into the collection.

"Send word ahead to Thornwatch," Ivo said. "Tell them to expect me in six days if I take the Hollow Combe route. Seven if the weather argues."

Crane raised an eyebrow. "You accept so readily?"

"I accept," Ivo said, "because you'd send me regardless, and this way I choose my own road." He paused at the door, and could not help adding, in the flat voice he used for reporting poison: "My lords. Whatever bought your wards for three hundred years — check your ledgers. Debts like that don't die. They come due, all at once, on a night with no stars."

No one answered. But as he left, he saw the treasury woman pull a ledger toward her and open it with fingers that were not quite steady.

* * *

He provisioned in the dead hours before dawn, when the palace kitchens belonged to him alone.

Bread, cheese, dried fruit, a skin of wine he tested twice. His old tasting-kit, cleaned and oiled. Antidotes for eleven common poisons and three uncommon ones, wrapped in oilcloth. And beneath the floor-slate where he kept the things that were not tools — a folded scrap of paper he had carried for ten years and read perhaps a hundred times, though he needed neither the paper nor the memory; both were carved too deep to wear.

*A promise,* it said, in a hand that slanted like wind-blown rain. *You'll owe me a road someday, taster. And I collect.*

Wren. Ten years gone, exiled by his own testimony — the price he'd paid to keep his post, his immunity, his skin — and every year on the anniversary he told himself the debt was settled, that betrayal was simply a flavor he'd swallowed whole and learned to live with.

But he had never learned to live with it. He had only learned to taste around it.

Now the stars over her mountains were gone, and the crown was sending him toward her on roads she had taught him, carrying orders from the men he had betrayed her to. Dread arrived on schedule, yes. But so, apparently, did the reckoning.

Ivo laced his boots, shouldered his pack, and slipped out through the cellar door that only tasters knew — the same door, he realized with a grim twist of the mouth, through which he had once smuggled contraband wine for a woman with rain-grey eyes and a laugh like breaking ice.

The sky above the palace was still empty. Still black. Still waiting.

Somewhere under that starless dark, the mountain breathed.

He walked toward it.
