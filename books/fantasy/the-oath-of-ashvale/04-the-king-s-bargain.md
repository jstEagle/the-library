## 4. The King's Bargain

They came for him at the hour when even the palace rats held their breath.

Ivo had not slept. He had spent the night in the taster's chamber with his ledger open before him, reading by candlelight the entries he had made across ten years — *henbane, third vintage, masked with honey; foxglove in the duchess's tonic, dose meant for a child; arsenic in the ambassador's salt* — and beneath all of them, in a hand steadier than it had any right to be, the single line he had written on the night without stars:

*The wards are down. The king knew.*

He had known it the way a man knows frost coming through a cracked window: not by seeing the crack, but by feeling the cold where cold should not be. The stars over Thornfell had gone out like snuffed candles, and in the panic that followed Ivo had watched faces instead of watching the sky, because that was his trade — watching faces. And the king's face, when the news was brought, had shown no surprise. Only calculation. A man doing sums.

Now the summons came, and it did not come through the usual channels. No chamberlain, no page with a silver rod. Instead two men of the king's own guard, faces he half-knew from years of standing behind the royal chair, and between them an old servant named Maren who had carried messages for three kings and whose eyes had seen everything and approved of nothing.

"The king will see you," Maren said. "Alone."

"Kings don't see tasters alone," Ivo said.

"This one does tonight." Maren's voice dropped. "Wear your plain coat, Ravensworth. What happens in that room never happened."

The corridors were wrong at this hour. Torchlight pooled where torchlight should have run along the walls; servants pressed themselves into alcoves as Ivo passed, and more than one looked at him with something that was not quite pity. They knew what he was. The king's taster. The man who swallowed death so others needn't. Useful the way a shield is useful, and as little mourned when dented.

The king's private study sat behind three doors and a turning stair, and Ivo had been there exactly once before — the day he'd taken the oath. Ten years ago. He remembered the taste of that day: iron and beeswax and fear.

Tonight it tasted of ash.

* * *

King Aldric IV was not at his desk. He stood at the window with his back turned, an old man in a room built for a young one, and the window gave onto the east — onto the black smear of the Thornfell mountains where stars ought to have been burning above the passes and were not.

"Do you know," the king said without turning, "what my grandfather paid for his victory at Redmere?"

Ivo said nothing. One learned quickly, in the presence of kings, that questions were often not questions.

"Nothing," Aldric said. "That is the answer everyone gives. Blood, they say. Steel. But it isn't true. He paid *something*. Everything is paid for." At last he turned, and his eyes were pale and patient, the eyes of a man who had spent sixty years being agreed with. "You have served my table ten years, Ravensworth. You have taken into your body enough poison to kill a garrison. Why?"

"It is my office, Majesty."

"It is your *office*, yes." The king crossed to the fire and warmed hands that did not look cold. "But why does the office work? Any man can be taught to detect henbane. Few survive the learning. You survived it whole. Your tongue, your blood, your—" he paused, selecting the word with a jeweler's care, "—your *soul*. There is something in you, Ravensworth, that poisons cannot grip. Do you know what that is worth?"

"No, Majesty."

"Neither do I," the king said pleasantly. "That has always troubled me. Sit."

Ivo sat. The chair across from the desk was low, deliberately so, and the king remained standing, and both facts were chosen, and Ivo noted them the way he noted the sheen of oil on soup.

"You know the Thornfell roads," the king said. "Do not insult us both by denying it. The border lords have complained for years that contraband moves through passes that appear on no surveyor's map. Someone has been teaching smugglers those roads, or someone who knows them has been smuggling himself. We have wondered which." His gaze settled on Ivo like a weight placed gently on a scale. "We stopped wondering last night. When the wards fell, we needed a man who could move through Thornfell unseen. And there was only ever one name on every list."

"Majesty—"

"I am not finished." The king opened a drawer and set something on the desk between them.

It was a casket. Small — small enough to carry under one arm — of dark wood bound in iron, sealed with wax the color of dried blood, and pressed into the wax, a seal Ivo knew better than his own name.

The royal seal. The crowned stag. But struck *twice*, once upright and once inverted, overlapping — and Ivo's stomach went cold, because he had seen that double-struck seal before. Not on proclamations. On documents that passed through the chancery at night, carried by men who did not salute. On papers the tasters were never meant to see but sometimes saw anyway, because a man who watches faces learns to watch hands, and hands carry paper.

Soul-contracts. That was the word the cellar-whispers used, the word the old servants used when they thought no one young was listening. *The crown sells what it cannot keep,* Maren had told him once, drunk on festival wine, her face terrible and calm. *A hundred years of favors, boy. You think power is free?*

"I require this delivered," the king said, "to a court beneath the mountain. You will be given the roads — the ones only you know. You will be given escort as far as the foothills, and after that, nothing, because nothing can follow you there. Before the new moon. Twelve days."

"And if I refuse?"

The king smiled, and it was almost kind. "Tasters who refuse are retired. You know how we retire tasters, Ravensworth. You've prepared enough of the dishes yourself."

Silence. The fire ticked. Somewhere far below, a door closed.

"What is in it?" Ivo asked.

"A debt," said the king. "Payment of a debt. That is all you need carry. You need not open it. You must not open it. It is sealed with my sign and sign reversed, and that seal is older than my crown, and what it binds, it binds."

Ivo looked at the casket and felt the ledger of flavors rise unbidden in his mind — the way a man's trade betrays him in quiet moments. The wood smelled of mountain pine and something beneath it, something sweetish and wrong, like fruit rotting in a locked room. He had smelled that sweetness once before. In the chancery, on the night papers. The smell of contracts written in something other than ink.

"There is a second matter," the king said, and now his voice changed, became softer, and Ivo understood with a lurch that the softness was the trap, had always been the trap, that everything before had merely been the setting of the table. "A woman. Wren, called the Wren of Thornfell. Courier, smuggler, outlaw. Condemned ten years ago for crimes against the supply lines of this crown. Exiled under sentence of death should she return." He watched Ivo with those pale patient eyes. "You knew her."

It was not a question. Ivo kept his face still, kept it the smooth blank mask he wore at the tasting table, but inside him something old and scarred turned over like a knife in a wound.

"I knew of her, Majesty."

"You loved her." Still not a question. "Do not lie to me. I have read your file. Every man who serves me is read entire. You loved her, and she was condemned, and you were not, and you rose to be the second most trusted mouth in this palace while she ran the mountains like a hunted fox." The king leaned forward. "Here is my bargain, Ravensworth, and hear all of it before you speak. Deliver the casket before the new moon, and Wren's sentence is struck from the rolls. Full pardon. She may walk through any gate in this kingdom with her head high. She may stand in the market square at Ashvale and no man may touch her. That is the offer."

"And the cost of refusing?"

"You asked about retirement already." The king straightened. "There is a third thing, and then you may go. If you deliver the casket and speak of what is in it — if you open it, if you read it, if you so much as breathe upon that seal — the pardon dies, the sentence revives, and I will spend whatever it costs to see her taken. Am I understood?"

"Understood, Majesty."

"Then take it up. It is heavy for its size. Most debts are."

Ivo rose and lifted the casket. The king was right — it weighed far more than its size should allow, a dense dead weight that seemed to pull toward the floor as though the mountain itself were already reeling in a line. Against his palms, through the iron binding, he felt a faint vibration, rhythmic and slow.

Like breathing.

*One favor at a time,* he thought. *One road, one delivery, one pardon.* It was the arithmetic every smuggler lived by, and he had lived by it longer than most, and he knew — he had always known — that the sums never came out clean.

"Twelve days," the king said. "The new moon waits for no courier. Go home, Ravensworth. Kiss your ledger goodbye. You will want your hands free."

At the door, Ivo paused. He could not help it. The question had been sitting in his chest since the night the stars went out, and it came out now quieter than he intended.

"The wards, Majesty. Did we pay for those too?"

The king returned to his window, to the starless east, and did not turn around.

"We paid for everything," he said. "Go."

* * *

Maren walked him back through the sleeping palace, carrying a lamp though the corridor torches burned fine, and Ivo understood that the lamp was for privacy — light held close shows less than darkness hides.

"You knew," he said. "When you fetched me."

"I suspected." The old woman did not look at him. "I've carried messages for three kings, boy. This is the first time I've carried a man to his selling."

"I'm not sold."

"No?" She stopped at the junction where their ways divided, and raised the lamp so it lit her face from below, hollowing her cheeks, making her look like something carved on a tomb. "Ten years you swallowed their poison and called it service. Tonight you swallowed something larger and called it a bargain. Same dish, bigger bowl." Her voice gentled, which was worse. "What's in the box, Ivo?"

"I'm not permitted to ask."

"That's not what I said."

He said nothing. The casket's weight shifted against his arm, that slow breathing pulse, and Maren's eyes went to it, and something moved through her face — recognition, and grief, and an old fear waking up.

"Double-struck seal," she whispered. "Gods preserve us. I haven't seen that since the old queen's day." She gripped his wrist, sudden and strong. "Listen to me, and listen with the part of you that tastes. Whatever they tell you is in that box — payment, debt, gift — boxes like that don't hold payments. They hold *names*. And a name in a box under that seal belongs to whoever holds the box, forever. Do you understand me? Forever is not a figure of speech below the mountain."

"You've seen one opened?"

"I've seen what came back." She released his wrist and stepped back into the shadow beyond her lamp's reach. "The new moon, he said. Twelve days. Then go faster than twelve days, Ivo Ravensworth. Because the moon isn't the only one counting."

She was gone before he could answer, her lamp bobbing away down the dark corridor, and Ivo stood alone in the palace that had fed him and poisoned him for ten years, holding a debt he was forbidden to read, purchased with a pardon for a woman he had betrayed.

*Wren,* he thought, and the name hurt going down like everything else he had ever swallowed.

Somewhere north, past the foothills, past the burned-out wards and the villages with chalked doors, the mountain waited with its courts and its ledgers and its breathing stone. Somewhere in it — or so the whispers said, so the desperate hoped — souls could be found, could be bought back, could be *held*. The king thought he was sending a courier. The king thought the bargain ran one way.

Ivo climbed to his chamber and set the casket on his table beside the ledger of flavors, the two books of his life side by side: one recording everything he had tasted, and one, sealed shut, holding everything he had lost.

He did not sleep. He sharpened his knives instead, and packed the plain coat, and memorized the first pass in his head — the smuggler's cut above Greywater, the one that opened onto the high moors — and all the while his thumb kept returning to the double-struck seal, tracing the crowned stag and its inverted twin, feeling beneath the cooling wax that faint, patient pulse.

*Breathing,* he thought again. *Like something asleep. Like something waiting to be woken.*

Near dawn, Maren's words came back to him with the clarity of things too late to matter: *boxes like that don't hold payments.*

He looked at the casket. The casket seemed, in the grey light, to look back.

He slung it under his arm, heavier than any debt he had ever carried, and went down to find the road.
