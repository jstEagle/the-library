## 6. The Ward-Keeper's Gallows

The town of Hollowmere had one gallows, and it sat in the market square like a piece of civic furniture — a thing the town was quietly proud of, the way a man is proud of a good roof. Ivo had passed through Hollowmere four times in his smuggling years, and each time the gallows had been empty, its beam bleached grey by weather, crows using it as a perch with the indifference of long tenancy.

It was not empty now.

He saw the crowd first from the ridge above the town, a knot of perhaps two hundred souls gathered in the cold morning light, breath steaming, children hoisted onto shoulders for the view. The road down wound through bare orchards, and Ivo rode it slowly, hood up, his horse's shoes wrapped in felt — an old smuggler's habit that had kept him alive on roads where sound traveled farther than sight. He should have skirted the town entirely. Every instinct he owned, every hard-won rule of survival, said *keep moving, keep quiet, be no one.*

But he could see the scaffold clearly from the orchard's edge, and on the scaffold stood a woman in the black-and-silver of the Wardens' Order — or what remained of it, the uniform torn at the shoulder where someone had ripped away her badge of rank, her hair cropped short in the style they gave prisoners so the rope would have nothing to catch on.

A placard hung around her neck, painted in careful official letters: **SERAH VAIL, WARD-KEEPER OF THORNFELL — FOR THE FAILURE OF HER WATCH.**

Ivo reined in among the apple trees and stared.

*For the failure of her watch.*

The wards were dead. He had seen it — the whole sky over Thornfell gone starless in a single night, the mountain's ancient protections snuffed like candles between a finger and thumb. And here, three days' ride east, the Wardens had found someone to blame for it. Found her quickly, too. Faster than any honest investigation could have managed.

Which meant they hadn't investigated. Which meant they already knew what they wanted the answer to be, and Serah Vail had simply been the nearest throat.

He knew how that worked. He had been on the other side of it once, the man who supplied the name while others built the rope. His hands went cold inside his gloves, and he told himself it was the morning air.

Below, a priest was reading the rites. His voice carried thin across the square, all the usual phrases about judgment and mercy, delivered with the enthusiasm of a man reading a grain inventory. The executioner stood beside the condemned woman, checking the knot with professional tenderness. A detachment of Wardens in full dress lined the scaffold's base — six of them, halberds grounded, faces expressionless behind their visors. Their commander stood apart, reading from a scroll, and even at this distance Ivo recognized the cadence of a sentence being pronounced: the formal, rehearsed music of law doing its work.

*Justice,* he thought, watching. *This is what it looks like from the outside.*

He had spent ten years tasting for a king. He understood institutions the way a fisherman understands water — intimately, practically, without illusions. Institutions did not seek truth. They sought closure. They took the vast, unanswerable horror of a dead night sky and shrank it down to the size of one woman, one crime, one drop through a trapdoor, and then everyone could go home and sleep.

Except that the stars were still gone. Hanging this woman would light none of them again.

Ivo sat his horse in the orchard and conducted, as he always did, a brief internal audit. It was a habit from the tasting table: assess the risk, weigh the cost, swallow or refuse. Riding into that square to interfere with a lawful execution — because it *was* lawful, that was the poison in it — against six armed Wardens and a town's worth of witnesses, in daylight, with the roads crawling after the ward-fall—

Every calculation came back the same way. *Don't.*

And underneath the calculations, quiet and immovable, sat the other fact: she was the only person he had found in three days of riding who might know what actually killed the wards. Not the Order's version. Not the crown's version. Hers. A disgraced Ward-keeper of Thornfell would have spent years inside the machinery — the maintenance schedules, the resonance records, the old fault lines in the mountain's skin. If anyone in the world could tell him what the spiral sigil on those chalked doors meant, whether the under-court of the old stories was real and walking again, it was her.

And in about four minutes she would be dead, and the knowledge would die with her, and Ivo Ravensworth would ride on toward the mountain knowing exactly as much as a blind man knows about the color blue.

*You're doing it again,* said a voice in his head that sounded unpleasantly like Wren. *Weighing it like a dish. Like you can taste the danger and spit it out if it's bitter.*

She had hated that about him, near the end. The way he treated every choice as a tasting — hold it on the tongue, catalog it, decide with the head and never the heart. She'd called it his cage. He'd called it survival. One of them had been right, and the evidence suggested it wasn't him.

The priest finished. The commander rolled up his scroll. The executioner offered Serah Vail the traditional hood, and she shook her head — a small, sharp motion, almost contemptuous — and said something Ivo couldn't hear that made the executioner step back half a pace.

She scanned the crowd then. Deliberately. Face by face, unhurried, as though she were memorizing them, or looking for someone. Her eyes were pale grey, and even at eighty yards Ivo felt the weight of them pass over the orchard, over him, and move on. There was no fear in her stance. That frightened him more than fear would have. He had seen brave men at the block, and they trembled somewhere — in the hands, in the jaw. This woman stood as though the gallows were a podium and the hanging merely the least interesting item on the morning's agenda.

The executioner moved to the lever.

Ivo was already moving.

Later — much later, in worse moments than this one — he would try to reconstruct the decision and find there was nothing to reconstruct. No weighing. No ledger. He had simply discovered himself in motion, boots out of the stirrups, horse wheeling back up through the trees, taking the drover's path he'd marked on his first pass through the valley twenty years ago — the one that let a rider come down behind the market square through the tanner's yard, where the smell kept everyone away and the fence was broken in exactly one place.

Some knowledge you never lose. Some roads live in your feet.

He came off the path into the yard's mud, through the gap in the fence, and into the back of the crowd just as the executioner's hand touched the lever — and the crowd, bless its soft-hearted, gossip-hungry mass, closed around him without a glance, because crowds always make room for a man pushing purposefully, assuming he is someone's business.

The scaffold was fifteen feet away. Six Wardens. A commander with a sword. Two hundred witnesses whose attention was fixed on the trapdoor with the terrible hunger people bring to executions, wanting it done, wanting it over, wanting to have seen it.

Ivo had spent a decade in the most dangerous room in the kingdom learning one lesson above all others: poison works not because it is strong but because it arrives where no one is looking. The principle scaled.

He pulled the felt from his horse's hooves? No — he'd left the horse in the yard. He pulled instead the small flask from his coat, the one every traveler carried, and he hurled it underhand into the scaffold's supports, and shouted, in the flat northern accent of his boyhood, the words every border town dreads:

*"Ward-plague! She's got the ward-plague — look at her neck!"*

It was nonsense. There was no such disease. But the words hit a crowd already primed by three days of rumors — the dead stars, the empty villages, the chalked doors — the way a spark hits a barn full of threshing dust. Heads turned toward the scaffold. Someone screamed. Someone else repeated it louder, and the repetition did what repetition does. The front rank of the crowd surged backward, away from the platform, and the surge took the Wardens' line with it — not breaking it, but bending it, turning six disciplined soldiers into six men trying to keep their footing while civilians clawed past.

The commander turned to shout order at the crowd, which meant his back was to Ivo for exactly as long as Ivo needed.

He crossed the space in eight strides, went up the scaffold steps two at a time, and put his knife — his tasting knife, slender and very sharp, the only weapon he'd carried for ten years — through the rope an inch above the knot.

Serah Vail dropped to the boards in a graceless sprawl, wrists still bound, and looked up at him with those pale eyes, and Ivo had one absurd instant to notice that she didn't look grateful. She looked *annoyed*, the way a scholar looks when someone spills ink on a manuscript.

"You," she said, "are the single stupidest man I have ever seen."

"Everyone says so." He cut her wrist-bonds. "Can you run?"

"Obviously." She got her feet under her, swayed once — the blood still finding its way back to her brain from the constricted neck — and then, before following him down the steps, she did something he would think about for years afterward. She picked up the placard from around her neck, snapped its cord deliberately, and laid it flat on the trapdoor, face up, where the commander would have to stand on her name to pursue them.

Then she ran.

* * *

They went through the tannery, through the orchards, up the drover's path, and did not slow until the town was a smudge of smoke behind them and the land rose into pine forest where the road became a suggestion. Ivo finally called a halt in a hollow where a stream cut through moss-covered stone, and they stood gasping in the cold air, steam rising off them both like a pair of kettle-horses.

Serah Vail pulled the rope-burn collar of her shirt away from her throat and examined the red line there with clinical detachment. "They'll have riders out within the hour," she said. "Verrick — the commander — keeps hounds. Slow ones, but persistent."

"I know a route north that avoids the ford."

"Of course you do." She looked at him properly for the first time, and Ivo felt the examination like a physician's touch — thorough, impersonal, slightly unnerving. "You cut me down. You had no plan beyond the knife, did you."

"I had a route."

"A route isn't a plan. A plan has an *after*." She waded to the stream, knelt, and drank directly from it, cupping her hands, unbothered by the cold. When she rose she said, "So tell me why. Men don't spend their own lives on strangers. What am I worth to you?"

Ivo considered lying. He was good at lying; it was most of his profession. But something in the directness of her made the lie feel like a wasted coin.

"The wards are dead," he said. "I've ridden through the passes. Villages empty. Doors marked with a spiral sigil in chalk. I'm going to the mountain, and you're the only person I've met who might know what I'm walking into."

"And the king sent you." It wasn't a question.

"How—"

"Nobody cuts down a Ward-keeper out of civic feeling, and nobody rides alone into Thornfell country three days after the fall unless they're carrying someone's errand." She wrung out her cropped hair. "The crown. Which means the crown knows something about the fall it isn't saying. Which means—" she stopped, and he watched her arrive at the thought the way a man watches someone step into cold water. "Which means my Order didn't fail. My Order was *silenced*. Or bought." She laughed, briefly, without any humor in it. "Ten years keeping the Thornfell line. I reported every anomaly, every resonance drift, every request for inspection that came back denied from above. Denied from above, Master—?"

"Ravensworth. Ivo Ravensworth."

"Denied from above, Ravensworth, and now the sky goes dark and they hang me for it in a market square before my own report can reach anyone who might read it." She sat down abruptly on a mossy stone, and for the first time he saw the cost surface in her — not fear, never fear, but a fury so deep and so cold it had passed through anger and come out the other side as something geological. "They didn't hang me for failing Thornfell. They hanged me for knowing Thornfell."

"What killed the wards?" Ivo asked. "Tell me."

She looked up at him, and whatever she saw in his face made her pause. "That depends on how much time you have, and how badly you want to sleep tonight."

"Badly enough to stay awake."

"Then walk," said Serah Vail, standing, brushing moss from her ruined uniform with brisk, furious precision. "And listen. Because the wards were never built to keep armies out, Ravensworth. And the sigil you saw chalked on those doors — the spiral — that isn't a threat. As far as the mountain is concerned, it's a *receipt*."

She started up the slope. After a moment, Ivo followed.

Behind them, far to the south, a hound began to bay.
