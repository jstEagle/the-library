## 7. What the Wards Were For

The rope had left a bruise in a perfect ring around her throat, and Serah Vail found she could not stop touching it.

They had made camp in the shell of a shepherd's hut below the gallows ridge, because Ivo Ravensworth argued — with the flat pragmatism of a man who counted drops for a living — that the Order would not look for their escaped prisoner in sight of her own noose. Arrogant. Correct. She hated that he was correct almost as much as she hated the rope.

Now he sat across the dead hearth from her, turning a strip of dried mutton over the coals with the patience of someone who had learned never to trust food left alone, even food he'd prepared himself. The smuggler — Wren — was not there. He traveled alone; that much Serah had gathered on the long walk down from the ridge, in the hours when her legs still shook and her voice came out scraped. Alone, and heading toward Thornfell, and carrying a royal casket whose contents he either knew or refused to know.

"You cut me down," she said.

"I did."

"Give me one reason that wasn't stupidity."

He didn't look up. "You were the only person on that scaffold who looked angry instead of afraid. Angry people know something." He turned the meat again. "Also, you're going to tell me what killed the wards."

"There it is," Serah said.

"There it is," he agreed.

The fire popped. Outside, the night was wrong in a way she had spent eleven years training herself to notice: no stars. Not clouds, not haze, not the soft grey lid of weather. Absence. Over Thornfell the sky was a page torn out of a book, and every hour it stayed empty the hair on her arms rose a little higher.

She should have let him hang her. That was the disciplined thought, the thought of a woman who had taken the oaths at fourteen and believed them until six days ago. The Order of the Vigil kept its secrets the way other institutions kept their silver — locked, catalogued, guarded by people who profited from the guarding. She had been Keeper-Second of the Thornfell line. She had held the keys to the high ward-stones. And when the wards died, she had done the arithmetic faster than her superiors could burn their correspondence, which was why she had been standing on a gallows built by her own brothers-in-oath.

Ivo watched her across the fire with those taster's eyes — grey, patient, the eyes of a man who had made a profession of noticing things too late to matter but early enough to survive.

"They'll have declared me traitor by now," she said. "Heretic, probably. They like to stack the charges."

"Will they come after us?"

"No. They'll come after *you*, once they learn who freed me, but only as an afterthought. You're a footnote." She pulled the rope-mark's ring with one finger. "I'm the scandal. A Keeper who failed her line gets a trial and a grave. A Keeper who *talks* gets worse."

"So talk," Ivo said. "To me first. Consider it practice."

And because she was tired, and bruised, and because the stars were gone and staying gone, and because some small stubborn ember in her had decided somewhere between the kick of the cart and his knife at the hemp that if she was going to be damned she would at least be *accurate* — she talked.

* * *

"Do you know what the border wards are for?" she asked him.

"Keeping things out." He said it easily, the way a man says something he has never had cause to question. "Armies. Raiders. Whatever comes down out of the Thornfell passes."

"That's what the crown tells its soldiers. It's what the Order tells its recruits. It's what's carved above the gatehouse at Highwatch, in letters big enough to read from horseback." Serah fed a twist of bark to the coals and watched it catch. "*Here we hold the line.* Very stirring. Very false."

Ivo's hands went still over the fire. Not dramatically — just the small cessation of motion she would later recognize as his version of flinching.

"The wards were raised nine hundred years ago," she said. "Before the kingdom had this name. Before the mountain had courts in it, or the courts had gentry in them, or the gentry had appetites. Back then the thing beneath Thornfell was young and hungry and shapeless, and the first ward-keepers did exactly what the carvings say. They held the line. Stone and oath and blood-price, and it worked, and the mountain sulked behind it for three hundred years like a wolf outside a fold."

"And then?"

"And then the kingdom got old, and clever, and poor in the ways that matter." She met his eyes. "Have you ever heard the phrase *borrowed power*, taster? In the treasury sense?"

"I've tasted enough wine bought with unpaid debts to know the concept."

"Then you understand kingdoms better than most chancellors." She laughed, and it hurt her throat, and she was glad of both. "A century ago — ninety-eight years, if you want the ugly precision — the crown had a problem. Wars on two borders, a plague in the riverlands, a treasury hollowed by a king who liked building things with his name on them. And someone at court — history has eaten his name, or paid to have it eaten — remembered the old stories. Remembered that the thing under the mountain was still hungry, and that hunger can be *negotiated with*."

The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the dark, wind moved through a valley that should have been full of sheep-bells and wasn't, and Serah waited until it passed.

"The wards don't keep armies out," she said. "No army in a hundred years has come down those passes worth the stone they're carved on. The wards keep the *court* in. Or they kept it in. They are a fence around a creditor. Every soul-contract the crown has signed since the reign of Aldric the Second — and there are thousands, taster, I have seen the vault where they sleep — every one of those contracts is enforceable only if the mountain's collectors can cross the border to collect. The wards were the one thing standing between the crown's paper and the crown's creditors. And the crown signed them anyway. Signed them knowing. Because borrowed power doesn't ask what happens when the note comes due. Borrowed power assumes you'll be dead, and someone else's problem."

Ivo had gone very pale. Not the theatrical pallor of a shocked courtier — she knew that species well — but the grey, controlled drain of a man watching his own ledger rebalance itself into ruin.

"How many," he said quietly.

"That's the horror of it. It isn't even a number you can scream about." Serah spread her hands. "It's paperwork. Do you understand? It is *paperwork*. Contracts in triplicate. Signatures, seals, renewal clauses, grace periods. A village here when a harvest fails. A regiment there after a lost battle — whole companies marched to the mountain's edge and told they were being honored with a ceremony. An entire island parish in the year of the Grey Fever, written off like spoiled grain. The clerks who maintain the archive receive pensions. There are *promotions*. Men spend thirty years filing souls the way other men file tax rolls, and then retire to a cottage by the sea, and no one at their farewell dinner thinks anything of it."

"Souls," Ivo said. "Harvested. For what?"

"Power. What else?" Her mouth twisted. "The gentry under the mountain are old now — truly old, older than the wards, older than most of their own names — and age like theirs requires fuel. A soul burns slow and bright. One soul keeps a gentry lord beautiful for a decade. A hundred keeps the court warm through a hard century. And in exchange—" she ticked the payments off on her fingers, and hated how practiced the gesture felt, "—victories that shouldn't have been possible. Plagues that burned out early. Winters that broke before they starved anyone important. The kingdom has been running on credit for ninety-eight years, taster, and every coin of it was minted out of somebody's name."

He was silent for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice had changed — lower, stripped of the dry irony she was beginning to suspect was armor.

"The casket," he said. "The king sent me to fetch a sealed casket from the mountain court. Royal seal. I'm supposed to believe it's ransom gold, or a hostage treaty, or—" He stopped himself. Looked at the fire. "You've seen the seal on those contracts."

"I filed them for six years. I know the sigil better than my own name."

"Describe it."

"A crowned hand, open, palm upward," she said. "With a spiral cut into the palm."

Ivo closed his eyes. Whatever he saw behind them, he kept it there, behind the lids, the way he presumably kept everything — a man built like a vault, she thought, and wondered briefly what it cost to be built that way, and whether the price was ever paid all at once.

"And the wards dying," he said at last. "Six nights ago. The stars going out over Thornfell. If the wards kept the collectors in —"

"Then their death isn't an accident, or an attack, or a failure of maintenance, whatever lie the Order is currently feeding its own chapters." Serah heard her voice go flat and cold and recognized, distantly, that it was her father's voice, the assessor's voice, the voice that read ledgers aloud at funeral tables. "The wards died because someone wanted them dead. Someone with access to the anchor-stones. Someone inside the Vigil, or paying the Vigil, opened the fence on purpose — because the debt has come due, taster. Ninety-eight years of compounding interest, and the crown has run out of collateral it can sell without permission. So it sold permission. It opened the door and invited the collectors across the border to take what the signatures say they're owed."

"Thousands of souls."

"Thousands of *contracts*." She shook her head. "Most of the named souls are already collected — quietly, over decades, one fevered bed and one unexplained wasting at a time, always within the letter of the agreement. What's left outstanding is the principal. The recent signings. The ones sold in the last ten years, while the crown grew desperate and the mountain grew impatient." She paused. "There's a courier class the crown uses for the worst roads. Smugglers' paths, because the honest roads are watched and the dishonest ones aren't. The contracts travel by courier. The couriers are expendable, and the crown has a bad habit of spending its couriers along with everything else."

Something crossed Ivo's face then — fast, buried, gone — like a fish turning under ice. He looked at the fire and said nothing, and Serah, who had spent eleven years reading ward-signatures and stone-grain and the small treacheries of men in committee, chose for the moment to file it rather than press it. She was learning him the way she learned stones: by what he didn't say.

"So the Order," she went on, because the words were loose in her now and she wanted them out, wanted them *outside* her body where they couldn't fester. "The Vigil has known for a century. Not the rank and file — the rank and file get the carvings and the hymns and the noble fiction. But the Conclave knows. The archive is *theirs*. The contracts pass through our own strongroom for countersignature — do you understand that? The order sworn to hold the line has been notarizing the line's sale, generation after generation, taking the mountain's quiet stipend in endowed chantries and comfortable prebends, and calling it *stipendia custodum*. The keepers' bread." She laughed again, and it came out wrong. "I found the ledger myself. Six days ago. A hundred years of entries in my own order's hand. And I made the mistake every idealist makes."

"What mistake?"

"I assumed everyone else didn't know. I took the ledger to the Conclave." She looked into the fire. "They thanked me for my diligence. They relieved me of my keys. And four days later I was standing on a gallows with a charge sheet longer than my arm, and the wards were ash, and the sky over Thornfell had been *torn out like a page*."

The silence that followed had weight. Ivo fed another stick to the coals, and she noticed — the taster's habit, she supposed — that he checked the stick first, sniffed it, turned it in the light. Even his firewood got inspected. A man who trusts nothing that enters his body. She wondered what had taught him that, and suspected the answer was also the reason a royal taster walked alone toward a haunted mountain carrying a sealed box.

"If what you're saying is true," he said slowly, "then the casket the king sent me for—"

"—is either a payment," Serah finished for him, "or a receipt. And I'd stake my second hanging on which."

He looked at her across the fire, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the hut seemed very small around the two of them: a hanged woman and a poisoned man, sitting in the dark under a starless sky, trading the last intact pieces of their former lives like children swapping broken toys.

"Why tell me all of it?" he asked finally. "You owe me nothing. I dragged you off your own scaffold into a war you didn't choose."

"You cut me down because I looked angry," Serah said. "So here's your return on the investment: I'm telling you because anger without knowledge is just noise, and I have wasted enough of my life being the Order's noise. And because—" she touched the rope-bruise again, lightly, the way one touches a scar that hasn't finished deciding whether to form, "—because whoever opened those wards used the Vigil's own rites to do it. My rites. My stones. My name is on the failure, taster, fair or not, and I intend to find the hand that actually turned the key. Which means I need to go under the mountain. Which means, unfortunately, I need you."

"Me."

"Your roads. Your smuggler's map of the Thornfell passes — the real one, not the crown's version. And whatever it is you're carrying in that casket-shaped hole in your story." She leaned forward. "I'm not asking you to trust me. Trust is for people with clean hands. I'm asking you to make the same arithmetic I did: alone, we're both footnotes. Together, we might be a problem."

Ivo studied her for a long moment. The firelight moved in his eyes, and behind the grey patience she saw something turn over — the same submerged movement as before, the fish under the ice, larger this time, closer to the surface.

"Tomorrow," he said at last, "we reach the first of the hidden passes. There's a way through the scree below Raven's Tooth that isn't on any map the crown owns. If your wards are dead all the way to the border, we'll see it there — and if we see it there, then I'll show you what I'm carrying, and you can tell me how badly I've already been used."

"Agreed."

"And Vail." He glanced up at the torn place in the sky, where stars should have been. "These contracts. The names in them. Is there any record of the couriers? The people who carried the papers?"

Serah felt the question land somewhere it wasn't aimed, the way an arrow meant for a shield sometimes finds the gap beneath the arm.

"There's a courier register," she said carefully. "Names, routes, dates of final delivery. Why?"

Ivo turned the meat over the coals one more time, though it was long past done.

"Someone I need to look for," he said, "may be in it."

* * *

She slept in watches, as Keepers are trained, and woke once deep in the black hour to the sound of Ivo talking in his sleep — not words, exactly, but numbers and flavors, a litany of bitter notes and measured grains, a man counting poisons the way monks count prayers. In the morning she pretended not to have heard, and he pretended not to have spoken, and they broke camp under the starless sky like two conspirators who had discovered, overnight, that their conspiracy had a third member: the truth, riding silently between them, growing heavier with every mile toward Thornfell.

The pass below Raven's Tooth opened ahead of them by mid-morning — a seam in the scree no wider than a cart-track, invisible until you stood inside it, exactly the sort of road a smuggler loves and a soldier never finds. And at its mouth, where the old boundary-marker should have stood with its ward-sigils bright, the stones lay shattered in a spiral pattern that Serah recognized from a hundred years of archived contracts.

The crowned hand, open. The spiral cut into the palm.

Chalk-white. Fresh. Waiting.

"Come on, then," Ivo said quietly, and stepped through.
