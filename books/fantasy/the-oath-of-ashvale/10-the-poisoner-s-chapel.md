## 10. The Poisoner's Chapel

They found the chapel by its smell, which was the wrong smell for a chapel and the right smell for a man who had once made his living killing things.

Ivo had been following the chalk spirals for two days — small ones, half-erased, drawn on doorposts and well-sides by hands that wanted to be found but not too quickly. Serah Vail walked beside him with her ward-keeper's badge torn from her coat but the coat itself still carrying the shape of authority, the way a widow keeps wearing her ring. Wren's casket rode wrapped in sacking across Ivo's back, and every mile it grew heavier, though he knew — he had weighed it against his own guilt often enough now — that the weight was not in the wood.

The ash inside did not rattle. It settled. Like something sleeping, or listening.

"Smoke," Serah said, lifting her chin. "But not burning houses. Burning *herbs*."

Ivo tasted the wind. Ten years at a king's table will teach a man to read air the way priests read scripture. Under the smoke: wormwood, certainly. And under that, sweeter, almost shy — foxglove. And beneath everything, faint as a confession at the back of a crowded church, hemlock.

"That's a pharmacopoeia," he said quietly. "Somebody's burning their whole library."

"Or cleansing it."

The valley opened ahead of them without warning, the way mountain valleys do — one turn of the path and the world simply unfolded, as if it had been waiting for them to deserve the view. At its floor sat a village that was not quite a village anymore. Half the cottages stood empty, doors ajar, and on each empty doorpost was chalked the spiral sigil of the under-court, large and careful and final. But the other half — the occupied half — had smaller spirals, and beside each one, in different hands, in charcoal and berry-stain and what Ivo sincerely hoped was red ochre, someone had written names.

Not the court's marks. *Answers* to the court's marks. A tally being kept against the tally.

At the center of it all stood a chapel that had clearly never been consecrated by any order Ivo recognized. It was built of fieldstone and salvaged roof-beams and, if he was not mistaken, the upended hull of a smuggler's boat, its keel forming the ridge-beam like the spine of some great beached fish. Smoke curled from its chimney in a thin, deliberate line. Over the door, where a god's name should have been, hung a wooden sign carved with a single word:

**HOSPITAL**

And below it, smaller, in a hand that trembled only slightly:

**ALL WHO ARE MARKED MAY ENTER. NO ONE ELSE NEEDS TO.**

"He knows we're coming," Serah said.

"Of course he does. He's been watching us since the pass." Ivo shifted the casket. "The question is whether that's hospitality or triage."

They crossed the empty half of the village with their footsteps loud as drumbeats. In the doorway of one abandoned house, Ivo paused. The spiral on the doorpost was fresh, the chalk still bright. Beside it, someone had written a name — *Marta, who kept bees* — and under the name, in smaller letters: *collected at dusk, third day of the waning moon.* A record. A ledger. His own trade, done in grief instead of ink.

He touched the doorframe once, the way you'd touch a grave-marker, and walked on.

* * *

The inside of the chapel smelled like mercy and murder having a civil conversation.

Beds lined the walls — pallets, mostly, and three actual beds scavenged from better houses — and on them lay perhaps twenty people, ranging from children to an old woman whose hands were so knotted with age they looked like roots. Every one of them bore the mark: the spiral sigil, but not in chalk. This was under the skin, a grey-blue shadowing that moved when you watched it too long, coiling slowly inward like water finding a drain.

Ivo had seen the chalk versions. He had seen the caskets. He had seen, in a bandit's dying grip, the way ash reforms into a name. But he had never seen the mark *living* on a person before, and it took everything his taster's discipline had taught him not to let his face change.

Serah's face changed for both of them.

"This is what collection looks like," she said, very quietly. "I wrote reports about wards failing. I never—" She stopped. Started again. "I never thought about where the debt went. Only that it was paid."

"It goes somewhere," said a voice from the far end of the room. "Everything goes somewhere. That's rather the whole problem with creation."

The man who rose from behind a curtain of stitched sacking was old in the way that mountains are old — not bent, exactly, but worn down to essentials, as though life had spent decades sanding away everything about him that wasn't load-bearing. He was tall, or had been; he stooped now out of habit, the way tall men do around low ceilings and short tempers. He wore a priest's cassock that had been patched so many times the original cloth existed only as a theory, and over it a leather apron stained with substances Ivo's professional eye cataloged automatically and with growing respect: tannin, iron salts, something alkaline, something he genuinely could not identify and which therefore worried him more than anything else in the room.

His hands were a poisoner's hands. Ivo knew them on sight — long-fingered, immaculately clean, nails trimmed to the quick, moving with the unconscious economy of a man who has spent his life measuring drops. He'd seen those hands in mirrors. He'd seen them, briefly, in his own father, who had died of nothing in particular at fifty because a careful man's body simply decides one day to stop keeping books.

"You're the one burning your herbs," Ivo said.

"I'm the one *finishing* my herbs." The old man smiled, and the smile was startlingly gentle on a face built for calculation. "A library is only useful while there are questions left to answer. Mine are nearly all answered now. Brother Quill," he added, with a small bow that managed to mock and honor the title simultaneously. "Formerly of no order you'd recognize. Formerly of several professions you would. And you are the taster."

It was not a question.

"News travels."

"News *burns*, young man, and Thornfell is full of tinder." Quill's eyes went to the casket on Ivo's back, then to the ledger-mark on Ivo's wrist — visible now whenever the light caught it wrong, a thin grey whorl like smoke trapped under glass — and last, longest, to Serah Vail. "And you are the ward-keeper they hanged. Or meant to hang. I do apologize for missing it. I was busy here. Dying people require such *scheduling*."

"They cut me down," Serah said flatly. "Your point?"

"My point," Quill said, turning to a table covered in mortars and clay pots and a scale whose brass pans had gone green with age, "is that this room is full of people the mountain is eating slowly, and the Order of Wardens responded to the first failure in a century by hanging its most competent officer. So you'll forgive me if I've developed opinions about institutions." He began grinding something that smelled of willow bark and, underneath, of far less kind things. "You want something. Everyone who walks through my door wants something. The marked want relief. The unmarked want information. The dead want nothing, which makes them my easiest patients. What do *you* want?"

Ivo set the casket on the table. The sound it made was small and enormous.

"Wren Ashvale," he said. "Her name's in here. Her soul's in the mountain. I mean to get it back."

Something moved behind Quill's eyes — not surprise, Ivo decided. Recognition. The look of a man hearing a debt read aloud that he'd known about for years.

"Ashvale," Quill repeated. "Yes. I've heard that name in the confessions of the marked. There was a courier, they say. Ran the passes when running them was possible. Carried things the crown pretended not to know about and the mountain pretended not to want." His gaze flicked to Ivo's wrist, to the ledger-mark. "And you burned her casket. Foolish. Brave, but foolish — the two are so often married, aren't they? Now the mountain has a claim on you as well."

"I know what I did."

"Do you?" Quill set down his pestle. For a moment the gentleness dropped away entirely, and underneath it Ivo saw the other man — the one who had measured drops for a living, whoever his customers had been. "Because the people in these beds also knew what they did. They signed nothing. They sold nothing. But their grandparents did, or their landlords did, or their *king* did, and the mountain keeps excellent books, and debts roll downhill in this country the way water does." He gestured at the nearest bed, where a boy of perhaps ten slept with the spiral coiling slow and grey at the hollow of his throat. "That is Tam. His grandfather sold a harvest-tithe to the under-court in the famine year. One contract. One signature. Three generations later, the mountain comes to collect the interest. Tell me again how well you understand what you did."

Ivo held the old man's stare. "I gave ten years of my life swallowing poison so a king could eat safely. I learned every hidden road through these mountains so I could serve him better. And the whole time, the crown was selling souls out the back door to pay for its power." His voice stayed level. It always stayed level. That was the cage and the craft of him. "So yes, Brother. I think I understand debts. I'm carrying one."

For a long moment Quill said nothing. Then he laughed — softly, ruefully, at himself as much as anyone.

"Grace," he said, "arrives in strange packaging. Very well, taster. You want the mountain. I know the mountain. I have known it longer than I have known God, which I confess is not saying much, as my acquaintance with God remains largely epistolary." He wiped his hands on the apron, leaving no stain, which Ivo noted with unease. "I will guide you to the first gate myself. Through the service ways, past the tolls, into the courts below. I will spend what knowledge I have left."

"And the price?"

Quill's smile returned, and this time it held something Ivo recognized from his own mirror: the particular sadness of a man who has already paid, and is only waiting for the bill to arrive.

"There is a debt of mine," Quill said, "older than every mark in this room. Older than the wards themselves, near enough. I poisoned something I should have protected, a long time ago, believing I served the poor by doing it. The stars over Thornfell went dark the night that sin finally came due — though it took a century to compound, which I grant you is impressive interest even by the mountain's standards." He turned back to his mortars, his voice going quiet and even, a man reading his own sentence aloud. "When we reach the place where that can be corrected, you will carry me there. That is my price. Not gold, not passage, not protection. Just that. Carry me to my sin, and don't let me turn back."

Silence filled the chapel, broken only by the labored breathing of the marked and the soft tick of the green-brass scale settling in a draft.

"And if you try to turn back?" Serah asked.

Quill did not look up from his grinding. "Then remind me of the boy in that bed," he said. "Tam. His grandfather signed one paper in a famine year. Remind me that grace isn't a feeling. It's a *payment plan*." He tapped the mortar twice, decisively, and the smell of willow and hemlock rose between them like incense. "We leave at dusk. The mountain breathes on a schedule, and I'd rather enter through the inhale than the exhale."

Ivo looked at the beds, at the grey spirals coiling inward, at the names written beside the doors by hands that refused to stop counting. He thought of Wren's laugh, already spent at a gate he hadn't reached yet — no. Not yet. Not yet spent. He still had it. He checked, the way a miser checks his purse, and found it there, and hated himself for checking.

"Dusk," he agreed.

* * *

They spent the waiting hours doing what healers and soldiers and smugglers do before a bad road: small necessary work, hands busy so the mind doesn't have to be.

Serah sat with the marked and asked them questions — proper ward-keeper's questions, methodical and gentle — filling a notebook with dates and symptoms and the names of grandparents. When Tam woke, frightened, she showed him her own wrist, where the rope's scar circled like a second, paler mark, and told him that some debts get noticed late but they do get noticed. Whether that helped the boy, Ivo couldn't tell. It visibly steadied Serah, which might have been the point.

Ivo found himself in the chapel's tiny kitchen, because kitchens were honest places, and discovered Quill brewing tea.

"Not for the sick," the old priest said, handing him a cup. "For us. Chamomile and something of my own devising. Calms the blood without dulling the mind. A poisoner's trick — the difference between medicine and murder is only ever a matter of degree, and I have spent forty years learning precisely how fine that line is drawn." He raised his own cup. "To precision."

"To precision," Ivo said, and drank, and felt the tension behind his eyes unknot by exactly the right amount. He looked at the old man over the rim. "You knew my trade before I said a word."

"Your hands told me. A taster holds a cup like it might betray him. Because it might." Quill's eyes crinkled. "I held cups the same way, once. For different masters. Mine were poorer, but no kinder." He sipped. "Ask me, taster. Whatever question has been sitting behind your teeth since you walked in. Ask it while the tea is working."

Ivo considered lying. Decided, with a weariness that felt almost holy, not to.

"Why become a priest? If you were already—" he gestured with the cup, taking in the apron, the stains, the whole architecture of the man, "—that."

Quill was quiet for a moment. Outside, the afternoon light was beginning to go amber, and through the chapel's single window it fell across the beds like something poured.

"Because I was very good at the other thing," he said finally. "And one night I counted what my skill had bought, and it came to a great deal of money and a great many graves, and none of the graves were mine, and I realized that was the entire trick of the profession. You kill at one remove until you forget you're holding the knife." He turned the cup slowly in those immaculate hands. "A priest is just a poisoner who changed customers, taster. Instead of selling death by the drop, I spend my days measuring out its opposite. Same hands. Same precision. Same scales." He looked up, and the amber light caught the deep lines of his face. "The mountain taught me the last lesson. All those souls, all that borrowed power, all those beautiful hollow gentry drinking centuries out of other people's lives — do you know what they are? They are what happens when a poisoner refuses to die. When skill becomes appetite and appetite becomes forever." His voice dropped. "I am going into that mountain to finish correcting my oldest error. And I find, at my age, that I am not afraid of the dying part. I am afraid only of arriving *unrepentant*. Everything else is pharmacy."

Ivo drank his tea to the dregs. It tasted — he cataloged it automatically, helplessly — of chamomile, honey, and something bitter underneath that he chose, deliberately, for the first time in ten years, not to identify.

Trust, it turned out, had a flavor. It tasted like not asking.

* * *

Dusk came down the valley like a held breath finally released. Quill locked the chapel door behind them — a strange, tender gesture, locking safety *in* — and left the key under the spiral-marked stone where Pip-like hands would find it, though Ivo didn't know yet that he knew the girl's name. The marked who could walk gathered to see them off, and Tam pressed a chalk stub into Serah's palm, solemn as a knighting.

"For marking our names," the boy said, "when we come back."

Serah closed her fist around it like it was the truest relic she'd ever held.

They climbed out of the valley on a smuggler track Ivo knew — knew *had* known, before the map of his memory became a map of losses — and behind them the chapel smoke rose straight and thin into the first starless evening. Ahead, the mass of Thornfell lifted against the dark, blacker than the sky, breathing.

Quill walked between them with a staff and a satchel and the unhurried gait of a man attending his own funeral and pleased with the arrangements.

At the treeline, where the mountain's shadow began in earnest, the old priest stopped and rested both hands on his staff.

"One thing more," he said. "Before we go further. The gates ahead demand payment. Memories, truths, sometimes worse. The first gate takes a memory — choose carefully what you feed it, taster, because the mountain does not give change." He looked at Ivo, and his voice softened into something dangerously close to kindness. "And the second gate takes a truth. I mention this now so you may begin deciding which truth is yours to pay. We all have one that fits the lock. The mountain knows it before we do."

Ivo's hand drifted to his wrist, to the ledger-mark coiling there like smoke under skin.

Behind them, in the valley, a bell in the little boat-hulled chapel rang once — though Ivo had seen no bell, and no rope — as if the building itself were counting them out.

Three travelers. Two gates. One new moon.

Quill smiled at the sound without turning around. "Ah," he said. "It always does that. Come along. The mountain's inhaling, and I promised you the service entrances."
