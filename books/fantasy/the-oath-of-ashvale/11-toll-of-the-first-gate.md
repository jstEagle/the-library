## 11. Toll of the First Gate

They smelled the gate before they saw it.

That was Quill's word for it — *smelled* — and Ivo had thought it one of the old priest's affectations until the wind turned in the pass and carried it to him: hot stone and cold iron, the scent of a forge left banked overnight, threaded through with something sweeter underneath. Something like honey left too long in the sun. Something that made his back teeth ache.

"Close now," Serah said. She had not spoken in an hour. She spoke rarely since the gallows, as if words were rations she'd learned to count. "The ward-stones used to sing here. You could hear them from the valley, my grandmother said. A chord that never ended." Her hand rested on her sword hilt without gripping it — the habit of a woman who had been disarmed by her own order and still reached for comfort in the wrong place. "Now there's nothing to sing."

"There's something," Quill murmured. His nose was actually working, nostrils flared, eyes half-closed, a connoisseur at a tasting. "Under the iron. Under the honey. Do you smell it, taster?"

Ivo did. He'd been smelling it for a mile and refusing to name it, because naming it would mean admitting what his palate was telling him, and his palate was never wrong. Ten years of swallowing death on behalf of a king had burned the error out of him. He knew the taste of henbane in a broth of forty ingredients. He knew arsenic from white wine at three paces. He knew, with terrible precision, the flavor of a thing before it happened.

What he tasted on the wind now was grief. Old grief, rendered down like fat, concentrated into something almost pleasant. The way sorrow smells when it has stopped hurting and become simply true.

"The gate is fed," he said quietly. "Not warded. Fed."

Serah looked at him. In the grey pre-dawn light her face was all angles and ash, and her eyes held the particular exhaustion of someone who had spent her life maintaining a machine and just learned what the machine ate. "Memories," she said. "The old texts called them offerings. I always thought it was poetry."

"Nothing about this mountain is poetry," Quill said. He was walking with his staff held crosswise before him, the way other men carried a crucifix or a blade. "Poetry is what liars make of truth when the truth is too expensive."

The path bent around a shoulder of black rock furred with frost, and the gate stood before them.

Ivo had expected something grander — Thornfell's smuggler roads were full of grand things built by dead kings, arches carved with conquests, bridges hung over gorges on chains of forged bronze. But this was small. Almost humble. Two pillars of unworked stone, taller than a man but not much, set across the narrowest point of the pass where the mountain pinched itself closed like a fist. No lintel. No carving. No sigil of the under-court spiraling across its face. Just two standing stones, older than any kingdom, wearing a skin of ice that did not melt though no breath of warmth had touched this valley in living memory.

And between them, the air *shimmered*.

It wasn't light, exactly. It was more like the heat-haze above a summer road, except it ran vertical instead of horizontal, a curtain of distortion hanging in the gap between pillars, and when Ivo looked directly at it his eyes slid off as if the thing had opinions about being seen. Behind it, the pass continued — he could make out the far wall, the scree slope, the thread of frozen waterfall — but the continuation felt wrong, like a painting of a room rather than a room.

He had crossed this pass eleven times in his smuggling years. He remembered the gate being nothing. A landmark. Wren had once dared him to piss on the western pillar, and he'd refused, and she'd laughed at him so hard she had to sit down in the snow.

The memory arrived whole and warm and Ivo held onto it deliberately, the way you cup a flame in wind.

"Well," Quill said. "It knows we're here."

It did. The shimmer had changed. Where their reflections should have been — faint ghosts in the distorted curtain — there was nothing. Instead the surface of the gate had begun, very slowly, to move inward, the way water moves around a dropped stone. Concentric rings spreading from some center that didn't exist, each ring passing through the space where their images should have been and finding them absent.

"It's reading us," Serah said. Her hand came off her sword entirely. She stepped forward, and the rings quickened around her, and she went very still, and Ivo saw her jaw tighten against something. When she stepped back her face was bloodless. "It asked me a question."

"What question?" Ivo asked.

"Not in words. It just — *took hold*. Like a hand going through your pockets." Her mouth twisted. "It found the day I failed Thornfell. It held it up to me like a mirror and waited."

"What did you do?"

"I told it no. That's not for sale." She said it flatly, but Ivo heard what it cost her, because the gate had found the wound every person carries and pressed its thumb into it. "Then it let go. But it's still hungry. Look at it. It hasn't eaten properly in years — the wards kept it fed, tribute from the crown, memories paid up on schedule by people who never knew they were paying. Now the wards are dead and it's starving, and we're the first meat to walk through its door in months." She looked at Ivo. "You know what happens to a starving dog."

"Yes," Ivo said. "But dogs don't negotiate."

"This isn't a negotiation either. It's a toll. You pay or you don't pass, and if you don't pass—" She gestured at the pillars. At the mountain rising behind them, black and patient, breathing that slow honey-iron breath. "There is no other road. Your smuggler maps are worthless past this point. Everything beyond belongs to the court."

Quill had lowered himself onto a frost-slick boulder, staff across his knees, watching the gate with the detached fascination of a man observing a patient die. "In the chapel records," he said, "there are accounts of the gates. Fragments. The ward-keepers destroyed most of the writing, of course — knowledge is dangerous to institutions — but enough survived. The gates predate the wards. Predate the crown, possibly. They're older law, Serah. The mountain's own law, and even your order only ever administered it, never wrote it." He tapped the stone with one knuckle. "Older law has simpler rules. What passes the gate must be worth the passage. Not gold — the mountain has no use for gold. Not blood — it can take blood whenever it likes. Worth, to a thing like this, means weight. And nothing a soul carries weighs anything except—"

"Memory," Ivo said.

Quill nodded. "Memory. Meaning. The shape of a life." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, gentle and ruined. "You'll forgive me if I don't volunteer first. I've given enough away in my time, and most of it to the wrong parties."

* * *

They argued about it the way exhausted people argue: briefly, viciously, and without resolution.

Serah wanted to try force. Ivo told her what he'd seen in villages along the road — doors chalked with the spiral, houses empty, and in one, a man who had tried to fight the collection. She stopped arguing after that.

Quill suggested they wait for another traveler to pay first and slip through behind them, and Serah looked at him with such contempt that the old priest actually laughed, a dry rustling sound like paper burning. "Yes," he agreed. "I know. Cruel arithmetic. Forgive an old sinner his habits."

Which left Ivo standing before the gate while the others watched, and the gate looking back at him — not with eyes, nothing so crude, but with that slow concentric turning, that patient hollow hunger, and beneath the iron-and-honey smell he could feel it reaching for him again the way it had reached for Serah. Not a hand through his pockets. Worse. A librarian's fingers, sliding along the spines of him.

Ten years of memory. A decade of flavors cataloged in a ledger he kept locked in his skull: nightshade in the duke's soup, bitter almond masked with apricot in the ambassador's wine, the green copper taste of verdigris in a child's sweetmeats that had nearly broken him because the child was six years old and the poisoner had never been found and the king had ordered the sweets served anyway to flush out a rival's cook. All of it filed. All of it weighed. All of it his.

And threaded through the ledger, in a different ink entirely, Wren.

Wren in the wine cellar, laughing at his solemnity. Wren teaching him the high pass over Raven's Tooth, her breath smoking, her hand warm around his wrist. Wren saying *you taste everything for everyone else, Ivo, when will you taste something for yourself*. Wren's laugh —

He caught himself reaching for it and stopped. Careful, he thought. Don't spend it in advance.

Because he already knew which memory the gate would want. He'd known since the smell hit him a mile back, since he'd named the grief in it. The gate was starving, and starving things took the richest food first, and there was nothing in him richer than that.

"You don't have to," Serah said behind him. Quietly, for her.

"I know."

"It might not take what you offer. Starving things aren't choosy."

"It'll take what's richest. That's how tolls work — they take what proves you can pay." He rolled his shoulders. His hands were steady. Ten years of lifting poisoned cups to his lips with a court full of enemies watching for a tremor had taught him that trick, at least: steadiness as performance until it became real. "Keep Quill back. If it takes more than I offer, get him out of range. He's the only one of us who knows where we're going after the second gate."

"And you?"

"I'm the one who knows where we're going after the last one."

He walked to the gate.

Up close the shimmer resolved into something almost like fabric, a weaving of fine dark threads, moving, alive. The cold coming off it was not winter-cold but vault-cold, the chill of deep places, of cellars and tombs and the undersides of mountains. He could see through it to the ordinary pass beyond, and the ordinariness was the most frightening thing about it — scree, ice, the dead waterfall, all of it waiting, indifferent, as if the gate between him and it were no stranger than weather.

He thought about the last time he'd passed this way with Wren. Four years ago. She'd had contraband wine in six skins and a royal pardon in her head that turned out to be fiction, and she'd kissed him at this exact spot, quick and salt-tasting, and said *next season, Ivo, next season we stop running errands*, and he had nodded and meant it and then gone home and sold her name to the Lord Chamberlain for the price of his own post.

The guilt rose in him like bile and he let it rise, because guilt had weight too, and maybe the gate would be satisfied with the cheapest coin he had.

The gate considered him.

The turning rings slowed. Stopped. And then the pressure came — not physical, nothing touched him, but something moved through the contents of his mind the way a jeweler moves a loupe across a tray, pausing, weighing, discarding. He felt it pause on the child's sweetmeats. Pause on his mother's kitchen, long dead. Pause on the ledger of flavors, page by page, and find them interesting but insufficient. Pause on the cellar door only tasters know. Pause on—

It found her.

Of course it found her. She was the richest thing in him. It gathered her up the way a current gathers a leaf, and suddenly she was everywhere in his memory at once, Wren at every age he'd known her, Wren in snowlight and lamplight and the green dark of the Thornfell pines, and the gate held all of it up to him like a merchant holding up cloth, and waited for him to choose.

*I'll give you the bad years,* Ivo thought at it. *The betrayal. The exile. Take the guilt, it's heavy, it's the heaviest thing I own.*

The gate declined. Politely. With the infinite courtesy of a thing that has taken ten thousand payments and heard ten thousand bargains.

His throat tightened. "No," he said aloud. "Take anything else. Take the poisons. Take the palace. Take ten years of—"

The pressure sharpened. Not cruel, exactly. Patient. A toll-keeper's patience. It showed him, briefly, the alternative: the shimmer thickening, closing, the two of them standing in this pass forever while the new moon came and went and Wren's soul was poured out like lamp-oil to feed beautiful hollow things beneath the earth. It showed him the cost of refusal the way a moneylender shows the interest schedule.

Love measured in installments, he thought. That's all this is. That's all anything has ever been, with me — I paid the crown in obedience, I paid Wren in betrayal, I paid myself in poison, and now the mountain presents the bill.

Fine.

He chose.

He reached into himself, into the warm room where he kept her, and he took out the taste of her laugh.

It was a specific thing. That was the cruelty of it — not a general fondness, not a glow, but a precise sensory fact, filed in the same ledger as the poisons because that was the only ledger he had. Wren's laugh had a taste. Salt first — she laughed hardest after crying, or during, or instead of. Then something bright and carbonated, like the first sip of the stolen champagne they'd split in the cellar the night he'd finally made her tell him about the Thornfell roads. And underneath, warm and dark, like bread fresh from a shared oven, like the inside of her coat collar in winter when he buried his face there and she pretended to mind.

He held it out.

The gate took it.

There was no pain. That was the horror. There was a moment of contact — a coldness moving through him, delicate as a fish through water — and then the memory was simply gone, and worse than gone: the space where it had been was gone, smoothed over, as if the room had never contained a shelf there at all. He knew, abstractly, that Wren had laughed. He knew the fact of it the way he knew the fact of the capital's population. But the *taste* — salt and brightness and bread-warmth — was nowhere in him. He reached for it and found polished emptiness, a socket with nothing missing because there was no longer a socket.

He swayed. Caught himself. Stood straight, because somewhere behind him Serah was watching and he would not give the gate the satisfaction of seeing him buckle.

"That's the toll," he said, and his voice came out level, which cost him more than the memory had. "Paid."

The shimmer thinned. Parted. Beyond it the pass lay open, ordinary, indifferent, the dead waterfall glittering in the first grey light.

He walked through.

The crossing itself was nothing — a step, a coolness on his skin like walking through a cellar door, and then he was on the far side, and the gate stood behind him, and the hunger-smell was fading, and ahead the mountain rose up black against the lightening sky, vast beyond sense, its peak lost in cloud.

He stood there and did the inventory, because inventory was what he did, it was the only discipline he had left. Wren's face: present. Wren's voice: present. Wren's hands, her walk, the scar on her thumb from a cut wine-skin: all present. The taste of her laugh: absent. One line struck from the ledger. He tested the wound of it, carefully, the way he'd press a bruise, and found it strange — it hurt less than he expected and more, both at once, because the hurt had no object anymore. Grief with the memory amputated. Pain in search of its reason.

*Next season,* ghost-Wren said at the gate behind him, in a voice whose sound remained though its flavor was gone. *Next season we stop running errands.*

"We will," Ivo said to no one. "One way or another."

Serah came through the gate pale and silent — she had paid something too, he saw it in the way she wouldn't look at the eastern horizon, and he didn't ask. Some prices were private. Quill crossed last, shuffling, muttering, and whatever he'd surrendered he surrendered without ceremony, though Ivo noticed the old priest's lips moving afterward in what might have been prayer or might have been rehearsal, trying to remember something he could no longer quite reach.

They stood together in the shadow of Thornfell, three marked travelers at the foot of a hungry god's stair.

And then the light changed.

Not sunrise — the sun was still below the ridge. It was the mountain. High above them, impossibly far up, a seam of gold opened along the black rock, thin as a knife-cut, and widened, and Ivo realized he was looking at windows. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Light kindling in the body of the mountain itself, floor upon floor upon floor ascending into the clouds, a palace built inside the stone and lit from within, and the light was warm and golden and welcoming and it made his skin crawl the way flattery does.

"Dear God," Serah whispered.

"Not God," Quill said. His voice had gone very soft, and very old. "Something that worships itself. Come, children. We're expected — can't you feel it? The mountain has seen our marks." He lifted his staff, and in its shadow Ivo saw the debt-spiral on his own wrist had begun, faintly, to glow. "And the court beneath does so love to collect."

The golden windows blazed. Somewhere far below, something began to sing — a chord that never ended, the ward-song's stolen twin.

The gates of the under-court opened to meet them.
