## 12. The Court Beneath

Serah Vail had spent eleven years learning the grammar of wards — the way a boundary spoke in pressure and hum, the way it flinched before it failed — and so she felt the mountain's threshold the way a deaf woman feels a drum: through her boots, through her teeth, through the wet meat of her heart.

They had come down through the service ways Pip had promised them, three days of crawling through veins of stone that predated every map Serah had ever sworn to protect. The girl walked ahead with a stub of chalk in her fist, marking turns that only she could read, and behind her came Brother Quill murmuring prayers that were half litany and half recipe, and behind him Ivo Ravensworth, silent as a man carrying something breakable he refused to name.

Now the tunnel ended at a wall that was not a wall.

It looked like stone. It behaved like water. When Pip pressed her palm against it, the surface dimpled inward and gave off light — not reflected light, but generated, as if the rock itself had been taught to shine and had never been allowed to forget the lesson.

"Last chance to turn around," Pip said, and grinned the gap-toothed grin of a child who had never once been offered a last chance and did not intend to start declining them now.

"Go on," Serah said.

The wall swallowed them one at a time, the way a throat swallows, and Serah went third because she had learned long ago that the person who goes first is the person who chooses what everyone else sees, and she wanted to see what Ivo saw when the mountain opened for him.

What they all saw was the court beneath.

* * *

Serah had imagined it wrong for years. In the order's oldest texts — the ones kept under lock, the ones she had copied as an apprentice with ink-stained fingers and no understanding — the under-court was drawn as a cave, a dark thing, a place of roots and worms. The illustrators had given it fangs. They had given it darkness.

They should have given it mirrors.

The cavern opened above them into a vaulted immensity, and every surface of it — floor, walls, pillars, the slow-dripping stalactites a hundred feet overhead — was polished to a reflectiveness no human hand could achieve. Light came from everywhere and nowhere, silver-white and sourceless, and it bounced and doubled and redoubled until Serah could see herself a hundred times over, a hundred small Serahs receding into distance, each one wearing the same expression she could not wipe from her own face: awe curdling, even now, into assessment.

Because this was not a ruin. This was not a tomb. This was *inhabited*.

A palace stood in the middle of the cavern — or perhaps the palace *was* the cavern, grown rather than built, its towers spiraling up out of the mirrored floor like crystallized breath. Bridges arced between spires without visible support. Staircases climbed in directions staircases should not climb. And moving across it all, along terraces and galleries and gardens that grew in beds of silver sand, were people.

The gentry.

Serah had read about them too, in the same locked books, and the books had lied again by telling the truth badly. They were beautiful. That part was accurate. They moved the way water moves, the way smoke moves, with a grace that had been refined past the point where effort showed. Their clothes were colors Serah had no names for — shades that seemed to exist only in reflection, visible in the mirrors but somehow absent from the wearers themselves. And their faces —

Their faces were the horror. Not because they were wrong. Because they were *finished*. Every gentry face Serah saw had the terrible completeness of a portrait, of a death-mask, of something that had stopped changing centuries ago and now simply persisted. Youth without youthfulness. Beauty without a single line earned by living. She watched a woman laugh at something on a terrace above them, and the laugh was perfect, musical, and utterly empty, the way a bell is empty, and Serah understood suddenly why the old texts called them *the hollow court*.

"They don't make anything," Quill whispered beside her. The old priest's hands were folded in his sleeves, and his voice had the careful flatness of a man managing his own nausea. "Look. Gardens, yes. Music, yes. But no forge-smoke. No kitchens. No children." He paused. "Nothing grows here except them."

"What feeds them, then?" Pip asked, though Serah could hear in the girl's voice that she already knew. The marked knew. The marked always knew; the chalk spiral on their doors was a receipt, not a warning.

Serah looked where the girl was looking.

At the center of everything, suspended in a lattice of silver filaments above the palace's highest tower, hung a cage.

Not one cage. Thousands. A constellation of them, drifting in slow orbits around some invisible heart, each cage a hand-span of woven light, and inside each cage burned a spark. Some sparks were white. Some were gold, or green, or the deep bruised violet of a bruise going away. They pulsed. They drifted against their filaments like moths testing glass. And with every pulse, Serah saw — she would swear on her order's broken name she saw — the nearest gentry brighten. A lord on a balcony below the cage-cloud straightened, color rising in his finished face like wine poured into a cup, and for one moment he looked almost alive, almost warm, almost *real* — and then the moment passed and he was porcelain again, and somewhere in the lattice a spark burned a little lower.

"There," Quill said softly. "That's the harvest. That's what the wards were holding shut for nine hundred years." He crossed himself with a poisoner's hands, thumb to brow to chest, an old gesture made obscene by context. "We thought we were keeping an army out. We were keeping a *pantry*."

"Don't," Serah said.

Quill glanced at her.

"Don't say *we*. You poisoned the stones. I kept them. I checked them every season for eleven years and reported them sound, because my order told me they were sound, because my order—" She stopped. Her jaw ached. She unclenched it. "I signed those reports, Brother. Whatever you did a century ago, I renewed it annually with my own seal. So when I say *we*, I will say it. But you don't get to soften it first."

Quill bowed his head. "No," he said. "I don't."

Pip, mercifully, was not listening. The girl had gone very still, her chalk forgotten, her whole small body aimed upward at the cage-lattice like a compass needle. Her lips moved. Counting, maybe. Or praying. Or reading names she recognized in the pattern of the lights, the way other children read stars.

"We need to get closer," Serah said, because someone had to be the officer here, and grief made a poor one. "The cages — there'll be a registry, a steward, some office where debts are recorded. That's how courts work. Even these. Especially these. Find the paperwork and we find Wren's entry, and find the entry and we find the way to void it."

"You still believe in paperwork," Ivo said.

It was the first thing he'd said since the wall swallowed them, and Serah turned to look at him properly. He stood at the edge of the mirrored floor with his taster's stillness wrapped around him like a coat, and his eyes were fixed on the lattice of lights with an intensity that had nothing calm underneath it. She had seen him taste poison for a duke and not blink. She had seen him cut her down from a gallows with steady hands. But looking at those caged sparks, his face had the quality of a held breath.

"I believe in records," Serah said. "Records can be challenged. Chains can't."

"And if the record says she belongs to them?"

"Then the record is wrong, and I will spend whatever it costs to prove it."

Ivo looked at her then — really looked, the way he rarely let himself, as if direct eye contact were a flavor he hadn't yet tested for safety — and something passed between them that Serah did not have a word for. Recognition, maybe. Two people who had both been instruments of a rotten system, standing in the rot together.

"The servants' ways," Pip said abruptly, tearing her gaze from the lights with visible effort. "There are corridors behind the mirrors. The marked who work up here — the ones who get taken for household service instead of harvest — they clean the reflections. There's a whole guild of them. Glass-polishers." Her voice cracked slightly on the word, and Serah realized with a cold turn of her stomach that Pip had probably lost someone to the polishing guild, someone who had gone up the mountain as property and never come back down. "If we move now, during the feast-preparations, nobody counts faces. Everybody's counting cups."

"Feast?" Quill asked.

Pip pointed with her chin toward the great terraces, where the gentry were converging in glittering streams. Servants moved among them — human servants, soul-marked, their wrists banded in silver, their eyes carefully fixed on the middle distance — bearing trays, arranging flowers that were surely not flowers, lighting lamps that burned without flame.

"The Court of Mirrors feasts every seventh night," Pip said. "Tonight's the night. Which means the kitchens are chaos, the halls are crowded, and every gentry in the mountain is about to be very drunk on something that isn't wine." She grinned again, and this time the grin had teeth in it. "Best time to be a thief in this place. Worst time to be a soul."

* * *

They went in as glass-polishers.

It was Pip's plan and Pip's craft: rags and vinegar-water and long-handled brushes, the uniform of a caste so low that the gentry's eyes slid off it like rain off oilcloth. Serah had spent her life in a ward-keeper's grey, a color designed to be unseen, and even so she was astonished by the invisibility of servitude. They walked the mirrored halls in plain sight, and the beautiful people looked *through* them, and Serah understood that this was the true architecture of the under-court — not the palaces, not the bridges, but the looking. Everything here was built on who was permitted to be seen.

The halls themselves were worse than the first vista. Up close, the mirrors were not honest. They did not show what stood before them.

Serah polished a panel of glass in a gallery lined with a hundred more, and her reflection showed her not the woman she was but the woman she had been at twenty-two, the morning of her first ward-walk, young and certain and unbloodied, smiling at a future that had not yet happened to her. She froze. Beside her, Ivo's reflection was not Ivo at all but a man she didn't recognize — older, hollow-cheeked, wearing the high-collared black of a court official, with a chain of office that caught the light like a noose catching sun.

"Look away from the glass," Serah said quietly. "All of you. The mirrors show regrets here, not faces. It's a defense. Or a cruelty. Possibly both."

"I know what mine shows," Ivo said, not looking away. His voice was level. His knuckles, wrapped around the polish-rag, were white. "It's been showing me the same thing since we came down."

Serah did not ask. Some doors you leave shut until the person behind them opens them, and she had learned that lesson at cost.

They worked their way upward through the palace, hall by hall, following Pip's instinct and the faint pull that all three of the marked guides had described — the sense, Serah had it too now, faint as a toothache, of being *known*, of the mountain's attention resting on them like a hand on the back of the neck. The registry, Pip believed, sat near the top of the central spire, in a round chamber called the Counting Room, where the steward kept the ledger of debts. But the spire was guarded by ceremony, and ceremony was a wall thicker than stone.

So they took the long way, and the long way led them past the feast.

* * *

The banquet hall was a wound of light.

Serah crouched with the others behind a screen of frosted glass, rag in hand, playing her role, watching through the fogged pane as the Court of Mirrors dined. The tables were laid with food that steamed and glistened and was, she would have bet her restored honor, entirely without substance — dishes made of memory, served on plates made of appetite. The gentry ate the way they breathed: beautifully, endlessly, without nourishment.

And between courses, they fed.

She saw it happen three times before she could bear to keep watching. A servant would approach with a covered dish, kneel, and lift the cover — and beneath it, instead of food, would burn a caged spark, carried down from the lattice on a silver chain. The gentry lord would lean close. The spark would rise out of its cage like a moth released. And it would go into the lord's mouth, or eyes, or the hollow place beneath the breastbone — Serah could not tell which, the light made it impossible to tell — and for a few minutes afterward the lord would *live*. Really live. Laughing with sudden warmth, weeping at remembered music, turning to a neighbor with urgent, human words — speaking, Serah realized with a lurch, in borrowed voices, with borrowed memories, the last thoughts and jokes and griefs of whoever that spark had been.

One lord, mid-meal, began to sing. A working song, a harvest song, the kind sung in villages with chalked doors. His beautiful finished face streamed tears, and his voice was a woman's voice, roughened by fields and weather, and the other gentry applauded politely and waited for him to finish so the next course could arrive.

Beside Serah, Quill had turned his face fully away and was whispering something — not prayer, she thought, though it had the rhythm of prayer. Confession, maybe. The kind a man repeats to himself when no god will take it.

And Ivo—

Ivo had gone rigid. Serah saw it happen: the taster's stillness cracking like ice over deep water. He was staring through the frosted glass at the head table, at the highest lords, at a lady in silver whose face was flickering — actually flickering, like a lamp in wind — and across her features, surfacing and sinking, surfacing and sinking, played memories that were not hers.

A river at dawn. A rope thrown from a boat. A laugh — Serah heard it, impossibly, through the glass, a young woman's laugh, quick and sharp and delighted — and a voice saying words Serah couldn't catch, words that made the silver lady smile with stolen warmth while somewhere in the lattice above, a gold-green spark guttered like a candle in a draft.

"Wren," Ivo said. Just the name. Nothing else. As if the name were the only load-bearing word left in his vocabulary, and everything else had been stripped out of him.

He was moving before Serah could catch his sleeve — out from behind the screen, into the open hall, walking toward the head table with the polish-rag still hanging forgotten from his hand, a servant-shaped man crossing a room full of things that ate souls, and every mirror he passed showed not his reflection but hers, Wren's, laughing on a river at dawn, and Serah lunged after him knowing that in four strides he would be seen, and in five he would be seized, and in six—

The lady in silver turned her flickering face toward him.

And smiled — with Wren's mouth's shape, with a dead woman's borrowed joy — and said, in a voice like honey poured over a blade:

"You're late, little taster. She told me you'd come."

Behind the screen, Pip grabbed Serah's arm. Quill went pale as the glass. And somewhere far above them, in the lattice of ten thousand caged lights, one small gold-green spark flared — once, hard, like a heart remembering how to beat — and every mirror in the hall turned, all at once, to look at Ivo Ravensworth.
