## 15. Running the Mirrored Halls

The steward's smile was the last thing I saw before the hall began to hunt us.

Not the steward himself — he had simply turned away after setting the hunt in motion, the way a man turns from a fire he has lit, confident in the wood. He had said only: *"The court will see you out. One way or another."* And then the mirrors had woken.

Ivo ran.

The mirrored palace of the under-court did not have corridors so much as suggestions of them — galleries that branched and doubled back on themselves like the roots of some pale tree, every wall surfaced in glass that had never known weather, never known dust, never known anything but the faces pressed against it from the inside. And those faces were not his own.

That was the first rule of the mirrored halls, learned in the first thirty strides: *do not look at yourself.*

He looked anyway. Everyone does. It is the oldest trap in any court, above or below — they show you what you cannot resist.

The reflection running beside him wore his body, his grey travel coat, his taster's pallor, but its face was twenty years old and slick with rain outside a cellar door in Thornfell's lower town, and it was saying, in his own voice, with his own easy smile: *"They only want a name, Wren. A name on paper means nothing."*

"No," Ivo said aloud, and ran faster.

Behind him, something moved through the gallery at the speed of falling. Not footsteps. The court did not send hounds; it sent *attention*, a pressure like deep water narrowing to a point, and where it touched the mirrors the reflections peeled off their surfaces and followed.

"Left!" Serah Vail barked ahead of him, one hand trailing along the wall as she ran, fingers reading the seams between mirror-panes like a blind woman reading scripture. Her ward-craft had been dying since Thornfell — he could see it guttering around her knuckles now, a faint gold shimmer that flared and failed like a candle in wind — but dying craft still knew things living ignorance did not. "There's a junction. Don't take it straight on — the court folds straight roads back on themselves. Take it crooked."

"Crooked," Ivo gasped. "I've spent ten years learning crooked roads."

"Then you'll die comfortable."

* * *

Quill brought up the rear, which should have been impossible for a man his age, and was possible only because Brother Quill had spent the last hundred paces scattering his trade behind him like a farmer sowing winter wheat.

"Little pinch of this," the old priest muttered, thumbing a grey powder from a paper twist and letting it drift across the floor behind them. "Blessed are the poor in breath. That one's nightshade cut with ash-salt — won't kill a gentry thing, nothing kills them, but it'll make their borrowed lungs forget how borrowing works." He glanced over his shoulder without slowing. "And if it touches your skin, boy, you'll lose the feeling in your left hand for a week, so mind the drift."

"You're poisoning the hallway," Ivo said.

"I'm *ministrating* the hallway. There's a difference. The difference is intent."

The attention behind them hit the powder. There was no sound, but every mirror down the length of the gallery fogged at once, white as breath on glass, and the peeling reflections caught inside it slowed, blurred, became smears of regret instead of specific ones. Somewhere in the fog, a voice that sounded like Ivo's mother said, *you could have written to her,* and another voice that sounded like the king said, *you were always mine to spend,* and then the fog thickened and swallowed even the accusations.

"That buys us a corridor," Quill said. "Maybe two. Then I'm down to the serious vintages, and I'd hoped to save those."

"For what?"

"For the day I meet the man who taught me my first recipe. I want to have something special ready." The old man smiled, mild and terrible. "Run, boy. Serah's found her door."

* * *

Serah had indeed found a door, though *door* was generous. It was a seam in the mirror-wall, hairline-thin, invisible unless you already knew courts build false walls the way kings build false smiles — to hide the plumbing. She pressed both palms flat against it, and the dying gold of her ward-craft crawled out along the seam like ivy remembering summer.

"It's an old servant-passage," she said through her teeth. "From before the gentry got beautiful. They sealed the honest ways when they started needing lies to live in." Sweat stood on her brow. The shimmer around her hands flickered — guttered — steadied. "It'll open. It just wants to know if I have the right."

"Do you?"

"I had the right once. The order took it when they took my cloak." Her jaw set. "But wards don't answer to cloaks. They answer to *keeping*. And I kept yours alive tonight, didn't I?" She said it to the wall, not to him, and the seam glowed, and split, and breathed out air that smelled of stone and cold water and — impossibly, wonderfully — of nothing at all. No perfume, no incense, no sweetness laid over rot. Just clean dead rock.

Ivo had never loved a smell more.

"In," Serah said. "Quill!"

The old man came backwards down the gallery, pouring the last of a brown vial in a wide arc, and whatever was in it made the mirrors weep — long silver tears running down every pane, and behind the tears the reflections writhed, because there is nothing in creation a regret hates more than being seen clearly enough to cry about.

Then he was through, and Serah let the seam close, and for one whole moment there was silence, and dark, and the three of them leaning on each other in a passage barely wider than a coffin, breathing like landed fish.

"Well," Quill said. "That went well."

"You call that well?" Ivo managed.

"The alternative," Quill said, "was standing still. In my experience the two outcomes look similar from the inside until quite late in the proceedings."

* * *

The servant-passage ran crooked, exactly as promised — down worn steps, along ledges above lightless shafts, through chambers where the mountain's old blood still moved in the walls like slow amber. And the mirrors found them anyway.

That was the second rule of the mirrored halls, and Ivo learned it the hard way, in a landing where a single hand-mirror hung on a rusted nail, placed there by some servant dead five hundred years, forgotten by everyone except the court, which forgot nothing it owned.

He saw it out of the corner of his eye and told himself: *don't.* He was better than this. He was a man who tasted poison nightly and did not flinch, who kept a ledger of flavors in his head because memory was the only estate a king's taster was permitted to hold.

He looked.

The face in the little glass was Wren's.

She was older than he remembered — exile will do that, exile and whatever the mountain had done to her soul — and she was not angry. That was the cruelty of it. She was sitting on the doorstep of some house he didn't know, turning a coin over in her fingers, and she looked up as if she'd heard his boots on gravel, and she said, gently, the way you say a thing you've had years to make peace with:

"You could have come with me, Ivo. They never asked you to stay. You asked *them*."

"I—" His voice cracked on it. "They would have taken the post. Ten years, Wren. Ten years of immunity bought at—"

"At whose price?" the mirror-Wren asked, and turned the coin again. "You keep saying *ten years* like it's a payment you made. It wasn't a payment, love. It was a purchase. There's a difference, and you know it, and that's why you keep the ledger."

A hand closed on his collar and hauled him backward, hard, and Serah's voice came low and furious beside his ear: "Rule two. *Never* the small mirrors. The big ones show you regrets. The little ones show you arguments — and arguments can talk you into staying." She slammed her palm over the hand-mirror, and the glass went black under her dying gold. "We do not have time for your conscience. It's very well-stocked. Congratulations. Move."

His legs moved before his pride finished bleeding. That, too, was a kind of training: ten years of walking toward dishes that might kill him had taught his body to obey when his mind wanted to stand in the doorway of a thing and hurt.

Quill fell in beside him, puffing. "What did yours show you?"

"Wren."

"Mm." The old man's eyes were distant. "Mine showed me the well at Ashvale. Where I drew the water to cut the first poison." He said it evenly, the way a man reads his own sentence. "Every hundred yards or so I remind myself: the court shows you true things. That's what makes it dangerous. Lies you can taste. Truth goes down smooth."

* * *

They ran. The mountain ran with them, or against them — it was hard to tell, and getting harder. Corridors breathed. Stairs arrived where stairs hadn't been, retreating like tide. Twice the passage ahead of them was simply a mirror, floor to ceiling, showing three exhausted fugitives approaching from the other side, and twice Serah put her failing gold against the glass and *argued* — actual words, old ones, in a language like river stones rolling — until the mirror admitted it was a wall pretending, and sulked aside.

"How long can you keep doing that?" Ivo asked, the third time.

"As long as it takes to be a funny story later," Serah said. "Ask me again when we're out."

"And if we're not out?"

"Then it won't be funny, and I won't be answering questions." She wiped blood from her nose — the ward-work was costing her, drawing on reserves that had been drawn on too many times already — and grinned, and it was a good grin, feral and unbothered, the grin of a woman who had already been hanged once in her life and found it clarifying. "Taster. When we get out of here, remind me to teach you ward-script. It's mostly swearing."

"He'd take to it," Quill said. "He swears beautifully under his breath. Very economical."

The attention found them again at the Long Gallery, and this time it did not come as fog or pressure but as *light* — every mirror in a hall half a mile long igniting at once, and out of the light stepped the hunt proper.

They looked like the gentry, because everything here looked like the gentry: tall, lovely, hollow-eyed, dressed in clothes that moved like poured mercury. But where the gentry were merely empty, these had been filled — stuffed, the way a trophy is stuffed — with harvested motion, with the muscle-memory of souls the court had spent. They did not run. They *arrived*, in increments, closer each time you blinked, and between arrivals they simply stood, patient as interest accruing.

"Oh," Quill said softly, unbuttoning the inner pocket of his cassock with the reverence of a man opening a reliquary. "Oh, they've sent the *collectors*. Well. I had hoped to save these for the feast, but grace is grace, and it rarely asks your schedule." He drew out a stoppered phial no longer than a finger, holding liquid so clear it was nearly invisible — visible only when it caught light, and then it caught all of it, greedily, the way a debt does.

"Quill," Serah said. "What is that?"

"A confession," the old priest said. "Distilled. Ninety-one ingredients, ninety-one sins, aged in oak I shouldn't have been able to afford." He pulled the stopper with his teeth. "It won't kill them. Nothing kills them. But it will give them, for perhaps four minutes, every symptom of every poison I ever brewed — all at once, in perfect sympathy — and I have brewed *many*, and I was *good*." His voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word, grief and pride in the same breath. "Forgive me, brothers. This is going to feel like church."

He threw it.

The phial shattered on the Gallery floor and the confession went up as vapor, colorless, scentless — Ivo knew, because he tasted the air out of pure habit and got nothing, which frightened him more than anything else that night — and where it touched the collectors, the collectors *stopped arriving.*

They stood instead. They sweated. Their beautiful borrowed faces went grey, then green, then a shade Ivo had only seen on men who had eaten what kings paid him to prevent. One of them dropped to its knees with the dignity of a felled statue. Another clutched its stomach with both elegant hands and made a sound like a harp being tuned by someone furious.

"Four minutes," Quill said. "Run, children. Sin has a long half-life but a short working window."

They ran. Behind them the Long Gallery filled with the sounds of immortality discovering nausea, and Ivo — God help him, ten years of gallows humor rising like bread — heard himself laugh, high and cracked and real.

"What?" Serah demanded.

"Nothing. It's just—" another laugh escaped him, wilder. "Ten years I sat at that table tasting death in thimblefuls, polite as a priest, and the whole time the cure for monsters was *an emetic.*"

"Everything is an emetic," Quill called back serenely, "if you believe in it strongly enough and dose correctly."

* * *

The exit, when Serah found it, was almost insulting in its plainness: a service door behind a wine-rack in a cellar that mirrored the palace cellars above so precisely that Ivo stopped dead in front of it, struck by vertigo.

He knew this cellar. He had *been* in this cellar — the one above, the true one, a thousand times, counting casks for the king's steward, memorizing which vintage hid in which shadow because that was his job, knowing everything and owning nothing.

"They copied it," he said.

"They copy everything worth having," Serah said, hauling at the rack. It swung on a pivot so well-oiled it whispered. "Souls, cellars, faces. Why do you think they built the halls out of mirrors? It's not decoration, taster. It's *doctrine*. The whole court is a room full of things stolen from upstairs, arranged to flatter the thieves." She glanced back at him, and for a moment the ferocity dropped and he saw the exhaustion underneath, the woman who had carried a dying craft through a dying night. "Including you, probably. Including me. Come on."

The door beyond the rack was iron, and ordinary, and opened onto a slope of raw tunnel sloping down and away, and the air coming through it was cold mountain air, real air, and somewhere far below it Ivo could hear water — the underground rivers that threaded Thornfell like veins, the smuggler's highway he had walked in another life.

"One problem," Quill said, looking back the way they'd come. The distant sounds of the collectors' suffering had faded — four minutes, as promised, no more. "Our hosts recover quickly, and they know these halls better than we ever will."

"So we go down fast," Serah said.

"Down is where they keep the vaults."

"Down," Ivo said quietly, "is also where the caches are." Both of them looked at him. He realized his hands had gone steady — the strange calm that had carried him through every poisoned dinner, arriving late as usual but arriving. "Before the wards fell, before any of this — smugglers stock the mountain. Way-stations. Food, water, bandages, and things I'm not going to describe in mixed company. I stocked three of them myself, on Wren's routes, years ago." His throat tightened on her name and he pushed through it. "One of them is two hours' walk from here, if the tunnels haven't grown new opinions."

Quill raised an eyebrow. "You built a hiding place in the mountain of the enemy?"

"I built hiding places everywhere. It's the first law of the road: assume the world will turn on you, and leave yourself a sandwich." He stepped through the iron door onto the downward slope, and the mirrored halls fell away behind him — but not entirely. Far up the gallery, in the last mirror before the dark took it, he saw movement. Not the collectors.

The steward. Standing perfectly still among the wreck of his hunt, hands folded, watching Ivo go with an expression that was not anger.

It was *patience.* The patience of a ledger waiting for an entry.

"Two hours," Ivo said, and started down.
