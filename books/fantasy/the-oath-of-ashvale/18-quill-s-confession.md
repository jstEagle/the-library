## 18. Quill's Confession

The chapel had no bell.

Brother Quill had taken it precisely because of that — a roofless shell of a shrine half-swallowed by the Thornfell foothills, its bell-loophole gaping like a socket after the eye is gone. A church that could not call anyone was a church that could not be blamed for calling the wrong people. He had ministered here eleven years, to the soul-marked and the soul-frightened, and in all that time he had never once rung anything, sung anything, or promised anything he did not intend to keep.

He kept his promises now the way he kept his poisons: in labeled jars, at measured doses, with a steady hand.

Outside, the stars were still missing. Eleven nights without them. The villagers had stopped looking up; that was the worst of it, he thought, ladling willow-bark tea into tin cups for the line of marked souls shuffling through his door. Not the dark itself but how quickly people learned to live beneath it, heads bowed, eyes on their feet, as though the sky had always been a ceiling and never a promise.

"You'll want to drink that while it's hot," he told a farmwife whose forearm bore the spiral sigil in chalk-grey lines that no water would wash away. "And you'll want to sleep on your left side. The mark spreads from the heart outward when it takes hold. Slower if the blood runs cool."

"And then?" she asked. They always asked. *And then?*

"Then," Quill said gently, "we pray the mountain forgets your name."

She smiled because he was old and kind-seeming and wore a priest's patched cassock, and because hope is a cup everyone drinks even knowing it's been refilled with something bitter. He watched her go and felt the familiar weight settle back onto his shoulders, the one he had carried so long it had worn a groove in him like a yoke-worn ox.

Guilt, properly aged, stops tasting like anything at all. That was the first thing they taught you in the poisoner's guild, before they taught you hemlock.

* * *

Ivo Ravensworth arrived near dusk with Serah Vail limping beside him and a girl — Pip, the marked one who organized the marked ones — carrying a satchel of stolen bread like it was a crown jewel. Quill saw them coming down the goat-track from the chapel door and knew, before any word was spoken, that the reckoning had come for him at last.

Not because of their faces. Because of the mountain.

For eleven nights he had felt it breathing under the soil, a slow tidal pull in the stones themselves, and each night the pull had grown more insistent, more *specific*, as though something vast below were reading a ledger and running its finger down the column of names. And every night his own name had sat there, near the top, inked in a hand he recognized because it had once been his own.

"Brother Quill." Ivo removed his hat. The taster looked thinner than the last time, greyer, with the particular hollowness of a man who has been drinking fear instead of water. "We need the tunnels. Pip says you know them."

"I know them," Quill said. "I've known them longer than she's been alive. Longer than her mother, come to that." He stepped back from the doorway. "Come in. All of you. There's stew enough, and I'd rather tell this sitting than standing."

Serah Vail's eyes narrowed. She was a ward-keeper stripped of her order's colors, and she carried suspicion the way other women carried knitting — constantly, and with small sharp movements. "Tell what?"

Quill busied himself with the pot. It was easier to confess to a hearth than to a face.

"The story you've been telling yourselves," he said, "is that the wards failed. That something broke them. That the mountain reached out and snuffed the stars like candles." He ladled four bowls. "That story has the great advantage of being almost true. The disadvantage is that it lets everyone off except the one man in these hills who deserves it least to be let off."

Pip stopped chewing.

"Sit down," Quill said. "All of you. And someone pour me a cup of my own tea, because my hands are about to be busy with something else."

* * *

They sat. He stood, because some confessions deserve a standing congregation, and he could not in good conscience be both priest and penitent at once — or rather, he could, and had been for a hundred years, which was rather the point.

"My name was not always Quill," he began. "Before I took orders I was called Aldous Fenwick, and I was the finest poisoner in the southern guilds. Not a boast. A diagnosis. I could kill a man with three drops of wine and make his death look like piety — the guilds prized that, in those days. Kings hired us. Guilds hired us against kings. And the poor—" he paused, and the hearth cracked a log, filling the silence for him, "—the poor died of everything, all the time, and nobody hired anyone on their behalf."

"So you poisoned the rich," Serah said. Not a question. An accusation shaped politely.

"I poisoned whoever paid," Quill said, "until the year I didn't." He turned to face them fully. "A century ago — by my count, ninety-seven years, and I have counted every one — a delegation came down out of Thornfell. Mountain folk. Herders, weavers, folk with dirt under their nails and hunger in their faces. They came to the guild with a commission, and the commission was strange: not a death, but a *dulling*. They wanted the border wards weakened. Softened. Just enough, they said, to let their dead cross back over."

Ivo set down his spoon. "Their dead."

"That's what they said." Quill laughed, and it came out wrong, dry as old paper. "You must understand what those years were. Plague in the lowlands. Tithe-war in the valleys. I had buried two apprentices and a wife and every ideal I'd ever owned. And here came poor folk asking me, the great poisoner, to perform one act of mercy with my terrible craft. To open a door just wide enough for grieving people to see their mothers again. I believed them. Gods forgive me, I *wanted* to believe them, and wanting is the solvent that dissolves every lock on a poisoner's conscience."

"The ward-stones," Serah said quietly. Her knuckles had gone white around her bowl. "You didn't dull the wards. You poisoned the stones themselves."

"Arsenic of a very particular preparation," Quill agreed. "Slow. Patient. A stone drinks poison the way a drunkard drinks promises — gladly, and without noticing until the legs give out. The ward-stones of Thornfell were living things, girl, older than any kingdom, fed on starlight and oath-light, and I fed them death for a full turning of the seasons. By the time the order noticed the wards going soft, the poison was in the roots of the thing. Irreversible. They blamed the keepers." He inclined his head toward Serah. "Your order has been hanging its own ever since, hunting a saboteur who died in a name-change and a cassock a very long time ago."

Serah made a sound that was not quite a word. Pip had gone pale as chalk.

"But the dead didn't come back," Ivo said. His voice was very level, the voice of a man tasting something and refusing to spit. "Did they."

"No." Quill closed his eyes. "What came through was the court. The wards were never only a wall, do you understand? They were a *sentence*. A judgment written in starlight across the whole range, saying: *these debts are discharged, these souls belong to themselves.* Every ward-stone was a clause in that sentence. Kill the clauses and the contract falls open — and the under-court has been collecting ever since. Slowly at first. A soul here, a soul there, always bought, always sold, always with a signature from above." He opened his eyes and found Ivo's. "The crown learned quickly what the mountain offered. I gave them the key, son. Everything since — the ledgers, the caskets, Wren Ashvale in her cage of light — every link in that chain was forged in the gap I made."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the bell-less tower and made no sound at all, because there was nothing left up there to ring.

"Why now?" Pip asked, barely above a whisper. "If it happened a hundred years ago, why did the stars go out *now*?"

"Ah." Quill smiled, and hated the smile, and wore it anyway. "Because sin compounds, child. Like interest. Like rot. For ninety-seven years the stones held — barely, the way a dying man holds a door — because the poison was finite and the stones were vast. But the crown kept selling. Soul after soul after soul, drawn through the wound I made, and every soul pulled through thins the ward further, the way a rope frays faster where it's already worn. Three weeks ago the last strand went. The mountain didn't break in. The mountain simply *arrived*, the way a creditor arrives, and took down the lights so no one could read the terms." He spread his hands, an old man's hands, spotted and steady. "The stars went out over Thornfell because I killed them. Slowly. With excellent craft and the very best intentions. I have spent a hundred years tending the wounded of the thing I built, and I would spend another hundred, but I don't think the mountain intends to grant me the time."

Silence. The specific silence of people recalculating everything they thought they knew about the man pouring their tea.

It was Serah who spoke first, and to Quill's lasting surprise there was no hatred in it — only the flat, exhausted recognition of a woman who had been punished for another's crime and had just been handed the criminal's name. "The order exiled me," she said, "for failing wards I never touched. They flogged the keeper-mistress. They burned Halric's credentials posthumously. And the man responsible was feeding soup to farmers in the foothills."

"Yes," Quill said simply.

"And you're telling us why?"

"Because you're going into the mountain," Quill said, "and I am going with you, and you deserve to know what walks beside you. Also—" and here the groove in him, the yoke-groove, ached so badly he nearly sat down, "—because there is one more thing. The heart-ward. The master-stone deep under the courts, the anchor of the whole sentence. When I poisoned the border stones, I prepared the dose at the source. I still remember the formulation. Every ratio. Every flaw I exploited." He met each of their eyes in turn. "Which means I am the only man alive who knows exactly where the stone is weakest, and exactly how to un-poison it. Reforging the wards isn't a metaphor, friends. It's chemistry. And the chemistry is mine."

* * *

Later — much later, when Pip slept curled by the hearth and Serah stood watch at the ruined door with her face turned to the starless sky — Ivo remained at the table, turning his cup slowly, the way he did when he was tasting a conversation instead of a wine.

"You believed them," Ivo said at last. "The delegation. Why?"

Quill considered lying, found the muscle atrophied beyond use, and told the truth.

"Because they showed me names," he said. "Little lists. Children who'd died of the flux, mothers lost in childbirth, a shepherd boy taken by the mountain itself. Handwritten. Tear-stained. And I thought: here, finally, is a client whose cause needs no explaining." He shook his head. "It wasn't until years later that I understood. Those lists weren't written by the grieving. They were written by recruiters. The court had studied us, Ravensworth — studied our pity the way you study a palate. They knew exactly which vintage of mercy would make a poisoner swallow his own poison." He laughed softly, terribly. "I was the first soul they collected who never knew he'd been bought."

Ivo was quiet a long moment. "And the mark," he said. "Yours. You bear it too."

Quill rolled back his sleeve without being asked. There, on the inside of his ancient forearm, the spiral sigil sat in lines of ash-grey — but unlike the farmwife's mark, his had gone silver, and silvered further each year, and at its center the spiral had begun to close upon itself like a fist.

"Ninety-seven years of installments," Quill said. "The mountain is patient with its oldest debtors. It wants me to arrive rested." He pulled the sleeve down. "But I'll tell you a secret, taster, since we're trading in secrets tonight. I'm glad the stars went out."

Ivo looked at him.

"Because for a hundred years I committed my sin anew every morning — by hiding it. Each dawn I chose the lie again, fresh, and each dawn the choosing weighed a little more. Now the lie is impossible. Now the truth sits in the open air of this kitchen, ugly and necessary, and for the first time since Aldous Fenwick mixed his first fatal draught, I am—" he searched for the word, found it, winced at it, "—*lighter*. Guilt borne in secret ferments, son. Guilt spoken goes to vinegar. Vinegar keeps. You can build with vinegar."

"You sound like a sermon," Ivo said.

"I am a sermon," Quill said. "An extremely long one. Pray God the ending's worth it."

He rose to bank the fire, and as he bent to the coals he felt it again — the mountain's breath, rising through the flagstones, slow and tidal and attentive. Reading its ledger. Running its finger down the column.

And then he felt something else, something new, something that stopped his old hands over the embers and turned them cold: beneath the mountain's slow respiration, deeper than the courts, deeper than the vaults, a third rhythm had woken. Quick. Hungry. Counting.

The new moon was four days off. He had known that, of course — had watched the sky's blank calendar the way a condemned man watches the scaffold-builders. But knowledge was one thing. This was the mountain *licking its lips*.

"Ravensworth," he said quietly, without turning. "How soon can your smuggler roads get us to the first gate?"

Ivo followed his gaze down through the floor, through the stone, to whatever lay listening below. "Two days. Maybe less, if the passes haven't shifted."

"They've shifted," Quill said. "Everything's shifted. But two days will do." He straightened, and took down from the shelf the one jar he had sworn never to reopen, and blew a century of dust from its lid. "Because in two days we walk into that mountain carrying the only confession it has ever feared — and I intend, my friend, to be heard."
