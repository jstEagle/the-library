## 19. The New Moon Deadline

The moon died on a Tuesday, and Ivo Ravensworth felt it the way a man feels a door close behind him.

He was sitting in the smuggler's cache — a hollow beneath a fallen watchtower, half a day's climb from Quill's chapel — when the sky above the crack in the roof went utterly black where the thin crescent should have hung. Not clouded. *Absent.* The stars around it still burned, indifferent and bright, but the space where the new moon would be born had been carved out of the night like a wound in a page.

"Quill," he said quietly.

The old priest looked up from his mortar. He had been grinding valerian and something bitterer — one of his own preparations, Ivo suspected, for the marked villagers who came to him at night with spiral sigils chalked behind their ears and light leaking from their eyes. "I see it."

"What is it?"

"The ledger closing." Quill wiped his hands on his robe with the unhurried care of a man who had long ago stopped being afraid of anything except himself. "A month ends, boy. And at the end of every month, accounts are settled."

Serah Vail sat apart from them, sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening. She had been doing that for two hours. "Settled how?"

"You know how," Quill said. "You kept the wards. You knew what fed them before they died."

She didn't answer. The knife whispered against the stone.

Ivo stood and went to the crack in the roof, because looking away from it seemed worse than looking. Below them, down the black shoulder of Thornfell, the villages of the marked lay dark. He thought of Pip's chalked sigil, of the way she'd said *they take us slow* without any drama at all, as if describing weather. Slow collection, Quill had called it. A soul drawn out drop by drop over years, the way a taster draws poison into his blood by increments until the poison has nowhere left to go.

But there was another kind of taking. He had seen it in the mirrored halls, in the caged lights hanging above the gentry's feast like lanterns at a festival.

*Spent,* the steward had said, in that voice like water running over coins. *Unspent souls are wasted, taster. We do not waste.*

Now he understood what spending meant.

* * *

The old woman who brought them bread that evening confirmed it without meaning to.

Her name was Maret; she had been Quill's first parishioner among the marked, back when his congregation numbered four and met in a sheep shed. Her husband had been collected three winters ago. She spoke of him the way people speak of the dead, which was itself a small horror, because he wasn't dead — he was *down there*, or some part of him was, burning in a gentry lord's veins.

"They told me he'd gone to service the crown," she said, setting out cups. "That's what the king's men said. A great honor. My Tam, serving the crown below the mountain." She laughed, and the laugh cracked down the middle. "And then last spring, my girl came home from market saying the Hollis family had a wedding. Young master Hollis, ninety years old and smooth-faced as a bridegroom. And everyone danced."

Ivo's hands were very still around his cup. "When was the wedding?"

"Spring." Maret frowned at him. "Why?"

Because Wren had been taken in autumn, he wanted to say. Because if the collections were spent seasonally, like harvests, then—

"How often?" Serah asked, saving him the words. "How often do they spend?"

Maret looked at her hands. "Every dark of the moon," she said. "Everyone knows that. It's why we chalk the doors. Why we don't go out after dusk, those nights. The old folk used to leave offerings at the mountain's foot — bread and salt, a coin, anything with your name scratched on it. So they'd know you were *paid*, see. Paid folk don't get collected twice." She shook her head. "Nobody leaves offerings anymore. Nobody can afford to be remembered by anything down there."

After she had gone, wrapping her shawl against the wind, no one spoke for a long time.

Then Quill said, gently, the way a physician says such things: "Three days."

"I heard her." Ivo's voice came out flat and strange, someone else's voice. "Three days, and they spend whatever's still in the vault. Whatever hasn't been claimed or bargained for or—" His throat closed. He made himself finish. "*Whatever I haven't gotten out first.*"

Wren's name written in ash, sealed in a royal casket, delivered by his own hand into the under-court's keeping. Every road he'd walked since the night without stars had led here, to this arithmetic: three days, one vault, one caged light with her laughter inside it, and between him and all of it a court of beautiful hollow things who had just watched him refuse their steward to his face.

"They know you're coming," Serah said. It wasn't cruelty; it was Serah, which had come to mean the same thing. "You refused the steward. You burned a casket and put your own mark in their ledger. They've had weeks to prepare a welcome."

"Good." He heard his own voice and disliked it — too hard, too quick, the bravado of a man whistling past a gate that took memories for toll. "Let them prepare."

"That isn't courage, Ivo. That's just noise."

"I know what it is." He turned on her, and the words came up hot before he could weigh them, the way poison comes up when you press on the stomach. "It's three days, Serah. Three days, and then she's fuel. Fuel for a man who's been ninety for a century. Do you understand what *spending* means? I've tasted what they drink. Ten years at that table, and I know exactly what power tastes like, and it tastes like—" He stopped. Breathed. The cache was silent except for the wind worrying the fallen tower's stones. "It tastes like nothing. That's the worst of it. All those souls, and it tastes like *nothing*, like watered wine, like ash. They burn people and get *nothing*, and they keep doing it, and in three days it's her turn, and I am standing in a hole in the ground sharpening knives about it—"

"That's mine," Serah said.

He blinked.

"The knife." She held it up, faintly amused, faintly not. "You're standing in a hole in the ground while I sharpen knives about it. You're planning. I can tell. You plan like a man counting spoons." She sheathed it. "So count."

* * *

He counted.

That was the truth of Ivo Ravensworth, the thing ten years of the king's table had built into his bones: when terror came, he catalogued it. Fear of drowning? Note the taste of the water. Fear of dying? Note the flavor of the cup. So he sat with Quill's ink and a scrap of vellum — the same kind of vellum, some cold corner of him observed, on which a king sold souls — and he counted.

Three days.

One day to reach the marked villages and raise Pip's people; they would need numbers for the service tunnels, and promises were cheap but had to be made anyway. Half a day after that to the second gate, which took truth instead of memory, which meant he would stand before it and speak his betrayal aloud to the mountain itself, and he did not know yet whether the words would survive being said. Then the deep roads, the ones only smugglers knew, the ones he'd memorized for a crown that had used his knowledge to build its own gallows. Then the heart-ward. Then the vault.

Against that: the steward's hunters, whom they had already run from once through the mirrored halls and would meet again on ground of the court's choosing. Against that: Quill's age and Serah's half-dead ward-craft and his own body, which had carried poison so long it had begun to feel less like armor than like sediment. Against that, largest of all: the possibility that a freed soul, once spent, could not be freed at all. Quill wouldn't lie to him. When he'd asked, the old man had been quiet a long time and then said, *I don't know, and a man who says he knows is selling something.*

Two hundred and eleven hours, give or take. He wrote the number at the top of the vellum and hated it. A number was a cage with bars made of arithmetic.

"You're doing the thing again," said a voice from the doorway, and Pip slipped in out of the dark with snow in her hair and her chalk-stained fingers full of something wrapped in cloth. Fifteen years old and marked since birth, she moved like the mountain owed her money. In a way, Ivo supposed it did. "The face. Like you're tasting something bad."

"I am."

"Pip." Serah straightened. "We said dawn. It's not safe—"

"No place is safe. That's the whole problem, isn't it?" She dropped onto a crate and unwrapped the cloth: dried meat, black bread, and a twist of paper that Ivo recognized with a jolt — salt, coarse and grey, of the kind the old folk had once scratched their names into. Offerings. "Gran sent it. She says if you're going to die in the mountain, do it paid." Pip grinned, and the grin didn't quite hold. "She also says — she remembers the last dark-of-the-moon before the wards fell. Says the gentry came up to the surface that night. First time in living memory. Stood at the tree line in their fine coats, watching the villages. Counting."

"Counting what?"

"Who was left." Pip looked down at her chalked fingers. "There are more marks this year than anyone remembers. More than the wards falling explains. Gran thinks—" She stopped herself, jaw tight, fifteen years old refusing to be afraid in front of adults who were visibly afraid. "Gran thinks they've been *hungry*. All these years, kept out by the wards, and now the door's open and the pantry's full, and the new moon is the night they eat."

* * *

They worked through what remained of the night, because sleep was a country none of them could afford the toll to enter.

Quill compounded what he could carry — poisons for gentry wine, antidotes for his own mistakes, a bitter paste that slowed the drawing-out of a marked soul by a few hours, a few hours being, in Quill's arithmetic, sometimes the whole distance between a grave and a miracle. Serah drew ward-sigils from memory, her order's true forms, the ones that predated the corruption, testing each against the mountain's breath until her fingers bled and the sigils held for seconds longer each time. And Ivo sat with his map — the map of losses, the map that had once been his pride — and traced the roads only smugglers know, finding the ways that still lived and mourning the ones that had closed like veins.

Somewhere past midnight, Quill came and sat beside him uninvited.

"In my order," the old man said, "before I poisoned everything I touched, we had a saying. *The moon's death is not an ending. It is a doorway swinging both ways.*"

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning the dark of the moon is when the veil between courts wears thinnest. It is when they collect." He turned his ruined, kind eyes on Ivo. "But it is also the only night a soul can pass *back*. The door swings both ways, boy. Their strength is our strength, if we are brave enough to walk through the same gap."

Ivo stared at him. "You're saying the new moon isn't just their deadline."

"I'm saying deadlines cut both directions." Quill smiled, and for a moment the poisoner showed through the priest, sharp and gleaming. "They have chosen the night of their greatest power to finalize their theft. Which means they have also chosen the night when a stolen soul, if someone is fool enough and fast enough, can be *carried out*."

Three days. Two hundred and eleven hours. And somewhere beneath the mountain, in a cage of lights, a soul that smelled like rain on hot stone and tasted like laughter was waiting to learn which way the door would swing.

Ivo folded the vellum with his counted hours and put it inside his shirt, over his heart, where the ledger-mark sat beneath the skin like a second pulse. Outside, the wound in the sky where the moon should have been born yawned wider, and far below, he could have sworn he felt it — the mountain breathing in.

Preparing to swallow.

Dawn was two hours off. Pip was already chalking a route on the floor, talking fast, her fear transmuted entirely into motion. And at the edge of the firelight, Serah caught his eye and gave him a single nod — the nod of a soldier who has done the counting and found the numbers wanting and is coming anyway.

"Three days," Ivo said aloud, to all of them, to himself, to the mountain. "Then we go down through the gap."

The wind picked up outside the fallen tower, carrying with it, from far below, a sound that might have been stone settling, and might have been applause.
