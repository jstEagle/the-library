## 21. The Gentry's Feast

The livery they stole was grey, and that was the first cruelty of it. Grey as ash, grey as the ledger-marks that crawled up Serah Vail's forearms beneath the sleeves, grey as everything in this mountain that had once been a person and had been spent.

She pulled the servant's coat over her shoulders and felt the fabric settle against her skin like a verdict she hadn't yet read aloud.

"Stop fidgeting," Ivo murmured beside her. He had his own grey coat buttoned to the throat, his dark hair combed flat, and he looked — this was the terrible part, the part she would not say where he could hear it — he looked like he belonged here. Ten years of standing silent at a king's elbow while other men ate had taught him how to be furniture. Furniture with a knife's memory.

"I'm not fidgeting," Serah said. "I'm memorizing."

"Memorizing what?"

"The exits." She let her gaze travel down the service corridor they'd been funneled through with thirty other servants whose eyes never quite met anyone else's. "There are four ways out of that hall. Two are real. One is a trap, because nothing down here is offered freely, and one is warded so old I can taste it." She paused. "I can't taste wards anymore, can I. That's the point of me being disgraced."

"You can taste them," Ivo said. "You just can't touch them without permission. There's a difference."

"There is," she agreed, "and the difference has cost me everything twice."

Behind them came Brother Quill, moving slowly, leaning on a tray of wine bottles as though the tray were a walking staff. The old priest had dressed himself in the same livery, but on him it hung like penance rather than disguise. His hands shook. They had shaken since the heart-ward, since the night under the new moon when he'd told them, voice steady as a funeral bell, exactly which stones he had poisoned and why. Pip walked at his side, small and chalk-dusted and utterly unafraid, carrying two pitchers of watered wine she could barely lift with both arms. The girl had insisted. *The marked can carry things into that hall that no one watches,* she'd said. *They watch beauty. They watch power. They don't watch servants.*

Serah had learned, over the years of her disgrace, to believe children about the blind spots of powerful people.

* * *

The feast hall opened before them like the inside of an instrument designed for one long note.

It was vast — vaster than the king's hall above, vaster than any hall had a right to be inside a mountain — and its ceiling was a slow-turning wheel of captured light: souls, dozens of them, hundreds perhaps, suspended in crystal cages and arranged into constellations that had no business existing. They were beautiful. That was the horror of the place; it did not bother hiding its horror behind ugliness. It dressed everything in beauty first, the way a con man dresses in his victim's favorite color.

And the gentry themselves —

Serah had imagined monsters. She had prepared herself for monsters. Rotting things, twisted things, something her order's oldest manuals might have named and diagrammed. Instead the gentry sat along a table of black glass that ran the length of the hall, and they were gorgeous, and they were wrong in a way her eye kept sliding off of. Their faces were too symmetrical. Their skin held light the way still water holds the moon. And their eyes — their eyes were the worst thing. Each pair of eyes held a different century, and none of them held anything recent.

A lord near the head of the table laughed at something, and the laugh was perfect, and it went on two seconds too long, and then stopped all at once like a door closing.

"They eat memories," Quill said quietly, at her shoulder. "That is the feast you're looking at. Not the food." He nodded toward the dishes the servants carried: pale meats, silver fruits, bread that gleamed faintly. "The food is theater. What they consume is in the lights above. When one is spent, they pour it out across the table, and each of them takes a portion, and for an evening they wear someone's whole life. A childhood. A wedding. A war. Whatever the soul carried best."

"And afterward?" Serah asked, though she already knew. She wanted him to say it anyway. Grief sometimes needed witnesses more than answers.

"Afterward there is less," Quill said. "Of the soul, and of them. They do not grow. They only accumulate."

They took their places among the serving line. Ivo stood at her left with a laden tray, spine straight, face smooth as poured wax. Serah had watched him build that face a hundred times in the weeks since Ashvale, and she knew now what it cost him, and she knew what lived underneath it: a man doing arithmetic with his own heart and hating every sum.

Wren's soul was up there somewhere. In the wheel of lights. In a cage.

*Serve the wine,* Serah told herself. *Walk the floor. Count the doors. Do not look up.*

She looked up anyway. She couldn't help it. The constellations turned overhead like a sky that belonged to someone else, and among those cold bright points she tried to find one that burned differently — warmer, quicker, stubborn. Wren would burn stubbornly. Even caged. Especially caged.

But the lights all looked alike from below, and that, too, was part of the design.

* * *

The service of the meal proceeded by rules Serah didn't know and therefore broke within the first ten minutes — a gentry lady with hair like poured pewter caught her refilling a cup from the wrong side and looked at her with an expression of mild, ancient amusement, the way one looks at a dog that has attempted arithmetic. Serah bowed, corrected, retreated, and filed the woman's face away in the part of her mind where she kept everyone she intended to survive past.

Then the pouring-out began.

She felt it before she understood it: a change in the air of the hall, a pressure like the moment before summer lightning. The music — there had been music, thin and sweet and sourceless — faltered. At the high table, a steward in white moved among the gentry with a decanter that held no wine, only light, thick and golden and moving with the sluggish reluctance of something that knew what was happening to it.

The gentry raised their cups.

And the memories began to play across their faces.

Serah had no words ready for what she saw, because there were no words for it in any language she spoke. A lord drank, and his handsome face became a farmyard — she watched a boyhood move across his features like weather: mud, a dog, a mother calling from a doorway, rain on a slate roof. Another drank and wept without tears, her cheeks tracking lines of pure light, and for a moment her mouth shaped words in some border dialect, and her hands remembered the shape of a loom. They wore these lives openly, greedily, the way mortals wear festival clothes. And when the memory faded, their own hollow beauty returned underneath, unchanged, untouched, and Serah understood that this was the whole obscene architecture of the place: the gentry could not feel, so they ate feeling. Could not live, so they consumed lives. Every face at that table was a mask worn over a hunger, and the hunger was older than the kingdom, and it had been fed for a hundred years on paper signed by a crown.

*This is what your king bought with borrowed power,* she thought. *He sold the harvest and called it policy.*

Ivo had gone very still beside her.

Not servant-still. Something else. She glanced at him and found his gaze fixed upward, on the wheel of lights, on one particular cage that hung near the turning rim — and his face, the smooth servant face, had developed a crack down the middle of it that only she could see, because she had spent weeks learning to read the taster the way he read poison.

"Ivo," she breathed.

"She's laughing," he said. His voice was almost nothing. "Look at the lord in blue. Third from the steward. Look at his face."

Serah looked.

The lord in blue had just drunk his portion of the poured-out soul, and his beautiful vacant features were rearranging themselves around someone else's joy. A woman's laugh moved across his mouth — a real laugh, rough-edged, the kind that comes out of the belly of someone who has been afraid and decided to laugh anyway. Her smile crooked leftward. Her eyebrows did something irreverent. For three heartbeats the lord's face was not his own, and it was warm, and it was alive, and it was *Wren's.*

Serah knew it from Ivo's face, which had broken open entirely now, quietly, the way ice breaks on a river in spring — all at once and without sound. He stood holding his tray while a dead-eyed immortal wore his beloved's laughter as a seasoning, and Serah watched him watch it, and she thought: *there it is.* Not rage yet. Rage would come. First there was this — the naked, unbearable intimacy of seeing the person you love worn by a stranger who doesn't even know what they're tasting.

"She liked thunderstorms," Ivo said, so softly only Serah could hear. "She used to say storms were the mountains arguing. That laugh — she laughed like that when I told her the palace kitchens put saffron in everything because the king thinks poverty is a flavor." His jaw tightened. "They're eating her. Piece by piece, evening by evening, and she's still in there watching them do it."

"Yes," Serah said. Because lying to him would have been a mercy, and mercy was not what he needed tonight. "And yes, she knows. And yes, that means she is enduring it. Which means she is stronger than every thing in this room."

His hand trembled on the tray. Then steadied. The crack in his face sealed itself over, slower than before, leaving a scar-line she suspected would never quite close.

"Grief is useful right now," she said. "Don't waste it on despair. Spend it on attention. Tell me everything you see in that face while it lasts. Everything. We may never get this close again."

He stared at her. And then — this was why she had cut him down off a gallows, why she had sworn an oath of ash with him instead of gold — he *did it.* He turned back to the lord in blue and studied the stolen life playing across it with a taster's discipline, cataloguing, noting, grieving with his eyes open.

"The cage third from the rim," he murmured. "Blue-white light, burns fast. The ward-lock on it bears a royal seal — I can see the stamp from here, they don't hide it, they don't think anyone down here will ever dare accuse them." A breath. "Two guards on the dais stair. The steward keeps the master keys in a ring on his left hip, worn inside a folded sash — he touches them when he lies, which is often. The gentry sit closest to whoever poured their cup. Rank by proximity. If we ever need to know which of them is strongest—" his eyes flicked along the table, counting, always counting— "the one they all lean away from sits at the far end. Alone. Old."

Serah committed every word to memory the way she'd once committed ward-sigils: precisely, permanently, in the place grief could not reach.

"That's reconnaissance," she said. "That's what your grief just bought us."

"It doesn't feel like enough."

"It never does." She lifted her pitcher. "Pour, taster. Smile at the monsters. We have a map to finish."

* * *

The rest of the feast passed in a haze of servility and horror, and Serah catalogued both.

She saw a gentry lady drink a soldier's death and spend twenty minutes reliving a charge across a frozen field, shouting orders in a dead language, her beautiful face lit with borrowed courage while her neighbors applauded politely. She saw a child's soul — or what remained of one — poured out as a digestif, and watched the entire table soften with a memory of a birthday, candles, a name sung off-key, and she had to grip her tray until the wood creaked, because if she had set it down she did not know what her hands would have done instead.

Quill stood near the far wall, motionless, tears running freely down the old priest's face. No one marked him. Servants weeping in corners were, apparently, part of the decor.

Pip worked the room with her pitchers and her chalk-dust innocence, and three times Serah lost sight of her and three times found her again, and each time the girl had drifted a little closer to the dais, a little closer to the steward, a little closer to the great sealed doors behind the high table — mapping, in her small silent way, the same territory Ivo was mapping with his eyes. The marked could walk where the mountain did not look. Pip was spending that invisibility like a miser spending a fortune she'd been saving her whole life.

Near midnight — if midnight meant anything down here, where no sky reached — the music changed. Deeper. Slower. The gentry rose as one, cups in hand, and turned toward the head of the table.

And the steward stepped forward to give the toast.

He was tall and grey-robed and ageless, and his voice filled the hall without effort, the way water fills a bowl.

"To the mountain," he said, "which provides. To the crown above, which pays its debts. And to the coming collection—" his gaze swept the hall, and for half a heartbeat Serah would have sworn it passed directly over her, over Ivo, over Pip, lingering the way a finger lingers on a page— "when all accounts will be brought current. Drink deep. The vintage is finest when the debt comes due."

The hall drank. Above them, the wheel of souls turned, and dimmed, very slightly, all together — a hundred little lights guttering in unison, like a city drawing a single breath of fear.

Serah poured wine and smiled and died a little more inside, and wrote the moment down in the permanent archive behind her eyes.

* * *

They shed the grey coats in the service tunnel afterward, and stood in the dark breathing the cold stone air, and for a long time nobody spoke.

Pip arrived last, slipping out of the shadows with chalk on her fingers and triumph and terror fighting for possession of her face.

"Got it," she whispered. "The doors behind the dais. Three locks, and the middle one's the royal seal — same as on Wren's cage. And there's a corridor behind them going down. Down-down. Toward the vaults." She wiped her nose. "Also I dropped a pitcher. Nobody cared. Servants drop things. We're furniture." Her voice cracked on the last word, and Quill put a hand on her shoulder, and she steadied.

"We have it all," Serah said. "Doors, guards, keys, seals, the vault road. Tomorrow night is the last night before the new moon. Tomorrow night, we go through those doors."

Ivo stood apart, still in his grey coat, staring back the way they had come — back toward the distant glow of the feast hall, toward the turning wheel where a blue-white light burned fast and stubborn in its cage.

"When he wore her laugh," he said quietly, to no one, to the stone, "he wasted it. He swallowed it and it slid off him like rain off glass. She's been poured out a dozen times, a hundred times, and none of it sticks, none of it *stays*, because they can't keep anything, that's the rot at the center of them — they consume and consume and remain empty, and she remains *her*, even in pieces, even caged—"

He stopped. His fists were shaking.

"Ivo," Serah said.

"He touched the key-ring when he toasted," Ivo said, and his voice came back level, cold, precise, the taster's voice, the ledger-keeper's voice. "The steward lied when he promised the collection. Lied about something. And gentlemen—" the ghost of a terrible smile crossed his face— "I have spent ten years learning what poison tastes like in a liar's mouth. I know exactly what we're going to serve him tomorrow night."

Serah looked at him in the tunnel-dark — the quiet man, the furniture-man, the taster with a decade of flavors and a broken heart and a plan assembling itself behind his eyes like a blade being ground — and she felt the oath of ash tighten around them both like a drawn bow.

"Then we'd better get Pip home," she said, "and start cooking."
