## 22. What Power Tastes Like

They came for him at the hour the under-court called evening, though no sun had ever set here and none ever would. The light in the mirrored halls simply *diminished*, the way a candle diminishes when someone cups their hand around it — a courtesy performed by unseen fingers, an imitation of night for creatures who had forgotten what night was.

Ivo knew it was coming because the servants changed his clothes.

He had been given a cell that was not a cell — a guest chamber, they called it, walled in polished obsidian so black it drank the torchlight and gave back only himself. He had seen three days pass in that room, or what he took for days, marked by meals he did not touch and sleep that came like a thief instead of a friend. And on this third evening, two gentry-servants arrived without knocking, bearing folded garments on their pale arms: a doublet of deep green silk, silver-threaded at the cuffs, cut to a fashion a century out of date and yet somehow more current than anything worn above ground.

"You will dress," said the taller one. Its voice was pleasant the way a still pond is pleasant, all surface and no bottom. "The Steward receives you."

"I've had better dinner invitations," Ivo said.

Neither servant smiled. Smiling, he had noticed, was something the gentry did deliberately, as an act, the way a merchant weighs out coin. These two had not been paid to smile. They simply waited, hands folded, until the silence became a kind of pressure and Ivo understood that dressing was not, in fact, optional.

The doublet fit perfectly. That frightened him more than anything else in the mountain had managed to frighten him yet — more than the caged lights, more than the spiral sigil chalked on empty doorways, more than the memory-toll at the first gate where he had paid away the taste of Wren's laugh and felt the hole where it used to live. Someone in this place had measured him. Had taken his shoulders, his chest, the length of his arms, and committed them to record while he slept or walked or bled. Power, he thought, begins with measurement. It wants to know exactly how much of you there is to take.

* * *

They led him through corridors he tried — and failed — to map.

This was the cruelest trick of the mirrored palace, he decided: every hall reflected itself infinitely, so that walking one passage felt like walking forty. His smuggler's instincts, honed across ten years of Thornfell passes and cellar doors, reached for landmarks and found only his own face repeated down an endless gallery, thinner each time, paler, receding toward some version of himself he did not want to meet. He counted turns instead. Left, right, right, a stair descending seven steps, left again. It was useless and he knew it, but counting kept the animal part of his brain quiet, and the animal part of his brain was the only part still insisting they would leave this place alive.

Serah and Quill were not with him. The steward had requested him alone — *requested*, in the language of courts, meaning commanded — and Serah had gripped his arm hard enough to bruise before the servants parted them. "It will offer you something," she'd said. "That's what it does. That's what power *is*, down here. Don't sit in any chair."

"Why not?"

"Because chairs are how they caught the first ones." She had said it flatly, the way she said everything, a woman who had been hanged once and found it educational. "Nobody falls from a standing position, Ravensworth. You have to be seated first."

Now, walking those infinite halls, he turned her words over like a stone in his pocket.

The throne room — if that is what it was; it might have been a dining hall or a tomb or all three at once — opened around him like the inside of an instrument. Vaulted ceilings ribbed with pale wood. Mirrors everywhere, but here they were old mirrors, spotted and warping, so that his reflections bent and swam like fish beneath ice. At the far end, upon a dais of grey stone, stood a table. Not a throne. A long, low table of black oak, set with a single chair on the near side and none on the far, and behind the table stood the Steward.

Ivo had met the creature twice before, in the judgment hall and at the gates, but he looked at it now with a taster's attention, and what he saw made his stomach tighten.

The Steward wore the shape of a tall man gone grey at the temples, dignified, precise, dressed in the livery of some royal house that had crumbled into dust before Thornfell had a name. But its eyes were wrong in a way Ivo recognized the way one recognizes a flavor one has swallowed before. He had spent ten years tasting the king's food, ten years learning the difference between poison that kills quickly and poison that kills slowly, between venom and rot, between a thing that is dead and a thing that was *emptied*. The Steward's eyes held nothing behind them. Whatever had lived there had been poured out, carefully, cup by cup, a very long time ago. Something else had moved into the empty house and arranged the furniture.

"Master Ravensworth." The Steward gestured to the chair. "Please."

"I'll stand."

"Of course." No offense taken, no offense possible. It seated itself with the unhurried grace of something that had never once needed to hurry. Between them on the table, it placed a decanter of wine the color of garnets and a single cup. "A vintage from your own world, actually. The year of the Ashvale harvest. You'll know it."

"I don't drink on duty."

"And is this duty?" The Steward tilted its head, and the gesture was almost human, almost warm, which was the worst thing about it. "What would you call this errand of yours, I wonder. The crown sent you to fetch a casket. The casket held a name. You burned the casket and took the debt onto yourself. You have since broken into my court's first gate on a memory you could not afford, consorted with a hanged woman and a poisoned priest, and infiltrated my lords' feast disguised as a scullery boy with ash still under his fingernails." It paused. "I would call that devotion. Would you call it duty?"

Ivo said nothing. In his experience, the man who spoke second learned more than the man who spoke first, and he had survived a decade of royal tables by being the second speaker always.

The Steward seemed content with his silence. It poured the wine — one measure, into the single cup — and pushed it across the oak until it sat equidistant between them, an offering and a test and a joke all at once. "You needn't fear it. There is nothing in that cup that can harm you. We both know there is very little in any world that can."

"That's a strange thing to say about a cup of wine."

"It is not a strange thing to say to a taster." The Steward folded its hands. "Do you know why we asked for you, Master Ravensworth? Not seized you — we could have seized you at any gate. Do you know why we *invited*?"

"Because the invitation costs you nothing and teaches you everything."

"Partly." Something flickered across the hollow face that was, in its way, the closest thing to honesty Ivo had seen from it. "But mostly because you are the rarest thing to walk into this mountain in three hundred years, and we have been waiting three hundred years for you to walk in."

"The soul-marked come through constantly, I'm told. Whole villages of them."

"Yes. The marked. Debtors. The sold and the selling, the borrowed and the spent." The Steward waved a hand, dismissive as a man brushing crumbs. "Common currency. But you—" it leaned forward, and the candlelight did something obscene in its empty eyes, "—you are *immune*. Ten years of the royal table. Henbane in the duke's stew. Foxglove in the queen's tonic — yes, we know, we know a great deal that happens above ground; the wards kept armies out, not whispers. Arsenic in your own wine, twice, in your third year, when someone at court decided a taster who had grown too valuable was a taster who had grown too dangerous. You swallowed it all and asked for seconds. Do you understand what that means, down here? Do you understand what your soul *is*?"

"A stubborn one."

"An *unpoisonable* one." For the first time, the Steward raised its voice, and the mirrors rang with it softly, a hundred grey reflections speaking in chorus. "Every soul in this court is a vessel, Master Ravensworth. Vessels crack. Vessels sour, curdle, leak. My lords must filter what they drink through layers of harvested souls precisely because their own vessels failed centuries ago — that is what it means to be gentry, to be beautiful and endless and *hollow*. But yours never cracked. A decade of the worst poisons men have devised, and the vessel holds. Clean walls. Unbroken. Do you know how many of those exist? I know of four. Two are mine already. One is buried under a hill in the east that I do not choose to disturb." It smiled then, deliberately, a merchant weighing out coin. "And one is standing in front of me, refusing a chair."

Ivo looked at the cup of wine between them. Garnet-dark. The year of the Ashvale harvest — Wren's vintage, the contraband he had run for her through the cellar door only tasters knew, the wine whose taste had been the last honest pleasure of his life before he traded her name for his post. They had chosen it deliberately. Of course they had. Everything in this room was deliberate: the outdated fashion, the perfect fit, the single chair, the wine that remembered her.

"You're going to offer me a seat at your table," he said. "Literally, I notice. There's one chair."

"Literally and otherwise." The Steward spread its hands. "Consider what you are above ground. A servant. A swallow-and-survive, a piece of palace furniture with a remarkable throat. The king you serve cannot look at you without remembering that your existence is an accusation — every dish you taste unpoisoned reminds him how often his food is *not*. He sent you into my mountain hoping to be rid of you, and hoping to use you, and those hopes cancelled each other out, which is why the errand was designed to kill you. The casket. The bandits. The new moon. You were meant to die in the dark carrying her name, Master Ravensworth. That was the whole of the plan. And you are too intelligent not to have worked that out."

Ivo had worked it out in chapter and verse. He said nothing, and the silence admitted everything.

"Now consider what you would be *here*." The Steward rose, and its shadow fell long across the black oak, and the shadows of the mirrors fell longer still. "My lords are dying. Oh, not quickly — they have been dying for a century, elegantly, in stages, spending other people's souls to postpone it — but the wells are running shallow. The collections slow. The stars went out because the ledger is overdrawn, and every gentry lord in this hall knows what overdrawn means, and they are beginning to *fear one another*, which is the true poison of any court. They poison each other, Master Ravensworth. Slowly. Subtly. A tincture in the bathwater. A dusting on the fruit. It is how they cull the weak and it is why they are weak, because the strong among them spend half their cunning guarding their cups." It stepped closer. "And here stands a man who cannot be culled. Who tastes the arsenic in the bathwater and drinks anyway and asks for seconds. Do you know what such a man becomes, in a court of poisoners?"

"Dead eventually. Everything here dies eventually. That's the whole engine of the place."

"Alive *longer*." The word cracked like a whip, and the mirrors shivered. Then the Steward composed itself, smooth as poured cream. "Longer than them. Longer than their grudges. Long enough to watch every rival fail, every faction burn itself down, until what remains of this court is yours — and then, in time, what remains of the world above, when the borders finish crumbling and the crown finishes rotting. You have spent ten years swallowing poison to keep a fat man alive. I am offering you a kingdom where the poison cannot touch you and the fat men kneel. All we ask—" and here its voice dropped, gentle, reasonable, the voice of every superior who had ever asked Ivo to do a small ugly thing for a large stated good, "—is cooperation. Resume your old work at a higher table. Taste for us. Tell us what is in the cups. And in exchange, a certain courier's light leaves its cage restored. Whole. Free. Walking out of this mountain under the returned stars with no memory of the cage at all."

There it was. Ivo had known it was coming since the servants brought the green doublet, had felt it approaching the way he felt henbane under honey, sweetness laid over something bitter, and hearing it spoken aloud still landed in his chest like a fist.

Because it was a *good* offer. That was the obscenity of it. It was a better offer than his own king had ever made him — the crown had bought Wren's freedom at the price of a suicide errand, and this creature offered the same prize for a decade of honest work at an honest wage. His immunity, which had always been a cage above ground, would be a crown below it. He would be valued. Measured, certainly — they had already measured him — but *valued*, seated, named, safe, and Wren free.

He thought of the duke saved by his tongue, who had thanked him with a nod colder than a closed door. He thought of the queen's tonic, foxglove-sweet, and how he had drunk it and said nothing because saying nothing was his function. He thought of the cellar door, and Wren's laugh, and the hole in him where the memory of that laugh used to live, paid out at the first gate like a coin bitten and passed along. He thought of the bandits opening the casket, and the name in ash written in a hand he recognized as his king's, and how he had carried that casket faithfully through the passes, believing — wanting to believe — that this time the errand was love instead of betrayal.

*Chairs,* Serah had said. *Nobody falls from a standing position.*

And now, looking at the single seat on the near side of the black oak table, Ivo understood the corruption from the inside, completely, the way only a taster understands a poison: not as a foreign thing but as a flavor, familiar, almost comforting. Corruption never kicked the door down. It set a chair by the fire. It poured the vintage you loved. It said *you were always meant for better than this* — and the terrible truth, the truth that made his hands want to shake, was that every word of it was accurate. He *had* been wasted above ground. He *had* been betrayed by the crown first. The Steward was not lying to him. That was the refinement of it, the distillation, the proof taken to its highest proof: corruption at its purest does not deceive you. It simply tells you the truth in an order designed to make you kneel.

"I have a question," Ivo said.

"Ask."

"The two immune souls you already own. The ones that are 'yours.'" He let the possessive hang in the air like smoke. "Were they also invited? Did they also get a chair, and a vintage, and a speech about how rare they were?"

The Steward regarded him for a long moment. The candlelight moved in its empty eyes and lit nothing.

"They sat," it said at last.

"And now they're what? Lining the walls of some lord's cellar? Filter-paper for a hollow man's wine?" Ivo picked up the cup. Held it. The garnet wine caught the light and held it too, beautifully, the way everything beautiful in this place held things it never gave back. "Here's what ten years of tasting taught me, and it's the only lesson worth the price. Every poisoner offers you a drink first. Every single one. Because the offer isn't about the drink — it's about seeing whether you'll take it. Whether you're the sort who takes things from hands like yours. And the moment you drink—" he turned the cup slowly, watching the wine turn, "—you're not a guest anymore. You're inventory."

"Or," said the Steward softly, "you are the one man in two worlds who cannot be poisoned, holding a cup that cannot harm him, refusing out of nothing but sentiment. Sentiment, Master Ravensworth. For a dead woman's echo. Is that really what you want carved on whatever the mountain leaves of you?"

"No," Ivo said. "What I want is for you to remember my name. Because you're going to hear it again — in a judgment hall, under older law than yours, with ten years of ledgers and seals and tasted poisons read aloud into the dark. And when the contracts void — and they will void — I want you to remember that you offered me a chair, and I stayed standing."

He set the cup down untasted, equidistant on the black oak, and walked out of the throne room without turning his back — sideways, step by step, the way one retreats from a snake one cannot yet see all of — past his hundred thinning reflections, past the servants who did not smile, into the halls of diminishing light.

Behind him, faint and courteous, he heard the Steward say to no one: "Then we proceed to collection."

And somewhere far below, in the dark of the service tunnels where a girl with chalk on her knuckles was tracing the mountain's wounds by feel, Pip looked up at the sound of distant stone shifting, and whispered: "It's started early. We have until tomorrow's dark, no more."
