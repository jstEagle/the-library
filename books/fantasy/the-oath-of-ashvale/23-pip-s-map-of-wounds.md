## 23. Pip's Map of Wounds

Pip had never shown anyone her map. She'd drawn it in the dark, mostly, by feel and by memory, on a scrap of tanned hide she kept rolled inside her boot where the chalk couldn't smear it. Four years she'd carried it — four years since the collectors came to Hollowmere and wrote their spiral on her doorframe and took her mother's name into the mountain while Pip hid in the flour bin with her hands over her own mouth.

The map was not a map of roads. Everyone else's maps were roads: Ivo Ravensworth's smuggler paths stitched through the passes like veins under skin; Serah Vail's ward-charts traced the old boundary lines with the precision of a woman who'd once been paid to maintain them. Pip's map was something no one else could have made, because it was a map of *hurts*. The mountain was alive — everyone who lived in its shadow knew that, though nobody said it above a whisper — and like any living thing it had places where it ached.

She unrolled it now on the floor of the marked village's meeting-house, weighting the corners with four river stones, and watched the faces of the three people who were about to ask her to risk everything she had left.

The lamplight made the hide glow amber. In the center she'd drawn the mountain itself, not as the pretty cone the surveyors drew for the king's tax rolls but as it actually was: a body curled around a heart. The service tunnels ran through it like the channels of an anthill gone mad — she'd drawn those from the memories the collectors' servants carried when they passed through Hollowmere, drunk on stolen wine and careless with talk, and later from her own two trips inside, both times as a runner carrying messages for the marked, both times certain she would die, both times too small and too quick to be worth stopping.

"There," she said, and put her finger on a spot near the mountain's root, where she'd drawn a wound in red ochre. It looked like nothing — a smudge, a stain — but her finger shook anyway. "That's where it's weakest. That's where we go."

Ivo leaned over the hide. He was thinner than he'd been when he came down out of the passes, Pip thought. They all were. But there was still something steady about him, the way a man is steady when he has already decided how much he is willing to lose and found the number acceptable. "How do you know it's weak?"

"Because it's *scarred*," Pip said. "You can't see it from outside. The gentry keep their courts up in the bright parts, all mirrors and music. But the ward-stones run through the whole mountain like bones. And this one—" she tapped the ochre smudge, "—this one is broken. Not dead. Broken, like a bone that healed crooked. Everything that flows through the wards flows wrong at this point. It leaks. It *aches*. The marked can feel it." She looked up at them. "Can't you? Some nights, all of us together, we feel it. Like a tooth that never stops hurting."

Brother Quill had gone very quiet. He was always quiet, but this was a different quality of quiet — the silence of a man listening for his own name in a crowd. Pip had noticed it before, in the chapel, whenever anyone spoke of the ward-stones. She'd thought it was piety. Now, watching him stare at her little red smudge as if it were a face, she understood it was something closer to guilt.

"Show me the shape of the break," he said.

Pip dipped a stick of charcoal in the lamp-soot and drew beside the mountain: a long stone rendered in cross-section, veined with lines of light, and across its middle a jagged gap where the light simply stopped — didn't fade, *stopped*, cut clean as if by a knife, and then resumed below in a sickly, stuttering pattern.

Quill made a sound low in his throat. He put one knotted hand flat on the floor as if the room were tilting.

"That's not age," he said. "Wards wear thin, they fray at the edges, they go soft like bread going stale. This is a wound. Deliberate. Someone poisoned this stone."

"You said you knew the old rites," Serah said. She stood apart from the map with her arms crossed, the way she always did, as if standing close to hope might cost her something. "A century ago."

"A century ago I knew a great many things," Quill said, "and most of what I knew, I knew wrong." He lowered himself to sit cross-legged by the hide with the groan of an old man whose knees had opinions. His eyes stayed on the drawn stone. "Tell me, girl. The scar. Is the poison still active, or has it burned itself out?"

"It's still *there*," Pip said. "It doesn't burn anymore, but it doesn't wash out either. Like soot in a well. The water's been bad ever since." She hesitated. Then, because these people were about to bet their lives on her knowledge, she gave them the rest of it, the part she'd never told anyone, the part that made her ashamed every time she thought of it. "Sometimes the marked dream about it. All of us, the same night, the same dream. We're standing in a corridor of black stone and there's a crack in the wall, and behind the crack there's a smell. Sweet. Like honey left too long. My mam dreamed it the week before they took her name. I've dreamed it every year since, on the same night. The night the collectors first came to Hollowmere."

"Anniversary dreams," Serah murmured. "The wards remember dates."

"The wards remember *wounds*," Quill said softly. "And someone taught them to."

* * *

They talked until the candle burned down to a puddle. Pip mostly listened, which was her habit, and answered questions, which was her burden, and slowly the shape of a thing assembled itself on the hide between them, drawn and redrawn in charcoal and ochre and finally in Serah Vail's precise, angry hand.

It went like this:

The heart-ward — the anchor-stone, the first stone laid when the mountain was young and the court beneath it still kept honest bargains — ran through the mountain's root like a spine. Every soul held in the vaults above was tethered to it. Every debt the crown had sold was written against it. The gentry drew their long lives up through it like water through a taproot. And at the broken place, the poisoned scar, the whole system leaked: souls' light bleeding away into the rock, debts blurring at their edges, the mountain's own strength seeping out drop by drop for a hundred years.

"If the ward were whole," Serah said, tracing the spine of stones, "the court couldn't hold what it holds. The contracts are written against a flawed anchor. A flawed anchor makes flawed claims. Reforge the break, and every tether attached to it snaps back to its owner."

"All of them?" Ivo asked. His voice was careful, the voice of a man not letting himself want the answer too much. "Every held soul? At once?"

"Every one the contracts touch," Serah said. "Hundreds of names. Maybe thousands. A century of the crown's borrowing, undone in an hour." She looked at the map the way a starving woman looks through a window at bread. "That's why the order never let anyone study the heart-ward. That's why the records call it maintenance-only. Because a whole ward ends everything the court owns."

"And the gentry know it," Ivo said.

"The gentry have known it for a hundred years," Pip said. "Why do you think the scar's never been repaired? There are ward-smiths down there — I've seen their forges. They *could* fix it. They won't. Fixing the stone kills their immortality, so they've spent a century calling the wound sacred instead." She spat, quietly, in the direction of the mountain. "My gran used to say: when the powerful call a hurt holy, check whose hands are clean."

Quill laughed then — a dry, cracked sound, like a door opening on an empty house. "Your gran sounds wiser than my whole order."

"Your order hanged Serah," Pip said. "So that's not a high bar."

Serah's mouth twitched. It was the closest thing to humor Pip had seen on her face since the gallows, and Pip counted it like a coin saved.

* * *

But here was the problem, and Pip made herself say it plainly, because she had learned that plainness was the only gift she owned that these people needed:

The scar lay deep. Deeper than the courts, deeper than the vaults, down where the service tunnels ended and the mountain's own dark began. To reach it, they would have to pass the second gate — the gate that took truths — and then travel the long root-corridors where the gentry's hunters walked, and then stand before a wounded god-stone and mend it while the whole mountain screamed.

And mending it required more than craft. Serah explained it without decoration, the way she explained everything, which Pip was starting to understand was its own kind of kindness.

"A reforging needs an anchor," Serah said. "Not tools. Not skill — I have the skill, or the ruin of it. An anchor. The new ward has to be set into something living, something willing, or it's just another cage. The original anchor was a soul freely given, back when the mountain was young and the giving meant something. The court replaced free giving with theft, and theft is why everything rots." She paused. "Someone will have to give themselves. Willingly. Fully. Not a toll, not a trade — a gift."

The silence after that was the worst silence Pip had ever sat in, and she had sat in the flour bin while her mother's name was read off a scroll upstairs.

Ivo looked at the map. Quill looked at his own hands. Serah looked at neither of them, and Pip, watching her, saw the truth arrive in the woman's shoulders before she said a word: *she had already decided it would be her.* Had probably decided it back at the gallows, when the rope failed and she'd seemed almost disappointed.

"No," Ivo said.

"I haven't asked yet."

"You're asking with your face."

"My face," Serah said, "is none of your business."

Pip rolled up her map with fingers that weren't quite steady and said, before the argument could catch fire and burn down the plan along with the people: "There's a third thing you need to know. Before you fight over who dies."

They turned to her. She hated being looked at. She did it anyway.

"The marked will come with you." She said it flat, the way you state weather. "All of us who can walk. Two hundred and eleven, as of last count. We know the service tunnels better than the collectors do — we've been carrying messages through them for years, little ghosts, too small to bother killing. You need numbers at the gates. You need diversions. You need people who can hold a doorway while the important ones run for the heart." She met Ivo's eyes, then Serah's, then old Quill's. "In exchange: when the ward is reforged, the collections end. Not just Wren. Not just the ones the crown sold recently. *All of it.* The whole rotten ledger, burned. You swear it. Sworn on whatever you people swear on — ash, blood, stars, I don't care. But sworn out loud, where everyone can hear."

Ivo was quiet a moment. Then he did something Pip hadn't expected. He held out his hand — palm up, open, the hand of a man offering rather than taking.

"On ash," he said. "On the mountain's ash, and on my name, which after tonight isn't worth much but is at least mine. If we reach the heart, the ledger burns. Every name. Including mine, if the mountain finds a reason to write me in again."

Pip took his hand. His grip was warm and careful, the grip of someone accustomed to handling things that broke.

"One more thing," she said, because the map was rolled and the candle was dead and dawn was coming gray through the shutter-cracks, and some things were easier said in the dark. "The scar. The sweet smell behind the crack. Whoever poisoned that stone knew exactly what they were doing. It wasn't a stranger. It was someone the wards trusted." She looked at Brother Quill, whose face had gone the color of tallow. "Wasn't it."

The old priest closed his eyes.

"A hundred years ago," he said, "I carried a flask of my best work down a corridor of black stone. And the mountain let me pass, because I smelled of incense and good intentions." He opened his eyes, and they were terrible and clear. "I can take you to the scar, children. I know the way better than any map. Because I'm the reason there's a wound to heal."

Outside, far above, the sun crested the shoulder of Thornfell — and for the first time in four years, Pip felt the mountain flinch.
