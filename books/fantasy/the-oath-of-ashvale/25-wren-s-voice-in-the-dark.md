## 25. Wren's Voice in the Dark

Sleep, when it finally took him, took him like a thief — one moment Ivo was lying in the hollow beneath the split granite with Serah's ward-chalk drying on his knuckles and Quill's snore rasping somewhere to his left, and the next he was standing on a road he knew better than his own hands.

The smuggler's road above Ashvale. The high path that threaded between two shoulders of Thornfell where the rock grew lichen the color of old pewter and the wind came down from the peaks smelling of snow and iron. He had walked it eleven times in another life, carrying contraband wine and stolen letters and once — only once — a casket of the kind kings seal with wax and lies.

Except the sky was wrong.

There were no stars. He had expected that; the stars over Thornfell had been the first thing to die, and even in dreams the mountain kept its griefs accurate. But there was no moon either, no dark-blue wash of night at all — just a black so total and soft that it seemed less like absence of light than presence of something else, something velvet and attentive, pressing close against the road as if the whole heavens had leaned in to listen.

And the lichen was glowing.

Not brightly. Not like lantern-light or witchfire or any of the hundred small cheats of brightness he'd cataloged across ten years of court service. It glowed the way a memory glows — faintly, unevenly, in patches — so that the road ahead of him appeared and disappeared with each step, as though the mountain could only bear to show him what he had already lost.

"Wren?" His voice came out small. In dreams you know when you're dreaming, and knowing made the name taste like ash before he even finished saying it.

The road answered by lengthening. That was the way of this place, he understood without being told — nothing here moved the way things moved above. The path unspooled beneath his boots not because he walked but because it wished to be walked, and the pewter lichen brightened and dimmed in slow waves, like breathing, like the mountain inhaling through a hundred miles of stone.

He walked. There was nowhere else to go, and besides — some part of him, the part that had spent a decade swallowing poison for other men's safety, recognized this feeling. The sensation of being exactly where something vast wanted him. You could fight it. He never had.

The road descended into the mountain's flank through a crevasse he did not remember, and the walls of it were veined with more of the glowing lichen, and then — gradually, then all at once — he understood that it was not lichen at all.

It was writing.

Thousands upon thousands of lines of it, spiraling up the crevasse walls in a script older than any alphabet the crown used, older perhaps than the crown itself, and every line was written in the same faint silver-grey luminescence, and every line was a name.

He knew this because one of them was hers.

*Wren.* Just that. No family name — she'd never had one, having been born in the saddle between two smuggling runs, her mother's blood and her father's road and nothing written down anywhere until the day Ivo Ravensworth wrote her name in a ledger himself, sold it upward with steady fingers and a sick stomach. The name glowed on the stone wall at the height of his heart, and around it the spiral continued, name after name after name, tens of thousands of them, the whole history of the crown's secret debts rendered in light.

"I know," he said aloud, to the wall, to the mountain, to whoever was listening. "I know what they did. I'm going to fix it."

The names did not answer. Names never do. But deeper in the crevasse, past the last of the writing, something *did* answer — a sound like a struck bell heard through water, low and round and achingly familiar, and Ivo's body moved toward it before his mind gave permission, the way his tongue had always moved toward bitterness first, tasting danger before sweetness ever got its turn.

* * *

The chamber at the bottom of the crevasse was round, and it was full of birds.

That was his first thought, anyway — that someone had filled an underground room with songbirds trapped in cages of glass, hundreds of them, thousands, each cage no bigger than a fist, hanging from the ceiling and the walls and rising out of the floor in tiers, and each bird was burning. Not with fire. With *color*. Every cage held a small storm of light that shifted and wheeled like something alive, because it was something alive, and the chamber rang with their motion — that bell-through-water sound multiplied into a chorus, a congregation, a city of souls singing wordlessly to themselves in the dark.

The soul-vault. He had never seen it, but he knew it the way you know a place from a story told too often — Pip had drawn it in chalk on the floor of the marked village, Serah had described its architecture from the order's oldest forbidden texts, and Quill had gone quiet in the particular way he went quiet about his own sins. Here it was. Here they all were. The harvested. The collected. A century of the crown's quiet sales, caged and burning and waiting to be spent.

He found her cage the way water finds a drain.

He didn't search. He didn't scan the tiers or count the lights. Some homing sense older than thought simply turned his feet and carried him across the chamber floor to a niche in the eastern wall where a single cage hung apart from the others — set slightly higher, slightly darker, its light dimmer than the rest, as if whatever burned inside it had been burning a long time against its will and was tired.

"Wren."

His voice cracked on the second syllable. Ten years of training held a taster's voice steady through anything — through the duke's poisoning, through the steward's smile, through the toll-gate that had taken the taste of her laugh out of his mouth forever — but it could not hold it now, here, in front of a fist-sized cage containing everything he had betrayed and crossed a kingdom to steal back.

The light inside the cage stilled.

Then it turned toward him.

He had expected — he didn't know what he had expected. Anger, maybe. The cold fury of the betrayed, which she had every right to carry, which he had rehearsed receiving a hundred times on the long roads since the casket opened. Or worse, indifference: the light dimming further, turning away, the soul inside deciding he wasn't worth the effort of hatred. Both would have been justice. He had built his whole plan on the assumption that love, even freed, might refuse him.

Instead the light reached for him.

There was no hand in it. No shape. But the glow gathered itself against the near side of the glass like a face pressed to a window, and warmth came off it — actual warmth, he felt it on his cheeks and the backs of his hands, the first warmth the mountain had shown him — and along with the warmth came her voice, and it was not coming through his ears.

*Ivo.*

Her voice sounded the way it had sounded at twenty, on the high passes, laughing at him for flinching at a thunderclap. But underneath the youth of it was something else, something the mountain had added or the captivity had deepened — a stillness, a weight, the sound of a person who has had a very long time to think.

"You're real," he whispered. "You're really in there. I found you."

*You found me,* she agreed, and there was no blame in it. That was somehow worse than blame. Blame he could have carried; he'd been carrying guilt for years and had grown strong under the load. This was gentleness, and gentleness undid him. *I felt you coming. For days. Like weather.*

"Three days," he said. "The new moon. Then we come through the vault, and Serah holds the gate, and Quill does what Quill does, and Pip's people open the tunnels, and we take you out. All of you. I've sworn it."

*I know your oaths.* Something moved in the light, almost amusement, almost sorrow. *You swear them beautifully. You always did. Do you remember the oath you swore me on the pass?*

He remembered. *Whatever happens, I'll find you. Whatever they take, I'll bring it back.* He had meant it then. He had broken it within the year, and they both knew it, and she was asking him anyway, gently, to say it again.

"I remember."

*Say the rest of it.*

"Whatever they take, I'll bring it back." The words came out ragged. "I'm bringing *you* back."

The light pressed closer to the glass. And now the quality of her attention changed — sharpened — and Ivo felt, with the same instinct that had detected henbane in a duke's stew a lifetime ago, that something essential was approaching. A question carried on her voice like a toxin carried in wine: invisible until it touched you, harmless-seeming, fatal to swallow carelessly.

*Ivo. What will you become to save me?*

The chamber went silent. Even the thousand caged lights seemed to pause in their wheeling.

"What?"

*The steward offered you a seat at court.* Her voice was soft, factual, terrible. *Word travels down here faster than up there. A chair at the gentry's table. Immortality served in courses. You refused — good, my love, that was good — but refusal is only the first time they ask. They asked me too, in the beginning. Did you know that?*

His throat closed. "No."

*They offered me my freedom for a name. One name, delivered to the mountain, and I could walk out of Thornfell with my bones and my breath and my road. I was young and I was hungry and I said yes. I gave them a name, Ivo.* The light flickered, guttered, steadied. *It was a courier who owed me money. She's in a cage three tiers below mine. I have had four years to look at what I became for a debt smaller than yours.*

"That's not—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "You were starving. They cornered you. It's not the same as—"

*As what? As a man with a warm bed and a royal post giving up the woman he loved to keep his salary?* No cruelty in it. Only precision. Wren had always been precise; it was what made her such a good smuggler and such a devastating conversationalist. *I'm not counting your sins, love. I'm asking you the only question that matters anymore, because in three days you're going to walk into a hall where everything you are will be weighed, and the mountain is very old, and it doesn't weigh intentions. It weighs what you become.*

"I become free," he said. "I become the man who gets you out."

*And after?* The light turned, slowly, and he saw that it was not one color but many, layered — gold under green under a deep blue he had no name for, her colors, the colors of her. *After you've opened the cages and burned the contracts and walked out into the sun. What walks out wearing your skin? A man, or a weapon the crown can pick back up? A lover, or a ledger? You've spent ten years being useful to monsters, Ivo. Usefulness is a habit. It's the hardest one to break, because it feels exactly like virtue from the inside.*

He wanted to argue. He opened his mouth to argue — to tell her about Serah's verdict, *guilty but worth saving*, about the oath they'd sworn on ash instead of gold, about Quill dying — no, Quill not dead yet, Quill still snoring under granite, still owing his century-old debt — and about Pip, and the plan, and the heart-ward, and every proof he'd assembled that he was becoming something new.

But the dream knew him. The dream had read his ledgers.

Because the truth was that standing in front of her cage, he could already feel the old architecture of himself reassembling: the taster's calm settling over his fear like a lid, the competence clicking into place, the part of him that met catastrophe by becoming *useful* — and usefulness, she was right, had always felt exactly like virtue. He had saved a duke's life and called the cold gratitude enough. He had traded a woman's freedom for a salary and called it survival. He had accepted a king's errand and called it rescue. Every step of the descent had been dressed as duty, and duty was the finest costume corruption owned.

*I don't need saving,* Wren's voice said, gentle as snowfall. *I need you back.*

"I'm here."

*Part of you. The part that swallows things.* The light drew back from the glass, and he felt the distance like a wound. *The other part — the boy who drank bad wine with me on the pass and laughed with his whole chest — I haven't seen him yet. Not in the halls. Not in the plans. Everyone down here has a plan, Ivo. Serah has a plan. The steward has a plan. The mountain itself has a plan. Plans are how power talks. Love doesn't make plans. Love makes choices, ugly ones, ones that cost you your armor. So I'm asking, while there's still time to answer honestly: when they finally give me back to you — what am I getting?*

He stood in the ringing dark and could not answer.

He tried. He assembled sentences — *the man who loves you, the man who chose you over the crown, the man who gave up his immunity* — but each one dissolved before it reached his mouth, because each one was a claim about the future, and the future is where liars live. The present tense was thinner than he'd hoped. The present tense contained a man who had just realized, standing before the sum of his sin, that he did not yet know who would be standing there when the cages opened.

The light dimmed — not in anger, he understood somehow, but in mercy. Giving him room.

*Think about it,* she said. *You have three days. And Ivo — one more thing.*

"Anything."

*The gate at the end of the ascent. The old one, the fair one, the one that asks for truth. When it asks you — and it will ask you — don't give it the confession you've practiced. Give it the one you haven't. The true shape of that night. Not that you sold me. You know that part by heart; you've punished yourself with it for years and worn it smooth as river stone. Tell it why. Tell it the thing underneath the why.* A pause, and in the pause he heard her breath, impossibly, the living rhythm of lungs that hadn't breathed in four years. *The mountain forgave me, love. Me, with my courier's blood-debt and my cowardice. If it can forgive me, it can hear you. But it can't hear you until you can.*

The light began to fade. Not all at once — in the way the lichen had glowed, in patches, memory-wise — and he lunged forward and put his palm flat against the glass of the cage, and the glass was warm, warm as skin, and for one heartbeat he felt her press back from the other side.

*Come and get me,* Wren said, and now there was laughter in it, buried deep but unmistakable, the laugh whose taste he had paid away at the first gate and was only now learning he'd paid wrongly — he'd surrendered the memory, but the needing of it had stayed, and the needing was louder than the memory had ever been. *And when you come — come as the answer. Not the plan. The answer.*

The chamber folded away like a letter being closed.

* * *

He woke with wet eyes and didn't bother hiding it.

The hollow beneath the split granite was grey with pre-dawn. Quill lay exactly as he'd been, snoring his ruined-lung snore, one hand curled around his pilgrim's staff. Serah sat propped against the far wall, awake — of course she was awake, ward-keepers slept like cats, in shifts, with one ear on the world — and her eyes found his across the dark and asked the question without words.

"Dream," he managed. His voice was gravel. "She talked to me. Through the wards, through the mountain, through — I don't know. She talked."

Serah considered him for a long moment. Chalk dust still clung to her sleeves from the night's work; tomorrow they'd descend to the second gate, and the gate would want a truth, and she'd told him to sleep so he'd have something left to pay it with.

"What did she say?"

He looked at his hands. They were shaking, very slightly, and he let them.

"She asked me what I'm going to be," Ivo said, "when this is over. And I couldn't answer her."

Serah nodded slowly, the way people nod at wounds they recognize. Then she rose, brushed off her knees, and held out the water skin and the last of the hard bread.

"Eat," she said. "We reach the second gate by dusk. And Ivo — gates that ask for truth can smell a rehearsed confession. Whatever you're carrying that you haven't said out loud yet—" her eyes held his, level and unblinking, "—start practicing. Because the mountain won't accept the version you've polished. It wants the raw ore."

Beyond the mouth of the hollow, the eastern sky was paling toward a dawn that would burn one day off their three, and somewhere below them, behind miles of stone, a small cage of light waited for an answer he did not yet have.
