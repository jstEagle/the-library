## 28. The Steward's True Face

The steward did not walk so much as arrive.

One moment the judgment hall's antechamber was empty — a long gallery of black stone lit by lamps that burned without flame, their light the color of old honey — and the next he was simply there, standing at the far end with his hands folded before him, as if he had been waiting for them since the mountain was young and had merely grown impatient in the last century or two.

"Lord Ravensworth," he said. His voice was soft as flour sifted through cloth. "You've been busy."

Ivo's hand went to his belt where the vials hung, six small glass teeth against his hip, each one a different kind of ending. He did not draw one. Drawing would have been an admission that he thought it might work, and after everything the mountain had shown him, Ivo no longer believed in *might*.

Behind him, Pip pressed close to his back. She was fourteen years old and her chalk-sigil mark crawled along her collarbone like a living thing, and she had walked into hell on her own two feet because someone had to, but she was still fourteen, and her fingers found the hem of his coat and held on.

"You know my name," Ivo said.

"I knew your name before you were born." The steward tilted his head. The gesture was almost gentle. Almost human. That was the worst of it — how much of the human was left in him, arranged just so, like furniture in a house where nobody lived. "I knew it when the crown first wrote it into its ledgers. I keep all the ledgers now. It is what I am *for*."

*It is what I am for.* The phrase landed in Ivo's chest like a hook set behind the breastbone. He knew that construction. He had used it himself, ten years ago, kneeling on cold flagstones while the king's chamberlain explained the terms of his employment: *You will taste what we tell you to taste, Lord Ravensworth, because that is what you are for.* He had been twenty-two and hungry and clever enough to survive poison and not clever enough to see that surviving was the trap.

"We need to pass," Ivo said. "The vault. Wren Ashvale's soul is in there, and the contracts holding it are void. Your own court ruled it. You heard the verdict same as I did." His voice came out level. He was proud of that, distantly, the way a man drowning is proud of the bubbles.

The steward smiled.

I want to tell you what his smile looked like, but the truth is that it looked like mine. That is the sentence I have written and crossed out forty times in this account, and it is the only honest one. When the steward of the court beneath Thornfell smiled, it was the way I smile — one corner first, the left, a habit from a childhood I barely remember, a woman's name attached to it that I never speak aloud. And I stood in that honeyed light looking at my own smile on another man's face, and something in me went very quiet and very cold, the way a cellar goes at midnight.

"The verdict," the steward repeated, tasting the word. He had a palate; I recognized it immediately, the way one dog knows another. "Yes. The court ruled. The court is very old and very fond of its rules, and rules are such beautiful things right up until someone must enforce them." He began to walk toward them, unhurried, his footsteps making no sound on the black stone. "Enforcement requires hands, Lord Ravensworth. Willing ones. The gentry have refused theirs. And so the task falls to me, as most tasks do."

"Stay back," Pip said, and her voice cracked in the middle of it, and I loved her for the crack, because it meant she hadn't yet learned to hide it.

The steward stopped. He looked at her — really looked, the way a taster looks at an unfamiliar dish, cataloguing, weighing, assigning it a place in some inner ledger — and then his gaze moved past her to me, and settled there, and stayed.

"Do you know," he said conversationally, "what happens to a man who cannot be poisoned?"

* * *

I have tasted four hundred and eleven poisons in my life. I kept the ledger myself, in a little book bound in grey calfskin that I hid inside the false bottom of my wine rack at court, because a king's taster who admits to keeping records is a taster who expects to be replaced. Henbane, number sixty-three. Aconite, eighty-eight. The white arsenic the duke's cousin favored, one hundred and twelve, subtle as a rumor. Each entry had a flavor note, the way a sommelier writes of vintages. *Henbane: bitter green, a ghost of smoke at the finish.* I told myself it was scholarship. I told myself it was armor.

What it was, I understand now, was inventory.

"Immunity," the steward said, "is not the absence of poison. It is the accumulation of it. Every dose survived is a debt the body pays forward. Your crown understood this — that part of it, they got right, though they stole the understanding fair and square." He was closer now. Twenty feet. Fifteen. The lamplight did something wrong to his face, made it seem to flicker between ages, young and ancient and neither. "They found a man who could drink anything and live. Do you know why he could?"

"Because he'd swallowed poison by increments," Ivo said. His mouth was dry. "Like me. Like every taster they ever trained on the method they took from him."

"Because," the steward said softly, "he agreed to it. Every dose. Every increment. He signed, and he swallowed, and he signed again. Consent is the oldest magic there is, older than wards, older than courts. And a man who consents to be a vessel may be *filled*." He spread his hands, palms up, an offering, a confession. "They filled him. Slowly. A century of doses, a century of signatures, each one a little more of himself poured out to make room. They called it service. They called it honor. They paid him beautifully, and he spent his gold on a cottage in the lowlands and a woman whose face I can no longer—" The steward stopped. For one heartbeat the mask slipped, and underneath it there was nothing at all, a smooth grey absence like the inside of an empty jar, and then the mask returned, seamless, smiling. "Forgive me. I find that memory is one of the things I no longer keep. Only ledger entries. *First Taster. Asset acquired. Yield: exceptional.*"

Pip's grip on my coat had gone white-knuckled. I put my free hand over hers. Her knuckles were ice.

"That was a hundred years ago," I said.

"Was it?" The steward considered. "Time moves strangely here. There was a war once. Or a wedding. There was certainly wine." He resumed walking, and now I could see the details I had refused to see: the way his cuffs were frayed at the edges, fine cloth worn to threads; the way his eyes were the exact amber of the lamp-honey and reflected nothing; the faint, sweetish smell that reached me on the still air, and which my traitor tongue identified before my mind could stop it — henbane, aged, mellowed, turned from poison into perfume. He smelled like my ledger. Entry sixty-three. He smelled like the thing that had nearly killed a duke and saved my reputation, and he wore it like cologne.

"They harvested his immunity," Ivo said. The words tasted of copper. "Copied it. Bottled it. Fed it to the first tasters of the new line, generation after generation, and each generation—"

"—poured out a little more to make room," the steward finished. "You are the ninth. Did you know that? Ninth of your line to hold the post under the current arrangement. The eight before you all came down the mountain eventually, one way or another. Some as debtors. One as dinner." He said *dinner* the way a priest says *amen*. "But none of them lasted as long as you. None of them built up such reserves. Ten years, Lord Ravensworth. Four hundred and eleven entries. Do you have any idea how rich a soul that makes yours? How deep the vessel? How much room there is in you now for something *else* to fill?"

* * *

Here is the thing about mirror-horror that no one warns you: it does not feel like fear. Fear is fast; fear is the flash of aconite, the racing heart, the animal bolt for the door. What I felt in the honeyed light of that gallery was slow. It seeped. It was arsenic — entry one hundred and twelve, subtle as a rumor — working its patient way through the water of my blood, and what it carried was recognition.

Because I had seen the steward's posture before. In polished silver spoons at the royal table. In the dark glass of the palace windows at night, when I stood alone after a tasting and watched my reflection watch me back. I had seen the careful folding of the hands. The tilt of the head. The smile that began on the left. I had seen a man make himself into a perfect instrument and call the making a career, and I had thought, in my proudest moments, that it was mastery. That I was the knife and the crown merely held the handle.

The steward was what the knife looked like after a hundred years of being held.

And the crown had never needed to threaten me, not once, not in ten years. It had only needed to feed me. Gratitude, wages, the warm weight of the grey ledger-book in my hidden shelf, the small daily drama of *saved the duke, earned cold thanks*. Every meal a dose. Every dose a signature. Every signature a little more of Ivo Ravensworth decanted out of Ivo Ravensworth to make room for the thing the crown actually wanted, which was not a servant.

A vessel.

"You're staring," the steward observed, pleased.

"I'm appraising," I said, and heard Wren in my own voice, her smuggler's gallows humor rising in me like groundwater. "Old habit. Everything I look at, I price."

"And what am I worth?"

"Nothing," I said. "You're spent coin. You're the empty bottle they kept because throwing it away would mean admitting what they drank." It was cruel, and I meant it to be, because cruelty was the only weapon I had that could reach him, and because — gods forgive me, and Serah would say justice doesn't require forgiveness, only accounting — some part of me needed to hurt the thing I was afraid of becoming more than I needed to save myself from it.

The steward's smile did not falter. That was the horror of it, the true horror, worse than the hollow eyes and the henbane perfume and the memory that had slipped off him like a wet cloak: my cruelty did not even land. There was nothing left to land on. I had thrown a stone into a well and heard no splash, and I understood, with the slow certainty of arsenic doing its work, that no stone ever would.

"Yes," he said gently. "That's exactly what they'll say about you, someday. If you let them." And then, before I could answer, before I could reach for a vial or a lie or Pip's cold hand, he stepped aside with a servant's fluid grace and gestured down the corridor beyond, toward the vault doors, toward Wren. "Go, then. The contracts are void; I won't dispute the court. Take your smuggler's soul and run for the sunrise. Refuse me everything, as you refused the trade, as you refused the chair."

Suspicion is a taster's native tongue; I read it fluently. "And the cost?"

"Oh, there's always a cost." The amber eyes glittered. "But it isn't mine to collect. I only keep the ledger, Lord Ravensworth. I don't write it." He paused at the threshold, half-turned, and for a single unguarded instant the mask thinned again and I saw — not nothing, this time, but something worse: a flicker, a residue, a ghost of the man who had once wanted a cottage in the lowlands and a woman's face he could no longer hold. He looked at me the way a drowning man looks at a rope thrown from a ship already too far away.

"When you stand where I stand," the first taster said quietly, "and you will, sooner than you think, unless you burn the whole ledger down — remember that I also said no. Once. At the beginning. To the very first offer." The mask sealed over him like frost closing on a window. "Remember that saying no was not enough."

He bowed — precisely, correctly, the bow of a servant dismissed — and was gone between one lamp's flicker and the next, leaving only the smell of aged henbane and the echo of his footsteps that had never made any sound at all.

* * *

Pip didn't speak until we reached the vault doors. Then she tugged my sleeve, twice, the signal we'd agreed on — *danger, talk to me* — and when I looked down at her, her chin was trembling and her eyes were furious with unshed tears.

"He talked about you like you were already his," she whispered.

"He talked about me like I was inventory," I corrected, because precision mattered; because if I let the terror blur into metaphor I would start believing I had time. "There's a difference. Inventory can still be stolen back."

But my hands were shaking as I laid them on the vault's seal, and I could not make them stop, and I knew — the way you know the flavor of a thing before you can name it — that the shaking wasn't fear of what waited inside the cage of lights.

It was the knowledge that somewhere behind us, in the honeyed dark, the mountain had just shown me the last page of my own ledger, and the handwriting had been my own, and the final entry read: *Asset acquired.*

Beyond the seal, faint and far and impossibly dear, I heard Wren's voice calling my name — and beneath it, deeper in the dark, something vast turning over in its sleep, beginning to wake, because the gentry had lost the verdict and would not, could not, accept the cost.

"Run," I told Pip, and shouldered the door.
