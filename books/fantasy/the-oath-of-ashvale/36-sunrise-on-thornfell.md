## 36. Sunrise on Thornfell

Wren woke to the smell of pine and cold stone, and for a long moment she did not know where she was.

That alone should have frightened her. Wren Halloway had spent her whole life knowing exactly where she was — every switchback of the Thornfell passes mapped behind her eyes, every smuggler's cache and burned-out waystation, every place a body could hide and every place a body could be found. She had slept in tree hollows and hay lofts and once, memorably, in the bell tower of a shrine whose priest owed her money. She did not wake up lost.

But she woke up lost now, lying on a slope of grey ash and new grass at the mountain's foot, staring up into a sky that was doing something impossible.

The stars were coming back.

She watched them as though watching rain fall upward — one point of light, then another, then a scatter of them like sparks off a whetstone, filling in the dark overhead the way water fills a dry riverbed. She knew, without knowing how she knew, that they had been gone. That the sky over Thornfell had been empty for weeks, a blind black ceiling, and that something had ended in the night and let the stars come home.

She sat up too fast and the world tilted.

Her hands were clean. That was wrong too — Wren's hands were never clean; they were cracked with rope-burn and stained with saddle soap and wine-lees and the particular grime of someone who lived by moving what other people didn't want found. These hands looked washed. Scrubbed. The calluses were still there, the old scar across her left palm where a customs blade had caught her at the Ashvale ford, but everything else had been scoured away, like a ledger wiped down to the binding.

*Ledger,* she thought, and the word rang strangely in her, like a bell struck in an empty room.

"Wren."

She turned toward the voice, and there he was.

Ivo Ravensworth sat against a boulder ten feet away, wrapped in a cloak she recognized as his own — dark wool, frayed at the hem, smelling faintly of bitterleaf and candle smoke. He was thinner than she remembered. That was the first thing. The second thing was his face: he was smiling at her, and it was a real smile, warm and crooked and glad, but it sat on his face like light through dirty glass. Something about him had been dimmed. Not broken — dimmed. Like a lamp turned low to make its oil last.

"Ivo." His name came out rough, half-swallowed. "Why am I— what happened? Last thing I remember is—" She reached for it and came up with nothing. A blank. Not a fog, not a blur — a clean absence, smooth-edged, like a page torn out rather than burned. "I don't remember."

"What do you remember?" He didn't move from the boulder. His voice was careful the way a man's voice is careful when he's walking on ice over deep water.

"The last run," she said slowly, testing each piece before trusting it. "Autumn. I had a shipment of Vellish red going over the pass — the high road, under the old ward-stones. There was frost on the stones, which was strange because the wards were supposed to keep the frost off—" She stopped. Her own words sounded distant to her, a story told by someone else. "And then you. You were at the cellar door in Corvane. You used to open that door for me. And then—" Nothing. Smooth white nothing. "Then I woke up here."

Ivo nodded slowly. He did not fill the gap. She watched him decide not to fill it, saw the truth move behind his eyes and get gently set aside, and something cold slid down Wren's spine, because she had spent ten years reading faces across smuggling tables and she knew a concealed thing when she saw one.

"How long?" she asked.

"Does it matter?"

"It matters."

He looked up at the returning stars instead of at her. "Sixty-one days since I last opened that cellar door."

*Sixty-one days.* Sixty-one days of her life, gone, torn out clean. She felt for the fear of it and found something stranger underneath: not horror, but a hollow ache, the phantom pain of a limb she couldn't name having lost. Somewhere in that missing stretch of time there had been something she ought to grieve, and her heart kept reaching for it and closing on air.

"Ivo." She got up, unsteady, grass and ash sticking to her palms, and crossed to him. Up close he was worse — the dimness wasn't just in his face. It was in his hands, which lay too still in his lap; in his eyes, which were the same grey-brown they'd always been but somehow less lit, like coals banked for a long night. "What happened to you?"

"What happened to *you* is the better question," he said, "and the answer is: you're free. Whole. Every part of you accounted for." He said it with such fierce precision that she understood it was the most important sentence in the world to him. "You're free, Wren. That's all I need you to know right now."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed mildly. "It isn't."

There were others on the slope. She noticed them now as her eyes adjusted — figures scattered among the rocks and young pines, some sleeping, some sitting up and staring at the sky with the same stunned reverence she felt. A girl of perhaps twelve with chalk dust ghosting her sleeves, talking softly to an old woman who wept without sound. A broad-shouldered man in the ragged vestments of a lay brother, dozing upright with his hands folded. A woman in travel-stained ward-keeper's leathers, standing apart, watching the treeline with a soldier's patience — she caught Wren looking and gave her a nod, brief and assessing, the look of someone deciding whether a stranger was a burden or an asset.

"Who are they?" Wren asked.

"People the mountain took," Ivo said. "Or almost took. They're coming home."

*The mountain took.* The phrase settled into her missing days like a key finding a lock it almost fit. She looked up at Thornfell rising black and enormous above them, its summit lost in cloud, and felt — distinctly, undeniably — that the mountain was looking back. Not hostile. Just aware, the way an old dog is aware, the way a grave is aware.

"There's something I'm supposed to be afraid of," she said quietly. "Isn't there. Something in those sixty-one days. I can feel the shape of it and I can't see it."

Ivo was silent for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was gentle and absolutely steady. "You'll remember or you won't. If you don't, count yourself lucky. If you do—" He stopped. Smiled again, that banked-lamp smile. "If you do, I'll be here."

"You're always here," she said. "That's the one thing I've never doubted. Even when I hated you for it."

Something flickered across his face — pain, quickly mastered. "Even then," he said.

* *

They walked down out of the foothills as the sky went from star-scattered black to the deep bruised blue that comes before dawn. The girl with the chalk-dusted sleeves — Pip, the others called her, and the name snagged oddly in Wren's memory, a word from a dream she hadn't had — walked beside Wren for a while, chattering with the tireless brightness of a child who has been very frightened for a very long time and has just decided to stop.

"The stars came back at moonset," Pip said. "I counted them coming back. Forty-one before I got tired of counting. Serah says the ward will hold now, the new one, because it's anchored on something that can't be bought." She said this with the simple certainty of scripture. Then she looked up at Wren, and her expression changed — went suddenly older, sadder, the look of a child carrying a secret too heavy for her pockets. "He gave it away, you know."

"Gave what away?"

"Whatever it was that made him safe." Pip shrugged, uncomfortable. "Nobody explains things to me. But I heard Quill talk about it, before—" She stopped herself. Before *what*, Wren didn't ask; the girl's face closed like a door. "Before the end. Your soul was caged, and the only way to spring the cage was to put something else in the lock, and he put himself. That's all I know. That's probably more than I was supposed to know."

Wren walked the next hundred yards without hearing the wind or the pines or anything at all.

*Caged.* The word dropped through the torn place in her memory and echoed in a darkness below it, and for one vertiginous instant she almost — *almost* — touched it. A sense of smallness. Of being held. Of light pressing in around her like water around a lantern. Then it was gone, and she was standing on a mountain path at dawn with a child watching her worriedly.

"Are you all right?" Pip asked.

"No," Wren said honestly. "But I'm walking, which is most of it."

Ahead of them, Ivo had stopped at the edge of a clearing where the trees broke open onto the eastern valley. He stood very still, facing the horizon, and even from thirty feet away Wren could read the set of his shoulders — the stillness of a man bracing himself.

She came to stand beside him.

The east was catching fire. Not sunrise yet, but the herald of it — a thin line of gold along the world's rim, spreading, the clouds above it going rose and ember. Below, the valley of the Ashvale unfolded in shadow and silver, the river a bright thread, the little towns not yet smoking their morning fires. It was, objectively, one of the finest views in the Thornfells. Wren had seen it a hundred times from a dozen ridgelines, usually while checking sightlines for pursuit.

She glanced at Ivo to say something wry about it, and stopped.

He was squinting. Not the squint of a man dazzled — the squint of a man *straining*. His head was tilted slightly, his brow furrowed, and he was looking at the growing gold the way one looks at a page written too faintly, coaxing meaning out of it. And when the sun finally broke the horizon — a single blinding coin, sudden as a struck match — he flinched. Actually flinched, a small involuntary jerk, and turned his head, and passed a hand over his eyes.

"Ivo."

"It's beautiful," he said, still half-turned. "Look at it. Look as long as you want. Don't mind me."

A cold certainty settled over Wren then, quiet and total, the way frost settles on a window — pattern by pattern until the whole pane is claimed. She thought of the dimmed face. The still hands. The careful voice walking on ice. The child's words: *he gave whatever it was that made him safe.* She thought of a man who had spent ten years swallowing poison for a king, proof against every venom ever brewed, and she understood — not the details, never the details, those were locked in her missing days — but the shape of the cost.

"What did you give away?" she asked.

"Nothing that matters."

"Ivo Ravensworth, I have lied to border guards, tax collectors, two husbands, and a dragon-cultist, and I have never once believed your lies, so don't insult us both." Her voice shook. She let it. "Look at the sun. Look at it properly."

He was quiet long enough that she thought he would refuse. Then, slowly, he turned his head and raised his eyes to the risen morning, and Wren watched him try — watched him genuinely, valiantly try — and saw him fail. His eyes watered. He blinked against the light like a man emerging from a cellar, and the gold of the sunrise reached his face and gave him back nothing, no lit edge, no warmth, and she understood that for him the great burning coin of the sun was a rumor reported by others. A fact he knew. Not a thing he saw.

"Oh," she said. Very softly. The syllable fell out of her like a stone down a well.

"It's fine," he said immediately, automatically, the reflex of a man who has spent a decade reassuring dukes. "It's fine, Wren. It's early days. Quill thought—" He caught the name and something moved through him, quick and grieving, and he set it aside with visible effort. "Serah thinks the damage may fade. The ward took root in me, and roots can shift as they grow. We'll know more in time."

"Time," Wren repeated.

"Time," he agreed, "which we finally have."

She wanted to hit him. She wanted to hold him. She did neither; she stood there with her fists at her sides and the sunrise pouring over both of them, warming her shoulders and, apparently, giving him nothing at all, and she understood that this — this exact moment, the two of them framed against a dawn one of them could not properly see — was what the rest of her life was going to feel like. Joy with a hole cut out of it. Light with a man-shaped shadow standing in it.

"You did this for me," she said. It wasn't a question. She didn't have the memory of it, but she had the shape of it now, the negative space of it pressed into her like a seal into wax. "Those sixty-one days. Whatever happened. You paid something, and I'm the one holding the change."

"You're holding *everything*," he said. "That was the entire point."

"And you decided that without asking me."

"Yes," he said simply. "Because if I'd asked, you'd have said no, and you'd have been right to, and I'd have done it anyway. Some things you have to do behind a person's back or not at all." A ghost of the old crooked smile. "You taught me that, actually. The cellar door."

Despite everything — despite the dread and the grief and the fury climbing her throat like floodwater — Wren laughed. One short, cracked note. "You are the worst man I have ever loved."

"I've been called worse by better."

"Shut up." She grabbed the front of his cloak and kissed him, hard, there in the clearing with the marked and the freed scattered behind them and the sun climbing over Thornfell and the stars' pale ghosts fading out of the brightening sky. He kissed her back — carefully, tenderly, with a kind of astonished gratitude, as if she were a letter he'd mailed years ago and never expected delivered. When she pulled back, his eyes were wet.

"I can't see the sunrise," he told her, "but I'll tell you what I can do. I can hear it. Birds first, always, before the light even clears the ridge — then the river picking up the wind, then the towns waking, one fire at a time. It's a good sound. I've been listening to it my whole life and I never once paid attention, because I could always just *look*." His voice went rough. "Turns out listening was the part worth keeping."

Wren looked at him — this dimmed, diminished, stubborn, magnificent man — and made a decision with the same part of her that planned runs over frozen passes and opened cellar doors for contraband wine. The part that did not ask permission.

"All right," she said. "New arrangement. You can't see sunrises anymore. Fine. From now on, I see them *for* you. Every one. And I describe them, and you correct my descriptions, because you're insufferable and you'll have notes. That's the deal."

"That's a terrible deal."

"It's the only one on offer. Take it or I carry you down this mountain myself."

He studied her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face made him stop pretending to be fine. The banked lamp in him flared — briefly, guttering, but it flared. "Deal," he said.

* *

They came down out of the foothills near midmorning, and that was when Wren learned that the world above had not been idle while the mountain collected its debts.

Serah Vail met them at the treeline — the ward-keeper woman from the slope, though Wren now knew her name and roughly her story, pieced together from fragments over the morning's walk. She was riding hard, leading a second horse, and her face wore the specific grimness of a soldier bringing bad news she has already triple-checked.

"Ravensworth." She swung down. "Word from Corvane. Fast rider, crown livery, going town to town." She paused, and Wren watched her choose her words with the care of a fencer. "The palace is saying the border wards failed through natural decay. They're calling it an act of God and announcing a day of thanksgiving. And they're asking — publicly, formally — after a royal taster named Ivo Ravensworth, missing these two months. They want him found." Her eyes went to Wren, measured her, returned to Ivo. "*Returned.*"

"They want their taster back," Ivo said.

"They want their taster back," Serah agreed. "And more than that." She drew a folded paper from inside her leathers — cheap paper, printed, a proclamation. "Every courier post between here and the capital. There's also a warrant out for a smuggler named Wren Halloway, wanted for questioning in connection with the disappearance of certain items from the royal cellars. Dated sixty-three days ago."

Sixty-three days. Two days before the cellar door. Wren took the paper and read her own name in cold official type and felt, beneath the strangeness, a slow anger beginning to build — the first emotion she'd felt that seemed to belong to her missing days, reaching backward like a hand through torn pages.

"They were hunting me before the wards fell," she said. "Ivo. What did I *carry*?"

"You carried wine," he said, and there was something in his voice — guilt, ancient and worn smooth, like a river stone. "The wine mattered. You didn't know why. I promise you didn't know why."

"And the king wants us both found."

"The king wants *me* found," Ivo said quietly. "You're the excuse. A taster doesn't leave his post; if I'm dragged back, the story holds. But Wren—" he turned to her fully, and the morning light that he could not see nonetheless seemed to reach him through her, through the sheer force of his looking — "the evidence exists. Everything. Ledgers, contracts, seals, testimony — lodged somewhere the crown can't burn it, in a court older than the crown. Serah's order will find it. The right people will find it. And when it surfaces, the king will need someone to blame, and he has already chosen the two of us."

Silence, except the wind in the pines and, far off, the sound of the river taking the morning to the sea.

"So," Wren said at last, folding the warrant with a smuggler's neat economy and tucking it away — evidence, habit, instinct. "We run."

"We run," Serah said. "For now. Until the reckoning comes."

Ivo looked east, squinting at the light he couldn't see, and smiled — that crooked, banked-lamp smile that Wren was already learning to read by touch instead of sight.

"Then let's take roads they don't know," he said. "I happen to know several."

Behind them, high on Thornfell, the new ward-stones caught the sun and threw it back down the mountain in a thin bright line — a thread of gold running from the peak to the valley floor, pointing, like a finger, toward the capital.
