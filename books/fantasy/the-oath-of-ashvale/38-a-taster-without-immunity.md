## 38. A Taster Without Immunity

The poison announced itself the way all good poisons do: politely.

Ivo had spent ten years cataloguing the manners of toxins — henbane's bluster, foxglove's sweet insistence, the quiet aristocratic patience of certain mineral salts that took weeks to finish a sentence they'd begun with a single syllable of numbness. The curse he'd taken from Wren was older than any of them. It did not bluster. It did not insist. It simply moved in, the way a tenant moves into a house whose owner has gone away, and began rearranging things.

First his sense of smell thinned, then went entirely, one morning in the third week after the mountain. He stood over the breakfast kettle and smelled nothing at all, and understood that the world had begun closing its doors to him one by one, in reverse order of how he'd earned them. His tongue would go last. He was almost grateful for the sequence. A taster should lose his palate last; it gave him time to say goodbye to it properly.

He told no one. That, too, was discipline. Ten years of swallowing death in small measured doses had taught him that fear spreads faster than poison, and Wren had carried enough weight for three lifetimes already. She thought the dimness behind his eyes was grief settling, or exhaustion, or the ordinary cost of what they'd done beneath Thornfell. Let her think it. There would be a season, soon enough, when she could not help but know, and until then he intended to spend every hour she didn't know as if it were gold he was smuggling past a customs house.

So he rose early each morning while she still slept, and he wrote.

* * *

The account began, as all true accounts must, with a lie — the official one. *In the year the stars went out over Thornfell,* he wrote, and then crossed it out, because that was how the ballads would begin, and the ballads were already wrong. They had Wren dying heroically. They had Serah Vail striking down the steward with a sword Serah had never carried. They had Ivo Ravensworth himself as a tragic nobleman, which was the funniest error of all, since he had been neither noble nor, in any way that mattered, a gentleman.

He started again on fresh paper, in the small tight hand he'd once used for flavor ledgers:

*I am Ivo Ravensworth, and for ten years I was the king's taster. This is not a confession, though you may treat it as one. This is an inventory. Everything I tasted, everything I saw swallowed, everything I signed my name against and pretended not to understand — it is written here, and where my memory failed me I have attached the documents, and where the documents burned I have written down who burned them.*

It was strange work, writing a book out of a ledger. For a decade his pages had held only columns: date, dish, compound, dose, outcome. Flavor had been data. Henbane in the duke's stew, detected by its green ghost-note under the rosemary; a bitter almond whisper in the queen's cordial that turned out to be nothing, and cost him a sleepless week anyway. He had kept those books like a miser keeps coins, believing they were merely the record of a trade. Now he saw them for what they had always been — testimony waiting for a court, evidence accumulating in the dark hands of a man everyone assumed was furniture.

He wrote about the cellar door that only tasters knew. He wrote about Wren's contraband wine and the shape of her laughter in the dark between the casks. His pen hesitated there, not from shame but from a tenderness so sharp it felt like exposure. Then he wrote it anyway, because the book was meant to be true, and truth does not get to skip the parts that hurt to look at.

He wrote about giving her name to the crown. He wrote it plainly, in ink that seemed darker than the rest, the letters standing up off the page like little accusatory figures. *I did it to keep my post,* he wrote. *I told myself a post was a life, and a life was worth a name. I have had ten years to discover what a lie costs when it compounds interest, and I am writing this so that no one — no clerk, no taster, no frightened man at any table — can ever again tell himself that selling a soul is a transaction rather than a theft.*

Some days the writing went easily, whole chapters pouring out of him like the confession at the second gate. Other days the curse pressed on his skull like a thumb on wet paper, and he managed three lines before his hand shook too badly to trust. On those days he wrote the most important parts anyway. He noticed the pattern early: the days the poison sat heaviest on him were the days the truth came cleanest. Perhaps pain was a solvent. Perhaps honesty, like everything else, required heat.

* * *

In the afternoons, he taught roads.

They had taken a cottage at the foot of Thornfell — Serah had arranged it through channels she refused to explain, channels that apparently still owed a disgraced ward-keeper favors — and the mountains rose behind it like a patient audience. Wren had been a courier of the Thornfell passes herself once; she knew the main arteries, the smuggler roads any serious runner could recite. But Ivo had spent ten years memorizing the veins beneath the veins, the paths the crown's maps never showed because the crown preferred its own couriers lost.

"The Shepherd's Cut," he said, sketching in the ash of the hearth with a stick, because paper was expensive and ash wiped clean. "Everyone thinks it's a dead end above the treeline. It isn't. There's a chimney — natural, not built — that drops you into the valley behind the tollhouse. You'll need rope and you'll need nerve, and you'll need to go at dawn, because the rock sweats loose stones after noon."

"Show me the chimney," Wren said. Not *tell me*. Show me.

So he showed her. That was their rhythm, that autumn: mornings of ink, afternoons of stone. He walked her up the lower passes even as his legs grew less trustworthy, leaning on her arm and calling it an old man's habit, and she let him call it that because she was kind, and because she was watching him with those grey eyes that missed nothing and said nothing yet. At each road's crux he made her lead the last hundred yards alone, then walk back and do it again blindfolded, then describe it to him aloud until her words matched his memory exactly.

"Why?" she asked once, sitting beside him on a shelf of granite with the whole eastern range laid out below like a sleeping beast. "You're giving me a decade of your secrets. Most men hoard less than this."

"Roads die with their finders," he said. "That's how the crown liked it. Every taster, every courier, carries knowledge the kingdom depends on and no one else possesses, and that's not security, Wren — it's hostage-taking. The moment I died, half these passes would've become rumors." He watched a hawk turn circles on the thermals, envying it its simplicity. "Knowledge that lives in one skull is a debt the mountain collects eventually. Knowledge shared is a road that stays open."

She was quiet a long moment. "You're talking about more than roads."

"I'm always talking about more than roads," he admitted. "Occupational hazard. When your whole trade is noticing what's in the stew, you start noticing what's in everything."

He almost told her then. The word *poison* sat right behind his teeth, familiar as an old coin under the tongue. But her face was open in the wind, unguarded in a way he'd seen maybe five times in all their years, and he found he could not be the one to close it. Not yet. He had bought himself seasons with silence before; he spent another now, and hated the purchase, and made it anyway.

* * *

Serah came at month's end, riding down from the reforged ward-stones with frost in her hair and news in her saddlebags.

"They're calling it the Reckoning in the capital," she said, warming her hands at his fire without asking, in the way of people who had earned informality at great price. "Your book — the copy you lodged with the mountain's court — surfaced. Quill's ledgers surfaced with it. Half the chancery is under arrest, and the other half is testifying to save their necks. The king has taken to his bed, which is either illness or strategy; with that man it was always difficult to distinguish."

"And the order?"

Serah smiled the thin smile of a woman who had burned a bridge and enjoyed the warmth. "Dissolved. Or reformed — the words matter less than the fact that Vail no longer belongs to it. I keep the lowland wards now. Private commission. Farmers pay in cheese and honest gratitude, which I'm learning to digest."

Ivo laughed, and the laugh cost him a coughing fit that he turned toward the hearth and disguised as amusement run riot. Serah watched him with ward-keeper's eyes — eyes trained to read the health of boundaries — and said nothing, but he saw her file something away, and knew she would come back to it.

"You should rest," she said instead. "Even heroes are permitted."

"I'm not a hero. I'm an inventory with legs."

"Then rest the inventory." She rose, paused at the door. "Ivo. Whatever you're carrying — the mountain taught me that burdens shared weigh differently. Not less. Differently."

After she left he sat with that a long while, watching the fire. *Not less. Differently.* He wrote it down in the margin of his manuscript, because it sounded like something the book needed, and because writing things down was the only way he knew to keep himself from carrying them alone.

* * *

Winter came early that year, or perhaps it only came at the speed of his blood, which had begun moving through him like a messenger who suspected the war was lost. His hands grew cold at the wrists first. Then the taste of things began to simplify — bread becoming mostly texture, wine becoming mostly temperature — and he started keeping a new ledger, the inverse of his old one: not flavors detected, but flavors savored. Deliberately, ritually, like a man counting coins he knows he will soon stop being able to count.

Wren cooked, those nights. She had discovered that feeding him steadied something in her that talk could not reach, and he let her believe cooking was a neutral act. Each dish she set before him, he tasted with the full ceremony of his old office — the pause, the attention, the slow second spoonful — and each time she watched him with an anxiety she thought she was hiding, and each time he pronounced judgment: *good, better, best*, with annotations. The barley soup with too much thyme. The river fish caught on the Shepherd's Cut approach, tasting faintly of iron and altitude. The honey-cakes she made badly on purpose to make him laugh.

"You eat like a king," she teased him once. "All ritual and gravity."

"No," he said. "Kings eat like frightened men. Every bite a negotiation, every flavor a possible enemy. I used to taste that way." He turned his cup slowly on the table. "Now I just taste."

She reached across and put her hand over his, and her fingers were warm, and he committed the warmth to the ledger.

* * *

On the night the first snow sealed the high passes, he finished the book.

He read it through one final time by lamplight, checking names against dates, dates against documents, the way he'd once checked a suspicious dish twice before declaring it clean. It was not elegant prose. He was a taster, not a poet; the sentences marched in columns even when they tried not to. But it was complete, and it was true, and it named every name that deserved naming — including his own, three times in accusation and once, grudgingly, in defense, because Serah had insisted a fair verdict required both.

At the end he added a passage he had been circling for weeks:

*If you are reading this, then the crown fell or the crown confessed, and either way the world learned that souls were currency here for a century, and that the men who ran the exchange drank from guarded cups and slept soundly. Do not hate them overmuch. I was one of them, and I can report from inside that corruption never asks you to be wicked. It offers you a chair. It asks you to sit. And the sitting feels, every single day, exactly like prudence.*

*What saved me was not courage. It was arithmetic. Eventually the debts outweighed the post, and I chose the debts. Choose them sooner than I did. That is the entire moral of this inventory, and I apologize that it took a mountain, a curse, and a woman's stolen soul to render it legible.*

He closed the book and blew out the lamp, and in the dark he allowed himself, finally, the accounting he had postponed since the morning he'd smelled nothing from the kettle.

The curse was in his marrow now. He could feel it the way he'd once felt henbane approaching — not as pain but as presence, a slow cold tenant rearranging the furniture of him. Months, perhaps. A year if the winter was kind, and winters here were not kind. His sight would thin next; the healers' texts he'd consulted in disguise had been clear on the progression, and his body was following the text faithfully, like a dutiful student.

He was not afraid. That surprised him most of all. Ten years of swallowing death had apparently cured him of fearing it — or perhaps loving someone had done the cure, and the decade of poison had merely been the long preparation, teaching him that dread was a worse toxin than anything it warned about.

But there were things yet undone, and he was a man who finished his ledgers.

In the morning he would give Wren the book — no. No, he corrected himself, lying awake in the snow-hushed dark, listening to her breathe beside him. In the morning he would take her up the Shepherd's Cut itself, all the way, the road he'd only ever sketched in ash. She would need to know it with her feet, not her ears, and his legs would carry him that far; he was nearly sure they would. And on the ridge, with the valley spread below them and the reforged stars still burning overhead in patterns the astronomers were calling impossible, he would hand her the manuscript and watch her face as she understood what it was, and what it cost, and why he had waited.

And then, he supposed, she would be angry. She had a right to it. Anger was a flavor he knew well enough to recognize on anyone's palate, and hers would be magnificent.

He smiled into the darkness and reached for her hand in sleep, and outside the window the snow began to fall on Thornfell, soft as ash, gentle as a closing book — and somewhere far below, in the deep and quiet places where the new ward anchored the mountain's heart, something that had once been a taster's immunity kept the lights burning, and the stars, faithful at last, kept their watch.
