## 39. The Last Dish

Wren had stolen a king's ransom in wine and never once been caught, but she could not steal an egg without her hands shaking.

She stood in the kitchen of the croft above Ashvale — the one Serah had claimed from a village half-emptied by the mountain's old hunger, its other houses shuttered or burned, its well still sweet — and she watched the pan as if it might betray her. Fat rendered slow from a strip of salt pork Ivo had carried down from the last market town on his own two legs, refusing her offer to go for him, refusing everything that smelled of being carried. The fat spat. The egg went in and frilled gold at its edges.

Behind her, on the bench by the window, Ivo sat with a ledger across his knees.

Not the crown's ledger. His own — the little oilskin-bound book he had kept for ten years in the lining of his sleeve, the taster's catalogue, every flavor entered in a hand so small and neat it looked like something grown rather than written. *Bitter almond, faint, beneath the honeyed partridge — warn no one, note everything.* He had spent the morning writing in it, but not entries now. Pages of instruction. Roads. She had watched him do it for three days: the Thornfell passes unspooling from his memory onto paper, each one annotated in that tiny hand — *scree field after the second cairn, keep left where the goat track forks; the ford at Marlow is safe only before midsummer; if you smell juniper smoke near the high shelf, turn back, that was always a marker for the under-court's patrols and it may outlive them.*

He was leaving her a map of himself. That was what the pages were. He thought she didn't know.

The egg needed turning. Wren turned it, and let herself cry for exactly the length of time it took, which was four seconds, because she had been a courier for eleven years and couriers learned to ration their grief like hardtack.

*He tastes things,* she thought, watching the white go opaque. *Ten years he tasted things for a man who never once said thank you.* Henbane in a duke's stew. Bitter almond under honey. The slow sweet of lead in an old man's cups. He had swallowed the poisons meant for kings in increments too small to kill him — that was the whole trick of his immunity, the gift they'd ground him down to make, poison after measured poison until his soul itself turned poison-proof, until even the mountain's curse couldn't get a grip on him.

Until he gave it away. Until he stood in the burning heart of the under-court and offered up the only thing he owned, the thing they had built out of his suffering year by year, and said *take mine instead of hers.*

And now there was something in him. Something the mountain's collapse had shaken loose, some residue of the steward's malice or the old ward's hunger settling into a body no longer armored against it. She had seen him hide it twice — the gray mornings, the way he held the doorframe going down the stairs as though the floor were tilting, the single moment when he'd thought she wasn't looking and pressed the back of his wrist against his mouth as if keeping something down.

He hid it because he loved her. She hid knowing because she loved him back. Between those two silences, they were building a house made entirely of doors neither would open.

So she cooked.

It was the only language left to them that told the truth.

* * *

The menu had taken her two days of quiet theft from his own ledger.

She had read it while he slept — not all of it, she wasn't cruel enough to read the parts about her, the entry from the cellar years ago that began *Wren laughs like — * and then crossed out so thoroughly the page had nearly torn — but she'd read the food. Ten years of dishes tasted at the king's left hand, catalogued with a precision that broke her heart the longer she looked at it, because every entry was beautiful and none of it was his. He had described a hundred perfect meals and eaten none of them. Tasters got the risk and the spit-spoon; the flavor went to the crown.

*Suckling pig glazed in quince and black pepper — the crackle gives like thin ice, the fat underneath is almost floral.*

*Lampreys in their blood, the court's favorite — coppery, thick, a taste like swallowing winter.*

*Pears baked in wine with saffron — the saffron is a lie over the pear's honesty, but it is a kind lie, and the king smiles, so note it.*

Wren had stood in Serah's kitchen with the ledger open and realized she was looking at the loneliest book ever written. A man who knew the taste of everything and had never once been fed.

Well. She was a smuggler. Smuggling was simply moving goods past people who thought they had the right to stop you, and if the goods were pears and saffron and a river trout poached in wine instead of lampreys' blood, then she'd run this cargo if it killed her.

It might, a little. The market towns below the mountain were waking up strange since the stars came back — gentry ashes on the wind, wards humming in the stones again, Serah's old order crawling over the passes looking for someone to blame — and every road had its dangers. But Wren had walked worse roads with less reason.

* * *

"You're doing it wrong," Ivo said from the bench.

She jumped. "You're supposed to be resting."

"I'm dying, love, not deaf." He said it lightly. He said everything lightly now; it was his newest poison-proofing, a glaze of humor over whatever the mornings were taking out of him. "You're crowding the trout. Fish wants room in the pan or it steams."

"It's not fish yet, it's—"

"Trout. I can smell the river on it. Marlow water, upper stretch, below the willows. You went through the ford." He smiled without opening his eyes, face tipped to the window where the afternoon came in long and amber. "Left bank. You kept your boots dry."

"You're insufferable."

"I'm a taster. It's the whole job." He opened his eyes then, and looked at her over the ledger, and for a moment the lightness fell away and she saw what lived underneath: a man inventorying her. Her hands, her color, the set of her shoulders. Checking her for symptoms. As if she were the dish and he couldn't help tasting for danger even now, even with his tongue ruined for the work, even with nothing left in him worth poisoning. "You shouldn't have gone alone."

"And you shouldn't have given away the only thing standing between you and—" She stopped. Rationed it. Four seconds. She turned the trout. "There. Room in the pan. Happy?"

"Ecstatic," he said, and picked his pen back up, and wrote another road into the map of himself, and neither of them said anything else for a long time except the sound of fat singing and nib scratching and the mountain breathing outside the window, easy now, alive now, its new ward anchored in a taster's given soul and holding the way good stone holds — quietly, forever, without asking anyone's thanks.

* * *

They ate at dusk, at the table by the window, because he could still see color best at dusk and she wanted him to have every advantage the light could give.

She'd laid it out like contraband, which is to say carefully and with an escape route planned: the trout in its wine sauce with the herbs she'd found growing wild along the ash-grey slopes — thyme gone feral and stronger for the hardship, marjoram, a wild garlic she'd have traded her knife for in the smuggling years; bread she'd baked badly and refused to apologize for; the pears, baked soft in the last of the Marlow wine, saffron threaded through them in honest apology to no one; and at the center, on the chipped blue plate she'd carried out of Thornfell in a courier's pack through three checkpoints and one avalanche, a small dish of the stew.

Mutton and root vegetables, browned onions, barley, a bay leaf. Peasant food. The kind of food no king had ever eaten, and therefore the only kind in the world that had never once appeared in his ledger.

"What is it?" he asked, looking at the spread the way men look at altars.

"Dinner," she said. "Sit down."

He sat.

She watched him take the first bite of the trout and she forgot how to breathe.

Because she had expected — she didn't know what she'd expected. Gratitude. Performance. The polite pleasure of a man being kind to a woman who was trying too hard. What she got instead was Ivo Ravensworth going utterly, completely silent, spoon halfway to his mouth, eyes closed, and his whole face changing — not softening, *opening*, like a door she'd only ever seen locked swinging wide onto rooms she hadn't known the house contained.

"The wine," he said finally, hoarse. "Marlow red. It's — the tannin's softened. It was a rough vintage, should be rougher than this. You cooked it down with the trout's bones?"

"Two hours."

"With the rosemary stem in it."

"You could smell that too?"

"I can *taste* it." His eyes opened. They were bright, and not entirely with health. "Wren. This is — I've tasted the king's pheasant. I've tasted lampreys in their blood off silver. I once identified nine ingredients in a single sauce while a man watched to see if I'd die eating it. And this—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "This trout is better than any of it."

"It's a river fish and a cheap wine."

"That's why." He laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle, and he pressed his knuckles briefly to his mouth the way he did when something wanted to come up, and she pretended not to see, and he pretended she hadn't, and between those two mercies the meal went on.

He ate the way he catalogued: completely. She watched him build the taste of the stew in his mouth and name its parts under his breath — *mutton, older animal, good; the barley's toasted first, who taught you that; there's a marrow bone in here, don't lie to me* — and she told him about the butcher in the valley hamlet who'd traded her the shank for a true story about the fall of the gentry, and he listened to her the way he tasted, wholly, missing nothing, and somewhere in the middle of it she understood that this was what had been missing from the ledger all along. Not flavor. Company. A meal was only data when you ate it alone at a king's elbow, waiting to die. Eaten across a table from someone who'd walked through hell beside you, the same mutton became something the crown had never once served him.

Grace, Quill would have called it. The old poisoner-priest had died in the mountain's deeps completing his century-old correction, and he'd left behind a saying that Serah now repeated like scripture: *the antidote was never clever. It was just given freely.*

The pears, last. He took one bite of the saffron-and-wine sweetness and put down his spoon and was quiet for so long she started to reach for him.

"Don't," he said, wet-eyed, laughing at himself. "Give me a moment. I'm cataloguing."

"What does it say?"

He looked at the pear. Looked at her.

"It says," he managed, "*the saffron is a lie over the pear's honesty, but it is a kind lie.*" His voice failed and recovered. "I wrote that eight years ago about the king's dessert. I was wrong. There's no lie in it at all. It's just — sweet. Someone made it sweet *for me*. Do you know that's never—" He shook his head. "Ten years. Ten years of the finest table in the kingdom, and no one ever once cooked for me."

"I did it wrong somewhere," she said, because her voice was going and jokes were cheaper. "The bread's a disaster."

"The bread's perfect."

"The bread is a weapon."

"The bread," Ivo said, tearing off a piece and eating it plain, closing his eyes, "is the best thing I have ever tasted, and tomorrow I will write it in the ledger, and the entry will be one word."

"What word?"

"*Enough.*"

* * *

Later, when the plates were cleared and the candle burned low and he'd gone drowsy in the chair with the ledger open on his knees, she took it gently from his hands to mark his place — and saw the last entry, fresh ink, written that morning while she'd been at the market.

Not a road. Not a flavor.

*If you are reading this, Wren, then you stole my book, which means you also ignored my instructions, which means you are exactly the woman I betrayed and exactly the woman I love, and I have no defense. So: the truth, in ink, where you'll find it eventually anyway. What the steward put in me isn't fast. I have seasons, maybe a year, maybe less if the winters are hard. I'm telling you this nowhere but here because I cannot bear to watch your face while you hear it, and because you deserve to hear it in my own hand, in my own words, without my cowardice interrupting. I spent ten years learning every flavor in the world and I never learned the name of this one. It tastes like ashvale. It tastes like morning. It tastes like enough.*

*I am not afraid. I only wish there were more pages.*

She stood in the candlelight with the book open and did not ration this one. She wept until the candle guttered, silently, standing, the way you weep on a road when stopping isn't an option.

Then she wiped her face, marked the page, closed the ledger, and set it on the shelf where he'd find it moved — a message of her own, in the only ink she had: *I know. I've known. We will spend the pages we have, and they will be enough.*

Outside, over the black shoulder of Thornfell, the first stars were coming out — every one of them returned, burning steady over the reforged ward, and low on the eastern horizon the sky had already begun to gather the pale grey that meant dawn was only hours off.

Wren looked at the sleeping man in the chair, and the grey edge of the sky, and made her decision about tomorrow.

There was a sunrise coming. Whatever his eyes could still take of it, he was going to be there to meet it — and she would carry him up the ash-grey slope herself, if it came to that.

It was going to come to that.
