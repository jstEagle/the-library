## 40. Oath of Ashvale

### I.

The last stars were going out one by one, the way candles do when no one snuffs them — politely, in their own time — and Wren decided she would not wait for dawn to come to him. She would carry it.

Ivo had grown lighter over the winter. That was the cruelty of it: the poison that was eating him from some hidden place behind his eyes did not waste his flesh the way fever or famine would. It wasted everything else. His hands stayed strong; his shoulders kept their shape under her arms. But something inside him was being spent, coin by coin, and every week he weighed a little less against her heart, as if the mountain were still collecting on him by installments.

So she carried him. Not because he asked — he never asked, he only said *the ridge, before first light, I want to try* — but because the path up Ashvale's grey shoulder was slick with old snowmelt, and because she had spent ten years carrying contraband through worse country than this, and because love, she had learned, was mostly logistics with a flame at the center of it.

"You're breathing too hard," Ivo said into her ear. "You've been breathing too hard since the second switchback."

"I'm savoring the climb."

"Liar."

"Smuggler." She adjusted her grip beneath his knees. "We're allowed our lies. It's in the code. Page one."

"There is no page one. You wrote the code on the back of a wine manifest."

"And you memorized it," she said, "because you're a romantic pretending to be an accountant."

He laughed — a real laugh, short and startled out of him, and she felt it land in her own chest like a bird settling. Ten years ago she would have cataloged that laugh the way he cataloged flavors. Now she just let it happen, and climbed.

The slopes of Thornfell were ash-grey still, and would be for years, Serah said. The mountain had burned its old skin away in the reforging; the gentry's palace had gone up like dry paper, and what the fire left was a fine pale dust that lay on everything like a blessing nobody had ordered. Grass was coming back anyway. Wren saw it in the seams between stones, small green insolence pushing up through the grey. Pip swore the wildflowers would be back by midsummer, blue ones, the kind that grew where wards had been. The girl knew things like that now. The whole village of the soul-marked knew things like that. You spend a year half-collected by the court below and you come out of it knowing where the world keeps its seams.

Wren herself had come out of it knowing almost nothing.

That was the other mercy and the other wound. Her memory of the cage was gone — taken, spent, or simply burned away with the rest of the mountain's hoard when Ivo's soul went into the new ward-stone. She remembered the wine cellar. She remembered the betrayal, the cold morning she learned her name had been sold. She remembered the chase, the gates, the tolls, the feast where a gentry lord wore her own laughter across his face like stolen jewelry. And then there was a gap, smooth as river stone, and on the far side of the gap she woke at the mountain's foot in the sunrise with Ivo kneeling over her, changed, dimmed, smiling like a man who has paid a debt so long carried he'd forgotten what it was to stand unburdened.

She did not remember being saved.

She only knew that she had been, and at what price, and that he hid the worst of it behind jokes about his eyesight, and that she loved him with a ferocity that frightened her some mornings, the way weather frightens people who have lived through storms.

"The ridge," Ivo said quietly. "I can hear the light coming. Isn't that absurd? Ten years of tasting and now my ears do the work of my tongue."

"Nothing about you is absurd, Ravensworth."

"Everything about me is absurd. I'm a king's taster with no king worth tasting for, walking a dead smuggler road on a mountain that owes me nothing—" He stopped. Corrected himself. "—that paid me exactly what we agreed. I take it back. The mountain settles its debts better than the crown ever did."

Below them, spread along the valley floor, the refugee villages were waking. Smoke rose straight in the windless hour — cook-fires, forge-smoke, the chimney of the little house Serah had claimed and refused to call a home because, she said, institutions fell but stubbornness remained. Somewhere down there Pip was already awake; the girl was always already awake, chalk stub in her pocket, drawing ward-spirals on doorframes for luck even though the wards held now without them. Habit, she said. Or prayer. Same thing, probably, if you did either honestly enough.

And above them — Wren tipped her head back and her breath caught, the way it caught every clear night now, the way it would catch until she died — the stars were burning.

All of them. Every star over Thornfell, returned the night the reforging completed, crowding the sky like a congregation that had been locked out of its own church for a hundred years and had finally, finally gotten the door open.

"They're louder tonight," Ivo said.

"Stars don't have sound."

"These do. They sound like—" He searched. He was always searching now, reaching for words the way he used to reach for flavors, precise and patient. "Like a ledger being balanced. A very large one. Very slowly."

Wren crested the ridge and set him down gently on the flat stone they had chosen weeks ago, the one facing east, and sat beside him and pulled his cloak around them both, and waited with him for the sun.

* * *

### II.

Serah Vail had resigned in writing, which the Wardens' Order found insulting, and then in person, which they found terrifying.

She stood in the order's chapter hall in Lowmark with the resignation letter nailed — actually nailed, Quill would have appreciated the theatricality — to the lectern, and informed the assembled keepers that the order's charter had been voided by a court older than any of their grandmothers, that the wards they had guarded were reforged on the soul of a taster rather than the obedience of a keeper, and that she was therefore, by her own reading of the law she had once sworn to uphold, free.

"You cannot simply walk away," the Grand Keeper said. He was a small man made smaller by the hall. "Your oath—"

"My oath was to the wards," Serah said. "Not to you. The wards are held. By a braver man than anyone in this room. Which means my oath transferred to him, and he released me, because unlike this order he does not collect debts he has no right to."

She walked out through doors that had opened for her since she was nine years old and felt the morning air hit her face like cold water, and she did not look back, and she did not feel triumphant. That surprised her. She had imagined triumph for years, in the dark of her cell before the gallows, in the long road after Ivo cut her down. What she felt instead was lighter and stranger: the sensation of a woman setting down a rope she'd been holding so long her palms had grown around it.

Justice, she was learning, was not an institution. Institutions were just scaffolding people built around justice while they argued about who got to hold the ladder. When the scaffolding came down — and it always came down, she had watched the crown's come down in a single season, its soul-contracts read aloud in market squares by clerks with shaking voices — what remained was people. Pip organizing the marked into a militia of caretakers. Quill dead and absolved and buried under the blue-flowered slope he'd poisoned a century ago. Ivo dying by inches in a cottage full of maps, teaching every road he knew to anyone who would learn them, writing a book in a hand that trembled more each month.

And Wren, carrying him up a mountain before dawn, because he had asked, and because asking was the bravest thing Ivo Ravensworth ever did.

Serah climbed the ash-grey slopes that morning because she had been invited, and because she suspected — correctly — that oaths were going to be spoken, and because she had discovered that she liked witnessing things. Judging she could no longer bear. Witnessing was different. Witnessing was saying *I see this, and it counts.*

She crested the ridge as the eastern sky began to change.

They were sitting close together on the flat stone, the taster and the smuggler, his head against her shoulder, her cloak around them both, two figures outlined against a horizon going from iron to ember. Serah hung back among the rocks, out of sight, out of courtesy, and watched the light come.

It came the way it always came over Thornfell now — slow, unhurried, gilding the ash-grey slopes until they looked less like ruins and more like the foundations of something. The grass was winning its argument with the dust, blade by blade. Down-valley the smoke columns stood up straight as incense. And on the ridge, Wren turned Ivo's face toward the sunrise with both hands, gently, the way you turn a page you're afraid of tearing, and said something too quiet for Serah to hear.

Ivo was quiet a long moment.

Then he said, clearly enough to carry, "It's there. Just barely. Like remembering a taste instead of tasting it." A pause. "But it's there, Wren. It's there."

Serah Vail, who had hanged men and cut friends down from ropes and argued with a mountain's court and won, sat down on a cold rock and cried, silently, like a professional, until the sun cleared the ridge.

* * *

### III.

"I want to say vows," Ivo said.

They had eaten — Wren had packed bread and honey and the bitter mountain tea he could still taste fully, thank all the older gods, because his tongue had survived what his eyes had not — and the sun was warm on the flat stone, and Pip's blue flowers were indeed coming, little buds clenched like fists along the ridge trail. Serah had emerged from the rocks looking guilty and damp-eyed, and Wren had pretended not to notice, and Serah had pretended to be made of stone, and everyone had agreed silently to continue this arrangement indefinitely.

"Vows," Wren repeated.

"Not crown-vows. Not duty-vows. I've said those. I said them to a king who was selling souls by the ledger-page, and I meant them, and they nearly hollowed me into a steward." His hand found hers. His hands were still steady; it was only the world behind his eyes that dimmed. "I mean choice-vows. The kind you make when you could walk away and don't."

"You could walk away," Wren said. "Right now. There's a road south. I've checked it twice."

"I know. I mapped it myself. It's a good road." He smiled. "I'm choosing not to take it. That's the whole vow, really. Everything else is decoration."

"Say it anyway," she said, and her voice cracked on the middle word, and she let it crack, because she had carried him up a mountain and she was allowed.

So he said it anyway.

"Wren of Thornfell. I choose you. Not because fate arranged us, fate did nothing for us but make everything harder. Not because I owe you — though I do, ten years' interest, compounding. Because on the best day I've had in my life, which was any day you laughed at my expense, I chose you, and on the worst day, which was every day I betrayed you, I still chose you underneath the fear like a coal under ash. I vow to tell you the truth even when it costs, especially when it costs, because I learned at the second gate that truth is the only toll worth paying cleanly. I vow to teach you every road I know, and then to help you find the roads I don't. And I vow that whatever the poison takes from me, it will not take my choice. That stays mine. That stays yours."

The wind moved the grass. Far below, a bell rang in one of the villages — Pip's doing, no doubt, marking some ordinary hour in an extraordinary year.

Wren took a breath that shook going in and came out steady, the way a courier's breath learns to.

"Ivo Ravensworth. I choose you. Not the taster, not the traitor, not the hero of the reforging — the man who memorized my laugh so carefully he could pay it at a gate, and then grieved for it like a limb. I choose the accountant-romantic. I choose the liar with the honest mouth. I vow to carry you whenever the road needs it and sit beside you when it doesn't, and to know the difference. I vow to learn your roads so well that when you're gone, no one who walks them will ever be lost." Her thumb moved across his knuckles. "And I vow that the sunrise will be seen. Every morning. If not by your eyes, then by mine, out loud, described to you, in detail, whether you want it or not."

"That sounds tedious."

"Page one of the code," she said. "Love is mostly logistics."

"With a flame at the center," he finished, and pulled her in, and kissed her on the flat stone on the ash-grey shoulder of the mountain that had tried to devour them both and failed, while the sun came up gold over Thornfell and the stars' faint daytime ghosts faded into a clean blue sky.

Behind the rocks, Serah Vail stood witness, and spoke aloud, because a witness speaks: "Seen and counted. Let it hold."

Old law. Older than the order, older than the crown. The kind of law the mountain itself ran on.

Somewhere beneath their feet, deep in the stone, a soul woven into a ward-stone warmed — or Wren imagined it did — like a coal under ash, still burning, still choosing.

* * *

### IV.

They came down the mountain slowly, the three of them, because there was nowhere they needed to be faster than the morning would take them.

At the foot of the slopes, the day was assembling itself in its usual fashion: Pip directing a work-party of the formerly soul-marked with the authority of a girl three times her size, chalk dust on her sleeves; refugees arguing cheerfully about fence posts; smoke rising straight. Word of the crown's reckoning had spread through every market square in the borderlands — the contracts read aloud, the ministers arrested, the king's own seal entered into evidence by a court that existed beneath the world and had, for one shining season, dispensed justice upward. Some said the king would abdicate. Some said he would burn the archives and pretend. Serah, listening to both rumors over ale in the Lowmark tavern, had delivered her verdict with a shrug: "Institutions fall. People remain. Watch the people."

The people, this morning, were planting barley in ash-enriched soil and singing slightly off-key, and it seemed, for once, that the world might be allowed to be ordinary.

Ivo paused at the cottage door and looked back up at the mountain — squinting, Wren noticed, using the trick he'd developed, turning his head to catch the brightest edge of things — and said, quietly, to her, to Serah, to the morning:

"It holds. Can't you feel it? Underfoot. Steady as a heartbeat."

Wren could. She had felt it every day since the reforging, a faint deep pulse in the stone, patient as a tide. The mountain keeping its word. Keeping *him* — not his body, but the best of him, the immunity he'd surrendered, the soul that had refused every trade offered to it. The first anchor-stone in a century that had chosen its post.

"Come inside," she said. "The account book's waiting. You promised Pip you'd map the western passes before your eyes go."

"I did promise. I keep my promises now. It's a recent development. Very fashionable."

He went in ahead of them, hand trailing the doorframe he knew by heart, and Serah caught Wren's arm at the threshold — gently, ward-keeper's fingers, measuring a pulse.

"How bad?" she asked. No preamble. They had passed the point of preambles months ago.

"Soon," Wren said. "Weeks, maybe. He knows. He's known since midwinter. He's finishing the book." She looked toward the interior, where Ivo was settling into his chair by the window, unrolling a fresh sheet, dipping a pen whose line wobbled and steadied, wobbled and steadied. "He says a story isn't finished when the writer stops. It's finished when someone else carries it."

Serah nodded slowly. "Then it will be carried."

Inside, the pen scratched. Outside, the sun climbed over Ashvale, and the grass kept winning, and somewhere high on the ridge the first blue flower of the year opened its fist to the light — small, stubborn, planted by a girl who believed wards should be made of chalk and kindness, held by a man who had given everything he'd hoarded, and witnessed by a woman who had learned that justice, at the end of all its long arithmetic, comes down to this: showing up, seeing clearly, and saying *it counts.*

Wren stepped through the door into the smell of ink and honey-tea, and closed it softly behind her, and began — as she would every morning for the rest of her life — to describe the sunrise.

He listened with his eyes closed, smiling, and wrote it down.
