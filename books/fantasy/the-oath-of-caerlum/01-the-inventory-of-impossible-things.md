## 1. The Inventory of Impossible Things

The storeroom smelled of tallow and old paper and, faintly, of the sea—which was strange, because the sea was four streets away and the nearest thing to it in the guild's cellar was a jar labeled *Tide, one evening's worth, unsold*.

Petra Hartwell counted by candlelight. She had learned from Master Corvane that you never audited the inventory in daylight, because daylight made people honest, and honest light told impossible things they were ordinary, and ordinary things stopped working. Whatever truth lived in these shelves preferred to be taken seriously after dark.

She set the ledger open on the counting table, dipped her pen, and began at the top shelf, as always.

"Bottled echoes," she read aloud, because Master Corvane had also taught her that an inventory spoken aloud stayed true longer than one merely written. "Case nine, bottles one through fourteen."

Fourteen bottles sat in their straw bed, each stoppered with wax the color of dusk. She lifted the third one—*Echo: a mother calling a child home to supper, Vessany dialect, circa forty years past*—and held it to the flame. Inside, something shifted like a moth. If she unstopped it—and she would not; Corvane had died before teaching her which echoes could safely be opened—the voice would call out once, in a language the city had officially forgotten, and then the bottle would be empty forever.

Some things you sold. Some things you kept because selling them would be a kind of killing.

She wrote: *Case nine, bottles 1–14, all present, all sealed. Note: bottle 7 hums on nights when the harbor fog comes in. Cause unknown. Do not open.*

Her handwriting was small and exact, the way Corvane's had been, though hers slanted left where his slanted right. The ledgers of the Guild of Caerlum went back two hundred years, and every hand in them was different, and every hand was careful, because the ledgers were not records of the guild's trade. They were the trade. A debt owed by a dead man was only a story unless someone wrote it down. An echo in a bottle was only noise. The ledgers were the difference between magic and madness—or so Odile said, when Odile was lucid enough to say anything at all.

Petra worked down the shelf.

*A saint's second shadow*, folded into a cedar box no bigger than a hymnal. It had come in sixty years ago, payment for a debt nobody living remembered, and it was cold to the touch even in high summer. When she opened the lid to verify contents—a shadow, folded like linen, faintly luminous—it lay there being the shape of someone kneeling. Saints, apparently, cast two shadows, and the second one knew things the first one didn't. The box whispered when she closed it. She chose not to wonder what about.

*Debts owed by the dead*: a whole drawer, organized by year of death. Forty-one outstanding accounts. The dead were terrible debtors but excellent collateral, because grief made the living pay almost anything to settle a ghost's account, and the guild never pressed hard. Corvane's rule: *We collect from the grieving only what they offer freely, and we forgive the rest in ink.* Forgiveness in ink—that was the phrase. You wrote *forgiven* beside the entry and the debt actually dissolved, somewhere, in whatever place debts went to sleep.

Petra had forgiven eleven debts this year alone. The guild could not afford generosity, strictly speaking. But the guild could not afford much of anything anymore, and generosity was the last luxury she intended to surrender.

Down the rows she went. *One borrowed luck, medium grade, slightly spent* —a coin that had once belonged to a gambler who never lost, until he did. *A door-knock from a house that burned, preserved in oak*. *The last hour of a dying man*, corked in black glass, its label written in the dying man's own hand: *For whoever needs an hour more than I did. I had this one to spare.*

That one always stopped her. She stood with it in both hands and let the candle gutter and thought: *somebody loved him enough to bring us this instead of throwing it in the river.*

That was the guild, really. Not the bottles, not the shadows, not the luck. The guild was the fact that somebody, somewhere, two hundred years running, had agreed to keep the things other people couldn't carry and couldn't throw away.

*

The stair creaked at midnight exactly, which meant Bramble.

"I heard the pen scratching," the cook said, descending into the lamplight with a plate balanced on one forearm the way other people carried nothing at all. He was a wide man gone soft at the edges, with flour permanently in the creases of his knuckles and an expression of permanent mild grievance that fooled nobody, because he had fed the guild's strays for thirty years and would have fought a bear for any one of them. "Nobody audits on an empty stomach. That's how mistakes happen. That's how the Vellum Street fire happened."

"The Vellum Street fire happened because Odile tried to alphabetize a salamander," Petra said.

"And why was Odile doing inventory hungry? Exactly my point." He set down the plate: bread still warm, cheese, a pear sliced into fanned crescents because Bramble believed even austerity deserved presentation. He peered over her shoulder at the ledger with the proprietary air of a man reading a menu. "How are we dying tonight?"

"We're dying slowly." She tapped the page. "Three sales since midsummer. One acquisition. We're spending faster than we earn, and half our capital is things we can't sell without breaking the oath."

"What's the count?"

"Two hundred and six items. Fourteen clients with standing accounts. Five members." She said the number flatly. Five. Once the guild had filled three floors and a courtyard. Now it was five: Bramble the cook, Tamsin the forger, Wick the boy, Odile the ruin, and herself. Plus Master Corvane in the churchyard, plus everyone else in the ground, plus the ones who'd simply walked out one morning and not come back, because a guild that trades in impossibilities cannot promise its people anything possible, like wages.

Bramble was quiet a moment. Then he took the pear slice she hadn't touched and put it directly into her hand, the way you feed a horse you're worried about. "Eat. Dead keepers audit worse than live ones."

"You can't know that."

"I can guess. I've fed this guild thirty years, girl. I know what hunger does to judgment better than I know anything else." He settled onto the stool across from her with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions, and watched her work for a while in comfortable silence, the way family watches each other without needing to talk.

Family. She turned the word over privately, testing its weight. She'd been eleven when Corvane found her copying shop signs in chalk for coppers, reproducing lettering she'd seen once, perfectly, including the misspellings. He'd looked at her for a long moment and said, "You have the keeper's curse: you can't not record." She hadn't known then that he'd lost a daughter, or that the daughter had been ten, or that the guild took in orphans the way harbors take in wreckage—not out of policy but out of gravity. She knew it now. She knew everything now except how to be the last of something without it hurting every single day.

"Wick's still awake," Bramble offered. "He says the locks are nervous tonight."

"The locks are always nervous when the wind's off the water."

"No. He says they're nervous *tonight*." Bramble scratched his jaw. "Boy talked to the front lock for ten minutes before I got him up to bed. Said it tasted iron. Locks don't taste iron, do they?"

"Locks don't taste anything. Wick tastes things and calls it locks."

"Mm." Bramble didn't look convinced, and Petra noted, in the back room of her mind where she kept things that didn't fit the ledger yet, that neither was she entirely.

*

By the second bell she'd reached the bottom shelf, and the bottom shelf was where the guild kept the things it did not sell and did not display and mostly did not discuss. She verified them anyway, because the inventory was the inventory, and skipped entries had a way of becoming holes, and holes had a way of becoming doors.

Item by item: a jar of *silence, harvested from a confession never spoken aloud*. A key that unlocked nothing anyone had ever found, warm as a held hand. A wedding ring belonging to a marriage that had never quite happened, still faintly trying. And at the very end, wrapped in oilcloth and bound twice with cord, a ledger she had catalogued on her first night as keeper and never once been able to make herself open.

It had no title on the spine. Inside the wrapping, if memory served—and it did, perfectly, the keeper's curse—there was a first page in Master Corvane's slanting right-handed script, and the first word of it was *Vessany*.

She had asked him about it exactly once, at fifteen. He had been mending a pen by the kitchen fire, and he had gone very still, the way the whole room goes still when a clock stops, and he had said, "Not yet, Petra. Perhaps not ever. Some debts are owed in the other direction—we owe, not they."

Owed to whom? She hadn't asked. She'd been fifteen, and the fire had been warm, and asking twice felt like the kind of curiosity that got apprentices sent home.

Corvane was eight months in the churchyard now, and the ledger was still wrapped, and the question had grown teeth.

Petra looked at it a long while. The candle bent its flame toward the shelf, toward the oilcloth, as if leaning in to listen.

"Not tonight," she told it, and wrote in the margin of the inventory, in her small exact hand: *Bottom shelf, final item, present, sealed. Query: for how long?*

Then she heard the bells.

All of them. Not the hour—there was no hour at this bell—and not the alarm pattern either, which was two quick and one long. This was every bell in Caerlum ringing at once, ragged and uncoordinated, the way bells ring when the ringers haven't planned it, when men climb bell towers in their nightshirts because something is happening in the streets below that cannot wait for morning.

Bramble appeared at the foot of the stair already dressed, which meant he'd never undressed, which meant the cook slept armed and shod like a retired soldier, because he was one. "Harbor side," he said. "Shouting."

Above them, through the floorboards, came the sound of small bare feet running to the front door, and Wick's voice, high and certain and frightened, talking to the lock.

"It's not iron," the boy was saying. "It's salt. The lock says the night tastes of salt and something coming out of the water, and it wants to know if we're going to go see."

Petra closed the ledger on her unfinished inventory, left the pen in the gutter of the page, and picked up her coat.

"Yes," she said, to the boy, to the lock, to the night. "We're going to go see."

* * *

They went as four, because waking Odile required more time than the city seemed to have. Down Salt Lane, where the cobbles wore a skin of fog, past shuttered stalls and one fishmonger's window lit by a lamp someone had lit and forgotten in their rush to join the crowd. The shouting resolved, as they drew closer, into words, and the words resolved into one word, repeated, passed mouth to mouth through the press of bodies like a bucket at a fire:

*Up. Up. He's up. Out of the water. He's walking.*

The fishmarket opened onto the harbor steps, and the crowd stood packed there in the fog with their lamps held high, and beyond them, on the wet stones at the waterline, dripping and grey-lipped and unmistakably, impossibly alive, stood a young man Petra recognized from funeral announcements nailed to the market post all summer.

Drowned in July. Buried in August. Standing now, in October, blinking at the torchlight like a man woken too early, while an old fishwife pushed through the crowd toward him, screaming his name in a voice that cracked down the middle.

Petra stood on her toes, heart hammering, keeper's mind recording everything whether she willed it or not—and saw, just before the fishwife reached her son, the woman's lips move around words Petra couldn't hear, words shaped like a name, an old name, a name that wasn't the Harbormaster's god or the Cathedral's god or any god the city admitted to.

The lamps flickered, all together, like a held breath.

And the fog rolled back off the water as if something enormous had exhaled.
