## 2. A Prayer Answered in the Fishmarket

The fishmarket at dawn smelled like money and rot in equal measure, which was why I liked it. Nothing in Caerlum pretended to be what it wasn't. The gulls screamed their honest greed over the stalls, the ice men swore with real feeling, and old Maren Kessel gutted herring with a knife so worn it had gone soft at the edge, like her voice, like everything else that had been worked too long.

I'd come down to the harbor for an audit, of all things. The guild kept accounts with three of the ice merchants—debts of luck, mostly, small trades: a fair wind here, a market day without rain there—and Master Corvane's rules said even the smallest debts got walked twice a year. *A ledger is only as true as its last visit,* he used to say, tapping the page with one ink-stained finger. *Numbers you don't look at become lies you can live in.*

He'd been dead two years, and I still heard him everywhere. It was easier than hearing nothing.

So there I was, standing among the barrels with my ledger satchel under my coat, watching Maren Kessel work, when the screaming started.

You have to understand something about the fishmarket before I tell you what happened next. These people scream all morning. They scream about prices, about weather, about gulls and taxes and each other. A scream in the fishmarket is worth exactly nothing. So when the first one went up—a woman near the water stairs, high and cracked—I did what everyone did, which was nothing. I checked my ledger strap. I thought about the ice merchant on Salt Row who owed us nine days of clear skies and hadn't paid since spring.

Then more people started screaming, and they were running *toward* the water instead of away from it, and nobody runs toward anything in this city unless it's already too late to help.

* * *

The woman at the center of it was Maren Kessel. I want to be precise about that, because precision was drilled into me by people who are now dust and ledgers, and because what happened next has been told wrong in every tavern from here to the capital wall. It wasn't some stranger. It was Maren, who I'd been watching not five minutes earlier, gutting herring with her soft old knife.

She was on her knees at the water stairs, and she was praying.

Not the way people pray in Caerlum now—quick, embarrassed, the way you cough in church. Not to the Ninefold, whose statues line the Processional with their polished faces and their donation boxes. She was praying low and fast into her own hands, words I didn't know, in a rhythm that didn't belong to any liturgy I'd ever heard. Three beats and a silence. Three beats and a silence. Like rowing. Like hauling a net.

And I knew—I want to say I *knew immediately*, but that would be a lie, another thing the taverns get wrong—the truth is I didn't know anything yet. I just felt the hair on my arms lift, the way it does in the guild storeroom sometimes, near the bottles that hold echoes, near the saint's second shadow we keep folded in a cedar box like a winter cloak. The feeling of something present that has no business being present. The feeling of an account opened that shouldn't exist.

"Her boy," someone near me whispered. "Her Tam went out Tuesday and never came back. Boat went down off the Teeth."

There were forty of us gathered by then, maybe fifty, pressed along the quay rail, and every single one of us understood what she was asking for. That's the thing people leave out too. We all understood, and none of us moved to stop her, because half of us had lost someone to that gray water and the other half expected to someday, and if some nameless old god wanted to fish dead sons out of the harbor, well. Who among us was going to file an objection?

Maren prayed. Three beats and a silence. The tide went strange.

That's how I'd describe it, if anyone ever asked me to put it in a ledger, and eventually someone would. The tide went strange. It pulled backward—not far, a handspan, maybe two—but against itself, against its own grain, the way a man flinches from his own reflection. The water below the stairs went glassy and still while the rest of the harbor kept moving, a circle of held breath maybe eight feet across, and Maren Kessel knelt at the edge of it with her wet hands open like she was showing them empty to a customs officer.

Then her son walked out of it.

* * *

I've seen impossible things for a living. That's not a figure of speech; it's my trade. I've watched a bottled echo sing back a lullaby in a dead woman's voice. I've held a jar of borrowed luck and felt it lean toward me like a cat wanting to be let out. I keep the ledgers that prove all of it happened, because proof is the whole business—we deal in things no one would believe otherwise.

None of it prepared me for Tam Kessel walking up the harbor stairs with seawater pouring off him in sheets.

Because the echoes and the luck and the shadow—that's all *contained*. That's impossibility in jars and boxes, behind our locked door, paid for and receipted. This was impossibility loose in the ordinary world, wearing a fisherman's oilskins, dripping on public stones, and it broke something in the morning that I don't think ever quite healed after. You could see it break in the faces around me. Wonder first—that came like a wave, beautiful and total, fifty people inhaling at once. Old women crossing themselves with the wrong hands. A boy laughing until his father clapped a palm over his mouth.

Tam reached the top of the stairs. He looked wrong in a way I couldn't name at first, and I'm good at naming—it's most of my job. Then I had it. He was *dry-eyed*. Seawater ran off his clothes and hair, poured from his sleeves, pooled at his boots, but his eyes themselves were dry as paper, and he blinked at the crowd like a man reading a room where the furniture had been rearranged.

"Mam," he said. His voice sounded like it had been stored somewhere cold. "It's cold down there."

Maren made a sound I will hear until I die. Not joy. Joy doesn't sound like that. It was the sound of a woman getting exactly what she asked for and understanding, in the same instant, that the price had already been set and she just didn't know the amount yet.

She grabbed him anyway. Of course she did. She held him and rocked him and called him every endearment the coast has ever invented, and the crowd surged forward to touch him, and for a moment—one moment—the fishmarket was the best place in the world. Strangers wept on each other's shoulders. Someone started passing around a flask. A priest of the Ninefold arrived, took one look, and turned around and left at a pace just short of running, which I noted at the time and filed away and did not understand until much later.

The wonder lasted four minutes. I counted. Counting is what I do when I'm afraid; it's how I keep the floor under my feet.

Because in the fourth minute, a woman near the ice stall—Doretta Vance, who sells smoked eel and owes the guild nothing, I've checked—said, loudly enough to carry, "What did she promise it?"

The crowd went quiet the way a room goes quiet when someone mentions a debt.

"Nothing," Maren said. She hadn't let go of her son. "I promised nothing. There's no one left to promise anything *to*, that's the whole—" Her voice cracked. "They're gone. All the old names are gone. I just said the words my grandmother said. I didn't think anyone was listening."

"That's worse," Doretta Vance said.

And there it was. That's the exact seam where wonder turned, and I stood close enough to watch it happen, and I want it written down that it wasn't cruelty that turned it. It was bookkeeping. Fifty fishermen and fishwives and ice men looked at a drowned boy walking dry-eyed out of the sea, and every one of them did the same arithmetic: *nothing comes free, and we don't know the lender.* In Caerlum, that's not superstition. That's literacy.

"What's it want?" someone asked from the back.

"Who says it wants anything?"

"The plague years," said a voice I recognized—Hobb, the ice man, one of ours, a debtor of ours, God forgive me. "My gran talked about the plague years. When the old gods were still owed, before the bargain." He stopped himself on the word *bargain*, hard, like biting down on something. His eyes found mine across the crowd, and I saw him decide, deliberately, not to know me.

Nobody asked what bargain. Nobody dared. But I watched the word go through the crowd like a hook through a line of fish—all of them caught by it at once, all of them silent.

"They went unpaid," Hobb finished quietly. "That's all I know. They went unpaid, and then they stopped answering, and we were glad."

Maren Kessel stood up, pulling her son with her, and I'll say this for her: she faced the crowd down. "He's my son and he's alive," she said. "Whatever it costs, it's mine to pay. Now somebody lend me a cloak, because my boy is freezing."

Somebody did. And that should have been the end of it—a miracle, a scare, a story for the winter markets.

Except Tam Kessel spoke again.

* * *

He spoke from inside his mother's borrowed cloak, teeth chattering, eyes still that awful dry paper, and what he said was: "Mam, there's others coming up."

The crowd leaned in. So did I, ledger forgotten, heart hammering like a fist on a door.

"Behind me," Tam said. "In the deep water. The ones that went down with the *Sable Ann* eleven years back. The ones that went down before that. They're standing on the bottom, all of them, waiting. They're not—" He frowned, groping for words, and I realized with a lurch that the frown was the most human thing about him. "They're not angry. That's the worst part. They're just *waiting*, like it's a queue. Like they know something changed."

"Changed how?" someone whispered.

"I don't know. But the door's not shut anymore." He shivered. "It's been shut my whole time down there, and this morning it wasn't. Somebody left it open a crack. Somebody started saying the words again."

Fifty people turned, slowly, to look at Maren Kessel.

She drew herself up to her full unimpressive height, this woman who gutted herring for sixty years, and said, "I'll say them again tomorrow, too, if I like. And the day after. My son came home. Come and arrest me."

No one arrested her. No one moved at all. And that silence was worse than any shouting, because I could hear what was happening underneath it: fifty minds doing arithmetic. *If it worked for her. If the words are just words. If the door is open a crack—*

What do you pray for? Everyone in that crowd had something. A lung that wouldn't heal. A daughter gone south and silent. Rent. Luck. Time. Everyone had something, and until this morning, everyone knew better than to ask.

I slipped away while they were still pressing in on Tam with questions, and I made myself walk, not run, because running draws eyes and eyes draw questions, and the first rule of the guild is that we are invisible or we are inventory. But my hands were shaking so badly I couldn't work the ledger strap buckle at the corner of Salt Row, and I stood there like a fool, fighting a little brass buckle, while behind me the fishmarket buzzed like a struck hive.

Here is what I kept seeing: not the drowned boy walking. The priest turning and leaving. Fast. Like a man who recognizes smoke and knows whose house is burning.

* * *

I got back to Caerlum House before the ninth bell. It stands on Lantern Court behind a facade so dull that delivery boys forget its existence halfway down the street—you learn to love that about it. Inside, the storeroom was exactly as I'd left it, which these days counts as comfort: shelves of bottled echoes labeled in Corvane's hand, the cedar box with the saint's shadow, the strongroom with its debts owed by the dead, forty years of entries in six different inks because the mentors died faster than they agreed on stationery.

Odile was in the archive, sitting in the dark, which she does. Our eldest member, older than the guild's oldest debts, blind in one eye and strange in both. She didn't turn her head when I came in, but she said, "You're late, girl. And you're frightened. Which is new."

"There was a thing at the harbor," I said. "A prayer. To one of the old ones. It answered."

Now she turned. Odile moves slow, like furniture being rearranged by someone very tired, but when she turned it was all at once, and I stepped back without meaning to.

"Say the next part," she said.

"A drowned man walked out of the water. Alive. Wrong, but alive. He said there were others waiting below. He said the door's been opened a crack."

For a long moment Odile said nothing. Then she began, very calmly, to gather her shawl, her stick, her little tin of salt—which I'd always assumed was a madness and was beginning to suspect was not.

"You'll want the vault," she said. "Third stair down, past the wine racks. The sealed shelf. Corvane's seal, red wax, the one you've been pretending not to notice for two years because noticing it meant asking questions you weren't ready to ask." She fixed her good eye on me, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Petra Hartwell. If a door has opened a crack, then before this week is out, someone will come asking who holds the keys to this city's memory. And when they come, you will need to know what we did, forty years ago, and you will need to know it fast."

"What did we do?"

Odile patted my hand, which was somehow the most frightening thing that had happened all day.

"Read your ledgers, keeper," she said, and shuffled toward the stairs. "All of them. Even the one he hoped you'd never find."

Above us, faint through the stone, the city bells began ringing out of order—harbor quarter first, then Market, then the Processional, then harbor again, the same alarm passed hand to hand like a rumor. Somewhere out there, a fishwife was saying old words into her open hands, and half of Caerlum was deciding whether to kneel down beside her.

I lit a lamp and went down to the third stair, past the wine racks, to the shelf with the red wax seal, where a ledger waited with one word on its spine in Master Corvane's careful hand:

**Vessany.**
