## 3. What Master Corvane Kept Locked

The vault under Caerlum House had three locks and no key that lived anywhere honest. The first lock took a brass key kept in Odile's shoe. The second wanted a word spoken backward—*Caerlum* said as *Mulreac*, which was not Vessany's tongue or ours but some older thing neither of us could name—and the third lock was a boy called Pip, who talked to mechanisms the way other children talked to dogs.

"You're going down there at midnight," Pip said, "on the night after the fishwife's son walked out of the harbor."

"Yes."

"After the whole fishmarket saw a drowned man breathing."

"Which is why," I said, "I'm going down tonight."

He considered this with the gravity of a magistrate, then pressed his palm flat against the third lock and murmured something low and coaxing. The tumblers turned like a cat rolling over to be scratched. "Master Corvane said nobody goes below without two reasons," he told me. "One you say out loud and one you don't."

"What did you tell him?"

"That I liked the dark." Pip shrugged. "It was true enough. Go on, then. I'll keep the door."

The stairs down were cut from the city's oldest stone, older than the guild, older than the harbor wall, older maybe than the idea of Caerlum itself. I carried a lamp in one hand and my ledger-satchel in the other, because Master Corvane taught me that you never enter an archive empty-handed. *An auditor who arrives without her tools,* he used to say, *is a guest. An auditor who arrives with them is a keeper.* He'd been dead eleven months and I still heard him everywhere, in the creak of the shelves, in the smell of dust and tallow and old iron gall ink. Grief, for a guildsman, is mostly habit.

The vault ran deeper than the house above it. Three galleries fanned out from the central stair: Debts, Wonders, and the long gallery at the back that had no name on its door, only a blank plaque of polished wood, sanded smooth as if a label had once been removed with great care.

I had audited Debts and Wonders a hundred times. Bottled echoes on their racks, each stoppered with wax and a tag giving date, place, and the name of whoever had paid to be rid of the sound. A saint's second shadow, folded into a box the size of a hymnal. Borrowed luck in graduated measures, the good stuff decanted into thimbles because more than a thimble would curdle. The last hour of a dying man, purchased by his daughter, who came every year on his birthday and asked to hold it, and every year we let her sit in the back room with it while it glowed faintly against her palms like a coal wrapped in cloth. Impossible things, all catalogued, all cross-referenced, all mine to keep straight.

But the third gallery—the unnamed one—I had entered perhaps six times in four years, and never past the first shelf. There wasn't much there. A few crates of ledgers so old the vellum had gone amber. A rack of sealed jars whose tags had faded to ghosts. And one shelf, at eye height, that held exactly one item.

A ledger. Bound in grey leather gone silverish at the corners, thick as a Bible, chained shut with a strap and a seal of black wax the size of my palm. On the cover, in Master Corvane's hand—that upright, patient script I knew better than my own—one word:

**Vessany**

I stood very still. The lamp flame leaned toward it, which lamps don't do.

Here is what I knew about Vessany before that night, which is to say here is everything: it was a country that had been on maps forty years ago and wasn't now. Not conquered—conquered countries leave ruins and refugees and bitter songs. Unwritten. Somewhere around my mother's childhood, the maps had simply stopped showing it, the way a ledger line gets ruled out and rewritten over. People didn't remember it wrong. They didn't remember it at all. Ask anyone in Caerlum about Vessany and they'd look at you kindly, the way you look at someone who has said a word in their sleep, and they'd say, *Is that a kind of cheese?*

And here is what I knew about Master Aldous Corvane: he had run this guild for thirty years, he had taught me that a record is a promise made to the future, and he had died last autumn with his accounts balanced to the copper and his conscience, as far as I could ever see, clean.

His handwriting was on the seal. His hand had closed this book.

I set the lamp on the shelf. I put my thumb against the wax.

Then I stopped, because that's what the wax is *for*. A sealed ledger isn't locked against thieves; thieves can't read our books anyway, half of them are written in cipher and the other half in trades no outsider has words for. A seal is a message from the dead. It says: *not yet.* It says: *whoever comes after me, this is yours, but it will cost you something to open, and I want you to know that I knew.*

I thought about the drowned boy walking up out of the harbor with seawater running off him, blinking in the morning light like a man waking late. I thought about his mother on her knees among the fish guts, praying—not to any god with a temple, because the temples have been empty my whole life, the gods gone quiet or gone away—but just saying a name into the air, an old name, a shape of syllables her grandmother might have known, and the name *working.*

Impossible things were leaking into the ordinary. That meant the ordinary was thinner than we'd believed. That meant somewhere there might be a reason, a cause, a first entry in a chain of entries—and if it existed anywhere in this city, it existed below the guild, behind a seal my dead mentor had pressed with his own thumb.

*I'm sorry, Master,* I said, to no one, to him. Then I broke the wax.

* * *

The book opened onto forty years of nothing.

Not blank pages—there's a difference, and any keeper knows it. Blank pages are waiting. These pages had been written on and then struck through, entire columns of entries ruled out with a single unbroken line, the way you cancel a debt, the way you close an account. But the ink beneath the ruling was still legible if you tilted a page against the lamplight, and I am an auditor, and tilting pages against light is most of my profession.

The earliest entries were in a hand I didn't know, cramped and hurried. Trade records. *Received: one memory of the sea road, condition fair. Paid: three years of good harvests, guaranteed.* *Received: the name of the river (local usage only), paid in bells.* Entry after entry, and every one of them buying or selling pieces of a country—a bridge's name, a lullaby, the color the roofs were painted, a grandmother's recipe, the fact of a harbor—as if Vessany were being sold off not in land but in *details*, itemized, lot by lot.

And then, partway through, Master Corvane's hand takes over. Younger than the hand I knew—this was decades ago—but unmistakably his. His entries change in character. They're not sales anymore. They're *counts.*

*Inventory, Vessany remaining: 4,112 names in common use. Down from 9,000 at last count. The buyers are paying well. The buyers are always paying well.*

*Inventory: 1,880 names. The capital's plague-winter ended on schedule. They are calling it providence.*

*Inventory: 212 names. Mostly grandmothers. Mostly said to babies who will forget by spring. We discussed refusing further orders. We did not refuse. Note for the record: we did not refuse.*

My hands had gone cold around the page. I read faster.

*Inventory: 11 names, held by members of this guild only. By oath.*

*Final inventory: one name, held by this office. Vessany exists now only where this ledger says it does. Filed accordingly. Sealed accordingly. May God forgive the arithmetic.*

There was a last page, loose, laid in rather than bound. A single entry, undated, in the oldest hand again:

*Bargain complete. One century free of plague, purchased with a country. The gods have taken payment and gone quiet. Note: quiet is not the same as gone. Whoever keeps this book after me should understand that debts do not die when the debtor forgets them. They wait. They compound. When the gods wake hungry—and they will wake hungry—they will come looking for whoever holds the account.*

*We traded a country's memory. What do you suppose they'll take next?*

I sat down on the cold floor of the vault with a dead country open in my lap, and I understood two things at once, the way you sometimes understand things in the dark, all at once and too late.

The first: the Guild of Caerlum had never merely traded in impossibilities. We had *made* one. We were the mechanism of an erasure. Every impossible thing on our shelves—every echo in a bottle, every borrowed hour—was small change compared to this. My mentors, my masters, the warm dead whose portraits hung in the reading room, had helped sell a country out from under itself, name by name, lullaby by lullaby, and had then done the only thing left to do with the truth: they had written it down, sealed it, and put it in the dark.

The second thing: the fishwife's prayer hadn't worked *despite* the bargain. It had worked *because* the bargain was breaking. The gods were waking. The debt was coming due. And the account holder—the office that held the last name of Vessany, that held the proof, that held whatever remained of the door out of a country that had never stopped existing underneath its erasure—

That office was mine now. It had been mine for eleven months, and nobody had told me, and I suddenly understood why Master Corvane had spent his last year teaching me everything except this, drilling me in cipher and custody and care, making me love the ledgers so completely that when I found this one I would not burn it.

He hadn't hidden the truth from me. He'd *prepared* me for it. There's a difference, and it's not a comforting one.

* * *

I went up the stairs with the ledger inside my coat, against my chest, like something stolen, which I suppose it wasn't—it was mine, that was the horror of it—and like something stolen anyway.

Odile was awake. She was always awake; the guild's eldest member slept in snatches like a cat and spent the rest of the hours in the kitchen chair by the banked stove, doing nothing, watching everything. Her eyes followed me as I crossed the flagstones, and she said, in her cracked voice that always sounded half a question, "You've been below."

"Yes."

"To the third gallery."

"Yes."

Her hands, knotted around a cup of cold tea, went still. For a moment she looked at me the way you look at weather coming. "Was the seal broken when you got there?"

"I broke it."

Something moved through her face—not anger. Something older and tireder than anger. "Forty years," she said. "Forty years we kept that door shut, and it lasted until the week a drowned boy climbed out of the harbor. Well." She drank the cold tea like it was a medicine. "Ask your question, girl. You've got one. You've been carrying it since the stairs."

"Did we sell Vessany?"

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere above us the house settled, timber ticking, the ordinary sounds of an ordinary night, and none of it felt ordinary anymore.

"We," Odile repeated. She smiled, and it was the worst smile I've ever seen, because there was no blame in it at all, only a grief so worn smooth with handling it had become almost gentle. "You weren't born, child. Your mother wasn't born. But yes. *We.* The guild doesn't die when its members do. The guild is the ledger, and the ledger remembers, and so the guilt stays current." She set down the cup. "Yes. We sold it. Not alone—we had help from men in fine collars who needed a century without plague more than they needed a country—but we held the pen. We held the pen, and we wrote the names out, and we took the fee."

"Why?" My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "Why would anyone—"

"Because the plague was killing ten thousand a winter, and Vessany had—" she counted on twisted fingers, an old merchant's habit, "*maybe* forty thousand people, scattered, poor, far from anywhere that mattered to anyone who mattered. Do the arithmetic an auditor does. That's how Corvane did it. That's how they all did it, right up until the numbers ran out and there was nothing left to count but themselves." She looked at the stove, at the coals settling red. "They weren't monsters, Petra. That's the thing you'll have to live with. They were careful, decent, methodical people who did a monstrous piece of arithmetic and then spent the rest of their lives keeping the books on it, because someone had to, because if the record burned the country would truly be dead and not merely sleeping. They thought they were being merciful. Keeping it asleep instead of killing it."

"And were they?"

"Ask the drowned boy," Odile said. "Ask whoever answered his mother." She rose, joints cracking like green firewood, and paused beside me on her way to bed. She didn't touch the ledger under my coat, but her eyes flicked to it once. "You'll want to ask the others. Don't. Tamsin won't meet your eye, Bramble will feed you until you stop asking, and Pip is too young to carry it. There's no one left who'll talk but me, and I've said what I've got." At the doorway she stopped. "One more thing, since the seal's broken and it can't be unhappened. The last name of Vessany, the one Corvane kept—the one that's in that book you're hiding like a hot coal—is also a *key*. Everything true in our trade is both a record and a mechanism, girl. Names especially. Why do you think they sold the names *first*?"

She shuffled out. I sat alone in the kitchen with the stove ticking down and the ledger heavy against my ribs, and I did not sleep, and near dawn I heard hooves on the cobbles outside—an early hour for hooves, a wrong rhythm for a cart—and through the frost-furred window I watched a rider in travel-grey come up the street toward Caerlum House at an easy, patient pace, the way a person rides when they are not in a hurry because they already know where they are going.

On the rider's saddle-roll, catching the first grey light, was a scroll case stamped with the royal seal.

I put my hand flat on the ledger through my coat and waited for the knock.
