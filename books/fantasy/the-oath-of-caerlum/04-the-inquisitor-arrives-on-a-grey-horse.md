## 4. The Inquisitor Arrives on a Grey Horse

The horse was grey because grey horses died last in a long winter, and Serah Vole had learned to plan for winters the way other people planned for dinner. She rode through the Salt Gate at midmorning with a royal writ sealed in lead, a list of eleven names folded against her ribs, and no luggage worth stealing.

The gate sergeant read the writ twice, which was one time more than most men needed and one fewer than she'd have preferred. "Inquisitor," he said finally, the word sitting awkwardly in his mouth like a bone he'd been handed instead of meat. "We don't get many of your—"

"No," Serah said. "You don't."

She took the city's measure from the saddle as she rode in. Caerlum was a port town pretending it wasn't dying: cranes still working, but half of them idle; fish stalls open, but the fish small; a shrine on the corner where someone had left an offering of bread and salt to a god whose name had been chiseled off the stone so long ago that only the shape of the removal remained—a smooth wound in the granite, polished by weather or by hands.

She looked at the wound longer than she meant to.

*Eleven names,* she reminded herself, and rode on.

---

The Question House kept rooms in Caerlum above a chandler's shop, which was convenient, because wax was flammable and Serah liked her exits to have options. She unpacked nothing except the list, laid it flat on the table, and weighted its corners with coins.

Eleven names. Eleven people in Caerlum who, according to three years of correspondence, informants, and one very cooperative customs clerk, remembered Vessany.

Not *claimed* to remember. Not *spoke of*. Remembered. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole of her work. Memory of Vessany was not illegal—there was no law against remembering a country that did not exist, any more than there was a law against remembering a dream. But memory spread. Memory was a fire that didn't need tinder, only breath. Forty years ago the Crown had paid a terrible price for something—it was not written what—and part of the payment had been silence, and the silence had held for four decades, and now, this autumn, prayers were being answered in the fishmarket by gods with no names, and the Crown wanted to know if the two things were connected.

They were connected. Serah knew they were connected the way you know your own hand is attached to your wrist. She simply could not say how she knew, and that was the first entry in the private ledger she kept behind her eyes, the one no writ could touch:

*I know things about Vessany I was never taught.*

She had grown up in a town called Merrow's Bend. That was what the records said. She had checked, more than once, early in her career, with the particular thoroughness of a young woman who suspects her own past of lying to her. Merrow's Bend existed. It had always existed. There were tax rolls, parish records, a gravestone with her mother's name on it in a churchyard Serah had visited exactly once and found somehow familiar, like a room seen in a mirror.

Her mother's name was Aliss Vole. She had died when Serah was six. Serah had one memory of her: a kitchen, steam on the window, a song in a language the records said was Lowmarsh dialect.

Except Lowmarsh dialect didn't sound like that. Serah had studied languages the way other children collected river stones, compulsively, hungrily, and the song in her memory fit no tongue she'd ever catalogued. And sometimes, in the space between sleeping and waking, she could almost see the kitchen whole—the crooked shelf, the blue pot, the door at the back that her mother never let her open—

A door at the back of a house in a town that had always existed.

Serah pressed her thumbnail into the table until the pain gave her something honest to hold. Then she unfolded the list and began to work.

---

The first name was Odile Marchetti, age unknown, occupation listed as *none*, residence listed as *the old tannery row, or thereabouts*. The informant's note beneath it read: *Mad. Talks to the river. May be useless. May be the opposite.*

The second name was Tamsin Ferrow, scrivener, known associate of the Guild of Caerlum.

That was the phrase that mattered. *Known associate.* Three of the eleven names touched the guild directly; four more touched it by marriage, debt, or habit. The Guild of Caerlum traded in impossible things—that much was legend even in the capital, told with a wink, the way you tell stories you don't quite believe. Bottled echoes. Borrowed luck. The last hour of a dying man, sold to his widow so she could say goodbye properly.

Serah did not wink at such stories. Stories were how impossibilities dressed themselves before walking abroad in daylight.

And the guild kept ledgers. Everyone knew that too. A guild that wrote everything down, in a city where prayers were suddenly being answered, in a season when the Crown had sent her north with a list—

She put the list away and went out to look at the city, because a list tells you who to find, but only streets tell you where a thing is hiding.

---

Caerlum in harvest month smelled of brine and woodsmoke and, underneath both, something older—tar and cold stone, the smell of a place built before anyone living could remember why. She walked without escort. Inquisitors traveled alone or not at all; a soldier's shadow made people lie faster, not slower.

At the fishmarket she stopped to buy nothing and listen to everything.

The market women talked around a subject the way water talks around a stone. She caught fragments. *Walked right up out of the harbor, dripping, knew his own mother's name.* Caught another: *Don't say it, don't say the name, you'll call it down on us.* And a third, from an old man mending nets who thought he was speaking only to his grandson: *Forty years since anything answered. Now everything answers. That's not blessing, boy. That's hunger waking up.*

Serah bought a cup of cider from a stall and drank it slowly, watching the crowd the way she'd been trained to watch crowds—not for faces but for silences, for the places where conversation bent and flowed around some submerged shape.

There. By the harbor steps. A knot of women who weren't selling anything, standing in a loose ring around a spot of wet stone as though the stone were a grave. One of them was crying. Another was holding her arm as though the crying woman might bolt. And every few moments, all of them glanced toward the water, then away, quickly, like people checking on a fire they hoped would go out.

A drowned son walked out of the harbor. The old man's words, and here were the mourners to match them.

Serah finished her cider and set the cup down. Her hand was steady. Her hand was always steady; that was the first thing they'd trained into her at the Question House, before rhetoric, before law, before the reading of faces. *Steady hands, Vole. Everything else can shake as long as the hands don't.*

But something under her ribs was shaking, and it was not fear of drowned men coming home.

It was the door. The door at the back of her mother's kitchen, the one she hadn't thought about in twenty years until this morning, standing in a fishmarket that stank of answered prayers. Doors opened from both sides. That was the whole nature of doors. And if the gods of some erased country were pushing their way back into the world through harbors and fishwives' prayers, then somewhere, on the other side of whatever bargain had bought forty years of silence, something was pushing back.

Or pulling.

---

She spent the afternoon being unremarkable. She sat in a tavern near the guild quarter and listened to a customs clerk complain about tariffs. She walked past the Guild of Caerlum itself—a narrow building wedged between a rope-maker and a shuttered chapel, unremarkable except that it was *too* unremarkable, scrubbed of sign and shield, the windows clean in a street of dirty ones. Clean windows meant someone inside cared what could be seen through them. Or what couldn't.

On the chapel's boarded door, someone had chalked a mark. She stopped. It was a simple thing: a circle with a line drawn down through it, like a door seen edge-on, or a coin split in half. Old. The chalk was fresh over an older carving, the kind of symbol beggars scratched for luck in cities that had forgotten what it meant.

Serah stood looking at it for a long moment, and her body did something her training had never authorized: her mouth shaped a sound. Half a syllable. The beginning of a word in the language of her mother's song.

She walked away before the rest of it could come.

---

Evening found her back in the room above the chandler's shop, writing her first report by candlelight. She wrote it the way the Question House required—dates, observations, probabilities, recommendations—and then she sat looking at the last line, which read:

*Recommend surveillance of the Guild of Caerlum prior to formal inquiry. Subjects may destroy records if warned by official approach.*

True. Sensible. Standard procedure.

She struck it out anyway.

Because here was the thing she had not written in any report, the thing she barely admitted in the privacy of her own skull: she did not want the guild's records destroyed. Some part of her—some part older than her training, older than her name, older than the tidy fiction of Merrow's Bend—wanted those ledgers *read*. Wanted them opened and spread out under lamplight, every page, every entry, every impossible transaction recorded in a keeper's careful hand. Because ledgers were proof. Ledgers were the one form of writing that existed precisely so that forgetting could be called a lie.

Somewhere in Caerlum was a book that might contain the true name of a town that had always existed, and a door at the back of a kitchen, and a song.

Serah blew out the candle and sat in the dark, listening to the chandler below banking his fires, and let herself think the thought she'd ridden three weeks to avoid:

*If Vessany can be erased, so can anywhere. So can Merrow's Bend. So can me.*

The list of eleven names lay on the table, patient as a knife. Ten names, really, that mattered. The eleventh she had read a dozen times without letting it land, the way you don't look directly at a bright thing.

*Odile Marchetti. Mad. Talks to the river.*

Tomorrow, Serah decided, she would begin with the madwoman. Madwomen remembered what sensible people agreed to forget. And if half of what the informant wrote was true—if Odile Marchetti talked to the river the way Serah's mother had once sung to a closed door—then the old woman might know the rest of the syllable that had died on Serah's lips in front of a chalked circle.

Outside, far off toward the harbor, a bell began to ring. Not the hour bell. Not the alarm. Something between the two—urgent, arrhythmic, wrong—and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, voices rising from the direction of the fishmarket. Shouting. Not the shouting of a riot.

The shouting of people witnessing something.

Serah lit the candle again, took up her coat, and went down the stairs two at a time, steady-handed, heart hammering, toward the sound of the world coming apart at its oldest seam.
