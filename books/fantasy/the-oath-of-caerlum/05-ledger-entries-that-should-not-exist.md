## 5. Ledger Entries That Should Not Exist

The trouble with a ledger that never lies is that it has no mercy.

Petra had learned that at eleven years old, when Master Corvane caught her rounding down a debt by two coppers because she felt sorry for the debtor. He hadn't shouted. He'd sat her down with the ledger open between them and made her read the entry aloud until she understood what she'd done: not cheated the guild, but lied to the future. *Someone will come looking for this number,* he said. *Someone always comes looking. And when they find it wrong, they'll stop trusting everything else we ever wrote.* The ledgers were not a record of the guild's business. They were the guild's spine.

She had kept them straight for nine years since. Every bottle logged when it came in, every echo catalogued by pitch and provenance, every debt of the dead marked paid or unpaid with no sentiment whatsoever. It was the one part of the dying trade that still worked exactly as it had a hundred years ago, and Petra guarded it the way other people guarded their children.

Which was why, on the third night after the drowned boy walked out of the harbor, she sat down at the audit desk with a lamp, a pot of ink, and the growing certainty that something in the ledgers had gone wrong.

* * *

The fishmarket had not calmed. If anything it had gotten worse, the way a wound gets worse before it decides whether to heal or kill you. People still left offerings at the harbor steps—bread, coins, cups of wine poured into the water—but the offerings had changed character. The first day they'd been grateful. By the second they were pleading. By the third they were payment, and everyone knew it even if nobody said it aloud. The gods were answering prayers again after forty years of silence, and silence, Petra was beginning to understand, had been doing a kind of work all along.

Bramble had opinions about this, delivered over breakfast with a wooden spoon as a baton. "In my grandmother's village there was a well spirit," she said. "Sweet-tempered thing, so long as you thanked it every season. One year the village got proud and skipped the thanking. Well spirit didn't curse anybody. Didn't need to. It just stopped holding the water sweet." She jabbed the spoon at the window, toward the sea. "That's what's coming back to us. Not kindness. Habit. We're the ones who broke the habit."

Odile, in her chair by the stove, said nothing, but her eyes tracked Bramble's spoon like it was a bird she was deciding whether to catch.

Tamsin, meanwhile, had gone quiet in a way that Petra found more alarming than any sermon. The forger spent three days in her room with the door shut, and when she emerged she looked like a woman who'd been arguing with someone only she could hear. When Petra asked if she was well, Tamsin smiled the smile she used for customs men and said, "Never better," which from Tamsin meant *ask me later*, possibly much later, possibly never.

But that was the house above. Down here, in the vault under Caerlum House, the world was supposed to be orderly.

Petra pulled the current ledger off its shelf and set it beside the master index, and began where an auditor begins: with the entries that shouldn't be there yet.

* * *

The guild recorded things in advance—that was normal. A commission accepted was entered before it was fulfilled; a debt contracted was written before it was paid. But there were rules. Forward entries required a counter-signature from a second member. Forward entries were never narrative—they were transactional, dry as bone. *One bottled echo, minor key, owed to the widow Sarr by Michaelmas.* You did not write stories into the ledger. Stories were what happened between the lines, and Corvane had drilled that distinction into her until it was marrow.

So when she opened to the spring pages—the clean, unruled future, weeks away—and found paragraphs, her stomach dropped before her mind caught up.

She read the first one standing up. Then she sat down, because her knees informed her that reading it standing was no longer among her options.

*Entry the 14th of Thawmonth. Received into custody: one door, threshold-class, provenance Vessany. Condition: remembering. Location: beneath. Custodian: P. Hartwell.*

Her own handwriting.

Not similar. Not close. Her handwriting—the slight forward lean she'd never trained out of herself, the way her *th* ligature dragged, the ink pressure heavier on downstrokes because she pressed when she wrote numbers. Nine years of keeping the ledgers meant she knew her own hand the way she knew her own face in a bad mirror, and this was it, letter for letter, flaw for flaw.

And the date. Thawmonth. Next spring. Nearly half a year away.

Petra sat very still and listened to the vault breathe around her. Stone did not breathe. She knew that. But old buildings full of old magic developed habits, and Caerlum House had been swallowing impossible things for longer than the city above had had streetlamps, and some nights, if you listened too long, you could hear it digesting.

*Provenance Vessany.*

The word sat on the page like something dead that refused to stop being warm. She had heard it twice in her life: once as a child, when Odile sang in a language no one recognized and called the song *home* before catching herself; and once last week, muttered by Master Ellery-who-was-not-a-master-anymore when he thought no one was listening, in the tone men use for debts they hoped were dead. The eldest members went silent around it the way a room goes silent around a sickbed.

Now it was in the ledger. In her handwriting. Dated six months early, describing a door that did not exist, in a location given only as *beneath*.

She turned the page.

There were more.

*Entry the 15th of Thawmonth. The door remembers being opened. Note: it does not remember how. Query to custodian: whose memory completes it?*

*Entry the 22nd of Thawmonth. Five names added to the rolls for passage. Passage granted to those already written. Note for the next keeper: we did not choose quickly enough, and it cost us the salt cellar. Write faster.*

*Salt cellar.* Her eye snagged on it and refused to move on. It was such a small, stupid, domestic detail—exactly the kind of thing she herself would note in a margin, exactly the kind of joke grief makes. Whoever had written these had known her. Knew her hand, knew her habits, knew the shape of her mind well enough to leave her a message disguised as a ledger entry.

Or. Or.

Or she had written them herself, someday, and the ledger had simply filed them early.

Petra put both hands flat on the desk, because the vault seemed to tilt, very slightly, the way a ship tilts when it changes its mind about its course. The past and the future were supposed to be safe places. That was the entire architecture of her life: the ledgers held the past fixed so it couldn't be argued with, and the future was just the past that hadn't dried yet. Everything she loved lived inside that arrangement. Take it apart and—

Take it apart and a drowned boy walks out of the harbor. Take it apart and prayers get answered. Take it apart and the impossible stops staying politely in its bottles and starts leaking into fishmarkets and ledger books and the handwriting of a twenty-year-old keeper who had never once, not ever, falsified an entry.

*Query to custodian: whose memory completes it?*

She read that line four times. Then she did the thing Corvane would have done, which was stop reading and start checking.

* * *

She checked for two hours.

First, the obvious: forgery. She carried the ledger up to Tamsin's door, knocked, and asked—casually, she hoped—if Tamsin could come look at something. Tamsin examined the entries under lamplight, tilting the page so the ink's sheen shifted, running her thumb along the strokes, and her face did something complicated.

"That's your hand," she said finally.

"I know."

"No, I mean—" Tamsin tapped the paper. "I've forged your hand. Twice. For customs filings, don't ask, you sign too fast and I do it neater than you do. I'm good. This isn't mine. Look at the ink sit—it's soaked in, aged, whatever aging does. If I'd faked this last week it would sit on top of the page like grease. This ink thinks it's been here for months." She looked up, and behind the forger's professional calm there was something rawer. "Peta. Who wrote these?"

"Nobody," Petra said. "They're dated next spring."

Tamsin stared at her. Then she closed the ledger carefully, the way you close a door on a room where something is sleeping, and said, "I'm going to go make tea. Strong tea. And you're going to tell Odile, because I am not touching this alone, and neither are you."

Second check: the physical book. Petra took the ledger back to the vault and examined the binding, the stitching, the pagination. Nothing added. No inserted leaves, no palimpsest tricks. The entries were on pages that had always been blank, in a book that had been locked in the vault since midsummer, in a vault with one key, worn on a cord around Petra's own neck.

Third check: the other ledgers. She pulled the archive volume by volume—the sixty-year run, the hundred-and-twenty-year run, the black books from before the guild stopped admitting it traded in certain categories. In the oldest of them, a ledger bound in grey leather with a cracked spine, she found the pattern already waiting, like a word you suddenly see everywhere once you learn it.

Entries in hands she didn't recognize. Entries describing things that arrived years after they were written. A bottled echo received in one year and logged as received in another. A debt marked *paid* forty days before the debtor died. Marginal notes in a cramped antique script: *the book knows before we do. Corvane says stop asking why. Corvane says start asking what it wants us ready for.*

Corvane. Her master's name, in a book he must have handled, in a margin he might have written. Petra sat with the grey ledger open on her knees and felt the floor of her whole understanding give one soft, terrible creak.

The ledgers didn't just record the impossible. They never had. Somewhere back through the generations, the books had learned—or been taught, or had always known—to keep time loosely, the way a net keeps fish loosely: enough structure to hold them, enough give to let them move. The guild's masters had written forward entries not as predictions but as invitations. You wrote the impossible into the book, and the impossible, finding itself written, came true more easily. A ledger wasn't a mirror. It was a loom.

And now something was weaving without her.

* * *

She climbed the stairs near midnight and found Odile awake, as Odile was always awake, sitting in the dark kitchen with her hands around a cold cup, watching the window where snow should not fall for another two months and, Petra noticed with a fresh coldness in her gut, might not wait those two months.

"Odile," she said. "I need to show you something."

The old woman turned her head. In the dark her eyes were pale as shallow water. "You found the spring pages," she said.

The words landed like ice down Petra's collar. "You know about them."

"I know the books have been restless since the boy came out of the water." Odile said it simply, without drama, the way she said everything now that most of her mind had been spent decades ago. "Restless books write ahead. They always have. Yours writes further ahead than most, because you keep it honest, and honest books trust you with the truth early." She lifted the cold cup, remembered it was cold, and set it down again. "What did yours say?"

"A door. Provenance Vessany. Beneath." Petra's voice stayed level through sheer professional discipline. "Dated Thawmonth. There's a note addressed to me. To the custodian. Odile—who is the custodian? What door? What's *beneath*?"

For a long moment the old woman looked at her, and Petra had the vertiginous sense of being weighed against something—not judged, measured, the way you measure cloth before cutting.

"Beneath," Odile said at last, "has always been beneath. Under this house. Under the city. Under the whole lie we bought with our silence." She rose, slowly, joints cracking like green wood, and stood close enough that Petra could smell the lavender and dust of her. "You want to know what the ledgers are for, girl? Really for?"

"Yes."

"Not tonight." Something moved behind Odile's eyes—fear, or its fossil. "Tonight you'll sleep, because tomorrow there will be riders at the gate asking questions, and you'll want your wits about you. The books aren't the only things writing ahead." She shuffled toward the door, then paused. "When the time comes, don't burn the spring pages. Whatever happens. Whatever they tell you to do. A book that trusts you with the future is worth more than the safety of everyone who reads it."

She went out. Petra stood alone in the dark kitchen, heart hammering, and heard the sentence settle over her like a shroud sized for someone larger.

*Riders at the gate.* Tomorrow.

She went back down to the vault, because going back down was the only thing she knew how to do, and took out a fresh sheet of paper, and did the only sane thing left: she wrote a memo to the file. *Discovered anomalous forward entries in the current ledger, in the keeper's hand, undated by any member. Investigation pending. No action taken pending consultation.*

Then, because her hand was shaking and honesty was the one muscle she trusted, she added a final line:

*I did not write these. But I believe I will.*

She blotted it, filed it, locked the vault, and stood a moment in the corridor with the key warm against her chest, listening to the house above her—Bramble banking the stove, Tamsin pacing her room, the boy Pip murmuring goodnight to the locks as he passed them, each lock clicking softly back.

Tomorrow, riders. Tonight, a ledger that dreamed in her handwriting, of a door beneath her feet, and five names waiting on a page to find out if they were hers.
