## 6. The Family You Keep Anyway

Bramble had a rule about suppers: nobody ate alone, and nobody ate standing up, and nobody — *nobody* — talked business until the bread was gone.

She'd made the rule eleven years ago, back when the guild still had forty members and needed three cooks to feed them. Now it was just her, one dented copper pot big enough to bathe a child in, and six mouths on a good night. Tonight was a good night, which was to say everyone was home before dark, which was to say nothing had gone wrong yet.

She counted them as they came into the kitchen the way she counted spoons after market day.

Petra first, always first, with ink on her fingers and that look she got when the ledgers had said something she didn't like. She hung her coat on the peg by habit even though the fire was warm enough to cook a man's resolve out of him.

"Smells like rabbit," Petra said.

"Smells like *triumph*," Bramble corrected. "The rabbit is incidental."

Tamsin came next through the back stair from the workroom, carrying the particular stiffness of a woman who'd been hunched over a forgery bench for nine hours. She had a smudge of iron-gall ink at her jaw that she didn't know about, and Bramble decided not to tell her, because Tamsin with an unknown smudge was one of the few honest things left in Caerlum.

"Is that my good thyme I smell?" Tamsin asked.

"It's my thyme. You have no plants. You killed the last one by forgetting its name."

"I didn't forget it. I was testing whether names matter."

"And?"

"The basil disagrees."

Then Wren, the boy who talked to locks, sliding in sideways through the door like he wasn't sure he'd been invited, which after four years he still did every single night, and every single night Bramble pointed at his chair until he sat in it. He was fifteen now and growing like a weed in a crack, all wrists and elbows and eyes that went soft whenever anything mechanical was within reach of his hands.

"Gate lock's sticking," he reported, dropping into his seat. "I oiled it."

"You're supposed to report sticking locks to Odile."

"Odile told me to oil it."

"Odile tells you everything I tell her not to."

"That's why we get along," Wren said, with the serene confidence of a boy who had never once been wrong in his own estimation.

Hesk came last, because Hesk was always last, because Hesk checked the street twice and the roof once before he allowed himself the luxury of sitting down. The retired thief was sixty-two years old and moved like a man of forty who was very tired, and he took the chair nearest the door the way other men took chairs nearest the fire.

"All quiet?" Bramble asked.

"Fishmarket's still talking about the drowned man," Hesk said, and then caught himself, because the rule was *no business till the bread was gone*, and he had the decency to look abashed about it.

But it was too late; the word was out in the room like a dropped glass. Bramble saw it land on each of them. Petra's hands went still over the napkin she was folding. Tamsin's jaw tightened. Even Wren stopped fidgeting.

"Well," Bramble said briskly, "the bread isn't gone yet, so nobody's allowed to be frightened for another ten minutes. Petra, ladle. Tamsin, stop looking at your hands. Wren, if you put your fingers in the pot again I will break them at the knuckle and forge a letter saying it was an accident."

"You can't forge my handwriting."

"*Nobody* can forge your handwriting, child, that's what makes you useful and insufferable."

* * *

The stew was rabbit and root vegetables and the last of the good barley, thick enough to stand a spoon in, and Bramble watched them eat with the fierce proprietary attention she gave to nothing else in this world. This was the part of the day she lived for. Not the cooking itself — though she'd die before admitting the cooking wasn't sacred — but this: six people around one table, passing the bread, arguing about nothing, alive.

Odile came down halfway through, as she always did, drifting in from whatever room or century she'd been inhabiting, and took her seat without being asked, which was how they all knew she was having a clear day. On unclear days she had to be fetched. On clear days she arrived like weather.

"There's salt in the bread," Odile announced.

"There's salt in everything, Odile. It's salt."

"Not enough." The old woman tore her crust with great ceremony. "Salt remembers too, you know. That's why we put it on thresholds. It forgets slower than we do." She looked up, and her eyes were sharp and present and fixed on Petra. "Has the girl found the sealed ledger yet?"

The table went very quiet.

"Odile," Bramble said gently, "we're eating."

"I'm aware. I've been eating since before your grandmother was a disappointment." But she let it go, which was unlike her, and turned instead to buttering her bread with the concentration of a woman defusing something.

Bramble caught Petra's eye across the table. The girl shook her head, almost imperceptibly. *Not yet.* Or maybe *not telling you.* With Petra those were often the same thing, and Bramble had learned — slowly, painfully, over eleven years of loving a stubborn apprentice archivist — that you could not pry truth out of that girl any more than you could pry a nail out of wet wood. You waited for the wood to dry. You waited for her to come to you.

So Bramble did what she did best. She changed the subject by force.

"Wren. Tell us something cheerful. What did the locks say today?"

Wren brightened immediately, because Wren could be redirected like water. "The bank on Cordwainer Street has a new vault door. Vessany ironwork."

The word landed again, second time in ten minutes, and this time Bramble felt it go through the room like cold air under a door. *Vessany.* Nobody said it much anymore. Nobody said it much anymore the way you don't say a dead man's name at his widow's table.

"Old ironwork," Wren went on, oblivious or pretending to be. "Really old. The lock has a prayer stamped inside the keyhole cover. I could read most of it. It's not in any language I know, but the *shape* of it—" he made a gesture with both hands, cradling something invisible, "—it's shaped like a promise. Locks are mostly promises, when you get right down to it. *Stay shut. Keep safe. Hold.* This one was promising somebody it would hold forever."

"And did it?" Tamsin asked quietly.

"Oh, no. It's been opened hundreds of times. But it kept the promise anyway, somehow. It held everything it was supposed to hold until someone with the right key came along. That's all a promise can do." He shrugged, fifteen years old and casually wise, the way only children and madmen can be. "Anyway, the banker paid me two coppers to oil it and I charged him five because he was rude to the clerk."

"That's my boy," Bramble said, and meant it in the full and terrible weight of the phrase, and saw him glow, and hated herself a little for how easy he still was, how unguarded, how much he trusted the walls of this place to hold.

* * *

After supper there was the ritual of it: Petra and Wren clearing, Tamsin retreating to the workroom with a plate balanced on her forearm like a scribe's board, Hesk going out to walk the perimeter he walked every night, rain or frost or royal decree notwithstanding. Odile stayed at the table, as she always stayed, watching the fire with her hands folded like a woman waiting for a train that had been delayed forty years.

Bramble washed dishes. It was her meditation, her penance, and her throne, all three. Behind her she heard the small domestic music of the guild: Petra stacking bowls, Wren chattering about tumblers, the tick of the cooling stove.

"Bramble." Petra's voice, low, close. The girl had come to stand beside her at the basin, drying, which she never did unless something was wrong. "Can I ask you something strange?"

"You can ask me seven strange things before I charge you rent."

"When you took me in. Eleven years ago. Why?"

Bramble scrubbed a pot that did not need scrubbing. "You were a skinny orphan with ink-stained fingers who'd been sleeping in our reading room for a week and reorganizing it alphabetically. Corvane said send her away. I said over my dead body, and then he died, and I want it noted that I consider these two facts unrelated."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Bramble admitted. "It isn't." She set the pot down and looked at the girl properly. Petra was twenty-three now and still skinny, still ink-stained, still reorganizing things that didn't want organizing. "Here's the answer. When I was young I had a family. Real one, blood and all. Plague took them in the winter of the yellow snow, all four, in nine days. And afterward everyone was very kind and very useless, and I learned that grief doesn't kill you, it just makes you a house where nobody lives. And then I came here, because the Guild of Caerlum was the only place in the city hiring a cook who didn't ask questions about what was in the cellar." She picked the pot back up. "And Corvane sat me down my first week and said, *Bramble, none of us here are related, and that's the whole point. Blood is an accident. This is a decision.* And I thought he was being clever. And then thirty years went by and I understood he was being literal."

Petra was quiet a moment. "So we're a decision."

"We're the best decision anyone in this building ever made, and some of us made it drunk." Bramble bumped the girl's shoulder with her own. "Why? What's got you asking?"

Petra hesitated. Bramble watched the whole war happen behind her face — the ledgers, the fishmarket, whatever Odile knew, whatever Corvane had locked away — and then watched the girl lose the war to caution, as she always did, as she always would until the day she didn't.

"Nothing," Petra said. "Supper was good. Thank you."

"Everything's fine," Bramble agreed, "and I believe you exactly as far as I can throw the stove."

* * *

It was near midnight when the knock came.

Not the gate knock — Wren's knock, three-and-one, the pattern they'd agreed on. Not Hesk's either. A stranger's knock, polite, measured, at the front door, which almost nobody used and absolutely nobody used at midnight.

Bramble was down the stairs first because she was closest, with a cast-iron skillet in her hand because she was Bramble. Hesk materialized from the shadows of the hall before she reached the door, and Tamsin's light appeared at the top of the workroom stairs, and Petra came last with an unlit lamp held like a weapon.

Through the frosted glass: a shape. Tall. Still. Patient in the way that soldiers are patient.

The knock came again. Three knocks, evenly spaced, the knock of someone who had never once been made to wait.

"Guild of Caerlum," said a woman's voice through the door. Clear. Unhurried. The voice of someone reading from a document she had memorized. "My name is Serah Vole. I hold a writ of inquiry under the seal of the Crown. I am not here tonight to enter. I am here to inform you that I will return tomorrow at the tenth bell, with paper sufficient for a full audit of your customs records." A pause, precisely measured. "Please have your ledgers available."

Silence. The shape at the door did not move.

Bramble looked at Hesk. Hesk looked at the door the way a man looks at a snake he's decided not to step on yet. Above them, faintly, Odile began to laugh — a low, cracked sound drifting down the stairwell, laughing at something none of the rest of them could see.

"Ledgers," the voice repeated, softer now, almost gentle. "All of them."

And the footsteps went away down the front steps, unhurried, measured, and the grey shape passed the window and was gone into the fog off the harbor, and Bramble stood in her own hallway with a skillet in her hand and her family gathered behind her like a hand of cards, and thought:

*Well. There goes the rule about no business after supper.*
