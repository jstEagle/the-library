## 7. Questions in a Room Without Echoes

The summons came at dawn, delivered by a boy in the grey livery of the Crown Customs Office, and it was polite in the way that knives are polished.

*The Guild of Caerlum will present itself for examination regarding irregularities in declared goods, ninth bell, Customhouse Annex, Room Four.*

Petra read it three times by candlelight. *Irregularities in declared goods.* Someone had been through their manifests. Someone with a trained eye and a warrant behind them, because no customs clerk on his own initiative summons a guild that has paid its fees without fail for two hundred years.

She brought the note down to the kitchen, where Bramble was already up, hands dusted to the elbow with flour, pretending the world was an ordinary place because bread demanded it.

"Room Four," Petra said.

Bramble didn't look up from her dough. "I know Room Four. No windows. Stone walls so thick your own voice won't come back to you." She punched the dough once, hard. "They built it that way on purpose. A room without echoes. You say something in there and it's like saying it to God—no proof it ever happened except what they choose to write down."

Tamsin was already at the table, boots up, peeling an apple in one unbroken ribbon. "So we lie."

"We don't lie," Petra said. "We tell the truth narrowly."

"That's just lying wearing its good coat."

"It's the only coat we have."

Odile sat in the corner chair where she always sat, wrapped in three shawls against a cold that hadn't reached the kitchen yet, watching the window with eyes that had gone milky at the edges. At the word *Customhouse* she made a small sound, like a hinge nobody oiled anymore.

"They'll ask about Vessany," Odile said.

Nobody answered. Nobody needed to. The name had been sitting in the middle of every conversation for six days now, unspoken, taking up space like a corpse at dinner.

"The boy stays home," Bramble said. "Wren doesn't come."

"Wren comes," Petra said. "If we leave him behind he'll follow us anyway, and he'll be alone when he does. He's safer inside our lie than outside it."

"And can he keep his mouth shut?"

"He talks to locks, Bramble. Not to people. People are the ones he doesn't trust."

Bramble muttered something about children and locks and the general state of the world, and began shaping loaves with the violence of a woman who would very much like to shape an inquisitor.

* * *

Room Four was worse than Bramble had promised.

It was long and narrow, lit by oil lamps behind smoked glass, and the stone swallowed sound so completely that Petra could hear her own heartbeat and nothing else—not Wren breathing beside her, not Tamsin shifting her weight, not Bramble's knuckles cracking one by one like slow gunfire. Their footsteps crossed the floor and died without a whisper. It was like walking into a held breath.

Serah Vole waited at the far end, seated at a plain table with a clerk to her left and a stack of paper squared to precise angles before her. She wore no armor today, just a dark riding coat and the royal writ pinned at her shoulder, and she looked younger in lamplight than she had looked on her grey horse, or perhaps she looked exactly as young as she was, which was somehow more unsettling.

She did not rise. "Sit, please."

Six chairs had been set out. There were five of them. The empty sixth sat at the end like a question.

"You're missing a member," Serah said, as they settled. "An old woman. Odile Vash. The registry lists seven names for this guild."

"She wanders," Petra said. "She's old. Some mornings she forgets there's a world to wander in."

Something moved behind Serah's eyes—too quick to name—and was gone. "I see." She opened the top folder. "Let's begin simply. Your guild imports bottled echoes from the tidal caves at Marrow Point. Declared value: four coppers per unit. Is that accurate?"

"The caves give them freely," Petra said. "We pay the fishermen who row us out. Four coppers covers the boat."

"So the echoes themselves are worthless."

"The echoes themselves are a gift."

"From whom?"

"From the caves."

A pause. The clerk wrote something. Petra watched the pen move and understood, with a lurch, that everything said here was being built into something—that this was not an examination but a construction site, and every answer was another brick.

Serah turned a page. "Your manifests also list, in various quarters across the last decade: a saint's second shadow. Debts owed by eleven deceased persons. One hour, provenance disputed. And—" she read without inflection, "—a door."

The word dropped into the silence of that room and made no echo at all, and Petra felt the absence of it like a physical thing, the way you feel a stair that isn't there.

"A door," Petra repeated.

"Declared in the spring manifest of nine years ago. Item description: *one door, condition unknown, location pending.* Signed by Master Corvane." Serah looked up. "Master Corvane is dead."

"Two years now."

"What happened to the door?"

"I couldn't say. I never saw it. Corvane handled the unusual acquisitions personally in his last years." This was true. Every word of it true, and none of it sufficient, and Petra discovered that she could hold those two facts side by side without flinching, and the discovery frightened her more than the woman across the table.

"Location pending," Serah said softly. "Nine years pending. Your guild has been patient."

"We're a patient trade."

"What is your trade?"

"Memory services. Preservation. Appraisal." Petra folded her hands. "People bring us things that matter and we keep them safe or we tell them what they're worth. Sometimes both."

"Things that matter," Serah repeated. She let the phrase sit there. Then, mildly, as though changing weather: "Where were you born, Keeper Hartwell?"

"Caerlum. Saltgate parish."

"And your parents?"

"My father was a rope-maker. My mother died of the lung-rot when I was six."

"All true?"

Petra met her eyes. "You can check the parish rolls."

"Oh, I intend to check everything." Serah smiled—a small, correct, terrible smile. "That's rather the point of me."

Beside Petra, Wren had gone very still, the particular stillness of a boy listening to a lock he couldn't pick. When Serah's gaze slid to him, Petra's stomach tightened.

"And this is?"

"Wren. Our apprentice. He's mute."

He wasn't mute. He simply chose, most days, not to spend words on strangers. But Wren, bless him, understood the assignment; he stared at the table with the perfect blankness of a boy who had never had a thought in his life, and Serah studied him for a long moment and then, unexpectedly, looked away first.

"How many members does the guild currently have?" she asked.

"Five. Six with Odile."

"Seven were registered under the old charter."

"People die. Charters don't update themselves."

"No," Serah agreed. "Someone has to do the updating." She closed the folder. "Thank you for your candor, Keeper Hartwell. You've been remarkably helpful."

There it was again—the flicker behind the eyes, fast as a fish turning. And Petra, who had spent her whole life reading ledgers, reading people the same way she read entries—looking for the erasure, the smudge, the line written over an older line—caught it this time and held it.

Serah Vole was lying too. About something. In this room, to them, right now.

*Helpful,* she'd said. Nobody called you helpful unless you'd given them exactly what they wanted, and what Serah wanted was not customs fraud. Customs fraud was a coat the questions were wearing. Underneath, every single one of them had been the same question wearing different collars: *What do you remember? What do you keep? Who else remembers with you?*

"Will that be all?" Petra asked.

"For today." Serah rose, and the clerk rose, and somewhere in the walls a door unlocked itself for people who mattered. "The guild is free to go about its business. Do go about it quietly, though, Keeper. Caerlum is full of rumors this season—prayers answered, dead men walking out of harbors, snow in harvest month. Rumors make the Crown nervous. And when the Crown is nervous, it sends me." She gathered her folders. "I'd hate for our next conversation to be less comfortable than this one."

"It was comfortable?"

"No," Serah said, and for half a heartbeat something almost human crossed her face—weariness, maybe, or apology. "It never is. That's how I know when someone's lying well. The comfortable ones are the liars."

She walked out. The clerk followed. The lamps hissed.

In the silence, Tamsin exhaled through her teeth. "Well. That went—"

"Don't," Bramble said. "Don't jinx it. Walk. Talk later."

They walked. Out through corridors that smelled of ink and wet wool, past clerks who did not look up, into the grey morning where the cold had teeth in it now and the market bells rang wrong, flat, as if even bronze had caught the season's sickness.

Only when they were three streets from the Customhouse did Wren stop, tug Petra's sleeve, and hold up his slate.

He'd written while they walked, small letters, in his neat draftsman's hand:

*SHE HAS A LIST OF NAMES UNDER HER PAPERS. I SAW THE EDGE OF IT WHEN SHE MOVED THE FOLDERS. SEVEN NAMES. OURS ARE ON IT. AND ONE MORE AT THE BOTTOM, CROSSED OUT.*

"Crossed out," Petra said. "Whose?"

Wren shrugged and wiped the slate clean, and then, after a moment, wrote one more thing:

*THE CROSSED-OUT NAME WAS VESSANY.*

* * *

That night Petra sat in the archive with a lamp and the truth, and tried to make the two of them fit together, and failed.

She had lied. Not narrowly—fully, cleanly, five times in twenty minutes, and each lie had come out smooth and finished, like a coin struck from a die she hadn't known she carried. *She's mute. She wanders. I never saw the door.* She turned her hands over in the lamplight as if she might find the die stamped on her palms.

Her whole life she had believed the ledgers were the honest thing—the one honest thing—in a city full of bargains. Write it down, Master Corvane used to say, and it can't be unwritten. Truth was custody. Truth was the guild's whole reason for existing, older than its profits, older than its charter.

And today she had learned that she could abandon it in a warm room without her pulse rising, and that the person across from her had known, and had told her so to her face, and had let her walk out anyway.

*Why?*

Because Serah wanted them loose. Because a guild under watch tells you more than a guild in chains. Or because—

Petra pulled the sealed ledger from the vault drawer again, the one labeled *Vessany* in Corvane's hand, and set it on the desk, and did not open it. She'd tried twice. Wax that refused heat, that refused blades, that had cost Tamsin a broken knife and a bruised opinion of itself. Whatever Corvane had locked, he had locked it against his own people.

Against her.

Footsteps in the stairwell—slow, shuffling, familiar. Odile appeared in the doorway with her lamp, shawls trailing, and stood looking at the ledger on the desk with an expression Petra had never seen on her face before. It took her a moment to recognize it, because it belonged on someone young.

Fear.

"You went to Room Four today," Odile said.

"Yes."

"And you lied."

"Yes."

Odile came into the archive and lowered herself into the chair across the desk, joints crackling like green wood in fire. For a long time she said nothing. Then:

"When I was a girl in the fishmarket, there was a woman who sold eels, and she lied beautifully. Lied about prices, lies about weather, lies about whose child was whose. Everyone loved her." Odile's milky eyes fixed on Petra. "One day the bailiffs came asking about a smuggler, and she lied to them too, easily, kindly, and they hanged the wrong man for it, and she laughed about it at supper. And within the year she was lying to herself. Small things first. Then bigger ones. By the end she truly believed her drowned son had come home, because she'd told the story so many times the telling became the truth, and she'd sit at the harbor every evening waiting for a boy who was bones and silt." She leaned forward. "Lying isn't a coat, girl. It's a door. And doors don't care which way they open."

"I lied to protect this guild."

"You lied well. That's worse." Odile's hand crept across the desk and rested, light as ash, on the sealed ledger. "Do you know why Corvane locked this?"

"Tell me."

"He locked it because he knew a day would come when the questions would arrive, and he wanted to make sure that whatever we said under questioning—we couldn't accidentally say *this*." She tapped the wax seal once. "He didn't trust our loyalty, child. He trusted our ignorance. Ignorance keeps better than loyalty does. Loyalty cracks under pressure. Ignorance just goes quiet."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, the early snow ticked against the high windows, soft as fingers.

"But it's time you stopped being ignorant," Odile said. "Because the woman in Room Four is going to come back, and next time she won't ask about doors. Next time she'll ask about a country." Her hand withdrew. "And you'll need to decide, with your eyes open, whether the truth is something we keep—or something we spend."

She rose, gathered her shawls, and paused at the door.

"Come to my room tomorrow night," she said. "Bring the ledger. Bring your good memory and your bad new habit of lying, and bring nothing else." A pause. "Tomorrow night I'll tell you what we sold."

"What we sold," Petra repeated. "Not what was taken."

Odile smiled, and it was the saddest thing Petra had seen all winter.

"Nothing was ever taken from us, child. That was the whole trick of it."
