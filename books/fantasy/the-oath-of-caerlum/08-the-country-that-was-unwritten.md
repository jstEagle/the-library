## 8. The Country That Was Unwritten

Odile kept her room at the top of the guildhouse, behind a door that had no lock because she'd eaten the lock's name years ago—Bramble said it that way, *eaten it*, and nobody laughed, because with Odile you learned early not to assume metaphor.

Petra climbed the stairs on the third night after the fishwife's son walked out of the harbor, carrying a tray of bread and honey and a ledger under her arm like a shield. She told herself she was bringing supper. She had been telling herself that for two days.

The door was open. It was always open now. Odile sat in the window with her back to the room, watching the street below, where someone had chalked an old god's mark on the cobblestones and someone else had scrubbed at it until only a smear remained.

"You've been circling," Odile said without turning. "Like a moth that read a book about doors."

"I brought you supper."

"Bramble brought me supper. Twice." Odile turned then. She was small and brown as a walnut shell, with white hair cut short for practicality and eyes that were either the clearest in Caerlum or the most broken, depending on the hour. "You brought a ledger. I can smell the vellum from here. You think I don't know what that book is? I helped Corvane hide it before your mother was a rumor."

Petra set the tray down anyway, out of habit, and stood there with the ledger against her chest. "The Vessany ledger."

"Say it quieter. Names have weight. That's the first thing they teach you and the last thing anyone believes."

"The Vessany ledger," Petra repeated, barely above breath. "I found it in the vault. Master Corvane's hand. Sealed. And when I asked Master Ellery about it he looked at me like I'd asked him to dig up his own grave, which—" she stopped. Started again. "Which he may have done. In a way. I don't know. Nobody will tell me anything, Odile. They tell me everything else. They tell me how to bottle an echo and how to price a borrowed year of luck and what to do when a dead man's debt comes due at harvest. But not this."

Odile looked at her for a long moment. Then she patted the floor beside the window, an invitation so old-fashioned it was almost courtly.

Petra sat. The boards were cold through her skirt. Below them, Caerlum went on being Caerlum: lamplighters, drunks, a cart of winter turnips, ordinary life stacked up in layers like sediment. Somewhere in the fishmarket, people were still leaving offerings at the harbor edge. Small ones. Bread. Buttons. A child's shoe. As if the gods who'd started answering might be persuaded by volume.

"You want the truth," Odile said. "Everyone wants the truth until they have to carry it. Then they want a smaller one, one that fits in a pocket." She held out her hand. "Give me the book."

Petra gave it over.

Odile didn't open it. She just held it on her knees, both hands flat on the cover, the way you'd calm a nervous animal. "Corvane sealed this himself. Did you know that? Not the guild. Him. He said a record isn't a cage unless somebody means it to be one, and he meant this one to be a cage." Her thumb traced the wax seal's ghost, long since broken by Petra's careful knife. "He also said that someday a keeper would come asking, and if she asked with the whole city burning behind her eyes, we should answer her."

"And is the city burning?"

"Not yet." Odile smiled, and it was terrible. "But it's smoking. You saw it yourself. The fishwife's boy came home and half the city cheered and the other half went home and barred their doors, and every single one of them, cheering or barring, understood something had come back that they'd all agreed to forget." She looked out the window. "Forty years ago, girl, everyone in this country agreed to forget a country. And agreements like that need upkeep. Like a roof. Like a debt."

*Debt.* The word landed in Petra's chest and stayed there. Everything in her trade came back to debt eventually; she should have seen it sooner. "Tell me."

"All right." Odile closed her eyes. When she spoke again her voice changed—steadied, sharpened, the madwoman stepping aside for a moment to let the witness through. "I was born in Vessany. In a town called Hallowmere, on a river that doesn't have a name anymore because names die faster than towns. I was nine years old when the bargain was struck, and I remember my mother crying into bread dough, and I remember my father saying *it can't be legal*, and I remember the priests coming through with ledgers of their own, collecting our names like a tax."

Petra's hands had gone still in her lap. "Collected how?"

"By consent. That's the horror of it. Nobody conquered us. Nobody marched an army over the border, because there wasn't a border anymore by the time anyone thought to look for one. There was a famine of the spirit, do you understand? A plague was coming—the grey sweating sickness, the one that killed a third of the capital a century before you were born—and the capital's princes went looking for a cure that wasn't medicine. And they found the old gods." She opened her eyes. "Or rather, the old gods' creditors found them. Because that's what the old gods are, underneath the stories. Creditors. They hold the debts of the world, and the world has always been bad about paying."

"The capital bought a century without plague," Petra said slowly. It wasn't a question. Some part of her had been assembling the sentence for days, from fragments: the sealed ledger, Master Ellery's face, the way the oldest members went quiet whenever a certain word drifted near. "And paid with—"

"With Vessany. Its name, its memory, its place in the world's accounting. Every map redrawn. Every record burned or rewritten. Every mind touched—not emptied, nothing so clean—just *tilted*, so that thinking about Vessany felt like trying to remember a dream after waking, and then like trying to remember a dream you never had." Odile's voice stayed level, but her hands had begun to tremble on the ledger's cover. "A hundred years of health for the capital, purchased with forty years of forgetting for everyone else. The arithmetic was very good. That's what I want you to understand. It was *good*. Any guild worth its salt would have brokered it. Ours did."

The cold went through Petra all the way to the spine.

"Our guild," she said. "Caerlum brokered it."

"Corvane's master's master drew up the terms. Fair terms, as these things go—that's the joke, that's the filthy joke, the terms were *fair*. Both parties signed. The gods got their payment schedule. The crown got its century. And Vessany—" Odile laughed once, a dry sound like paper tearing, "—Vessany got to be a story nobody could finish. We weren't destroyed, Petra. We were *amortized*. Spread out across a century of payments. Except the payments weren't coin. They were remembering. Somebody had to keep paying attention to us, or the account would go into arrears, and the guild swore an oath to be those somebodies. To keep the ledgers. To keep the country alive in ink until the term ran out and the debt cleared."

"And did it run out?"

"It runs out soon. Next spring, by the old calendar. Which is why—" Odile tapped the ledger, twice, hard, "—why the gods have started answering prayers again. Why the drowned walk out of harbors. Why your impossible entries are appearing in books that haven't been printed yet. The term is ending. The account is waking up. And nobody, in forty years, ever planned for what happens when a creditor comes to collect on a country that almost nobody remembers paying attention to."

Petra sat with it. The city murmured below. A dog barked somewhere near the salt gate, and the sound seemed very far away, as if it belonged to another night entirely.

"But I remember," she said. "I know the name Vessany. Everyone in the guild does. If the forgetting took everyone—"

"It took the *country*, not the guild. That was the price's other half. Someone had to stand outside the forgetting and keep the records straight, or the whole arrangement would collapse the moment the last Vessany-born died. So the guild's founders wrote themselves into the bargain as custodians. Immune. Bound. Doomed." Odile's mouth twisted. "Every keeper since has carried a country in her head that she's never seen. You think Corvane loved those ledgers because he was a fussy old man? He loved them because they were the only place his mother's homeland existed. He used to read the inventory aloud on the longest night of the year. *One jar, containing the echo of a wedding bell, Hallowmere.* He cried doing it. He thought none of us knew."

Petra thought of Master Corvane—dead three years now, gone quietly in his chair with a pen still inked in his hand—and felt grief arrive for a home she had never had, for a river with no name, for a woman kneading tears into bread dough thirty years before Petra was born. It was a strange grief, secondhand and enormous. It didn't fit inside her. She let it sit there anyway, misshapen, hers.

"Why am I immune?" she asked. "I'm not guild-born. I came at fourteen off a farm with nothing but fast handwriting."

"Because the oath passes to whoever keeps the ledgers. Blood doesn't matter. Custody matters." Odile finally opened the book, gently, to the first page, where Corvane's hand marched across the vellum in columns: dates, items, valuations, all of it meticulous, all of it alive in a way that made Petra's chest ache. "And custody is about to matter more than it ever has. Look at the last entries. The ones in your hand that you didn't write."

Petra looked. She knew them by heart already—a door, dated next spring, recorded in her own looping script as though she'd sat down and written it herself. She had started to wonder, in the small hours, whether some future version of her would travel backward through time to do it. Odile disabused her of that in four words.

"That's not you. That's the ledger writing itself. The account is waking, and accounts keep their own books when their clerks fall asleep." She snapped the cover shut. "The gods aren't answering prayers out of kindness, girl. They're answering out of hunger. Forty years of silence, and the interest compounding, and the final payment due—and the final payment, the way these bargains close out, is usually the thing itself. The country. Returned. Or else—"

"Or else?"

"Or else collected some other way. Gods don't forgive default. They repossess." Odile stood, suddenly, with the quickness that startled people who thought of her as frail. She went to the window and looked down at the chalk-smear on the cobbles, at the little offerings accumulating at the harbor edge visible even from here, a scatter of pale shapes like gulls. "There's a door, Petra. Out of Vessany. There always was—it's written in the original terms, page one, clause eleven, the mercy clause, the founders insisted on it. A way back for anyone who kept faith with the memory. It opens when the term closes. It opens *only* for the remembered." She turned. Her eyes were clear. Completely, frighteningly clear. "And in six months' time, the number of people in this city who remember Vessany will be exactly the number of people in this room."

Petra's mouth was dry. "Then we're the payment."

"No. We're the *custodians*. The payment was made forty years ago, in advance, by everyone who agreed to forget." Odile crossed the room and put her small brown hands on either side of Petra's face, and her grip was strong as a root. "We're the witnesses. And witnesses, child, are the most valuable and most murdered class of person in history. Which is why Corvane hid this book. Which is why I'm telling you now, while the smoke is still smoke and not yet fire: the inquisitor who's coming—the one the whispers say rides a grey horse—she isn't hunting fraud. She's hunting *rememberers*. The crown's century is nearly up and they know it, and they would rather burn the last page of the account than pay what's owed."

"How do you know she's coming?"

"Because I remember things before they happen. It's the least useful kind of madness and the only kind I've got." Odile let go of her face and stepped back, and the clarity drained out of her eyes like water through sand, leaving the familiar soft confusion behind. She blinked at Petra, at the ledger, at the tray of untouched bread. "Did Bramble send you up? There was supper, I think. Someone was kind enough. Sit, sit, you look half-starved."

Petra gathered the tray with shaking hands. At the door she paused.

"Odile. The town you were born in. Hallowmere. What happened to it?"

The old woman smiled, distant and sweet, a girl of nine surfacing for one moment through seventy years of exile.

"It's waiting," she said. "That's the thing nobody understands about unwritten countries. They don't die. They just wait for someone to write them down again."

Petra went down the stairs with grief and terror walking beside her like siblings, and at the bottom, in the dark of the entrance hall, Master Ellery was waiting with a lantern and a face like ash.

"You told her," he said—not a question, and not to Petra. Behind him, in the doorway, snow was beginning to fall.

It was harvest month. It had never once, in living memory, snowed in harvest month.

Ellery looked past Petra, out into the falling white, and said, very quietly, "Winter's early. God help us, winter's early, and they're already praying."
