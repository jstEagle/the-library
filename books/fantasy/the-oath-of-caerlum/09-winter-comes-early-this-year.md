## 9. Winter Comes Early This Year

The snow came down on the harvest market like an insult.

Bramble stood in the doorway of the guild kitchen with a sack of flour under one arm and watched it fall—fat, deliberate flakes, the kind that meant business, the kind that came in the twelfth month and stayed until you forgot what mud looked like. Except it was the twenty-third day of Harvestmonth, and the turnips were still in the ground, and the apples hadn't finished, and every farmer within forty miles of Caerlum was standing in his ruined field right now doing arithmetic that ended in hunger.

"Close the door," Tamsin said from behind her, not looking up from her forgery table. "You're letting the cold eat my ink."

"It's snowing," Bramble said.

"I have eyes."

"In Harvestmonth."

Tamsin set down her pen. She looked at the window, at the white sky, at the flakes coming down past the glass with the patience of something that had all the time in the world. "Well," she said. "That's not weather. That's a message."

Everything was a message now. That was the trouble with gods coming back: they didn't come back quietly. They came back the way debtors did, knocking on every door, holding out their hands.

---

Bramble had cooked for the Guild of Caerlum for nineteen years, which made her, by seniority, the third-oldest member and by temperament the only one who understood that revolutions, miracles, and catastrophes all ran better on hot food. She had fed Master Corvane through his last winter. She had fed Odile through three of hers. She had fed Petra Hartwell since the girl was nine years old and small enough to fit inside the bread oven, a fact Bramble mentioned at least twice a season because it never stopped being funny.

She knew how to read a city the way she knew how to read a rising dough: by feel, by smell, by the little failures that came before the big ones.

The city was failing.

It started small. The fishmarket prayer—the drowned boy walking out of the harbor with his mother's name in his mouth—had happened eleven days ago, and by the third day afterward there were prayers everywhere. Not quiet prayers, either, not the private muttering of the pious. Loud ones. Desperate ones. A man on the Salt Bridge shouting the names of old gods into the river until soldiers dragged him off. Women leaving bowls of milk on their stoops for deities whose names they only half remembered, getting the syllables wrong, hoping the gods would accept the intention the way a shopkeeper accepts a clipped coin.

And the gods—hungry, forty years unpaid, waking up with their mouths already open—said yes.

That was the part nobody had figured out yet, except maybe Odile, who said very little these days except when she stood at the window at night watching the snow and murmuring, *they're counting, they're counting.* The gods were saying yes to everything. Sick children healed. Bad wells ran sweet. A gambler won eleven hands straight and then dropped dead of nothing at all, and when they opened him up—so the story went in the kitchens, and kitchens know everything—there wasn't anything wrong with him except that he'd stopped.

Payment, Bramble thought, kneading dough harder than it needed. Nothing comes free. Every cook knows that. You want the feast, somebody pays for the ox.

---

By the fifth day of snow the grain price tripled and the market half emptied, because farmers who could still get to town refused to sell what might be the last food of their lives. By the seventh day the bakeries had lines before dawn, and by the eighth day the lines had opinions.

Bramble went out for salt on the ninth morning and heard the word *foreigner* used as a sentence for the first time.

It was said about a spice-seller named Osk, a Vessic-born man—or what passed for Vessic-born now, forty years after the country stopped being on maps—who had lived above his shop since before Bramble came to Caerlum. He was being pressed against his own doorframe by four men who looked like dockworkers and talked like a mob, and the word kept coming out of them like spit. *Foreigner. Foreigner. Remembering too much. Asking too many questions. Praying to the wrong names.*

Because that was the other thing happening, underneath the panic like a stone under snow. The royal criers had been through the streets two days running with a proclamation: *the Crown reminds loyal subjects that certain cults proscribed in the year of the Reconciliation remain proscribed; persons possessing documents, records, or memories pertaining to erased territories are instructed to present them at the Question House.* Memories. They wanted people to hand in their memories, or at least pretend they didn't have any.

Osk's attackers hadn't waited for instructions. Mobs rarely do. Bramble was old and wide and carried a walking stick she'd never needed, and she walked straight into the middle of it the way she'd walk into her own kitchen.

"That man has sold me saffron for fifteen years," she announced, in the voice she used when the scullery boys were fighting over the good knives. "He's more Caerlum than any of you, because unlike you lot I've actually seen his accounts." She fixed the nearest one with a stare perfected over decades of catching apprentices stealing cream. "Go home. Snow's coming down worse."

They went. Mobs, like apprentices, respond badly to authority but worse to its absence.

Osk leaned against his doorframe, shaking. His shop behind him was wrecked—jars broken, saffron ground into the floorboards, his ledgers torn open and left bleeding ink across the counter.

"They took my grandmother's book," he said. "Prayer-book. Old country words. They said—" He stopped. Started again. "They said remembering is treason now."

"No," Bramble said, and put her hand flat on his chest, the way she'd steady a pan. "Remembering is *dangerous*. That's different. Treason they can hang you for. Dangerous just means you have to be smart about when." She helped him pick up the jars. "Come by the guild tonight. We'll feed you. We're good at feeding people the world's decided to forget."

She said it lightly. She meant it completely. And she noticed, carrying her salt home through the thickening snow, that she'd just invited a hunted man to the most hunted address in Caerlum without a second thought, and that some traitorous warm corner of her heart had done the math and found the risk acceptable, because Osk was one of theirs now—one of the family you keep anyway—and family ate first.

* * *

That night the guild supper was the biggest in months and the quietest too.

Word had gone round somehow—word always found the guild the way water finds low ground—and they came: Osk the spice-seller, the widow Fen with her twin boys, the lamplighter who'd lost his route when the oil ran short, two sisters who mended nets and prayed to a sea-god with no face, a clerk who'd smuggled his own mother's funeral ledger out of the Question House archives with his hat pulled low. Eleven extra mouths. Bramble stretched a barley stew with root vegetables and the last of the smoked ham bone, baked forty loaves instead of twelve, and told everyone the same thing she told Petra: sit down, stop apologizing, pass your bowl.

Petra sat at the end of the long table with the newest ledger open beside her plate, because the girl genuinely did not know how to exist without a book in reach, writing in her small exact hand between spoonfuls. Bramble caught fragments. *Snow, harvest month. Grain at 9 silver. Prayers answered: 40 this week by Odile's count, likely 200 actual. Payment observed: 3 deaths, 1 illness, 1 luck reversed.* The girl was keeping score of the apocalypse in double-entry.

"You should eat while it's hot," Bramble said, setting down the bread.

"After this entry."

"The dead aren't going anywhere. Neither are the entries. The bread will go cold, and that's a tragedy I take personally."

Petra almost smiled, wrote one more line, and shut the book. Bramble counted that as a victory and moved on to refilling bowls.

At the far end, Odile sat wrapped in three shawls though the fire was high, staring at the window where the snow came down without pause, and the boy Wren—the one who talked to locks, who was fourteen and all elbows and secrets—sat next to her listening to whatever the locks had told him today.

"The Question House doors are unhappy," Wren reported to the table, apropos of nothing, in the tone other boys used for reporting football scores. "All of them. They say there's too much paper inside. Paper screaming. They want out."

"Eat your soup," Bramble said.

"The south gate lock says soldiers doubled. It doesn't like the new ones. New locks are always rude."

"*Soup.*"

He grinned at her—gapped teeth, grease-stained chin—and obeyed, and Bramble felt the whole table loosen a degree, because that was the boy's real gift, though he didn't know it: not the locks. The loosening. A room with Wren in it remembered how to breathe.

Tamsin raised her cup. "A toast," she said. "To the weather. May it be a freak occurrence and not, as our eldest seems to believe, an invoice."

Odile turned from the window. Her eyes were pale and clear as ice water, and when she spoke her voice was thin but level. "Harvest snow is an invoice," she said. "Forty years ago we bought a century without plague. The coin that paid was a country. Now the country's coming back, and it wants its change counted." She looked around the table, at the strangers and strays and guild-folk, and Bramble saw her arrive somewhere and decide to say it. "Every answered prayer is a withdrawal. And the account is empty. So the bank takes collateral now." She touched her own wrist, where the veins showed blue and old. "Eat. Sleep. Love each other loudly. Winter is going to check what we've been keeping."

Silence. Then Osk, the spice-seller, quietly: "My grandmother used to say that. Before the erasure. *Love each other loudly.*" His voice cracked on *grandmother*, and the widow Fen reached over and gripped his hand, and Bramble stood up and started ladling seconds because if she didn't move she was going to cry into the stew pot and ruin the seasoning.

* * *

On the eleventh day of snow, the payment came to their street.

Not to the guild—to the widow Marta two doors down, the one who'd prayed so hard in Harvestmonth for her sick husband that the words wore grooves in her doorstone. Her husband woke on the ninth day healthy as a spring lamb, weeping with gratitude, telling everyone who'd listen that the old god of thresholds had answered him, that he owed everything, everything.

On the eleventh day, their daughter Annet—sixteen, strong, funny, forever underfoot in Bramble's kitchen stealing plums—did not wake up.

No wound. No sickness. Warm as bread and empty as a bell, the physician said, and could offer nothing else, because there was nothing else. The girl was simply *gone*, the way a candle is gone when someone else needs the flame.

Bramble stood in the crowd outside Marta's house and held the weeping mother up with both arms and felt the fist close around the whole city. Around her, the neighbors weren't comforting. They were *counting*. She could see it in their faces, the terrible new arithmetic: *what did they pray for? what did it cost? what have I asked for lately? what do I owe?* A woman near the wall was whispering, over and over, *I only asked for rain for the fields, I only asked for rain,* as if rain might be billed separately.

And further down the street, where the crowd thinned, a man in grey stood taking notes. Not a soldier. Grey coat, plain boots, eyes that moved like a bailiff's over a defaulting debtor's books. When he saw Bramble looking at him, he didn't look away. He wrote something down, and Bramble had cooked for liars, spies, thieves, and inquisitors' informants long enough to know exactly what kind of note a man writes when he's seen a guild cook holding up a grieving woman: *witness. connected. return.*

She walked home fast through the snow, breath burning, and let herself into the guild kitchen, and put her hands flat on the flour-dusted table, and stood there shaking—not with grief, grief could wait its turn, but with the cold clean knowledge of what was coming.

They would come for the guild. Soon. Because the guild remembered, and the guild kept records, and records were the one thing in Caerlum worth more than bread right now.

Petra found her there. Of course she did. The girl appeared in the doorway with snow melting in her dark hair, took one look at Bramble's face, and asked nothing—which was its own kind of love—but said, "Odile says the snow stops tomorrow night. For one hour. And during that hour, she says, we need to talk about Vessany. All of us. Everything."

"Then we'll need a bigger pot," Bramble said, and reached for her apron, because the world was tightening like a fist around them all, and the only thing she knew how to do inside a closing fist was make sure everyone got fed first.

Behind her, out in the white dark, the bells of the Question House began to ring—not the hour, not the alarm, but the third thing, the one old-timers called *the gathering peal*, and hadn't sounded in forty years.

Somewhere across the city, doors were being knocked on.

Bramble counted the rings, the way Odile counted prayers, and reached for the biggest knife she owned.
