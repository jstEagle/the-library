## 10. A Debt Collected in Bone

The knock came at half past midnight, and it was wrong.

I don't mean it was loud. I mean it had the cadence of someone who didn't know how to knock—three raps, a pause too long, then two more, like the hand doing it was remembering the shape of the gesture from a description. Bramble heard it first. He was already in the hall with a poker when I got to the door, and behind him Tamsin stood at the top of the stairs with a candle she hadn't bothered to pretend was anything but a signal.

"It's the Widow Sarelle," I said, because I could see her through the frosted glass, or the shape of her—a stooped silhouette swaying slightly, like something moored.

"At this hour?" Bramble muttered.

"At this hour," I said, "you knock like that when it's bad."

I opened the door. The cold came in with her, harvest-month cold that had no business existing, and so did the smell of the fishmarket—brine and smoke and under it something sweeter, like flowers left too long in water.

Widow Sarelle lived two doors down from the guildhall her whole life, which meant she'd spent forty years not seeing us, the way everyone on our street had learned not to see us. She sold eels. She had a daughter named Maren who was eleven and loud and once threw a rock through our window by accident and paid for it with a month of swept steps, giggling the whole time.

Maren wasn't with her.

"Widow Sarelle," I said gently. "Come in. Bramble, the kettle."

She let me take her elbow. Her arm was cold as a ledger's spine. She came into the front room the way people come into churches they've only ever passed outside, looking up at everything, touching nothing, and when Bramble pressed a cup of tea into her hands she held it without drinking, staring into it like it might show her something.

"Petra Hartwell," she said. My full name. Nobody on this street used my full name; they called me *the guild girl* or *little keeper* or, when Maren was being contrite after the window, *miss.* Hearing it now was like hearing a lock click. "You keep accounts."

"I keep ledgers, yes."

"Debts." Her voice was very steady, which is how you can tell when someone is holding themselves together with both hands. "You know about debts."

I looked at Tamsin. Tamsin looked at the ceiling. This was the conversation we had all been avoiding for two weeks, since the fishwife's son walked out of the harbor wringing seawater out of his shirt, since the snow fell in the harvest month, since the prayers started getting answered like invoices finally being processed by a clerk forty years late.

"Everyone knows about debts, Widow Sarelle," I said carefully.

"Not everyone." She set down the cup. Her hands were shaking now, now that she didn't need them steady. "Not everyone knows what happens after. Everyone saw Josiah come out of the water. Nobody asked what he owed." She swallowed. "Nobody asked what the *asking* cost."

The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs the boy who talks to locks—I could hear him faintly, murmuring to the bolt on the attic window, telling it a story, keeping it calm. Even the locks were nervous lately. Even the locks knew.

"Tell me what happened," I said.

She told it plainly, which was the worst way to hear it. No embellishment, no drama, just a mother's account delivered like a market list.

Maren had been sick since the first snow. Not badly—feverish, coughing, the kind of illness that runs through children every winter and leaves them thin and cross and fine. But the winter was coming early and hard, and Sarelle had watched the fever climb for three nights, and on the third night, with Maren shivering and muttering, Sarelle had done what her own grandmother had taught her and half the old women of Caerlum were suddenly doing: she went down to the water at low tide, and she prayed.

Not to the Nine. The Nine had temples and tithes and priests who took your coin and gave you back ceremony. Sarelle prayed the old way, the way you pray to something with no name because naming it would be an invitation. She knelt in the wet sand and she said: *Take this fever from my daughter. Take it, whatever you are, whoever you were, and I will owe you.*

"You said *I will owe you,*" I repeated. My mouth had gone dry. "Those words?"

"Those words." She nodded slowly. "My grandmother's words. She said the old gods don't want worship. They want acknowledgment. A debt spoken aloud is a debt they can collect." She looked at me then, directly, and her eyes were red-rimmed and terribly clear. "The fever broke before dawn. Maren sat up asking for breakfast. I thought—" Her voice cracked for the first time. "I thought I'd been given a gift."

* * *

They found Maren's body at dawn on the breakwater, and I'm going to write down what I saw because the ledgers exist so that things are not smoothed over by retelling, and because if I don't write it while my hands still shake, I'll wake up tomorrow having made it gentler, and it must not be made gentle.

She wasn't drowned. That was the first wrong thing. She was dry. Dry as paper, dry as the inside of a sealed jar, lying on the stones of the breakwater in her nightgown with her arms crossed over her chest—not laid out that way, *crossed that way*, fingers interlaced like a child playing patience, like something had arranged her with a care that was worse than violence.

Her eyes were open. There was frost on her eyelashes, though the air wasn't freezing enough, and when the harbor watch turned her over—and I was there, I'll never know why I was there, except that I think some part of me had been walking toward the harbor since Sarelle knocked—the skin of her face came away with the frost, lightly, like the bloom off a plum.

No. I won't write that. Some things even a ledger doesn't need.

What matters is this: there was no mark on her. No water in her lungs. The watch surgeon said her heart had simply stopped, sometime between midnight and dawn, between the moment her fever broke and the moment her mother woke to find her bed empty and the window open. And tucked into her folded hands, dry despite the spray, was a single dried eel-skin, curled like a signature.

Sarelle sold eels. The god had taken payment in kind.

I stood on the breakwater in the grey light with the crowd around me murmuring, and I understood, finally, completely, with the whole of myself, what Odile had been trying to tell us in her scattered way for weeks: *they're not coming home, girl, they're coming to collect.* We had all of us—the fishwives, the desperate mothers, half the city—been treating the answered prayers as miracles. As the past reaching out kindly. As the forgotten gods remembering us with fondness.

They weren't remembering. They were *billing.*

Forty years ago Vessany's name was sold, and its gods went unpaid—hungry, patient, sealed behind a country that no longer existed. Now the seal was thinning, and everything leaking through was leak-shaped: cold, precise, and owed. Every prayer answered created a debt, and the gods collected those debts according to their own arithmetic, which had never once included mercy. Sarelle had asked for her daughter's fever to be taken. The god had taken the fever. It had never promised to leave the rest of Maren behind while it did so.

Wonder died on that breakwater, in me, permanently. Whatever was coming through the thinning places in the world, it wasn't salvation. It was hunger with excellent manners.

* * *

We buried Maren two days later, and the whole street came, and half the street prayed afterward, and I stood at the edge of the churchyard wanting to scream *don't, don't say it aloud, don't speak debts into the air*—and then I caught sight of Serah Vole's grey horse tied outside the tavern across the lane, and the inquisitor herself watching the funeral with a list in her hand, and I understood we were caught between two hungers: the gods' and the Crown's, and both kept immaculate records.

That night I went down into the archive alone. I pulled every ledger entry mentioning answered prayers since the fishmarket, and I found the pattern within the hour, because patterns are what I'm for: every answered prayer had a collection attached, dated within three days. Fever taken, daughter taken. Drowned son returned, and the fishwife's memory of her husband's face gone blank as fresh paper when I asked about him. Luck borrowed, and the borrower's shadow missing from his doorstep at noon.

And beside each entry, in handwriting I am beginning to suspect is mine and is certainly not yet, the same notation, small and neat:

*Paid in full. Balance pending.*

Balance pending. The gods weren't finished with Caerlum. They were working down a list.

At the bottom of the newest page, in that impossible hand, one line more:

*The guild owes forty years of interest. Bring the ledger to the water tonight, keeper, or we will choose what settles it.*

I closed the book. My hands were steady now, which frightened me more than shaking. Somewhere above me, the family was setting supper, arguing gently about whether to flee before winter closed the roads, lighting candles against a dark that had teeth in it now. And I stood in the vault holding a debt forty years old with my name written into it, and I knew exactly what I had to do, and I knew it would cost more than a daughter's fever, and I picked up the lamp and headed for the harbor anyway.
