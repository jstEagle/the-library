## 11. The Forger's Confession

Tamsin didn't come down to supper.

That alone would have been strange. Tamsin came down to supper the way tides came in—unfailingly, complaining, drawn by forces older than herself. But Bramble had set out six bowls instead of seven and said nothing, which meant she knew something, which meant everyone knew something except Petra, which was becoming a pattern she liked less every day.

She found Tamsin in her workroom on the third floor, the one with the north-facing window that never got direct light because direct light was bad for ink and worse for lies. Tamsin sat at her desk with a magnifying loupe pushed up on her forehead like a second eye, and she was not working. She was sitting very still over a piece of paper, the way you sit over a wound.

"Tamsin."

"Don't." She didn't look up. "Whatever you're going to ask, don't. I'm having a moment of professional crisis and I'd like to enjoy it privately before it becomes an actual crisis."

Petra came in anyway and closed the door. The workroom smelled of iron-gall ink and beeswax and the particular sourness of vinegar used to age paper. Every surface was covered with documents in various stages of being true: bills of lading, letters of passage, a marriage certificate for a couple who had died before Petra was born but whose granddaughter wanted very much to exist. Tamsin's craft had always struck Petra as a kind of mercy. She gave people paperwork for lives they should have had.

On the desk, under Tamsin's motionless hands, lay a bill of sale.

It was beautiful. Of course it was beautiful; everything Tamsin made was beautiful. The vellum had the right tooth, the ink had oxidized to exactly the brown of forty-year-old oak gall, the seal was flawless—the double stag of Vessany, antlers interlaced, pressed deep enough to have cracked naturally at the left edge. It was dated three years before the Unwriting. And it was signed by Serah Vole's mother.

"I know that name now," Petra said quietly. "From Odile's telling. Maren Vole."

"Maren Vole bought a loom," Tamsin said. "In 1189. From a man named Aldous Ferren, who was real, whose shop was real, whose signature I copied from a genuine document I obtained through channels I will not describe while you are standing there looking at me like that." She finally looked up. Her eyes were red-rimmed and furious, mostly at herself. "I made this eleven years ago, Petra. Eleven. Before Serah Vole had a title. Before anyone was hunting anybody. I made it for a scared girl of nineteen who came to my stall in the Shambles with a handful of coins and a story about needing proof of inheritance from a country that doesn't exist, and I took her money and I made her mother buy a loom."

Petra felt the floor tilt slightly, the way it did when a ledger refused to balance. "Serah came to *you*?"

"Serah came to me." Tamsin laughed, and it was an awful sound. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? The woman who walked into our common room with royal writ and a list of names—she has *my* forgery. She has carried my work in her satchel for eleven years. It is probably the most precious thing she owns. And if she ever looks at it closely enough—if she ever runs her thumb along that seal crack and realizes cracks like that don't happen naturally, they happen because I score the wax with a wire before pressing—"

"Then she knows where it came from."

"Then she knows where it came from." Tamsin pushed the loupe off her forehead and dropped it on the desk. "And here is the part I've been chewing on since the fishmarket, since the harbor, since Odile started talking: I didn't make one document for her. I made *nine*. Over four years. Inheritance claims, land deeds, a christening record so she could prove she'd been born somewhere. She paid me in installments. She was poor, Petra. She was a dock clerk's daughter with no country and a head full of memories nobody else had, and everyone told her she was mad, and she saved up coppers for months at a time to buy pieces of paper that said she wasn't."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, Bramble was banging a pot, calling supper for the sixth time.

"She remembers," Petra said slowly. "Serah Vole remembers Vessany."

"Of course she remembers. That's what she's been hunting all along—not the guild, not the door. Proof. She wants someone to tell her she isn't insane. And when no one would—" Tamsin spread her hands. "She became the thing that makes other people admit it. An inquisitor. You corner a person long enough, they'll confess to anything, even a country."

Petra thought of Serah in the interrogation room, her cold precision, the list of names. Thought of her asking about ledgers with something that hadn't quite been hunger in her voice. Hunger had a shape. This had been closer to thirst.

"You have to tell her."

"I have to do *nothing* of the sort—"

"If she finds out some other way, she'll assume we used it against her. Eleven years, Tamsin. If she learns the truth as an attack, we lose her forever. If she hears it as a confession—" Petra stopped, hearing herself. "*As a confession.* Gods. That's what this is. That's why you're sitting here like this. You weren't deciding whether to tell her. You already decided. You're deciding whether you get to be forgiven."

Tamsin's jaw worked. For a long moment she looked less like the guild's sharpest tongue and more like what she'd been at nineteen, selling forged letters outside the fishmarket to people who needed their pasts back.

"There were others," she said finally, barely audible. "Not just Serah. Dock workers. Old women. A man who swore he could still sing songs in a language I couldn't verify. They'd find each other somehow, the rememberers, and they'd come to me, and I'd sell them their childhoods back one page at a time. I told myself it was commerce. It wasn't commerce. It was—" She exhaled. "It was the same thing you do with your ledgers. Holding something in place. Except mine was fake, and yours might be the only real thing left, and I can't tell anymore which side of that line I've been standing on."

Petra picked up the bill of sale. The vellum was warm from Tamsin's hands. Somewhere under her ribs, the impossible ledger entries whispered—*a door, next spring*—and she understood suddenly that this document, fake as sin, was doing the same work as those entries. Keeping a country alive by insisting on it.

"No clean sides," Petra said. "Just tangled ones. Come downstairs. Eat something. And then tomorrow we're going to write Serah Vole a letter."

"A letter?"

"The guild trades in impossible things. Let's trade her the only one she ever actually wanted."

* * *

They wrote the letter together at the kitchen table after midnight, while the snow kept falling in harvest month and Bramble pretended not to listen from the stove.

Petra insisted on honesty as structure: plain words, no forgery, Tamsin's own hand, which Serah would not recognize but which would carry the texture of truth anyway. Nine documents, itemized, dates and prices. The last line Petra wrote herself:

*You were never mad. We have records. Some of them are even real.*

"How do we get it to her?" Tamsin asked. "Walk up to the Question House and hand it to the guards? 'Excuse me, sirs, delivery for the inquisitor, from the criminals she's hunting'?"

"We don't deliver it." Petra folded the pages. "We let it be found. Which means someone has to be caught carrying it."

The silence around the table went very wide. Bramble turned from the stove. Wick, the boy who talked to locks, looked up from his bowl with the expression of someone about to volunteer, and three people started talking at once—

Which was when the knock came.

Four knocks. Measured. Official. At this hour, in this weather, there was only one kind of visitor it could be, and every plan they had just written was suddenly, catastrophically premature.

Wick was already moving toward the door—not the front door, the little one behind the salt barrels—and Petra was already reaching for the ledger, and through the wood of the front door came a voice they all knew, cold and clear and carrying something underneath it none of them had heard before:

Something that sounded almost like hope.
