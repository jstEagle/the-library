## 13. Arrest at the Salt Gate

Petra heard it before she understood it: bells. Not the harbor bells that marked the tides, not the cathedral's slow bronze hour—these were the quick iron bells of the watch, rung in threes, and they were coming from the direction of the Salt Gate.

She was in the archive with the Vessany ledger open under her hands when the sound reached her, and she had learned enough in the last month to know that when the city rang its alarm bells at midmorning on a day with no fire and no flood, someone was being taken.

She ran down three flights of stairs two at a time, through the counting room where Tamsin was already standing at the window, through the kitchen where Bramble dropped a wooden spoon and didn't stop to pick it up. The boy, Wren, was faster than all of them. He went out the side door like water finding a crack, and by the time Petra reached the street he was gone.

The Salt Gate was four streets away. They could hear it from the guildhouse steps: the ring of boots on cobbles, a voice calling for order in the flat official tone that never once meant order, and beneath it all, rising over everything, an old woman's voice—cracked, clear, absolutely unafraid—singing something in a language Petra did not know but recognized anyway. It was the same language as the ledger entries that should not exist. The language of the door.

Odile was singing in Vessany.

They pushed through the crowd gathered along the fishmongers' row. Serah Vole sat her grey horse at the mouth of the gate like a drawn line, and six soldiers stood around a cart where Odile had been put—not thrown, Petra noticed, but seated, wrapped in a soldier's cloak against the cold, as though even the men carrying out the arrest couldn't quite bring themselves to treat her like a criminal. She was ninety-one years old and half-mad and she had walked into the market every morning for forty years to buy herring no one could prove she ever ate, and today the soldiers had come for her because her name was on a list.

"Vessany," Odile sang, to the tune of a lullaby, to no one, to everyone. "Vessany, my green country, my salt-country, my—"

"Hold your tongue," said one of the soldiers, young, miserable.

"I held it forty years," Odile said pleasantly. "It's full."

Serah Vole turned her head and looked directly across the crowd, directly at Petra, and Petra understood in one cold instant that this was not a random arrest. This was a message. *I know who you are. I know what you keep. Come and discuss terms.*

Then the cart rolled through the gate, the bells stopped, and the crowd began, horribly, to disperse—to go back to their herring and their bread and their fear—as if nothing had happened at all.

* * *

Bramble called the meeting in the kitchen, because Bramble believed that no decision worth making could be made without bread in reach. By late afternoon everyone who mattered was crowded around the long table: Tamsin with her ink-stained fingers pressed flat against the wood; old Hesper the retired thief, who had come out of retirement twice already this winter and resented both occasions; Marta the forger's apprentice—no, Petra corrected herself, Tamsin was the forger, Marta was the honest one who kept the accounts straight—and Wren, twelve years old, sitting at the far end with his boots not touching the floor and his eyes bright with something Petra recognized too late as purpose.

"They'll question her," Bramble said. He had his arms crossed over his apron like a man armoring himself. "In the Question House. And Odile is many things, but quiet under pressure is not one of them."

"She won't break," Tamsin said. "She doesn't have anything left to protect except us, which is exactly why she'd let them break her instead."

"She'll give them Caerlum inside a day," Hesper said flatly. "Not because she wants to. Because they'll ask her questions in a way that makes her want to answer. That's what the Question House is. It isn't pain. It's patience." He had been there once, thirty years ago, for a theft so petty the statute had since been repealed. He did not talk about it. The shape of his silence talked about it constantly.

"So we get her out," Petra said.

The word landed in the room like a dropped knife. Everyone looked at it. Nobody picked it up.

"Petra," Bramble said gently, in the voice he used for telling children the truth about dead pets. "Six soldiers took her in daylight, in front of a hundred witnesses. The Question House sits inside the garrison compound behind walls older than the guild. You're proposing we walk into the seat of royal authority and take a prisoner out."

"I'm proposing," Petra said, "that we don't let her die in there for remembering."

"And then what?" Hesper spread his hands. "We flee tonight, now, while the trail is cold. We take the ledgers and we go north, or east, anywhere, and we let the old woman be the price of the rest of us surviving. She'd tell us to. She has told us to. She wrote it down last month, in case—" He stopped.

"In case it came to this," Petra finished.

"She wrote her own abandonment clause," Tamsin said quietly. "That's the most Odile thing I've ever heard."

"It's the most sensible thing I've ever heard," said Hesper.

"It's cowardice with good penmanship," said Wren.

Every head turned. Wren almost never spoke in meetings. He spoke to locks, to hinges, to the secret mechanisms of the city's oldest buildings, in a murmur nobody else could hear, and the locks answered him. He was looking at the table now, drawing a circle in spilled flour with one finger.

"There's a gate," he said. "Under the Question House. Old one, from before the garrison, from before the cathedral maybe. Iron, but old iron, and old iron listens. There's a culvert runs from it out past the tanneries. I've been through it. Twice."

"You've been *through* the culvert under the garrison," Bramble said.

"The first time was an accident."

"And the second?"

Wren shrugged. "I wanted to see if it would still open."

The silence that followed had a different texture than before. Petra watched her family look at each other—the cook and the thief and the forger and the apprentice—and saw the fracture happen in real time, clean as a bone breaking. Flight was sane. Flight was survivable. Flight meant living, diminished, somewhere the writ of the crown still reached, running until they died tired.

Rescue was madness. Rescue was also the only thing any of them actually wanted.

"We vote," Petra said.

"No," said Hesper. "You don't vote on this. If you vote, the people who say yes are bound, and the people who say no are shamed, and either way this family cracks down a seam that won't close. We decide separately. Anyone who wants out leaves tonight with a share of the ledgers and no blame. Anyone who stays—" he looked around the table, at the faces of people he had stolen with and cooked for and lied to and loved for longer than some of them had been alive—"anyone who stays does it knowing exactly what it costs."

Nobody moved toward the door.

That was its own answer, and everybody knew it, and Hesper closed his eyes briefly as if pained by how predictable love made people.

* * *

Tamsin found Petra in the archive an hour after midnight, sitting with the Vessany ledger open and the lamp low.

"He's already gone, you know," Tamsin said.

"Wren?"

"Left from the scullery window an hour ago. Took his kit. The wax picks, the little mirror on the wire." Tamsin leaned against the shelf. "Left a note. It's addressed to you."

The note was written on the back of a storeroom inventory slip, in Wren's careful, lock-taught hand:

*Gate opens from the culvert side. Culvert entrance behind the third tannery, under the broken arch. I can hold the gate open from inside until moonset. Don't be late. Don't bring anyone who hesitates. — W.*

P.S. *I know nobody approved. That's why I'm doing it now, before you could say no properly.*

Petra read it twice. Then she laughed—a short, helpless sound that frightened her a little, because it came from the same place the dread lived.

"He's twelve," she said.

"He's the best of us," Tamsin said. "Which is worse." She pulled a stool over and sat, and for a moment the two of them just breathed together in the lamplight among the shelves of impossible things. "Hesper's already scouting the tannery road. Bramble is packing food and pretending it's for a normal trip. Marta's copying the essential ledgers so we can leave duplicates if we have to run. Your family," Tamsin said, "is going to rescue a ninety-one-year-old woman from the royal Question House using a drainage ditch and a child."

"When you say it like that—"

"Somebody has to say it like that. Someone should always be able to hear how it sounds."

Petra looked down at the ledger. In the margin of the page where the impossible entries clustered, in handwriting that was hers and was not hers, new words had appeared sometime during the evening, small and patient as frost:

*The gate remembers the way. Bring the keeper.*

She showed Tamsin. Tamsin read it, went slightly grey, and nodded once.

"Well," she said. "At least the book agrees with the boy."

Outside, the wind had turned, and it smelled of snow again, though the sky was clear. Somewhere across the dark city, under the garrison walls, a twelve-year-old boy was whispering to a gate older than the kingdom that had built its prison on top of it, and the gate—if the books were honest, and the books were always honest—was listening.

Moonset was in four hours.

They had a rescue to run.
