## 14. Breaking Into the Question House

The Question House had three locks on its front door and a garrison of twelve soldiers behind it, which was why we went in through the laundry chute.

I want it noted that I said this was a bad idea before we did it. I want it noted in ink, in my own hand, because if this goes wrong I intend to be remembered as the forger who objected, not the forger who led them into a fortress through a hole where dirty sheets go.

"Say it again," Wren whispered beside me in the alley, hugging his knees like a boy at a fire. He's fourteen and he talks to locks the way other children talk to imaginary friends, except his friends answer.

"It's a bad idea," I said.

"Good." He grinned. "Means nobody's lying to me."

Behind us, Bramble shifted her enormous frame against the wall and said nothing, which from Bramble is a sermon. She'd brought bread. She always brings bread. *If they're going to hang us,* she'd said that afternoon, kneading dough with the violence of a woman settling an argument with God, *they'll do it on a full stomach.*

We were going to get Odile back. That was the whole plan. That was the entire architecture of it: walk in, walk out, old woman in the middle. The boy who talks to locks had drawn the Question House from memory — vents, cellars, the guard rotations he'd learned by pressing his ear to drainpipes for a week — and when Petra saw the drawing she'd gone pale and quiet in the way she does when a ledger tells her something true, and she'd said, "The laundry chute," as if it were scripture.

So. The laundry chute.

* * *

Here's what you need to know about me, since I'm the one telling this: I have spent twenty years making false things look true. Deeds, writs, seals, signatures. I once forged a bishop's letter so well that the bishop quoted it in a sermon. It's not art, whatever people say. Art wants to be seen. A forgery wants to be believed and then forgotten, and there is no craft on earth lonelier than that.

And here's the thing I don't say out loud, the thing that has been sitting in my chest since Serah Vole rode into Caerlum on her grey horse: I knew her before she was an inquisitor. Before the writ, before the list of names. I made documents for her. Years of them. And some part of me has been waiting ever since for the bill to come due.

Not tonight, though. Tonight the bill could wait. Tonight we had Odile.

Wren put his palm flat against the chute's iron hatch and murmured to it — actual words, low ones, the way you'd soothe a horse — and the lock gave a click like a knuckle cracking. "She's friendly," he said. "Old lock. Nobody's loved her in years."

"Everything you love gets us arrested," I said, and went in first.

The chute was exactly as pleasant as you'd imagine: brick slick with lye, the smell of a hundred soldiers' linens boiled in tallow soap, and a drop at the end that Bramble caught me out of like I weighed nothing, which flattered neither of us. We came up in the wash-yard, crouched behind drying sheets, white ghosts swaying in the wind off the harbor.

Three guards by the well. One walking the wall. Wren counted them with his fingers, then held up four and pointed at himself, then two, pointing at me and Bramble. His plan — *nobody approved*, per guild law, per common sense — was that he would talk the yard's padlock on the cellar door into opening while we kept watch, and we'd go down into the holding cells from below, because the cells' main entrance had a desk, and desks have clerks, and clerks have memories, and memories are the one thing I can't forge.

It worked. That's the part I keep coming back to. It *worked*. The padlock opened for him like a flower, we slipped down the cellar stairs past barrels of salt fish, and the holding cells smelled of wet stone and despair, and there she was.

Odile. Ninety-one years old, oldest member of the Guild of Caerlum, keeper of the truth about Vessany, sitting on a straw pallet with her hands folded like a queen between audiences.

"Took you long enough," she said.

"We came the scenic route," I whispered.

"I raised idiots," she said, "but they're *my* idiots," and she stood up without help, which was a lie, because halfway up her knees buckled and Bramble caught her the same way she'd caught me, and Odile hissed something in a language I didn't know — Vessany's tongue, I realized, the first living words of it I'd ever heard — and then we were moving.

* * *

This is where I tell you about my mistake. I've turned it over enough times now that I can hold it up to the light without flinching. Mostly.

Coming back up through the cellar, we passed the clerk's desk at the top of the stairs — the one we'd avoided going down. Empty now; the clerk had stepped away, cup still steaming. And on the desk, half-finished, was a document. A transfer order. Odile's name on it, and a date three days hence, and at the bottom, a seal I recognized with a feeling like ice water in my veins.

Because I'd taught that seal to someone. Years ago. A frightened girl with a head for detail who wanted papers that would let her leave a town that no longer existed on any map.

Serah Vole had learned forgery from me before she learned interrogation from anyone. And the seal on Odile's transfer order was good — very good — but it was *mine*, her version of mine, and no one else alive would have noticed.

I stopped. I picked it up. And here is the mistake, the one I'll carry across any door we ever find: I took it.

Not to destroy it. Not even consciously. I took it because a forger sees another forger's work the way a musician hears a wrong note, and my hands moved before my sense did, and the paper left the desk, and the desk had been dusted with resin — I know that now — the kind of trap a careful inquisitor sets when she suspects the people she's hunting will not be able to resist touching things.

A thread of grey silk snapped somewhere under the blotter. Soft as a sigh.

Bramble looked at me. I looked at the paper. Somewhere above us, a bell began to ring — not the alarm bell, not yet, just the hour, but my heart had already decided otherwise.

"Run," I said.

* * *

We ran. Odile ran, gods bless her, ninety-one years old and swearing in a dead country's language, Wren ahead of us whispering encouragement to every lock between us and the wash-yard, and the wash-yard was empty now, the drying sheets whipping in wind that smelled of snow, and we got over the wall — Bramble throwing Odile up like a sack of flour, Wren flowing after, me last, tearing my sleeve on the iron spikes and leaving blood and green wool on top of a royal garrison wall like a signature.

I should have thought about that. I think about everything. It's my trade. But my hands had already betrayed me once that night and apparently the rest of me wanted to keep pace.

We made the safehouse on Tanner's Row by midnight. Petra was there, white-faced, having aged ten years in six hours, and when Odile walked through the door Petra made a sound I'll never repeat to anyone, small and broken and relieved, and held onto the old woman like the tide was trying to take her.

Then she looked at me. At the paper in my hand.

"What is that."

"A transfer order," I said. "Signed with a seal I invented."

Petra read it twice. Then she sat down slowly, the way people sit down when the floor has become untrustworthy, and said, "Tamsin. What did you touch?"

"A document."

"*What did you touch.*"

And I told her about the resin, or guessed at it, because I'm honest in exactly one direction — backward — and by the time I finished, Petra had gone from white to grey, and Bramble had stopped slicing bread, and Wren had gone very still by the window, listening to locks that weren't there.

"The safehouse," Petra said. "They'll follow the trail. Your blood on the wall, the paper missing from the desk — Tamsin, they won't need informants. They'll just need to ask who bled on a garrison wall tonight, and every cutpurse in Caerlum will sell them your name before dawn."

"I know."

"And this house is known. To you, to me, to—"

"I *know*."

Odile lowered herself into Bramble's chair, took the bread out of Bramble's frozen hands, and tore it. "Then we don't sleep here," she said. "We don't sleep anywhere twice. And we stop pretending," she went on, looking around at all of us, chewing, "that we have until winter to decide. We have until morning. Maybe less."

Nobody argued. That was the worst part. In the Guild of Caerlum, somebody always argues — Bramble about portions, Wren about methods, me about everything. But the room just absorbed Odile's words like water into bread, and I understood that we had crossed some line none of us had marked.

We'd gotten her back. We'd won. And standing there in our dying safehouse with snow starting to fall past the window — real snow, in harvest month, thick and wrong — I felt no victory at all. I felt a countdown. Somewhere under the city, under the guild itself, there was a door that only opened for names written in our ledgers, and every hour we stayed in Caerlum was an hour the Question House spent learning exactly where we slept.

"They'll come to the guild next," Petra said quietly. "Not to arrest us. To burn it. If they can't control the records, they'll destroy them. And if they destroy them—" She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Burn the ledgers and Vessany dies forever, and everyone in that room knew it, including the old woman eating bread in the dark.

"Then we move tomorrow," Bramble said, "and we take everything we can carry."

"You can't carry a library," I said.

"No," Petra said, and her eyes had that ledger-light in them, the terrible clarity. "But we know which pages matter."

Outside, the snow fell harder. Somewhere across the city, I imagined Serah Vole holding the empty space on her desk where a transfer order used to be, and smiling, because a missing document is a confession, and a confession is a map.

I'd taught her that too.

* * *

We left Tanner's Row before dawn, in ones and twos, carrying the archive in flour sacks and Bramble's bread baskets, and I swear to you the city watched us go. Shutters cracked. Dogs lifted their heads. Caerlum had spent forty years not seeing the Guild of Caerlum, and now it was beginning to see, and being seen is the one thing a guild of impossible things cannot survive.

Halfway down Salt Lane, Wren caught my sleeve.

"Tamsin," he said. "The locks are talking."

"Wren—"

"All of them. All night. Every lock in the city." His eyes were huge. "They say something's coming up from underneath. Something old. And they say—" he swallowed— "they say it knows our names."

I looked back the way we'd come, toward the guild hall squatting over its secret like a hen over one impossible egg, and I picked up my sack of stolen country, and we walked faster.
