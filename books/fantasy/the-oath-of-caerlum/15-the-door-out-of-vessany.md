## 15. The Door Out of Vessany

The ledger led us down.

That was the only way to describe it. I had spent three days with the impossible entries—my handwriting that wasn't mine, dated next spring—and on the third night the entries stopped describing and started directing. *Descend past the wine cellar,* they said. *Count the steps twice. The second count will not match the first. Trust the second.*

"You know," Tamsin said behind me, holding a lamp that guttered in air with no draft, "when I forged documents for a living, I at least had the decency to make them *plausible*."

"Shh," said Bramble, though there was nothing down here to wake but stone.

We went through the wine cellar, which the guild had kept stocked for a hundred years out of pure stubbornness—we were four people and a boy, and we drank like twelve—and past it to a door Master Corvane had always kept locked. I had assumed, all my life, that it held the really dangerous inventory: the plague jars, the contracts written in blood, the saint's second shadow's *first* shadow, if such a thing existed. Odile came last, leaning on Wren's arm, and when we reached the door she said, softly, "It was never locked against you, girl. It was locked against everyone else."

The lock knew Wren. Of course it did; every lock in Caerlum knew Wren by now. It opened with a sound like a sigh held forty years.

Inside: no storeroom. No jars, no shelves, no dust. Just a stair going down into a darkness that the lamplight refused to enter, as if the light itself understood it was unwelcome.

"Second count," I reminded them, and we descended, and I counted thirty-one steps, and then I counted again and got twenty-eight, and Bramble counted and got thirty-four, and none of us said anything about it because saying something would have made it worse.

At the bottom, the dark ended.

Not gradually. Not like a wall. The darkness simply stopped, the way a sentence stops, and beyond it was—

I don't have the words. I am a keeper of ledgers; words are supposed to be my trade. But what lay beyond the edge of that darkness was not a room. It was *remembering*, given weight and place. A threshold made of everything Vessany had ever been, pressed together by forty years of refusal—the fishwife who still salted her bread the Vessany way, Odile humming songs in a language the capital called gibberish, Serah Vole's mother singing in a kitchen Serah could no longer picture, ten thousand small stubborn memories that nobody had managed to sell or burn or tax. All of it gathered under the guild of Caerlum, because the guild had been keeping accounts, and accounts are how memory becomes permanent.

"Oh," said Bramble, who was not a woman given to eloquence. "Oh, *that's* what we've been sitting on."

The door—if I must call it a door—stood where the dark ended. It was not wood. It was not stone. It looked, depending on how I turned my head, like a winter field at dusk, or like the inside of a name being spoken, or like the space between one heartbeat and the next. Its frame was made of light the color of lamplight through honey, and along its edges, faint as watermark, ran writing in no alphabet I knew but which I could somehow read anyway, the way you can read a face.

*What does it say?* Wren asked—not aloud; the boy talked to locks and doors and hinges, and this one he addressed the way you'd address a frightened animal.

"It says," Odile answered for it, her voice gone young and strange, "it says *who may pass*. It has been waiting to say it for forty years."

Tamsin stepped forward, lamp raised, and the light bent around the doorway like water around a stone. "And who may pass?"

"Those whose names are written." Odile touched the ledger under my arm without looking at it. "In those books. Every entry, every debt, every impossible thing the guild ever recorded—it was never just bookkeeping, child. It was an enrollment. The ledgers are a list of everyone who chose to remember. And the door opens only for the remembered."

My hands were shaking. I set the ledger on the flat stone that served as a sill and opened it to the entries in my own handwriting that I had never written, and read them properly for the first time, aloud, because some things need a voice:

*"Petra Hartwell, keeper. Bramble Osk, cook. Tamsin Vale, forger. Wren, who speaks to locks. Odile of Vessany, eldest. Crossed over in the first snow. All names present. All debts paid."*

"All debts paid," Tamsin repeated. "That's doing a lot of work in that sentence."

"The door will do the accounting," Odile said. "Doors like this always do. Nothing crosses for free. You'll each give up a piece of what you carry—a memory, fairly taken. That's the toll. That's always been the toll. It's why the bargain-makers preferred to sell whole countries instead. Cheaper."

Bramble put a hand on Wren's shoulder. The boy didn't flinch. He had his palm flat against the doorframe now, and his eyes were wide, and whatever the threshold was saying to him, he liked it. "It's warm," he reported. "Underneath. Like a house where somebody's been cooking all day and nobody's told them to stop."

I knelt at the sill. Close up, the surface of the door wasn't a surface at all—it was depth, layered and layered, and in the layers I could see fragments: a harbor town with grey-green water. A market where they sold eels and amber. A kitchen—I caught my breath—a kitchen with blue tiles and a copper pot, and a woman standing at it with her back half-turned, and I knew without knowing how that this was Serah Vole's mother, preserved here because somewhere in the guild's oldest ledgers, in an entry Master Corvane had written before I was born, her name was inked alongside a debt the capital never intended to pay.

Forty years of silence, and she was still here. Still cooking. Waiting for someone to remember her daughter home.

"We can't go yet," I said, and heard my own voice crack on it. "Odile is in the Question House. Serah has her. We're not crossing without her."

"No," Odile agreed, from behind me, calm as Sunday. "We're not."

* * *

We climbed back up into a guild that felt smaller than it had an hour ago, the way a house feels smaller after you've seen the sea. Nobody spoke much. Bramble went to the kitchen and cooked, because Bramble cooked when the world broke; there was rabbit and barley and too much salt, and we ate it around the long table like the last members of something, which is what we were.

It was Wren who broke the silence. "The door asked me something."

Everyone looked up.

"When I had my hand on it. It asked if I'd bring my name or borrow one." He turned his spoon over. "I said bring it. It seemed rude not to."

"Borrow one?" Tamsin's voice had gone careful.

"People whose names aren't in the ledgers," Odile said quietly. "They could cross holding someone's hand. Carrying someone's word. But they'd arrive owing, and the country remembers its debtors." She looked at each of us down the length of the table. "Which is why, when we go back for Odile—and we will go back for me, so stop planning it like I'm not in the room—we should consider who else might want to come. There are more people in this city who remember Vessany than the ledgers hold. Fishwives. Dockhands. An inquisitor, possibly, though that one's a knife I wouldn't want to turn until I knew which way it pointed."

"Serah Vole," I said.

"She grew up there," Odile said. "You didn't know? No. Of course you didn't. Master Corvane kept it even from me for a long time. Her mother sold bread on the Amber Row. Her father drowned in the Greywater the year before the bargain. She was six when the country was unwritten, and someone very powerful decided she'd be more useful as a weapon with no homeland than a child with a grief. They didn't erase her memory by accident, girl. They erased it *on purpose*, and then they trained what was left." She sipped her tea. "She isn't hunting the door because the crown wants it sealed. She's hunting it because something in her has been hungry for forty years and she doesn't know what for."

The fire popped. Somewhere above us, the early snow creaked on the roof, harvest-month snow that no one could explain and everyone feared.

"So we rescue Odile," Bramble said flatly, "and we offer the door to anyone who wants it, and we let the inquisitor decide what she is. Is that the plan?"

"That's the shape of it," I said. "The plan comes after we survive tomorrow."

Tamsin laughed, short and sharp. "Tomorrow. Right. Because breaking into the Question House is a thing we've all done dozens of times."

"I've done it once," Wren said.

Every head turned. The boy shrugged, eleven years old and unbothered, stirring his rabbit stew. "Last summer. Before I came here. I wanted to see what they did with the people who talked to locks. I got in through the evidence cellar. The locks in there aren't even trying. They're *lonely*." He frowned, remembering. "There's a cell on the third level with a window facing the harbor. If she's anywhere, she's there. The locks told me that's where they keep the ones they want to watch think."

"And how," Tamsin said, "exactly, did you get out?"

"I told the front door I was expected." He smiled, and for a moment he looked much older, and much sadder. "It believed me. Doors usually do. People are the hard part."

* * *

I went down alone that night, after the others slept, because I couldn't not. The stair still disagreed with itself about how many steps it had, and the dark still ended like a sentence, and the door still burned there, patient as a debt.

I brought the ledger. I sat on the cold stone with my back to the frame—warm, Wren had said, and he was right; warmth came off it like a hearth through a wall—and I read entries until my candle burned low. Names. Hundreds of years of names. Masters and apprentices, clients and creditors, saints and thieves. Every one of them enrolled in remembering. Every one of them, I realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the vault's cold, already written on the other side of this door in some accounting I couldn't see. The guild hadn't just traded in impossibilities. We had been building a lifeboat for a century and calling it bookkeeping.

Master Corvane's handwriting appeared near the end of the oldest volume, in the entry I had found sealed in the vault, and beneath his signature, in fresh ink that could not possibly be fresh, a line I had not seen before tonight:

*"Petra. If you are reading this at the door, then you counted the steps twice, and you trusted the second count, and I am proud of you, and I am sorry. Sorry for the seal, sorry for the silence, sorry for the weight. The door was never ours to open. It was only ours to keep until someone brave enough came. Bring them all. Leave nothing that screams."*

*Leave nothing that screams.* The storeroom. The bottled echoes. The saint's second shadow. Everything we'd be leaving behind in a city about to eat itself.

I pressed my palm flat to the frame, the way Wren had, and the warmth rose to meet me, and for one moment—one only—I smelled woodsmoke and eels and bread baking, heard gulls and a language like water over stones, saw snowfields and lit windows and people turning toward me across a square as if they'd been waiting at their own doors for forty years, watching the horizon, watching the dark, watching for us.

Then it let go, gently, the way you set down something you intend to pick up again.

"Tomorrow," I told it. My breath fogged in the vault cold. "Tomorrow we come back with everyone."

The door said nothing. Doors never do. But the warmth held a moment longer than warmth should have, and I chose—that is the word, I *chose*—to believe it meant yes.

Above me, far away, the city slept uneasily under snow that shouldn't exist, and in the Question House on the third level, in a cell with a window facing the harbor, a woman with an erased childhood sat awake, I would learn later, staring at her own hands, wondering why they suddenly remembered how to knead bread.

The rescue would cost us everything we couldn't carry. But that was tomorrow's arithmetic.

Tonight there was only the door, and the ledger open on my knees, and the last blank page waiting for my hand.
