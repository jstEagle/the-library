## 16. Serah Vole Remembers Her Mother's Kitchen

The Question House smelled of tallow and old iron, and Serah Vole had always found the smell comforting. Tonight she did not.

She sat across a scarred oak table from an old woman who would not stop humming.

Odile of Caerlum — *the eldest, half-mad*, the file said, though Serah had learned in twenty years of service that files said what someone wanted them to say — sat with her wrists bound loosely to the arms of her chair. Not because she struggled. Because procedure required it. She had come without resistance from the Salt Gate cell, blinking at the soldiers as if they were weather, and she had been humming ever since. Some tuneless, wandering thing that slid between notes like water finding cracks.

"You are not required to speak," Serah said, for the fourth time. It was true. The writ permitted interrogation by silence as much as by question; there were men in this building whose whole art was the space between words. But the old woman's humming was worse than any answer. It filled the room wrong. The walls here were plastered thick with felt and lime to deaden sound — every question room in the Question House was built so that nothing echoed — and yet the humming seemed to reach corners the room didn't have.

Serah opened the folder on the table before her.

They had taken it from Odile at the gate: a single ledger page, folded twice, soft as cloth from handling. Evidence, the soldiers called it. Serah had read it once on horseback and once again by lamplight in her quarters, and both times it had made no sense, which was itself a kind of sense, because nothing about Caerlum had made sense since she arrived.

*Received of the Widow Marsh, one grief, bottled, payment deferred against the birth of her granddaughter.* That was the first line. *Paid out: one memory of rain on the roof at Vessany-in-the-Weald.*

Vessany.

The word sat on the page the way a stone sits on a grave — flatly, finally, daring you to move it.

"Where did you get this?" Serah asked.

Odile hummed.

"The Guild of Caerlum is charged with customs fraud, forgery, and the trafficking of contraband antiquities." The charges were thin and Serah knew it; she had built them herself, and knew their seams. "You may assist your companions by cooperating."

Nothing. The humming wound on, patient as tide.

Serah looked down at the page again. Under the lamplight the ink had a faint brown shimmer, like dried blood or old honey. She had handled ten thousand documents in her career. Confessions. Writs. The last letters of condemned men, filed neatly beside the records of their executions. Paper did not frighten her. Paper was where truth went to be held still.

But this page seemed to hold something still the way a jar holds a wasp.

"Your guild," she said slowly, "traded in memories. Of Vessany."

The humming stopped.

It stopped so completely that Serah heard, for the first time since the old woman entered the room, the actual sounds of the building: boots overhead, wind at the arrow-slit, the tick of the lamp's metal cooling. Odile raised her head. Her eyes were pale as skimmed milk, and for a moment they focused on Serah with terrible precision, the way a hawk focuses.

"You touched it," Odile said. "Just now. With two fingers. You're touching it now."

Serah looked down. Her fingers rested on the edge of the ledger page. She had not noticed doing it.

"It's only paper," she said.

"No," Odile said. "It isn't. Nothing in that guild is only anything." And then, softly, almost kindly, in the voice one uses to warn a child away from a well: "Read it with your hands, girl. That's what it's for."

Serah should have pulled her hand back. Twenty years of discipline said pull the hand back, call the guards, have the page sealed in lead and shipped to the capital with the rest of the evidence. Instead she watched her own thumb move across the ink, tracing the last line — *one memory of rain on the roof at Vessany-in-the-Weald* — and the world tilted sideways through a door she had not known was there.

Rain on a roof.

Not the hard drumming rain of the capital's slate, but soft rain on wood shingles, on cedar gone silver with age, each drop landing with its own small wooden note so that the whole roof played like an instrument played by weather. She could smell it — wet cedar, woodsmoke, bread proving overnight near the banked oven. She could hear her mother's voice through the floorboards, singing low, a song with no words she could name but a melody that lived in the same place the humming lived, the crack-between-notes place—

Her mother's kitchen.

Green paint peeling from the window frames. A copper pot on the wall that caught the morning light and threw coins of it across the ceiling. Flour on her mother's forearms to the elbow, and her mother laughing at something her father said — except Serah could not see her father's face, could not see *either* face, the way you cannot see the faces in dreams, and the laughter had a strange doubled quality, warm and hollowed at once, like a house after everyone has left it.

"Mother," Serah said aloud, in a voice she did not recognize as her own.

The room came back. The felt-deadened walls. The lamp. The old woman watching her with milk-pale eyes full of something that was not triumph and not pity, but grief — plain, enormous, patient grief.

"There it is," Odile said gently. "Forty years, and it's still in there. They couldn't take it out, love. They could only paper over the door."

Serah stood. Her chair scraped back and the sound died instantly in the padded room, swallowed whole, and that was somehow the worst thing — even her own movements had no echo here. "What did you do to me?"

"Nothing. I'm tied to a chair." Odile lifted her bound wrists an inch, an absurd little gesture. "That page is forty years old and it remembers better than either of us. The guild doesn't record the impossible, girl. It *keeps* it. Somebody has to, when a whole country is sold out from under the people standing on it."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"Don't you?" Odile tilted her head. "Say your own name."

"Serah Vole."

"Where were you born?"

"In the capital." The answer came instantly, smoothly, drilled into her by thirty years of forms. *Place of birth: the capital. Parents: deceased. Origin: none recorded.* She had written those lines herself on a hundred documents. She had never once wondered why the line for origin said *none recorded* instead of blank.

"Where," Odile said, "did you learn that song?"

Serah opened her mouth and no answer came out, because the melody was rising in her throat unbidden, the crack-between-notes song, her mother's song, and her mouth remembered the shape of words her mind had never held.

She sat back down. Slowly. Like an old woman herself.

"What is happening to me?"

"You're remembering," Odile said. "It's painful. I won't pretend it isn't. But it's less painful than the other thing, which is spending your whole life guarding the lock on a door you paid to have bricked up." She leaned forward as far as her bonds allowed. "You want to know why the prayers are being answered, inquisitor? Why the drowned walk out of harbors and the snow falls in harvest month? Because a bargain is coming due. Your capital bought itself a hundred years without plague, and the price was a country. Its name. Its gods. Its *kitchens*. And the gods went unpaid, and now the bill has landed, and the men who signed it are all comfortably dead, and the only ones left holding the paper are people like me. People like you."

"I am an inquisitor of the Crown."

"You are a girl from a kitchen with green window frames," Odile said, "who was carried out at four years old and set down somewhere else, and given a new story, and told the new story so many times she became it. They didn't erase Vessany in one day. They erased it in children first. Children forget fastest. Children make the cleanest instruments." Her voice softened. "You were very good at forgetting, love. They chose you for it."

Serah's hands were flat on the table on either side of the ledger page, not touching it now, keeping deliberately clear of it, the way one keeps clear of an open flame. "This is sedition. This is a trick of some kind. Memory-mongering — it's exactly what the guild is accused of—"

"Then arrest me for it," Odile said placidly. "You already have. Go on, write it down: the old woman made me remember my mother. Add it to the charges. See how it looks in the file next spring, when the snow's up to the eaves and half the city is praying to names it can't pronounce."

Silence. The lamp ticked.

And beneath the fear, beneath the vertigo, something in Serah was moving with terrible steadiness toward a conclusion she had been circling for weeks without knowing it. All the small wrongnesses of her life stood up at once and arranged themselves into a shape. Why she had volunteered for this assignment with an eagerness that embarrassed her even then. Why the word *Vessany* on the royal list had struck her not as unfamiliar but as *familiar-strange*, a word seen through frosted glass. Why she kept, in a locked drawer she never opened, a wooden spoon worn smooth on one side, and could not have said under oath where it came from — only that burning it had never once seemed possible.

"My mother," Serah said. Her voice was level. It cost her everything to keep it level. "In the memory. I couldn't see her face."

"No," Odile said quietly. "You gave that up. Or it was taken as part of the price. Faces are the dearest coin, love. Names can be rewritten, houses can be re-described, but a face—" she shook her head. "A face is where the country actually lives."

Somewhere above them, boots crossed a floor. The Question House went on being the Question House, and Serah sat in it, holding the edges of herself like a woman holding the rails of a ship in weather.

"If what you say is true," she said at last, "then my career is built on the erasure of my own home. Every conviction. Every writ. The Crown I serve bought its clean century with my mother's kitchen, and I've spent twenty years enforcing the receipt."

"Yes," said Odile, simply, without cruelty. "That's what I'd have guessed. You have the look of someone who checks the arithmetic."

"And if it's false — if this is the guild's cleverest work, a forgery aimed straight at the one inquisitor with a hole in her past—"

"Then burn the page," Odile said. "Right now. Strike flint and burn it and see whether the kitchen goes with it." She smiled, gap-toothed and terrible. "You won't. You know why? Because you've already checked the arithmetic. Somewhere in that locked drawer of yours there's a thing you've never been able to explain, and you just realized it isn't from the capital at all."

The wooden spoon. Smooth on one side. Serah felt the blood leave her face.

"How do you know about the drawer?"

"I don't," Odile said. "But everyone from home has a drawer like that."

Serah rose. She took the ledger page from the table, folded it twice along its soft old creases, and put it inside her coat, over her heart, which was beating hard enough that she suspected the old woman could hear it.

"That page is evidence," she said. "I'm confiscating it."

"Of course you are," said Odile, and began to hum.

At the door Serah paused, her hand on the iron ring, her back to the prisoner. The felt-deadened room drank every sound, and yet she spoke loudly enough to be sure of being heard, because she needed to hear her own voice say it.

"Why tell me? You owe me nothing. I dragged you here in irons. Your family will be trying some foolhardy rescue within the day, and my soldiers will be waiting, and people will die. Telling me the truth helps none of you."

"Oh, it isn't for you," Odile said. "Well — it is, but not the way you mean. It's bookkeeping, girl. Everything in this affair is bookkeeping. Someone wrote your name out of the country's books forty years ago, and tonight someone has written it back in, and the ledger doesn't care whether you like the entry." The milk-pale eyes gleamed in the lamplight. "You'll find your family coming for me soon enough. When they do, inquisitor, you'll have a choice to make, and I wanted you to make it knowing what your mother's kitchen looked like. Green window frames. Copper pot on the wall. There's bread proving near the oven right now, if you know how to listen."

Serah walked out of the Question House into the grey pre-dawn and stood in the courtyard until the cold made her eyes run, and she told herself it was the cold.

Inside her coat, over her heart, the page lay soft and warm as a living thing.

She thought about the drawer. She thought about the spoon. She thought about a hundred interrogations ended with her signature at the bottom, the small neat hand of a woman with no origin recorded, and for the first time in twenty years she asked herself whose hand had signed the original — whose order had carried her out at four years old, whose seal had sold a country and bought a childhood's forgetting.

By the time the bell rang for prime, Inquisitor Serah Vole had reached a decision, though she had not yet dared to think it in words.

She went to her quarters, unlocked the drawer she never opened, and took out the wooden spoon. Then she sat at her desk, drew a fresh sheet toward her, and began — in a hand that shook only slightly — to draft her report to the capital.

Outside, the early snow was falling again, thicker than yesterday, settling on the city like a shroud or a blanket, depending on what you believed.

She got as far as *Regarding the Guild of Caerlum* before she stopped, pen hovering, and looked at the spoon lying on the blotter, smooth side up.

Then she crossed out the heading, and underneath it wrote instead: *Regarding the Country of Vessany* — and the words did not vanish, and the ink did not refuse her, and somewhere far below the frozen streets, beneath a guildhouse older than the maps, something that had waited forty years behind a threshold felt a name settle back into place, and turned, very slightly, like a key.
