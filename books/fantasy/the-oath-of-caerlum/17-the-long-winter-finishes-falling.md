## 17. The Long Winter Finishes Falling

The snow came down like a verdict.

Bramble had cooked through hard winters before—the lean year after the fever, the year the harbor froze and fish cost more than silver—but she had never seen a harvest month turn white. The grain barges stopped coming upriver on the ninth day. By the twelfth, the bakeries were selling bread made with sawdust and calling it *hearty*. By the fifteenth, they were not selling bread at all.

She stood in the guild kitchen window with her flour-blackened hands pressed flat against the glass and watched Caerlum go quiet in a way that was worse than noise. Smoke rose from too few chimneys. In the square below, a woman knelt in the snow before a fountain that had frozen mid-pour, praying aloud to a god whose name she pronounced carefully, like a word learned from a stranger.

The fountain did not answer. But something in the alley behind it moved, and the woman stood up weeping, and Bramble turned away from the window because she already knew how answered prayers ended now.

"Soup's on," she said to the empty kitchen, because saying it out loud was a kind of prayer too, and this one had never once failed her.

* * *

They ate in the long hall, all seven of them, because Odile was back among them—thinner, quieter, with bruised wrists she refused to discuss—and because Bramble had decided weeks ago that fear would be served at no table of hers. Tamsin sat picking at her bowl. Wren, the boy who talked to locks, had his boots on under the table, which meant he'd been out again, which meant he'd heard things.

"The Salt Gate's closed," Wren said, when the silence got heavy enough. "Soldiers checking papers. Real ones, stamped ones. They're asking people to say where they were born."

"Normal question," said Ferro, the retired thief, who was old enough to remember when it hadn't been.

"Not if you get it wrong." Wren pushed soup around his bowl. "A man at the gate said *Vessany* by accident. Just slipped out. They took him."

Nobody looked at Odile. Everybody looked at Odile.

"I told them nothing," she said, in her cracked-bell voice. "I told them I was born nowhere, which is nearly true, for a woman my age. Places stop existing. People are harder to erase." She smiled, and it was the smile of someone holding a door shut from the inside. "But thank you all for the rescue. Even the parts nobody approved."

"You're welcome," said Wren, with dignity.

Petra set down her spoon. She had ink on three fingers and a look on her face that Bramble recognized from the worst nights of inventory: the look of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't want finished.

"The archive district burned last night," Petra said. "All of it. The Hall of Records, the Notaries' Annex, the map houses. Serah Vole's soldiers didn't start it—the mob did. But the soldiers stood around and let it finish."

"They're burning paper," Ferro said, "because paper remembers. And hungry people will burn anything that reminds them the world used to be bigger than their stomachs."

"It's not just paper," Petra said. Her voice stayed level, which was how Bramble knew it was costing her. "It's every deed, every birth record, every treaty. And—" she stopped.

"And," said Odile gently, "every ledger that mentions Vessany. Which is to say, ours."

The fire popped in the hearth. Outside, the snow kept falling, patient as debt.

"So here is what I know," Petra said, standing, because she was twenty-four years old and had never learned to make announcements sitting down. "The ledgers don't just record the impossible. They hold it. Master Corvane taught me that without ever saying it straight. Every entry about Vessany in our vault is a thread still tied to a country that officially doesn't exist. We are, as far as I can determine, the last library of Vessany on earth."

"And the city is burning libraries," Tamsin said quietly.

"Yes."

"Then we have a problem," said Bramble, "and the problem is that I am not letting anyone carry ledgers out of this house until everyone has eaten properly. Grief on an empty stomach makes stupid decisions. I've watched it happen."

For a moment—one small, ordinary moment—they almost laughed, and Bramble counted it a victory worth more than any in the vault.

* * *

They went down to the storeroom together, all seven, carrying lamps, and Bramble thought: *this is what a family looks like when it's deciding what to save.*

The storeroom of the Guild of Caerlum held impossible things, and in lamplight they looked like what they were: the life's work of dead men and women who had believed remembering was a trade worth dying poor in. Bottled echoes in their straw. A saint's second shadow, folded like laundry. Debts owed by the dead, filed under names no living person could pronounce. And beneath all of it, in the iron cabinets Corvane had built with his own hands, the Vessany ledgers—forty years of entries in a dozen hands, recording a country that had been sold out from under itself.

"We can't take it all," Petra said. She walked the shelves with her ledger-clerk's stride, touching nothing, cataloguing everything. "We can't even take most of it. Whatever we carry has to fit through whatever door exists, and none of us knows yet what that door allows."

"Then we choose," said Odile.

"That's the part I hate."

"Choosing is the whole job," Odile said. "Custody is just choosing, over and over, until your arms give out. Corvane knew that. Why do you think he left it all to you?"

Petra flinched. Bramble stepped closer to her without seeming to, the way you lean into wind.

They worked until dawn. It was the strangest night of Bramble's long strange life. Tamsin laid documents out on the floor and sorted them by hand—not by value, she said, but by *voice*, by which pages sounded most like living people. Ferro packed the echoes first, then argued himself out of it and unpacked them; "nobody can eat an echo," he muttered, and then repacked two anyway, the ones that sounded like children laughing. Wren sat cross-legged with the saint's second shadow across his knees, and the shadow, unaccountably, seemed comfortable there.

And Petra stood at the iron cabinets with the Vessany ledgers stacked around her feet, reading.

Not skimming. Reading. Entry by entry, name by name. Bramble brought her tea twice and both times found her lips moving, and both times heard the same thing: names. *Maren Ostrevik, wheelwright. The Aldous twins, born in the same hour. Yeva Vole, seamstress, paid in full.*

"Yeva Vole," Bramble repeated softly, later, when Petra finally came up for air.

"Inquisitor Serah Vole's mother," Petra said. "Born in Vessany. The ledgers have her wedding entry. Her daughter's birth entry. Forty years of a life the crown says never happened." She looked down at the stack, and Bramble saw her make a choice, and saw what it cost. "This one comes with us even if we have to leave everything else."

* * *

The mob reached the guild quarter on the third day.

Bramble heard it before she understood it—a sound like the sea, if the sea were angry and coming uphill. She was rendering the last of the fat, because fat was food and food was hope, and she stood at her window with a spoon in her hand and watched maybe two hundred people come up Candler's Row with torches lit against the noonday snow.

They weren't screaming. That was the terrible part. They were chanting, low and rhythmic, and it took her a moment to catch the words: *remember less, remember less, remember less.* Hungry people begging the world to stop reminding them of what they'd lost. Somewhere in the crowd a priest of the new compact gods walked with his arms raised, and every torch he passed burned a little brighter.

"Ferro," Bramble called down the stairs, calm as Sunday. "Company."

They gathered in the hall. Below, the crowd split around the guildhouse like water around a stone—not because they didn't see it, but because the guild had spent a century buying its invisibility, and even now, even fraying, the wards held enough. Mostly. A few faces turned upward toward the windows. One man pointed. Then the crowd moved on, downhill, toward the customs house, toward anything official, toward anything that smelled of records.

"They'll be back," Ferro said. "Wards don't stop hunger. Nothing stops hunger."

"Then we finish tonight," Petra said. "Whatever fits, we pack. Whatever doesn't—" She stopped. Started again. "Whatever doesn't, we leave. And I want it said plainly, once, so we all carry it: leaving it isn't losing it. Leaving it is trusting whoever comes after. That's the whole faith of this guild. Custody passes forward or it dies."

Odile put her ruined hand on Petra's shoulder. "Corvane would have said it worse and meant it the same."

So they worked again, and this time the work had teeth in it. Down in the storeroom, by lamplight, while the city chanted itself hoarse, they chose. The bottled echoes stayed—all but two. The debts of the dead stayed; the dead could wait, they were good at waiting. The saint's second shadow went into Wren's pack because the boy refused to put it down and nobody had the heart to fight him. And the Vessany ledgers—there were thirty-one of them, and they could carry nine.

Nine. Petra counted them onto the table like a woman dealing out grief in portions. The founding ledger. The bargain-ledger, with the terms of the sale written in a hand that shook. The ledger of names, every citizen recorded before the erasure, because Odile had insisted and Odile had been right. And six more, chosen by voice, by weight, by arguments that lasted hours and ended in silence.

The other twenty-two went back into the iron cabinets, and Petra locked them, and stood there a moment with her forehead against the cold iron.

"Goodbye," she said, to the room, to the work, to the dead who had trusted her. "Someone will come. I'm writing it down so it's true."

* * *

They loaded at midnight, in the falling snow, because the falling snow hid them and because Bramble had declared that no one crossed a threshold hungry. She filled a sack with hard bread, smoked fish, the rendered fat, dried apples from the cellar's deepest shelf—food for a journey nobody could describe. Around her the others moved like a crew that had sailed together for years instead of surviving together for months: Tamsin with the document satchels strapped across her chest like armor; Ferro with the coin and the lockpicks and, hidden badly, a bottle of the good brandy; Wren with his shadow and his picks and his terrible grin; Odile wrapped in every shawl in the house, carrying nothing, carried by everything.

Petra came up from the vault last, with nine ledgers in a oilcloth-wrapped bundle and her own current ledger—the living one, the one with tomorrow's entries in her own handwriting—clutched against her ribs.

"Where do we even go?" Tamsin asked. It wasn't despair. It was logistics. "The door, if it exists, if the impossible pages are true—where?"

"Under us," Petra said. "Under the guild. Where it's always been. The entries don't say *go somewhere*. They say *go down, and be remembered*." She looked around at them, at her ragged, impossible, chosen family, standing in the snow with their lives in sacks. "Once we open it, everything changes. Once we open it, we're not hiding anymore. There's no version of this where we slip away quiet. The door opening means the country coming back, and the country coming back means every soldier in Caerlum will feel it, and they will come, and we will be standing in the middle of it holding books."

"Then we'd best hold them well," said Odile.

Bramble shouldered her sack. Under her feet, faint as a heartbeat through a floor, she felt something shift—something old, something patient, something that had waited forty years under the flagstones for exactly this weight of footsteps, exactly this many remembered names.

Above them, on the ridge of the guildhouse roof, a soldier's lantern swung. Then another. Then a dozen, silent, spreading along the rooftops like a lit fuse.

"They've found us," Ferro said. "That was fast."

"No," said Wren, tilting his head, listening the way he listened to locks. "They were already here. They've been here since the rescue. Someone's been waiting for us to walk to the door." He swallowed. "And I think—I think she's coming down the stairs."
