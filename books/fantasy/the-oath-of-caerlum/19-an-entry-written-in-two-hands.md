## 19. An Entry Written in Two Hands

The ledger lay open between them on the vault floor, and the whole world had gone quiet enough that Petra could hear the ink drying.

Serah Vole stood with her sword half-raised and her eyes fixed on a single line of her mother's handwriting: *Maren Vole, of the Weavers' Row, Vessany. Paid in full: one daughter's memory, held in trust.*

"You knew," Serah said. Her voice was very steady, the way a rope is steady right before it snaps. "You knew my name was in there."

"I didn't," Petra said, and it was true. "The ledger did."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have." Petra kept her hands where they were—open, empty, at her sides. Behind her, pressed against the door that was not a door, Bramble and Tamsin and Odile and little Fen waited in a silence so complete it felt like held breath. Above them, muffled through forty feet of stone and cellar earth, the Question House was burning its own archives, and the city was screaming about it, and winter was coming down like a verdict.

Serah's soldiers filled the stairwell behind her. Six of them, pikes leveled, faces gray in the lamplight. Their captain, a broad man named Hask whose family had sold fish two stalls down from the drowned boy's mother, shifted his weight from foot to foot.

"Inquisitor," Hask said. "Your orders were to seal it. Burn what won't seal."

"My orders." Serah said the word as if tasting it for rot.

She looked down at the page again. Petra watched her read it the way you'd read a letter from someone you'd buried. Maren Vole. A kitchen with blue tiles. Bread on a windowsill to cool. Forty years ago those things had been taken out of the world like teeth pulled from a jaw, and every gap they left had been filled with smooth, official nothing—and this woman had grown up in that nothing, had been shaped by it, sharpened by it, sent out of it with a royal writ and a list of names.

*Paid in full: one daughter's memory, held in trust.*

Not erased. *Held.* The guild did not destroy what it took custody of. That was the whole point of the ledgers. That was the whole point of everything.

"The bargain bought your capital a hundred years without plague," Odile said from beside the door. Her voice was thin and cracked and carried anyway, the way old bells carry. "It was paid for with a country. And the country's people weren't killed, girl. They were *kept*. Every soul in Vessany is on the far side of that door right now, waiting for someone to remember them properly. Waiting for their names to be said by mouths that mean it."

"Then why does it need mine?" Serah asked.

Petra understood before she could stop herself from answering. "Because the door opens for names written in the ledgers. Your mother wrote hers when she crossed. But the bargain took the memory of Vessany from everyone who stayed—including you. You can't open a door to a home you've been made to forget." She swallowed. "Unless you choose it back."

"And if I don't?"

"Then we burn the ledgers ourselves, right here, and Vessany dies forever instead of just being asleep, and your mother's name dies with it, and the gods go back to being hungry, and in a hundred years somebody else sells something else to buy something else, because nobody wrote any of it down." Petra heard her own voice shake and let it. She was done lying, even with her posture. "That's the choice. It's not a good one. It's just the only real one in the room."

The torches hissed. Somewhere far above, snow ticked against the Question House roof like fingernails on a coffin lid.

Hask cleared his throat. "Inquisitor. With respect. Whatever's written in that book, it's witch-writing. You've seen what the answered prayers do. You saw the Harrow girl."

"I saw what forty years of silence does," Serah said quietly. "I've been looking at it in the mirror my whole life."

She lowered the sword.

Not dropped it—lowered it, point to the floorstones, deliberate, the way a judge sets down a gavel, and the sound it made was small and enormous at once. Six soldiers stared at her. Petra stared at her. The ledger lay open, patient as a grave, patient as a cradle.

"Serah," Tamsin said from the doorway—it was the first time anyone had used her given name in Petra's hearing—"you don't have to. There's another way. There's always—"

"There isn't," Serah said. "There never was. That's what I never understood until tonight. I thought the law was a wall I stood on. It's a ledger too. Somebody wrote it. Somebody signed it." She crouched, slow, keeping her eyes on Petra as though asking permission, and picked up the pen from where it had rolled against the vault's iron ring.

"And somebody," she said, "can sign differently."

She turned to the last blank line of the page—the line beneath Maren Vole, beneath the entry in Corvane's hand from forty years ago, beneath all the careful custody of all the impossible years—and she wrote:

*Serah Vole, of nowhere, of Caerlum, of Vessany. Returning.*

Her handwriting was beautiful. Of course it was; everything about the woman was precise. But at the end of the name her hand faltered, just once, and the final letter came out slightly larger than the rest, the way a child writes, the way someone writes who has just remembered how.

The lamp flames all leaned sideways at once, though there was no draft.

"Oh," Odile breathed. "Oh, it's been listening. It's been listening this whole time."

The door began to open.

It was not a door opening the way doors open. It was more like fog lifting, or like a word you'd mispronounced your entire life suddenly said correctly in someone else's mouth. The threshold that had been memory-solid and dark became a threshold of evening light—cold gold, low sun, the kind of light that lives at the edge of winter days in northern countries. Air came through it that smelled of woodsmoke and bread and clean snow, and under that, faintly, the sea.

And voices. Distant, ordinary, human voices. Someone calling a name—not Serah's, not anyone's—a mother's voice calling a child in for supper, in an accent that existed nowhere in the capital, an accent that lived in the ledgers and in Odile's ruined mind and now, spilling through a hole in the world, in a cellar beneath a burning archive.

Fen started to cry. Not frightened crying. The other kind, the kind that has no words yet.

"It's real," Bramble whispered. The big cook had one hand over his mouth as if he might be sick or laugh or pray. "It's actually—Tamsin, look, the lights, there are *windows*—"

Through the threshold, maybe half a mile off across a snowfield, a village sat in the dusk with its windows lit. Chimney smoke rose straight up in the still air. A dog barked, impossibly clear across the distance. Figures moved between the houses, unhurried, unafraid, doing the thousand small things people do at day's end, and every single one of them was supposed to have been unwritten forty years ago.

"They never stopped," Odile said. Tears ran freely down the old woman's face and froze in the cold air coming through the door. "They never stopped lighting the windows. Every night. In case."

Hask made a noise in his throat. When Petra turned, the captain had his pike grounded, butt-down, and he was staring through the door with an expression she would remember for the rest of her life: the expression of a man watching his own orders die.

"Inquisitor," he said again, but it wasn't an order-seeking anymore. It was a question. Maybe the question. *What are we, now?*

Serah stood. She picked up her sword—and Petra's heart clenched—but she only slung it onto her back, the way you carry something you may need but no longer believe in.

"Hask," she said. "You have a wife in the Harrow district. A daughter."

"Yes."

"When the mobs come for the archives tomorrow—and they'll come tonight, once the fires spread—they won't ask whether you followed orders. They'll ask whether you were seen here." She met his eyes. "I'm going to turn around in a moment, and I'm going to order you to arrest me. And you're going to refuse. Loudly. And then you're going to take your men and secure the Salt Gate road, away from here, and lose perhaps ten minutes doing it thoroughly. Can you do that?"

Hask looked at her for a long moment. Then at the lit windows in the snowfield. Then at his own hands, which had held a pike for the crown for nineteen years.

"Ten minutes," he said. "Fifteen, if it snows harder." He turned to his men. "You heard the inquisitor. Gate road. Move."

They went. Boots on stairs, fading. One of them, the youngest, paused at the top and looked back—at the door, at the light—and then ran after the others, and Petra filed his face away somewhere permanent, because that was her whole self, that was what she was *for*: remembering.

Then it was seven of them in a vault at the bottom of the world, and a door standing open on a country.

Serah turned. For a moment the inquisitor's mask was gone entirely and what was underneath was just a tired woman in her thirties who had spent her whole career hunting a thing she was.

"Petra Hartwell," she said. "Keeper of the Ledgers of Caerlum." She inclined her head, formally, the way you'd greet a foreign minister—which, Petra realized, was exactly what she was doing. "My mother's name is in your books. My mother's *country* is in your books. What happens to me now?"

"Now?" Petra picked up the ledger and closed it gently. "Now you carry it. Everyone who crosses carries something. That's the toll."

"I know about tolls."

"I know you do." Petra held out the book. "Take the front. Bramble takes the back. Nobody drops it. Nobody drops it ever again."

Bramble laughed wetly and took his end of the burden, and it was absurd, the two of them carrying a ledger together like pallbearers or midwives, and it was also the truest image of the last year of Petra's life that she could imagine.

Odile went first. Of course she did. The eldest walked through the threshold of the country she'd been exiled from in her youth without hesitating, and halfway through the passage she stopped, and stood, and let the remembered air hit her face, and Petra heard her say, in a language none of them spoke but all of them somehow understood: *I'm home, I'm home, I'm sorry it took so long.*

Then Fen, dragging Tamsin by the hand. Then Tamsin, who paused at the line and looked back at the burning city above them with an expression Petra couldn't read—grief, maybe, or apology, or both wearing each other's clothes.

Bramble and Petra lifted the ledger and stepped toward the light—

"Wait."

They turned. Serah stood at the edge of the threshold, and beyond her shoulder, far above, came the first sounds of the mob reaching the Question House square: shouting, glass, a bell rung wrong.

"The soldiers," she said. "Some of them will talk. Hask will hold them as long as he can, but by morning the crown will know exactly where this door is, and the king's answer will be fire and mortar and priests. This door can't stay findable."

"I know," Petra said. "The ledgers say so."

"The ledgers say so." Serah almost smiled. "Of course they do." She squared her shoulders. "Then we cross, and we close it behind us, and we leave the way-marked page where only a keeper would think to look. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Petra said.

"Then walk, Keeper. Fifteen minutes is shorter than it used to be."

They stepped through together, the ledger between them, into cold gold light and the smell of bread, and behind them the threshold began, slowly, gently, like a wound deciding to heal, to close—

Which is when Petra heard, from the direction of the village, running footsteps and a voice shouting a warning in old Vessany speech, and saw the people in the lit windows begin, all at once, to put out their lights.
