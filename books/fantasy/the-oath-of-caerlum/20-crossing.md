## 20. Crossing

The door did not look like a door.

That was the first thing I understood about it, standing in the vault beneath the guild with snowmelt seeping through my boots and the city burning somewhere above: a door is something you open from one side. This was a threshold that had opened itself, slowly, the way a wound opens—reluctantly, and only because it had been pressed long enough.

It stood in the middle of the far wall of Corvane's deepest vault, where the ledgers said there was nothing but stone. It was made of nothing at all. Or rather, it was made of everything that had ever been remembered about Vessany, compressed into a standing absence—a shape like a doorway drawn in the air by someone who had loved the room on the other side very much and for a very long time. Through it I could see snowfields under a low gold sky, lit windows, smoke rising straight up from chimneys in the windless cold.

People were waiting on the other side. I could see them as silhouettes, small against the windows, patient in the way that people are patient when they have been waiting forty years and have gotten good at it.

"The door takes a toll," Odile said. She was leaning on Tamsin's arm, still bruised from the Question House, her voice scraped thin but steady. "I told you. Memory apiece. Taken fairly—it doesn't choose what hurts most. It chooses what you can spare without breaking. Mostly."

"Mostly," Bramble repeated flatly. He had his kitchen knives strapped to his belt, which everyone had pretended not to notice. He was holding Pip's shoulder with the other hand, and Pip was holding the lock-picks he talked to, and the boy was looking at the threshold the way he looked at any mechanism: with respect and a certain professional suspicion.

"It's fair," I said. "Everything in the ledgers says so." And then, because they all turned to look at me: "Fair isn't kind. But it's fair."

* * *

We had maybe an hour. Maybe less. Serah Vole had written her name into the ledger with a hand that shook only once, and she had turned on her own soldiers with her sword still drawn, and whatever happened above us now was happening because an inquisitor of Vessany's erasure had decided, in the space of one breath, to become its witness instead. But soldiers do not stay confused forever. Confused soldiers become angry soldiers, and angry soldiers follow orders, and somewhere above us was a royal order to seal the country permanently, stamped and signed and waiting to be obeyed.

Tamsin had barred the vault door with every trick she knew, and the boy who talks to locks had whispered to it besides, told it to be stubborn, told it to jam and sulk and forget how hinges work. Pip said locks liked him. He'd never met one that didn't. But locks were built by men, and men could break what men had built.

So: an hour. We spent five minutes of it arguing, which was four more than we could afford, and I let them argue because I understood what the arguing was really for. It was for Bramble saying, "I'm not leaving my ovens," and Odile answering, "Your ovens have been cold since the harvest snows, you great sentimental fool, and there will be flour on the other side, there has to be, someone has kept the mills running or I'm a liar"—and it was for Tamsin laughing, wet-eyed, at the idea that anyone would burn down Caerlum over a customs fraud, and it was for Pip asking, very quietly, whether the door would take his memory of his mother, who had died last spring, and whether he'd know he was supposed to miss her.

I answered that one. I had read enough ledger entries by then to answer it honestly.

"It takes what you can spare," I said. "Not what you can't. Your grief is load-bearing, Pip. The door knows the difference. It was built by people who understood keeping."

He nodded. Children believe adults who sound certain. I sounded certain. Inside, I was doing arithmetic I hated: counting my own memories like coins, trying to guess which one the door would find lightest.

* * *

Serah came down while we were loading what we could carry. She came down alone, having dismissed—or lost, or outlived—her escort, and she stood at the edge of the threshold with her sword sheathed now and her face doing something complicated that a face trained for interrogation should never have been asked to do.

"I can hold the stairwell," she said. "Maybe twenty minutes. Maybe thirty if they're cautious, and they'll be cautious, because I told them there's an unstable working down here and anyone who rushes it gets unmade." She looked at the silhouettes in the far windows. Her jaw worked. "My mother's kitchen had blue tiles. I remembered that an hour ago for the first time in my life. I don't remember her face yet. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That the toll hasn't finished collecting," Odile said gently.

"That the toll hasn't finished collecting." Serah looked at me. "You wrote me into your book."

"Your name was already in it. I just made it legible."

She almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, and a true one. "Then write well, keeper. Write everything." And she turned and went back up toward the stairwell to buy us our twenty minutes, carrying her royal order rolled up under her arm like a torch she intended to put out.

* * *

Crossing is not dramatic. That surprised me. I had imagined light and thunder, the way impossible things are supposed to arrive. Instead, when Bramble went first—he insisted, said someone had to check the kitchens, and none of us had the heart to tell him there might not be one—he simply walked forward and thinned. That's the only word for it. He went thin as paper at the edges, and then the paper turned, and then he was on the other side, whole, shouting something about the cold and laughing, actually laughing, and the sound came through to us slightly delayed, like thunder after lightning.

One by one. That was the rule. The door took them singly, the way a careful lender does business.

Tamsin crossed second, clutching her forged-and-true documents, and came out the far side patting herself down like a woman checking her pockets after a pickpocket. "It took—" she started, and stopped, and frowned. "Something. I can feel the hole where it was. Isn't that strange? You can feel the shape of what's missing even when you can't remember what filled it."

"A debt leaves a dent," Odile said. "Even paid." She kissed Tamsin's cheek and sent her through.

Pip went third, holding the lock-picks, and on the other side he immediately began introducing himself to the door's mechanism—which was not a mechanism, but which he insisted had a *feel*, a grain, a willingness—and I heard him say, faintly, delightedly, "Oh, you're old. You're so old. Who made you? No—don't tell me—I want to work it out."

Then it was Odile, and then me, and then the question I had been avoiding since the fishmarket, since the vault, since the moment I first understood what the ledgers were for.

Odile stopped at the threshold and took my hands. Her grip was stronger than it had any right to be. "Keeper," she said. "Listen. Whatever it takes, it will take fairly. And what you keep will be enough. That's the whole secret of Vessany, child. Enough is enough. It always was. It's why we sold too much of ourselves once, and it's why we get to come home now—the ones who stayed behind kept just enough."

"What did the door take from you?" I asked. "When you left. Forty years ago."

Her eyes went distant and then focused, sharp as needles. "I don't know," she said. "And that is the truest thing I've ever told anyone. Go on now. Write us home."

She walked through, thinned, turned, was gone.

* * *

I stood alone before the threshold with the finished ledger under my arm and the unfinished one in my satchel and the weight of two hundred years of custody pressing down on me like snow.

I thought about Master Corvane.

I thought about the night I found the sealed ledger labeled *Vessany* in his hand, and the betrayal I'd felt—one remove, at first, and then not removed at all, because I had loved him and he had helped erase a country and both of those things were simply true, the way most important things are simply true and unbearable together. I thought about his lessons. *A record is not a mirror, Petra. A mirror shows you what is. A record holds what is in place. That's a different job, and it's harder, and no one thanks you.* I thought about his hands on the ledger spines, dusting them like livestock. I thought about his voice.

And I realized—with the strange calm that comes in the last minute before a thing becomes unavoidable—that I could not remember, with any certainty, whether his beard had grey in it or whether that was another man entirely, some guildmaster from a story, some composite. Forty years of other people's descriptions had worn grooves in my memory of him. I had his lessons perfectly. His face had been softening for years, like a watermark, like writing left in rain.

The door took it gently. There was no pain. There was a moment of reaching—for a face, for the specific beloved geography of brow and mouth and the way one eyebrow sat higher than the other when he was about to say something dry—and finding my hand closing on smooth, warm nothing. And then the grief of that, brief and clean, and then beneath the grief, untouched, entire, every lesson, every sentence, every principle, laid down in me like foundation stones.

*A record holds what is in place.*

I still knew that. I would always know that. It turned out that a man's teaching lives deeper than his face, and the door, being fair, knew it too.

I stepped through.

* * *

Cold air. Real cold, the honest cold of a country that had never agreed to stop existing. Snow under my boots—not the wrong snow of the harvest-month miracle in Caerlum, but old snow, layered snow, snow that had fallen in November and meant it. Woodsmoke. Bread. Someone singing, badly, three streets over, a song I didn't know and felt I should.

Bramble caught my elbow before I staggered. Behind him: a street. An actual street, cobbled, lamplit, lined with houses whose windows glowed amber, and at the end of it a square where people stood in the falling snow watching us come through a doorway that hung in the air beside a shuttered chandlery, a doorway of accumulated memory that was, even now, going quiet, going dim, going done—its work finished, its debts collected, its country returned.

The people in the square weren't cheering. They were doing something stranger. They were taking off their hats. All of them, old and young, silently, hats off in the snow, the gesture you make at a funeral and at a coronation and, apparently, at six ragged strangers walking out of a hole in the world.

An old woman detached herself from the crowd and came toward Odile, and the two of them stood facing each other for a long moment, and then the old woman said, "You're late," in the tone of someone greeting a sister, and Odile said, "Forty years. I had errands," and the whole square laughed and wept and surged forward, and Bramble was asking someone, urgently, about flour, and Tamsin was already scanning faces for the local forgers' guild, and Pip had found the town clock tower and was talking to it, and it was, I swear, talking back.

I stood apart, the way a keeper stands, and I opened the finished ledger to a fresh page.

The ink nearly froze in the well. I breathed on it. I wrote:

*Twenty-second day of the early winter. Crossed: six souls, one ledger, one set of kitchen knives, one boy's lock-picks. Toll collected: one memory apiece, taken fairly. Mine was Master Corvane's face. I report without sorrow beyond what is owed: I kept the lessons. They weigh more.*

*Vessany is real. The windows are lit. Begin again.*

Someone touched my shoulder. I looked up, expecting Bramble, expecting tears and bread.

It was neither. It was a girl of perhaps twelve, muffled to the eyes, snow in her lashes, staring at the ledger in my hands with an expression I recognized from the inside—from mirrors, from dark storerooms, from every night I had spent loving records no one else could read.

"You're the keeper," she said. Not a question. Word had traveled ahead of us; word had been traveling for forty years. "There are things here that need writing down. Things that happened while nobody was left who could prove them."

I closed the ledger and held it against my chest, and I felt its full weight settle onto me all over again, familiar as a yoke, and I heard myself say the words that begin everything:

"Then we'd best make an inventory."
