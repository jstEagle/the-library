## 22. The Last Page of the Caerlum Ledgers

The ink froze twice before I got the date right.

That was the thing nobody warned you about, in all the guild's old manuals: that a country could come back and still be cold. Vessany had waited forty years under its erasure like a sleeper waiting out a fever, and when it woke it woke hungry—for names, for bread, for the small ordinary heat of lit windows in winter—and it had not yet learned to warm itself again.

I sat in the kitchen of what had been Master Corvane's house, back when Corvane was a name that belonged to Caerlum instead of here, and I wrote.

*Entry the last,* I wrote, and then stopped, because my hands were shaking, and because Bramble had put a cup of something hot at my elbow without being asked, and because Odile was singing in the next room, badly, a harvest song with the words worn off it, and I wanted to get all of that down before it faded into just another day.

Ledger-keeping is not writing. That was the first lesson Corvane ever taught me, back when I was eleven years old and thought a ledger was a book where you wrote down what happened. *A ledger is a promise you make to someone who isn't born yet.* You don't write what happened. You write what must be remembered, and you write it so carefully that even a stranger—a fishwife's granddaughter, an inquisitor's clerk, some child digging through a sealed room forty winters from now—can hold the thing in their hands and feel its weight.

So let me do it properly. One last time.

*Entry the last.*

*In the year of the returned gods, in the first true winter of Vessany, the following persons crossed the door and were entered into the country's memory: Petra Hartwell, keeper of ledgers. Bramble Odo, cook, whose stews have kept more people alive than any medicine in either country. Tamsin Ferrow, forger, whose lies were truer than most truths. Wick, who talks to locks, and whom the locks talk back to. Odile of the old guild, eldest and last of the founders' generation, who remembered when remembering was a crime. And Serah Vole, once royal inquisitor, who set down her sword and picked up a pen, which is the harder discipline by far.*

*We paid the toll. Each of us gave up one memory, taken fairly, chosen by the door. I will not list them here. They are not mine to record anymore.*

I paused there, because my pen wanted to keep going—to write *I gave up Master Corvane's face*, to write about standing on the threshold and feeling it lift out of me like a splinter drawn clean, painless and total, so that afterward I knew everything he ever taught me and nothing at all of how he looked saying it. The others had gone through ahead of me. Serah had gone last but one, her hand still bleeding where she'd cut it on the ledger's clasp, and then it had been me and the door and the dark beneath the guildhall, and the door had asked—not in words, doors never ask in words—and I had said yes.

I remember his voice. I remember the shape of every lesson. I remember that he smelled of lamp oil and that he hummed when he audited, off-key, always the same three bars. But his face is a smooth place now, like a page someone scraped for reuse. When I try to see him I see instead the ledger he held, and I suppose—if I'm honest, and this book has no room left for dishonesty—that's the trade I'd make again. Faces fade anyway. Lessons keep.

But that's not what this entry is for. This entry is for whoever finds it.

* * *

Here is what happened after the crossing, briefly, because a closing entry should close things.

Vessany took us in the way a house takes in travelers—it fed us before it asked our names. There were people waiting on the far side. Of course there were; that was the whole point, that was the entire forty-year miracle of the place: a country exists only as long as somebody refuses to stop remembering it, and there had always been somebodies. Farmers who taught their children a forbidden alphabet in chalk on barn floors. Old women who set a place at the table for a country. A whole village that had answered every census with the same wrong answer until the clerks gave up asking.

They came out of the snowfields with lanterns, maybe thirty of them, and when they saw Odile they made a sound I will hear until I die—the sound a crowd makes when it recognizes something it had decided was gone forever. She stood there in the falling snow, ninety-one years old and half-mad and entirely sane at last, and she said, in the old tongue, "I kept the receipts," and thirty grown people wept like children.

Serah walked through behind us with her sword still sheathed and her writ of office still folded in her coat, and for a moment nobody knew what to do with her, least of all herself. Then an old man stepped forward—he turned out to be the reeve of the nearest village, a position that had continued existing for forty years on pure stubbornness—and asked her name, and she said "Vole," and he frowned and asked her mother's name, and she said it, and he nodded slowly and said, "Then your people are from the Marches. Your grandmother sold bread in the capital and sent money home every month. We wondered what happened to her."

I watched Serah take that in. I watched a woman who had built her whole self on the ruins of a hometown she wasn't allowed to mourn learn, in one sentence, that the hometown had been mourning her right back. She didn't cry. She did the thing she does instead of crying, which is stand very straight and say thank you in a voice like a locked door opening anyway.

She keeps the records now. Our records, and Vessany's—the ones the villages hid in flour barrels and false-bottomed chests for four decades. She is building the new archive in the old customs house, and she has started it, at her own insistence, with a complete account of the erasure: the bargain, the price, the unpaid gods, the names of everyone who kept the memory alive against law and sense. *The first honest history of our own disappearance,* she calls it. She says a country that doesn't write down its own worst day will only sell itself twice. She says it the way she used to say things as an inquisitor, flat and final, and then she'll look up and catch me watching and almost smile, which is new, and which I intend to keep.

The gods, paid at last—the debt settled in full when the door opened and forty years of accumulated memory poured back into the country like water into a dry well—the gods have gone quiet. Almost kind. The shrines that were stones all autumn are stones again, but warm ones now, and if you sit near one long enough you get the feeling of being listened to by something patient rather than something hungry. Wick swears the shrine near the mill told him a joke. Nobody believes Wick, including the shrine, probably. But nobody moves away from the stones anymore, either. That's the difference, and it's the whole difference.

Tamsin has stopped forging. Mostly. She says a country needs at least one forger the way a body needs an appendix—you don't use it daily, but you're glad it's there. She's teaching Wick letters, and Wick is teaching her lock-speech, and between them they've already opened two sealed rooms in the ruined quarter that nobody else could get into. What was inside them is Tamsin's story to tell, not mine. Some of it made her cry. Some of it made her laugh. It's good material, whatever it is. She'll want it written properly, with her name spelled correctly, which means I'll have to forge her signature, which she'll pretend to be furious about.

And Bramble—

Bramble is feeding everyone. That's all. That's everything. There are forty-three people eating at the long tables in what we've started calling the hall, though officially it's still a farmhouse, and Bramble knows all their names and all their allergies and exactly which of the Marches orphans won't eat anything brown. Last night I heard her tell the boy from the mill that seconds existed and were not a moral failing, and I thought: this is what a guild is. Not bottled echoes, not borrowed luck. A person who decides that strangers are hers, and then simply refuses to stop.

We named it aloud, once, the night before the crossing—our last supper in Caerlum, candles low because we were hiding from the mobs, and Odile raised her cup and called us family, each by name, the way you swear someone in. I am recording it here because Odile asked me to, and because Odile's requests are the closest thing I have left to scripture. *Family:* Hartwell. Odo. Ferrow. Wick. Odile. Vole. Written down, it holds.

* * *

Which brings me to the reason for this entry, and to the reason I'm sitting alone in a cold kitchen while the rest of them sing badly in the next room.

The door had to be sealed.

Not destroyed—destroying it would have killed the memory it was made of, and besides, Serah argued, and she was right, that a country should always know where its exits are. Sealed. Closed and quiet, the way you close a book you've finished but intend to lend out someday. The threshold still stands beneath what used to be the guildhall, made of accumulated memory, older than any wood, and it opens only for those whose names are written in the ledgers. So the question became: what happens to the ledgers?

Burn them, part of me said, the old fear talking. Burn them and no one follows us over. Keep the door shut forever.

But I sat with the archive one last time before we sealed it—all of it, every volume, forty years of impossible inventory plus a century of Vessany hidden underneath—and I understood finally, fully, what Corvane had spent his life trying to teach me and what it had taken a war's worth of winter to make me believe:

The ledgers don't record the impossible. They hold it in place.

Every entry is a nail. Every careful line of a keeper's hand is a wall holding back the forgetting. If I burned the Caerlum books, the door wouldn't just close—it would unravel, and Vessany with it, and every person on this side of the threshold would wake one morning in a country that had been unwritten twice. Custody doesn't end when you cross over. It ends when you find the next keeper.

So I copied.

It took nine days. I sat in the customs house while the snow came down and Serah brought me tea and corrected my dates, and I copied out the essential volumes—not everything, we couldn't carry everything, most of the storeroom stayed behind in Caerlum to burn beautifully when the mob reached it, and I have grieved that the way you grieve a library—but the core. The inventory of impossible things. The accounts of the bargain. The names of the rememberers. And this final book, the one in front of me now, which contains the whole of it in summary: what was lost, how it was found, what it cost, who paid, who kept the faith, and where the door stands.

Tomorrow we seal the passage. From this side. With Wick's help and the gods' permission and a rite Odile remembers from before the erasure, which involves honey, salt, and telling the door a true story it hasn't heard before. We'll give it this one.

And then I leave the book.

On the far side of the sealed door—in Caerlum, in the old vault, under the floor where Master Corvane kept his secrets—this ledger goes, wrapped in oilcloth, beside a lamp and a box of matches and a note that says only: *For the next keeper. Start anywhere. It all connects.*

Because somewhere in that city—maybe not today, maybe not for fifty years—there is a child crouched in a cellar reading a book they shouldn't have, or a clerk who asks one question too many, or a fishwife praying to a god with no name and getting an answer. Someone always remembers too much. That's how it started for me. That's how it starts for everyone. And when they come looking for answers, I want the answers to be waiting, warm as a lamp, patient as a ledger, saying: *you're not mad, it's real, here's the proof, here's the way, and you are not the first, and you will not be the last.*

Custody passed forward. That's the whole job. That was always the whole job.

* * *

So: the closing entries, properly made.

*Item: one country, recovered, population stubborn. Condition: improving. Payment rendered in full; no further debts outstanding.*

*Item: one door, sealed, sound, and sleeping. Opens to the named. Do not knock unless you mean it.*

*Item: six members of the Guild of Caerlum, accounted for, all living, all fed, all home—which is a word I am using deliberately, and which I checked twice before committing to the page.*

*Item: one guild, dissolved. Not dead. Dissolved, the way a seed dissolves—everything it was going into everything it will be. Any future keeper may consider themselves admitted upon reading this line.*

*Item: one keeper, retiring. Petra Hartwell, apprenticed at eleven, promoted by catastrophe, faithful to the end. Her memory of her master's face is forfeit to the door; his lessons remain in full working order. She regrets nothing except the handwriting on pages 114 through 118, which she did while crying, and asks that historians judge her accordingly.*

Bramble just looked in. She didn't say anything—she never does, when I'm working; she just sets down whatever the pot has and leaves, which is her way of saying *the world is still turning, come eat when you're done saving it*. The stew smells like barley and onions and the particular mercy of enough. In the next room Odile has moved on from the harvest song to a lullaby, worse, and Wick is laughing, and Tamsin is pretending not to conduct, and Serah's quill scratches in the corner where she works past midnight now, always, building her honest history one unflinching page at a time.

This is the warmth. I want whoever finds this to know that the story ends in warmth. Not triumph—triumph is loud and doesn't last—but warmth, which is quieter and lasts longer. We got out. We got through. We paid fair and we kept faith and we arrived, and tonight there is stew, and tomorrow there is work, and the gods are listening kindly, and my family is within shouting distance, which is the only wealth I ever actually wanted.

One last item, then. The first item. The one I wrote on my very first night in the storeroom, eleven years old, copying Corvane's own opening page because I couldn't think of anything better and he said that was the correct instinct—copy the masters until your own hand shows up, and then trust it.

He was right. My hand showed up. Here it is, at the end, saying what it said at the beginning, because some inventories are also prayers:

*Bottled echoes, forty-one. Borrowed luck, nearly all returned. The last hour of a dying man, unspent, and may it stay that way.*

*All present. All accounted for.*

*Entry closed.*

I set down the pen. I let the ink dry by the fire, properly, the way Corvane taught me—no smudged legacies, no blurred promises—and then I closed the cover, and the clasps caught with a sound like a door deciding.

Tomorrow, the sealing. Tonight, supper.

I blew out the lamp and went to where my family was singing.
