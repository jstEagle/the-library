## 1. The Valley Where Magic Cannot Follow

The trouble with bounties in Highmoor Valley was that nobody ever ran far enough to make the chase interesting.

Elodie Nakamura knew this because she'd been chasing them for eleven years, and in all that time the farthest anyone had gotten was the millpond at Thistlebrook, where a debtor named Corwin Hale had waded out to his chin and declared he'd rather drown than pay the tanner what he owed. He hadn't drowned. He'd caught a cold, paid the tanner, and apologized to Elodie for the inconvenience of the whole affair. That was Highmoor for you. Even its criminals were polite.

She rode down from the ridge at first light, breath steaming, the valley spread below her like something out of one of the peddler's ballads — patchwork fields going gold at the edges where autumn had begun its slow argument with summer, the river Vey running silver through the middle of it all, and the mountains ringing everything like cupped hands. Somewhere beyond those mountains was the rest of the world. Elodie had seen it exactly once, from the pass at Cold Saddle, when she was nineteen and stupid and full of a purpose that had since gone out of her like a coal left overnight in an untended grate.

She didn't look at the pass. She never looked at the pass.

Her horse — a dun mare named Biscuit, because her daughter had named her before there was a daughter, back when Elodie had still been the sort of person who found that kind of thing funny — picked her way down the switchbacks without being asked. Twenty years in this valley and the horse knew the roads better than she did. That was fine. The roads never changed. Nothing here changed. That was the point of the place, though it took Elodie most of those twenty years to understand that this was not a flaw in the design but the entire design itself.

Highmoor Valley was the one place in the world where magic could not follow.

Everyone said it differently. The farmers said it plain: nothing uncanny crossed the ridgeline. The priestess said it fancy, talking about wards and veils and the mercy of powers unnamed. The peddlers who came through twice a year said it with a shudder, describing cities where lamps burned without flame and bridges stood on pillars of hardened air, where a man could buy a love-charm off a corner table and wake up married to a stranger. In Corvassa — if half the stories were true — the Crown's own spires were grown, not built, coaxed up out of the earth by artificers whose grandfathers' grandfathers had signed contracts with things that lived under the world.

None of it worked here. Bring an enchanted blade across Cold Saddle and it was just steel by the time you reached the valley floor. Bring a curse and it curdled into nothing but bad temper. Elodie had tested it herself once, early on, pressing her thumb to the oath-mark on her inner wrist until it flared white-hot outside the ward line — and felt it go cool and dumb as an old scar the moment she descended past the boundary stones.

Twenty years ago, that silence would have broken her.

Now it was the only reason she could sleep.

* * *

The bounty was waiting for her at the Wayfarer's Rest, as posted bounties always were, nailed to the board beside the door between a notice about a lost goat (reward: one wheel of cheese) and a proclamation from the reeve about the proper disposal of slops. Elodie read it over a mug of small beer while the tavern keeper's boy pretended not to stare at the sword at her hip.

**WANTED**, the notice said, in the reeve's cramped hand. **DAGMAR OTT, WANTED FOR THEFT OF LIVESTOCK AND ASSAULT UPON THE TITHERMAN. LAST SEEN AT HIS BROTHER'S STEADING NEAR FALLOWMERE. 40 SILVER, ALIVE AND UNHARMED. 20 IF LESS COOPERATIVE.**

"Forty silver," she said to the boy. "What did Dagmar do, kick the titherman or marry his daughter?"

"Both," the boy said, with the gravity of youth delivering scandal. "In that order."

Elodie left the mug half-finished and went to earn her keep.

This was the work, then. This was what twenty years came down to: debtors in millponds, wife-beaters dragged before the reeve, sheep-thieves hauled back to answer for their crimes against the tithe. Small justice for small wrongs, done cleanly and without cruelty, because that was the code she'd built for herself out of the wreckage of the old one. No lies to the people who hired her. No harm to anyone's children, ever, no matter what they'd done. And no job that required leaving the valley.

People asked sometimes — usually travelers, usually drunk — why a woman with her scars and her sword-hand stayed somewhere so quiet, doing work any big farmhand could do. She'd developed answers over the years. The air agreed with her. Her knees were too old for long roads. She liked the cheese.

The truth was simpler and she kept it folded away like a letter she couldn't bear to reread: she had made a promise once, in another life, kneeling on stone with four other young fools while something vast and warm poured through her like sunlight through water. She had sworn herself to a god. And the god had answered — oh, the god had *answered*, had filled her up and named her and set a mark on her wrist like a brand of honor —

And then, one day in the worst year of her life, mid-battle, with blood in her mouth and her shield-sister screaming her name, the god had simply stopped answering. Mid-word. Mid-*breath*. Like a door closing in an empty house.

They called it the Silence. Everyone did, everywhere, though outside the valley they said it with different faces — grief in some places, relief in others, and in Corvassa, they said, a certain grim vindication. The Order of the Quiet had built a whole institution out of it. Elodie wouldn't know. She'd stopped reading outside news around the same time she stopped looking at the pass.

Here was the thing she had learned in the years after, the thing that had brought her home with her sword still sharp and her faith in pieces: in a world full of living magic, an oath-sworn mercenary with a dead god's mark on her arm was a target, a curiosity, a tool someone would eventually want to pick up and use. But inside Highmoor Valley, where magic couldn't follow, the mark was nothing. Just a scar. Here she was only a woman with a sword and a code, and the world was only the size of the next farmstead, and the worst thing that could happen on any given morning was a man named Dagmar Ott making poor choices about livestock and tithemen.

Restless contentment, the priestess Maren had called it once, watching her across the chapel candles with those knowing gray eyes. You've built yourself a very comfortable room, Elodie, and you pace it like a cage.

Elodie had told Maren that priests should stick to funerals. They'd laughed. It had been true anyway.

* * *

Fallowmere sat in the valley's southern crook, six farms strung along a creek like beads on wet string. Dagmar's brother's steading was the last of them, and Dagmar himself was in the barley field behind it, which told Elodie two things: he wasn't hiding hard, and he wasn't expecting her yet.

She tied Biscuit to the fence rather than riding up, which was a courtesy she'd learned cost her nothing and bought her more goodwill than any speech. Then she walked out into the stubble with her hands visible and her voice pitched easy.

"Morning, Dagmar."

He spun, pitchfork first, and immediately looked sorry about it. He was maybe thirty, big through the shoulders, with a black eye going yellow that matched the season. "Mercenary," he said, the way other men said *plague*. "I'm not going anywhere."

"I can see that." She stopped ten feet off, close enough to talk, far enough that the fork mattered. "You want to tell me your side before we walk back to Millbrook? I find the walking goes easier when I already know the story."

"There's no story." His knuckles were white on the haft. "Titherman Roke came out here, said my father owed back-tithe from three seasons, said he was taking the cow to cover it. My father's dead. The cow is my brother's widow's milk for the winter." His jaw worked. "So I told him what he could do with his ledger, and he swung at me, and I put him in the creek. That's the whole of it. Forty silver says I go stand trial in Millbrook, is that right?"

"That's right. Alive and unharmed gets you the full forty to me, which doesn't help you, but it does mean I have a reason to be gentle." She let him look at her a moment — the scar through her eyebrow, the gray coming in at her temples, the general weathered fact of her — and watched the fight go out of him by degrees, the way it did with most men once they understood she wasn't there to hurt them unless they made her. "Roke hit you first?"

"He swung first. Missed."

"And the cow stays put till the reeve rules on it?"

"The reeve can rule whatever he likes. Widow Brenna keeps her milk."

"Then we agree on everything except where you spend the next few nights." She held out the iron cuffs she carried for exactly this purpose, open, an offer rather than a threat. "These or my rope, Dagmar. One of them's kinder."

He looked at the cuffs for a long moment. Then, quietly, almost to himself: "There's nowhere to run anyway, is there. Not really. Nobody leaves the valley."

It wasn't bitterness. It was something older and stranger, a sentence every soul in Highmoor carried without thinking, the way they carried their names. *Nobody leaves.* As if the valley were less a place than a condition.

"No," Elodie said, and heard something in her own voice she didn't care for. "Nobody leaves."

* * *

The walk back to Millbrook took the better part of the day, and Dagmar turned out to be decent company, which was often how it went. He talked about his brother's widow and the cow — a good milker, apparently, with a personality — and asked careful questions about whether the reeve was likely to fine him into ruin or just give him a week's labor on the roads. Elodie gave him honest answers and thought, not for the first time, that this was the strangest possible shape her life could have taken. She had once stood in a circle of firelight with four companions while a god spoke through all of them at once, five voices saying one word, and the word had been like standing inside a bell.

Now she walked a sheep-thief to jail and discussed cows.

And here was the thing — the shameful thing, the thing she'd admit to no one, maybe not even to herself in the clear light of noon: some days, most days, she was *content*. Genuinely. The work was honest. The valley was beautiful in the way that only places you've memorized can be beautiful. There was a girl back in Millbrook — Tam, fourteen now, all elbows and opinions — whom Elodie had taken in at eight and who was teaching herself letters off the chapel's hymnal and arguing theology with Priestess Maren at a rate that suggested a future either very holy or very criminal. Elodie had a cottage with a leaky roof she kept meaning to fix and a herb garden she kept meaning to weed and a life assembled piece by piece out of small, solid, reliable things.

No gods in it. No magic. No voices.

Just quiet. The deep, sealed, mountain-kept quiet of Highmoor Valley, where the mark on her wrist was only a scar and the past could not follow, because nothing uncanny could cross the ridgeline, and her past was the most uncanny thing she'd ever known.

She was content. She was. It was only at night, some nights, lying awake listening to the wind come down off the peaks — wind that had crossed the whole width of the world outside, wind that had touched living magic and carried it right up to the ward line and no farther — that the contentment felt less like peace and more like holding your breath. Less like a home and more like a room with a very comfortable lock on the inside.

On those nights she pressed her thumb to the mark, feeling for heat, feeling for anything.

Cold. Always cold. Twenty years cold.

* * *

Millbrook at dusk smelled of woodsmoke and bread and the river. Elodie handed Dagmar to the reeve's deputy with a full account — self-defense, mitigating circumstances, the cow situation explained in detail sufficient to make the deputy's eyes glaze — collected her forty silver, and was halfway home along the river path when Tam came pelting toward her from the village, barefoot despite the season, which meant either disaster or excitement, and with Tam it was usually both.

"You're back!" The girl skidded to a stop, breathless. "Maren said to fetch you the second you came through. She's got someone at the chapel."

"It's nearly dark. Who's at the chapel?"

"That's the thing." Tam fell into step beside her, and Elodie saw now that beneath the excitement there was something else, something that had put a crack in the girl's voice. "Nobody knows. A child. Showed up on the road an hour ago, all alone, covered in mud like — like they crawled here from somewhere. And Elodie—" Tam grabbed her sleeve, and her eyes were wide and frightened in the fading light. "Nobody can understand a word the child says. Not Maren, not anybody. It's not any tongue anyone's ever heard."

Elodie stopped walking.

The river ran past them, silver-gray in the dusk, going about its ancient business. Above the ridgeline the first stars were coming out over the mountains, over the ward line, over the border between her quiet world and everything else.

"A child," Elodie said slowly. "Alone. On the road." From outside. From beyond. From the direction of Cold Saddle and the pass and the wide world where magic lived like a wolf lives in the woods. "Tam, where are the parents? Was there anyone with—"

"That's the other thing." Tam's grip tightened on her sleeve. "Maren sent me to get you special. She said—" The girl swallowed. "She said to tell you the child won't stop crying, and none of us can help, and that you'd want to see what it's holding in its hand."

"What is it holding?"

"A rock," Tam said. "White stone. Like chalk. And Elodie — it hasn't stopped humming since they picked it up. You can feel it in your teeth."

Elodie stood very still on the river path, forty silver heavy in her pocket, the dusk settling around her like a held breath.

Somewhere beneath her ribs, old and cold as a grave, something stirred.

She started walking toward the chapel, faster, then faster still, Tam trotting at her side — and on her wrist, for the first time in twenty years, the scar began, faintly, unmistakably, to burn.
