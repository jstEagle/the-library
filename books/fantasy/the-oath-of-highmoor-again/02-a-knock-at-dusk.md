## 2. A Knock at Dusk

The knock came at dusk, and it was wrong.

Not the rhythm—three raps, spaced like a courteous stranger's. It was the weight. Elodie Nakamura had lived alone at the edge of Highmoor Valley for eleven years, long enough to know every knock her door could make. The miller's boy delivering flour knocked twice, quick, already turning away. Old Tam with his aching teeth used the flat of his palm. Merchants on the valley road hammered like they were trying to wake the dead.

This knock was soft. Almost apologetic. Like whoever stood outside wasn't sure the door was real.

Elodie set down her whetstone and the skinning knife she'd been working over a hare carcass, and listened. Wind moved through the pines above the cottage in its usual evening complaint. Somewhere downslope, the stream muttered over stones. No horses. No second set of feet shifting weight on her porch. Just that silence after a knock, the kind that waits.

She crossed the room without hurry and took her sword off its pegs out of habit rather than fear. Eleven years of taking bounties in the Valley taught you that the dangerous ones never sounded dangerous. She didn't strap it on. She just carried it, loose in her right hand behind her thigh, and opened the door.

The child stood on the third step down because the first two were broken and she'd learned—or been taught—not to trust them. Mud to the knees, mud to the elbows, mud dried in pale crescents under her eyes where she'd wiped her face with dirty hands and only made it worse. Maybe seven years old. Maybe eight. Small for either. Her hair was dark and cut badly, hacked short in places with something dull, left long in others, as if she'd done it herself by feel in the dark.

Her clothes were worse than mud. They were wrong-cut, sewn in a style Elodie had never seen, dyed a faded rust-red with some pattern worked into the fabric that might have been leaves or might have been feathers or might have been nothing at all, worn past reading.

And she was staring at Elodie's left forearm.

Elodie glanced down. The oath-mark sat where it always sat, coiled around her wrist and up toward the elbow: a line of silvered script in no alphabet any scribe in the Valley could read, inked into her skin the night she swore herself to Veyra twenty years ago, back when Veyra still answered. It did what it always did, which was nothing. It hadn't done anything in twenty years. It was just a scar with pretensions.

Except the child was looking at it the way you look at a fire in winter.

"You're lost," Elodie said. "Valley road is east. Follow the stream and you'll hit the mill before full dark."

The child said something.

It was not Valley speech. It was not low-tongue, not high-tongue, not the trader's cant Elodie had picked up from caravans passing through, not the border dialects she'd heard from bounty jobs up near the passes. Elodie had a decent ear for languages—mercenary's necessity—and this one fit none of them. The vowels came from somewhere deep in the chest. The consonants clicked soft at the edges, like water over round stones. There was a cadence to it, rising and falling in deliberate patterns, that made it sound less like speech and more like recitation.

Like liturgy.

"Slower," Elodie said, which was stupid, because slow wouldn't help.

The child tried again, patiently, as though *she* were the reasonable one here. Same words. Elodie caught nothing except the shape of a question at the end—the tone rose, held, fell—and then the girl stopped, and her face did something complicated. Not frustration. Something older than frustration. A kind of recognition running into disappointment, like she'd expected the words to work and couldn't understand why they didn't.

Then her eyes went back to the arm.

"Stop looking at that," Elodie said, more sharply than she meant to.

The child flinched. Actually flinched, whole body, small shoulders jumping like a bird startled off a fence. And then—this was the part Elodie would turn over for nights afterward—she *apologized*. Bowed her head, pressed both muddy hands together, and produced three soft syllables with a falling, rueful intonation that needed no translation whatsoever. *Sorry. I'm sorry.*

Elodie had taken bounties on men who begged less sincerely.

"Gods' teeth." She exhaled through her nose and looked past the child, down the track, across her frost-browned garden, to the tree line going blue and dim with evening. No one. No tracks she could read from here in the failing light, but the ground between the pines would tell stories tomorrow if she cared to read them. "Where's your mother? Your—anyone? Who walked you up my hill?"

A shrug. A universal shrug, thank all the quiet gods, and then a burst of that impossible language, fast now, urgent, hands moving—the girl pointed east, toward the valley road, then north, toward the high passes, then made a shape with both hands like wings folding, like something descending, and pressed those folded hands against her own sternum.

Then she reached into her shirt.

Elodie's hand tightened on the sword behind her leg. Twenty years of instinct said *blade, always blade, even from children, especially from children*—and then the girl drew out not a weapon but a shard of white stone on a cord of braided sinew, no bigger than a man's thumb, and the world changed key.

Elodie felt it before she understood it.

Her oath-mark woke.

Twenty years of silence ended in a single heartbeat. The silvered script around her wrist flushed warm—then hot—then *singing*, a vibration climbing the bones of her forearm like a struck bell settling into resonance, and Elodie staggered half a step and caught the doorframe and heard herself make a sound she hadn't made since Tomas died, since the battlefield, since the moment the god's presence had withdrawn from her like a tide going out and never come back.

Because that was exactly what this was. That was the tide. That faint, vast pressure against the inside of her skin, that sense of something enormous paying attention—

The shard was humming too. A thin, clear note, audible now, physical, the cord vibrating against the child's muddy fingers. And the child was watching Elodie's arm with an expression of naked relief so total it looked like pain, saying the same three syllables over and over—*found you, found you, found you*—in whatever tongue the old world had spoken before kings.

"No," Elodie said. Her voice came out rough. "No, absolutely not. Whatever this is, whatever you think I am—" She crouched, slowly, putting herself below the child's eyeline the way you'd approach a spooked animal, and kept the sword hand empty and visible now, because the girl's gaze kept flicking to it. "I don't know what you've been told. I don't know who sent you. But I'm not—"

The child reached out and touched the oath-mark.

One muddy finger, light as a moth, laid against the first character of the script.

The hum resolved into a word.

Elodie didn't hear it with her ears. She heard it in the mark itself, in the scar-tissue script that had been dead lettering for two decades, and the word was *Veyra*, and it was spoken in the child's voice and in something behind the child's voice, something layered beneath it like bedrock under soil, and the sensation was so precisely, so unbearably *familiar*—the god's attention, the god's regard, the feeling of being known down to the grain—that Elodie's eyes filled before she could stop them.

She hadn't cried at Tomas's grave. She hadn't cried when the other two oath-brothers rode south and never wrote. She had built eleven years of competent, careful, restless contentment on the understanding that Veyra was gone, silent, dead or departed, and that grief was a debt paid in full.

One touch of a muddy child's finger, and the whole ledger tore open.

"*Don't*," Elodie whispered.

The child withdrew her hand. Sat back on her heels on the cold step. And waited—with that terrible, bottomless patience again, the patience of someone who has walked a very long way through mud for exactly this and will not be turned away by one weeping mercenary with a sword in her hand.

The wind picked up. The first flakes of evening frost began ticking softly against the porch boards. Full dark was perhaps forty minutes out, and the temperature was falling like a dropped stone, and the nearest roof other than Elodie's own belonged to the miller, six miles east, and the girl had clearly walked farther than that today given the state of her feet—Elodie could see now that one boot was split along the sole and bound with rag.

Every code she had, the whole tattered mercenary catechism she'd lived by since seventeen—*finish the job, guard your back, take no contract you can't carry, leave no one bleeding on the road*—that last one had teeth, and it had teeth because of Tomas, because she'd sworn beside him and failed him, and it closed around her throat now with the gentle, inexorable grip of a debt coming due.

"You can't stay," Elodie said.

The child tilted her head. Waited.

"I'm not a temple. I'm not a priestess. I kill people for coin and I skin hares on my own doorstep and I haven't prayed in eleven years because there hasn't been anything on the other side worth praying *to*." The words came out low and fast, and the girl understood none of them and somehow all of them. "Whatever woke up in my arm just now—I spent twenty years learning to live without it. You don't get to walk out of nowhere and hand it back."

The child considered this. Then she shivered—a full-body shiver, involuntary, the cold finally getting through whatever stubbornness was holding her upright—and pulled the shard against her chest with both hands, and looked at Elodie with an expression no mercenary alive could have withstood: not pleading. *Trust.* The absolute, unguarded, idiotic trust of a child standing on a stranger's step at dusk, in enemy territory by every law she knew, having decided—by means Elodie could not fathom—that this was the one door in the world that would open.

Elodie stood there a moment longer, sword hanging forgotten at her side, oath-mark still singing faintly against her skin like a tuning fork remembering its note.

Then she stepped back from the doorway.

"Inside," she said. "Before you freeze. And tomorrow—" she pointed east, down the darkening valley, "—tomorrow we go see Maren at the chapel, because if anyone in this gods-forsaken valley knows what language you're speaking, it's her, and if she doesn't, then nobody does, and then I genuinely don't know what I'll do with you."

The child stepped over the threshold. Paused on the worn boards, mud flaking from her boots onto floor Elodie had swept that morning, and looked up—way up—at Elodie's face with an expression of such solemn courtesy that it was almost funny, and pressed her muddy hands together again, and bowed.

Three soft syllables. The same falling, grateful lilt as before.

"Yeah," Elodie said, closing the door on the wind and the last grey light. "Yeah. Don't mention it."

She got the girl fed—hare stew, which the child ate with a restraint that broke Elodie's heart slightly, small careful spoonfuls, eyes flicking up between bites as if checking the food might be rescinded—and washed the worst of the mud from her face and hands with water warmed on the stove, and discovered beneath the grime a fine-boned, serious little face with a fading bruise along one jaw that made Elodie's blood move cold and slow in her veins. Someone had handled this child roughly, and recently. Someone had hurt her and lost her anyway.

She put her own oldest blanket around the girl's shoulders and built the fire up high, and the child sat close to it holding the white shard in both cupped palms, warming her fingers around it like a coal, and hummed to it sometimes, tunelessly, the way children hum to themselves. Each time she did, Elodie's mark answered—a faint warmth, a faint pulse, patient as a heartbeat—and each time Elodie told herself she was imagining it, and each time she knew she wasn't.

The girl fell asleep sitting up, mid-hum, somewhere past full dark. Elodie eased her down onto the cot by the fire, pulled the blanket to her chin, and stood a long time watching her breathe.

Then she took the whetstone to her sword by firelight, because her hands wanted work and her thoughts wanted somewhere to go, and the rhythmic rasp of steel was the only prayer she'd offered in eleven years.

Outside, the frost thickened. Inside, the child slept the deep, exhausted sleep of the very young and the very far from home, one fist curled around a shard of white stone, and the shard hummed on, barely audible, a note held steady beneath the wind.

Toward morning—it must have been near dawn, because the fire was down to embers—Elodie woke in her chair with a crick in her neck and realized the humming had changed. It wasn't random anymore. It was structured. Repeating. Rising and falling in deliberate patterns, vowels from deep in the chest, consonants clicking soft at the edges.

The child, asleep, eyes shut, breath slow, was speaking in her dreams.

And the shard was translating—no. The shard was *answering*. Note answering note, call responding to call, until the two voices braided into a single thread of sound that ran through the cottage like a plumb line dropped into the earth's deep places.

Elodie sat motionless in the dark, listening, and the hair rose slowly on her arms, and her oath-mark burned like a brand held to a candle flame.

Because she knew that pattern. She'd sworn an oath to that pattern, twenty years ago, kneeling in a grove with four companions while a living god looked down at them through the leaves.

The child was dreaming in Veyra's liturgy.
