## 3. Words Older Than Kingdoms

The child slept in Elodie's bed and Elodie took the floor.

Not out of kindness — or not only. A mercenary learned early that the person in the bed was the one you watched. She lay on her back on the cold boards with her sword across her knees and listened to the small, ragged breathing above her, and she told herself that come morning she'd walk the child to Maren at the chapel, hand the problem off to someone whose job was problems like this, and get back to the business of being a woman who killed wolves for coin.

The shard of white stone sat on the table where the child had left it. In the dark it gave off no light at all, which was somehow worse than if it had glowed.

Elodie did not sleep. She counted the cracks in the ceiling instead, and somewhere past the two hundredth crack she admitted what had been sitting in her chest since dusk, heavy as a swallowed stone: when the child's fingers had touched hers on the doorstep, when the shard had hummed against her oath-mark, she had felt something she hadn't felt in twenty years.

She had felt *answered*.

* * *

Morning came gray and thin, frost furring the window glass. The child woke without crying, which Elodie noted the way a soldier notes a comrade who doesn't flinch at thunder — with respect and a low, quiet alarm. The girl sat up, looked around the room as if confirming its shape against some memory, and then looked at Elodie with those too-old eyes and spoke again.

That language. Elodie had heard a hundred tongues in her years at the valley's edge — traders' pidgin from the eastern passes, the clipped mountain speech of the Korren clans, even once, drunk and boasting, a caravan guard swearing in old Imperial. This was none of them. It moved differently. It didn't rise and fall so much as *turn*, each phrase circling back on itself like a bird riding a thermal, and there were sounds in it Elodie's own mouth felt sure no human throat should make — soft stops, breath shaped into meaning.

The child said something long and careful, watching Elodie's face for understanding, and got none, and something in the small shoulders dropped half an inch. Then the girl tried again, slower, pointing at herself.

"Ilyen."

A name. Maybe a name. Elodie pointed at the child. "Ilyen?"

The girl nodded, quick and hungry.

Elodie pointed at herself. "Elodie."

*Ilyen* tried it. What came out was closer to *Ell-o-dee-ah*, four syllables where three belonged, and something about the wrongness of it snagged against the oath-mark under Elodie's sleeve like a fishhook. She ignored that. She was getting good at ignoring things before dawn.

She fed the child porridge with the last of her honey. Ilyen ate like someone who had known hunger not as an event but as a climate — carefully, completely, no crumb wasted — and Elodie found herself eating less than usual so the bowl would last longer, and hating herself for how easily the instinct came.

"Wash your face," she said, knowing the words meant nothing, and demonstrated at the basin. Ilyen copied her exactly, water dripping off a chin sharp as a bird's.

Then Elodie wrapped the shard in cloth, tucked it inside her coat — because leaving it in the cottage while they walked out felt like leaving a lit lamp in a hay barn — and took the child by the hand.

Maren would know. Maren knew everything, which was the most irritating thing about her.

* * *

Ashford-on-the-Wold woke slow, as it always did. Smoke from chimneys stood straight up in the windless cold. A dog followed them the length of the high street, decided they were boring, and lost interest. Elodie nodded to the miller's wife, who nodded back and looked at the child with the particular open curiosity of a woman already composing the story she'd tell at market: *found on Elodie Winterbourne's doorstep, mud to the ears, talking gibberish.*

By noon the whole village would have opinions. By evening the opinions would have wives and children and occupations of their own. That was how Ashford worked. Elodie had chosen to live in a place where nothing stayed secret for more than a day, on the theory that a life with no secrets was a life with nothing worth digging up. Twenty years of testing had mostly held.

The chapel of the Silent Vigil squatted at the end of the lane, stone gone green at the base, its single bell unringed so long that birds nested in the yoke. Inside smelled of tallow and old incense and the particular dust of places where people had wept.

Maren was on her knees scrubbing the flagstones, sleeves rolled, gray hair pinned up with what appeared to be a pencil. She rose with the groan of a woman whose knees had opinions, saw Elodie, saw the child, and set down her brush with great deliberateness.

"So," she said. "It's finally happened. You've stolen a baby."

"She came to me. Last night." Elodie unwrapped the shard and set it on the chapel table between a candle stub and a chipped dish of consecrated salt. "This came with her. And this—" she tapped her own forearm through the sleeve, where the mark sat "—did something I haven't felt in twenty years."

Maren went still in the way that only very old people and very good hunters can go still. Her eyes moved from the shard to Elodie's arm to the child standing half behind Elodie's skirt, gripping a fistful of wool.

"What," Maren said quietly, "did it feel like."

"Like someone knocking on a door I'd nailed shut."

"Show me your arm."

"Maren—"

"*Show me your arm*, Edmund's ghost, you stubborn woman, this is not a morning for pride." And there it was, the other thing about Maren: under the scolding lived iron, and every few years the iron showed itself, and when it did, people obeyed.

Elodie pushed back her sleeve.

The oath-mark sat on her inner forearm as it always had: five lines converging on a circle, inked in silver-wash the night she and four others knelt in the rain and swore themselves to Veyra. Twenty years faded, the color of old pewter. Dead work. Every sworn sword in the valley carried one, and every one of them had gone dark the same season, mid-battle, mid-prayer, mid-everything, when their god stopped speaking.

But this morning the lines were not quite dead. They were warm. They were, if she tilted her arm to the light, faintly gold at the edges, like embers remembered rather than seen.

Maren touched the mark with one fingertip and drew a breath through her teeth.

"Now the stone," she said.

Elodie lifted the shard free of the cloth. Up close, in daylight, it was worse than it had been by firelight — not opaque but *deep*, white going down into white, the way snow goes down into a crevasse. And as her bare skin met its surface, the thing began to sing.

Not aloud. Not a sound anyone else could hear; Maren's face confirmed that, tilted, listening, hearing nothing. But against Elodie's palm and up through the bones of her wrist and into the oath-mark it sang, a single sustained tone like the moment after a bell is struck, and the mark on her arm answered it, warming, brightening, until the five converging lines stood out gold as fresh-struck coin.

Ilyen made a sound — the circling language, urgent now — and reached for the shard, and when her fingers closed over Elodie's around the stone the tone *doubled*. Two voices. A chord.

The candle stub on the table ignited. No flame touched the wick; the flame simply *was*, standing upright in still air, burning blue-white and cold.

Maren stepped back. Knocked over the salt dish. Did not notice.

"Put it down," she said. "Slowly. Put it *down*, girl, before the whole vigil-house learns what fire feels like."

Elodie laid the shard on the table. The flame guttered out. The singing faded to silence, and the mark dimmed to ember-gray, and the absence of it hit Elodie like stepping out of sunlight into a cellar. She hadn't realized, until it left, how much of her tension had been the stone humming in her bones.

For a long moment nobody spoke. Outside, a cart rattled past. Somewhere a rooster made its idiot announcement. The world continued to be ordinary, which felt increasingly like a lie everyone had agreed to tell.

"Maren." Elodie's voice came out lower than she intended. "What is that stone?"

"I don't know what it is." Maren braced both hands on the table and stared at it without touching. "But I think I know what the child is saying."

"You understand her?"

"No." Maren laughed, short and unhappy. "Gods' truth, no. But I've read it." She wiped her hands on her apron as though the morning had dirtied them. "Before I took the vigil here, I studied at the seminary at Corvassa. Before they asked me to leave, which is a story for another decade. In the archive levels there are books no one reads — liturgies from before the kingdoms, before the Empire, from the time when the faiths were young enough to be honest about what they were worshipping. We called the language First Tongue. Nobody living speaks it. Nobody living has spoken it in eight hundred years."

"And she does."

"The child has been speaking it since she walked in. You may not hear it plainly, but I do — fragments. Phrases." Maren turned to face Ilyen, and crouched, slowly, to bring her ancient eyes level with the girl's. "Say it again," she said gently, uselessly, in Common. "Whatever you said when you first came in. Say it again, little one."

Ilyen looked at Elodie. Elodie nodded.

The child spoke. Six syllables, circling, turning, ending on a sound like a held breath released.

Maren closed her eyes. When she opened them they were wet.

"That's not a sentence," she whispered. "That's an invocation. The opening line of the Liturgy of the Keeping. *We hold the door. We hold the door shut.*" Her voice cracked on the last word. "It's a prayer of sealing, Elodie. A prayer from the oldest rite in existence, the one the seminary fathers said was never used because no one alive remembered what it sealed."

The chapel felt colder. Elodie became aware of her own heartbeat, steady and stupid, doing its work regardless.

"A seal," she repeated.

"Mm." Maren straightened, wincing. "And the stone that sings to your dead god-mark, and the child who arrived out of nowhere wearing mud from ground I'd wager my vestments isn't valley mud, all on the same night, after twenty years of your god saying nothing?" She shook her head. "In my experience, the divine doesn't knock twice."

* * *

They sat afterward in Maren's little cell behind the sanctuary, tea that tasted mostly of bark, Ilyen asleep on the narrow cot with the shard clutched to her chest even in sleep, the way other children clutch dolls.

"She won't give it up," Elodie murmured.

"No. And I wouldn't try to take it." Maren wrapped her hands around her cup. Steam curled past her face, blurring and unblurring her expression. "I'm going to tell you something I've told no one in forty years, and you're going to sit there and listen without interrupting, because interrupting is the only skill you've ever mastered."

Elodie drank her terrible tea.

"When my god fell silent," Maren said, "the seminary had answers. They always have answers; it's their trade. They said Veyra had withdrawn in displeasure. They said the faithful had failed some test. They said pray harder, tithe more, doubt less." Her mouth twisted. "But there was an old man in the archive levels — a copyist, half-blind, half-mad, the kind of man institutions keep because throwing him out would be admitting he existed. And he told me once, when I was young enough to listen to madmen, that the Silence wasn't a withdrawal at all. He said the god hadn't gone anywhere. He said the god had been *put* somewhere."

Elodie set down her cup. It clicked against the saucer louder than she liked.

"He said the valley was involved," Maren went on. "He said Highmoor wasn't a refuge, wasn't holy ground, wasn't any of the comfortable things the faithful say. He said it was built. Warded. That magic doesn't cross the valley line because something on the inside holds it out — holds everything out — the way a wall holds out weather." She looked up. "You've lived your whole life here, child. Have you ever wondered why our valley alone in all the world is silent to the gods? Why spells die at our borders? Why nothing wild grows past the ridgelines except what men planted?"

"Everyone says it's blessed."

"Everyone says lots of things." Maren's voice was very dry. "Blessed ground doesn't need walls. Prisons do."

The word landed in Elodie's stomach and stayed there, cold.

"I told myself he was mad," Maren said. "Forty years, I told myself. It was easier. And then last night a mute-by-choice child walks out of nowhere speaking a dead prayer-language, carrying a stone that wakes your oath like a struck match, and lands on the doorstep of the one woman in this valley whose mark is older and deeper than any other sworn sword's — because yours wasn't just a soldier's oath, was it? Yours was made at the Godstone, with four others, in the last hour before the Silence. Yours was the last vow Veyra ever heard."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I buried Tomas," Maren said simply. "And because I've kept records nobody asked me to keep. It's what priests do when the gods go quiet. We write things down and wait."

The fire popped. On the cot, Ilyen shifted, and the shard in her arms caught the firelight and threw back a gleam that was too white, too clean, like a sliver cut from something enormous.

"If the madman was right," Maren said softly, "then the Silence has a lock. And locks have keys. And keys—" she nodded at the sleeping child "—walk out of nowhere when the mechanism starts to fail."

"Fail how?"

"I don't know. But I know what happens next, and so do you, because you've been waiting for it for twenty years without letting yourself say it." Maren leaned forward. "If that stone is waking, if the ward is thinning, then someone will come. The Crown keeps the Order of the Quiet for exactly one purpose, and it isn't charity. When they learn a vessel of the covenant has surfaced in Highmoor Valley, they will send riders. Not soldiers — riders. Quiet ones. And they will ask the villages polite questions, and the villages will answer, because villages always answer, and then they will come to your door, Elodie Winterbourne, and they will not be asking."

Outside, far off across the fields, a church bell in some neighboring parish rang the hour. Elodie counted the strokes without meaning to. Four o'clock. The light through the cell's one window had gone amber and long.

She looked at the child. Small. Mud-caked still, despite the washing. Clutching a piece of a god the way a drowning sailor clutches driftwood — and maybe that was exactly what she was doing. Maybe the child had been drowning for a long time, in whatever deep water the madman's story implied, and had swum toward the one light left burning.

Or maybe she was bait. Or a weapon. Or a mistake.

"Twenty years ago I swore an oath," Elodie said, "and my god went silent in the middle of it, and a man died, and I have spent every year since telling myself the oath was finished. Done. A debt to a creditor who left town." She turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "If you're right — if any part of this is right — then the oath wasn't finished. It was *paused*."

"Yes."

"And you're telling me to run."

"I'm telling you," Maren said, "that you have until the riders crest the ridge to decide whether you're the woman who ran, or the woman the oath was waiting for."

Which was, of course, the exact moment when Tam Whitlow's boy came pounding up the chapel path, breathless, boots hammering the frozen mud, and burst through the door without knocking the way boys do, shouting that there were horses on the south ridgeline, six of them, gray-cloaked, flying no colors at all — and that the reeve had sent him running to fetch the priestess, because the riders had asked, politely, in accents from the capital, whether anyone had seen a child.
