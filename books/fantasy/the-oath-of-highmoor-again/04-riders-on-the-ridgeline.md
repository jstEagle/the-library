## 4. Riders on the Ridgeline

The horses came before the men did.

Ilyen heard them first as a tremor in the ground, a faint drumming felt through her bare feet on Elodie's floorboards, and she went still the way small animals go still, the way she had gone still once before in another life she could not remember but sometimes dreamed: a white place, a voice like snowfall, hands made of light setting something precious into her chest.

Then the sound was gone, and she almost let herself believe it had been nothing.

* * *

The village of Thornmere woke to the riders the way a body wakes to cold water.

Ilyen stood at the window of the cottage with the stone shard pressed against her collarbone where it lived now, warm as a second heart. Down the lane, six figures crested the ridgeline above the barley fields, black-cloaked against the grey morning sky, and even from here — even without words, which were still mostly rain-sound to her, shapes that slid off meaning — Ilyen understood what they were.

She understood it in the way the shard understood.

The stone at her throat grew hot. Not painfully hot, not yet, but a deep banked heat, like a hearthstone hours after the fire dies, and beneath the heat there was a hum, low and constant, that she felt in her teeth and behind her eyes and in the soft hollows of her wrists. The hum said one thing, over and over, in no language at all:

*Wrong. Wrong. Wrong.*

She pressed her palm flat against the glass.

Below, the riders had reached the first fence line. They did not slow for it. One of them simply put his horse over the rail and kept coming, and the splintering crack of wood carried up the hill and into the cottage like a knock.

Behind her, Elodie was already moving.

Ilyen watched her in the reflection — the tall woman moving through the small room with a terrible economy, pulling on boots without sitting down, sweeping three knives from a drawer into a belt, checking each blade by thumb with the absent ritual of someone who has done this ten thousand mornings and never needed any of it until now. Her face gave away nothing. That was the thing about the mercenary woman that frightened Ilyen most, more than the riders, more than the hum: Elodie's face was a door that closed.

But her hands shook.

Only a little. Only when she thought no one was looking. She buckled the belt and her fingers slipped the pin twice, and Ilyen saw it, because Ilyen had spent her whole short remembering life learning to read what people hid. It was how you survived when you had no words. You learned the language underneath the language.

Elodie's hands were saying *again*. Whatever this was, it had happened to her before, and it had not ended well.

* * *

They walked down into the village together, because Elodie said they would be less remarkable walking than hiding, and hiding was an answer to a question not yet asked. Ilyen held her hand. Elodie's grip was dry and calloused and did not tighten or soften, just held, steady as a fencepost, and Ilyen found that she loved the hand a little, in the fierce wordless way she loved the shard — not because it was kind, but because it was *there*, and everything else in the world seemed lately to be leaving.

Thornmere's single street had filled with people standing in doorways. The smith. The miller's wife with flour still on her arms. Old Tam with his milking pail forgotten in his fist. They lined the lane the way trees line a road, silent, watching the six riders come, and Ilyen read the crowd the way she read everything.

Fear. But layered fear, like sediment.

There was the surface fear, ordinary and animal, the fear anyone feels when armed strangers arrive uninvited. And beneath it, older and stranger, there was something else — a shame, almost, a flinching, the look of people who have been told all their lives that they are protected from something and are only now realizing that protection has a price, and the price is obedience, and obedience is being collected today.

And beneath *that*, deepest of all, in a few faces — the priestess's, the cooper's youngest son, a girl selling apples who could not have been more than ten — Ilyen found something that made the shard flare hot against her skin.

Longing.

They looked at the riders the way starving people look at a full table. As if the riders carried something those few had been denied their whole lives and knew, dimly, forever, that they were missing.

The hum in the stone shifted. Warmed. Ilyen filed it away in the place where she kept everything she could not yet say.

* * *

The lead rider reined in at the well at the center of the village square, and his five companions fanned out behind him with drilled precision, two to each end of the street, one circling back toward the fields. Cutting off the road north. Ilyen understood that too, without understanding how she knew: these were movements designed long ago, by people who were not here, for moments exactly like this one.

The leader pulled off his gauntlet and drew back his hood.

He was younger than Elodie, grey at the temples anyway, with a face like a coin worn smooth — features rubbed down by repetition into something generic and official, the face of an office rather than a man. On his breast, worked in silver thread, was an emblem Ilyen had never seen and recognized instantly, the way she'd recognized the liturgy-fragments the priestess had gasped over: a closed eye inside a closed mouth, stitched shut with silver wire.

The Order of the Quiet. The name arrived in her head whole, unasked for, the way the old words arrived. She did not know where her knowledge came from. She was beginning to suspect she did not want to know.

"Hearth and hold," the rider called out, in a voice pitched to carry, warm as a merchant's greeting. "Be easy, goodfolk of Thornmere. We are no taxmen." A ripple of laughter, thin and dutiful, died fast. "We ride for the Crown's Office of Inquiry, on a matter touching the safety of the realm. We ask only your help."

He let the silence work. Ilyen watched him let it work. He was good at this; she could see he was good at this, that the warmth was a tool honed as fine as Elodie's knives.

"Three nights past," he said, "a child passed along this valley road. Small. Dark-haired. Perhaps seven years, perhaps eight. Alone." He paused, and smiled, and the smile did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "Perhaps not alone. She may have been confused, or hurt, or fleeing some cruelty — poor mite. The Crown wishes only to see her safe."

Every word was true. Every word was a lie. Ilyen felt the double-ness of it like a smell, thick in her nose, and the stone burned hotter, and she had to bite down hard on the inside of her cheek to keep her face still. Elodie's hand tightened around hers. Once. Deliberately. *Say nothing. Show nothing.* As if she'd spoken aloud.

An old woman near the well — Ilyen didn't know her name yet, but the village knew her, everyone deferred to the space around her — cleared her throat. "Haven't seen any such child, m'lord."

"No?" The rider turned his smooth coin-face toward the crowd, unhurried, letting his gaze travel across them the way a man runs a hand across a fence checking for loose rails. "That is strange. Because our trackers found small footprints at the ford below Harrow Fell, heading south. Toward Thornmere. And the mud was fresh."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature. Somewhere a dog began to bark and was hastily hushed.

"We are patient people," the rider said pleasantly. "The Office rewards honesty generously. Silver, for information. Land, for cooperation." He drew out a purse and let it swing once from his finger, and Ilyen watched the sound of the coins move through the crowd like a wind through wheat. She watched the smith glance at it. Watched the miller's wife glance at it. Watched old Tam's knuckles whiten around his pail.

And then she watched the priestess step forward.

Maren was small and stooped and dressed in the faded blue of a valley order so old its god had no name anymore, and when she stepped into the open her hands were folded and her spine was straight, and Ilyen felt the shard go suddenly, shockingly quiet — attentive, like a held breath.

"Riders," Maren said, in a cracked voice that nevertheless carried, "this is Highmoor Valley. You know what that means, or you should, wearing that badge. Nothing of the art can cross our bounds. Your office has no writ here that means anything, because there is nothing here for your writ to touch." She lifted her chin. "No child of ours has fallen to sorcery in living memory, because there is no sorcery to fall to. You've ridden two days for nothing. Take your silver home."

It was brave. Ilyen could see that it was brave, and see also that it was a mistake, because the rider's smile widened by a precise fraction, the smile of a fisherman feeling a tug on the line.

"The valley ward," he said softly. "Yes. The famous ward. Centuries old, isn't it? Older than the kingdom. Older than the crown it predates." He dismounted, slowly, boots hitting the packed earth, and walked toward Maren with his gauntlets tucked under one arm. "You know what interests me, mother? Wards don't last centuries. Nothing lasts centuries. Stone crumbles. Iron rusts. Even mountains wear down, given enough rain." He stopped an arm's length from her. "Wards have to be *maintained*. Fed. Tended." His voice dropped, gentle as a lullaby. "So tell me, mother of a nameless god — who tends yours?"

Nobody breathed.

And Ilyen, watching, felt the shard pulse once — hard, urgent, a heartbeat's worth of heat — and in that pulse came something new. Not the warning-hum of *wrong*. Something else. Something that felt horribly like recognition.

Because the rider was wrong about one thing. He thought he was asking a question.

He wasn't. He was answering one. The question the shard had been humming since the day Ilyen woke in the mud outside Elodie's door with a hole in her memory shaped exactly like a purpose. *Who tends the ward?*

*I do,* said the heat against her collarbone. *I am the tending. I am what's left of it.*

And the rider knew it too, somehow, or half-knew it, or was close enough to knowing that his office had sent him sniffing up valleys after footprints. *The vessel.* She'd felt the shape of that word in his mouth before he spoke it, felt it waiting behind his teeth like a hook behind bait.

*Safe,* he'd said. *The Crown wishes only to see her safe.*

The stone burned. Ilyen looked down at her own feet, small and muddy in Elodie's borrowed boots, and understood with perfect clarity that every person in this square was deciding, right now, in the privacy of their own frightened hearts, whether she was a child to be sheltered or a problem to be handed over. She could see the decision happening behind the smith's eyes. Behind the miller's wife's. Silver for information. Land for cooperation.

Her chest hurt. She realized she was gripping Elodie's hand hard enough to bruise and made herself loosen, finger by finger, because a child who clings looks guilty, and guilty things get given away.

Elodie looked down at her. Just for a moment. Their eyes met, and the mercenary's face was still a closed door — but her thumb moved once across Ilyen's knuckles, slow and deliberate, and Ilyen translated it easily, because translation was the one thing she was made for:

*Not yet. Not while I stand here.*

It was not a promise about tomorrow. Ilyen noticed that. She was learning, very fast, how much of adult kindness was true only in the present tense.

* * *

The rider — Serane, someone whispered, Inquisitor-Captain Serane, though Ilyen stored the name without understanding why it made Elodie's breathing change — asked his questions for the better part of an hour.

He was patient. He sat his horse in the square and let his men do the knocking, door to door, polite and thorough, and he thanked each villager for their time with the same coin-smile, and little by little the picture assembled itself in Ilyen's mind, clear as frost forming on glass. He would not find her by asking. He knew that. The asking was theater. What he was actually doing was watching faces — watching who wouldn't meet his eye, whose answers came too quickly, who glanced, even for half a heartbeat, toward the cottages on the hill.

Toward, Ilyen realized with a cold drop in her stomach, the direction of Elodie's door.

Twice, the rider's gaze drifted up the lane and rested, briefly, on the lone cottage at its end. Twice. No more. A man counting something. A man confirming that a house existed where his map said a house should be, occupied by a woman his files said should occupy it.

Elodie stood in the crowd with her arms crossed and her face bored, and when Serane's eyes touched her she looked back at him with the flat incurious stare of a woman watching weather, and it was masterful, it was flawless, and Ilyen — pressed against her side, hidden in the shadow of her arm — felt her heart hammering so hard she was certain the whole square could hear it.

The miller's wife broke first.

Not badly. Not dramatically. Just — when Serane's man knocked on her door and asked again, gently, about footprints by the ford, her eyes flicked upward. Half a heartbeat. Toward the ridge road, the north road, the road that led past Elodie's cottage and out of the valley.

Half a heartbeat, and the rider's man smiled and wrote something in a little book, and the miller's wife went white and said she hadn't seen anything, sir, nothing at all, and closed her door, and stood behind it shaking.

Ilyen buried her face in Elodie's coat and breathed.

* * *

They left at dusk. That was the worst part, Ilyen thought later — that they left. Five riders took the south road, back the way they'd come, unhurried, almost jaunty, and the sixth turned his horse north at the crossroads and rode slowly up the valley with a lantern swinging at his saddlebow, taking his time, looking at fences, looking at fields, looking at the darkening windows of the last cottage on the lane.

Looking at home.

The village gathered in the square as the hoofbeats faded, and the argument began before the sound was fully gone — low and furious and frightened, everyone talking over everyone, and Ilyen stood in the middle of it holding the shard through her shirt and listening to the language underneath the language.

Old Tam wanted her gone tonight, carried out over the fell, given to nobody, just *gone*, because his father had told him stories of what came before the Quiet and none of the stories ended with the village still standing. The smith said nothing, which was its own kind of answer. The cooper's son — the one with longing behind his eyes — argued fiercely, stupidly, bravely, that no child should be fed to any office, and his father dragged him home by the collar.

And Maren the priestess stood apart from all of it, arms folded into her sleeves, looking north up the darkening lane toward a cottage with a lamp beginning to glow in its window.

Finally she turned to Elodie.

"She cannot stay," the old woman said, and her voice cracked on it, and Ilyen saw that saying it cost her something real. "Child, listen to me. That man will come back. Men like that always come back, and next time they won't knock. There is one road out of this valley that they don't watch, because no one who takes it ever comes back — and I am asking you, woman, I am begging you, to take my heretic's blessing and walk it."

Elodie was quiet a long moment. The lamp in her window threw her shadow long across the frozen ground, all the way to Ilyen's feet.

"I swore an oath once," she said at last, to no one, to the dark, to the silent god whose name the valley had forgotten. "It didn't take. Maybe this one will."

She picked Ilyen up — Ilyen let herself be picked up, boneless, exhausted, the shard humming its low warm note against both their chests now, as if it too had chosen — and turned north, toward the fells, toward the boundary no one crossed, toward whatever waited past the edge of the only world either of them had ever known.

Behind them, far up the valley, a lantern swung gently in the dark.

Still coming.
