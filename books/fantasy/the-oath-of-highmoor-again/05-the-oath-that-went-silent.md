## 5. The Oath That Went Silent

The child slept in the loft above the hearth, and Elodie sat below with a whetstone and a sword she no longer needed to sharpen, because the work was for her hands and not the blade.

She had done this before. Sat awake in the small hours with someone else's breathing overhead and her own thoughts circling like crows. Twenty years ago it had been Tomas snoring in the next room over, loud enough to wake the dead, and she'd complained about it every night right up until the night there was nothing left to complain about.

The whetstone whispered along the steel. *Shhk. Shhk.*

She should sleep. Tomorrow — or the day after, or however many days the priestess needed — someone would come asking questions about the girl in her loft, and Elodie wanted her edge keen and her mind keener. Instead she found herself staring at the back of her own hand, at the mark that lived there beneath the skin like a coin dropped in milk.

Five interlocking rings, pale as old scar tissue.

*You swore,* said the memory, in Tomas's voice, because of course it was Tomas's voice. It was always Tomas's voice when she was too tired to hold the door shut. *We all swore. You don't get to pretend you didn't hear the answer.*

"I didn't hear an answer," she muttered to the empty room. "That's rather the whole problem."

The fire ticked. Above her, the child turned over in her sleep and murmured something in that language older than any kingdom, and the shard of white stone on the table — wrapped now in wool, muffled as a struck bell — hummed once against the wood.

Against the wood, and against the oath-mark under Elodie's skin, which warmed like a hand laid flat on a wound.

She looked at it a long time.

Then she set down the sword, poured herself the last two fingers of plum brandy, and let the door in her head swing open, because some nights you can hold it and some nights you can't, and twenty years is a long time to lean on a door.

* * *

They had been five, in those days. Five young fools with good boots and better opinions, walking the long roads out of Highmoor Valley doing contract work for anyone who'd pay: caravans guarded, bandit camps burned, one memorable season clearing river-wyrms from the ferry crossings near Marrow Ford. Sarel had kept their books. Halvard had kept their spirits up, mostly through volume. Serane had kept them alive, because Serane noticed everything — the loose roof tile, the too-friendly innkeeper, the second shadow behind the third pillar — and said so in that dry voice that made you feel foolish and grateful at once.

Tomas had kept them laughing. And Elodie?

Elodie had kept the faith. That was the joke they'd made about it, back when jokes about gods were still allowed at their table. *Elodie keeps the faith.* Because she was the one who still stopped at the roadside shrines, the little stone waymarkers with the five-ringed sigil worn smooth by centuries of travelers' thumbs. She'd press her palm to the ring-mark and say the old words her grandmother taught her — not prayers exactly, more like courtesies. *Walk with us, Quiet One. We remember you're listening. Or we hope you are. Either way, the road's long, and company helps.*

Veyra, the Quiet God. The Hushed One. The Listener at the Edge of the World.

Every child in Highmoor grew up on the same stories: how Veyra had walked the mortal realm in the elder days and spoken with such power that mountains moved to make room; how the god had grown weary of speech, or wise enough to renounce it, and had gone silent — not absent, never absent, just *quiet*, the way a father is quiet in the next room. Still there. Still keeping watch. The silence wasn't abandonment. The silence was *trust*. Veyra trusted mortals to govern themselves, and in return mortals kept the covenant: oaths sworn in the god's name held true, promises made on the five rings could not be broken, and as long as the faithful remembered, the world stayed gentle at its edges.

It was a comfortable theology. It asked very little and promised much. Elodie had loved it the way you love a house you grew up in — without ever once examining the foundations.

She was twenty-three the year the war came north.

Not a great war. Not a war with a name in the histories. A border war, the ugly kind, all mud and fever and villages burned by both sides for reasons nobody could explain by the end of it. Their little company took a contract holding the crossing at Callow Bridge, five mercenaries and forty militia against a professional column, and everyone knew how that math ended, and they took the bridge anyway because the contract said *hold* and because the refugees funneling across behind them had nowhere else to go.

The night before the battle, Sarel came to Elodie's fire with a book — an actual bound book, leather cracked, pages soft as cloth — and said, "I found it in the chapel ruins at Duncarrow. There's a rite."

"A rite."

"For covenant-oaths." Sarel was twenty-five and already had the eyes of a woman who read things twice. "Real ones. Not soldier's swearing, not *by Veyra's rings, I'll see you paid*. The old form. Five sworn together, bound to the god and to each other. It says—" She paused, and Elodie would remember that pause for the rest of her life, the way Sarel's thumb pressed white against the page. "It says the god hears it. Actually hears it. Not the way a stone hears rain. The way a person hears a name."

"Superstition," Halvard said from across the fire, around a mouthful of hard sausage. But he shuffled closer to look anyway. Halvard always did.

They argued about it until midnight, the way they argued about everything, and then Serane — who never argued, only decided — said, "If we die tomorrow, I'd rather die sworn to something than hired to it," and that settled it, the way Serane settling things always settled them.

So five of them knelt in the mud of a riverbank with a burning army two miles off, and Sarel read the words, and Elodie spoke them, and here is the thing Elodie could never make anyone understand afterward, the thing that made priests pat her hand with pity and tavern philosophers nod sagely about battlefield hysteria:

*Something answered.*

Not words. Not light. Nothing so generous as evidence. It was more like — a stillness. A vast attention turning toward them out of the dark, the way you feel watched in an empty field, except multiplied past bearing, except it wasn't frightening, it was the opposite of frightening, it was the most *seen* she had ever been in her life. Every cowardly thought she'd ever had, every kindness she'd hidden out of embarrassment, every private grief — all of it known, all of it held, and none of it judged.

And on the backs of their hands, five rings bloomed like frost forming, silver-white, warm as a heartbeat.

Tomas laughed. Of course Tomas laughed — whooped, actually, loud enough to startle sentries, and grabbed Halvard and spun him around, and said, "We're *bound*, you great ox, do you understand? Bound! Nothing gets to take us that the god doesn't first agree to!"

The mark gave them strength. That part was real, whatever else was. They held Callow Bridge for three days against odds that should have buried them, and when Elodie's sword-arm shook too badly to lift the blade, the mark would flood warmth down her arm and the shaking stopped, and she told herself it was adrenaline, and knew she was lying, and didn't care.

Three days. On the fourth morning the column broke and retreated south, and the refugees crossed, and the five of them stood on the bridge with the sunrise coming up red through the smoke and felt, all of them, immortal.

That was the high point. Elodie wanted that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger the universe kept. That dawn on Callow Bridge was the highest point of her entire life, and it lasted about four hours.

Because the god fell silent on the fifth day.

* * *

In the present, the brandy glass was empty and Elodie didn't remember drinking it. The child murmured again upstairs. The shard hummed.

She went on anyway. She always went on. That was the trouble with memories this old — they wore grooves, and your mind ran in them like water finding low ground.

After Callow Bridge came a year of the five of them riding high. Sworn companies were rare; a sworn company with visible marks was a wonder, and wonder opened doors. They took the contracts that mattered. Escorting grain convoys through famine country. Standing between a mining town and the tax-collectors' enforcers until the Crown itself backed down. Once — Elodie's favorite story, the one she told at fires for years after — they'd stood in a plague village that three lords' worth of physicians had abandoned, and none of them caught so much as a cough, and the villagers built a little shrine to Veyra out of fence posts and gratitude, and Halvard had cried, which they teased him about for a decade.

The god didn't speak. They hadn't expected it to — the whole theology was built on silence. But the silence had *texture*, then. It had presence. Praying felt less like talking into a well and more like talking into a room where someone sat just out of sight, breathing. You couldn't prove it. You didn't need to.

Then, in the autumn of the second year, the war came back. Bigger. The border lords had found patrons across the sea, and the skirmishing curdled into campaign, and the campaign rolled north toward the valley passes, and the five of them took the worst contract of their lives: hold the pass at Redmantle against the vanguard, buy the valley time to evacuate.

Redmantle was a meat grinder wearing a mountain's shape. They fought in it for six days.

On the sixth day, mid-battle — mid-*breath*, Elodie could still place it exactly, between one parry and the next riposte, Tomas three feet to her left calling out a warning about the pikemen wheeling on their flank —

The mark went cold.

Not faded. Not dimmed. *Severed.* Like a hand letting go. One instant the warmth was running through her, the borrowed certainty, the sense of being held in a vast and patient regard — and the next instant there was nothing, and worse than nothing, there was the unmistakable sensation of a door closing. Of having been heard for two years and then, abruptly, deliberately, *not*.

She faltered. Half a heartbeat, sword drifting, staring at her own hand while steel rang around her — half a heartbeat in which anything could have happened.

What happened was Tomas.

He saw the flank turn. He saw her frozen. He did what Tomas always did, which was throw himself bodily into the gap between danger and the people he loved, roaring a laugh even then, even at the end, because Tomas thought dying dramatically was funny right up until it wasn't.

The pike took him under the ribs and came out his back, and Elodie killed four men in the following ten seconds with a fury she had no name for, and none of it mattered, none of it has ever mattered, because by the time the line held and the horns sounded withdrawal, Tomas was dead in the mud with the five rings on his hand already fading, going gray, going ordinary, going *scar*.

The others' marks stayed lit. That was the detail that haunted her worst in the early years, the one she circled like a tongue finding a broken tooth. All five of them had been severed in the same moment — she'd confirmed it after, shaking each of them by the wrist like a physician checking pulses. But theirs recovered within the hour. Faint, thinned, like a fire banked to coals, but *there*.

Hers came back too. Eventually. Dimmer than the rest. Dimmer every year since.

As if the door hadn't closed all the way for the others. As if for her alone it had closed almost entirely — or as if she alone had somehow, without meaning to, without wanting to, given offense.

*You don't get to pretend you didn't hear the answer,* Tomas said, in the groove-worn voice of the dead.

"But I didn't," she told the fire, quietly, for maybe the ten-thousandth time. "That's the whole point. Nobody heard anything ever again."

Except that wasn't quite true either, was it. Because the others' marks had stayed lit, faint but lit, for years — until one by one they'd gone quiet too, Serane's last, sometime around the year the Order of the Quiet was chartered and the Crown started collecting gods the way it collected taxes. And the shrines had begun to crumble, and the covenant-feasts had thinned to folklore, and Highmoor Valley had settled deeper and deeper into its magicless quiet like an old dog circling down onto a familiar rug.

Everyone said the god had died, or left, or never existed. Everyone had a theory and none of them had grief, not like hers, and Elodie had learned to keep hers folded up small and carry it inside the restless contentment of a working mercenary's life. Bounty jobs. Village quarrels. The valley, always the valley, never the boundary line, never beyond, because beyond was where the world had living magic in it and living magic had never once done her any favors.

She stayed where the god's last silence still hung in the air like incense. She told herself it was home. Some nights she even believed it.

* * *

A floorboard creaked above her.

Elodie was on her feet with a knife in her hand before the sound finished, which was stupid, which was the brandy and the memory loosening her, and she stood there in the dark feeling like an idiot while small footsteps crossed the loft and stopped at the top of the ladder.

The child peered down at her. Moonlight through the shutters striped her face. She didn't look frightened, exactly. She looked — attentive. The way you look at someone crying when you've seen a lot of people cry.

"I'm fine," Elodie said, uselessly, in common, and then, because the girl's head tilted at the tone if not the words: "Go back to sleep."

The child didn't move. Instead she lifted one small hand and pressed it flat against her own chest, over her heart, and then extended it outward — a gesture Elodie's body understood a full second before her mind did, because it was in the old rite, the words Sarel had read from the cracked leather book by a dying fire: *what is held here, I hold also.*

The girl was saying it in gesture. *What is held here, I hold also.*

Elodie's throat went tight.

"You don't know what you're saying," she said roughly. "Whatever's in you, whoever put it there — you don't know what it cost the last people who carried it."

The child regarded her with those bottomless dark eyes. Then she reached up, slow and deliberate, and touched the ladder rail, and the white stone shard in Elodie's pocket flared warm against her hip — and for one vertiginous instant Elodie felt it again, that vast attention turning toward her out of the dark, that terrible gentleness of being utterly known.

It lasted less than a heartbeat. Then it was gone, and the girl yawned hugely, like any child at any hour of any century, and padded back to her blankets.

Elodie stood in the cold hearth-light a long while, hand pressed over the pocket where the shard lay, heart slamming.

Twenty years of silence. Twenty years of telling herself the door had closed, the god had gone, the covenant was a beautiful lie that had gotten her best friend killed for nothing.

And tonight, in her own kitchen, something had *looked* at her.

She banked the fire, took up her sword, and made herself a hard promise: first light tomorrow, she was taking the girl and the stone to Priestess Maren, and Maren was going to tell her everything the old woman had been not-saying since the moment she'd seen that shard — everything, or Elodie would shake it out of her.

Because either she was going mad, or her past had finally come up her path.

She genuinely no longer knew which prospect frightened her more.
