## 6. What the Priestess Knew

Maren had spent forty years learning how to lie with a straight face, and she was good at it. She lied every morning when she lit the chapel candles and murmured the dawn office to a god who never answered. She lied when the farmers came to her with their troubles and asked if Veyra watched over them — *He watches*, she'd say, *in His way*. She lied so smoothly and for so long that the lies had worn smooth as river stones, and she could pick one up and set it down without anyone noticing the weight.

But tonight, sitting across her own hearth from Elodie Nakamura and the mud-caked child wrapped in Elodie's spare blanket, Maren found that all her practiced lies had deserted her at once.

The child sat by the fire with her knees drawn up, watching the flames the way other children watched birds — with that total, unblinking hunger. The white stone shard lay in her lap. Even from across the room Maren could feel it, the way you can feel a storm building behind a mountain: a pressure in the teeth, a hum in the old scar tissue of her palms where she'd gripped the altar rail too hard as a girl.

And Elodie — Elodie sat like a drawn blade. She'd put herself between the child and the door without seeming to, and now she looked at Maren with those flat grey eyes and said, "You knew what the words were before I finished translating them. You didn't even hesitate."

"I studied," Maren said. "Before my vows. At the seminary in Corvassa—"

"You hesitated on everything else tonight. The candles. Whether to bar the door. You checked the window twice." Elodie's voice stayed level, which was worse than shouting. "But not those words. Those came out of you like blood out of a cut. So either you're lying to me about something, or you've been lying to everyone else for a long time. Which is it?"

The fire popped. Outside, the wind worried at the eaves, and somewhere down the lane a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

Maren looked at the child. The child looked back at her, unafraid, and said something in that old tongue — three syllables, soft as ash falling — and Maren's whole life tilted on its axis, because she understood it. Not translated it. *Understood* it, the way you understand your own name.

*You carry the light still.*

She closed her eyes. "Elodie," she said, "how much do you know about why this valley has no magic?"

* * *

Everyone in Highmoor knew the story they told children. That the valley was blessed, that Veyra had made it a sanctuary from the sorceries and horrors of the outside world, that no witch's curse or demon's whisper could cross its borders. It was the founding legend, carved over the chapel door in letters two hundred years old: **HERE THE SILENCE IS A SHIELD**.

"And you believed it?" Elodie said.

"I believed it because it was easier than the truth." Maren folded her hands in her lap to keep them from shaking. "I went to seminary believing it. I came home ordained, took this parish, and found in the rectory archives — locked, warded, supposed to be burned — a box of documents older than the chapel itself. Pre-kingdom. And Elodie, I read them, and I have spent forty years being the only person alive who knows what our valley actually is."

"Tell me."

"The valley isn't a sanctuary." Maren heard her own voice go quiet, the way it did at funerals. "It's a ward. But not the kind we were taught. A sanctuary keeps danger out. A ward like this—" she nodded toward the child, toward the humming shard, "—a ward like this keeps something *in*. Or holds something shut. The documents called it the Stillness. Veyra's final act, written in His own hand, or as close as mortals ever came to His hand. He didn't bless this valley, Elodie. He *sealed* it. Sealed Himself into it, or sealed something through it, and then fell silent forever. Every prayer since has been falling into a well with no bottom."

Elodie hadn't moved. But Maren saw her hand drift to her forearm, where beneath the sleeve the oath-mark lived — the brand every sworn mercenary of Veyra carried, the sigil that let them work small miracles in His name. Except here, where nothing worked at all.

"My oath-mark," Elodie said slowly. "It's been dead since the day I swore it. Twenty years. I thought—"

"You thought you were forsaken. Or unworthy. Or that the god had abandoned His faithful." Maren shook her head. "No. No one's oath has worked inside these borders in living memory, because there's nothing left on this side of the seal to answer. The god's silence isn't absence, child. It's architecture. We live inside the silence. We are built into it — the valley, the mountains ringing us, the very ground. The Stillness isn't around us. We're inside the Stillness like a bird inside a bell."

The child made a sound. Small, questioning. She touched the shard and looked between the two women, and Maren realized with a lurch that the girl had followed all of it — or enough of it.

"She understands common speech," Elodie said. "She just can't — or won't — speak it."

"Or her voice belongs to another language entirely." Maren leaned forward. "That's the part I haven't told you yet. The hardest part."

"Then tell me the hard part."

Maren rose and went to the rectory's back wall, behind the vestment chest, and moved aside the loose stone she'd loosened and re-loosened ten thousand times without ever once daring to act on what lay behind it. From the cavity she drew an oilcloth bundle, and from the bundle a book — no, not a book, a *codex*, bound in hide gone dark with age, its cover stamped with the same sigil that lived under Elodie's sleeve.

She set it on the table between them. The shard in the child's lap began, very softly, to sing.

"The documents call the child a vessel," Maren said. "There were five, once. Five seals upon the Stillness, each one a living soul marked by the god's own will, spread through the world so that no one hand could undo them. Four are dust. This one—" she looked at the girl, and felt her throat close, "—this one was made last. Grown, the texts say, from the god's final act of will, in the shape of a child, carrying the key to the entire covenant. She can renew the seal. Bind the Stillness fast for another age. Or she can unmake it — open the way, end the silence, let everything the seal holds come flooding back into the world."

"Let *what* flood back?"

"I don't know." And Maren meant it, and hated meaning it. "The texts are careful. They say only that the god gave everything to hold it shut. Everything, Elodie. His voice, His presence, His church — He spent Himself like a coin to make this prison, whatever the prison holds. And the vessel is the lock's key, walking around in the shape of a little girl who likes to watch fires."

Silence. The wind. The shard singing its low note.

"So the Crown's inquisitors," Elodie said at last. "'The vessel.' They know."

"They know something. Perhaps more than I do — the Order of the Quiet has roots deeper than any archive I ever robbed." Maren smiled bitterly at the phrase; Elodie's eyes flickered. "Yes. I was not always a village priestess. Another sin for the pile."

"Why hide the codex? Why not burn it, or give it to the Order yourself? Forty years, Maren. You sat on this while I prayed to a dead god and wondered what I'd done wrong."

It was the question Maren had dreaded, and it deserved the truth, and the truth was shameful.

"Because I couldn't bear to find out I was wrong," she said. "Do you understand that? For forty years I've led services to a silent god, comforted dying men with promises I couldn't verify, buried children with rites I half-believed. If I opened that book and found proof the silence meant abandonment — that everything was ash — I would have had nothing. So I kept it locked. Faith as cowardice. I confess it, Elodie, formally, before you and before whatever's left listening: I chose comfortable ignorance over truth, for four decades, because I was afraid."

The child slid off her chair. Crossed the floor. Took Maren's hand — the scarred, knotted, lying hand — and pressed the white shard into it.

The moment Maren's skin touched the stone, she felt it: a thread, thin as spider silk and strong as anchor chain, running out of her chest and up through the roof and into the sky, into something vast and patient and *listening*. Not dead. Not absent. Just — waiting. Holding its breath for twenty years and more.

Her legs gave out. Elodie caught her before she hit the floor.

"It answered," Maren whispered, from her knees, tears running freely down a face that had performed solemnity for forty years and finally forgot how. "Oh, gods, it answered. He's not gone. He's *here*. He's been here the whole time, under us, around us, and I prayed into Him every day of my life like rain falling on a roof—"

"Maren." Elodie's grip was iron. "Look at me. Later. Grieve later. Right now — the inquisitors crossed the ridgeline today. If what you're saying is true, they're not here for a fugitive child. They're here for the key to the thing their whole order exists around. Which means they won't stop, and they won't negotiate, and every hour we sit in this house they get closer. What does the codex say happens if the Order takes her?"

Maren wiped her face with the heel of her hand, and the priestess fell away, and the woman underneath — the one who'd robbed archives and read forbidden books and kept a knife in her boot for forty years — looked up coldly.

"If the Order takes the vessel," she said, "they can do one of two things. Renew the seal under Crown control, and hold the world hostage with it. Or break it deliberately, and use what comes out." She stood, steadied by Elodie's arm, and met the mercenary's eyes. "Either way, Highmoor Valley stops being a backwater and starts being the most important piece of ground in the world. And everyone in it becomes either a tool or an obstacle."

Down the lane, the dog started barking again. This time it didn't stop.

Elodie was already moving, snuffing candles, pulling her blade free of its oilcloth. The child gathered the shard against her chest with both hands, and Maren saw — clearly, unmistakably, in the last red light of the hearth — that the girl was looking at her with pity.

Pity. From the child. For her.

"Back door," Elodie said quietly. "Now. And Maren — whatever else you've hidden in forty years, now's the time to spend it."

Maren took up the codex, and her knife, and her God, and followed.
