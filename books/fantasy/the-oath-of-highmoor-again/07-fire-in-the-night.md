## 7. Fire in the Night

The chapel bell rang wrong.

Elodie had lived her whole life within earshot of that bell, and she knew its voice the way she knew her own heartbeat — the fat morning toll, the thin evening call, the cracked half-note old Tomas had joked was the god clearing his throat. This was none of those. This was a single stroke, struck hard and fast, over and over, no rhythm to it at all.

Alarm.

She was out of bed before the second stroke finished, knife already in her hand from the hook by the door, because twenty years of mercenary habit did not wait for thought. Ilyen sat up on the pallet by the hearth, white stone shard clutched to her chest, eyes enormous in the dark.

"Stay here," Elodie said, and then, because the child wouldn't understand a word of it, pressed her flat palm against the floorboards. *Stay.* The girl nodded once, solemn as a grave marker.

Outside, the village was waking badly. Doors banging. Dogs going mad. And above the rooftops, on the ridgeline road where the chapel stood with its squat stone tower and its roof of slate gone green with moss, there were lights. Too many lights, moving in formation. Torchlight didn't move like that. Lantern light carried by disciplined men did.

Elodie counted eleven points of flame before she stopped counting, because the number had stopped mattering.

She ran.

* * *

Maren met her at the chapel door, still in her nightclothes under a wool cloak, her gray hair loose and wild. Behind her, a handful of villagers had gathered with whatever they'd grabbed — hay forks, a wood axe, Bram Tanner holding a boat hook like it might mean anything against soldiers.

"Eleven riders," Maren said. "Came down from the north pass an hour ago. They've been asking at every house." Her voice was steady, but her hands weren't. "Asking after a child."

"What did you tell them?"

"The truth. That we're sheep farmers and we don't know any children but our own." Maren's mouth twisted. "They didn't believe me. Their captain has a way of looking at you like he's reading a ledger and you've already been entered in it."

"Where are they now?"

"At the well. Making camp, they said." Maren pulled her inside and shut the door. "Elodie. They have a warrant. Serjeant Pell saw it — wax seal of the Crown, and beneath it another seal he didn't know. A circle with a line through it."

The Order of the Quiet. Elodie's stomach went cold and stayed cold. She'd heard the name whispered in taverns beyond the valley, back when she'd still taken jobs on the borders — inquisitors who hunted not heretics but *resonance*, anyone or anything that carried too much of the old magic. They were supposed to be a southern thing. A story mothers told to keep children from humming the old hymns.

"They can't cross the ward," she said. "No magic crosses the ward. Whatever they are, in here they're just men."

"Men with torches are still men with torches." Maren looked at her, and in the candlelight her face was older than it had been three days ago, when a mud-caked child had fallen through Elodie's door speaking a language older than kingdoms. "You should take her and go. Tonight. Through the fen path, north, before they think to watch it."

"And go where? You know what's north of here, Maren. Nothing is north of here. That's rather the point of Highmoor."

"Then south, past the shrine road—"

"You know I can't take the shrine road." The words came out flat and final, and Maren flinched, because she knew why. Everyone in the valley who was old enough knew why. Twenty years ago five people had walked out of this valley swearing an oath to Veyra, and only four had come back, and one of those four had never spoken of it again except once, drunk, to a priestess she trusted.

"I'm sorry," Maren said quietly. "I forget sometimes that you have a before."

"Everyone has a before. Most of us are lucky enough that it stays buried." Elodie rubbed her face. Under her palm, the oath-mark on her forearm — the coiled spiral inked into her skin at nineteen, blessed by a god who had answered — gave its faint, familiar warmth. It always warmed when the child was near. She'd told herself it meant nothing. She was running out of ways to tell herself that. "Get the villagers inside their houses. Bar the doors. If these Quiet men want to ask questions, let them ask empty lanes."

"And you?"

"I'm going to get Ilyen and be gone before dawn."

Maren caught her arm. "Not alone. Take Pell's mule, take food, take—"

"Maren." Elodie covered the old woman's hand with her own. "If I leave with a parade, they'll follow a parade. If I leave with nothing, maybe they'll follow nothing." She managed something that was almost a smile. "I've slipped out of worse places than a valley full of people who love me."

"That's a lie," Maren said. "There's nowhere worse."

* * *

It went wrong the way things go wrong: quickly, stupidly, at the exact moment when everything seemed to be working.

Elodie had Ilyen bundled in her biggest cloak, hood up, the shard wrapped in sacking and strapped beneath the girl's coat where no lantern would catch it. They went out the back of the cottage, through the bean rows, along the drainage ditch that ran behind the tannery — a route Elodie had used since she was nine years old and sneaking out to watch the border riders. The village slept around them, shutters closed, dogs finally quiet. The inquisitors' campfire glowed orange at the well square, two hundred paces east. Far enough.

They reached the fen gate. Elodie lifted the latch.

That was when the bell began again — not the alarm this time but the first stroke of it, one clean note rolling down from the chapel tower, and then a second note that wasn't a note at all but the sound of glass breaking, and then the smell.

Smoke.

Elodie turned and saw the flame catch the chapel roof like a hand closing.

Later, she would learn how it happened: that the inquisitors' captain had sent four men ahead while the rest made their polite camp at the well, that they'd gone up to the chapel to search it for "resonant materials," that Maren had stood in the doorway and refused them entry with nothing but her office and her spine, and that one of them — young, jumpy, holding a torch in the wind off the ridge — had simply pushed past her, and the oil lamp in the vestry had done the rest. Whether it was accident or order would never matter. Fire doesn't keep records.

What mattered was what Elodie saw in the moment she turned: Maren in the chapel doorway, silhouetted against her own burning god-house, swinging the great iron door-bar at a man in gray-and-white livery, and going down under him.

Everything after that, Elodie did not decide so much as stop deciding.

"Ilyen. The ditch. Don't come out until I call you." She shoved the girl down into the drainage cut and ran up the hill with her knife open in her hand, and the last coherent thought she had for some minutes was a cold, clear surprise: *so this is what it feels like when the oath moves me instead of the other way around.*

* * *

The chapel was lost. Flame stood up out of the roof and leaned east on the wind, and the heat of it hit Elodie's face at thirty paces like an opened oven. Villagers were spilling out of their houses now, buckets appearing from nowhere, a bucket chain forming with the grim automatic competence of people who had drilled for this since childhood — but everyone could see the slate was gone, the beams were catching, and the tower would stand alone by morning or not at all.

Three men in gray-and-white stood apart from the chaos, watching. Not helping. One held a lantern; one held a ledger, actually writing in it, which was somehow the worst thing Elodie had ever seen; and the third — tall, narrow-faced, with the captain's badge of crossed quills on his collar — was giving orders in a low voice to a fourth man who was walking toward the ditch.

Toward the ditch where Elodie had put a child.

She came out of the smoke at a dead run and hit the walking man from the side, and they went down together into the wet grass, and everything got very simple.

He was stronger than her. Younger, heavier, armored at the shoulders, and he got a forearm across her throat and started to squeeze with the patient strength of a man who had done this before and expected it to work. Stars crowded the edges of her vision. His face was very close, ordinary, sweating, maybe twenty-five years old, and some distant part of her noted that he had freckles, and that part of her was screaming, and the rest of her was perfectly calm.

Her knife found the gap under his jaw.

Afterward she could never say whether she had aimed it or whether her hand had simply known, the way her hands knew rope knots and sword drills, the way they had known at nineteen when the god's warmth had filled her arms and she had sworn herself to Veyra's service. There was no warmth now. There was just the give of flesh, the hot spill over her wrist, and the freckled face going slack with a look not of pain but of genuine astonishment, as if he had genuinely believed, right up to the end, that the world worked differently than this.

He rolled off her. He did not get up.

Elodie lay in the grass for one breath, two, listening to her own heart slam, looking at the sky where the smoke had blotted out half the stars. Then she rolled to her feet, because the captain was shouting, because the man with the lantern was running toward her, because somewhere below the hill a small figure had risen out of the drainage ditch despite everything and was standing frozen in the firelight with the sacking-wrapped shard visible against her chest.

*Ilyen, no.*

The second man came at her with a short sword, and he was better trained than the first, and it should have been hard. It wasn't. That frightened her more than the killing had. Twenty years of drills and border skirmishes and bounty work had kept her sharp, yes, but this was different — this was the old warmth after all, rising up her arm from the oath-mark like water finding a channel, guiding her blade past his guard, into the meat of his thigh, turning him, putting her boot on his chest and her point at his throat, all in the time it took him to finish a swing.

"Don't," she said.

He looked up at her. At the mark on her bare forearm, where her sleeve had torn, where the coiled spiral was glowing faintly gold in the firelight.

"*Veyra's oath,*" he breathed. "You're one of the—"

"Run," Elodie said, "or I'll send you to report to your god personally."

He ran. She let him. She would think about that later too — about the fact that she'd killed one man without hesitating and spared another without hesitating, and that she couldn't explain either choice, and that both of them had felt less like decisions than like sentences she was merely the hand that wrote.

The captain had not run. The captain stood at the edge of the burning chapel's light with his ledger under his arm, and he looked at her the way Maren had described: like a man reading a ledger, entering figures. Two dead, one fled, one target acquired. He raised two fingers, and from the dark below the ridge came the sound of hooves.

Elodie scooped Ilyen off her feet and ran.

* * *

They went north through the fen path with the chapel burning at their backs and the whole valley lit orange behind them, and Elodie did not look back, because looking back was how people died, and also because if she looked back she would see Maren's house, and she did not yet know whether Maren was alive, and she could not carry that answer and run at the same time.

Bram Tanner caught them at the fen gate — the old man materializing out of the reeds with his boat hook and, miracle of miracles, Pell's mule on a lead rope, saddlebags bulging.

"Heard the bell," Bram said, shoving the reins into her hands. "Figured." His face was lit red by the fire, and wet. "Maren's alive. Took a bar to the shoulder, broke her collarbone, cursing fit to blister paint. She said—" he swallowed— "she said tell you the ward holds as long as the key walks. Whatever that means."

"It means she's alive and insufferable," Elodie said, and her voice cracked on the second word, and she let it. "Thank you, Bram."

"Go on, then." He stepped back into the reeds. "And girl—" he called after her, and she turned the mule, and the old man looked at her with seventy years of the valley in his face. "Whatever's out there that wants that child. Don't bring it back here."

"I won't," Elodie said. "I promise you. Whatever comes next, it ends away from Highmoor."

She meant it. She had no idea yet how much it would cost to keep.

* * *

They rode north through the black hours, following the fen path until it climbed into the high pasture, then the shepherd tracks, then nothing at all but starlight and the mule's sure feet. The fire glow faded behind them. Somewhere past midnight the child, riding pillion with her arms around Elodie's waist, began to shake — the delayed trembling of shock, the body settling its accounts after the mind has spent everything.

Elodie pulled up, dismounted, built a tiny fire in the lee of a boulder — risk be damned, the girl needed warmth — and wrapped them both in the big cloak. Ilyen stared into the flames. Her lips moved, shaping sounds in that old language, and for the first time Elodie caught enough cadence to recognize it as a question, asked over and over.

"I don't know," Elodie said. "I don't know what happens now."

The girl looked at her. Reached out one small hand and touched the torn sleeve, the exposed oath-mark, still faintly warm, faintly gold, warmer than it had been in twenty years. Then she touched her own chest, where the shard lay hidden, and then she pointed north, into the dark, with a certainty that had nothing childish left in it.

North. Toward the Godspine, though neither of them had the word for it yet. Toward everything.

"We can't go back," Elodie said quietly. The admission tasted like ash and like freedom, both at once. "You understand that? There's no going back after tonight. I killed a man tonight. For you. Do you—" she stopped, because the girl was watching her with those grave dark eyes, and there was no judgment in them, only attention, total and unafraid, as if Elodie were a text she was learning to read. "I killed a man," Elodie said again, to herself this time. "First time in six years. And it didn't even feel like a choice."

The fire crackled. Far to the south, a second glow had risen over the ridgeline — smaller than the chapel fire, wrong-colored, too white. Lanterns. Many lanterns, moving along the ridge road in formation, fanning out.

Searching. Already searching.

Elodie kicked dirt over the little fire, swung Ilyen up onto the mule, and took the lead rope in her burned, bloodstained hand. The oath-mark pulsed against her forearm like a second heartbeat, pointing north the way a compass needle points, and for the first time since the god had fallen silent, Elodie Winterbourne felt certain of exactly one thing:

Whatever waited beyond the valley's edge, it had been waiting for her a long time.

She turned the mule north, and rode into the dark.
