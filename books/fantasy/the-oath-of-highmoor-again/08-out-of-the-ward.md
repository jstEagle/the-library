## 8. Out of the Ward

They traveled through the night, and by the gray hour before dawn Elodie had learned three things about Ilyen.

First: the child did not sleep so much as shutter herself, like a house closing for a storm. She would sit against whatever wall or tree was nearest, hands folded over the white shard, eyes open but fixed on nothing, breathing slow as a sleeping woman's. Ten minutes of that and she'd rise again, restored in some way Elodie couldn't account for.

Second: she never asked for food. She ate what Elodie gave her — bread, dried apples, half a wedge of hard cheese — with grave politeness, as if accepting tribute. But if Elodie offered nothing, the child went without and gave no sign it mattered.

Third: she watched the road behind them more than the road ahead. Not with fear, exactly. With recognition. As if she already knew what was coming up that road, and was only waiting for it to arrive.

Elodie didn't like any of these things. She liked even less how little she understood them. Twenty years a bounty hunter taught her to read people the way she read tracks — weight, gait, intent — and every reading she took of this child came back wrong, like footprints that started mid-stride.

The chapel fire was hours behind them now, a smudge of orange still staining the southern sky when they paused at a stream to drink. Maren had stayed. That thought arrived and re-arrived, dull as a bruise. The old woman had stood in her burning doorway and pushed them both out into the dark with the last of her strength, and then turned back toward the flames because someone had to pull the wounded out, and someone had to be the priestess, right to the end. Elodie hadn't said goodbye. There hadn't been time for goodbyes, only for grabbing the child's wrist and running.

*She'll live,* Elodie told herself, for the hundredth time. *Old women survive fires. It's practically their trade.*

She didn't believe it, but she kept saying it, because the alternative was to stop walking, and stopping meant dying.

"Drink," she told Ilyen, holding out a cupped handful of stream water. The child drank from her palm instead, delicately, watching the eastern ridgeline the whole while. "We're making good time. By midday we'll—"

Ilyen touched her arm. Pointed east. Said something in that language of hers — four syllables, falling cadence, urgent.

"I know," Elodie said. "Riders. I hear them."

She didn't, yet. But she knew the arithmetic of pursuit better than she knew her own prayers. Serane's knights had burned a chapel; they wouldn't let a witness walk away from that. They'd ride the valley roads through the night, fan out at dawn, sweep north. Which meant the only direction left was the one direction nobody went.

Out of the ward.

Elodie stood, wiped her hands on her trousers, and looked north along the river valley where the water ran down from the mountains. Somewhere up there, past the last farms, past the shepherd huts, past the standing stone the villagers called the Old Marker, lay the boundary. Nobody spoke about it much. Children were told the world beyond Highmoor was sick with sorcery, poisoned by the wild arts, unfit for honest folk. Pilgrims occasionally arrived from outside, pale and blinking, muttering that inside the valley their headaches stopped, their charms went dead, their familiars sulked like scolded cats. They called it blessed. The valley folk just called it home.

Twenty years ago, five people had sworn an oath on a mountaintop far south of here, and a god had answered, and then the god had gone silent, and Elodie had come here — everyone had scattered, but she had come *here*, of all places, the one spot in all creation where magic could not follow. She'd told herself for two decades it was coincidence. A mercenary goes where work is quiet.

Nobody quiet chooses a place where magic cannot go. You choose it because you're hiding from something, or you're hiding something, and you don't even let yourself know which.

"All right," she said aloud, mostly to hear a human voice. "All right, little one. North."

Ilyen looked up at her, and for the first time since the chapel, something moved across the child's face that wasn't gravity. It might have been trust. It frightened Elodie worse than the riders.

* * *

They climbed all morning, following a drover's track that narrowed to a footpath, then to a suggestion, then to a memory. The farms thinned out. The last shepherd hut they passed had been empty for years, its roof fallen in, sheep bones bleaching by the door. The air grew colder and cleaner. And all the while, Elodie felt it — a faint pressure building between her shoulder blades, or rather the *absence* of a pressure she'd carried so long she'd forgotten it was there.

Her oath-mark sat under the skin of her left forearm like a buried splinter. In the valley, it was nothing. Dead ink, dead scar, twenty years of silence. She'd learned to ignore it completely, the way you ignore an old injury on a cold morning.

But they were climbing, and with every mile the splinter woke a little more. A warmth first. Then a prickle, like a limb remembering itself after sleep. By early afternoon, when they stopped to eat among a ring of leaning stones, the mark had begun to itch in a way that made her want to peel her own arm open to scratch it.

Ilyen noticed. Of course she did. The child reached out and laid two fingers on Elodie's forearm, just above the mark, and the itching stilled instantly — not gone, but held, the way a hand can hold a trembling bird.

"Don't do that," Elodie said quietly.

Ilyen tilted her head.

"It's not yours to fix." She gentled it, because the child wasn't doing anything wrong, precisely. "And it doesn't need fixing. It's been silent for twenty years. Whatever it's waking up for, I don't want to know yet."

But of course she wanted to know. Her whole body wanted to know. Beneath the fear there was another thing, shameful and enormous: *hunger*. Twenty years of silence, and now, mile by mile, something in her blood was turning toward the boundary like a compass needle, and some starved animal part of her was thinking, *yes, yes, closer.*

She hated that. She hated that the oath still owned a room in her whether the god answered or not.

They walked on. Toward evening the path crested a long shoulder of moorland, and Elodie saw it: the Old Marker, a granite pillar taller than two men, standing alone in the heather with the light going amber around it. Beyond it, the land dropped away into a different country — darker forest, stranger hills, a haze on the horizon that wasn't cloud. The valley ended there. Not gradually. Like a page ending.

She'd never once seen it from this side. Nobody had. You lived your whole life inside the sentence and never saw the period at the end of it.

"We camp here," she said. "Tonight. We cross at dawn."

Because you didn't cross such a line tired. Because whatever waited beyond it, she wanted daylight and dry ground and a full skin of water. Because part of her — a large part, a coward's part, honestly tallied — hoped that in the night she'd find a reason to turn around.

Ilyen shook her head. Once. Firm.

"Yes, we camp," Elodie said.

The child pointed north, past the marker, then pressed her palm flat over the shard at her chest, then pointed north again. Her expression was patient and absolute, the way a mountain is patient.

"No," Elodie said. "Whatever's out there, it'll keep till morning. I'm not taking you over that line in the dark, exhausted, with inquisitors at our backs and no idea what—"

Ilyen stepped forward and gripped Elodie's sleeve. And her eyes — those eyes that had watched the southern road all day — went wide and wet and terrified, and she spoke, fast, in the old language, syllables tumbling over each other, and though Elodie couldn't understand a word, she understood everything: *please, please, please.*

Elodie looked at the marker. Looked south, where the sky was finally, fully dark and the chapel-smell of smoke seemed to cling to the wind itself. Looked at the child.

"Gods' teeth," she muttered, which was as close as she'd come in years to praying. "Fine. Fine! But we cross together, we stay together, and if anything out there tries to eat us, you run behind me and you don't stop. Agreed?"

Ilyen nodded solemnly, and then, horribly, smiled.

* * *

The last hundred yards, Elodie's oath-mark began to scream.

Not literally — she'd have preferred literally. It started as heat, then as light behind her eyes, then as a soundless roar climbing her arm into her shoulder into her chest, as if twenty years of dammed-up river had found a crack in the masonry and was shouldering through. She staggered. Kept walking. Each step was a step deeper into floodwater.

Ten yards from the marker, her knees buckled.

She caught herself on the heather, gasping, and rolled up her sleeve, and by starlight she saw it: the oath-mark glowing. Not reflecting light. Making it. The lines of the old vow — the knotwork circle, the five-pointed weave she'd traced with her own blood at nineteen years old — shone beneath her skin like coals breathed upon, and with every pulse, the glow brightened, and the pain sharpened from ache to agony to a white edge that made her vision swim.

"El—" she tried to say, and couldn't finish her own name.

Ilyen crouched beside her, and the shard at the child's chest was glowing too, answering, the two lights calling to each other across inches of cold air like struck bells. The child didn't touch her this time. She just knelt there, present, steady, while the god's old signature burned itself awake in Elodie's arm after two decades of silence.

*It remembers me.* The thought came through the pain, absurd and devastating. *It remembers me, which means somewhere, something is listening.*

Or something was *holding*. She couldn't tell the difference, and that was worse.

"Go," she rasped. "If it gets worse — go on without me, get past the line, find whoever—"

Ilyen took her face in both small hands, and looked at her, and shook her head slowly, the way you correct a child who has said something foolish.

Then the child stood, took Elodie's wrist gently, and pulled. And Elodie, who had dragged grown men bleeding out of burning buildings, who had carried bounties twice her weight down frozen ravines, let herself be pulled — ten yards, fifteen — until she crossed the line.

There was no wall. No flash, no thunderclap, no visible seam in the world. Just one step of heather, and then the next step landing in grass that bent differently in the wind, under stars that seemed fractionally nearer, in air that tasted faintly of — she couldn't name it. Ozone? Rain on hot stone? Magic, she supposed. The smell of magic, which she had never smelled in her life, because her whole life had been spent in the one place it couldn't reach.

And the pain stopped. All at once, like a hand lifting from a bell. What flooded in behind it was not relief but *presence* — the oath-mark blazing warm and alive along her arm, singing silently, full to the brim for the first time in twenty years. She lay back in the strange grass and laughed, breathlessly, helplessly, at the sheer unbearable aliveness of it, tears freezing on her temples.

Ilyen sat down beside her and watched the southern horizon with those grave eyes.

That was when the child said her first word.

Not in the old language. Not in the tongue of the valley. In common speech — flat, clear, carefully formed, as if the word had been carried a long distance and set down with both hands:

"Hurry."

Elodie sat up. The word hung in the cold air between them.

"You can talk," she said stupidly.

Ilyen pointed south. Down the slope, far off, tiny as embers, torchlight. Many torches. Moving fast along the valley road, spreading out, fanning wide, sweeping the moorland edge in a pattern Elodie recognized from her own hunts: the drive, the net, the close.

"Hurry," the child said again, and stood, and pulled Elodie's wrist, and this time Elodie needed no persuading. She scrambled up, arm still singing, legs shaking, and together they ran north into the dark country — away from the valley that had kept her safe for twenty years, away from the god whose voice was suddenly, terrifyingly, everywhere in her blood.

Behind them, at the crest of the ridge, the first rider crested into view against the stars, and raised a horn to his lips.
