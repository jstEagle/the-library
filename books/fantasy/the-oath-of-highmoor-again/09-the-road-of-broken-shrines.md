## 9. The Road of Broken Shrines

They walked north for three days before Elodie understood what she was looking at.

At first she took the ruins for ordinary decay — the way things rotted in the low country where no one swept a path twice. A leaning post in a turnip field. A pile of mossy stones by a ford. A niche above a wellhead, empty, its shelf dark with old rain.

Then she saw the chisel marks.

The third shrine of the morning — because that's what they were, she finally admitted; she'd grown up around shrines and knew their bones even headless — stood in a coppice of birches off the cart track. Someone had taken a maul to it with real intent. Not weather. Weather is indifferent. This had been furious. The statue's face was gone, but not broken away: *peeled*, each feature struck with small precise blows, as if the vandal had wanted to erase a person rather than a stone.

The body beneath the ruined face still held its shape. Hands open. Palms up.

Elodie knew those hands. She'd knelt before them every morning of her childhood, in the valley, where the shrine-stones were whole because nothing in Highmoor changed — not stone, not oath, not the silence of the god they'd been raised to love without ever hearing an answer.

"Veyra," Ilyen said. Not a question. The girl had learned the word from her, on the first night out of the valley, pointing at Elodie's oath-mark through the firelight. Now she pointed at the hands.

"That's right." Elodie crouched, touched the pedestal. Fresh chips under the moss. "Someone hated this one recently."

Ilyen frowned at the word. *Hated.* Elodie was starting to build the child's vocabulary piece by piece, trading words for bread, for warmth, for sleep. Some words landed easy. Some — like *hated* — seemed to fall into her and keep falling.

In the valley, Veyra was loved the way rain is loved: without thought, without alternative. Elodie had never once considered that beyond the ward-line, people might have opinions about her god. Opinions with mauls in them.

She stood, brushed off her knees, and did not pray. Twenty years of habit said she should. Twenty years of silence said why bother.

"Come on," she said. "Daylight's spending itself."

* * *

The second day, the shrines got worse.

Or better, depending on how you looked at it — because some of them hadn't been destroyed at all. They'd been *converted*. In a market town called Brassen's Ford, where the road crossed water brown as tea, a shrine to Veyra stood in the square with a new face fitted over the old: a fat-cheeked harvest godling, gilded, grinning, its plaster cheeks slapped directly onto the surviving shoulders of Veyra's statue. The original hands were still there underneath, palms up. They'd been painted gold and filled with coins.

An offering box. The open hands of a silent god turned into a change purse.

Elodie stood in front of it longer than was safe, feeling Ilyen's small hand find hers.

"You're staring," Ilyen said. Her common speech came slow, gathered like dropped stitches, but it came faster every day.

"Yes."

"Why?"

Because twenty years ago, mid-battle, with Tomas bleeding out and four oaths ringing hollow, Veyra had gone quiet — and Elodie had built her whole life inside that quiet like a hermit builds inside a cave. Because she'd assumed the silence was grief, or judgment, or mystery, something vast enough to deserve her loyalty. It had never occurred to her that outside the valley, the silence might just be *unpopular*.

"I'm staring," Elodie said, "because I thought everyone missed her. And nobody here even noticed she was gone."

A fruit-seller nearby laughed — not cruelly, just the laugh of a woman who'd heard a strange thing from a stranger. "Missed who, dear? That old stone? My gran said the Quiet God's folk went up into their valley and shut the door behind them. Said good riddance, too." She weighed plums with quick hands. "Gods that make you kneel are gods that make you bleed. We do better with the fat one. He only asks for coins."

*The Quiet God.* Not Veyra. Not even a name. A description, worn smooth by generations of not-caring.

Elodie bought three plums she didn't want, because the woman had been kind, and walked out of the square with Ilyen's hand in hers and something cold settling behind her ribs that wasn't quite anger and wasn't quite grief and was learning, slowly, to be both.

* * *

They slept that night in a hay barn, and Elodie dreamed of Tomas.

Not the battle — she never dreamed the battle, her mind kept that room locked and posted guards. She dreamed the night before it, five of them around a fire on the valley rim, swearing themselves to Veyra with wine and blood and the particular solemn stupidity of the young. Sarel's voice steady as a bell. Halvard already laughing at his own vows. Serane seventeen and fierce, watching Elodie across the flames with an expression she hadn't understood then and understood too well now. And Tomas, last to swear, grinning, saying, *Whatever she asks, we answer. That's the whole religion.*

Whatever she asks, we answer.

He'd answered until the moment the asking stopped. Then he'd died on a hillside with his god's name in his mouth and nothing in his ears.

Elodie woke before dawn with her heart going hard and the shard of white stone glowing faintly through Ilyen's shirt collar, the way it did when the girl dreamed. It hummed against Elodie's oath-mark through two layers of cloth — a low note, persistent, like a finger run around the rim of a glass.

"Bad dream?" Ilyen murmured.

"No," Elodie lied, and then, because lying to this child was getting harder every day: "Old ones don't stop being bad. You just get better at carrying them."

Ilyen considered this with the grave economy of someone rationing words. "Carry mine too. When yours are heavy."

Elodie looked at her in the dark and felt the thing in her chest she refused to name yet, because naming it would mean admitting how much there was to lose.

"Sleep, troublemaker," she said. "We've miles yet."

* * *

The hedgewitch's cottage found them more than they found it.

Rain drove them off the high track on the fourth afternoon — a cold, sideways rain with winter's teeth already in it — and they cut down through alder scrub toward a light, and the light resolved into a house that seemed to have been grown rather than built, all crooked timber and turf roof, chimney smoking against the grey. Bones and bundles hung under the eaves. A goat regarded them from atop a woodpile with an expression of professional contempt.

Elodie had survived twenty mercenary years by reading rooms fast. This room read: *witch. Armed. Old.* Every instinct said walk past, sleep wet.

But the shard had started humming louder as they descended, tugging at Elodie's mark like a hound scenting home, and Ilyen had stopped walking entirely, rooted to the mud, both hands pressed over the stone at her throat, saying one word over and over in that pre-kingdom tongue that made the hairs on Elodie's arms stand up.

So Elodie knocked.

The door opened on a woman sixty-ish and built like a fence post, iron-grey braid, eyes the pale yellow of old honey. She looked at Elodie. At Ilyen. At the glow leaking from the girl's collar.

"Huh," said the hedgewitch. "Took you long enough."

"You were expecting us?"

"I was expecting *trouble*." She stepped back from the doorway. "You'll do. In. Boots off. If my goat follows you in, put him back out, he's a liar and a thief."

Her name, extracted over barley soup, was Odile. She asked no questions through the meal, which told Elodie more than questions would have. Witches who don't ask already know. Or already suspect. Either way, the soup was good, the fire was dry, and Ilyen fell asleep sitting upright, mid-chew, the way children do when their bodies simply file for bankruptcy.

Odile caught her, lowered her to a bench, tucked a blanket with the brisk tenderness of long practice.

"She's the vessel," Odile said quietly. Not a question either.

Elodie set down her spoon. "Where did you hear that word?"

"Nowhere. Saw it." The witch nodded at the sleeping girl. "Been reading omens forty years, soldier. That child reads like a page with everything written on it at once. My cards won't even shuffle near her — they just lie flat and sulk." She sat back, cradling her own bowl. "And that stone she's wearing has been shouting at me since you crested the hill. So. Show me, or take your soup and go back into my rain."

Every caution Elodie owned said *no*. But the shard hummed, and Ilyen breathed slow, and the rain hammered the turf roof like a crowd demanding an answer.

She drew the stone out gently — Ilyen let go of it even asleep, fingers uncurling trustingly — and laid it on the table between them.

Odile leaned close. Didn't touch it. Her breath fogged the air above it, and the fog didn't drift. It *spiraled*, drawn inward, and the shard drank the light of the fire and threw it back white.

"Covenant-key," Odile said at last. Her voice had changed — flatter, careful, the voice of a woman walking on river ice. "I read one of these once. Forty years ago. There was a man came through with a fragment no bigger than a thumbnail, wanted to know if it was worth anything."

"What did you tell him?"

"Told him it was worthless." Her mouth twisted. "Then I told him to leave and never come back, and I burned my scrying mirror after he went, and I didn't sleep right for a season." She looked up. "That man served the Order of the Quiet. Crown livery under his coat, if you're wondering how I know. They've been hunting pieces of this for decades, soldier. Longer than your girl's been alive. Longer than whatever's eating your face right now."

"My face isn't being eaten."

"It is from where I sit." Odile tapped the table beside the shard, once, not touching it. "This is a key to a covenant older than any kingdom in this region. Pre-liturgical. I've seen script fragments in barrow-graffiti and that's it — the living tongue died out everywhere except, apparently, in the mouth of one mud-covered child who shouldn't exist." She fixed Elodie with those honey-pale eyes. "Keys don't wander. Keys get carried. Which means somewhere there's a lock, and someone means to open it or seal it forever, and either way the Order wants the key in their fist first."

Outside, the goat screamed at nothing. Rain. Just rain. Probably rain.

"The lock," Elodie said slowly, "is a valley."

Odile went very still. "Say that again."

"Highmoor Valley. Where I was born. Nothing magical crosses its boundary. Nothing has, for centuries. Everyone says it's a blessing. A sanctuary. The god's last gift to the faithful." Elodie heard her own voice go distant. "It never once occurred to me that a sanctuary and a prison look identical from inside."

The witch studied her for a long moment. Then she rose, went to a chest in the corner, and came back with a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, which she unwrapped across the table: a book. Handwritten. Water-swollen and re-dried so many times the pages rippled like frozen pondwater.

"Grandmother's grandmother's grandmother," Odile said. "Back when hedge-witches still remembered what the hedge was *for*." She opened it to a page marked with a crow's feather and turned it toward Elodie. "There's a reason your god's shrines are broken out here, soldier. And it isn't forgetting."

The page showed a drawing, crude but unmistakable: a figure with open hands, palms up, standing over a burning field. Beneath it, in faded ink, a line of old liturgical script — and beneath that, a translation in a later, crabbed hand:

*She who asked everything and gave nothing back. The Taker. May her silence be long and her hunger starve.*

Elodie read it three times. The words didn't rearrange themselves into sense. They stayed exactly as horrible as they were.

"That's not—" She stopped. Started again. "Veyra ended wars. Veyra answered oaths. Veyra—"

"Veyra *asked*," Odile said. "Everything. From everyone. Blood, obedience, children sworn to service before they could walk. Your valley remembers a loving god because your valley is the one place her faithful got to write the story." She closed the book softly. "Out here? Out here they remember why they chiseled off her face."

The fire popped. On the bench, Ilyen stirred and murmured something in the old tongue — soft, questioning, a child asking a parent for water — and the shard glowed once and dimmed.

Elodie sat with her hands flat on the table, on either side of the stone, not touching it. Twenty years of kneeling. Twenty years of answering a silence with loyalty, because the alternative was admitting the silence might be contempt, or absence, or — worse, far worse — *justice*.

Whatever she asks, we answer, Tomas had said. Grinning.

What if what she asked was monstrous?

"You look like a woman deciding whether to burn her own house down," Odile observed.

"I look like a woman whose house burned down twenty years ago and she's only now smelling the smoke."

"Mm." The witch gathered the book, rewrapped it, but left the shard on the table between them, glowing its low patient white. "There's a price for shelter, in my house. There's always a price. Yours can wait till morning — you're dead on your feet and the girl's dead on hers, and even I'm not wicked enough to haggle over sleeping children." She paused at the stair, one hand on the rail, and looked back.

"But hear me tonight, soldier, so it doesn't hit you cold tomorrow: I know what that stone opens. I've known since the man with the Order's coin came through. And if you're truly thinking of carrying that child to the lock—" her eyes flicked to Ilyen, sleeping, glowing faintly, trusting, "—then you'd best decide what kind of story you're in. Because the last people who tried this walk ended up writing that page in my family's book. And none of them died well."

She climbed the stairs. The goat screamed again at the rain, or the dark, or the future.

Elodie sat alone by the fire with a god's broken reputation and a sleeping child and a stone that hummed like something waiting to be wound.

Slowly, unable to stop herself, she reached out and rested two fingers on the shard.

It sang against her oath-mark — one clear, rising note — and for the first time in twenty years, in the space between that note and her heartbeat, Elodie would have sworn she felt something answer.

Not silence.

*Listening.*

Above her, a floorboard creaked. Odile, awake, watching. Deciding.

By morning, Elode knew, the witch would name her price.
