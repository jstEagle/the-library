## 10. The Hedgewitch's Price

Odile Fenwick had lived at the crook of the Marrow Road for thirty-one years, and in that time she had learned three things worth teaching: never trust a gift with teeth in it, never brew for a man who won't tell you what ails him, and never — *never* — let anything made of white stone past your doorstep.

She broke the third rule on the ninth night of autumn.

She'd seen them coming an hour before they arrived. That was the point of the charms strung along her fence line, rowan berries and knucklebones and bits of mirror glass, each one tuned to a different flavor of trouble. The mirrors went dark first, which meant iron and ill intent. Then the rowans shivered without wind, which meant something old. And the knucklebones simply fell off their strings, one after another, like small hanged men, which meant nothing Odile had a name for.

So she put the kettle on, banked the fire low, and sat in the dark of her kitchen counting the minutes until the knocking came, because it always came, and it was better to be seated when it did.

Three knocks. Evenly spaced. A soldier's knock, or someone who'd spent enough years around soldiers to forget how to knock any other way.

"Door's unlatched," she called. "If you're here to rob me, mind the jars on the second shelf. The blue ones bite."

The door opened on a woman built like a drawn bow — tall, weathered, sword riding easy on one hip in the way of someone who'd worn it so long it had become part of the skeleton. Mud to the knees. Eyes that did a full circuit of the room before they did a full circuit of Odile, and not in that order. Behind her, half-hidden in the doorway's shadow, a small girl with mud in her hair and a look in her eyes that Odile had only ever seen twice before, and both times on things that were not children.

"You're the hedgewitch," the woman said. Not a question.

"I'm the woman who lives here," Odile said. "Hedgewitch is what people call me when they want something. What do you want?"

The woman hesitated, and Odile watched her weigh lies and find them all too heavy. Good. Liars took longer.

"A child walked into my valley speaking no tongue I know," the woman said. "Carrying this." She held out a shard of white stone the length of a finger, wrapped in cloth, and even through the wrapping Odile felt it — a hum in her back teeth, a pressure behind her eyes, like the moment before lightning finds the tallest tree. "The priestess said some words over it. Old ones. Then she told us to find you."

"Maren." Odile snorted. "Still farming out the work that frightens her. Sit down, both of you. There's bread in the box and the kettle's hot, and if you touch the blue jars I'll turn you into something with more manners."

* * *

They ate. The woman — Elodie, though she gave the name grudging, like coin counted out one piece at a time — ate little and watched much. The child ate everything, slowly, deliberately, as though eating were a duty she performed rather than a pleasure, and that too was wrong. Children bolted food or refused it. They did not eat like penitents.

Odile set the kettle aside and held out her hand for the shard.

Elodie didn't give it to her immediately. She turned it over once more in her palm, and Odile saw the mark on the back of her hand as she did — a fine-lined sigil, faded almost to nothing, the kind of brand that oath-marks left when the thing they were sworn to had gone quiet. Twenty years quiet, by the grayness of it. Maybe more.

"You've been marked," Odile said. "Sworn to Veyra, unless I miss my guess. The silent god. The valley god."

Something moved behind Elodie's eyes, quick and painful, like a fish under ice. "You know about the valley."

"I know the valley is a hole in the world where nothing answers," Odile said. "I grew up two days' ride from its edge, and my grandmother wouldn't let us near it. Said the quiet there wasn't empty. Said it was *held*. Now." She wiggled her fingers. "The stone."

Elodie placed it in her palm.

The world tilted.

Odile had handled relics before — saint's bones, martyr's teeth, a splinter of the First Bell that a bishop had paid her handsomely to authenticate (and which she'd pronounced genuine because the fee was good). They carried weight, those things. History's weight. Dead men's weight.

This was not dead. This was a held breath twenty years long, and Odile was holding one end of it.

She kept her face still — a lifetime of practice — and closed her fingers around the shard and listened the way her grandmother had taught her to listen, not with ears but with the marrow. The stone sang one note. Below the note, beneath it, holding it up the way a keel holds a ship, was silence. Not absence. *Imprisonment.* Silence the way a door is silent when it's locked from the outside.

"Well?" Elodie said.

"It's a key," Odile said. She set it down on the table between them, carefully, the way you'd set down a sleeping viper. "And I'll thank you not to hand it around like a trinket. It's a covenant-key. Do you know what a covenant is, mercenary? Not a contract. Contracts are between equals and end in courts. A covenant is between unequal things — mortal and divine — and it ends in blood or miracle, and usually both. This shard is a piece of such a covenant. Living piece. Growing piece, if I'm any judge, and I am." She nodded at the girl. "It came out of her, or she came out of it. Which is it?"

The child looked at Elodie. Elodie looked at the table. The silence stretched until Odile thought neither of them would answer, and then the girl spoke — that impossible language again, vowels like water over old stones — and touched her own chest, and then touched the shard, and then, with terrible simplicity, folded both hands together as if closing a book.

"She says they're the same thing," Odile translated quietly, though she couldn't have said how she knew. The language moved through her like cold water through a crack; she caught meanings the way you catch fish with your bare hands, by being very still and very lucky. "Or she's saying she is what's left of it. The covenant. The last living piece of whatever bargain your god struck with the world."

"That's madness," Elodie said. But she said it the way people say things they've already begun to believe.

"It usually is," Odile agreed. "Madness is just truth wearing its traveling clothes." She stood, went to the shelf above the hearth, and took down a jar of ordinary goose fat, because her hands wanted something honest to do. "Here's what I can tell you and what I can't. I can tell you the shard is a key. I can tell you it opens something — or locks it, keys go both ways, that's the whole treachery of keys. I cannot tell you what door it fits, because that knowledge isn't in the stone. It's in the covenant itself, and the covenant is—" she gestured at the child— "sitting at my table eating my bread."

"So find another way to ask it," Elodie said.

Odile laughed, short and hard. "Oh, I intend to. But knowledge has a price, mercenary. Everything true does. And mine—" she turned, wiping her hands— "is name."

* * *

"Name," Elodie repeated.

"The child's true name." Odile sat back down across from her. "Not what you call her. What she *is*. Every living thing has one — the word the world used when it made you, before your mother picked something pretty. Most folk never learn theirs and live happier for it. But names are power, and power is currency, and I'm asking to be paid."

"No."

It came fast and flat, no thought in it at all, and Odile found herself revising her estimate of the woman upward. She'd expected haggling. Counter-offers. *What else do you want, surely there's something else.* Mercenaries always haggled. It was practically breathing.

Instead Elodie had simply said *no*, and her hand had come to rest on the back of the girl's neck — lightly, barely touching, but resting there all the same, the way a wall rests on its foundations.

"You haven't heard the price and refused it," Odile said, because fairness demanded the record show.

"I heard it. Her name. No."

"A name freely given is the oldest payment there is. I could buy a great deal with it. Protection for her, perhaps, in ways your sword can't manage. Or—" she let it hang— "a great deal against her, if I were the sort who sold. You don't know which sort I am. That's the risk of every purchase, and yet you buy bread from strangers."

"I bake my own," Elodie said.

Odile smiled despite herself. "I imagine you do."

The girl tugged at Elodie's sleeve and spoke — three soft syllables in that drowned-old language — and Odile caught the shape of it: *it's all right, it's all right, it's all right,* repeated like a charm, like a prayer, like a child soothing a frightened animal. Except the child wasn't the frightened one.

"She's offering herself," Odile said. "Freely. You understand that makes it worse, not better? A given name binds deeper than a taken one."

"I don't care what it makes," Elodie said. "She doesn't get to pay for me. She's a child. Whatever debt this road runs up, it's mine to carry." Her voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which was worse. "You want payment, witch. Name something else."

"There is nothing else of equal weight."

"Then name something unequal and I'll decide what it costs me."

And there it was. Odile had spent thirty-one years at the crook of the Marrow Road watching travelers lie to themselves — about their errands, their gods, their griefs — and she knew the sound of a person telling the truth at last. It sounded like this. Quiet. Inflexible. A little afraid, and going forward anyway.

She looked at the girl. The girl looked back, and her eyes were old, old, old, older than the kingdom, older than the road, and Odile thought: *you're not really a child at all, are you, whatever you're wearing.* And then the girl blinked, and smeared bread crumbs off her chin with her sleeve, and was a child again, and Odile's heart did something complicated and unwelcome that she decided to examine later, or never.

"All right," Odile said. "Blood-oath service. One favor owed, called in at a time of my choosing, sealed the old way — cut palms, spoken intent, no weaseling. That's the price instead of the name. And understand, mercenary: the old way means the debt follows you. Across borders. Across death, some say. I choose when the bill comes due and I won't tell you now what it buys. That's the trade. Take it or walk out my door."

* * *

Elodie didn't hesitate long. Odile would remember that. Most people, offered a blank check written on their own future, dithered, prayed, bargained for limits. Elodie Nakamura drew her belt knife, turned it, and held it out handle-first across the table.

"Your palm or mine first?"

"Mine. I'm the creditor. We start as we mean to continue."

The cutting was quick and unceremonious. Odile opened her own palm along the life-line, pressed it to Elodie's, and spoke the words her grandmother had taught her in a kitchen much like this one, in a dialect that predated the Crown and would outlast it:

*"By the red that runs in both, one debt is made. Called when needed, paid when named, binding beyond doors and distances. So say I, Odile Fenwick, and so accepts the debtor."*

"And so accepts the debtor," Elodie repeated.

The blood warmed where the cuts met — not painfully, but unmistakably, a thread of heat sewing itself through both hands — and Odile felt the debt settle into place like a stone dropping into a well. Deep down. A long time falling. Somewhere far below, a faint splash.

She bandaged both palms with linen and goose fat, grumbling professionally about infection, and while she worked she talked, because talking was how she thought and also how she spied.

"The Order of the Quiet will have trackers on you by now. Inquisitors don't lose vessels; vessels are the whole of their employment." She tied off the knot. "There's a back way north from here, along the Marrow, that avoids the toll bridge and the garrison at Ford Ansel. I'll draw it for you. That part's free — consider it a wedding gift."

"We're not married."

"Shame. You'd be easy to keep. You fix fences without being asked." She glanced at the window, where the light had gone the color of weak tea. "You'll stay the night. Not charity — tactics. Nobody crosses the Marrow ford in the dark, and the Order knows that, so anyone smart moves at dawn, and anyone moving at dawn should sleep first. There's straw in the loft and the girl takes the bed, and before you argue, mercenary, note that you're bleeding from a sacred contract and I outrank you tonight."

For a moment Elodie looked like she might argue anyway. Then the girl took her bandaged hand — the oath-marked one, Odile noticed, the one that had gone gray and silent for twenty years — and held it, and said something soft in the old tongue, and Elodie stopped arguing the way a storm stops when it runs out of coast.

*Later*, Odile thought. *Examine that later.*

* * *

She woke once in the small hours and came down for water and stopped on the stairs.

The loft lamp was still lit. Elodie sat beneath it with the shard in her lap, turning it over and over, and the girl slept curled against her side with her head on the mercenary's shoulder, and Elodie had gone absolutely still — the stillness of a woman who has discovered that moving would wake the sleeper, and who would sooner sit until her legs died than allow that.

Twenty years, Odile thought. Twenty years sworn to a god who wouldn't answer, carrying a dead man's ghost and four broken oaths, and the first time someone leans on you trusting you completely, you sit like stone so as not to disturb them.

*Careful, mercenary,* Odile thought, climbing back upstairs with her untouched water. *That's how it starts. First you don't move. Then you don't leave. Then you'd burn the world for her and call it Tuesday.*

She'd seen it before. She'd *been* it before, once, a long time ago, for someone whose name she kept in a box under the floorboards with the other things that had cost too much.

At dawn she fed them eggs and drew the map, and stood in her doorway with her bandaged hand raised in something between farewell and benediction.

"One more thing," she said, as they reached the gate. "The shard — I read it deeper than I told you. There's a second note under the first. Under the lock, under the silence. Something listening." She paused. "It knows your name now, mercenary. Both of you. Whatever door you're walking toward — walk faster."

Elodie looked back at her, and the morning light caught the mark on her hand, and for just a heartbeat the faded gray sigil seemed to flicker — one pulse, faint as a dying ember remembering it had been fire.

Then it was gone, and they were on the road, and Odile went inside to pour herself a drink and wonder, not for the first time and certainly not for the last, what in the wide ruined world she had just tied herself to.
