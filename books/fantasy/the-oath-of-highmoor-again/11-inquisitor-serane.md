## 11. Inquisitor Serane

The chapel at Ashford Vale had burned down twice before Serane ever saw it, and the villagers had rebuilt it both times in the same stubborn footprint, as if the ground itself insisted on the shape of their devotion. She stood in the third incarnation's shadow now — unburned, whitewashed, small — and felt the familiar tightness behind her sternum that she had learned, over eleven years of service, to call certainty.

It was not certainty. But the Order did not have a word for what it actually was, and so certainty would do.

"Captain." Brother Callow, her second, held out the ledger of parish records. "No births unregistered in forty years. No foundlings taken in. The priestess keeps a clean house."

"The priestess keeps a *quiet* house," Serane said. "There's a difference."

Callow frowned. He was a good man, Callow — pious in the way the Crown preferred, faith folded neatly into obedience like a letter into an envelope. He could not yet hear the difference between silence and quiet. Perhaps he never would. That was the point of the training.

Serane took the ledger and flipped through it herself, though she already knew what it would say. Records lied by omission; that was their native tongue. What she wanted was not in the ledger. It was in the way the miller wouldn't meet her eyes. In the extra chair, still warm, that the innkeeper's wife had swept out of the common room when the riders were sighted on the ridge. In the smell of porridge for three where the household swore they lived alone.

Someone had passed through Ashford Vale recently. Someone small.

"A vessel," she said aloud, tasting the word the way the old liturgies tasted it — *vesselum, vas Dei* — and hating how much it sounded like reverence even in her own mouth.

"You believe the report, Captain?" Callow asked. "A child? Walking out of the high passes alone?"

"I believe the shard-sense registered on three separate relay stones between here and the border," Serane said. "I believe whatever walked out of those passes made dead stone sing. Children or otherwise, we follow it."

She closed the ledger and handed it back, and her gloved hand did not shake, because she had trained it not to, because she had trained everything not to, because that was the whole of the last eleven years: a long curriculum in not-shaking.

* * *

They made camp that night in the ruins of a roadside shrine to Veyra.

This was policy, not blasphemy — the Order of the Quiet requisitioned defaced holy sites wherever it marched, on the principle that a god's abandoned houses made the best stables. There was something fitting about it. The headless statue in the alcove still had its hands, though. They were open, palms up, in the gesture the old icons called *the receiving*. Serane had looked at those hands every night for a decade and a half, on shrine after shrine after shrine, and had never once decided whether they were begging or offering.

She sat apart from the men, cleaned her blade, and let herself do the thing she only permitted at night, alone, with steel in her hands: she remembered.

The oath had been sworn on a midsummer morning twenty-two years ago, in a circle of five, on the hill above Highmoor where the grass grew silver in the wind. She could still recite it, though recitation was forbidden to her now under penalty of unmaking. The words had weight the way river-stones have weight — smooth, cold, cumulative.

*I swear my voice to your silence. I swear my hand to your peace. Where you walk unseen, I will be your seeing. Where you cannot speak, I will be your speaking.*

Five voices. Elodie Nakamura's had been the steadiest. Tomas had laughed halfway through and been elbowed by Halvard. Sarel had wept without shame. And Serane herself — nineteen years old, knees bleeding from the vigil, madly, stupidly happy — had thought: *this is the truest thing I will ever do.*

She had been right about that. That was the bitter joke of her life. Everything since had been less true.

"Tomas," she said quietly, to the headless statue, because some nights his name was the prayer. "You should have run slower."

Tomas had died at Redmere Ford, in the first hour of the battle, in the first hour of the Silence — the god's voice cut off mid-word like a lute string snapped under a boot. One moment Veyra's power had flowed through all five of them, warm as sunlight through glass, and the next there was nothing, just five mortals standing in a field full of screaming, holding oaths to a god who had stopped existing. Or stopped answering. Eleven years of inquiry had never settled which, and the Order's scholars had fought duels over the distinction.

Elodie had carried Tomas out of the water herself. Serane remembered the weight of him in both their arms, the way the river ran red downstream, the way Elodie had laid him on the bank and then simply knelt there, not praying — praying had stopped working that day — just kneeling, as if her body had forgotten any other position.

And then Elodie had gone home to the valley. And Serane had gone to the capital. And that was the whole story of them, really: two women who walked away from the same corpse in opposite directions.

Callow appeared at her shoulder with tea. "Captain. The relay stone at the crossroads hummed again at dusk. Stronger. Whatever carries the shard is moving north."

"North toward Corvassa?"

"Or toward the Godspine roads."

Serane looked at the cup in her hands and thought about the last time she'd drunk tea on a shrine floor, which had been with Elodie, in a chapel very like this one, before either of them had titles. Elodie had always taken hers too sweet. *Sugar's for people who've earned it,* Serane used to tease, and Elodie had said, *then I'll earn it,* and had gone out the next morning and earned it, somehow, always, infuriatingly.

"Double the pickets," she said. "And send a rider ahead to the Archive liaison in Corvassa. If the vessel means to reach the city, I want eyes on every gate before we are."

"Yes, Captain." Callow hesitated. "The men are asking. About the child. Whether we'd truly—"

"Tell them the Order does not harm vessels," Serane said. "We *contain* them. There is a difference."

"There is?" he asked, honestly.

She almost smiled. "Pray you never learn it."

He withdrew. Serane sat a while longer with the tea going cold and the headless god's hands open above her, and permitted herself one more memory, the expensive kind:

The night before the Silence, she and Elodie had stood watch together on the walls at Redmere, and Elodie had said, out of nowhere, *if the god ever goes quiet — really quiet — what do you think the oath becomes?*

*A promise kept to an empty house,* Serane had answered, meaning to be funny.

And Elodie had been silent a long moment, looking east toward Highmoor, and had said: *then somebody ought to knock.*

* * *

Three days later, they found the chapel at Thornhill still smoking.

Serane rode into the yard at a canter and knew before she dismounted that it was wrong. Her orders regarding the vessel had been explicit and she had repeated them twice: track, contain, report. No fires. No forced entry while civilians sheltered inside. The Order of the Quiet did not burn churches; it *inherited* them.

Lieutenant Vasse met her in the yard, helmet under his arm, face carefully blank in the way of men about to lie to their superior.

"Report," Serane said.

"We received intelligence the vessel was housed here. Prior to your arrival, Captain. From a reliable source within the village."

"Whose order?"

Vasse's jaw tightened. "Acting Warden's authority. The detachment from Corvassa arrived this morning. They said—" He stopped.

"They said what, Lieutenant?"

"They said the Order's standing instruction had been amended. That the vessel is to be recovered by any means. That witnesses to the recovery are to be..." He chose the word the way a man chooses a step over a cliff. "*Minimized.*"

Serane looked past him at the smoking shell of the chapel. Through the broken doorway she could see the scorch-shadow of a pew, and beside it, smaller, the scorch-shadow of something that had once been a kneeler, and she thought of Maren — no, she didn't know the priestess's name, she refused to know it, names made the arithmetic harder — she thought of *a priestess*, elderly presumably, who had fed a stranger's child and paid for it with her life and her church.

"Names," Serane said. "Of the detachment commander."

"Warden Cray. He left a writ under seal."

She read the writ in her tent. It was authentic — the countersignature belonged to the Archon of the Quiet himself — and it was a license for butchery dressed in administrative language, and it told her something she had suspected for two years and had managed, through discipline, not to know: the Order was no longer containing the covenant's failure. It was *accelerating* it. Somewhere above her, men had decided that if the old covenant must die, it would die quickly, on their terms, with the vessel in hand and no loose ends singing on relay stones.

And beneath that knowledge, colder, sat another: the shard-sense readings were getting stronger, and closer, and moving along exactly the road she herself would choose toward the capital, which meant that sooner or later — probably sooner — she was going to catch up to the child.

And behind the child, because the child was a child and children travel badly, would be whoever was carrying her.

Serane knew who that would be. She had known since the first relay stone sang, if she was honest, known in the marrow-place where oaths live after the god leaves them. Elodie Nakamura had spent twenty years in the valley like a knife in a drawer, and now the drawer was open.

She spread the campaign map on her table and put one gloved finger on the river road north of Thornhill. There was a ferry crossing at Weir's Bend, a chokepoint, perfect for an ambush. Warden Cray would already know about it. Cray would already be arranging it, with his fifty men and his sealed writ and his minimized witnesses.

If she sent warning — an unsigned note, a delayed order, anything — she would be committing treason against the Order. If she did not, she would be handing Elodie Nakamura into a massacre, along with a child whom the oldest texts called *vas Dei*, the vessel of god, and whom Serane, in the privacy of her one permitted nightly memory, had already begun to think of as a little girl with mud on her feet.

Callow ducked into the tent. "Riders ready on your word, Captain. Do we make for Weir's Bend?"

Serane looked at the map. Twenty-two years ago she had sworn her voice to a silent god, her hand to its peace. The god had never thanked her. The Order had promoted her instead, which amounted to the same thing from most employers, and she had tried very hard to let it.

*Then somebody ought to knock.*

"Send a courier ahead," she heard herself say. "To the garrison at Weir's Bend. Order them to hold the crossing — hold it, Lieutenant, not close it. Any Crown detachment arriving without my countersignature is to be detained at the bridge until I arrive in person." She lifted her eyes to Callow's puzzled face. "Warden Cray will object."

"And if he overrides us?"

"Then he'll have to explain to the Archon why an inquisitor captain of the Quiet was detained by her own garrison," Serane said. "Ride."

When he was gone she sat down slowly, and discovered that her hands were shaking after all — eleven years of training undone by one forged order and one sweet tooth she hadn't thought of in a decade. She pressed her palms flat against the map, over the river, over the road, over the small inked square that marked a burned chapel, until the trembling stopped.

She was still serving the Crown. She was still hunting the vessel. She told herself both things were true, and mostly believed it, the way you can mostly believe a roof will hold when the snow is coming down heavy — right up until you hear the first creak.

Far to the north, on a road she couldn't see, a child was carrying a piece of a god toward the mountains, and Serane Vail, last faithful liar of the Order of the Quiet, had just bought her — for however briefly — a clear bridge.

She only prayed, insofar as she still prayed to anything, that Elodie Nakamura would never find out who had cleared the way.

Because if Elodie ever learned it, she would ask why. And Serane had spent eleven years building an answer to that question, and knew, deep in the oath-scarred marrow of her, that the answer was a lie with about a month left to live.
