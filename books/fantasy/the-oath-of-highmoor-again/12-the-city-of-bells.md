## 12. The City of Bells

You heard Corvassa before you saw it.

The bells started at dawn, a mile out, when the road was still climbing through orchards gone wild with late frost. A hundred towers' worth of bronze rolling over the hills like weather. Rennick, who had not stopped talking since the hedgewitch's cottage, went quiet for exactly as long as it took the first peal to die.

"There she is," he said. "The City of Bells. Loudest place in the world. You get used to it the way you get used to a bad knee."

Ilyen stood on her toes in the ditch beside the road, trying to see over the hedge. She'd been silent for three days — her usual silence, the one that wasn't mute so much as patient — but now she made a small sound, almost a word, and grabbed my sleeve and pointed.

"I know," I said. "Big."

*Very big,* said her face.

Rennick grinned at her. He'd been doing that a lot lately, working at her like a man coaxing a fire. She hadn't given him much. But she'd stopped flinching when he moved fast, which by Rennick's standards was probably a marriage proposal.

"Rule one," he said, holding up a finger. "Corvassa rules. One: nobody looks at you twice if you look like you belong. Walk like you're bored. Bored people are invisible."

"Rule two?" I asked.

"Never eat the fish. Doesn't matter how good it smells. It came out of the Vell and the Vell comes out of every sewer in the city."

"Rule three?"

He hesitated. The grin faded into something more honest. "Trust no one who smiles first."

We walked on toward the noise.

* * *

I had never seen a city. That was the truth of my life that I'd never examined until now: twenty-eight years in Highmoor Valley, where the largest settlement was a market village of four hundred souls and the tallest structure was Maren's chapel steeple, and even that was mostly scaffolding and hope. I'd told myself I stayed because of the oath. Because the god was silent everywhere except in the valley's quiet, because an oath-bound sword should stay where the oath still meant something. All true, maybe. But standing on the ridge with Corvassa spread below me — ten thousand rooftops, smoke plumes, the brown ribbon of the Vell cutting through it all, bridges stacked on bridges like a child's fantasy — I understood there'd been another reason too, simpler and less noble.

The world was enormous, and I was afraid of it.

"Close your mouth," Rennick said kindly. "Flies."

The gates took an hour. Not because of us — refugees from the dying harvests had been trickling in for weeks, thin-faced farmers with everything they owned in bundles, and the gate sergeants were waving most of them through with a boredom that felt obscene given what they were fleeing. It took an hour because there were that many people. I kept Ilyen between me and Rennick, her hand in mine, and tried not to stare, and stared anyway.

Inside the walls the smell hit first: woodsmoke, horse dung, frying onions, tar, something sweet rotting somewhere, and under all of it the river. Then the sound — the bells overhead braided with a thousand voices, cart wheels, a fiddler, a woman screaming cheerfully about cabbages. Then the crowd itself, a river of its own, and we were stones in it.

Ilyen's grip on my hand turned painful.

She pressed close to my leg. Her eyes were huge, tracking everything — the jugglers, the beggars, a dog stealing a sausage to a chorus of laughter, a man beating a mule, the painted faces of two women laughing on a balcony. I couldn't tell what frightened her until I followed her gaze properly and understood it wasn't any single thing.

It was all of it. So much life, spent so carelessly, in such quantity.

In Highmoor you knew everyone whose path crossed yours. Here, ten thousand strangers brushed past us and not one looked. Ilyen had come out of nowhere speaking a dead language, carrying a shard of a god, and here she was nobody. Invisible. Safe, maybe. Or lost.

"Hungry?" Rennick asked.

She nodded.

We ate fried dough from a stall, sitting on the steps of a fountain choked with leaves, and Ilyen ate hers with the focused solemnity she brought to all meals, as if food might be revoked at any moment. It probably had been, before. Before the valley, before my door. There were years in that child I would never see and could not imagine.

"The Archive," I said. "Where is it."

Rennick licked his fingers. "Hill district. Old town. You can't miss it — big black building, used to be a temple back when temples were allowed architecture." He squinted at me. "You understand what you're asking for. The Archive of Covenants isn't a library with a reading room. It's a vault. The Order of the Quiet keeps its own archivists inside. You want to walk into the Order's house and read their secrets off their own shelves."

"That's why I brought a thief."

"A *disgraced* archive-thief. Important distinction. Disgraced means they caught me." He rubbed his jaw. "There are ways in. Ways I knew, five years ago. Some of them will still work. Give me tonight to find out which."

"And until tonight?"

"We hide in plain sight." He nodded across the square, at a boarding house with a sign showing a leaping fish. "Worst beds in Corvassa, which means nobody asks questions. Rule four: cheap rooms are honest rooms. Nobody lies to you for two coppers a night."

Ilyen tugged my sleeve and pointed at the fountain, where a fat boy was sailing a paper boat. She watched it with an expression I'd learned to read as *wanting*. I dug out a scrap of paper from my pack, folded it badly into a boat, and set it on the water. It sank in nine seconds.

She looked up at me with devastating patience, took the next sheet, folded it herself — crisp, precise creases, better than mine in every way — and sailed it. It crossed the entire basin and bumped the far wall.

"Show-off," I said.

Something happened to her face then. Small, quick, gone in a breath. Later, turning it over in memory, I would name it: it was the first time she'd smiled.

That's how I'll remember our last good hour. A paper boat and fried dough, with the bells ringing above us, and no idea yet what the city had planned.

* * *

The boarding house was exactly as advertised. Our room was on the third floor, under the roof, hot as a kiln, with a window overlooking an airshaft and a bed wide enough for Ilyen and narrow enough for arguments. Rennick went out to "make inquiries" — his phrase, delivered with a wink that suggested the inquiries might involve cards or dice or other men's pockets — and I barred the door behind him and sat with my back to it and did what I always did before sleep.

Checked the shard. Checked the mark.

The white stone sat warm in Ilyen's cupped hands. Against my forearm, beneath the wrapped linen, my oath-mark hummed its low note, the same note it had held since the day the priestess put them near each other and the whole room's candle flames leaned like grass in wind. Two weeks on the road and it hadn't changed. Steady. Waiting.

"Maren said the Archive keeps the old covenant records," Ilyen said suddenly.

I blinked. Full sentences from her were rare enough to feel like gifts. Common tongue, flat accentless, learned — I still didn't know how — somewhere in whatever she'd been before my door.

"She did."

"You think they have the words. The true ones. What the oath *was*."

"I think somebody wrote it down. Somebody always writes things down." I looked at her. "Why?"

She was quiet long enough that I thought she wouldn't answer. The bells rang the hour — eight, I counted, learning the city's arithmetic against my will.

"When I remember things," she said carefully, "they aren't pictures. They're more like... doors. Closed ones. I can feel how many." She held up her hand, fingers spread, then curled two down. "Three open. Lots closed."

"What's behind the closed ones?"

"I don't know. That's why they're doors." She looked at me with those gray eyes that were older than her body and younger than her voice, both at once. "Elodie. If we open them and something bad is behind them. Will you still—"

"Yes," I said.

She hadn't finished the question. I answered it anyway, because I already knew how it ended. *Will you still want me.* As if there were a version of the answer that wasn't yes. As if anything she'd been built to do could outweigh the child who folded paper boats and ate like each meal was stolen.

She nodded slowly, satisfied, and lay down on the bed, and was asleep within minutes the way children sleep — instantly, totally, as though consciousness were a chore she'd finished for the day.

I stayed at the door with my sword across my knees and listened to the city breathe.

* * *

Rennick came back after midnight smelling of cheap wine and expensive trouble.

"Good news and bad news," he whispered, easing the bar aside. He glanced at Ilyen, still asleep, and lowered himself onto the floor beside me with a wince. "Good news: the archive has a delivery entrance on Cordwainer Lane. Coal, candles, ink. Comes in before dawn. Guards check the carts but they don't check the coal."

"And the bad news?"

"The Order of the Quiet doubled its patrols this week." He said it flatly, watching me. "Not just around the archive. Whole city. They're searching for someone."

My stomach went cold and stayed there. "Describe the someone."

"They weren't describing anyone. That's the point. Just — checking faces. More than usual. And there's a notice board outside the Chancellery with new postings, and I couldn't read them from the street, but I recognized the seal." He tapped his own shoulder, where a sigil would sit on an Order livery. "They know a vessel exists. If they didn't before the valley, they do now. Serane doesn't write reports saying 'lost them.' She writes reports saying 'located, contained, retrieval pending.'"

I looked at Ilyen, asleep with one fist curled under her chin.

"So we go tomorrow," I said. "Dawn delivery. In the coal."

"In the coal," he agreed. "God help us, in the coal."

* * *

It should have worked.

We were up before the bells, moving through streets still blue with night, and the coal cart was where he said, and the driver was bribable, and for one shining hour I let myself believe the city was going to let us win. We crouched in the yard behind the black bulk of the old temple — and it *was* a temple once, you could see it in the bones of it, pillars and high blind windows, now bricked and barred and hung with the Order's grey banners — waiting for the yard gate to open.

That's when the second cart arrived.

Not coal. Slavers ran their trade openly enough in Corvassa's margins, licensed and taxed, human cargo listed as indentures. This one was a plain covered wagon, and the men unloading it moved wrong — too quiet, too coordinated, working the edges of the yard rather than the center. Rennick saw it the same moment I did. His hand found my arm.

"Not slavers," he breathed. "Order plainclothes. Look at the boots."

I looked. Grey riding boots, mud from the hills still on them. Valley mud.

And then I understood the trap entire: the wagon wasn't delivering anything. It was bait dressed as opportunity — or worse, it was a net they cast every morning, because they'd figured out what I'd figured out, that a fugitive with a child would eventually need the archive, and the only way in was through the cracks, and cracks were where hunters wait.

"Back," I whispered. "Slow."

Too slow. A door opened in the archive's flank — a servant's postern — and a man stepped out carrying a ledger, and his eyes swept the yard with the flat professional patience of someone counting shadows, and they snagged on our shadow, the one detail that didn't fit.

"Oi," he said.

Everything after that happened fast, the way violence does, without transitions.

Rennick threw a coin purse high and left — an old street conjuring, every eye in the yard following money — and I pulled Ilyen through the gap between the coal cart and the wall, and then a hand shot out of the covered wagon and closed on Ilyen's braid.

She screamed. First time I'd ever heard her truly scream.

The world went narrow and red. There were three of them coming out of the wagon, quick men with sap and cord, professionals, the kind who take children for coin and sleep well after. The first got a boot to the knee from me and went down gurgling. The second swung the sap at my head and I caught his wrist and broke it against my pauldron and put him into the wall hard enough to leave an outline. The third had a knife at Ilyen's throat and was saying something — *"easy, easy, nobody needs to—"*

I showed him my forearm. Linen torn away, the oath-mark bare, and I don't know what he saw in it — light, or just the certainty — but his nerve broke. He shoved Ilyen at me and ran, and I let him, because Ilyen was in my arms shaking like a snared bird and chasing him meant letting go.

"Yard's clear!" Rennick shouted from the gate, where he stood over the first man, breathing hard. "For about ninety seconds! Move!"

* * *

We moved. Alleys, a fish market, a drainage tunnel Rennick swore he'd crawled through at nineteen and clearly had not missed. By full light we were three districts away, in a tanner's row where the stink covered everything including fear, and Ilyen had stopped shaking and gone into the flat silent state I hated worst — the one where she became very still and very polite and looked at you like she was memorizing your face in case she lost you.

Kneeling in front of her, I took her hands. They were ice.

"Look at me."

She looked.

"No one takes you," I said. "Not the Order. Not slavers. Not gods. No one. Do you hear me? While I'm alive, no one takes you."

Her lip trembled. "You killed them."

"One's alive."

"You killed them *for me*."

"Yes."

She studied me for a long time, the way she studied everything, thoroughly and without mercy. Then she said, in the tone of someone confirming a terrible and wonderful suspicion:

"You'd do it again."

"I'd do it again."

She nodded once, filed it away, and leaned her forehead against mine. Above us the bells rang the hour, indifferent and beautiful, and I held the last living seal of a dying covenant in a city that wanted to sell her, cage her, or burn her name from its books, and I understood — finally, fully, in my bones instead of my head — what Maren had tried to tell me at her kitchen table.

The valley hadn't been hiding me from the world.

It had been hiding the world from me.

* * *

Rennick found us an hour later, ghosting out of the crowd with his collar up. He looked older than he had yesterday.

"Two things," he said, sitting heavily. "One: the men you fought. I checked. They weren't Order. They were hired muscle, but hired through a broker who works exclusively for the Chancery. So — Order, laundered. Which means they wanted it deniable. Which means Serane isn't sure where you are, or she'd have sent knights, not kidnappers." He paused. "Kidnappers mean she wants the child intact and unmarked. There's a procedure for vessels. I've heard rumors."

"Don't," I said.

"Didn't say it." He rubbed his face. "Two: the archive's burned."

The word landed wrong, like a stair missing in the dark. "What?"

"Last night. After we left. Fire in the west stacks, started in the covenant wing — the exact shelves, Elodie. Not an accident. Somebody got there ahead of us and burned the records." He met my eyes. "Somebody who knew what we were looking for."

I sat very still. The bells rang on. Somewhere behind my ribs, the oath-mark hummed its steady, maddening note, and for the first time in two weeks it sounded less like guidance and more like laughter.

"Then the answers are gone," I said.

"No." Rennick reached into his coat and drew out a slim book, leather-wrapped, scorched at one corner. He set it in my hands like it was made of eggshell. "Answers are never gone. Someone always steals them first. Question is whether they're friend or thief."

"Which are you?"

He smiled, tired and crooked. "Both. Ask me again tomorrow."

The book had no title on its cover. Inside the cover, in a dead hand on dead vellum, was written a single line in the language older than kingdoms — and Ilyen, leaning over my arm, read it aloud without hesitation, in a voice not entirely her own.

*"Here begins the account of the five who bound the god."*
