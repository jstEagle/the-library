## 14. The First Covenant

The Archive of Covenants smelled of dust and candle fat and, beneath both, something older — a smell Rennick had never managed to name in eleven years of stealing from it. He'd settled on *patience*. The place reeked of patience. Centuries of it, pressed flat between vellum leaves and stacked to the ceiling in iron cabinets that no honest scholar could afford.

He led them down the service stair at the back of the scriptorium, past the sleeping archivist's office — old Corvus snored like a collapsing mine shaft, bless him — and into the sub-levels where the real collection lived. Not the public reading rooms with their pretty illuminated genealogies. The deep shelves. The ones you needed a key for, or a ladder, or in Rennick's case, a grudge and a lockpick set he'd made himself from a broken harp.

"Stay close," he whispered. "And don't touch anything with a red spine. Those are the Crown's copies, and they're warded."

"Warded how?" Elodie asked. She moved behind him with the child on her hip like she'd been doing it all her life, which was absurd, because three days ago she hadn't known the child existed.

"You sneeze near one and it screams your name to the night-watch." He glanced back. "I'm not being colorful. It happened to a colleague. Danner. They still call his cell 'the Sneeze.'"

Behind Elodie, the mute girl — Ilyen, though they'd only just learned her name — clutched the white stone shard against her chest. Even here, three floors below ground, away from any window, it gave off a faint light. Like moonlight through milk. Rennick had been trying not to look at it since the river gate. Trying and failing.

*Eleven years,* he thought. *Eleven years of lifting ledgers and forging seals and selling secrets to merchants who hated each other, and now this.* A mercenary woman who spoke to gods that didn't answer. A child who hummed when she slept. A stone that glowed.

It was ridiculous. It was wonderful. He hated how much he wanted to know more.

"Here." He stopped before a cabinet marked with a sigil Rennick had spent two years earning the right to see: a circle bisected by a vertical line. The mark of the First Conclave. "The pre-kingdom holdings. Everything the Crown didn't burn."

"They burned things?" Elodie asked.

"The Crown burns everything it can't control. Why do you think the Order of the Quiet exists?" He knelt and went to work on the lock. "Officially, they preserve sacred history. Unofficially, they edit it. There are three versions of every important event in Corvassa: what happened, what the Crown says happened, and what's actually in this cabinet."

*Click.*

He swung the doors open onto a wall of grey spines. No gilt, no illumination. Just plain binding and plain labels in a hand so old the ink had gone brown.

"What are we looking for?" Elodie asked.

"Honestly? Anything about Veyra." He pulled a volume out, blew off the dust, opened it. Genealogies. Put it back. Another. Land grants along the Godspine foothills. Another. "The Order keeps its own version of the Silence locked up tighter than this. But the Conclave predates the Crown. If Veyra fell silent twenty years ago, there should be *something*—"

"Ilyen."

Rennick looked up.

The child had slid down from Elodie's hip without a sound. She was standing before the open cabinet, head tilted, staring at the grey spines the way other children stared at sweet-sellers' windows. Her small hand rose, hovered, and then — deliberate as a priest at altar — touched the third volume from the left.

"That one," she said.

Her second word in the common tongue, as far as Rennick knew. The first had been *hurry*, screamed on a riverbank while slavers closed in. This one was quiet. Certain. A command.

Elodie looked at Rennick. Rennick looked at the book. The book sat on the shelf radiating nothing but dust and age, utterly ordinary, and somehow the most frightening object in the room.

"She's never wrong," Elodie said quietly. "About things like this."

"She's six."

"She's never wrong," Elodie repeated, in the tone of a woman who had stopped arguing with the universe some days ago.

Rennick took the volume down. It was heavier than it looked — the pages were lead-backed, which explained the weight and the price of the thing — and bound in hide that might have been goat or might have been something else entirely. The label read only: *COVENANT OF THE FIVE. DO NOT COPY.*

"Covenants plural," Rennick murmured. "There are dozens of covenant records in this archive. Contracts between gods and mortal lines, mostly dead. But 'the Five'..." He shook his head. "That phrase doesn't appear anywhere else. Believe me. I've looked."

"Why have you looked?" Elodie asked. Sharp. Suspicious. She always got that way when a piece fit too neatly.

Rennick weighed the question and decided — not for the first time since meeting these two — that lying would be more work than truth. "Because eleven years ago I stole a document for a client. A fragment. One page, half-burned, mentioning five names and a mountain. My client paid me a fortune and then vanished. And the Order burned my archive access the same week, stripped my credentials, and put a notice out for my arrest." He shrugged. "I've been curious ever since. Curiosity's expensive."

"What were the names?"

"I only remember one." He opened the leaden cover. "Because it was carved into a shrine I used to sleep behind as a boy. **Sarel**."

Elodie went very still.

* * *

The book did not begin with words. It began with a map.

A full-page rendering of Highmoor Valley — Rennick recognized it instantly from Elodie's descriptions, the long green trough between the ridgelines, the river running north — but drawn wrong. Drawn as architecture. Every hill rendered as a buttress. Every stream as an aqueduct. And at the valley's heart, where Elodie's cottage stood, where the village chapel stood, where the well stood, a single symbol repeated over and over in fine red ink:

**THE LOCK.**

"It's a diagram," Rennick breathed. He turned the page. Another map, wider now — the whole realm — and Highmoor Valley sat at its center like a keystone in an arch. Lines ran outward from it, dozens of them, each terminating in a name. He scanned the names. Shrines. Every defaced, broken shrine they'd passed on the road north. Every one anchored by a line back to the valley.

"It's not a ward," Elodie said. Her voice had gone strange. Flat. The voice of someone reading their own death warrant and finding the handwriting familiar. "Everyone says the valley is protected. That magic can't cross in. That Veyra shielded us."

"Keep reading," said Ilyen.

They both stared at her. She pointed one small finger at the next page, patient as winter.

The next page was text. Old liturgical script, but Rennick had spent enough years forging church documents to read it slowly, painfully, with his lips moving. He wished he couldn't.

*"...and it came to pass in the eleventh year of the War of Voices that the faithful despaired, for Veyra's voice, raised in battle, had become a weapon that unmade the very fields it sought to save. Where the god spoke, rivers turned. Where the god listened, mountains walked. Five servants of the god came before the Conclave and proposed the Unspeakable Act: that the voice of Veyra be bound, that the war be ended not by victory but by silence—"*

"No," Elodie said.

"—that five oath-bearers be sealed to the binding, their oaths becoming the lock's five tumblers, and that the valley of Highmoor be consecrated as the housing of the seal, that no magic might cross its borders lest the prison be tested—"

"*No.*" Elodie's hand shot out and gripped the edge of the shelf. Her knuckles had gone white. "Veyra fell silent. Twenty years ago. Mid-battle. We *felt* it go. Tomas died because the god wasn't there anymore, because the healing wouldn't come, because—"

She stopped. Rennick watched her do the arithmetic. Watched it land.

"The god didn't fall silent," he said softly. "The god was silenced. By its own faithful. By five people who swore beside it and then swore it shut."

"And my oath—" Elodie's voice cracked down the middle. "My oath is a *tumbler*. Twenty years I prayed into a dead line and thought I'd been abandoned, and the whole time I was the *lock*. I was the cage. I've lived inside the prison my entire life and called it home and called it protection and thanked the god for walls that were built to keep *it* in—"

"Elodie."

She didn't hear him. She was shaking. Ilyen crossed the space between them and pressed herself against Elodie's leg, and the white shard flared once, bright as a struck match, and dimmed.

Rennick turned the page because someone had to.

*"...and the fifth tumbler, Sarel the Steadfast, did speak the sealing words, and the first tumbler, Halvard, did set his seal upon the valley stone, and the second, Maren-of-the-First-Faith, did consecrate the housing—"*

"Maren," Rennick said. "Your priestess is named Maren."

"That's a common name." But Elodie didn't sound like she believed it.

"—and the third tumbler, Tomas the Kind, refused the Act at the last hour and did turn his oath against the others, and was unmade in the struggle, and the fourth tumbler was chosen in his stead from among the faithful: a soldier of no lineage, whose name is struck here per her own request, that the lock might have no face and thus no weakness—"

The room tilted.

Rennick had seen people receive terrible news before. He'd delivered some of it himself, for pay. But he had never watched a woman learn, in a single breath, that her whole life was a lie stitched together by strangers — that her god hadn't abandoned her but had been muzzled; that her dead friend hadn't died of absence but of *resistance*; that she herself had been conscripted into the greatest imprisonment in history without her knowledge or consent, chosen precisely because she was nobody, because she had no family to ask questions, because she had asked for her name to be struck from the record—

Except she hadn't. She'd never asked anything. She'd been a soldier. Someone had chosen *for* her.

"Twenty years ago," Elodie said. Each word placed like a foot on ice. "Tomas died. And afterward, the four of us who survived — Serane, Halvard, me — we all heard the same thing. Silence. And the priests called it a test of faith. And we believed them." She laughed, and it was the worst sound Rennick had ever heard. "*We're the lock.* All those prayers I sent up. They weren't going nowhere. They were going *into the mechanism*."

Ilyen tugged Elodie's sleeve. When Elodie looked down, the child held up the white shard, and for the first time since the riverbank, she spoke a full sentence in the common tongue, her voice thin and clear as a bell in the dark:

"He's still in there. He can hear you. He's been hearing you the whole time."

* * *

Later — much later, hours past midnight, sitting on the cold archive floor among open books because none of them could bear to move — Rennick found his voice again.

"I need to say something," he said, "and you're both going to hate it."

Elodie didn't look up from the map. Ilyen was asleep against her shoulder, the shard glowing faintly under her small chin like a lantern.

"Say it anyway," Elodie said.

"Everything you just learned? The Order knows it. Has always known it. Sarel — if that's the fifth name, the one leading them now — Sarel was *there*. She spoke the sealing words." He tapped the text. "'The fifth tumbler, Sarel the Steadfast.' Your companion. Your oath-sister. She didn't lose her faith when the god went silent, Elodie. She *made* the silence. And now there's a child walking around who's apparently the living key to the whole arrangement, and the Order's inquisitors have been chasing her across two provinces."

"You think Sarel wants to kill her."

"I think Sarel wants to keep the lock shut. And I think a living vessel who can open it is either going to be locked in a cell forever or killed. And given how fast those riders move—" He spread his hands. "I think we're not hiding from the Crown anymore. I think we're hiding from the person who built the cage your whole life has been standing in."

Silence. The good kind and the bad kind tangled together.

Then Elodie stood, carefully, so as not to wake the child, and looked down at the map of her valley — her prison, her home, her life's great joke — for a long time.

"Tomas tried to stop it," she said at last. "He refused, and they unmade him for it, and then they wrote him down as a casualty of the Silence and let me grieve him wrong for twenty years." Her jaw tightened. "I want the rest of the names. Halvard. The fourth tumbler. Everyone who did this. And then I want to know how a lock gets *opened*."

Rennick grinned despite everything — despite the arrest notice with his name on it, despite the fact that he was now unquestionably an accomplice to whatever this was. Because this was the moment he'd been waiting eleven years for, the crack of the safe door, the click of the true lock turning.

"Lucky for you," he said, pulling another volume from the cabinet, "breaking locks is rather my specialty."

Above them, faint through three floors of stone, the bells of Corvassa began to ring the hour — and beneath their peal, so faint he might have imagined it, Rennick heard something else. Footsteps. Many of them. Moving with purpose through the archive above.

He caught Elodie's eye. She'd heard them too.

The search had begun.
