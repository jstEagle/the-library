## 15. Four Names, One Grave

The candle had burned down to a stub before Elodie moved.

She sat in the archive's undercroft with Rennick's stolen folio open in her lap and the white shard warm against her sternum where it hung on its cord, and for a long time she did nothing at all except breathe and read the same paragraph again, as though repetition might change what it said.

*On the third day of the Sealing, oath-bearer Tomas of Ashford turned against his fellows at the Godspine gate. He was struck down by Serane of the Vale, who wept as she did it.*

Not *killed by the enemy*. Not *fell in the war against the Unmade*. Struck down by Serane of the Vale, who wept as she did it.

Elodie read it a fourth time. A fifth. The words did not rearrange themselves into something survivable. They just sat there on the page in faded iron-gall ink, patient as gravestones, while twenty years of her life quietly came apart around her.

"Elodie." Rennick's voice, low, from the top of the stair. "You've been down there all night."

"I know."

"You haven't slept."

"No."

Footsteps on the steps, careful, the way he'd learned to move around her these last two days — like a man crossing ice that had already cracked once. He crouched beside her chair, glanced at the folio, and winced. Ilyen was asleep upstairs in the loft above the tannery where they'd made their den, curled around the shard like it was a hearthstone, and Rennick had spent the night keeping watch and pretending he wasn't afraid of both of them.

"Tomas," he said. Not a question. He'd heard enough of her stories by now to know the name's weight. *Tomas, who laughed when he fought. Tomas, who carried me three miles on his back after the ford at Marrow Creek. Tomas, who died so the rest of us could live.* She'd told those stories a hundred times, in a hundred taverns, always the same way, always ending the same way: *the god went silent mid-battle, and the silence killed him.*

"He didn't die in battle," she said. Her voice came out flat, which surprised her. She'd expected it to break. "He died here. On this mountain. At the hands of our own."

Rennick said nothing. He'd been a thief in an archive long enough to know when a person needed silence more than comfort.

She turned the page with a finger that refused to tremble. There were four names in the record, written in a formal hand, each followed by its fate:

*Tomas of Ashford — slain at the gate, in defiance of the Sealing.*
*Sarel of the Vale — First of the Order of the Quiet, Warden of the Godspine.*
*Halvard of Dunmark — whereabouts unknown.*
*Serane of the Vale — Captain of Inquisitors, servant of the Crown.*
*And beneath them, in fresher ink, added later:* *Elodie Nakamura — oath renewed at Highmoor, keeper of the valley ward.*

Her name. Her actual name, in an archive she'd never entered, in a city she'd visited twice, recorded by clerks she'd never met. All her life she'd thought of herself as the last one, the forgotten one, the one left behind when the god fell silent and the others scattered. And all her life she had been exactly what they intended her to be. A lock, turning faithfully in its door.

"They knew," she said. "Halvard and Sarel and Serane. They sealed Veyra away, they wrote it down, they built an Order around keeping the secret — and then they let me swear my oath believing the god had abandoned us. Believing Tomas died because the god went quiet mid-battle and couldn't hold the wards." She looked up at Rennick, and her eyes were dry, which somehow made it worse. "They let me grieve wrong for twenty years."

"Maybe they thought—"

"Don't." The word cracked out of her like a whip. Rennick flinched. She made herself unclench her jaw. "I'm sorry. Don't defend them. Not yet. Let me be angry first."

He nodded slowly. "How long do you need?"

"I'll tell you when I'm done."

* * *

The trouble with anger, Elodie had learned over forty years of soldiering, was that it wanted somewhere to go. It was a blade with no scabbard. You could hold it, but you'd bleed.

She walked the pre-dawn streets of Corvassa with her hood up and her hand nowhere near her sword, because the one thing she could not afford in the capital of the Order of the Quiet was to look like what she was. Bell towers stood black against a bruised sky. Bakers were lighting their ovens. Somewhere a drunk sang to himself in an alley, badly, about a girl from the coast. The world went on being ordinary, which felt like an insult.

Twenty years ago, on the night after Tomas burned, Elodie had knelt in the mud outside a field hospital and sworn her renewal of the oath with blood still drying on her hands. She remembered every word. She remembered thinking: *this is what holds me together now. This is what Tomas's death means. I will carry his share.* The priest had pressed her palm to the oath-mark and the mark had taken — a pale scar in the shape of a mountain range across the back of her hand — and she had believed, with her whole stupid grieving heart, that she was binding herself to a silent god's unfinished work.

She hadn't been binding herself to anything. She'd been volunteering for a prison guard's shift.

Because that was the shape of it now, wasn't it? The valley wasn't sanctuary. The valley was the cell. Her oath-mark hadn't been a blessing. It was the keyhole. And the god she'd mourned for two decades hadn't fallen silent at all — it had been screaming behind a wall built by the five people it trusted most, and she'd spent her entire adult life standing on top of that wall, sweeping it clean, proud of the view.

And Tomas. Gods. Tomas.

She stopped at the mouth of an alley and pressed her forehead against cold brick until the stone printed itself into her skin.

The story she'd been told — the story they'd *all* been told, the surviving oath-bearers had gone home and told the widows and the war-brothers the same tidy lie — was that the final battle at the Godspine broke the covenant. That Veyra's voice cut off mid-word, mid-*prayer*, and the wards failed, and the Unmade poured through the gap, and Tomas held the gate alone while the rest of them retreated with the wounded, and when they got back there was nothing left of him to bury but his boots and half his shield.

She had wept over those boots. She had carried them home to Ashford. She had watched his mother touch them the way you touch something that used to be your son.

And the truth, according to the archive, was that there had been no breach. No final battle. There had been a *sealing* — planned, deliberate, argued over in chambers — and when the moment came, Tomas had said no. Had drawn steel on his own oath-brothers and sisters rather than let them chain their god. And Serane — Serane, who had held his hair back when he drank too much after Marrow Creek, Serane, whom Elodie had last seen across a chapel fire two nights ago with murder in her face — Serane had cut him down, weeping, and then everyone had agreed on the story that would make a traitor into a martyr, because martyrs kept the other three faithful and dead men kept no secrets.

*Struck down by Serane of the Vale, who wept as she did it.*

Even the clerk who wrote it had known. Even the ink was ashamed.

* * *

She found Rennick and Ilyen at the tannery loft as the sun came up gray through the smoke-haze. Ilyen was awake, sitting cross-legged on the floor, watching the shard hum against the floorboards with an expression Elodie was coming to recognize: not fear, not wonder, but the terrible patience of someone listening to a voice only she could hear. When Elodie came up the ladder, the child looked at her — really looked, that old-soul stare that no six-year-old face should carry — and reached out and touched the back of Elodie's hand, right on the oath-mark.

The mark flared. Warmth, then ache, deep as bone.

"What did it say?" Elodie asked quietly. She'd stopped pretending she didn't believe the child communed with the thing in the stone.

Ilyen considered. Words came hard to her still, one at a time, dragged up from wherever her language lived. "It says... you are loud. Inside. It says your loud is... hurting you."

"It would know," Elodie said. "It watched me build the cage it lives in."

"That's not—" Rennick started, and she held up a hand.

"Yes. It is. That's exactly what I did. Twenty years of keeping the valley, keeping the faith, keeping *quiet* — and every prayer I ever said bounced off the inside of a prison door." She crouched in front of Ilyen. The child's eyes were dark and calm and older than kingdoms, and Elodie hated how much comfort she took from them. "I'm not angry at you, little one. Or at it. I'm angry at the people who decided for everyone. Who decided for *me*, before I was old enough to have a word in it."

Ilyen tilted her head. Then, slowly, she lifted her small hand and pointed at Elodie's chest. "Here," she said. "Also loud."

"Aye. Also loud."

"Halvard?" Rennick asked. He'd spread the folio's pages across the floorboards, weighting the corners with boot-polish tins. "Dunmark's a week east. If he's alive—"

"He's alive. The archive lists him as *whereabouts unknown*, not deceased, and men like that don't stay unknown by dying." Elodie studied the page without seeing it. "But he can wait. Because there's a name higher up that list, and she's closer than a week, and she has a hundred knights and a chapel-burning habit."

"Serane."

"Serane." Saying the name aloud put iron in her spine, and she was grateful for it, because underneath the iron was something softer and far more dangerous. "She was the best of us, you know. Before. Better than me. I was the one who counted coins and sharpened blades and kept us fed; Serane was the one who believed. She believed so hard it embarrassed the rest of us. She'd pray at roadside shrines when nobody was watching." Elodie's laugh came out with no humor in it at all. "And then she cut down the man she loved rather than let him stop the sealing, and she's spent twenty years building an Order to make sure it was worth it. Because if it wasn't worth it — if the god didn't need caging — then she's just a murderer who cried."

"And if you bring her proof?"

"Then I take away the thing holding her upright. Which is either mercy or cruelty, and I genuinely don't know which." Elodie straightened. "Either way, she won't stop hunting Ilyen. Believers don't stop because the belief breaks. They just get worse."

Ilyen touched the shard. It pulsed once, slow, like a heartbeat deciding whether to bother.

"She's coming," the child said. Not frightened. Just reporting weather. "The one who cries. She's coming fast."

* * *

They were on the river by midmorning, and Elodie spent the whole first mile staring back at Corvassa's skyline and composing letters she would never send.

*Dear Halvard, you drunken coward, you let me sweep a prison floor for twenty years and call it devotion.*

*Dear Sarel, whoever you are, whatever throne you sit on at the Godspine — I am coming, and I am bringing everything you buried.*

*Dear Tomas. I'm sorry. I'm sorry I mourned you wrong. I'm sorry I built a life on the story they gave me instead of finding yours. Hold on. I'll dig up the truth of you if it kills every one of them.*

The barge slid past defaced shrines — Veyra's face chiseled off, Veyra's name scraped from the stones, twenty years of official forgetting — and Elodie realized, with a strange lurch, that she was no longer looking at vandalism. She was looking at evidence. Every smashed shrine was a confession. Somebody, somewhere high up, had been very afraid of what the god's memory might grow into if anyone tended it.

Rennick sat down beside her at the rail and handed her bread she didn't want and ate his own in silence for a while.

"For what it's worth," he said eventually, "grief that turns into anger is the kind that gets things done. I've seen the other kind."

"I know the other kind too."

"Yeah." He picked at the crust. "So what's the plan? Genuinely asking. Because as far as I can map it, we've got a child carrying a god's skeleton key, an inquisitor captain who loves you and wants to kill us, a warden on a mountain who's willing to murder a six-year-old for the greater good, and a drunk in Dunmark who might know where any of them keep their spare courage. Pick one."

Elodie watched the water. Under the gray surface it moved the way it always moved — patient, certain, going where the fall of the land told it to go.

"Halvard," she said. "Sarel and Serane I have to face eventually, and I will, with everything. But Halvard ran. Men who run leave loose ends. Guilt. Letters they couldn't burn. And besides—" her grip tightened on the rail until the wood complained, "—he owes me a funeral. Tomas's funeral, the real one. He's going to stand on Tomas's grave and tell me the truth, or I'm going to plant him next to his brother-in-arms and let the ground sort out the difference."

Behind them, Ilyen laughed — a small, startled sound, the first Elodie had heard from her — at something only the shard had said.

Ahead, past the last bridges of Corvassa, the river bent north toward Dunmark, toward the grave, toward everything she'd been lied to about. Elodie turned her back on the city and did not look at it again.

The oath-mark on her hand ached all day, steady as a second pulse, like something behind the wall had begun, at last, to knock.
