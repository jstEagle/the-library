## 17. Refusal

The safehouse was a tanner's loft above the tallow-shops of the Tanners' Reach, and it stank like every honest thing in Corvassa — of fat and smoke and the long labor of making something clean out of something dead. Elodie had chosen it for that reason. Nobody lingered in the Reach who didn't have work to do, and nobody asked questions about strangers with work to do.

Rennick slept against the door with his boots on. Ilyen slept curled in the crook of Elodie's arm, her breath slow and even, one small fist still closed around the white shard as if even sleep couldn't convince her to let go of it.

Elodie did not sleep. She lay awake in the dark and listened to the city breathe, and she thought about Serane's face when they had stood ten feet apart in the Archive district two nights past — the way Serane's hand had gone to her sword hilt and stayed there, gripping, not drawing.

*Come with me,* Elodie had almost said. She hadn't. Some doors you didn't open from your side. You waited to see whether the other person opened theirs.

She heard the knock at midnight exactly. Three soft raps, spaced like a heartbeat that was trying very hard to stay calm.

Rennick came up off the floor with a knife already in his hand. Elodie touched his shoulder — *stay* — and rose without waking Ilyen. She took her sword off the wall but left it sheathed, and went down the ladder into the reek of the tallow-works, and opened the door.

Serane stood alone in the alley. No cloak of office. No knights. Plain leathers, travel-stained, her gray braid damp with river fog. She looked smaller without the Order's colors, which was strange, because Elodie had spent twenty years remembering her as larger than life.

"You're alone," Elodie said.

"I'm alone."

"Prove it."

Serane spread her arms, turned slowly. "Search me if you want. I'd rather you didn't touch me, but I'll allow it."

That was so much like her — dry and precise and hiding everything behind procedure — that Elodie felt the old ache move through her chest like weather. She stepped aside. "Upstairs will wake them. Talk here."

"There are things I can't say here." Serane's eyes flicked up toward the loft. "Is the child—"

"She has a name."

A silence. The tallow-vats below them cooled with soft ticking sounds, like a house settling.

"Ilyen," Serane said quietly. "Yes. I know. It was in the first report out of Highmoor. Maren gave it up under questioning." Something moved across her face — guilt, quickly filed away. "She's alive. That's more than most get from the Order's questioning."

"That's meant to comfort me?"

"It's meant to be true. Truth is all I've got tonight." Serane looked at her hands. "I bought an hour from my own patrol captain. An hour, Elodie. After that, the Order knows where this loft is, because I will have to report what I found or someone else finds it for me. So listen fast."

"I've been listening to the Order for weeks. It says the same thing every time. Give us the vessel and the valley lives."

"And I told you before — I wasn't lying when I said that offer stands. It still stands." Serane lifted her head. "Surrender the child. Walk away. You, Rennick, the hedgewitch's boy, all of you walk free. The Order seals the valley back up, tightens the ward, and Highmoor goes on being the quiet place it's always been. Everyone you grew up with keeps breathing."

"And Ilyen?"

"The child goes to the Godspine. Lives out its days in the Order's keeping. Fed, sheltered, educated. It's not a death sentence, Elodie. It's a—" She faltered, and for one unguarded moment Elodie saw the girl she'd known on the training fields at Veyra's shrine, the one who could never tell a lie cleanly. "It's a cage. A kind one. But a cage."

"A cage." Elodie leaned against the doorframe. Below them, somewhere in the Reach, a dog started up and quit again. "You know what I learned in the Archive? Ask me what I learned, Serane. Go on. Ask me what the first covenant actually was."

"I don't want—"

"Veyra didn't fall silent. Five people locked it in a box and let the world believe it died of grief. And one of those five—" Her voice caught; she pushed through it. "One of them was Tomas. He died trying to *stop* them. And then they buried him under a hero's stone and let me spend twenty years grieving for a lie."

Serane flinched. "Elodie—"

"So don't stand in a stinking alley and offer me a kinder cage. There's no version of this where I put that child in a cell so the rest of us can keep pretending our god chose silence over us." She stepped closer. "There's a version where you come with me instead."

Something raw crossed Serane's face. For a moment she looked young again — the oath-sworn novice, laughing, mud-splattered, certain the world was simple.

"You don't understand what's coming if the covenant fails," she said. "The wild magic bleeding through. The wards failing. Winter coming early to half the realm. Sarel has read the old war-scrolls. She's seen what happens when Veyra walks unbound — whole provinces drowned in living magic, cities that dreamed themselves to death. Millions, Elodie. Sarel sealed the god to save millions, and she has carried that weight for thirty years without once setting it down."

"I know."

"You *know*?" Serane laughed, and it was an ugly sound. "How could you possibly—"

"Because I met her ghost in every defaced shrine between here and the mountains. Because Halvard drinks himself blind over it every night. Because Tomas died for it. Because I've been carrying my piece of the same weight since I was nineteen years old, and mine just turned out to be built on a lie." She held Serane's gaze. "The difference between you and me isn't conviction. It's that I stopped believing the lie was holy."

"The lie kept the peace."

"The lie kept a prison. And the prison is failing anyway, Serane. Your Order can't hold it forever. The child exists *because* it's failing. The covenant is dying no matter what you do — the only question left is whether it dies with a little girl inside it."

Serane said nothing. The fog thickened in the alley mouth. Somewhere a watch-bell counted the third hour, muffled and far.

"I loved you," Serane said finally, very quietly. "Before the silence. After. Both. It made every order harder and I obeyed them anyway, and I have hated myself for twenty years in ways I don't expect you to forgive." Her hand drifted toward her sword, stopped. "So here is my last truth, freely given, oath-breaker's privilege: when I leave this alley, I report what I found. Not because I want to. Because if I don't, someone less tired than me finds this place, and they won't knock first. You have until dawn. Maybe less. Take the river — the east gate watches are Order-sworn, but the barges out of Tanners' Stairs aren't. Bribe the master of the *Pale Ferret*, ugly man, bad teeth, takes silver. Get downstream past the Weir before the Order can mount a proper pursuit."

"Why?" Elodie's voice cracked on it. "Why help us and hunt us in the same breath?"

"Because both are the same act." Serane's eyes were wet and furious. "Because I can't strike you, Elodie. Twenty years, and I still can't strike you. So I'll do the next best thing. I'll give you a head start, and I'll hate myself for it tomorrow, and I'll keep carrying the weight, because somebody has to." She stepped back, into the fog. "You always were the better of us. That was the problem."

Then she was gone, and the alley was just an alley, full of tallow-smell and river damp, and Elodie stood in the doorway with her heart hammering like a fist on a locked door.

* * *

She woke Rennick by dropping his pack on his head.

"We're leaving. Now."

He was upright before he finished swearing. "Order?"

"Order. Dawn, maybe sooner. River."

"Tanners' Stairs." He was already stuffing the shard-cloths, the maps, the archive-notes into their rolls. "You talked to her."

"Yes."

"And?"

"And she gave us the way out and kept her conscience intact. Same as she's done her whole life." Elodie lifted Ilyen gently; the child stirred, blinked up at her, and — miracle of miracles — didn't cry, just wrapped her arms around Elodie's neck and held on. "Get the boy. Move."

They went down through the sleeping Reach single file, Rennick leading, the hedgewitch's messenger-boy Yorren stumbling along behind with his eyes huge. Corvassa at night was a different animal — bell-towers dark, watch-fires low, the great bells silent as the god they'd been named for. Elodie kept to the tanner's alleys, counting turns, and behind her she felt the city begin, very faintly, to wake.

The *Pale Ferret* was moored at the bottom of Tanners' Stairs, squat and tar-black, its master a barrel of a man with teeth like a broken fence. Rennick haggled while Elodie watched the stairs. Silver changed hands. The master spat over the rail.

"Downstream to Marlow's Weir, no stops. You're cargo, not passengers. Cargo stays quiet, cargo stays below, cargo doesn't ask why we don't fly the ferry-lantern." He grinned with the broken fence. "Cargo pays double if anyone official comes alongside."

"Done," Rennick said.

They boarded in the gray before gray. The barge slid out into the Marle with the current taking it gentle as a hand under the spine, and Corvassa fell away behind them — the Bells, the Archive towers, the smoke of a hundred thousand hearths — and Elodie stood at the stern with Ilyen on her hip and watched the capital shrink to a smudge of light.

Below decks, in the reek of tar and turnips, Rennick spread the maps and tried to plan and mostly failed, because his hands shook. Yorren slept. Ilyen sat beside Elodie and traced the faded lines of the oath-mark on her forearm with one finger, over and over, the way she did when she was working up to words.

"He came," the child said at last. "The sad one with the gray hair. She stood under the window. I heard."

Elodie went very still. "What did you hear?"

Ilyen considered the question with the terrible thoroughness of children. "Her heart was loud. Hearts are loud when they're fighting themselves." She looked up. "She didn't want to hit you. Why doesn't she want to hit you?"

Because love is a debt some people pay their whole lives without ever collecting, Elodie thought. Aloud she said, "We swore oaths together once. Long ago. To the same god."

"To the god in the mountain?"

"The same."

Ilyen nodded slowly, accepting this, and then said the thing that undid Elodie completely: "She should come say sorry to it. It's been alone a long time." Then she laid her head against Elodie's side and was asleep within four breaths, the shard glowing faintly against her palm like a coal banking down for the night.

Rennick looked over. "Well," he said. "That settles that, then. Even the god's prisoners feel sorry for it."

Elodie didn't answer. She sat with the child warm against her and watched the black water slide past, and she thought about Serane standing in the fog with wet eyes and a dry voice, choosing duty one more time, always one more time, the way a woman might keep paying rent on a burning house.

*I can't strike you.*

No, Elodie thought. But someday soon, someone would make her choose — really choose — and Elodie prayed to the imprisoned god she no longer served that when that day came, it wouldn't be Elodie's blade waiting on the other side of it.

The barge ran on through the dark. Ahead, far downstream, lightning flickered once, twice, over the low hills beyond Marlow's Weir — though there was no cloud in the sky, and the air smelled of nothing but river and rain that hadn't fallen yet.

Wild magic, Rennick had called it, reading the signs off the archive notes. The covenant dying. Time running out.

Elodie pulled her cloak tighter around the sleeping child and stared into that distant, impossible light, and did not look away.
