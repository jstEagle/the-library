## 18. The Child Speaks

The river has a sound the valley never had.

Ilyen lies on her back in the barge's hold and listens to it: a long low breathing beneath the boards, patient as something that has been walking since before there were feet. In Highmoor, water only ever fell or pooled or froze. Here it *goes* somewhere. Everything here goes somewhere.

She likes that. She does not know why she knows to like it, but she does, and lately there are many things she does not know why she knows. The words for them come first, then the knowing, like rain arriving after the smell of it.

Above her, Elodie is arguing with the bargeman about the price of passage past the mill-weir. Rennick is pretending to sleep with one eye open — Ilyen can see the shine of it through the gap in his hat brim, because he always leaves exactly that much of a gap, as if watching were a chore he could do halfway. The lantern swings on its hook. The shadows swing with it.

*Ilyen.*

The word arrives again, the way it has been arriving since the third morning on the water. Not spoken. Remembered. It comes up out of her the way stones rise in a field after frost: slowly, inevitably, pushed by something underneath.

That is her name. She is certain of it now. Three days ago she was not certain of anything except that Elodie's hand on her hair made the fear go small, and that Rennick stole apples badly and shared them anyway, and that the white shard slept against her chest like a bird with its head under its wing.

Now she knows her name, and knowing her name is like pulling the first thread of a knot. Everything else is starting to loosen.

She sits up. The barge rocks. Cargo shifts somewhere behind her — bolts of dye-cloth, the bargeman said, though he would not meet her eyes when he said it, and Elodie's mouth had gone flat.

*Ilyen,* says the remembering again. *Little winter-will. Little last-made.*

She puts her hand over the shard through her shirt. Warm. Always warm now, where on the road it used to be cool as a river stone until Elodie touched it.

"Hey." Rennick, without moving anything but his mouth. "Bad dream?"

"No dream," she says. Her common speech is still strange in her own ears, each word set down carefully like a cup that might spill. "Remembering."

His eye opens all the way. On the barge deck above, Elodie's voice stops mid-sentence, and boots thump toward the hatch, and Ilyen understands — with another knowing arriving from nowhere, or from everywhere — that they have both been waiting for this the way farmers wait for a storm they can hear but cannot see.

Elodie drops down into the hold, lantern light catching the grey in her cropped hair. She crouches in front of Ilyen, forearms on her knees, the way she does when she wants a person to know they are being looked at properly. There is a new scar on her forearm from the slavers' knives in Corvassa. Ilyen has been counting her scars. Eleven. She does not know why counting matters either, but it does, the way the number five keeps mattering, the way the number four keeps mattering, the way some part of her grieves for a man named Tomas whose face she has never seen.

"What are you remembering?" Elodie asks. Gentle. Too gentle, the way people are gentle with things they think might break.

"Ilyen," Ilyen says. "Me. That is my name."

Something moves through Elodie's face — relief and sorrow tangled so tightly together that they look, for a moment, like the same expression.

"You didn't know your own name?"

"I knew it the way you know a house you lived in before you could walk." She frowns, hearing herself. The words are too old for her mouth. They keep coming out that way, older every day, and she has stopped trying to make them smaller. "You know its shape. Then you go back and stand inside it."

Rennick has given up pretending. He sits cross-legged against the dye-cloth, hat off, and he looks younger without the hat, and more afraid.

"And what's inside it?" he asks. "This house?"

Ilyen opens her mouth to say *I don't know*, which is what she has said every time, and what she means to say now.

Instead the river speaks.

Not the bargeman's river — the other one, the deep one, the one that has been running under everything since the night the god went quiet. It rises in her all at once and she cannot stop it any more than she could stop growing, and the hold fills with a voice that is hers and also much, much older:

*In the last hour of the war, when the five had already turned their faces away and the chains were half-forged, Veyra gathered what remained unchained — one breath, one will, one seed of mercy — and planted it in mortal clay. Not to fight. Not to escape. To remember. For a covenant is only a promise, and a promise must be kept by someone, and if no one kept it, the god would not merely be imprisoned. The god would be forgotten, and forgotten gods rot, and rotten gods become the thing their jailers feared.*

The voice stops. The lantern flame stands perfectly still, though the barge rocks.

Ilyen is on her hands and knees on the boards. She does not remember falling. Her nose is bleeding; she wipes it with her sleeve and finds the blood bright and ordinary and is absurdly glad of it.

Elodie has her by the shoulders. "Ilyen. Look at me. Are you—"

"I'm the keeping," Ilyen says.

The words come out calm, and that frightens her most of all, because nothing about them should be calm.

"I'm the part that remembers. When they sealed Veyra — the five, my lady, your five, the ones who swore beside you — the god could not stop them. So instead of stopping them, the god made me. A vessel. Grown from the last of its will, the way a tree grows one last seed before winter takes it. I am what Veyra did with its final hour." She hears herself say it and watches Elodie's face change and understands that these are terrible words, words that should break something open in the room, and yet inside her they settle gently, like a child coming home. "The covenant isn't failing because it was broken. The covenant is failing because it was finished. It was only ever supposed to last until I grew enough to carry it."

"To carry it where?" Rennick asks hoarsely.

"To the Godspine." She touches the shard. Beneath her palm it hums, and the hum has words in it now, syllables she suddenly knows the way she knows her name. "To the prison. Because a seal made by oath-bearers can only be remade by an oath-bearer — or opened by the covenant itself. And I am the covenant. All of it. Every vow anyone ever swore to Veyra, folded down small enough to walk around in."

She holds out her arms a little, presenting herself. *Here. This is what I am.* The gesture feels right and ridiculous at once.

Elodie has gone very still. Ilyen has learned to read her stillnesses. There is the stillness of the hunt, the stillness of anger, the stillness of grief. This one is none of them. This one is the stillness of a woman doing arithmetic she hates.

"The Order wants you," Elodie says slowly, "because you're not just the key to the prison. You're the lock. If they take you—"

"If they take me, they can keep Veyra sealed forever. Or—" the knowledge turns over, showing its other face, cold-bellied like a fish, "—they can use me to end it. Unmake the covenant entirely. A dead covenant doesn't need a vessel. A dead covenant doesn't need *me*."

The lantern swings again. The river breathes below them. Rennick says, very quietly, "Kid," and then doesn't finish, which is unlike him, because Rennick always finishes, usually with three unnecessary sentences.

Ilyen looks at him. She looks at Elodie. And here is the thing she discovers in that moment, the newest knowing of all, arriving not from the deep river but from somewhere shallow and warm and entirely her own: she is not afraid for herself.

She is afraid for them. Because whatever she is — vessel, key, seed, last-made — she has only been alive for as long as she can remember walking up a muddy road to a cottage door, and in all that time the only things she has ever truly possessed are a humming stone and two people who chose to run with her. The god's purpose lives in her like a tenant. But Elodie's rough hand smoothing her hair, and Rennick's badly stolen apples split four ways among three people — those live in her like blood.

"They will try to kill me," she tells them, matter-of-fact, because they deserve plainness. "Or take me. Probably at the weir, because the weir is the last place the barge must slow, and Serane thinks in places where things must slow. I don't remember her face yet. I will soon. I remember more every day. That is what I am *for*."

Elodie flinches at the name. Ilyen catalogues the flinch and adds it to the growing shelf of things she knows without knowing how: Serane, Halvard, Sarel, Tomas. Four names and a grave. Five oaths, one broken.

"You shouldn't know that," Elodie says. "Any of that. You're six years old, or near enough. You should know frogs and bread crusts and how to whistle."

"I should," Ilyen agrees solemnly, and then, because she has watched Rennick do it a hundred times and wanted to: "Teach me whistling after the weir."

For a heartbeat — one full swing of the lantern — Elodie laughs. It is a cracked, wet, real laugh, and Ilyen treasures it, files it away with the apples and the hand in her hair, evidence for a case she is quietly building: *I am not only the covenant. I am also a girl on a barge, and someone taught me to want things.*

Then Elodie's face closes into its working shape, the mercenary shape, the one that plans.

"All right," Elodie says. "If they hit us at the weir, we're ready. Rennick — how many ways off a barge?"

"Three good ones and one embarrassing one."

"Show me the embarrassing one later. Might need it." She turns back to Ilyen, and her voice gentles again, but differently now — less like handling glass, more like speaking across a table to someone who has earned a seat. "What else do you remember? About the Godspine. About what happens when we get there. I need to know what I'm carrying you into."

And Ilyen opens her mouth to answer —

—and the deep river rises again, faster this time, eager, and she understands dimly that it is not only remembering *through* her but *to* her, that the imprisoned god has been whispering up through the shard for twenty years into a valley where nothing magical could answer, and now that the vessel has left the ward, the whispers have become a flood—

*Child. Little winter-will. Hear the last of it:*

*There is a price at the mountain that the five never wrote down, because writing it down would have stopped even them. The seal can be renewed — but only by a vessel who stays. Forever. Or it can be opened — but only by breaking every oath ever sworn to me, including hers, including yours, and broken oaths of that size tear the world along the seam.*

*Or—*

The voice frays. The shard burns hot against her chest, painfully hot, and Ilyen gasps, and the barge lurches sideways under her as the bargeman shouts something from the stern — and through the hatch she sees it ahead, black against the last light:

The weir. Water pouring white over the sill of it, mist standing up like ghosts. And on the far bank, tiny in the dusk, torchlight. Many torches. Arranged not like a camp.

Arranged like an ambush that has been waiting for exactly this stretch of slow water.

"They're early," Ilyen says, wiping the last of the blood from her nose, holding out her hand to Elodie in the dark. "Hurry. Before the barge slows."
