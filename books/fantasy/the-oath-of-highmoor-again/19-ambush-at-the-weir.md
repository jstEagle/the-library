## 19. Ambush at the Weir

Rennick had stolen from the Archive of Covenants eleven times before anyone caught him, and he'd learned one thing about danger: it always announced itself wrong. The truly bad moments never looked like danger. They looked like quiet.

The river was quiet.

Too quiet for a stretch of water that fed three mill towns downstream. No barges ahead of them, none behind. No gulls working the shallows. Even the wind had gone flat, pressing the reeds down along both banks like a crowd holding its breath.

He stood at the stern of the hired barge with his arms crossed and tried to look like a man who belonged there, which was difficult, because Rennick had never once in his life belonged anywhere. He was a thief by trade and temperament — a thin, quick man with ink-stained fingers and a face people forgot two seconds after looking away from it. For twenty years that had been enough. Then a woman and a mute child had walked into his archive with a shard of white stone humming like a struck bell, and somewhere between the stacks and the river he had stopped being a thief and started being something else. He still wasn't sure what. It sat in his chest like an unpaid debt.

Elodie stood at the bow. She hadn't moved in an hour. She stood the way she did everything — economical, deliberate, like a blade sheathed on purpose. Her eyes kept going to the weirs upstream: the low stone dams the millers used to pool the water, each one a white seam of falling foam across the brown current. There were four of them between here and the open country north of Corvassa. The barge would have to lock through each one, slow as a funeral.

That was the problem. Rennick could see her seeing it.

"You're thinking what I'm thinking," he said, coming up beside her.

"I doubt it."

"A weir's a choke point. You can't run water through fast, can't turn a barge around in a lock. If I were setting an ambush—"

"You're not setting an ambush. You're a librarian."

"Archivist," Rennick said. "And I've been shot at twice, which is more than most archivists can say."

Behind them, under the barge's canvas awning, Ilyen sat cross-legged with the white shard cupped in both hands. The child hadn't spoken since they'd fled Corvassa — not even *Hurry*, the word that had cracked out of her at the valley boundary like the first stone of an avalanche. But she didn't need to speak. The shard hummed louder every hour. Rennick could feel it through the deck boards now, a fine tremor in his boot soles, like standing on the skin of a drum someone was about to beat.

Ilyen's eyes were closed. Her lips moved sometimes, shaping syllables in that old language older than any kingdom, and when they moved, the water around the barge did small impossible things. Little spirals in the wake. Fish rising in perfect rings. Once, for three full seconds, the river had flowed backward, gently, as if considering it.

Rennick had spent his life around books that described such things. Books were safer. Books stayed on their shelves.

"The locks are the risk," Elodie said quietly. "If they wanted us, they'd take us at a weir. Walls on both sides. One way through."

"So we go around? Overland?"

"With a wounded child and no horses? Through country where every shrine we pass has Veyra's face chiseled off?" Elodie shook her head once. "We go through. We just go through awake."

Rennick looked back at Ilyen. "She's not wounded anymore. Look at her."

It was true, and it unsettled him more than the wound had. Three days ago the girl had been feverish, hollow-eyed, worn down to a wick. Now she glowed — not visibly, nothing so honest as a glow, but there was a fullness to her, a density, like she weighed more than a child should. Like she was filling up with something that had nowhere else to go.

"She's getting stronger," Rennick said. "Which means whatever's hunting her is running out of time."

Elodie said nothing. But her hand drifted to her forearm, where beneath the sleeve the oath-mark lay — the brand every sworn mercenary of Veyra carried, silent for twenty years, dead as a cold coal. Rennick had seen it once, by firelight. A spiral broken in the middle, like a snake interrupted mid-sentence.

* * *

The second weir came into view an hour past noon.

It was bigger than the first — a long stone lip across the whole river, water sliding over it in a sheet of hammered silver, and beside it the lock: a stone channel with wooden gates at either end, big enough for one barge at a time. A lockkeeper's hut squatted on the near bank. Smoke rose from its chimney. A dog lay on the path.

All of it ordinary. All of it wrong.

"No birds," Rennick murmured. "Same as before. And that dog—" He squinted. "Dogs bark at barges. Every dog on this river barks at barges. That one hasn't lifted its head."

Elodie was already moving. She crouched by Ilyen and spoke low and level, the voice Rennick had heard her use only a handful of times — the voice that wasn't soft so much as certain. "Ilyen. Listen to me. When the barge stops in the lock, you get down behind the cargo. You don't come up until I say my own name. Do you understand?"

Ilyen opened her eyes. They were gray, the gray of river fog, and far too old for the face around them. She looked at Elodie for a long moment. Then she reached out and touched the woman's wrist, right over the oath-mark, and held it there.

The mark answered.

Rennick saw it happen and would deny afterward that his knees went loose. Under Elodie's sleeve, light bloomed — a slow red-gold pulse, like coals breathed upon after twenty years of ash. The broken spiral filled in. For one heartbeat the whole mark burned visible through the cloth, complete, whole, *awake*, and the white shard in Ilyen's hands sang one clear note across the water.

Then it faded. Elodie stared at her own arm as if it belonged to a stranger.

"It's never done that," she said.

"Ilyen," Rennick started, "what did you—"

The lock gates began to open.

* * *

They came out of the lockkeeper's hut and the treeline at the same moment, which told Rennick everything: the hut had been taken hours ago, the keeper dead or bound inside, the smoke left burning as bait. Six knights in the livery of the Order of the Quiet — dove-gray cloaks, blank silver masks where faces should be — and behind them, walking the lock wall with the unhurried stride of a woman arriving to collect a debt, came Serane.

Rennick knew her by reputation alone: the Crown's chief inquisitor, the Quiet Hand, the woman who had burned the chapel at Highmoor. What he knew now — what Elodie had told him on the second night of the river, in a voice scraped nearly flat — was the rest of it. That Serane had once knelt beside her at the same altar. That they had sworn the same oath to the same silent god. That Serane had loved her, or something adjacent to love, and had spent twenty years turning that love into doctrine.

"Elodie Nakamura." Serane's voice carried easily over the water, trained for courtrooms and pyres. "By authority of the Order of the Quiet and the Crown it serves, surrender the vessel. You will be treated fairly. The child will be treated humanely. This is the last time I offer."

Elodie stepped forward to the rail. "Serane." Just the name. Rennick watched her say it and understood that some words are heavier than others.

"You know I'll take her if I must."

"You know I won't let you."

Something crossed Serane's masked face — masks hide everything except what the wearer wants hidden, and Serane wanted this hidden badly. "You were the best of us," she said. "That's why this hurts. Stand aside, and live."

"Last time someone asked me to stand aside," Elodie said, "Tomas died."

The knights drew steel in unison, six soft slides of metal, and the first crossbow bolt came off the treeline.

Rennick grabbed Ilyen and folded them both down behind the cargo as the bolt buried itself in the mast above them with a sound like a knuckle cracking. The second and third followed. The barge pilot — a fat, sensible man who had been paid triple to ask no questions — took one look and went over the side, swimming hard for the far bank, and Rennick couldn't blame him. Found family forged in blood was a pretty phrase. Blood was also the price of admission, and the pilot hadn't paid anything yet.

"Stay down," he told Ilyen. "Whatever happens, stay—"

She wasn't listening. She was watching Elodie with those fog-gray eyes, and her hands were pressed flat against the deck, and the white shard beside her was blazing now, throwing shadows the wrong direction.

On the bank, the knights were wading into the shallows toward the barge. Elodie met the first one at the rail.

Rennick had seen her fight before — in Corvassa, briefly, brutally, all economy and angles. This was different. This was a woman who had spent twenty years fighting with one hand tied behind her soul, and the knot had just come undone.

The knight's sword came up. Elodie caught his wrist, turned, and *spoke* — one word in the old language, the liturgy-words Maren had half-recognized, the pre-kingdom tongue of covenant and binding — and the oath-mark on her arm ignited like a sunrise.

The light hit the knight and he simply stopped. Not dead. Stopped — frozen mid-stride, sword raised, a statue of a man wearing a look of absolute astonishment. The second knight swung at her and she caught *that* blade bare-handed, and where her palm touched the steel the metal glowed cherry-red and the knight dropped it howling. The third got close enough to strike, and Elodie's counterblow — plain, mortal, mercenary-simple — put him in the water.

But there were six, and the crossbows were reloading, and Rennick saw the fourth and fifth knights break wide, splashing around the barge's flank toward the stern. Toward the canvas. Toward the child.

"No," Rennick said. It came out very small. Then again, louder, and he was already moving, scrambling over the cargo, putting himself between the gunwale and Ilyen with a thief's speed and absolutely nothing else — no armor, no weapon but a stolen archive-knife and twenty years of wanting to matter.

The first knight came over the gunwale like a gray spider.

Rennick did the arithmetic in half a second: too heavy, too fast, too armed. So he didn't fight him. He grabbed Ilyen and threw them both sideways off the cargo pile as the sword came down, and the blade meant for the child's spine opened Rennick instead — a long tearing line across his back and shoulder, fire and ice at once, and he heard himself make a sound he'd never made before, high and involuntary, and then the deck was coming up and the world tilted and there was blood everywhere, his blood, an absurd quantity of it, spreading across the boards in a slick black-red fan.

*I'm going to die on a barge,* he thought, with strange clarity. *A river. I've never even liked rivers.*

The knight raised the sword again.

And the world broke open.

* * *

Later — much later, when he could bear to remember it — Rennick would try to describe what Elodie became, and fail every time.

The oath-mark didn't glow this time. It *detonated*. Light poured off her arm in a flood, and with it came her voice, and her voice was not one voice but five — five women's voices braided into one, the voices of the five who had sworn at the altar twenty years ago, the living and the dead and the fallen, all speaking through her in the old tongue. The air itself went taut as a bowstring. The river flattened. Every bird within a mile rose screaming into the sky.

The knight above Rennick froze exactly where he stood, sword raised, and then the light took him too and he crumpled like wet paper, unconscious before he hit the deck.

The remaining knights on the bank staggered. Two fell outright. The crossbowmen dropped their weapons and clutched their heads. And Serane —

Serane stood her ground. Masked, unmoving, boots planted on the lock wall while the covenant-light washed over her like a wave over a seawall. And Rennick, bleeding out on the deck of a barge, saw her raise one hand — not to attack, not to shield, but almost as if in greeting, or grief — and heard her say, barely audible across the water:

"*Veyra.*"

Just the name. Just the name of a god everyone believed dead, spoken by the woman who had helped bury it, in a voice that shook.

Then Elodie faltered.

Rennick saw it happen — the light guttering, the five voices fraying apart, Elodie dropping to one knee on the blood-slick deck with her arm smoking and her breath coming in ragged tears. Twenty years of silence, and she had just poured all of it out in ten seconds. Whatever well she'd drawn from, it was nearly empty, and there were still knights on the bank, and Serane was already signaling the retreat — no, not retreat, *regroup*, falling back to the treeline with her surviving men, buying time, because Serane was patient, Serane always—

"Elodie." Rennick's voice came out as a whisper. He tried again. "Elodie. The girl."

Ilyen was kneeling beside him. He didn't remember her crossing the deck. Her small hands were pressed against his wound, and they were *hot*, unbearably hot, and the white shard lay against his chest where she'd placed it, singing a note that lived in his bones rather than his ears. Warmth spread through him from the wound outward — not healing, not quite, but *holding*, stitching the worst of the tear closed with threads of light he could feel but not see. The blood slowed. The gray at the edge of his vision receded, grudgingly, like a tide deciding to wait.

"Stupid," Elodie rasped, crawling to him. "Stupid, brave, idiot thief."

"Archivist," Rennick managed.

"You jumped in front of a sword."

"You jumped in front of six." He coughed. "We're a matched set."

Elodie laughed — a broken, wet, disbelieving sound — and then she pulled him upright against the cargo and held him there, one arm around his shoulders, careful of the wound, and Ilyen leaned into his other side, small and warm and humming, and the three of them sat on a drifting barge in the middle of a river while the Order of the Quiet melted back into the trees.

"We can't stay on the water," Elodie said at last. Her voice was steadier now, but Rennick heard the cost underneath it. "She'll regroup. There's a third weir ahead, and a fourth. She knows this river better than we do."

"Overland, then." Rennick winced as he shifted. "North. There's a road — old post road, runs through hill country. Slow, but no choke points."

"And a healer," Elodie said. "You need a healer."

"I need a drink," Rennick said. "The healer can come after."

Ilyen stirred against his side. She looked up at Elodie, then at Rennick, and her mouth worked for a moment — the old language flickering across her lips, then fading, then, with visible effort, forcing itself into the shapes of common speech.

"Halvard," she said.

Elodie went rigid. "What did you say?"

The child's gray eyes were distant, fixed on something far north of the river, beyond the hills, beyond the map. "Halvard," she said again, clearer now, the word strange and heavy in her young voice. "Find Halvard. Before they do."

Rennick looked at Elodie. Elodie looked at Rennick. Somewhere in the treeline, a crow called once and fell silent.

"Halvard," Elodie repeated softly, and her hand went to the oath-mark — dimmed now, ash-gray again, spent — "was the second of the five. My oath-brother. Dead these fifteen years."

Ilyen shook her head slowly, and the shard in her lap pulsed once, gentle as a sleeping heartbeat.

"Not dead," she said. "Sleeping. And the ones who sealed the god are waking too."

* * *

They buried the pilot's abandoned hat at dusk, mostly as an excuse to stop moving, and Rennick slept propped against the cargo with Ilyen's warmth on one side and Elodie's watchfulness on the other, and dreamed of shelves. In the dream, every book was open to the same page, and the page read: *found family is forged in blood, and blood is the only ink that doesn't fade.*

He woke at dawn with the wound stiff but closed, the shard quiet, and Elodie already standing at the bow, facing north.

"Road's a mile east," she said. "We walk from here."

"What's north?"

Elodie was quiet a moment. "An innkeeper who's supposed to be dead," she said. "And answers I've been afraid of for twenty years."

She shouldered her pack and offered Rennick her hand, and he took it, and together — thief, soldier, and child — they climbed the muddy bank toward the old post road, leaving the river and its weirs and its ambushes behind.

Behind them, far to the south, the bells of Corvassa began to ring — though no one had ordered them rung, and no one ever learned why.
