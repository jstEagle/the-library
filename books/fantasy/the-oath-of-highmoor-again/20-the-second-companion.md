## 20. The Second Companion

The inn was called the Drowned Bell, which struck Elodie as either very honest or very bitter, and after three days on the river road she had learned to trust bitterness when she found it.

It sat at the crook of a town called Marrowford, where the road bent around a hill as if the hill had refused to move and everyone else had simply given up arguing. The sign hanging over the door had once been a bell, cast in green bronze, but someone had sunk it in a barrel of tar up to its crown so that only the top showed, dull and drowned-looking. Ilyen stared at it the whole length of the street.

"It's just a sign," Rennick said. He walked with a new carefulness since the weir, his left arm bound tight against his ribs, and he'd stopped pretending he didn't hurt sometime around dawn. "Inns have names. Some of them are stupid."

Ilyen didn't answer. She hadn't spoken much since the water. But her hand found Elodie's without looking for it, small fingers closing around two of hers, and that was answer enough about what she thought of bells that had been made to drown.

* * *

The common room smelled of spilled ale and old smoke, and it was nearly empty at midday, which suited Elodie fine. A fat man dozed by the fire. Two carters argued over dice in low voices. Behind the bar stood a mountain of a man gone soft at the edges — the kind of big that had once been muscle and had spent fifteen years marinating in its own kitchen. His beard was gray-streaked and unwashed. His eyes, when they lifted from the tankard he was polishing with a rag that had never once been clean, were bloodshot and deeply, profoundly tired.

Elodie knew those eyes.

She had sworn an oath beside them twenty years ago, kneeling in the mud at Veyra's shrine with four other fools while the rain came down like the sky was trying to drown the world before it could end it.

Halvard's hand stopped moving. The tankard wobbled. He set it down on the bar very slowly, the way a man sets down something he is afraid might go off.

"No," he said.

"Hello to you too," Elodie said.

"No." Louder now. The dozing man by the fire snorted and slept on; the carters glanced over and decided it wasn't their business. Halvard leaned across the bar, and this close she could smell the ale under the kitchen grease, years deep. "No. Whatever you're here for. Whatever you think I know. No. Get out."

"You look well," Rennick said mildly, from the door, where he had positioned himself with his good arm against the frame in the manner of a man who intended to be difficult to remove.

"I said get out—"

"Halvard." Elodie kept her voice low. She put her hand flat on the bar, between them, palm down. "Show me your wrist."

He went still in a way that confirmed everything before she said another word. Twenty years ago that wrist had borne the same mark hers did: five lines interlaced, the knot of the covenant, inked into the skin by Veyra's own light on the worst night of all their lives. She watched him decide whether to show her, watch the drunkard and the oath-bearer fight behind his face like two dogs in a sack.

Then he shoved his sleeve up and slapped his forearm on the bar.

The mark was scarred over. Burned, deliberately, with something hot and flat — she could see the shape of an iron in the crosshatch of old tissue. He had done it himself, or had it done, and the edges were ugly in the way wounds are ugly when no one who loves you is tending them.

"You burned it out," she said.

"Took three tries." He smiled, and it was the worst thing she'd seen in weeks, and she had seen a chapel burn and a boy take a crossbow bolt through the chest. "First two didn't take. Thing fought me. Like it wanted to stay." He pulled the sleeve back down, gentle about it, almost tender, the way you'd cover a corpse. "Now. We've compared scars. Get out of my inn."

"The child," Elodie said. "Her name is Ilyen. The Order calls her the vessel. Serane has riders on the road behind us, and Sarel knows exactly where we're going, because there's only one place left to go, and we both know what's at the top of the Godspine."

Something moved through Halvard's face then, quick as a fish under ice — and it wasn't surprise. That was the part that turned Elodie's stomach cold. Not surprise. Recognition. Grief arriving right on schedule, like a debt collector who'd known the date all along.

"You don't want to know what's at the top," he said quietly.

"Too late."

"Then you already talked to someone." His eyes flicked to Rennick, dismissed him, came back to her. "The archive. You got into the archive." He laughed, one dry bark with nothing in it. "Twenty years I kept my mouth shut. Kept the inn. Poured drink for farmers. And some stack-thief tells a girl the whole rotten truth in an afternoon."

"Not the whole truth," Elodie said. "That's why I'm here. Rennick found records. Records aren't memory. You were *there*. You knelt beside me. Tell me it's lies, Halvard. Tell me the five of us sealed Veyra away in the middle of a war, and tell me why, and tell me why Tomas is dead, and I will walk out of here and never come back. Tell me it isn't true and I'll believe you, because you swore beside me and my word has meant something for twenty years and I need yours to mean something now."

The room was very quiet. One of the carters had stopped rolling his dice.

Halvard looked at Ilyen.

Elodie saw it happen — saw the moment his eyes found the child half-hidden behind her hip, saw him see the white stone shard at her throat, saw him understand. He swayed slightly, like a man taking a blow he'd been bracing for since yesterday.

"She's the key," he said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"And you're taking her up the mountain."

"Yes."

"To open it." His voice cracked down the middle. "*Gods*, Elodie. To open it."

"To find out the truth. From the source. And if the god wants out—" 

"If the god wants out?" Halvard came around the bar fast, and for one second the size of him remembered itself, filled the space between them, and Elodie's hand drifted toward her knife not from fear but from old training. Then he deflated, all at once, like a bellows kicked over. He gripped the bar's edge with both hands and hung off it. "Do you know what was happening? Down there? In the war? Do you actually know, or do you have the pretty archive version?"

"The war ended," Elodie said. "When the god went silent."

"The war *ended*," Halvard agreed, "because Veyra was the one holding the line. Literally. At the Fords of Ashen, the third year — you weren't there yet, you joined late — there was a night when the eastern host broke and Veyra stood in the river itself and held it. Held *both armies' terror* in its hands. Every man on that field felt the god take his fear out of him and carry it, and every man turned and fought like the dead because there was nothing left inside them to run with. That was Veyra. Not thunderbolts. It carried what we couldn't." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "And then the war got worse. And worse. And Veyra got bigger. Because it fed on what we gave it — our oaths, our grief, our fear — and the more we poured into it, the more it could hold, and the more it could hold, the more we poured. You see it? It was going to eat the whole world's sorrow and keep growing until there was no world left to be sorry in. Sarel saw it first. Sarel always sees things first."

"So the five of you sealed it," Elodie said. "To save the world."

"We sealed it to stop the feeding," Halvard said. "Nobody asked it what it wanted. Nobody ever asks the god what the god wants." He laughed again, that empty sound. "You know what the last thing it said was? Before the silence? It wasn't a battle cry. It was at the binding, when our marks burned and it understood what we were doing — it looked at us, all five, and it said, *as you wish.*"

The words landed in Elodie's chest like stones dropped down a well.

"As you wish," she repeated.

"As you wish." Halvard nodded slowly. "Twenty years I hear it when I'm falling asleep. As you wish. Like a father letting children tie him up because they cried hard enough." He straightened, and the tiredness in his face settled into something harder. "So no. I won't help you. I'll tell you what I told myself the night I burned this out of my arm: it's done, it's holding, leave it alone. The valley holds. The seal holds. Everything holds, as long as fools don't come asking questions with a living key on a string around their neck."

"The seal is failing," Elodie said. "Maren said it. The archive says it. Winter came early, Halvard. Wards are dying. Crops. The covenant is rotting out from under us whether we touch it or not. Doing nothing is also a choice. It's just the coward's version."

"I've been the coward twenty years," Halvard said. "Best decision I ever made. Look at me. Fat, drunk, alive. Tomas was brave." His voice went strange on the name, and Elodie seized it like a hook.

"Tomas," she said. "Tell me how Tomas died."

"You know how Tomas died. You buried him. He fell at the sealing."

"He fell at the sealing," Elodie said, "and for twenty years I thought he fell fighting the enemy. Rennick found a line in the archive. One line. That the fifth bearer resisted the rite. That she — that someone — cut him down." She stepped closer. "Was it Sarel, Halvard?"

The silence stretched so long the fire popped twice.

"Sarel doesn't talk about it," Halvard said finally, "and I don't talk about Sarel. Ask me anything else. Ask me about the binding, ask me about the valley, ask me about what's at the peak — I'll even tell you that, since you're determined to die — but don't ask me about Tomas."

"You owe me his death," Elodie said. "I carried his body out of that circle. I dug his grave with my hands because nobody else would touch him. I have knelt at it every year for twenty years believing he died for the god, and now you're telling me he maybe died fighting *for* the god, against *us*, and you want to pick which questions I'm allowed?"

"Yes," Halvard said. "Because I'm a coward, and cowards live long enough to pour your ale, and I am begging you—" and here his voice did break, fully, the big man suddenly looking smaller than anyone Elodie had seen in years, "—I am *begging* you, take the child and go home. Go back to the valley. Bar your door. Let the world outside rot; it's been rotting anyway. The seal will hold another winter, maybe two, maybe ten, and you'll be warm, and the girl will grow up, and someday you'll both be dead and it won't be your problem anymore. That's what I chose. That's what being second means — you're the one who says *stop, wait, not yet* — and I have been saying it for twenty years to a god that answered *as you wish* and to a woman I loved like a sister, and I am saying it one last time now. Stop. Wait. Go home."

Behind her, Ilyen let go of Elodie's hand.

Elodie heard it — the small release of her fingers — and turned. The child had come forward, out from behind her hip, into the open floor between Elodie and the broken giant behind the bar. She looked up at Halvard with those old, unchildlike eyes, and she reached up and touched the sleeve covering his scarred wrist, very lightly, the way you'd touch a wound.

She said something in the old tongue. Three words, soft.

Halvard made a sound like he'd been struck.

"What did she say," he whispered. "What did she say?"

"I wouldn't know," Elodie said. "She hasn't taught me that one yet."

Ilyen looked at Elodie then. And Elodie had learned, these past weeks, to read the child's face the way sailors read weather — but this time there was nothing to read, no storm coming, no calm. Just a look, level and ancient and unbearably kind, and then Ilyen spoke the common tongue, carefully, each word set down like a stone in a wall:

"He carries it still. Inside. Where the mark was burned. The god left a piece there, to keep him company. He was never alone. He just stopped listening."

Halvard sat down. There was no chair involved. He simply folded, down behind the bar until only his head showed above it, and put his face in his huge hands, and shook. The sound he made wasn't crying, exactly. It was the sound a wall makes before it decides.

Elodie crouched, laid her arms over the bar, and gripped his shoulder. Under her hand the mountain of him trembled like a rope in wind.

"Come with us," she said.

"No." Muffled. Immediate. Then, after a moment, quieter, muffled into his palms: "I can't. If I stand on that mountain again, I'll kneel. Don't ask me why. You'll understand at the peak. Everyone kneels, in the end, up there. Sarel knows it. That's why she built her order there. It's not a fortress, Elodie. It's an altar. And altars want offerings." He lifted his head. His eyes were wrecked and red and clearer than they'd been all day. "But I'll tell you the passes. The ones only a bearer can open — the old ways through the Godspine's knee, the ones the Order thinks collapsed. I'll draw them. I'll give you food, and horses, and everything in my cellar, because I've got twenty years of guilt and nowhere to put it. That's my price. That's all I am now. A map and a cellar."

"That's more than most men ever are," Elodie said.

He laughed — wet, broken, real. "Get your thief to buy the horses. Mine are terrible and I'll cheat you anyway." Then, as they turned to go, as Ilyen took Elodie's hand again and Rennick pushed off the doorframe with a wince:

"Elodie."

She paused.

"When you reach the prison heart — and you will, because you were always the stubborn one — when you stand in front of it and it asks you something, listen to the question underneath the question." He wiped his face with his sleeve like a boy. "Tomas heard it. That's why he drew steel against us. He wasn't mad. He was the only one of us listening."

* * *

They rode out at dusk, three horses heavier with provisions and lighter in purse, and the map of the hidden passes folded in Elodie's breast pocket, drawn in charcoal on the back of a tavern ledger page in a hand that had shaken and steadied and shaken again.

At the crest of the first hill Elodie reined in and looked back. The Drowned Bell's windows glowed yellow in the blue dark, and standing in the doorway, framed in that light, was the biggest man in Marrowford, watching them go with his ruined wrist pressed to his chest.

As they crested the ridge and the inn dropped out of sight, Rennick drew alongside. "Well," he said. "That was cheerful. Half the party's dead or drinking, the enemy's ahead of us and behind us, and our guide is a tavern sign." He shifted his splinted arm. "So. What's at the top of a mountain that makes a man burn out his own soul-mark rather than remember it?"

Elodie looked north, where the Godspine waited under early snow, white and vast and patient as an unanswered prayer.

"I don't know," she said. "But Tomas heard it. And Ilyen's been listening longer than any of us."

Ahead, the child rode silently in the saddle before her, and did not look back at all — as if, Elodie thought, she already knew exactly what was waiting, and had known all along, and had chosen, in her own impossible way, to climb toward it anyway.
