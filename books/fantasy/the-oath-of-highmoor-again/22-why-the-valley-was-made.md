## 22. Why the Valley Was Made

The fire had burned down to a red heart, and Ilyen watched it the way she watched everything now — as if the world were a book someone was slowly teaching her to read, and every flame and shadow held a word.

They had made camp in the ruins of an old waystation, three walls and half a roof, somewhere south of Halvard's inn. Rennick slept sitting up against the broken wall with his knife still in his hand, because that was how Rennick did everything — halfway, ready to run. Elodie sat across from her on a fallen beam, sharpening a blade that did not need sharpening, because that was how Elodie did everything — over and over, so her hands would not have to be empty.

Ilyen had words now. Not many, not yet. Common speech came to her slow and strange, like wading through cold water, but it came. And there were other words too, older ones, the ones that lived in the white stone shard against her chest, and those words had been getting louder all day.

Since Halvard's inn. Since the drunkard with the ruined hands had said *prison* and *sealing* and looked at Elodie like a man looking at a wall he'd helped build.

Ilyen had understood more of that conversation than either of them knew. She always understood more than they knew. The old language didn't need her mouth; it lived in her blood, in the shard, in the humming place behind her ribs where something vast and quiet pressed close like a face against frosted glass.

Tonight the pressing was different. Tonight it was insistent.

*Ilyen.*

Her name, spoken without sound. She had learned to know Veyra's voice the way you know rain coming — not hearing it, feeling it.

*Tell her.*

She looked at Elodie. Elodie looked back, caught watching, and gave the small crooked almost-smile she gave when she thought Ilyen needed comforting. It was such a careful smile. Everything about Elodie was careful, the way a house is careful when it is built on a crack in the earth.

"Elodie," Ilyen said.

The whetstone stopped.

"You sleep," Elodie said gently. "Big day tomorrow."

"No." Ilyen's tongue stumbled on the word and she pushed through anyway, because some things could not wait for grammar. "No sleep. Now. I remember."

*Remember* came out wrong — too much like *re-mem-ber*, each part its own small stone — but Elodie heard it. Elodie set the blade down. That was one thing Ilyen had learned early and loved hard: when Elodie decided to listen, she listened with her whole body, turned toward you like a door opening.

"Remember what?" Elodie asked. Her voice was even. Her hands, folded around the whetstone, were not.

Ilyen reached up and drew the shard out from under her shirt. In the firelight it glowed faintly, the way it always did near Elodie, near the oath-mark on her arm — and Ilyen saw Elodie's eyes go to that mark, the faded white scar shaped like a falling feather, and Ilyen felt the truth rise in her like water rising in a well, cold and unstoppable.

"I show," Ilyen said. And then, because the old language was faster, because some things could only be said properly in the tongue that had made her: *"Come and see what you have been living inside."*

She touched the shard to the dirt floor.

* * *

It was not magic, exactly. Or it was magic the way breathing is air — you don't summon it, you just stop holding it out.

The firelight bent. The ruined walls went soft at their edges, like wet ink. And the vision opened around them, not grand, not golden, but plain as a memory — which is what it was. Veyra's memory, poured through the shard, through Ilyen, into the two mortals who sat frozen on either side of her.

They stood — or seemed to stand — on a green hill under a sky torn open with light.

Ilyen had seen this before, in the long dark behind her eyelids during the years before she had a body, before the god's last will had gathered itself into flesh and set her walking down out of the mountains. But seeing it from inside a body, with eyes, with Elodie's hand finding her shoulder — it was different. It hurt differently.

Five figures on the hill. She knew them. She had been made from them, in a sense; the covenant had been woven from their oaths, and she was the covenant's last thread given breath.

Tomas, young and frightened, his oath-mark already bleeding. Halvard, big and weeping openly. Sarel — Sarel standing straightest of all, jaw set, eyes dry, because someone had to be strong and she had decided it would be her forever. Serane, barely grown, gripping her sword like a rail in a storm.

And Elodie. Younger Elodie. Elodie with no gray in her braid, kneeling in the grass with her arm raised, the oath-mark bright as a brand.

And above them — around them — *everywhere* —

Veyra.

Ilyen felt Elodie's grip tighten on her shoulder until it nearly hurt. Twenty years of praying to silence, twenty years of loving a god who would not answer, and here was the answer: the god had never left. The god had been *held*.

The vision moved the way memories move, skipping, sliding, refusing order. The war. She let them see the war because Veyra insisted — *they must understand why, they must understand why* — the burning plains, the armies wearing crowns of stolen light, sorcerers unmaking whole cities out of spite, the dead so numerous the rivers ran sluggish. Mortal mages had torn the boundary between worlds to feed their power, and through the tear had come things that wore mortal shapes and ate mortal souls, and they had called themselves kings, and people had knelt.

Veyra had fought them. Ilyen felt the fighting like a fever: the god pouring itself out, spending centuries of hoarded grace in decades, healing, shielding, striking down the false kings — and the more it gave, the more mortals feared it, because nothing that powerful can be loved for long. Power invites worship, and worship invites terror, and terror invites the oldest human arithmetic: *if we cannot control it, we must cage it.*

The five oath-bearers had not set out to imprison their god.

That was the part Ilyen needed Elodie to understand most, and she pushed it forward in the vision, gentle as arranging flowers: they had set out to *end the war*. The sealing had begun as a covenant — Veyra's power anchored in five mortal vows, a dam against the flood of wild magic, a way to close the tear between worlds without destroying every mage in the realm. A shared burden. A marriage between god and mortals.

But vows are doors, and doors swing both ways. The same five locks that anchored Veyra's grace could bind it entire. And when the war ended — when the tear was closed and the false kings were ash — the survivors of the burning world looked at the god in their midst and did not feel saved.

They felt *next*.

Sarel had seen it first. Sarel, who had lost everyone, who had walked through a countryside of mass graves, who had learned the lesson the burned world taught best: *nothing this powerful stays kind.* She had gone to the other four with tears in her eyes and iron in her voice and asked them the question that would split history:

*If Veyra ever changes — if grief or age or eternity turns it — who stops a god?*

No one answered. So Sarel answered herself: *We do. We already hold the locks. We simply... turn them.*

Tomas had refused. Ilyen lingered on Tomas — she felt Elodie go rigid beside her, felt the name rise in her throat like a wound reopening — Tomas arguing through the night, Tomas saying *an oath freely sworn cannot become a chain, that's the whole meaning of oath*, Tomas riding for the Godspine alone to warn Veyra, to unmake his own fifth of the covenant if he had to—

Tomas dying on the mountain road, cut down by the very knights of the new Order, sworn men who believed they were saving the world.

The vision did not flinch from it. Veyra had watched. Veyra, bound already by four-fifths of a turned lock, had watched its faithful son die trying to free it, and could do nothing, and had spent its final unbound moment — the last sliver of its will, the last coin in its purse — on one act.

Not vengeance. Not escape.

A valley.

* * *

The vision shifted, and now Ilyen showed her the part she had carried longest, the part that had kept her awake since Halvard's inn, the part that tasted like copper every time she thought it.

The ward was not a shield.

Everyone in Highmoor Valley believed the same story, worn smooth as a river stone: the god's last act of love was to hide a piece of the world from magic, a sanctuary where the wars could never reach, where children could grow up safe. A gift. A blessing. The Silent God's mercy.

It was true the way a cage is true when you call it a sanctuary.

Highmoor Valley was not hidden *from* magic. Highmoor Valley was the housing of the lock. The ward that kept magic out was the same ward that kept Veyra in — one wall, seen from either side. From inside, it read as protection. From outside, from the god's side, it read as the inside of a cell. The valley was the shape of the prison, laid flat upon the earth, and every soul born within it had lived out their lives as mortar in the walls.

Ilyen turned and looked at Elodie inside the vision — really looked, the way you look at someone when you are about to break something in them that cannot be unbroken.

Because it was worse than that. It was personal in a way that made Ilyen's chest ache with something that wasn't pity and wasn't guilt but was some terrible compound of both.

The covenant needed anchors. Five oaths, five lives, five mortal hearts to turn the lock or hold it shut. Four had been bound at the sealing — Sarel, Halvard, and the two others whose names had been struck from every record. But a lock wants a keyhole, and a keyhole must live somewhere stable, somewhere the ward runs deepest, somewhere no outside power can reach to pick it.

So the covenant had chosen its keyhole. A child born inside the valley. An orphan with no family to miss her, no ties to pull her away. A girl who prayed faithfully to the silent god, who took the bounty work and lived the plain life and never, ever left — because she couldn't have left, not truly; the mark on her arm was a root sunk into the ward's foundation, and to walk beyond the valley's edge would have been to tear herself loose from the very structure of her life.

Elodie had thought she was staying home out of loyalty.

Elodie had thought the restless contentment, the grief that never quite healed, the sense of a door somewhere in her life that would not open — she had thought these were flaws in her own heart.

They were architecture. They were the cage expressing itself through her. Every year she stayed, the lock grew stronger. Every prayer she spoke to the silent god was not devotion but *maintenance* — her faith was the oil in the mechanism, her oath the bolt, her whole quiet, dutiful, carefully bounded life the turning of a key she never knew she held.

*You were not my faithful servant,* Veyra said through the shard, and the god's voice in the vision was not angry, which somehow made it infinitely worse; it was tired, and gentle, and full of a sorrow older than kingdoms. *You were my prison's heart. I am sorry. Of all the cruelties done in my name, yours grieves me most — that I used a child's faith as the hasp of my own chains, because it was the strongest metal I was given.*

The vision held on young Elodie kneeling in the grass, swearing joyfully, radiant with purpose, and then dissolved her into the woman sitting by the fire — twenty years of bounty work and solitary suppers and prayers into silence — and laid the two images together like matching halves of a broken seal.

*A cage does not feel like a cage from inside,* Veyra said. *That is what makes it perfect. You loved your valley. You loved your life. And your love was the bar across my door.*

* * *

The vision broke.

The fire snapped back into being an ordinary fire. The ruined walls were ordinary walls. The night was ordinary night, full of ordinary crickets and the ordinary smell of woodsmoke and cold ash.

Elodie had not moved. She sat exactly as she had sat, hands around the whetstone, but her face in the firelight was the face of someone reading a letter that arrives after the funeral — every line of it known too late, every word a fresh wound precisely because it explained everything.

Ilyen knew that face. She had worn it herself, in her first weeks, when the remembering began — when she'd understood that her whole childhood of dreams had been a god's dictation, that her body was a vessel built for a purpose she hadn't chosen, that her innocence had been manufactured the way a tool is manufactured. She remembered the vertigo of it. *My life was not mine. My life was a sentence written before I was born.*

She crawled across the space between them — she was still small enough, still quick enough, that crawling was faster than walking and she had never seen a reason to pretend otherwise — and put her hand flat on Elodie's knee.

"Elodie," she said. The common word was easy now; it was practically the first word she'd ever said to anyone. "Not cage. Not only."

Elodie's eyes came down to her. Red-rimmed. Steady. Elodie was very good at steady; Ilyen suspected it was the same skill as sharpening a blade that didn't need it — repetition against emptiness.

"Not only," Elodie repeated. Her voice came out sanded flat. "Twenty years, Ilyen. Twenty years I prayed to her. I built my whole life—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Every time I thought about leaving, something in me just — settled. Like a stone finding its socket. I thought it was peace. I thought I was *content*."

"You were," Ilyen said. It was important to her to say it correctly, and she arranged the words with care. "Content was real. Home was real. Cage and home are same wall, Elodie. Different side."

Rennick stirred against the wall, awake after all — of course he was awake, Rennick slept like a cat with one ear rotating — and Ilyen felt him deciding not to say anything, which was its own kind of kindness, and filed it away among the growing pile of reasons she had decided he was family.

"The ward's failing," Elodie said. It wasn't a question. She was already past shock into the cold country beyond it, where Elodie did her best work. "That's why the seasons are wrong. Why the wards are collapsing everywhere. If the valley was the lock—"

"The lock rusts," Ilyen said. She knew the word *rusts* only from Halvard's drunken muttering about his sword, but it fit, and language was hers now, she took what she needed. "Lock is made of oaths. Oaths are made of believers. Believers die. Sarel holds it with fear instead of faith, and fear is bad metal. Lock rusts. When lock breaks—" she touched the shard at her throat, and felt beneath her fingers the vast pressure, the face against the glass, the god who had spent its last freedom making her — "when lock breaks, Veyra comes out. Or war comes back. Or both. Nobody knows. Even Veyra doesn't know what it is now, after twenty years in the dark."

Elodie was silent for a long moment. Then she laughed — one short, cracked note with no humor in it at all.

"All those years," she said, "I thought she was silent because she'd abandoned us. Because we weren't worth answering. I hated her a little, at the bottom of everything. And she was right there. Right there under my feet, under my *church*, listening to every prayer, and she couldn't—" Her jaw tightened. "She was in the cell I was keeping locked."

"She chose it," Ilyen said quickly — and then stopped, because that was not entirely true, was it? That was the thing she had not yet told them, the thing the god had pressed against the glass about all day, insistent, urgent, the thing that had finally forced this night's reckoning. Veyra had consented to the first binding. Consent given in the wreckage of a burned world by a god drowning in guilt — *my power fed the war, my existence invited the fear* — was still consent. But consent to a wound is not the same as wanting the wound, and twenty years of darkness had changed the terms of everything.

"There is more," Ilyen said.

Elodie closed her eyes briefly, the way soldiers do before the next volley. "Of course there is."

"The seal needs a vessel," Ilyen said. "To renew it. To make the lock new, so it holds another hundred years, another thousand." She lifted the shard, and let the firelight pour through it, and watched Elodie understand, watch the horror arrive in stages the way winter arrives — first the wind, then the frost, then the deep snow. "Living vessel. Made from the covenant. Grown in the valley. That is what I am, Elodie. That is what I was *for*. If we reach the Godspine and reforge the oath—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. She watched Elodie finish it for her, watched the color leave the woman's face, watched twenty years of mercenary pragmatism collide head-on with whatever had grown in her chest since a mud-caked child knocked at her door at dusk.

"They want me to give you to the lock," Elodie said. Very quietly. "Forever."

"Sarel wants it," Ilyen said. "Sarel would say it is mercy. One child against the world." She had Sarel's memories folded inside her, along with everyone's; the covenant remembered its makers. "Sarel is not wrong. That is the worst thing about Sarel."

"And you?" Elodie's hand came up and cupped Ilyen's jaw, rough palm, careful thumb — the gesture that had taught Ilyen more about common tongues than any words. "What do *you* want?"

No one had asked her that before. Not Veyra, who had made her. Not the covenant, which had shaped her. Not Maren, kind as she was, who looked at her and saw a miracle needing shelter. Only this woman whose whole life had just been revealed to her as a cage wall, this woman who should by rights be thinking of nothing but her own shattered world.

Ilyen found that her eyes were wet. She was still learning crying; she did it clumsily, with great expenditure of water.

"I want to be Ilyen," she said. "Not vessel. Not seal. Ilyen. Who likes the fire and hates fish and thinks Rennick snores. If the price of the world is me, then—" she steadied herself with a breath, the way she'd seen Elodie steady before a fight — "then I will pay it. But I want someone to know I paid. I want it to be a choice, not a use. That is all. That is everything."

For a moment Elodie said nothing at all. Then she pulled Ilyen in, and Ilyen went, into the smell of leather and woodsmoke and steel that meant *safe* in a language deeper than any kingdom's, and over her head she heard Elodie speak — low, fierce, to the fire, to the shard, to the god listening behind the glass:

"You hear me in there? Whatever you are now, whatever twenty years in the dark made you — nobody pays that price. Not her. Not while I'm breathing. Find another way or we'll die finding one, but she is *not* a lock."

The shard pulsed once against Ilyen's chest. Warm. Startled. Almost — could a god be startled? — almost hopeful.

And far off to the north, past the black ridgelines, past the sleeping country, at the top of the Godspine where the storm never stopped, something ancient felt the lock rattle and turned its attention southward — and in a frozen gatehouse below the peak, a woman named Sarel woke from a dreamless sleep with her hand already on her sword, knowing, with the certainty of twenty years of iron, that the vessel was coming.

And that she would be waiting.
