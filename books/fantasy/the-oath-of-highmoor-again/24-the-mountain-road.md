## 24. The Mountain Road

The blizzard found them on the third day of the climb, and after that it never left.

It came down off the Godspine like something with intent, a white wall that ate the trail, the sky, the distinction between up and down. Elodie walked at the front because Halvard told her to walk behind him and she had spent twenty years not taking orders from Halvard, and old habits were the last things to die in a cold place.

"Rope," Halvard shouted through the wind, for the fourth time.

"I heard you the first time."

"You didn't *listen*." He held out the coil anyway, arm shaking, snow crusted in his beard. Twenty years of wine had softened him everywhere except his hands; those were still an oath-bearer's hands, scarred and steady, and they had not forgotten anything even if the rest of him pretended to. "The pass narrows ahead. Ice under the powder. One wrong step and you're a red smear on the rocks below, and I'll have wasted my redemption on a corpse."

Elodie tied the rope around Ilyen's waist first, then Rennick's, then her own, then last of all Halvard's, because he was oldest and she trusted him least. The child stood in the whiteout without complaint, small and still as a cairn stone, her hood rimed with frost, the shard of white stone hanging against her chest where it had lived since Corvassa. It did not hum anymore. It *sang*, low and constant, a note Elodie felt in her teeth more than heard, growing louder with every mile north.

They climbed.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow beneath an overhang, three walls of stone and one wall of fire that Rennick coaxed from pitch-wood he'd been hoarding like a miser. The wind screamed over the top of them but could not get down into the hollow. Elodie counted their supplies by touch — hardtack, smoked goat, a skin of melted snow — and did the arithmetic she hated: five days of food, four days of climb, if the weather held, which it would not.

Ilyen sat close against her side, the way she always did now, close enough that Elodie could feel the child breathing. She hadn't spoken much since the weir — since Rennick had taken a lance meant for her and gone gray-faced and feverish for two days, since Elodie had burned three knights out of the air with light that came out of her own bones. But when she spoke it was less often the old language and more often common words, halting, careful, like someone learning to swim.

"Cold," Ilyen said now, pressing Elodie's hand against her own cheek, as if Elodie might not have noticed.

"Not for long. Fire's good."

"Fire is good," Ilyen repeated solemnly, tasting each word. Then, after a while: "You are angry."

"I'm tired."

"You are angry *and* tired." The child looked up at her with those eyes that were too old for her face, that had been looking at Elodie with too-old eyes since the first night on her doorstep. "At Halvard."

Elodie fed a stick into the fire instead of answering.

Because she was. Not for the sealing — she'd burned through most of that rage on the river, watching Halvard weep into his cups and confess it all, the war and the prison and Sarel's name spoken like a curse. What remained was smaller and meaner: that he had known. That he had sat in his inn for twenty years pouring ale over the truth while she knelt before a silent altar every morning of her life, praying to a god who was locked in a box, and he had let her.

"He should have told me," she said finally.

"He was afraid."

"So am I. Every day. I tell people anyway."

"Do you?" Ilyen asked, mild as morning, and Elodie had no answer for that either. The child leaned her head against Elodie's shoulder and within minutes was asleep, breath fogging in the cold, one small fist closed around the covenant-shard.

Across the fire, Rennick watched them both. He'd lost weight since the wound; his face had gone sharp and hungry-looking, but his eyes were clear, and he'd stopped making jokes about everything, which Elodie privately considered a great loss. "She's right, you know," he said quietly. "About Halvard."

"She's seven."

"Older than that inside. You know it too." He shifted, wincing, hand going to the bandages under his coat. "He's carrying us up a mountain at sixty years old in a killing storm. Whatever he owes you, he's paying it."

"It's not payment I want."

"No," Rennick agreed. "But it's what he has."

Halvard had taken the first watch, out at the mouth of the hollow where the wind couldn't quite reach, sitting with his back to a boulder and his sword across his knees — an oath-blade, one of five forged for the original covenant, its steel gone dark with age. From this angle he looked like what he had once been, before the wine: a big, grave, dependable man, the kind who stood at the front of battles so others wouldn't have to.

Elodie remembered standing behind him. She remembered being nineteen and certain of everything.

* * *

Serane's riders came at dawn. Or rather, Elodie knew they had come at dawn, though she saw nothing — just a flicker at the ridgeline south of them, there and gone, dark shapes moving through the falling snow with the patience of wolves trailing a wounded elk. They didn't press. They didn't need to. The Order knew where the only pass to the Godspine lay, and they knew the storm would do half their work for them.

"They're letting us tire ourselves out," Rennick said, squinting south through the white.

"They're letting the mountain fight us," Elodie corrected. "They'll take whatever's left."

"Cheerful."

"I'm a mercenary. Cheerful costs extra."

But that evening, when they made camp in the ruins of an old waystation — a shrine, actually, one of Veyra's, its walls tumbled and its altar-stone split down the middle exactly like every other shrine on the road of broken shrines — Halvard did something strange. He cleared the snow from the broken altar with his bare hands, knelt, and pressed his palm flat against the stone.

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened; the god was locked away, the shrines were dead. But he stayed there a long time, kneeling in the wind, and when he came back to the fire his face was wet and it wasn't entirely the snow.

"There's a shortcut," he said. "Through the Serpent's Teeth. Saves a full day, maybe two. Nobody uses it because nobody knows it."

"Nobody knows it how?"

"Because it's marked in the old liturgy." He looked at Ilyen, asleep against Elodie's pack. "And because the only map of it lives in an oath-bearer's memory. We swore on those stones, Elodie. All five of us, right there, twenty-six years ago. The mountain remembers us. If I walk the Teeth, the mountain will know me."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we all die in a pretty little canyon, and you can spend eternity telling my ghost you were right not to trust me."

Elodie looked at the fire. Looked at Ilyen, sleeping. Looked at Halvard, who had knelt in the snow before a dead god's broken altar and wept.

"One condition," she said. "If it's a trap — if any part of this is you leading us to Serane —"

"If any part of this is me leading you to Serane," Halvard said, "then I'll already be dead, because I'll throw myself off the first ledge before I let them touch that child. On Veyra's name."

"The name of a god you helped cage."

"Yes," he said simply. "That's rather the point."

* * *

The Serpent's Teeth were a knife-fight between the earth and the sky, a mile of jagged spires where the wind funneled through gaps narrow enough to touch both walls. The storm above became something worse here — not falling snow but flying ice, horizontal, flaying. Halvard led them through it reading marks no one else could see: a chip in a stone that meant *lean left*, a wedge of stacked pebbles that meant *ice bridge, cross quickly*, a carved symbol half-eaten by lichen that made him stop dead and stand with his head bowed before waving them on.

"What does that one say?" Elodie shouted.

"It says *Veyra watches*. The oath-bearers cut them as we climbed, the year we sealed the passes against the war." He laughed, a terrible sound. "We thought we were protecting pilgrims. We thought we were building something permanent."

They crossed an ice bridge over a gorge that went down farther than the eye could follow, Ilyen strapped to Elodie's back because the child's legs were too short for the drifts and Elodie refused to slow. The shard sang louder on the far side. When they stopped to breathe, Ilyen pulled back her hood and said, clearly, over the wind:

"We are close. It knows we are close."

"What knows?" Rennick asked.

"The mountain." She touched the rock beside her, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "It is dreaming about the door at the top."

Halvard and Elodie exchanged a look over the child's head. Neither of them said what both were thinking: that the mountain dreaming was exactly the kind of thing that used to happen before Veyra went silent, according to the old stories, and that the world was coming apart at the seams precisely as the covenant decayed — the failed wards, the dying crops, the wild magic bleeding through cracks that shouldn't exist.

"Move," Elodie said.

* * *

They camped that night in the Teeth themselves, wedged into a slot canyon, and it was there that the riders finally stopped shadowing and struck — or tried to.

Elodie woke to Halvard's hand clamped over her mouth and his other arm pointing up. Against the stars at the canyon's rim: torchlight. Four, five, six points of flame, and the silhouettes of horses that should not have been able to stand on that slope, ridden by men and women in the grey-and-silver of the Order of the Quiet.

"They've been following our fires," Halvard breathed. "Every night. They know exactly where we—"

The first rope came down.

It happened fast after that. Elodie got Ilyen behind her and put her back to the canyon wall, and the oath-mark on her forearm woke like a struck bell, light running up her veins, and when the first knight dropped the last ten feet into the slot she caught his blade on hers and *pushed* — not strength, not skill, but the old borrowed power of the covenant flooding through her, and the knight went backward into the canyon wall hard enough to crack stone.

Then Halvard roared — actually roared, like a man half his age, like the man he'd been at nineteen — and met the second knight head-on, dark blade ringing silver, and Rennick, fever-bright and half-dead, came up from the fire with his knife in his fist and a look on his face that said he'd decided dying here was better than dying slowly somewhere warm.

Three knights in the slot. Two holding the ropes above.

Elodie fought the first one down — disarmed him, broke his sword arm against her knee, left him groaning — and turned for the second and found Halvard already there, and the third knight was climbing toward Ilyen—

—and stopped. Stood perfectly still, sword lowered, staring at the child with an expression Elodie had seen before, once, on a hedgewitch's face: recognition, and awe, and horror.

"She's real," the knight whispered. "The vessel. The stories said—" And then the knight tore her own helmet off, and it was a young woman, barely older than Elodie had been at the swearing, and she was crying. "I joined the Order to *serve* her. Nobody said anything about—"

"Hold!" The shout came from above, from the rim. A voice Elodie knew. A voice she'd heard across a parley table in Corvassa, offering her the valley's life for the child's freedom.

Serane herself rappelled down the rope into the slot canyon, boots hitting stone, sword drawn, and the whole fight stopped around her like a held breath. Her eyes went from Elodie, to Ilyen, to the defected knight, to Halvard, and something in her face folded and refolded.

"Stand down," she said to her knights. Quiet. Absolute. They obeyed.

"You're letting us go," Elodie said. It wasn't a question.

"I'm choosing my ground." Serane's voice was level, but her sword-hand wasn't steady. "Not here. Not with half my company ready to kneel to a seven-year-old." She looked at Ilyen for a long moment, and Elodie watched her swallow something enormous. "The gatehouse at the peak is garrisoned. Two hundred of the Order. Sarel commands in person. Whatever you think awaits you at the top, Elodie — it isn't mercy, and it isn't a negotiation. She will kill the child. She told me so herself."

"Then why warn me?"

"Because you swore the same oath I did." Serane sheathed her sword. For one heartbeat she looked, not like an inquisitor captain, but like the woman Elodie had once loved across a campfire, twenty years and a lifetime ago. "And because part of me still hopes I'm wrong about everything."

She grabbed the defected knight's rope and went up, and the torches withdrew along the rim, and the canyon was suddenly very quiet, full of snow and blood and breathing.

Rennick sat down hard on a rock. "Did we just get rescued by the antagonist?"

"She's not the antagonist," Halvard said grimly, cleaning his blade. "Sarel is. Serane's just the poor fool still pretending duty is a shape love can wear."

Elodie knelt by Ilyen. The child was unhurt, unbothered, watching the empty rim with those ancient eyes.

"You knew she'd come," Elodie said.

"I knew someone would come." Ilyen touched Elodie's cheek with cold fingers. "You are angry again."

"A little."

"At her, or yourself?"

Elodie didn't answer. Above them, far up the black spine of the mountain, through a tear in the storm clouds, the peak of the Godspine showed itself for the first time — and at its summit, faint but unmistakable, a white light was burning. Steady. Patient. Like a lamp left burning in a window by someone who has been waiting a very long time.

The shard against Ilyen's chest flared in answer, singing loud enough now that all of them could hear it.

"That's not a beacon," Rennick whispered. "That's a countdown."
