## 25. Sarel

The Godspine did not sleep, and neither did Sarel Voss.

She stood at the window of the Gatehouse of the Quiet, forty stories of mountain below her feet and a storm coming down the valley like an army. Frost feathered the glass. Beyond it, the peaks went up and up into cloud, white on white, until the world simply stopped and something older began. Somewhere above that ceiling of cloud was the prison. Somewhere above that was Veyra.

Twenty years she had kept this watch. Twenty years of ledgers and liturgies and knights who saluted too sharply, and every night she climbed to this window and asked herself the only question that mattered: *Was it enough? Was it still enough?*

The answer had always been yes.

It had to be.

* * *

They had been five, once. Five young fools kneeling in a circle of ash at the foot of the Godspine while a god's voice poured through them like molten silver. Elodie had been the youngest — seventeen, all elbows and stubbornness, her oath-mark still raw and pink on her wrist. Halvard had wept when he swore. Tomas had laughed. Serane had gripped Sarel's hand so hard their knuckles cracked. And Sarel herself had felt the god settle into her bones and thought: *this is what I was made for.*

Veyra had spoken to each of them differently. To Halvard, comfort. To Tomas, courage. To Serane, purpose. To Elodie —

To Elodie it had given silence. A quiet so deep and complete that Sarel had envied it for years afterward, not knowing then what it would cost the girl.

And to Sarel? Sarel had been given sight. She had seen the shape of the covenant from the inside, seen how the god's power flowed through the oath-bearers into the world, seen the seams where it could fray. It was sight that let her do what needed doing, later. Sight that let her hold the knife.

She pressed her palm flat against the cold glass. Below, far below, the last lights of the Order's outposts winked out one by one as the watch changed.

*I did it to save them,* she told the mountain, as she told it every night. *I did it to save everyone.*

The mountain said nothing. The mountain never said anything. That was rather the point of it.

* * *

The war had not been glorious. Wars never were, whatever the balladeers sang. It had been three years of burning fields and villages emptied into mass graves, of magic gone feral because the god's attention was stretched too thin across too many oaths sworn by too many desperate people. Every kingdom had wanted a piece of Veyra. Every warlord with a sword and a grievance had learned to swear by the Silent-Then-Speaking God, and every oath gave them power, and every ounce of that power came out of the same well, and the well was running dry.

Sarel had watched a man pull a village's entire harvest out of frozen ground with a word of power, and then watched the ground around it die for a mile in every direction. She had watched children born without shadows in the war's last year, little hollow-eyed things that screamed whenever anyone spoke Veyra's name. She had watched Tomas die — or believed she had; that lie was the Order's oldest wound and she kept it bandaged with discipline — and she had decided.

Not alone. Never alone. That was the mercy and the horror of it: four others had agreed. Four others had looked at the dying world and the drowning god and chosen arithmetic over faith. Millions against one. It was not even a hard sum.

Elodie had not been there when they voted. Someone — Halvard, Sarel thought, though he denied it even now — had arranged for the girl to be elsewhere that night, and Sarel had never forgiven herself for allowing it. They should have told her. They should have made her choose too, made her carry the weight, instead of leaving her twenty years to build a cathedral of grief on top of a lie.

But they hadn't. And now the girl was a woman with a sword and a dead god's mark on her wrist, and she was coming up this mountain with a child, and Sarel would have to kill her or turn her, and either way the last of the five would be spent.

She turned from the window.

"Captain." A knight in the doorway, snow melting off his pauldrons. "Serane's riders report contact lost at the weir. The barge burned. Survivors unknown."

"Unknown," Sarel repeated. "Meaning escaped."

"Meaning—" He hesitated. "Yes, Captain."

"Then they're on the mountain road." She crossed to the great table where the maps lay, the passes marked in red ink like wounds. "Double the watches on the high col. No one ascends past the third cairn. And send for me the moment Serane reports — *if* Serane reports."

He saluted and withdrew. Sarel looked down at the map, at the small red line crawling northward that was Elodie Winterbourne, and felt something twist under her ribs that was not fear.

It was recognition.

* * *

Here was the thing no one in the Order understood, the thing Sarel would take to her grave: she did not hate the child.

She had seen the reports, of course. The vessel grown from Veyra's final act of will, the living seal, the key. She had read Maren's intercepted prayers, the priestess's trembling confessions to a god who could not hear her. She knew exactly what walked beside Elodie on the mountain road — knew it better than anyone alive, because Sarel had helped write the rite that made such vessels possible, back when the sealing was done and the god, in its last free hour, had fought back the only way it could.

Veyra had made a child. Had poured its final unbound will into flesh small enough to hide inside a ward, and hidden her in the one place no magic could reach, and left her there like a message tucked under a door: *when you are ready to undo this, here is how.*

Sarel had understood that message within a year of the sealing. She had spent every year since making sure no one else ever would.

And the child herself — Ilyen, the reports named her — was innocent. Of course she was. She was a weapon forged without being asked, aimed without consent, and none of it was her doing. If Sarel killed her, it would be murder in every way that mattered, dressed up in necessity the way all her murders were dressed.

She had made peace with that long ago. Or she had built something next to the guilt and called it peace, which amounted to the same thing in practice, and practice was what mattered. Faith was practice. Duty was practice. You did the terrible thing cleanly, you did it completely, and you carried it, because someone had to, and the someone was always the one who could see the whole board.

That was what Elodie had never understood. Elodie saw one child. Sarel saw the harvest rotting in the fields as wild magic bled through the failing ward. Sarel saw the wards collapsing along the borderlands, the towns where things were already moving in the dark. Sarel saw the war starting again, not because anyone chose it but because the seal was failing *anyway* — and if it failed anyway, then it would fail chaotically, catastrophically, with the god waking furious and half-mad after two decades in the dark.

A clean death for one child, or a dirty death for a million. There was no third column in that ledger. She had checked. She had checked for twenty years.

Haven't you? whispered something in the back of her skull, in a voice that sounded uncomfortably like Tomas.

* * *

At midnight she went down to the reliquary beneath the Gatehouse, as she did every night, though there was no rule requiring it and no witness to see.

Four stones sat in a velvet-lined case, each cut from the original covenant-shard when the five had split it at the sealing. Four, because hers she kept on a cord at her throat, and because the fifth stone — the one taken from Tomas's body at Coldmere — she had never been able to throw away.

She picked it up. It was dull, dead, dark as river slate. Oath-stones died when their bearer died. Everyone knew that.

Except that lately, on certain nights, when the storm pressed close and the prison overhead groaned in its sleep, the stone grew warm in her hand. Faintly. Falsely, she told herself. Residual heat. Old memory in the mineral.

Except that three nights ago it had pulsed, once, in rhythm with her heartbeat, the way a stone pulses when its bearer is alive and swearing somewhere in the world.

She closed her fist around it until the edges bit her palm.

"Tomas," she said quietly. "If you're alive, you coward, and you're helping her—"

She couldn't finish. She never could. Because the alternative to *Tomas is dead* was *Tomas chose the other side*, and if Tomas — gentle, laughing Tomas, who had loved Veyra more purely than any of them — if *he* had come to believe the sealing was wrong, then everything Sarel had built her life upon was not duty.

It was just a cage. And she was just a jailer.

She put the stone back in its case and locked it, and stood a moment in the dark listening to the mountain groan.

* * *

Dawn brought the report she had dreaded.

Serane stood before her desk, snow-crusted, hollow-eyed, and did not salute. That omission rang through the room like a dropped bell.

"They passed the second cairn in the night," Serane said. "Halvard guided them through the Serpent's Teeth. Only an oath-bearer could have found that line in a whiteout."

"Halvard." Sarel closed her eyes briefly. Two of the five, then, walking up her mountain with the enemy. "And your riders?"

"Scattered at the pass. I've regrouped what's left at the third cairn." Serane's jaw worked. "Captain — Sarel. The child spoke at the weir. Rennick's survivors heard it. When the ward-magic hit her, she spoke the old tongue fluently, and the ice on the river broke in a perfect ring around the barge. She is what the archives say she is. And she isn't suffering. Whatever Veyra made her for, she doesn't seem to know it's a cage."

"Nothing that lives in a cage knows it's a cage until the door opens," Sarel said. "That's what cages are *for*."

"She's eight years old."

"She's a lock with a heartbeat." Sarel heard her own voice go flat and cold, the voice she used for orders, and hated it, and used it anyway. "You held her at Corvassa and let her go. You hesitated at the parley. Now you stand in my office telling me about her age as though tenderness were tactics. Look at me, Serane."

Serane looked. And Sarel saw it there, plain as a scar: the doubt. The thing she had spent two decades keeping out of her own eyes was blooming in her friend's, and there was nothing on this earth she could do about it except give her something harder than doubt to hold.

"You swore the same oath I did," Sarel said. "You watched the same fields burn. You buried the same children at Coldmere. Everything we are, we are because we chose millions over one — and if we flinch now, at the very door of the vault, then those twenty years were for nothing and every grave we filled was filled in vain. Is that what you want? Tell me it isn't."

"No," Serane said. Too slowly. "No. It isn't."

"Then hold the third cairn. Nothing passes. *No one* passes. And Serane—" Sarel rose, came around the desk, and did the cruelest thing she knew how to do: she took her old companion's hands in both of hers, the way they had done at the swearing, twenty years ago, ash and silver light. "When it's done, when the covenant is reforged and the world holds, I will release you from the Order. You and Elodie can grow old together anywhere the Crown allows. I swear it on my stone. Just bring me the child."

Something moved behind Serane's eyes — hope, or its corpse — and she bowed and left.

Sarel stood alone in the grey morning light, and admitted to the empty room what she could admit nowhere else:

She had lied.

There would be no release. There was no place in a reforged world for people who had done what they'd done; the Order saw to that itself, folding its instruments away when the work was finished. She had promised Serane a future because Serane needed one to fight for, and Sarel had long since learned that the difference between a lie and a tool was only a matter of grip.

She touched the dead stone at her throat and thought of Elodie climbing through the blizzard toward her, carrying a child, carrying a grief Sarel herself had authored, and she understood — with a clarity that felt almost like grace — that the woman coming up the mountain was what Sarel might have become, if she had been spared the vote. Spared the knowledge. Allowed twenty years of beautiful ignorance.

One of us gets to be the hero, she thought. And one of us keeps the door shut.

Outside, the storm thickened, and somewhere above the clouds, the Godspine groaned — long and low, like a voice remembering how to speak.

Sarel fastened her cloak, took up her staff of office, and went to make ready for the ascent.

Whatever was coming down the mountain to meet her, it was no longer only Elodie.

The stone in the reliquary behind her pulsed again in the dark — twice, patient, like footsteps climbing.
