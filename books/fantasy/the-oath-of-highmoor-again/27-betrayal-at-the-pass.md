## 27. Betrayal at the Pass

Rennick had stolen things his whole life — purses, relics, a bishop's seal once, which was a long and funny story he'd never gotten to tell anyone sober — so he knew how theft worked. You learned the shape of a place. You learned where the weight sat.

And the weight of their little company had been sitting wrong for three days.

It was the messenger boy. Tam, the hedgewitch Odile had sent along with them as guide to the mountain road — sixteen, maybe seventeen, all elbows and earnest eyes, forever volunteering for first watch, forever asking questions that sounded like curiosity and landed like surveying. How many knights did Serane have? How fast could Ilyen walk? Which passes did Halvard know?

Rennick had spent twenty years reading locks. People were just locks with worse manners.

He didn't say anything at first. He told himself he was being paranoid, that a boy who asked too many questions was just a boy who asked too many questions, that Rennick's whole life had trained him to see traitors in every friendly face and half the time he'd been wrong and the other half it hadn't mattered anyway. But on the third night, camped in the black throat of a ravine below the pass, he watched Tam get up from his bedroll, quiet as smoke, and stand facing north for a long time.

Not looking at the stars. Looking for them.

*Signal fires*, Rennick thought. *Or lantern code, or some fool thing.* He drifted over, casual, hands in pockets, and said, "Can't sleep either?"

Tam started like a deer. "Just — checking the weather."

"Weather," Rennick said. "In the dark."

"The wind's changed." The boy's voice was steady enough. His feet weren't; they'd shifted, weight rolling toward escape before his brain caught up. "Storm coming down off the Godspine. Halvard says we should move at first light or lose the window."

"Did he." Rennick smiled the smile that had opened more doors than his picks ever had. "Halvard says a lot of things. Drunk old men are fountains."

"He's not drunk anymore," Tam said, and there was something sharp in it, something protective, and Rennick thought: *well, that's interesting*, because loyalty was a strange thing for a spy to fake, but it was also exactly what a clever handler would build into him, real affection wrapped around a rotten core, and now Rennick couldn't tell anymore, and not knowing was worse than knowing.

He went back to his bedroll and lay staring at the sky and did not sleep.

* * *

At dawn they climbed.

The pass above the ravine was called the Shepherd's Notch, and Halvard said it was the only way through the wall of the Godspine that didn't require wings or a death wish. He'd been different since leaving the inn — three weeks sober, moving like a man remembering his own body, and there were moments when you caught him looking at the mountains with an expression Rennick could only call homesickness, which made no sense, because Halvard had never lived here.

Except maybe he had. In the way that mattered.

"Gatehouse is ahead," Halvard said, breathing hard, pointing with his chin. "Old watchpost from the war. Sarel's people hold it — two men, maybe four, if she thinks anyone's fool enough to come this way in early winter."

"And if she does think that?" Elodie asked.

Halvard's mouth twisted. "Then it's not four men."

Ilyen rode on Elodie's back, wrapped in wool, her small face peeking over Elodie's shoulder. She hadn't spoken much since the river — since the ambush at the weir, since Rennick had taken a knight's blade meant for her through the meat of his side and woken up three days later with a scar that pulled when he breathed deep. She spoke to Veyra now, sometimes, in that old liturgical tongue, her lips moving while her eyes went somewhere else entirely. It scared him more than the knights did.

"How's the side?" Elodie asked him quietly.

"Lovely. Purple's very in this season."

"Rennick."

"It holds," he said. "Ask me again when someone's hitting me."

She almost smiled. That was the thing about Elodie Nakamura that nobody who hadn't traveled with her would believe: under all that iron was a woman who laughed easy when you earned it. He'd been trying to earn it since Corvassa. Some part of him had already decided, without consulting him, that he was going to keep earning it for a long time.

Which meant the thing he had to say next was going to cost him.

"We've got a problem," he said. "Walk with me."

They dropped back, out of earshot of Halvard and the boy. Elodie listened without interrupting while he laid it out — the watches volunteered, the questions, the standing in the dark facing north, the wind that hadn't changed.

"You're sure?" she said.

"I'm sure about the pattern. I'm not sure about the boy. Could be nothing. Could be a frightened kid doing what a witch told him to do without understanding it." He shrugged, winced at the pull in his side. "Could be exactly what it looks like."

Elodie was silent for ten steps. Then: "If we're wrong, and we accuse him, and he's innocent — we're four against a mountain, and we'll have cut our own rope."

"If we're right and we don't act—"

"I know." She rubbed her eyes. "I know. Give me until the gatehouse. If there's an Order garrison waiting there that Halvard didn't promise us, we'll know."

"That's a terrible plan."

"Yes," she agreed. "It's the only one I have."

* * *

The gatehouse came into view an hour later, squatting across the throat of the notch like a stoppered bottle: grey stone, slotted windows, a gate of iron-banded oak that had been closed for a generation. Halvard studied it through the snow-haze and frowned.

"One man on the wall," he said. "Maybe. Hard to tell. Should be more, if they knew we were coming."

"Should be?" Rennick said. "You said two to four."

"I said if she thought anyone was coming." Halvard's frown deepened. He looked at the gatehouse the way a man looks at a room he's walked into and can't name what's wrong with it. And Rennick watched him look, and watched the color change in the old man's face, slow as dawn, and understood that Halvard had found the wrong thing.

There wasn't a garrison in the gatehouse.

There was a garrison *behind* it. You could tell by the horses — a picket line visible past the eastern tower where the trees thinned, thirty mounts at least, steam rising off them, riders mounted and waiting. Positioned to sweep down on anyone who committed to the narrow approach, where there was no cover and no room to maneuver and no way back but the way they'd come.

"They're not holding the pass," Halvard said, very quietly. "They're holding the *mouth* of it. They want us inside."

Elodie turned. "Where's Tam?"

The question hit the air and hung there.

Because Tam was gone. His bedroll still lay by the fire-ring they'd left at the treeline an hour back, and his pack was gone with him, and the snow showed his tracks — fresh, unhurried, angling up the eastern slope toward the gatehouse's blind side, toward a postern door that Halvard would have known about and mentioned and Tam would have heard him mention.

"No," Elodie said. Just that. Like she could unsay it.

"Hedgewitch's messenger," Rennick said, and hated himself for the satisfaction in it, because there was no room for satisfaction here, only arithmetic. "Odile. Or someone leaning on Odile. Either way — he's been reporting our route since the shrine."

Halvard had gone very still. When he spoke, his voice had changed — stripped of the tavern warmth, stripped of everything except something old and cold and professional. "Then we don't go through the notch. There's another way. Harder. Three days north, the Pilgrim's Stair — my body hates the idea, but it can't be ambushed, because it can't be ridden, and Serane's knights can't climb it in armor."

"Three days," Elodie said. "With Ilyen. In winter."

"With me carrying her half the time," Rennick said.

"She can't be carried up the Stair. No one can. It's handholds and ledges and—" Halvard stopped. Rubbed his face. "Gods. Twenty years ago I'd have done it in a day and complained about the wine at the top."

Behind them, downslope, a horn blew. One long note, then two short ones. From the gatehouse, voices — orders being given, boots on stone. The trap wasn't springing yet. They were being given time to do the obvious thing: commit to the notch, hurry through the kill zone, die efficiently.

"They'll wait till we're halfway," Halvard said. "Cavalry from behind the gatehouse, archers off the walls. We'd last a minute. Two."

"So we fall back to the Stair."

"The Stair entrance is six miles north. We won't make it before dark, and they'll see us turn and ride us down in the open."

Rennick looked at the map in his head. Looked at the old drunkard who used to be an oath-bearer. Looked at Elodie, whose jaw had gone tight in the way it did when she was about to do something that would shorten her life.

"There's a third option," Halvard said.

"Don't," Elodie said.

"Somebody has to hold the gatehouse." Halvard said it lightly, the way he ordered ale. "Long enough for you to reach the Stair in the dark. The postern, the inner stair, the murder-hole gallery — I know every stone of it. I helped *build* the defenses, back when. One man in a gatehouse like that is worth twenty in the field, if the man is mean enough and knows the ground." He grinned, and it was the grin from the inn, the one over the spilled beer, except his eyes weren't in it at all. "And I am extremely mean."

"Halvard." Elodie's voice cracked on the second syllable. "No."

"Listen to me." He took her shoulders, and Rennick looked away, and then made himself look back, because some things you owed your eyes. "Twenty years I sat in that inn hating myself for running. For taking the silence and drinking it instead of fighting it. Tomas is dead because I was a coward at the wrong moment, and everything after has been interest on that debt." His voice steadied. "This isn't a sacrifice, girl. This is the first fair trade I've been offered in two decades. Let me take it."

"You don't get to decide—" She stopped. Breathed. Started again, lower. "We could find another way. We could—"

"There isn't one, and you know it, and Ilyen doesn't have three days." He touched her cheek, rough and brief, like a father fixing a collar. "Get her up the Stair. Get her to the Godspine. Finish it. And when you drink to me afterward — and you will, because you're the sentimental one — make it the good stuff. None of that valley swill."

Rennick cleared his throat. "For what it's worth, I've seen the good stuff in his cellar. It's terrible."

"See?" Halvard said. "Reasons."

* * *

They moved at dusk.

The plan was simple, which was the only kind that survived contact: Halvard would circle east through the trees, take the postern from the blind side — Tam's door, ironically, the boy's own shortcut turned against him — and seize the gatehouse from within. Noise, fire, chaos. Every eye in the garrison would fix on the gatehouse. And in that fixed attention, Elodie, Rennick, and Ilyen would slip wide along the western slope, six dark miles to the mouth of the Pilgrim's Stair, and be gone up it by midnight.

Rennick wanted to stay and fight beside him. He said so. Halvard told him that with his ribs he'd be less use than a warm coat, and that somebody had to carry the child, and that arguing with an oath-bearer on his own ground was a losing game. Then he'd hugged Rennick, quick and crushing, and whispered, "Watch her. Both of them. That's the job now," and shoved him away before Rennick could answer.

They watched him go — a big grey man moving soft through the pines, impossibly quiet for his size, and Rennick thought: *that's what he was, before the inn. That's what the silence took.*

They waited in the tree shadow as full dark fell. An hour. Two.

Then the gatehouse lit up.

Fire first — a bloom of orange from the eastern tower, Halvard's work, oil and pitch and a flint he must have carried twenty years for exactly this. Shouting. A bell, rung twice then silenced mid-swing. Silhouettes boiling onto the walls, torches streaming, and somewhere in the middle of it a big voice roaring defiance in a register that carried clear across the valley, taunting them, drawing them, *come on then, come on*—

"It's working," Elodie breathed. "Move. Now."

They moved. West slope, Ilyen on Rennick's back, her thin arms around his neck, her breath fogging past his ear. The snow squeaked under them. Behind, the gatehouse burned and fought, and the sound of it followed them like a tide — steel, screaming, Halvard laughing, actually *laughing*, and Rennick ran faster.

They were two miles out, the gatehouse a smudge of orange behind them, when the second horn blew.

Different note. Different direction. North.

Rennick stopped so hard Ilyen's chin knocked his shoulder. On the ridge ahead, against the stars, riders. A dozen of them. Not charging — sitting, waiting, torches hooded, positioned precisely across the line between them and the Stair.

Serane hadn't put all her strength in the trap. She'd kept a reserve for exactly this — for the clever party that refused to walk into the obvious kill box. And the only person who could have told her that this company was clever enough to refuse, that they'd try the west slope, that they'd need the Stair —

"Tam," Rennick said. "He gave her both ends."

Downslope, hoofbeats. Flankers climbing through the trees, closing the last gap. Elodie drew her sword, set herself in front of Ilyen, and Rennick got his knife out and hated everything about the math: two of them, wounded, a child, a dozen riders, and behind them the burning gatehouse where a good man was spending his life to buy them minutes they were about to waste.

Then the gatehouse exploded.

Not the fire — the *gate*. The great oak doors burst outward from inside, and Halvard came through them like an avalanche wearing a man, sword in one hand, torch in the other, and behind him the garrison's own powder store went up — he'd found it, gods only knew how, in the dark, in the fight — and the shockwave rolled across the valley and knocked two of the flanker riders from their saddles and every horse on the ridgeline screamed and bolted.

Halvard stood in the burning gateway, outlined in fire, and raised his sword to them — a salute, a wave, a farewell, all three.

An arrow took him through the chest.

He didn't fall. Of course he didn't fall. He turned back toward the gatehouse interior, toward the men pouring out of the smoke, and Rennick saw him set his feet the way a man sets them when he has decided the ground under them will be his last, and then the smoke and the riders and the dark closed over him, and the last thing that carried across the distance was that laugh.

"GO!" Elodie screamed, shoving Rennick upslope.

They ran through the hole the explosion had torn in the cordon — horses scattered, riders unhorsed, order dissolved into noise and flame — and Rennick ran with Ilyen clinging to him and Elodie cutting down the one rider who got close enough to matter, and they ran until the gatehouse was a glow behind the shoulder of the mountain and the sound of Halvard's last stand was lost under Rennick's own heartbeat.

At the foot of the Pilgrim's Stair, in the black beneath a cliff face that went up forever, Elodie finally stopped. She stood with her head bowed, sword point resting on stone, and her shoulders shook once, twice, and did not break, because they couldn't, because there wasn't time.

"He bought it," Rennick said hoarsely. "Whatever else happens — he bought it. Twenty years of debt, paid in full."

"I know." Her voice was ash. She sheathed the sword. Looked up the Stair, where the handholds vanished into snow and starless dark. "And now we spend it well. Every step. Come on."

Rennick lifted Ilyen onto his back. The child pressed her cheek against his neck, and he felt her speak — not words, not yet, just a small tremor, like a bell struck too softly to hear.

Above them, the Godspine waited. Below them, the mountain burned.

He started climbing.
