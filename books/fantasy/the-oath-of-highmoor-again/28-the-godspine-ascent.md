## 28. The Godspine Ascent

The storm found them at the tree line, as if it had been waiting there all along.

Elodie felt it before she saw it—a pressure change in her ears, a taste of iron on the wind—and she knew what it meant. Not weather. Weather didn't have intent. This was the mountain itself deciding whether they would be permitted to climb.

"Keep moving," she said, and her voice came out a rasp. She hadn't stopped talking in full sentences for two days. There wasn't breath to spare. "Don't stop unless I tell you."

Rennick grunted acknowledgment somewhere behind her. He'd been carrying most of the supplies since the pass—Halvard's pack, now, along with his own—and Elodie could hear the effort in every footstep he took. He didn't complain. She suspected that if she asked him about it later, he'd say something cynical and deflecting, because that was how Rennick handled everything: theft, wonder, grief. But right now he just climbed.

Ilyen was on her back.

The child weighed almost nothing, which frightened Elodie more than if she'd been heavy. Three days ago at the weir, an Order knight's blade had opened a long shallow cut across Ilyen's side—not deep enough to kill, deep enough to bleed, and the wound had refused to close since. The hedgewitch's salves were gone. Rennick's needlework held the edges together, but the skin around the stitches had gone hot and wrong, and when Elodie pressed her palm flat against it each morning, Ilyen made no sound at all. That silence scared her worse than crying would have.

So Elodie carried her. It wasn't a decision so much as an arithmetic: Ilyen couldn't walk the pace they needed; Elodie was the strongest climber among them; therefore Ilyen rode on Elodie's back with her thin arms hooked over her collarbone and her cheek against the back of her neck. The child's breath came warm and too fast against her ear. Every hour or so, Elodie reached up and touched one of those arms to make sure the grip was still there.

* * *

They climbed through the afternoon, and the afternoon went strange.

Light failed early under the storm, but the snow gave it back—the whole world turned a dim, sourceless grey, like being inside a closed fist. The wind came off the peak in long hard shoves, knocking them sideways, and Elodie learned to lean into it the way you lean into a living thing, giving ground and taking it back.

Her oath-mark burned.

It had been burning since the ward broke, since the day she'd walked out of Highmoor Valley and twenty years of silence had come flooding into her arm like water through a burst dam. She'd learned to live with the low ache of it. But here, this high, this close to whatever waited at the summit, the mark had stopped aching and started speaking. Pulses of heat ran down her forearm in rhythm—slow, patient, enormous, like something breathing underneath stone.

*Soon,* the pulse said. Or maybe that was just her own heart, translated.

She checked Ilyen again. The child's eyes were open, watching the peak through the blowing white, and there was something in her face that Elodie had never seen there before—not fear, not pain. Recognition. Like a traveler who has been walking toward a city all her life and finally sees its walls.

"You know what's up there," Elodie said. It wasn't really a question.

Ilyen's lips moved. No sound came out—or rather, sound came out but the wind ate it. She leaned closer to Elodie's ear, and her voice, when it arrived, was thin as thread: *"It knows we're coming."*

Elodie adjusted the child's weight and kept climbing.

* * *

They sheltered at dusk in a hollow beneath an overhang of black rock, and Elodie permitted herself thirty minutes of stillness, which felt like theft from some greater account.

Rennick built no fire. Firelight carried, and they both knew what else carried: Serane's riders were behind them. They'd seen the torches at the last switchback before dark, a strung necklace of small orange lights descending into the valley below, then rising again, patient as a tide. The Order didn't have to be fast. They only had to be certain. Halvard's death had bought them half a day, and half a day was already spent.

Elodie got Ilyen down off her back and propped her against the rock, and Rennick changed the dressing by touch, working blind in the grey dark with hands steadier than his voice.

"It's going bad," he said quietly. "I won't dress it up."

"I know."

"She needs a real healer. Or—" He stopped. They both heard the word he didn't say. *Or the Godspine.* Whatever waited at the summit, whatever the shard and the child and the covenant wanted, it was the only medicine left within reach. That was either hope or desperation, and Elodie had stopped trying to tell the difference three days ago, when Halvard died holding a gate so that a child he'd known for a week could live.

"How is she?" Elodie asked.

Rennick paused. "Ask her yourself. She's awake."

He was right. Ilyen's eyes were open in the dark, fixed on Elodie with that awful calm.

*"Tired,"* the child said, in her old language, and then, in common, halting and precious, the vocabulary she'd gathered like river stones on their long road: *"But. Almost. Home."*

Home. Elodie's throat tightened. A prison at the top of the world, and the child called it home, and the worst part—the part that kept Elodie lying awake in the cold while the others slept—was that Elodie understood her. She'd spent her whole life in Highmoor Valley calling it home, never knowing she lived inside the walls of a cage. Maybe home was just the place that shaped you. Maybe it didn't care whether it loved you back.

"Eat," Elodie said, and pressed dried meat and a heel of bread into the small hands. "All of it."

* * *

They climbed through the night.

There was no good reason to. Night climbing on scree in a blizzard was how people died on mountains, and Elodie knew it, and she did it anyway, because the arithmetic had changed: every hour they rested, the riders gained, and Ilyen's fever climbed with them. Elodie could feel the heat of it radiating off the child's body even through the layers between them, a small furnace strapped to her spine.

Somewhere past midnight, the oath-mark stopped pulsing and began to pull.

That was the only word she had for it. Heat became hunger; the mark tugged at her arm like a fish on a line, toward the peak, toward the summit, toward the source. And with the pulling came something else—fragments, impressions, not words but *near*-words, the way you can almost hear a conversation through a wall. Vastness. Patience. A sorrow so large it had stopped being an emotion and become a landscape.

Veyra. The god who had gone silent. The god who had never been silent at all, only sealed away by five sworn companions while a war raged and millions burned—one of whom had been Elodie herself, without knowing it. Her oath, spoken young and sincere beside four friends, had been a lock. Tomas had died learning that truth. Halvard had died confirming it. And now the lock was walking up the mountain to meet the door.

*I'm coming,* she thought at the pull, not knowing if the thought went anywhere. *Whatever you are. Whatever they did. I'm coming.*

The pull gentled, briefly, like a hand resting on her shoulder.

Then the wind screamed, and she climbed.

* * *

Dawn came as a lightening of the grey, nothing more. By its mercy Elodie saw the last stretch of the climb: a ridge like a knife's spine, wind-scoured nearly bare, running half a mile to the summit dome where the prison waited. She couldn't see the prison itself. The peak was wrapped in cloud. But she could see what stood below it, carved into the ridge's shoulder—

A gatehouse. Ruined, ancient, half-swallowed by ice, twin pillars leaning like drunkards. Halvard had told them about it with his dying breaths: *the old gate, the first seal's threshold, where the five once passed.* Beyond it, the path narrowed, and beyond that—

Torches. Moving ones. A line of riders dismounting at the gatehouse base, small with distance, spreading out with the unhurried discipline of people who have done this many times and expect to do it successfully.

"They're ahead of us," Rennick said. He'd come up beside her, chest heaving, ice in his eyebrows. "How in the hells are they ahead of us?"

"The other pass." Elodie's mind moved sluggishly, cold-slowed, but the answer surfaced anyway. "Halvard said there were two roads. We took the one he knew. They took the one he didn't."

Below, the searchers had found the party's trail. One of them raised a horn, and the sound came up the mountain thin and clear, a hunting note, and was answered from somewhere above—from the gatehouse, from the summit—until the whole peak rang with it.

Elodie looked at the ridge. Looked at the riders. Did the sums she'd been doing all night and didn't like any of them. Half a mile of exposed spine, in daylight, with wounded cargo, against soldiers who could wait for them at the narrow places.

"We could go around," Rennick said. He didn't sound like he believed it. "West face. Longer, but—"

"—three days, and she doesn't have three days." Ilyen had gone quiet again against her back, quieter than sleep. Elodie could feel the fever like a second heartbeat. "And the Order would find us anyway. No."

She shifted the child higher, settled the weight, and rolled her shoulders until something in her back cracked.

"So we don't go around," she said. "We go through."

* * *

The ridge was everything the valley had taught her and nothing she'd expected.

Wind hit them from the left in gusts strong enough to lift a grown man off his feet, and the trick—the trick she'd learned at seventeen, bounty-hunting ibex hunters in the high passes of Highmoor—was not to fight it but to time it. Wait for the gust to spend itself. Move during the lull. Freeze when the world roared. It was slow, maddening work, and it saved their lives, and Elodie fell into it the way she fell into old prayers: gratefully, mechanically, with the part of her that was tired beyond tired simply stepping aside and letting the body remember what it knew.

This was the thing nobody told you about exhaustion, she thought, somewhere in the middle of the crossing. It wasn't emptiness. It was *clarity*. Past a certain point, you stopped carrying everything—all the griefs, the guilts, the twenty years of questions—and what remained was simple and bright and terribly light. Move. Breathe. Hold the child. Reach the peak. Four things. She could do four things.

The oath-mark pulled, and she let it pull.

Halfway along the spine, the wind dropped all at once, the way a door closes, and in the sudden ringing silence Elodie heard voices below—shouting, orders, the scrape of boots on ice. The riders were starting up the ridge after them.

"Faster," she said to Rennick, and didn't look back.

They crossed the shoulder below the summit dome and the cloud swallowed them, and inside the cloud the world shrank to ten feet of visibility and the pull of the mark, which had become a constant insistent drag, like wading upstream. Elodie's legs shook. Her lungs burned with cold air that didn't seem to contain enough of anything. Twice she stumbled and caught herself on hands and knees in the snow, and twice she got up because Ilyen's arms were around her neck and that fact admitted no alternative.

The third time she fell, she didn't get up immediately.

She knelt in the snow, gasping, the child's weight sagging against her, and for one long moment her body offered her a bargain: *stay down. Just stay down. The snow is soft. The war is someone else's.*

And Ilyen stirred against her back. Small hands slid down from her shoulders and around, and found Elodie's clenched fists in the snow, and covered them—the child kneeling behind her, cheek pressed to her spine, holding on. No words. None needed. Just the grip, and the fever-heat of her, and the stubborn animal fact of her continued existence, which had cost Halvard his life at the gate and Rennick his blood at the weir and Maren her chapel and Serane, maybe, her soul.

*You don't get to stop,* the grip said. *Not while I'm holding on.*

"Right," Elodie said out loud, to no one, to everyone. "Right."

She got up.

* * *

They came out of the cloud at the summit, and Elodie forgot, for one full stride, how to breathe.

The prison didn't look like a prison. It looked like a mountain wearing a crown: a ring of standing stones taller than chapel spires, black and seamless, arranged in a perfect circle around a hollow of packed snow. Frost crawled up the stones in patterns too regular to be natural—spirals, oaths, script in a language older than kingdoms, the language Ilyen spoke in her sleep. And above the circle, where sky should have been, there was nothing. A absence shaped like a hole, a column of pure un-light, going up forever.

The silence of the god. Made visible. Twenty years of it, hanging over the peak like a held breath.

At the ring's edge, between two stones, stood figures in white-and-grey—Order knights, a dozen of them, drawn up in formation. And before them, alone, hands folded, waiting, a tall woman whose face Elodie had known for thirty years and hated for three days.

Sarel.

She didn't look surprised. She looked like a woman attending the inevitable, and she raised her voice—not shouting, just projecting, the way you speak across a grave—to carry over the wind:

"Elodie Nakamura. You brought the vessel to me. That ends the pursuit, and the valley lives, and the girl dies quickly and cleanly. Or you turn back now, and we take her tomorrow, and the valley burns regardless. Those are your choices. I'm sorry they're poor ones. I've had the same ones for twenty years."

Behind her, faintly, from far down the ridge: horns. Serane's riders, closing.

Elodie stood at the edge of the cloud-line with a dying child on her back and a dead friend's pack on Rennick's shoulders and an oath-mark pulling her forward like a hook behind her sternum, and she looked at the circle of stones, at the hole in the sky, at the woman she might become if she ever stopped choosing.

Her legs were shaking. Her lungs were ruined. Every part of her wanted to lie down in the snow and be finished.

Instead she shifted Ilyen higher on her back, set her eyes on the gap between the two nearest stones, and started walking.

"Sarel," she called, and her voice came out steady, which surprised her, which pleased her. "You should know—I'm not here for your choices."

The pull of the mark became a roar. The frost-script on the stones began to glow.

"I'm here to break them all."
