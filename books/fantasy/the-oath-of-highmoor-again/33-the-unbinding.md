## 33. The Unbinding

The chamber at the heart of the Godspine was not silent.

I had thought it would be. I had thought a prison built to hold a god would be quiet as a tomb, sealed and airless and dead. But the silence of Veyra was not an absence. It was a presence — a held breath two thousand years long, pressing against the walls from the inside.

I stood on a floor of white stone that was warm beneath my bare feet. Warm, like skin. The walls curved away above me into darkness shot through with veins of light, and I understood, in the way I understood my own name, that this was not architecture at all. There were no walls. There was only Veyra, folded around itself so many times it had become a place.

*You came,* said the god.

Not with sound. Not even with words, exactly. With knowing. It poured into me the way water pours into a cup, and for the first time since Elodie found me shivering on her doorstep with a shard of stone in my fist, I did not feel small.

*I came,* I answered, and my voice worked here, in both tongues, the old one and the new one, and they were the same tongue in this place.

Behind me, at the mouth of the chamber, I could hear the battle — steel ringing on steel, Rennick shouting, the crack and flare of Elodie's oath-magic burning through her like a wick through candle-fat. I could hear Serane's soldiers fighting beside us now, men and women who had turned their coats in a single breath when their captain fell. I heard Sarel's voice rising over all of it, cold and clear as ice cracking, calling down the unmaking words.

I could not help them. That was the truth I had to make peace with, standing there with my hand pressed flat against the god's warm stone flesh. Whatever happened out there, my part was here. My whole life had been here, walking toward this room without knowing it. Every step of the road, every shrine we passed, every night Elodie sat awake watching over me — all of it was the long approach to this floor.

*I know what you are going to do,* Veyra said.

*Then you know why.*

*I know what it will cost you.*

I looked up into the veined dark. *Everything costs something. You taught the world that. Or your faithful did, in your name, while you screamed where no one could hear.*

The light in the walls flickered. Grief, I realized. A god's grief moves like weather.

*Child,* Veyra said, and the word wrapped around me like a blanket pulled up by someone who loved me, *I have been alone in the dark for twenty years of your counting, and longer than kingdoms before that. I have had nothing to do but think about what should be done with me. Do you know what I concluded?*

I shook my head.

*That it does not matter what should be done with me.* The warmth of the floor pulsed once, slow as a heartbeat. *It matters what is done by. Every covenant ever sworn has been a chain with a pretty clasp. Swear me back into the world, and I become your masters' master again, however gently you hold the leash. Free me outright, and I am a storm with no riverbank. I have lain here learning humility the hard way, little vessel. I no longer trust anyone's hands with my power. Including mine.*

*Then don't trust hands,* I said. *Trust everyone.*

The chamber went very still. Even the distant battle seemed to lean in.

*Say that again,* the god said, and its voice had changed — sharper now, hungry, the hunger of a mind that has been circling a locked door for centuries and just heard the key turn somewhere inside itself.

*Don't put your power in anyone's hands,* I said, and the words came faster now because I could feel them assembling themselves out of everything Maren taught me, everything the shard sang to me on the road, everything I was grown from. *Put it in the ground. In the rain. In the blood and breath of every living thing. No oath. No covenant. No five bearers, no Order, no valley-ward, no vessel. Dissolve it. Scatter it so fine that no one can gather it, and give it to everyone so no one can hoard it. Hold it in trust — not by kings or priests or sworn swords. By all of them. By every mortal who draws breath.*

*They will misuse it,* Veyra said.

*Yes.*

*They will hurt each other with it.*

*They already do. They hurt each other with sticks and stones and lies. Power doesn't teach cruelty; hoarding does.* I pressed my palm harder against the warm stone. *Sarel kept you locked away because she believed five people could be trusted with a god and millions could not. Halvard drank himself blind because he believed he'd failed a god instead of failing people. The Order burns villages because it believes duty belongs to the few. Everyone who ever swore an oath to you ended up either broken or cruel, and most of them both. Maybe oaths are the problem. Maybe the answer was never a better oath. Maybe the answer is no oath at all.*

Silence. But it was a different silence now — the silence of a mind working, vast gears turning in the deep of the mountain, weighing two thousand years of thought against one child's impossible arithmetic.

*And you?* Veyra asked at last, softly. *You were made for this. Woven from my last act of will, grown in secret, sent down the mountain as a living key. If I dissolve the covenant, what are you?*

I had known the question was coming. I had felt it waiting for me all the way up the Godspine, patient as winter.

*I don't know,* I admitted. *Maybe nothing. Maybe I unravel with the rest of it. Maybe I finally get to just be a girl who likes bread with honey and is afraid of thunder.* My throat tightened. *But Ilyen was never real anyway, was she? Ilyen was a shape you gave your purpose so someone would carry it. So if the purpose ends —*

*Stop,* the god said.

The light in the veins flared white.

*Do not finish that sentence. I have watched you since the day you were woven. I have watched you sleep in ditches and laugh at Rennick's terrible songs and stand between Elodie and drawn blades with nothing but your small body and your stubborn heart. You think I made a tool and dressed it as a child.* The warmth under my feet trembled. *No, little one. It went the other way. I reached for a purpose and found you. You are not what I made. You are what I hoped for. And hope is the one thing in creation I refuse to spend.*

Something broke open in my chest. I didn't have a word for it. Maybe there wasn't one in any language, old or new.

*Then let's do it together,* I whispered. *Not a sacrifice. Not a sealing. A giving. Both of us letting go at the same time.*

*Both of us,* Veyra agreed, and for the first time in all the long ages of its imprisonment, the god laughed — a low rolling sound like spring thaw moving down a mountainside. *Oh, child. Oh, stubborn, impossible gift. Very well. Take the shard. Speak the rite as I taught it to your makers, and then break it, the way only you can. And whatever happens after — whatever becomes of either of us — remember this: I was never silent. I was listening. All this time, I was listening to the world, and it was more beautiful than anything I ever ruled.*

I drew the shard from the pouch at my belt.

It had changed on the climb. Once it had been a splinter of pale stone no bigger than my thumb, humming against Elodie's oath-mark. Now it blazed like a captive star, and I could feel the whole covenant running through it — every ward, every binding, every thread of the great web the five oath-bearers had woven across the world. I could feel Highmoor Valley at the center of it, the prison cell I had been born to keep. I could feel Elodie's mark, the lock's last living tooth, burning on her arm three chambers below.

I raised the shard above my head.

Outside, the battle roared. Sarel's voice climbed to a shriek as the unmaking words tore at the chamber door, and I heard Rennick cry out, and I heard Elodie answer him with a roar that had nothing human left in it, and I knew they were losing, knew they might all die in the next hundred heartbeats, and none of it mattered, and all of it mattered, and I spoke.

I spoke the First Covenant.

Not to bind — to unbind. I took the ancient liturgy, the words older than any kingdom, the words Maren had recognized in fragments and the archive had preserved in full, and I walked backward through them. Every vow became its own undoing. Every clause of captivity opened like a flower into release. The language poured out of me in a torrent, my mouth shaping sounds no living throat had made since the war that ended the age of wonders, and the shard drank the words and grew brighter and brighter until I could no longer see the chamber, only light, only warmth, only the god's heartbeat and my own beating together like two hands clasped.

At the rite's center — the hinge, the point where the original five had turned freedom into a cage — I stopped.

This was the moment. This was where Sarel and her four companions had spoken the sealing word. This was where I was meant to choose: speak it forward, and reforge the covenant with myself as its eternal prisoner-vessel; speak it reversed, and crack the prison open and loose a god's full wrath on a world half-ready to receive it.

Instead, I did neither.

I set the shard against my chest, over my heart, and I said — in plain common speech, the language Elodie had taught me one hard-won word at a time on the road north:

"You belong to no one."

The shard shattered.

Not into fragments. Into everything. Into light that poured out of me and through the walls and down through the roots of the Godspine and out into the world beyond, into soil and river and rain and the blood of every sleeping farmer and marching soldier and fishing-boat child from the Weir to the western sea. I felt it go — felt the covenant's great web dissolve thread by thread, each strand becoming ten thousand smaller strands too fine for any hand, mortal or divine, to gather. I felt Highmoor Valley's ward come apart like morning fog, and somewhere far south I knew Elodie's oath-mark cooled and faded, though she wouldn't understand why yet. I felt the Order's authority gutter out mid-word, Sarel's unmaking spell dying on her lips as the power behind it scattered into the wind.

And I felt Veyra rise.

The chamber unfolded. The walls that were not walls spread outward and upward and became sky, became wings, became a presence so vast that the mountain itself seemed suddenly small and temporary, a pebble the god had been sitting on. Light without color filled the peak. Warmth without weight. And through it all ran something I had not expected, something that undid me completely:

Relief. Bottomless, oceanic relief, the relief of a held breath finally released, of a scream finally finished, of a prisoner stepping blinking into daylight and finding it kind.

*Oh,* Veyra said, to no one, to everyone, to the world entire. *Oh, look at it. Look at all of it still going on.*

I collapsed to my knees on the cooling stone. My body felt hollowed and remade, lighter than air and heavy as lead at once, and I did not know if I was dying or being born, and I found, kneeling there in the ruin of the oldest covenant in the world, that I did not much care which.

Then arms closed around me, and I was lifted off the ground, and a voice I knew better than my own heartbeat was saying my name over and over — *Ilyen, Ilyen, Ilyen* — rough and ragged and alive.

Elodie. Bloodied, burned, her left sleeve soaked red, her face streaked with ash and tears, holding me like she would never let go.

"You did it," she said into my hair. "Whatever it was — you did it, sweetheart, you did it—"

"Where's Sarel?" I asked. "Rennick?"

"Rennick's breathing. Barely." She pulled back to look at me, and her eyes went wide, and her grip tightened. "Ilyen — your eyes."

I couldn't see them. But I could feel what she saw: the light still draining out of me, the last of the covenant's glow leaving my body like a tide going out. When it finished, when the final spark faded, I would be — what? I reached inside myself, searching for the ancient knowing, the second tongue, the purpose that had always hummed beneath my skin.

Gone. Quiet. Just a girl's ordinary mind, ordinary and new-minted and terrified and free.

"Ilyen," Elodie said again, urgent now. "What's happening to you?"

Above us, the vast presence paused at the threshold of the world — a god standing in an open door, looking back at the house it had left. I felt its attention settle on me one last time, gentle as snowfall.

And Veyra, unchained, unbowed, belonging to no one, said:

*She is exactly what she always was. Tell her I said thank you. And tell her—*

But the rest of it was lost, because the mountain chose that moment to begin coming down around our ears, and Elodie threw me over her shoulder and ran.
