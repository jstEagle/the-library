## 34. Veyra Unchained

The light did not arrive. It *unmade*.

One moment the prison chamber was stone and storm and blood, the next it was nothing but presence — a pressure behind the world, a held breath twenty years long finally released. Elodie felt her knees buckle, felt Rennick catch her arm, felt the whole mountain groan around them like a ship in a rising sea.

Then Veyra rose.

There were no words for what she saw, so her mind gave her the nearest shapes it had: a figure of white fire tall as the valley itself, shoulders that brushed the vaulted dark of the sky, a face like dawn breaking over water. The god filled the chamber and then outgrew it, passing through walls of granite as though they were mist, standing upon the peak with its feet among the clouds and its head somewhere above the weather, above the stars themselves.

And it was grieving.

Elodie had imagined this moment a hundred ways across twenty silent years — rage, judgment, the cold accounting of an abandoned servant. She had never once imagined sorrow. But sorrow poured off the god like heat from a forge, and every living thing on that mountainside felt it. The wind died. The snow hung suspended, unwilling to fall into such sadness. Even Sarel's knights, scattered and broken across the lower terraces, had stopped fighting. Some had fallen to their knees. Some were weeping without knowing why.

*I am seen,* the silence said. Not in words. In certainty. It moved through Elodie's bones the way her oath-mark used to, warm and absolute. *After all this time. I am seen.*

"Veyra." Elodie's voice came out cracked and small. She stood anyway. Beside her, Ilyen swayed, grey-faced, drained nearly empty by the rite, and Elodie put a hand under the child's arm and held her up by sheer stubbornness. "We see you."

The god's attention settled on her like a hand the size of the horizon. She did not flinch from it. Twenty years of silence, four dead companions' worth of grief, a valley's worth of lies — all of it lay open between them now, and there was no more hiding from either direction.

*You kept faith,* Veyra said, and the words broke something loose in her chest. *When I could not answer. When they told you I had chosen silence. You kept faith.*

"I kept *nothing*," Elodie said, and heard her own voice shake with fury she hadn't known was still in her. "I knelt at a dead altar for twenty years while you sat sealed beneath my own home. Tomas died believing you'd forsaken him. Halvard drank himself hollow. Serane—"

She stopped. Serane's body lay back down the pass somewhere, struck down by the woman she'd once loved for the crime of hesitating. The grief of it hit her sideways, fresh and raw, and she let it. The god deserved her anger unfiltered. That much courtesy, at least.

*Yes,* Veyra said. *Grieve. I have twenty years of it myself.*

*And the old war?* The thought came not from Elodie but from the mountain itself, from the covenant-threads unraveling through the air — she could feel them now, could feel everything, the rite having opened some door in her that would never fully close. The question the whole realm was asking. *Does the war resume?*

The god was quiet for a long moment. Snow began falling again, slowly, like ash.

*That is the question,* Veyra said. *Is it not.*

* * *

They came up onto the peak one by one while the god considered.

Rennick first, limping, his bandaged side soaked through again, refusing help with a snarl that fooled no one. Then a handful of Serane's soldiers who had thrown down their blades when the Order's banner burned — they clustered at the chamber mouth, weapons still in reach, faces turned upward like men watching a second sun. Maren had stayed below with the wounded. Halvard was buried under the collapsed gatehouse. There would be no burying Serane; the Order would do that, or the snow would.

Sarel was gone. Walked into the collapsing prison before any of them could reach her, and the mountain had taken her, and Elodie found she could not even hate her for it. Pity was heavier than hate. It weighed more going down.

Now they all waited on the roof of the world while a god decided whether their grandparents' war would come again.

Elodie knew the shape of that war from fragments — archive pages, Rennick's stolen knowledge, the things Halvard had confessed with a bottle in his fist. Before the sealing, Veyra's power had run unbound through mortal hands, and mortal hands had made of it a weapon. Kingdoms had drowned. Cities had burned in fires that burned green. The five oath-bearers had ended it the only way they could see: by locking the god away and building a lie called the Silent Faith on top of the lock. Millions lived because of it. One god had suffocated because of it. Both things true. Both things unbearable.

*If I rise,* Veyra said, and its voice was the creak of glaciers, *the old claims wake with me. Every crown that swore by me will want my favor. Every hand that touched my power will reach for it again. You know this. Your thief knows this.*

Rennick shifted beside her. "Yeah," he said hoarsely. "I know this. I've read how it ends. Twice."

*And yet you unbound me anyway.*

"We unbound the *covenant*," Elodie said. "What you do with your freedom is yours. That was the whole point."

*Ilyen.*

The name moved through the air like a physical thing. The child stiffened against Elodie's side.

The god's gaze came down — vast, terrible, tender — and found the small mud-streaked girl who had walked out of a ward-wall and spoken a language older than kingdoms. Who had carried the covenant in her body since before she had a name. Who had just spent herself nearly to nothing dissolving it.

*You are my last work,* Veyra said. *Did they tell you?*

"They told me something," Ilyen said. Her voice was thin and steady. "Tell me the rest."

*When I saw what my faithful meant to do — the sealing — I could not stop them. Five oaths woven together are stronger than a god's single will; that is the cruelty of faith, that it can be used against its object. So I spent what will remained to me on one final act. Not a weapon. Not an escape. A child. Grown from my last free thought, seeded into the world beyond the ward, carrying everything I am in a shape too small for anyone to fear.* A pause, vast as winter. *I made you to be loved, little one. That was all. That was the whole design. Because I thought — if nothing else of me survives the cage — let something of mine be loved.*

The peak was absolutely silent. Even the wind had stopped pretending.

Ilyen's chin crumpled. For a moment she was only a child, eight years old and exhausted and hearing, for the first time, why she existed. Then she wiped her face with her sleeve, the way Elodie had taught her, brisk and unimpressed, and looked up at the towering brightness.

"You made me so someone would love me," she said. "Someone did. That part worked."

Elodie's throat closed. She put her hand on the child's head and could not speak, and did not need to. The god saw. Gods, it turned out, saw everything.

*Ilyen,* Veyra said gently. *The war. If I stay, it returns. Perhaps not tomorrow. Perhaps not in my choosing — but power calls to power, and mortals are mortals. If I go — truly go, beyond the world's edge where no oath can reach — the magic remains. Unowned. Held in trust by all, claimed by none, as your rite decreed. But I will never come back. Not to answer prayers. Not to punish the wicked. Never again a god among you.*

"That sounds," Rennick said quietly, "like every good bargain ever struck. Terrible terms. No refunds."

"You're asking her," Elodie realized suddenly. She stared up at the burning vastness. "You're asking *her*. The fate of the realm, and you're putting it in the hands of an eight-year-old."

*No,* Veyra said. *I am asking the only being in creation who was made entirely of love and nothing else. If she says stay, I stay, and we face the war together. If she says go, I go, and I trust what I leave behind. My faithful built cages. Let my last work build the door.*

Every eye on the peak turned to the child.

Ilyen looked at the god for a long time. Then at Elodie. Then at Rennick, grey and stubborn and alive. Then out, past the clouds, toward the south, toward a valley that had been a prison cell and a home both, where a priestess was tending the wounded and waiting.

"You gave me to the world," she said finally, "so I'd be loved instead of feared." Her voice wavered, steadied. "If you stay, everyone will fear you again. And I'd rather be an orphan with a family than a god's daughter with a war." She swallowed. "Go. We'll hold what you left. All of us. Together."

The silence stretched so long Elodie's heart began to break —

— and then Veyra laughed.

It was not a sound. It was spring. It was the first thaw after the longest winter in memory, rolling down the mountain, through the valleys, across the whole sleeping realm, and Elodie felt it pass through her and knew that every field, every river, every grieving heart from here to the southern sea felt it too.

*Then I go,* the god said. *Blessed, unbowed, and believed in. The best of endings.*

It began to diminish — not shrinking but *departing*, folding its vastness inward like a sail coming down, and as it went it touched each of them. A warmth across Rennick's wound that made him gasp. A weight lifted from the soldiers at the terrace edge, who sagged as if set down after carrying stones for years. A brush against Elodie's chest, where the oath-mark sat fading to a pale scar, and in that touch was twenty years of answered-but-unheard prayers, every one of them received, every one of them mourned.

Last it knelt — the notion of a god kneeling should have been absurd, and was not — before Ilyen.

*Be loved,* it said. *That is all I ever asked of anything.*

And it was gone.

Not dead. Gone — past the world's hem, beyond the reach of any oath or altar, into whatever waits outside the story. The light withdrew like a tide. The storm returned, ordinary and clean. The stars came out overhead, hard and cold and beautiful, and for the first time in Elodie's life the magic in the air belonged to no one.

She sank down onto the snow. Ilyen folded into her arms, already half-asleep, worn hollow by the rite and the farewell. Rennick sat heavily beside them, hissing through his teeth, laughing wetly at nothing.

"So," he managed. "We just fired a god. Anybody else need a drink?"

Above them, the mountain stood quiet. Below, far to the south, a valley that had been a cage for twenty centuries lay open under the same stars, waiting.

Elodie looked down at her daughter — because that was the word now, the only word — asleep against her shoulder, breathing, alive, free.

"Home," she said. "First, home."
