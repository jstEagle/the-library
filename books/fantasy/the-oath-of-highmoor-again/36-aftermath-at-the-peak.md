## 36. Aftermath at the Peak

Dawn came up over the Godspine like something being born slowly and against its will.

Elodie sat on a slab of shattered stone at the mountain's crown, her back against what had been, until last night, part of the prison's outer wall. The rock was still warm in places where power had passed through it. Below her feet, the peak itself breathed — long, slow exhalations of dust and light rising from the wound where the covenant had been unmade. Every few minutes a tremor rolled through the stone, gentler than the last, as if the mountain were settling into sleep.

She should have been watching the passes. Old habit said so. But there was no one left to come for them. Sarel was gone. Serane's knights had thrown down their arms or fled down the western switchbacks when Veyra rose, and whatever remained of the Order of the Quiet would take weeks to understand what it had lost. For the first time in twenty years, no enemy was climbing toward her.

The silence where her oath used to live was enormous.

She kept reaching for it without meaning to. All her life there had been that second pulse under her own — the mark on her forearm humming with borrowed divinity, warm as a coal carried from someone else's fire. She'd hated it sometimes. Resented it. Sworn at it in the dark after Tomas died. But it had never once been *absent*, and now she kept touching her wrist like a woman checking for a pulse that wasn't hers to lose.

Where the mark had been there was only a scar: a thin silver line coiling twice around her forearm like a snake grown old and tired. It caught the dawnlight and gave nothing back.

"You keep doing that," Rennick said behind her.

He came around the broken wall carrying two tin cups of melted snow, steeped with the last of his tea leaves, which he'd been saving for a celebration he'd clearly expected to be more festive. His left arm was bound tight against his chest — Odile's poultices and three days of healing would mend the weir-wound, Maren's letters had promised, but not quickly, and not without pain. He lowered himself onto the slab beside her with a grunt and handed her a cup.

"Doing what."

"Touching it. Like you're afraid someone stole it." He blew across his own cup. "You didn't steal anything. You spent it. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"Yes." He sipped. "I've stolen plenty. Nobody ever thanked me after. You'll notice an entire god bowed its head to you before it left."

She hadn't told him that part. Hadn't told anyone. But Ilyen had been there, and children repeat everything, and besides — some things you couldn't witness and keep quiet about forever.

Veyra had risen out of the broken prison like a season changing. Not a body, exactly. A presence vast enough that the blizzard itself had stopped mid-fall, every snowflake hanging in the air like held breath, while something older than kingdoms looked down at the four small figures standing in the ruin of its cage. Elodie remembered thinking, with absurd clarity: *so this is what Tomas died facing.* And then Veyra had turned its attention to Ilyen — to the child it had made from its final act of will, grown in a valley ward like a seed planted in the dark — and everything vast and terrible in it had simply… softened. Grieved. Let go.

*You chose,* it had seemed to say, though no words crossed the air. *So I choose.*

And it had gone. Out beyond the world, wherever gods go when they stop being needed. The snow had finished falling. Dawn had started coming.

"I keep expecting to feel guilty," Elodie said. "For letting it go. For breaking the covenant instead of renewing it. Twenty years I thought restoring the oath was the holiest work left in the world. Now it's done, and undone, and I just feel—" She searched for the word.

"Tired," Rennick offered.

"Lighter. Which is worse, somehow."

"No, it isn't." He nudged her boot with his. "It's just new. Give it time to become ordinary. That's all peace is, in my experience. Grief and relief that got bored of fighting each other."

Below them, at the edge of the ruined gatehouse, someone had laid out the dead.

They had done it in the hour before dawn, working by lantern-light because none of them could bear to wait for morning. Three bodies, side by side, each covered with what they had: Halvard under his old oath-cloak, the one he'd kept folded in a chest behind the bar of the Drowned Bell for fifteen years and swore he'd burned; Serane under the white-and-silver of the Order, stripped of rank-cord, her sword laid flat on her breast; and between them, wrapped in Rennick's spare shirt because they had nothing better, the messenger.

Wren, the boy had been called. Odile's messenger, her eyes and ears on the road — and Sarel's mole in their company since the hedgewitch's door. He had opened the pass to Serane's knights with a signal-lamp and a handful of coin, and he had been sixteen years old, and he had wept when they took him, and Serane's crossbowmen had shot him anyway, because that was what orders did to mercy.

Elodie had wanted to hate him. She had stood over his body in the grey light trying very hard to, and failed. He'd been frightened and bought and abandoned, same as half the people she'd ever known. Same as her, if you squinted at it wrong.

"Halvard held the gate alone for six minutes," Rennick said quietly. He'd counted. Of course he'd counted; the man catalogued everything, grief included. "I timed it. Six minutes against twelve knights, drunk or sober. I'd have lasted forty seconds."

"He'd have argued the number down. Said he got lucky."

"He did get lucky. He died fast." Rennick stared at the three shrouded shapes. "Serane didn't."

No. Serane hadn't. Elodie closed her eyes and saw it again, the way she suspected she would see it every night for a long time: Sarel's blade taking her former companion through the heart in the instant Serane's own sword wavered — the instant love finally outweighed duty by a single heartbeat, too late by exactly that much. Sarel had not even looked angry. She had looked like a woman correcting an error in a ledger.

And then, hours later, Sarel had refused Elodie's hand at the prison's edge, refused mercy, refused everything, and walked back into the collapsing dark beneath the peak rather than live in a world with no covenant left to serve.

Three deaths. One traitor, one convert, one true believer. Elodie had killed none of them and lost all of them, and she could not make the arithmetic sit still.

"They're going to want to bury them different ways," Rennick said. "Ilyen wants to sing for Halvard. Something in the old tongue. Maren sent word the villagers will come up the mountain road by noon with spades and a cart, gods help them, walking all that way for us." He paused. "Nobody's claimed Serane yet."

"I will," Elodie said.

Rennick glanced at her but, to his credit, asked nothing.

Footsteps crunched on frost behind them, and Ilyen came around the wall with Maren's heavy shawl dragged over her shoulders like a cloak of state. She was nine years old and yesterday she had dissolved a covenant older than civilization, and this morning she was nine years old again, hair full of snow, dragging the shawl because it was warm and because dragging things was what shawls were for.

She climbed onto the slab between them without asking and looked out at the sunrise with the frank, unimpressed expression she reserved for natural wonders.

"It's quieter," she said.

"What is?" Rennick asked.

"Everything. In here." She tapped her own chest, right over the place where the shard of white stone used to hum against her skin. The shard was gone — spent, dissolved into the rite, into her, into the world. "Before, I always heard it. Like a bell rung under water. Him. Asking. Waiting." She frowned, searching for words that the old language had for feelings common speech didn't. "Now it's just me in here. It's strange. Good-strange. Like taking off boots you forgot you were wearing."

Elodie put her arm around the girl and pulled her close, and Ilyen allowed it, which was its own kind of liturgy.

"Are you sad he's gone?" Elodie asked. It was the question she'd been circling since the peak went quiet, and she'd been afraid of the answer.

Ilyen considered it seriously, head tilted, the way she considered everything.

"He's not gone gone. He's just not *here* anymore. Those are different." She patted Elodie's scarred arm with a small cold hand. "Like your oath. It's not gone gone either. I can see it."

Elodie went still. "See what?"

"The shape of it. Under the skin. Where it used to burn." Ilyen shrugged, matter-of-fact, terrible. "It didn't leave. It just stopped being his. It's yours now. What you did with it — choosing the third thing, refusing both doors — that was an oath too. Maybe the first real one anyone ever swore to him." She wrinkled her nose. "He liked that one best. He said so. Before."

"Before he left."

"Before." Ilyen leaned her head against Elodie's shoulder. "He said the valley gets to open now. All the wards everywhere. Magic will come back to Highmoor like water finding an old riverbed. He said—" she yawned enormously, the exhaustion of miracles catching up with her at last, "—he said you should go home and see it."

She was asleep within a minute, the way only children and soldiers can sleep after the end of the world, her breath slow against Elodie's ribs.

Rennick watched her for a while, then said, softly: "Well. That settles the argument about whether we're going back."

"There was an argument?"

"There's always an argument. I was going to argue we go somewhere warm with wine and no mountains. Corvassa has excellent wine. But apparently the god himself issued travel directions, so." He raised his cup in mock surrender. "Highmoor it is. The valley with no magic in it, which as of yesterday is a lie, and probably the most magical place left in the world, which is a sentence I'd have laughed at a month ago."

Elodie looked south, past the smoking ruin of the gatehouse, down the long spine of the world toward home. Somewhere down there was a cottage with a leaky roof and a bounty board with her name on three notices. Somewhere down there was a chapel to rebuild and shrines to restore and a priestess who had kept a forbidden faith alive for thirty years alone. Somewhere down there, magic was already seeping back into soil that had never known it — and she found, testing the thought the way you test a bruise, that she wasn't afraid of it.

That was the strangest gift of all. Twenty years she had stayed in the valley because the valley was safe, because the valley was sealed, because outside those boundaries lived a world that could kill her in ways she couldn't fight. She had told herself it was duty. It had been fear wearing duty's coat.

She wasn't afraid anymore. Not of the world, not of the magic, not of the wide open future with its infinite ways to fail. Grief had burned off whatever was flammable in her, and what remained was simple and hard and hers.

"We bury them first," she said. "All three. Together. Wren too — he was somebody's son, and he was ours for three weeks, and that counts for something. Then we walk home."

"Together?" Rennick said, and there was nothing mocking in it at all.

"Together."

The sun cleared the eastern peaks then, and light poured down into the Godspine's broken crown, and far below on the mountain road Elodie saw them coming — a thin line of figures climbing through the snow with spades over their shoulders and lanterns still lit against the dawn, the whole stubborn village of Highmoor come to carry its dead home.

At their head, unmistakable even at that distance, walked Priestess Maren, and beside her, leaning on a farmwife's arm, gray-faced and furious at her own body for failing her, came Odile the hedgewitch — who had sold them out through her messenger and then, when she learned what her coin had bought, had set out to climb a mountain at seventy years old to beg forgiveness in person.

Rennick saw her too. He winced. "Oh, that's going to be a conversation."

"That's going to be a funeral," Elodie said, standing, lifting Ilyen easily onto her hip despite every bruise she owned. "The conversation comes after. If she earns it."

"And does she?"

Elodie watched the tiny figure struggle upward through the snow, refusing help, cursing the cold with a fluency that would have impressed a barracks.

"We'll see," she said. "We're all of us traitors to something. The question is what we do about it after."

She turned her face to the light and felt, where the oath-mark used to burn, the scar give one faint, answering warmth — like a coal remembered rather than carried — and started down the mountain toward home.
