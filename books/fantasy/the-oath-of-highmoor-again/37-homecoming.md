## 37. Homecoming

The road into Highmoor Valley looked the same, and that was the miracle of it.

Elodie had braced for ruin. She'd spent the last hundred miles cataloguing what the world's unraveling might have done to the one place she'd ever called home — burned fields, collapsed wards, the chapel a cinder scar where Maren's congregation once sang. But the valley opened before them green and whole, the river running silver through its spine, the terraced orchards heavy with late apples. The same valley she'd ridden out of in autumn with a mute child on her saddle and no plan worth the name.

Except now there was something *in* the air.

She felt it before she could name it — a pressure at her oath-mark like a hand pressed flat against an old scar. The mark had faded to a pale ghost of itself since the Godspine, since Veyra rose and departed and the covenant dissolved into nothing any priest or king could hold. She'd assumed the ache was grief. It wasn't.

"Elodie." Rennick rode close, his voice low. "You feel that?"

"Yeah."

"What is it?"

She watched a hawk turn above the ridgeline, and as it banked, the light bent around it — just slightly, just wrong, the way light bends through water. Magic. Loose, unchained, belonging to no oath and no god. Drifting over the valley like pollen.

"It's the ward," she said. "It's gone."

Ilyen stirred against her back. The girl was nine years old and carried more years than that in her eyes — the weight of being grown from a god's last act of will, of holding a covenant in her bones, of nearly becoming a prison forever. She'd slept most of the ride down from the pass, her small arms loose around Elodie's waist, but now she lifted her head and Elodie felt her go still in that particular way she did when she was listening to something no one else could hear.

"The valley remembers being closed," Ilyen said softly. "But it isn't anymore. Everything can come in now."

"Everything," Rennick repeated. His hand drifted toward the knife at his belt, old habit from a life in Corvassa's underbelly. "That includes everything bad."

"Everything," Ilyen agreed, without fear. "That's what freedom means."

Elodie nudged her horse forward. There was no use standing at the threshold mourning the safety they'd traded away. They'd made the choice at the top of the world, all three of them, and Halvard's ghost and Serane's ghost and Sarel's ghost had stood witness. You didn't get to take it back.

The first farmhouse they passed had its door open. A woman stood in the yard staring up at nothing, her hands raised slightly, palms turned inward — the posture of someone watching snow fall for the first time. When Elodie drew closer she saw why: motes of light were drifting down around the woman, slow as ash, dissolving before they touched the ground.

"That's new," the woman said, not looking away. "Started three days ago. Old Tam swears his lame knee mended itself while he slept. Marta's well water glows after dark." She finally turned, and her eyes found Elodie, then the child on her back, and went wide. "You're the Nakamura woman. We thought you dead."

"Got close a few times."

"And that's—" The woman's voice dropped. Word traveled fast even in a valley; word of the vessel, the key, the child the Crown wanted, had been whispered in taverns for months. "*Her.*"

Elodie dismounted, lifting Ilyen down before answering. She set the girl on the fence rail so the woman could see her plainly — not a relic, not a weapon, not a seal. A thin child with mud-colored hair and a chip in her front tooth and hay dust on her sleeves.

"This is Ilyen," Elodie said. "She's coming home."

* * *

They came out to meet them at the village proper, and Elodie hadn't prepared for it.

She'd imagined suspicion. Fear. The careful distance villagers keep from trouble, because trouble in Highmoor historically arrived wearing the Crown's colors and asked questions afterward. She'd rehearsed speeches on the road — *she's just a child, she never asked for any of this, the Order is broken, Sarel is dead, there's nothing left to hunt* — all the arguments she expected to need.

She didn't need one.

Maren reached them first. The old priestess came down the chapel path at a pace dangerous for a woman her age, skirts hiked, breath ragged, and stopped ten feet away as if afraid the sight would break if she moved closer. Her eyes went from Elodie to Rennick to Ilyen, and her mouth worked soundlessly twice before anything came out.

"You went up the mountain," she said.

"We went up the mountain."

"And you came back down." Maren's gaze settled on Ilyen, and something in the old woman's face cracked open — Elodie had seen her hold funerals with more composure. "Child. Do you know what you are? What you *were*?"

"Ilyen," the girl said simply. "Elodie's."

Maren made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and pressed both hands over her mouth.

Then the village arrived. Not armed, not wary — they came out of houses and fields and the smithy still holding his tongs, and they crowded around in a rough half-circle, and Elodie waited for the questions: *Is it true the god walked? Is the valley safe? Are we damned?* Instead an old farmer named Cedric, who'd sold Elodie arrows for fifteen years and never once asked about her business, stepped forward and knelt. Not to a god. To Ilyen. To the girl on the fence rail with hay in her hair.

"We heard the bells stop," he said. "In Corvassa. We heard the Order fell, and the mountain opened, and the god left the world walking. And we thought — the child who was the key—" He faltered, looking up. "We thought she'd be taken somewhere. Kept somewhere. Made important somewhere."

"She was almost made very important indeed," Rennick said dryly. "Nearly killed her twice."

"But she's here," Cedric said. "She came *here*. So we reckoned—" He glanced around at his neighbors, suddenly shy, a man in his sixties embarrassed before a nine-year-old. "We reckoned she should have a home. If she wants one. If she'll have ours."

Silence held the square. Ilyen slid off the rail and stood beside Elodie, her small hand finding Elodie's, and Elodie felt the tremor in it — the girl had faced inquisitors and knights and a god unbound without shaking, but this, this ordinary kindness, undid her.

"I don't know how to be a person," Ilyen said quietly. "Only a vessel. That's what I was made for."

"Well," said Marta the well-keeper, pushing forward with her sleeves rolled, "we've got children your age who only know how to be farmhands, and we love 'em anyway. You'll learn the rest."

And then the crowd closed around the girl like water around a stone, and Elodie stepped back, and let it happen. Villagers touched Ilyen's shoulders, offered her bread from their aprons, introduced their own children with the solemnity of diplomats. A boy of maybe ten — the cooper's son — showed her a grasshopper he'd caught, and Ilyen examined it with the gravity of a scholar and said something in that old pre-kingdom tongue, and the boy laughed like she'd told him a joke, though neither of them understood the other, and somehow that was fine.

Rennick leaned against Elodie's shoulder. "Look at that. Whole realm's been fighting over what she *is*, and these people just want to know if she likes honey cake."

"Do you?" Elodie asked Ilyen across the crowd.

"I don't know yet," Ilyen called back. "I want to find out."

* * *

Maren took them to the shrines at dusk.

There had been eleven of them once, wayshrines scattered along the valley paths where travelers paused to touch the stone and speak a word to Veyra. When the god fell silent twenty years ago, the shrines had gone dark — literally dark, Elodie remembered now, remembering what Maren had confessed in that terrible night of fire and flight: that the stones used to glow faintly at dawn, and then didn't, and Maren alone kept tending them anyway, faith without evidence, for two decades.

The Order had defaced them during the occupation. Chiseled faces, toppled plinths, one shrine blown apart with blackpowder out of pure spite.

Now Elodie stood at the first shrine on the chapel path and watched it shine.

Not blazing — glowing, soft and gold-white, the way embers breathe. The shattered shrine had been reassembled crudely, mortar still wet, and the old carved face of Veyra — serene, androgynous, eyes closed — bore fresh chips and old scars. And the light pooled in the damaged places like water filling cracks.

"They lit themselves," Maren said. Her voice was wrecked. She'd been crying since the square, Elodie realized, and had simply kept functioning through it, the way old soldiers do. "Three nights ago. I came out to sweep the path the way I do every morning, and the whole valley was full of little fires. I fell to my knees right here and couldn't get up for an hour." She laughed wetly. "Twenty years I tended dead stones. My own congregation thought me mad. Half of *me* thought me mad. And then—"

She broke off. Reached out and laid her palm against the glowing face of the god.

"She's really gone, isn't she," Maren said. Not a question. "Beyond the world. I can feel it — this light, it's not *her*. It's just... what she left behind. Like warmth in a wall after the fire goes out."

"Yes," Elodie said. "She chose to go."

"To leave us free." Maren nodded slowly, wiping her face with a rough sleeve, uncaring of dignity. "After all that. After everything we did to her — sealed her, caged her, built a prison valley and called it holy — she still left us the light." Her hand dropped. She turned to Ilyen, and her expression shifted into something older and fiercer. "And she left us *you*."

"Maren—" Elodie started, the old protectiveness rising.

"No. Hear me." Maren crouched, joints cracking, until she was eye-level with the girl. "I knew what you were before anyone. I read the liturgy in your babble and I knew, and I was afraid, and part of me saw you as a thing to be guarded rather than a soul to be loved. I ask your forgiveness for that. A priestess should know better."

Ilyen studied her with those too-old eyes. Then she reached out and touched Maren's cheek, right where the tears tracked, and said — in Common this time, carefully, each word chosen like a step across ice:

"You kept the lights company. When nobody else believed they'd wake. I remember. I was in the stones."

Maren made no sound at all this time. She simply folded forward and pressed her forehead to the girl's small shoulder and wept like a woman released from a twenty-year sentence, which, Elodie supposed, was exactly what she was.

* * *

Night came down soft on the cottage.

Hers — theirs now, apparently, by unanimous village decree; Marta had already stocked the larder and Cedric had left a bundle of good yew arrow-shafts on the doorstep with a note reading *FOR WHEN YOU TEACH THE YOUNG ONES, WHICH YOU WILL.* The hearth was lit. Rennick had claimed the chair by the window and was cleaning his knives with the contentment of a man who'd finally found a town whose criminals he could out-stare. Ilyen sat cross-legged on the floor, turning a honey cake over in her hands, deciding.

"Well?" Elodie asked.

Ilyen bit into it. Considered. Her whole face changed. "Oh," she breathed. "*Oh.*"

"Told you," Rennick said.

Later — after the cake, after Ilyen fell asleep mid-sentence trying to describe grasshoppers in two languages, after they'd carried her to the bed and she'd curled around the white shard of stone that no longer hummed but stayed warm, a keepsake now, a fossil of what she'd been — Elodie stepped outside.

The valley lay quiet under a spill of stars. And everywhere, faint as breath on glass, the motes of light drifted down. Over the fields. Over the chapel. Over the eleven shrines, each one a small golden ember in the dark, tended again, believed in again, needing no god behind them to be worth keeping lit.

She pressed her hand to the faded mark on her forearm. The oath was gone. Twenty years of duty, silence, guilt, grief — dissolved at the top of a mountain by a child's renunciation. And what remained underneath, now that the oath was peeled away, was startlingly simple: she was a woman standing outside her own house, in a valley full of people who had chosen her as surely as she had chosen them, and tomorrow morning there would be bread, and students, and a girl who needed to learn how to be a person.

Belonging, it turned out, wasn't granted. It was earned, mile by bloody mile, all the way up a mountain and back down.

Behind her, the door opened. Ilyen stood in the frame, sleep-rumpled, hugging the shard to her chest.

"There's someone at the edge of the light," the girl said. "By the north road. They've been waiting a long time, and they're cold."

Elodie's hand went to her bow before her mind caught up. "Friend or trouble?"

Ilyen tilted her head, listening to whatever she listened to now. And she smiled — small, tired, certain.

"I think," she said, "it's someone else who needs to learn how to be a person."

Elodie looked out past the shrines, toward the dark line of the north road, where — yes — a figure stood at the edge of the drifting light, still as a post, waiting.

She sighed, picked up her cloak, and went to open the door.
