## 38. A New Oath

### Elodie

Winter had come honest this year — the good kind, the kind that meant snow on the fields and smoke rising straight from every chimney in the valley instead of wards failing and wild magic bleeding through the hedgerows. Elodie stood at her window and watched it fall on Highmoor, and for the first time in her life she looked at the valley without feeling its walls.

The boundary was gone. She could feel it the way you feel a tooth finally pulled — an absence that ached more than the wound ever had. Where once her oath-mark had hummed a flat, dead note against the silence of the place, now the air itself was alive. Faint things moved in the frost: a wisp of green light drifting through Maren's chapel yard where the shrines stood newly scrubbed, a fox in the treeline whose eyes caught the lantern-glow with something too knowing to be animal. The villagers had stopped flinching from it. Mostly.

She'd come back to the cottage because there was nowhere else she wanted to be, and that, after forty years of telling herself she stayed out of duty, felt like the truest thing she'd ever learned about herself.

Behind her, the hearth crackled. Rennick had spent two days arguing with her chimney before conceding it worked fine and he simply enjoyed complaining. Ilyen sat cross-legged on the rug, teaching herself to whittle with a knife Rennick absolutely should not have given a nine-year-old, her tongue between her teeth, white stone shard — no longer humming, just warm, like a hearthstone — hanging at her throat.

"Stop watching me," Ilyen said without looking up. "I'll cut myself worse if you watch."

"You've already cut yourself twice."

"Learning," Ilyen said, with the gravity of a general reviewing troops.

Elodie smiled and let it go. There had been a time — not long ago, though it felt like another life, another woman — when she would have taken the knife away. When protecting Ilyen had meant walls and silence and carrying her up a mountain on her back while knights shot at them. Now protection looked like letting the child hold sharp things and bleed a little and learn.

Maren had told her, three nights past, standing in the chapel doorway with candlelight on her face: *You should mark this somehow. What the three of you did. What you are to each other.* And Elodie had started to say *we're nothing so formal* — and then stopped, because she remembered what formality without love had cost her. An oath to a god she'd never heard answer. Five names carved into a covenant none of them understood. Tomas dead in the mud. Sarel walking into collapsing stone rather than live unbound. Halvard's grave under cairn stones on the pass, which Rennick still climbed to visit when he thought nobody saw.

Formal wasn't the problem. Empty formal was.

"We should do something tonight," she said.

Rennick looked up from his maps — he was drawing the valley as it now stood, open on all sides, for reasons he refused to explain but which involved the word "trade routes" and a glint in his eye she distrusted deeply. "Do we have to? I did my ceremonial something last week. My knees haven't recovered."

"Not that kind." She came to sit by the fire, across from Ilyen. "Vows."

That got both their attention. Ilyen set down the knife. Rennick closed the map slowly, like a man closing a door on a room he wasn't sure he wanted to enter.

"The old ones didn't take," Elodie said. "So we say new ones. Not to Veyra. Not to any god or crown or archive or Order. Just—" she gestured at the small circle of firelight, the three of them and the dog-sized gap where Halvard should have been and always would be— "to each other. Plain words. No magic in it. That's the point."

"No magic?" Rennick raised an eyebrow. "You're asking me, a professional thief, to make promises with no escape clauses?"

"I'm asking you to mean one."

He was quiet a moment. The fire popped. Outside, the snow kept falling soft and unhurried, the way snow falls when nothing is hunting anyone.

"All right," Rennick said. "But I'm going first, because if I wait I'll think of seventeen reasons to be clever about it."

* * *

### Rennick

Here was the thing Rennick had learned about himself in the last year, and hated learning: he'd spent thirty years stealing because taking things was easier than being given them.

An archive-thief lived well. Good food, better wine, rooms above taverns with real glass in the windows. But you never stayed anywhere longer than a season, never owed anyone anything, never let anyone owe you. It was freedom, he'd told himself. It was, in fact, just loneliness with excellent bookkeeping.

Then a mud-caked mercenary and a mute child had broken into the Archive of Covenants, and he'd caught them, and instead of turning them in for the reward — which had been considerable; he'd checked — he'd shown them the restricted stacks, and somewhere between the sealed folios and the weir ambush where he'd taken a knight's blade through the shoulder, the ledger had stopped balancing. He owed them. They owed him. The debt went back and forth until it stopped being a debt at all.

Now he sat in a cottage in a valley that smelled like woodsmoke and bread, about to say words out loud that couldn't be stolen, sold, or fenced, and his hands were sweating like the first job he'd ever run.

"Plain words," Elodie said. "No cleverness."

"That's the crueler demand, and you know it." He cleared his throat. Looked at the fire, then made himself look at them instead, because a vow delivered to a fireplace was a coward's vow. "I'm bad at this, so I wrote it down, and yes, I know that's worse."

From inside his coat he produced a scrap of paper. Ilyen snorted. He pointed the paper at her. "Not one word, whittler."

*To Elodie,* he read. *I promise to stay even when the road is easier. I promise to tell you when I'm scared instead of making a joke, starting now: I'm scared, because I've never promised anyone anything that lasted, and I don't entirely trust me to keep it.*

*To Ilyen —* his voice caught, and he pushed through it, because pushing through was the whole vow, wasn't it — *I promise you'll never again be a thing people argue over. You're not a vessel or a seal or a key. You're a girl who carves terrible ducks and lies about practicing her letters. If anyone comes for you again, they come through me, and I'm faster than I look.*

*And I promise to visit Halvard's cairn every first-snow. Somebody should tell him how it ended. He'd complain about the climb. He'd be glad of it.*

He folded the paper. His hands weren't quite steady. "There. Witnessed by a hearth and a child with a knife. Legally dubious, spiritually unprecedented. I'm done."

Ilyen crossed the rug and hugged him, quick and fierce, the way she did everything — as if tenderness were a raid. He held on a beat longer than he meant to. The scar on his shoulder ached in the warmth, the way it always did now, and he found he didn't mind. Scars were receipts. Proof you'd paid for something worth having.

* * *

### Ilyen

Ilyen had been born from a dying act of love — that was what she knew now, what the god's leaving had left in her like light after a lamp is carried out of a room. She was made of Veyra's last will: a vessel, a seal, a living covenant grown from grief. For a long time she had thought that was all she was. A purpose wearing a child's body.

But purposes could be finished. This — the rug, the fire, the smell of the stew Elodie had left simmering, Rennick pretending his eyes weren't red — this was not purpose. This was just evening. And evening, she was learning, was the thing she'd been made for all along, though nobody who made her had known it.

Her turn. She had thought about hers the longest, because everyone expected her to say something holy, and she was done being holy. Holy had nearly gotten her killed four times and gotten Halvard killed once, and holy had a way of meaning *other people get to decide what you are*.

She knelt up straight on the rug, the way Maren had taught her to kneel for prayers, and then deliberately unfolded her legs and just sat, ordinary, because that was the prayer.

"My turn," she said. "And I'm not doing the kneeling thing."

"Wouldn't dream of expecting it," Rennick murmured.

"Ilyen," she began, giving herself her own name first, because Maren said vows should start with truth. "That's my name. Nobody gave it to me. I grew it, like a tooth. I'm keeping it."

"To Elodie: I promise to eat the vegetables even when they're the gray ones, because you fought a war for me and the least I can do is choke down parsnips." Elodie laughed — really laughed, the sound still rusty, like a gate opening for the first time in twenty years. "I promise to learn everything you can teach me, the knife-work and the tracking and the part where you know what people are going to do before they do. And I promise that when I'm grown, I won't become someone who decides other people are things. Even for good reasons. *Especially* for good reasons. That's how Sarel started. Good reasons and a very straight back."

The name settled over the firelight and none of them looked away from it. That was new too. Grief you could look at directly, together, instead of each carrying your piece alone in the dark.

"To Rennick: I promise to stop carving ducks into your furniture." A pause. "The *new* furniture. The old ducks are load-bearing now."

"They are *not*—"

"They're load-bearing," Ilyen repeated with finality, and moved on before he could appeal. "And I promise to remember. Everything. The shrine-roads and Hedgewitch Odile's name and the barge and the mountain and all of it — not because it was good, some of it was horrible, but because forgetting is how the Quiet got built in the first place. People forgot why the god mattered, so they chained it. People forgot the five were people, so they became an Order. I'll remember out loud. Every year. First snow."

She touched the white stone at her throat. It was just stone now — warm, inert, beautiful. Or maybe not just. Maybe a seed. She hadn't decided yet, and for the first time in her short strange life, nobody was rushing her to decide.

"And to myself," she said, quieter, "I promise I'm allowed to be a girl. Not a seal. Not a sign. Just — mine."

The fire cracked. Snow ticked softly against the window. Somewhere out in the dark, a nightbird sang — a real one, or one of the new faint magics wearing a bird's shape; these days nobody could tell, and nobody minded, and that was the whole miracle of it, folded up small enough to fit in a song.

* * *

### Elodie

It was her turn, and Elodie found that the vows she'd rehearsed in her head all day — careful, weighted, worthy of the occasion — all of them sounded wrong out loud. Too much like the old liturgy. Like the cadence of the Godspine rite. So she set them down and spoke plainly, the way she'd spoken to bounties and barkeeps and, once, to a god who never answered.

"I swore an oath when I was nineteen," she said. "Kneeled in this valley with four friends and gave my life to Veyra. I meant it with everything in me. And the god went quiet, and I spent twenty years keeping faith with a silence — doing the work, walking the patrol, staying inside the ward like a good hound stays in a yard. I called it devotion." She shook her head. "It was fear with good posture."

Rennick made a low sound of recognition. Ilyen leaned against her side, and Elodie put an arm around her without thinking, the way breathing happened without thinking now.

"So here's my vow, and it's the opposite of the first one. I promise to stop confusing staying with loving. I stayed in this valley for twenty years and called it loyalty, and I let Tomas die believing our oath meant something holy, and I almost handed a child to a prison because the oath said so. Never again. If I stay somewhere now, it's because I choose the people in the room. If I fight, it's because I choose what I'm fighting for. Choice, not duty. Every morning, chosen fresh." She looked around the fire. "You two. Chosen. Every morning. That's the whole vow. It doesn't need a god to hold it up. We hold each other up. Turns out that works better."

"It does work better," Rennick said. "Statistically. Anecdotally. I've seen both models in operation."

"Hush, you've said yours."

They had no ritual for sealing it — no blood, no stone, no liturgy in a language older than kingdoms. Instead Ilyen fetched the pot of stew, and Rennick poured three cups of the plum brandy he'd been hoarding since Corvassa, and they ate and drank by the fire while the snow fell on a valley that belonged to no god, no crown, and no covenant but its own.

Later, when the brandy was gone and Ilyen had gone heavy-headed against Elodie's shoulder, Rennick poked the fire and asked, quietly, "Think it counts? Without anything witnessing it?"

Elodie considered. Above the mantel hung the old oath-mark sigil of the five, which she'd never taken down and now never would — not as worship, but as memory, the way you keep a sword that saved you and broke you in the same year.

"Everything witnessed it," she said. "We did."

* * *

They slept, the three of them, in the deep untroubled way of people who have finally stopped listening for riders on the ridgeline.

Which was why, near dawn, the knock at the door woke all of them at once — three hands moving to three weapons out of old habit, before the sound had even finished falling.

Elodie rose. Through the frost-furred window, gray with early light, she could see a figure on her doorstep: small, travel-worn, dusted with snow, standing exactly where Ilyen had stood on the night all of this began.

Another knock. Soft. Patient. Waiting to be let in.

Elodie smiled, took down the bar, and opened wide.
