## 1. Ink and Indenture

There are two ways to count a debt in Highmoor: honestly, or the Concord's way.

I have copied enough ledgers to know the difference. The honest way is simple — you owe what you owe, you pay what you can, and when it's done it's done. The Concord's way is an art form. Interest compounds on interest. Fees appear like mushrooms after rain, each one with its own tidy name in its own tidy column: *clerical surcharge*, *vellum levy*, *the Prior's tithe for the maintenance of spiritual order*. My grandmother would have called it what it is — shearing a sheep that's already been sold — but my grandmother was a smuggler and died owing nothing to anyone, which is more than I can say.

My debt stands at four hundred and eleven marks, fourteen silvers, and six coppers as of this morning's tally. Six years ago it stood at three hundred marks even. You do not need to be a scribe to see where that arithmetic is going. You only need to be awake.

I was awake. That was most of the problem.

* * *

The scriptorium of the Highmoor temple smells of tallow, oak gall ink, and the particular despair of men who write for a living and are not permitted to say anything true. Forty desks in rows, forty scribes bent like question marks over their work, forty little clouds of breath in the cold because the Concord heats the Prior's quarters and lets the rest of us freeze on principle. Frugality builds character, Brother Ossian tells us. Brother Ossian has never once been cold in his life; he sleeps above the boiler room and considers this a theological accident rather than an arrangement.

My desk is the fourth from the window, which sounds like a mercy until you learn that the window faces north and the light it gives is the color of dishwater. In winter I work by feel half the day. My handwriting suffers for it, and every flaw in my hand is noted against me, because of course it is. Everything here is noted against you. The temple keeps records the way a spider keeps a web — not out of malice, but because the web is how it eats.

That morning I was copying tax assessments for the river villages. Quill, knife, inkpot, blotting sand, and page after page of names that were not mine attached to sums that did not belong to me, rendered unto an institution that had purchased my grandfather's failures at auction and been charging him compound interest ever since. Grandfather Halloran lost a boat on the Thornfell run — or so the record says; the record also says the boat was insured, and then says the insurance claim was denied for irregularities, and then says the denial itself incurred a fee. Three generations later, here I sit, paying for a boat I've never seen, on a river I'm not allowed to cross without a writ.

The ink was behaving badly. It does that when it's cold — goes thick and spiteful, drags across the page like something reluctant. I warmed the pot between my palms and thought, not for the first time, about the exact weight of a copper coin, and how many of them stood between me and a door.

"Bram." Brother Ossian's voice, from behind, mild as milk. "Your margin."

I looked down. My margin had wandered. A line of figures had drifted a finger's width off true, and in the drift — I swear I hadn't planned it, though I won't swear I regretted it — the numbers spelled out nothing at all, just sat there crooked, like a man leaning against a wall he'd been told not to touch.

"My apologies, Brother."

"Apologies cost nothing," Ossian said, "which is why we accept them freely. Errors cost a half-mark. Noted."

Noted. Of course. Somewhere in the great web, another thread tightened around me. Four hundred eleven marks, fourteen silvers, six coppers — make that five coppers owed and a half-mark entered, which by the alchemy of Concord accounting comes to roughly the same thing, only worse.

I straightened the line. I am very good at straightening lines. It is the one skill they gave me that I keep for myself.

* * *

Here is what the Concord knows about me: Bram Halloran, indentured scribe, third-class, competent, punctual, unremarkable, no family of record, no property, no prospects, no vices worth prosecuting. They know this because they wrote it, in their own hand, in their own book, and their own book says whatever serves them. Third-class means I copy things. Second-class means you may read what you copy. First-class means you may ask questions, and there are perhaps nine first-class scribes in all of Highmoor, and every one of them has the look of a man who has learned that curiosity is a disease you survive by never showing symptoms.

Here is what the Concord does not know about me: twice a month, sometimes three times if the moon cooperates, I leave my cot in the scribes' dormitory after the last bell, go over the wall by the laundry chute, and walk into the mountains to guide contraband through passes that appear on no map the Concord owns.

This is not as heroic as it sounds. Mostly it's cold. Mostly it's carrying other people's brandy over scree in the dark while praying your boots hold together for another season. But it pays in silver — real silver, unrecorded, unnoted — and silver is the only language the ledger respects. Every run buys back a few coppers of myself. At my current rate of both earning and being charged, I calculate freedom arriving sometime around my sixty-first year, provided the fees hold steady, which they will not, because fees never hold steady. Provided, in other words, that I die free at sixty-one or die tired at thirty, those being the available options.

You learn things on the night roads that no first-class scribe ever learns. You learn which passes breathe fog and which ones lie about their width. You learn that the Thornfells are full of ruins nobody official will name — standing stones with carvings worn to whispers, staircases that climb out of hillsides and end in air, doorways built into cliff faces that open onto solid rock, as if the country once had rooms it decided to stop having. The smugglers call the whole region the Quiet Quarter and treat it with a superstition that looks like ignorance but isn't. Old Sella Voss — no relation to anyone you'll meet later, or maybe relation to everyone — used to say the Quarter wasn't empty, only patient. She said it laughing, the way you say true things when truth is dangerous.

And you learn songs. My grandmother taught me songs before the temple taught me letters, and I have always suspected the songs came first in importance, though officially the letters outrank them. Songs about a green country beyond the passes. About a king under a hill who kept a fire that never went out. About doors that answer to names. Nonsense rhymes, the temple would call them, if the temple bothered to call them anything, which it doesn't, because the temple has a policy regarding the lands north of the watershed, and the policy is silence. Two hundred years ago something happened up there. Whatever it was, it was erased so thoroughly that even the erasure has been erased. There is no kingdom on any chart. There is no war in any chronicle. There is only a blank space on the maps, and the words *here be nothing*, written in a confident hand by someone who had never been.

I have been. Nothing, it turns out, has excellent acoustics.

* * *

At midday the bell rang for the meal, and forty scribes put down forty quills in unison, because even our pauses are scheduled. We filed to the refectory and ate barley porridge with a sincerity of salt that suggested the salt had been billed separately. I ate with Tam Coyle, second-class, a soft-bellied man with a talent for gossip and a wife in town he visits on forged leave-passes, an enterprise I admire and have never once helped him with, mostly because helping him would require forging, and forging is the one crime the Concord prosecutes with genuine enthusiasm. Hypocrisy they forgive. Competing with their monopoly on paperwork — that they burn you for.

"Rumor," Tam said, stirring his porridge as though it might confess something, "is the Prior's taken an interest in the northern assessments again."

"The Prior takes an interest in everything north of the wall," I said. "It's called ambition."

"He's pulled the old survey rolls. The pre-erasure ones. Nobody's allowed to see them but him and the Archivist, and the Archivist hasn't come down from his tower in a week."

"Perhaps he's dead."

"Perhaps he's found something."

"Perhaps," I said, "he's found nothing, and that frightens them more."

Tam laughed, because Tam laughs at everything, which is why he has survived twenty years in this place with his personality intact. Then he said, quieter, "You've walked up there, haven't you? On your little errands."

"I've walked everywhere," I said. "Walking is free. It's the standing still that costs."

He looked at me a moment too long, the way people do when they suspect you're more than your file says, and I looked back with exactly the amount of boredom a third-class scribe is licensed to display. The trick of surviving an institution is to be legible. Give them a version of yourself so dull they stop reading. I have spent six years perfecting mine. Somewhere in the archive there is a sheet of paper describing Bram Halloran completely, and it is wrong in every particular that matters, and that wrongness is the only property I own.

* * *

Afternoon brought the worst kind of work: debt reconciliations. Personal ledgers, the indenture books themselves, the great bound volumes in which the Concord keeps its people the way a farmer keeps stock — by ear-tag and yield. I copied entries from the master roll onto working copies, name after name after name, and beside each name a number, and beside each number a note: *deceased, balance transferred to kin*; *absconded, bounty issued*; *sold, price realized*.

Sold. That word appears more often than you'd think. People are bought out of one Concord house and into another, transferred like casks, and the debt travels with them, growing politely all the way. There was a girl on my page that morning, Maren Ostler, sixteen years old, whose father had died owing ninety marks, and whose entry now read *balance assumed by debtor's daughter, term extended accordingly*. Extended accordingly. She had been born free, presumably, some years before her father's luck ran out, and now she was ink. I wrote her number neatly in the margin and felt the quill press harder than it needed to, and the blot that resulted cost me another half-mark, and I paid it, because that is what owned men do. We pay. It's the whole of our biography.

Brother Ossian paused at my shoulder near dusk. He does that. He has a way of arriving precisely when your thoughts have gone somewhere they shouldn't, as if the thoughts made noise.

"You copy the indenture rolls well," he said.

"Thank you, Brother."

"It's a gift, seeing people as figures. Most find it distressing at first."

Most find it distressing forever, I didn't say. "I find figures restful," I said instead. "They don't lie."

Ossian smiled at that, thin as vellum. "No. Figures never lie. That's why we're careful who gets to arrange them."

He moved on down the row, and I sat with his sentence like a stone in my boot. Careful who gets to arrange them. Because that was the whole machine, wasn't it — not cruelty, cruelty was incidental, cruelty was a byproduct like smoke — the whole machine was arrangement. Someone arranged that Maren Ostler owed ninety marks for a debt she never signed. Someone arranged that my grandfather's insurance claim was irregular. Someone arranged the maps, and the silence, and the blank space where a country used to be, and wrote *here be nothing* in a fine confident hand. The Concord did not merely collect debts. It authored them. And I — I was the pen it used to make the authorship look like arithmetic.

I blotted my page. I squared my margins. I went on copying, because a man who stops copying in this place starts asking questions, and questions, in Highmoor, are entered in the ledger too.

* * *

Night came down gray and early, the way it does in the cold months, and the last bell rang, and the dormitory filled with the sound of forty men pretending to sleep. I lay on my cot listening to the building settle — pipes ticking, wind worrying the shutters, Brother Fenwick snoring in a key that could summon dogs — and I counted, as I always do, the distance between myself and the wall.

Four hundred eleven marks. Fourteen silvers. Five coppers, after today's blots. Against which: my hidden purse, sewn into the lining of my spare cassock, holding nine silvers and change from the autumn runs. Nine silvers against four hundred eleven marks. I have done this sum so many times it has worn smooth inside my head, a stone I worry the way other men worry prayer beads. Some nights the stone comforts me. Most nights it just sits there, heavy, round, and perfectly useless, like a coin for the eye of a god who isn't buying.

But tonight the moon was right, and the frost was hard enough to hold a print, and somewhere north of the wall a load of untaxed wool was waiting on a man who paid in silver and asked no questions except *which way*, and I knew the way better than I knew my own name, which is saying something, because my own name is mostly a fiction anyway.

I rose. I dressed without sound — a skill, like the margins, that they never meant to teach me. I took the cassock with the purse in it and left the cot made up with a bolster arranged to suggest a sleeping scribe, and I went out through the laundry chute into the freezing dark, and the wall took me over the way it always does, roughly, and let me down the other side into the part of my life that belongs to me.

The mountains stood up black against the stars. Somewhere among them were the ruins, and the stairs that end in air, and the doors that open onto stone, and the blank space on the map where my grandmother's songs said a green country used to be. I turned north and started walking, and the cold got into my teeth the way it always does, and I was — I want to say happy, but the word doesn't fit; call it *myself*, which is rarer and costs more.

Three hours up the shepherd trails, at the place where the trail bends around the shoulder of Crag Fell and the whole Quiet Quarter opens below you — that's where I saw it. A light. Down in the foothills, off every road I knew, a small orange flicker where no camp should be, no hut stood, no honest traveler had any business being. I stopped. The wind carried nothing up to me — no voices, no dog, no smell of woodsmoke, which was strange, because a fire that size should smell.

Just the light, burning alone in the dark of the erased country, steady as a held breath.

I told myself it was charcoal-burners. Charcoal-burners keep illegal fires all through these hills. I told myself that all the way down the far side and all the way through the wool delivery and all the way back over the wall before matins, and I almost believed it, right up until the morning bell rang and the temple woke shouting.

Because there, at the postern gate, mud to the waist and half-dead with cold, stood a child no older than seven — and the child was speaking, clearly, furiously, in a language no scribe in Highmoor could identify.

Except that I could.

It was the language of my grandmother's nonsense rhymes. The green-country songs. The words the maps say never existed.

And the child was looking directly at me.
