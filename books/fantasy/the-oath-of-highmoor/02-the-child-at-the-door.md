## 2. The Child at the Door

There is a particular quality to being recognized by something that should not know you exist.

I want to be precise about this, because in the years since I have told the story a dozen ways and gotten it wrong most of them. The child did not simply look at me. Children look at people all the time; it is one of their few marketable skills. This was different. This was the look of someone checking a face against a description — and finding it accurate down to details the describer could not possibly have had.

The storm chose that moment to lean on the temple like a drunk leaning on a fence post. Rain came through the postern arch sideways, and the lamp above the gate guttered, and in that stuttering light the child's eyes caught it — not reflected, but caught, held for a half-second longer than any eye has any business doing.

"Close the gate," said Brother Anselm, who had come up behind me with his keys and his permanent expression of a man who has smelled something disappointing. "Whatever it is, it isn't Concord business."

"It spoke," said Sister Maren, who had also come up, because in the temple of Highmoor nothing travels faster than the news that someone might be needed elsewhere. "It spoke words. Words are records. Records are Concord business by definition."

"You can't make a record of mud," Anselm said.

But he was already unlocking the gate, because Anselm's cynicism had never once survived contact with an order, implied or otherwise. That is what thirty years of indenture does to a man: it files down every edge except obedience, and then polishes what's left until you can see your own reflection in it and hate what you're wearing.

The child came through the arch like something the river had given up on. Mud from scalp to shin, so uniformly caked that at first I couldn't tell what was cloth and what was skin. Bare feet gone the color of old candle wax. It stopped three paces inside the gate and stood there, swaying slightly, breathing in great shuddering pulls as if air itself were a meal it hadn't had in days.

And it kept talking. That was the thing. Through chattering teeth, through lips cracked and bleeding, it kept producing those syllables in an unbroken stream — low, urgent, rhythmic, with a rise and fall that my hindbrain insisted on hearing as *meaning*, even though every educated bone in my body knew better.

*Every educated bone.* I was twenty-six years old and I already thought of myself in inventory. That's the Concord for you. Give them enough of your years and you start counting yourself the way they count you.

"It's fever-madness," Maren said. "Or hill-speech. Some shepherd brat wandered off the high pastures."

"It's neither," I said, before I could stop myself.

Three pairs of eyes turned to me. In the temple, volunteering information unasked is roughly equivalent to walking into the Prior's office and setting fire to the ledger. Information is currency; you spend it when spending profits you, not when some mud-caked foundling makes your tongue move without permission.

"And how," Anselm asked with terrible mildness, "would Halloran know?"

"I've heard hill-speech. This isn't it." True, as far as it went. Everything I said in those days was true only as far as it went; that was how I'd survived. "Hill-speech has Highmoor roots under it. You can hear the trade-tongue in it like bones. This doesn't have bones." I paused, hating myself for the next part and saying it anyway. "It has a different skeleton entirely."

Maren wrote that down. Of course she did. Somewhere in the temple archives there now exists a page in her careful hand recording that Bram Halloran, debt-number 4471, described an unknown language as having *a different skeleton entirely*, and I expect some future scholar will find it very illuminating. Scholars always do find illumination in exactly the places where the people involved were merely trying not to drown.

The child had gone quiet while we talked. It stood watching us with the stillness of prey animals and very good liars, and its eyes moved from Maren's habit to Anselm's keys to my ink-stained fingers, cataloguing, deciding. Then it spoke again — two words this time, short ones, pointed at me like thrown stones.

And my grandmother's kitchen rose up around me so suddenly and completely that I gripped the gatepost.

I have to explain about my grandmother, and I can't explain properly, because explaining requires admitting things I spent my whole indenture denying even to myself. My grandmother raised me between seasons, in the gap-years before the Concord bought my debt off my father's failure. She was mountain-born, smuggler-kin, illiterate by choice and proud of it, and she sang. She sang constantly, tunelessly, songs with no names that she claimed she'd made up but delivered with the word-perfect consistency of things learned young and never forgotten. Counting rhymes. Door rhymes. A lullaby about a green country where the rain fell upward, which she swore was nonsense and sang like scripture.

She made me swear never to sing them in town. Not because they were forbidden — she laughed at the idea that anyone would bother forbidding nonsense — but because, she said, songs were like knives: harmless in the hand that owns them, dangerous in the hand that wants what's yours.

Two words, thrown at me across a flooded courtyard, and they fit into one of her counting rhymes like a key into a lock I didn't know my childhood had been building.

*"Aethen varra,"* the child said.

*Varrath, varrath, count the doors,* my grandmother used to chant, tapping my nose on each number. Nine doors, ten doors, all the way to a number she'd deliver with a strange pause, as though the rhyme went further than she liked.

"Do you understand it?" Maren asked me, sharp with the particular hunger of a scribe confronting an uncatalogued thing.

"No," I said.

That was my first lie to the Concord about Wren. It would not be the last, and by the end they would stack up behind me like a wall, and I would stand on top of the wall, and it would be the only ground I ever truly owned. But I'm ahead of the story. In those days I still believed my lies were small, temporary things, pebbles I could pick back up later.

* *

They put the child in the warming room off the infirmary, because whatever else the Concord was — and it was many things, most of them billable — it maintained a genuine institutional anxiety about deaths on temple grounds. A dead debtor is a loss. A dead mystery, though. A dead mystery is a scandal, and scandals in Highmoor were treated like fires in a granary: everyone ran toward them, and no one admitted carrying the torch.

I should have gone back to the scriptorium. I had forty pages of tithing records due, and Prior Vane's steward had already noted twice that week that Halloran's output was adequate rather than exemplary, and *adequate* in that building was a word with a blade in it. I knew all this. I recited it to myself, in fact, standing in the corridor outside the warming room like an idiot, listening to a seven-year-old talk to the wall in a dead language.

Because it was talking to the wall. Or near it. Or past it. Its voice had dropped from the furious urgency of the gate to something lower, more conversational, with pauses — and the pauses were the worst part. Pauses meant turn-taking. Pauses meant it expected an answer.

"The wall isn't going to reply," I said, letting myself in.

The child spun, put its back to the actual corner of the room, and looked at me with an expression I recognized intimately, because I'd worn it myself for most of a decade: the look of someone calculating whether the window or the door offered better odds, and knowing both were bad.

"There's broth," I said. "If you don't eat, they'll force-feed you, and their idea of force involves a funnel. Take it from me."

It didn't move. It said something — one word, interrogative, rising at the end.

"Don't ask me questions in a language I'm pretending not to know," I said, and immediately heard the trap close around my own tongue.

Its head tilted. And here is the detail that I have turned over more times than any other, the stone under which everything else eventually slithered out: it smiled. Small, exhausted, mud-cracked, but unmistakably a smile — the smile of someone who has just watched a bad liar lie badly and finds it, against all sense, comforting.

It took the broth.

I sat on the floor opposite — sitting on furniture felt wrong, felt like presuming, and besides, from the floor I could see the door — and watched it eat with the controlled desperation of someone who has learned that eating too fast after starving kills you. Somebody had taught it that. Somebody had taught it manners and a language and how to survive cold rivers, and none of that somebody was anywhere in this room, and the child's eyes kept flicking to the doorway the way mine kept flicking to the ledger totals that governed my life: checking, always checking, whether the debt had grown.

"What's your name?" I asked, in the trade-tongue, slowly.

It considered me for a long time. Then it answered in its own language — one word — and touched its own chest with two fingers, and repeated the word, and I understood it was giving me the only thing it had left: a sound that meant *me*.

"Wren," I tried.

Something happened in its face. Not pleasure. Recognition running backward — as if it had expected me to know already, and was recalibrating its entire assessment of how much I knew, and arriving at conclusions it found either alarming or funny. Possibly both. With Wren, for years afterward, it was usually both.

"Wren," it agreed, and ate its broth, and I sat on the cold flagstones feeling like a man who has picked up a coin in the street and realized, too late, that it's bait.

* *

Prior Aldous Vane came within the hour, which tells you everything about how fast information moves uphill in Highmoor when the information smells like power.

I want to be fair to my memory here, because in later chapters of this account Vane becomes — deservedly — something other than human in my telling, and it matters that I record what he actually was that night, which was: impressive. He arrived without retinue, in a plain traveling cloak still dripping, and he knelt. He knelt in front of a mud-caked, half-drowned, terrified child, on wet flagstones, in full view of witnesses, and he smiled with such unhurried warmth that I felt it physically, like standing near a hearth.

"You're safe now," he said, in the gentle tone of a man who means it, or who has practiced meaning it so long that the difference has worn smooth. "No one will hurt you here. You're among servants of the Concord, and the Concord protects all souls under its roof."

All of which was true in the way that a cage protects a bird from cats.

Wren pressed back into the corner and said nothing. Vane didn't press. He simply stayed kneeling, patient as erosion, and let the silence do his work, and I remember thinking — with the small cynical pride that was then the only property I owned outright — *he's good. He's genuinely, professionally good. Someone taught him this the way my grandmother taught me the rhymes.*

Then he glanced up at me, and the warmth banked itself like a covered lamp, and he said, mildly:

"Halloran. You identified the speech?"

"A little, Your Holiness. Its structure. Nothing more." Pebble. Pick it up later.

"Mm." He rose, joints cracking, a big man aging honestly in body and dishonestly in every other respect. "You'll transcribe. Whatever it says, however it says it — every sound, every pause. You have the best ear among the scribes; Brother Anselm says so, and Anselm praises no one, which makes his praise nearly evidence." A thin smile. "Work in shifts. Rest when the child rests. The Concord thanks you for your service, and your service will be noted in your tally."

*Noted in your tally.* Four words, and my freedom flickered closer like the gate-lamp in the storm. Every favor logged against my debt brought the zero nearer — the beautiful, impossible zero, the day the ledger read *paid* and I walked out of Highmoor with nothing belonging to me but my own name, and even that barely.

"Thank you, Your Holiness," I said, and meant it, and hated that I meant it, and went to fetch ink.

That's the thing I need you to understand about complicity, since apparently this whole account is going to be an argument about it: it never arrives wearing its own face. It arrives wearing yours. It arrives looking like a tired man being offered credit toward a debt that has eaten fifteen years of his life, and it sounds like gratitude, and it feels like a ladder out of a well. You say yes to a transcription. That's all. Ink and paper and a child's voice. What could that possibly buy?

You find out later. You always find out later, and the interest is ruinous.

* *

I transcribed until dawn.

The warming room had one lamp and one hearth and Wren, who slept in snatches with the abrupt totality of the very young and very exhausted, and woke talking. Talking to the ceiling, the wall, the dark window. Once — and my pen actually stutters in the original manuscript, a blot where my hand jumped — talking to the fire, in a tone I can only describe as familiar. Not prayer. Not command. The register you use with a friend who has been absent a long time.

And through all of it, underneath the strangeness, my grandmother's voice kept surfacing like a drowned thing refusing to stay down. Every third phrase of Wren's speech had an echo somewhere in her nonsense: a cadence here, a doubled word there, once an entire construction that mapped word-for-word onto a skipping rhyme about a bridge that only bears the honest. She hadn't been singing nonsense. She'd been singing a language, worn smooth by generations of mouths that no longer knew what they carried, passed hand to hand like contraband until the contents were forgotten and only the smuggling remained.

My family. What were my family, that they carried a dead kingdom in their mouths and called it noise?

I didn't ask that question yet. I want to be honest about that too: I filed it. I was a scribe; filing was my craft. I put the question in the same deep drawer where I kept my grandmother's face, and the smell of her kitchen, and the memory of the one time I'd seen her cry — at nothing, at a song, at some word in a rhyme she'd stopped singing mid-line and never resumed — and I locked the drawer, and I copied the child's sounds, and I told myself the tightness in my chest was the cold.

Near morning, Wren woke from a nightmare — sudden, total, a small body jackknifing upright with a sound like tearing cloth — and looked straight through the dark at me, and said, clearly, deliberately, in that language older than every map I'd ever copied:

*Aethen varra. Bram.*

My name. My actual name, in the green-country tongue, attached to the phrase from the gate, the counting-rhyme phrase, *count the doors.*

"How do you know my name?" I whispered, in trade-tongue, in heretic's whisper, the pen forgotten.

Wren looked at me with those lamplit eyes that held light a half-second too long, and answered in plain, accented, carefully learned Highmoor speech — the first and for a long time the only sentence it would give me in my own language:

"The doors know their keeper."

Then the storm broke apart against the mountains, the lamp steadied, and from the corridor came footsteps — measured, unhurried, numerous — and the voice of Prior Aldous Vane saying, pleasantly, to someone whose answer I did not yet hear:

"Then let us see what the child knows. Wake the scribe. Both of them."
