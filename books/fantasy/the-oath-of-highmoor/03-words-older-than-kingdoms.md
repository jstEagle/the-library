## 3. Words Older than Kingdoms

They gave me a room with no windows and told me it was an honor.

That's how you know you're in trouble, in my experience. The Concord never wastes honors on things that don't frighten them. A scribe gets a window when the work is dull. A scribe gets a locked door and a brazier and two guards at the threshold when the work is the kind that makes priors stop sleeping.

"Transcribe everything," Brother Cassian told me, pressing a fresh quill into my hand like a man handing off something hot. "Every sound. Every repetition. The Prior wants it by matins."

"And if she says nothing?"

"She'll say something." He wouldn't meet my eyes. "She said your name in her sleep last night. That's why it's you."

I wanted to laugh at that — children babble nonsense, they grab at any face they've seen twice — but there was a look on Cassian's face I'd only seen once before, on a smuggler in the passes who'd heard something moving behind him in the dark and was trying very hard to believe it was wind.

* * *

The child had been cleaned up since the storm. Someone had scrubbed the mud away and put her in a temple smock three sizes too big, and the effect was worse somehow, the way a hawk looks worse in a cage than it ever did in the sky. She sat on the cot in the corner of the interrogation cell — we called it a guest room, because the Concord has always been fluent in euphemism — with her knees pulled up to her chin, watching me the way you'd watch a door you weren't sure would stay shut.

The guards withdrew when I entered. One of them looked relieved to go.

"Good morning," I said, in Trade, then in High Concord liturgical, which is just Trade wearing its Sunday clothes.

Nothing. Not even a blink.

I set out my tools on the little table: ink, sand for blotting, the transcription boards with their ruled lines. I did all of it slowly, loudly, the way you'd move around a strange dog. Then I sat down across from her, folded my hands, and waited.

An hour. I'm not exaggerating for effect. An hour of a child watching me breathe while my tea went cold and my backside went numb on a stone chair. Somewhere in that hour I stopped performing patience and actually found some, which is rare for me, and I have wondered since whether that hour wasn't the first honest thing I ever did for her.

It was raining again. It had been raining since the night she arrived, as though the storm had delivered her and then couldn't quite bring itself to leave.

"You're not going to talk," I said finally. "Are you."

Her eyes moved. That was all. But they moved from my face down to my hands, and stayed there, and I realized she was looking at the ink stain on my middle finger — the permanent bruise every scribe wears like a brand — and something in her expression shifted by a degree. Recognition, maybe. Or the animal calculus of one owned creature identifying another.

I turned my hand over and showed her the stain plainly. "Ink," I said. "It doesn't come off. Believe me, I've tried."

And she made a sound.

Not a word. A breath with edges to it, low in her throat, and it might have been a laugh or a warning, and I honestly couldn't tell you which. But it was the first sound I'd gotten out of her that wasn't silence, and I leaned forward like a fool, and she shrank back against the wall so fast the cot creaked, and I learned lesson two of the day: this child had been reached for before, by hands less patient than mine, and she remembered every one of them.

So I went back to waiting. And somewhere past midday, having exhausted silence as a strategy, I did the thing that changed everything, though I didn't know it at the time. I got bored. And being bored, I started to hum.

It was nothing. Just an old tune my grandmother used to sing while she mended nets, one of the dozen crooked little songs she claimed were hers alone, though every smuggler's grandmother claims the same ones. I hummed four bars of it while I re-cut a quill, absently, the way you'd tap a foot.

The child came off the cot like a spring released.

I want to be precise about what happened next, because I've had years to get it wrong. She didn't attack me. She crossed the cell in three strides, planted herself an arm's length away, and spoke — a full sentence, rapid, urgent, her first words in my presence — and then she stopped, chest heaving, staring at me with an expression I can only call accusation.

"I'm sorry," I said. "I don't—"

She said it again. Slower. Enunciating, the way you speak to someone you've decided is stupid rather than deaf. Her hand shot out and grabbed my wrist — the first time she'd touched anyone willingly, I'd learn later — and she pressed my ink-stained fingers against her own throat.

"Say it," she seemed to be saying. "You know it. Say it back."

* * *

Brother Cassian took my first transcription at dusk and read it standing, his lips moving, his frown deepening with each line.

"This is gibberish," he said.

"It's consistent gibberish," I said. "Same phonemes recurring. Same grammatical markers in the same positions. It's a language, Brother, not noise."

"A dead one, maybe." He dropped the pages on my table. "The Prior thinks it may be old Highmoor dialect. Pre-Unification."

"It isn't." I hadn't meant to say it that flatly. His head came up. I covered with a shrug. "I've read pre-Unification glosses. The vowel structures are wrong. This is something else."

"Something else," he repeated, in the tone priests reserve for heresy and bad debts. "Then what?"

And here is where I should tell you what I actually knew, which was more than I said and less than I understood. Because when the child had spoken that afternoon — really spoken, grabbing my wrist, demanding I answer — the sounds had gone into my ear and down some older road than understanding. They had sounded *right*. The way a key sounds right before you find the lock it belongs to. My grandmother's songs lived in that same country: crooked rhythms, vowels held long at the ends of lines, consonants softened like river stones. Songs she'd sung me to sleep with. Songs she'd told me, when I asked, were just smuggler nonsense, girl-talk, nothing.

I said none of this to Cassian. What I said was, "I'll need more time with her."

What he heard was diligence. What the walls heard, I suspect, was ambition. Neither of us knew yet that the third listener was the dangerous one.

* * *

Three more days. Three more hours in the windowless room, three more pages of transcription that grew longer and stranger, and every evening I carried them back to my cell and did the thing I hadn't dared do in daylight: I compared them against the carvings.

Every smuggler who works the Thornfell passes knows the carvings. You find them in the deep cuts, under the overhangs where weather can't reach them — spirals and running lines and little clustered marks that the trade calls *old-man writing*, and pays no more attention to than it pays to lichen. I'd traced them with my fingers a hundred times while waiting out storms, half-reading them the way you half-read a language you dreamed you once knew. There was one overhang above the Cold Larch trail where the marks ran twenty feet along the stone, and I'd memorized their shapes without meaning to, the way you memorize the face of someone you see every day without ever learning their name.

On the fourth night I laid my transcription beside my memory of those marks, and my hands started shaking.

Because they matched. Not approximately. Not in spirit. Stroke for stroke, curve for curve — the doubled mark she used for the long vowels was the doubled groove in the stone; the little hook she made with two fingers when she repeated her phrase was cut into the lintel-stone above the Cold Larch trail, and above a hundred other places besides, and I had walked beneath it my whole adult life without once asking what it said.

Thornfell. The word rose in me with the weight of a body rising in water. Thornfell, the kingdom that wasn't. Two centuries gone — not conquered, not fallen, per the histories, but *removed*, unwritten, its name scraped from every charter and chiseled off every boundary stone by an edict so thorough that the Concord's own archives contained nothing about it except the fact of the erasure itself. Even the mountains had been renamed. We called them the Highmoor Fells now, politely, the way you'd call a widow by her husband's name.

And a mud-caked child pulled out of a storm was speaking it fluently, and cutting it into the air with her fingers, and demanding — that was the part that kept me awake — *demanding* that I answer.

* * *

The Prior sent for me on the fifth morning.

Prior Aldous Vane received me in his study, which overlooked the temple gardens, which were beautiful, which was the point. Everything about Vane was the point of something. He was a spare, grey man with the kind of face that improved with authority, and he gestured me to a chair with a courtesy that I distrusted immediately and completely, on the principle that kindness from the powerful is a bill presented early.

"Scribe Halloran." He pronounced my whole name, which almost no one did; to the temple I was Halloran, or boy, or you-there. Hearing both names together felt like being dressed in clothes that fit. I hated how much I noticed that. "You've made progress with our guest."

"Our guest," I agreed.

"Brother Cassian tells me the language resists classification." He smiled. It was a good smile. It was designed. "He also tells me you've developed theories. I value theories, Scribe. The Concord is built on them."

Here was the moment to mention the carvings. Here was the moment a better man lays his cards down and lets the institution decide. I opened my mouth to do exactly that — and found I couldn't. Some instinct older than my debt closed my throat around it. The child had grabbed my wrist and demanded an answer, and I realized, sitting in that beautiful room, that I did not want the Concord to have this answer. I didn't know why. I told myself it was scholarly jealousy, possessiveness over a discovery, the small vanity of a small man. That explanation cost me nothing and satisfied everyone, which is how you know an explanation is a lie.

"The language shows internal consistency," I said carefully. "Beyond that, I'd want more sessions before speculating."

"Of course." He rose and walked to the window, hands clasped behind him, contemplating his flowers. "You understand the child cannot remain here indefinitely. The storm that brought her has passed. Questions will be asked about where she came from, whose she is." A pause, measured like everything he did. "There are those in the city who would pay well for a curiosity such as this. Foreign envoys. Collectors. Men who deal in rare things."

The temperature of the room changed. I couldn't have told you how.

"You mean sell her."

"I mean *place* her," Vane said mildly, still looking at the garden, "in circumstances where her... qualities... are best served. The Concord is not a prison, Scribe. It is a stewardship. But stewardship requires resources, and your own account—" he let it hang there, delicately, the way he'd let everything hang, "—remains substantial. Fourteen years of interest compounds impressively."

Fourteen years. He'd looked up my numbers personally. I sat very still and did the arithmetic I'd done ten thousand times: six more years of copying ledgers at current remission rates, if the fees didn't rise, if my health held, if the temple didn't reassess. Six years. And here was the Prior himself suggesting that a few months' diligent service on an unusual assignment might be viewed... favorably.

"I'll continue the work," I said.

"I know you will." He turned from the window then, and looked at me directly, and for half a heartbeat the courtesy dropped and I saw the thing underneath, which was not cruelty exactly — cruelty is passionate — but appetite, patient and cold, an appetite that had already eaten and was deciding what came next. "You have a gift for languages, Scribe Halloran. It would grieve me to see it buried in the ledger rooms. Serve the Concord well in this, and we will discuss your account at winter's end. Personally. Between ourselves."

Between ourselves. I bowed, because bowing was free, and walked out through the beautiful garden, and stood a long moment among the roses feeling like a man who has just signed something without reading it.

* * *

That afternoon, the child gave me the phrase.

She'd been building toward it for days — I could see that now, looking back at my own transcriptions. The same cluster of marks recurred at the start of her sentences like a signature, a formula, and each session she spent longer on it, watching my face with increasing frustration, a teacher failing with a hopeless student. That afternoon she abandoned speech entirely. She took my chalk — snatched it, glared her warning — and knelt on the flagstones and drew the marks large, over and over, until the floor around her was ringed with them.

Spiral. Running line. Doubled vowel-hook. The little clustered marks I knew from the overhang above the Cold Larch trail, cut into stone by hands two hundred years dead.

Then she pointed at the ring on the floor. Pointed at me. Pressed her palm flat against her own chest, hard, hard enough to leave the chalk ghost of her hand on the smock.

And she said it. Slowly. Each syllable placed like a stone in a wall. And this time — this time something in me answered before my mind could intervene, some buried channel my grandmother's songs had kept open without my knowledge, and I heard the phrase not as noise but as *words*, and the words meant —

Well. Ilex would give me the true translation years later, and it would rearrange everything I thought I knew about my family, my grandmother, my own blood. But in that moment, kneeling on a cold floor with a feral child, what I caught was enough. Enough to make the hair stand up on my arms. Enough to make me glance at the door where the guards stood, and lower my voice, and lean close.

The phrase meant something close to: *the fire remembers its keeper.*

She watched me understand it. I saw the exact moment — her whole body eased, her shoulders coming down from around her ears for the first time since she'd arrived, and she exhaled a breath she'd apparently been holding for five days. Then she did the second unexpected thing. She picked up the chalk and held it out to me, handle-first.

Asking me to write it back.

And I — fool, coward, scribe, whatever I was — I took the chalk, and I wrote the marks in my own hand, clumsy but true, and when I finished she took my ink-stained fingers in both her small hands and held them, briefly, like a person testing a rope before trusting it with their weight.

Which was precisely when the door opened, and Prior Vane stepped into the room, flanked by two guards, and looked down at the chalk ring on the floor, and at my hand in the child's grip, and smiled his designed smile.

"Well," he said softly. "Now *this* is progress."

And the dread that settled over me in that moment was not the ordinary dread of a debtor before his creditor. It was older than that, colder, the dread of a man who has just shown a hungry thing exactly where the food is kept — and realized, too late, that he is standing between them.
