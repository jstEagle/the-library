## 5. A Debt Called In

The summons came at the hour when even candles get tired, which in my experience is when the Concord does its best work.

A novice in grey scratched at my cell door — I say *cell* because that's what it was, six feet of stone with a cot and a shelf for my pens, but the ledger called it "accommodation," and the ledger was very good at calling things what they weren't. Debts were "investments." Interest was "stewardship." The collar they made us wear to services was a "mark of fellowship." Somewhere in Highmoor there was a room full of clerks whose entire job was teaching words to lie down with fleas.

"Prior Vane requests the scribe Halloran," the novice said, in the flat recitation of someone who'd been told to say it exactly so and had no interest in improving on scripture. "Immediately."

"Requests," I said. "That's generous of him. It's nearly midnight."

The novice looked at me with the particular blankness of someone who has never once considered that a Prior's request might be declined, and I understood that I was being witty at a wall. I put down my pen — mid-word, which any scribe will tell you is a small death — and followed.

* * *

Vane kept a study at the top of the Scriptorium tower, which everyone understood to be an honor and no one enjoyed receiving. The climb was four hundred steps, by design. You arrived at the Prior's presence already punished, breath short and legs burning, and he received you sitting comfortably by his fire as though your exhaustion were a tribute you'd carried up personally.

He was not what people expected from the descriptions that circulated among the indentured. They imagined a monster, some vast red-faced butcher of a man. What they got instead was a spare, grey, tidy person with kind eyes and ink-stained fingers — a scholar's hands, a shepherd's face — and a voice like a well-read uncle explaining why the beating is for your own good. The eyes were the worst part. They looked at you the way I looked at ledgers: assessing value, noting discrepancies, deciding where the red line went.

"Scribe Halloran." He gestured to the chair opposite him without looking up from the document he was annotating. "Sit. Wine?"

"I don't drink on duty, Your Holiness."

"Duty ended at sundown. This is a private conversation between two men who appreciate precision." He poured two cups anyway, set one within my reach, and returned to his page for a full minute, letting the silence do its work. Silence was another of his tools. I'd seen him deploy it on visiting merchants until they started offering discounts just to hear a voice again.

I sat. I didn't touch the wine. Small defiance; it cost nothing and bought less, but a man has to keep his hand in.

"Do you know," he said at last, setting down his pen, "how much you owe the Concord?"

"To the copper," I said. "It's posted quarterly. Eleven marks, seven crowns, forty-one pennies, as of last month's tally. With this quarter's stewardship it'll be—"

"Twelve marks, three crowns, nine pennies," Vane said. "Yes. I read your file before you were sent for. Do you know what interests me about it? Not the sum. The shape." He leaned back, folding those scholar's hands over his knee. "You came to us at fourteen, bonded against your mother's apothecary debts. In eleven years you have paid down precisely nothing. Not because you're idle — your output is exceptional, Halloran, truly. Best copyist in the eastern scriptoriums, and I say that having read the comparisons myself. No. You pay nothing because every penny above your subsistence goes somewhere we cannot trace. Remittances to a mother now deceased. Purchases of mountain goods no temple man needs. Rope. Cold-weather oil. A second pair of boots, purchased twice in one year."

My face, I am pleased to report, did nothing at all. Inside, I was doing arithmetic at panic speed. He knew about the boots. If he knew about the boots he knew about the passes, or suspected, or suspected enough to spend a midnight summoning me up four hundred stairs rather than simply having me flogged in the yard, which was cheaper and required no wine.

"You guide smugglers through the Thornfell passes," Vane said. Conversationally. As though remarking on weather.

"Your Holiness—"

"Don't insult us both." He said it gently, almost kindly, which was somehow worse than shouting. "I've had eleven years to watch you, Bram Halloran. You think because your treason happens at night and off the books that it doesn't leave a trail. But men who buy rope buy rope from someone. Men who know cold-weather oil have smelled it. And once a year, without fail, a scribe of unblemished conduct takes a fever that keeps him abed for six days while certain wagons move through country only he could navigate. I don't need proof. Proof is for tribunals. I have a *shape*, and the shape is a man who knows the Thornfells better than any living servant of the Concord."

The fire cracked. Down in the scriptorium below us, faint through the floor, I could hear the night shift scratching away at their copies — hundreds of pens, all writing other people's words in their own hands, which is more or less the definition of indenture if you think about it too long, and I tried not to.

"What do you want?" I asked. Because with men like Vane, you skip to it. Courtesy just gives them more silence to work with.

"What I have wanted for twenty years." He rose and went to the window — the tower's high east window, and beyond the glass, black against the lesser black of the sky, stood the Thornfells. Even at midnight I could feel them looking back. "There is a kingdom in those mountains, Halloran. Or there was. You know the name, though you've never said it aloud in this building. Say it now. Indulge me."

"Thornfell," I said.

"Thornfell." He tasted it like wine he'd paid too much for. "Erased from every map. Struck from every record. Two hundred years of diligent forgetting, conducted at ruinous expense by every prior before me, and do you know what I found when I finally asked *why*? Nothing. No reason preserved anywhere. We spent a fortune making a hole in the world and then forgot what the hole was for." His voice stayed mild. His knuckles whitened on the sill. "Until a child walked out of a storm speaking the dead tongue, and I remembered that holes are made to hide something in."

I thought of Wren — the mud-caked scrap they'd pulled through the postern gate three nights ago, all ribs and glaring, speaking syllables that had crawled up my spine because I'd heard their bones before, in my grandmother's kitchen, in songs she swore were nonsense. I thought of the interrogation rooms under the chapel. I thought: *they haven't broken the child yet.* Then I thought: *yet,* and hated the word for how naturally it fit.

"The child knows the way," Vane continued. "Or carries it, or *is* it — our scholars are divided and the child is not cooperative. But keys are not maps, Halloran. A key opens a specific lock. A map shows a man how to reach the door in the first place. I have the key." He turned from the window, and the firelight moved across that kind shepherd face, and I understood I was looking at hunger wearing patience like a coat. "You are the map."

"And if I say no?"

"Then the shape becomes proof, and proof becomes a tribunal, and a tribunal becomes the pits, and your eleven years of careful nothing become eleven years wasted." He said it the way he'd annotate a marginal note. *Cf. the pits. See also: wasted years.* "But I would rather not. Tribunals generate paperwork, and paperwork generates questions, and questions are untidy. I am offering you something better than survival. I am offering you freedom."

He let the word sit there between us, fat and shining.

"Full remission," he said. "Every mark, every crown, every copper. Your bond struck through in red ink by my own hand, witnessed and filed. You walk out of Highmoor a free man with a clean name. All you must do is take a company of my soldiers through the smuggler roads to a door in the Hollow Crown pass. Guide them there. That is all. You needn't fight, needn't touch the child, needn't even understand what waits behind the stone. Just be the map. Then be free."

And here is the thing I want written plainly, because I've had eleven years since to turn it over and I still can't make it prettier: I believed him. That was the horror of it, the thing that shames me more than anything that came after. I sat in that warm room with the wine going flat beside me and I *believed him*, because I wanted to so badly that belief arrived fully formed, no labor required. Twelve marks and change. Eleven years of counting coppers, of guiding strangers through killing cold for scraps, of watching my own life parceled out in quarterly tallies. One road. One walk. Freedom, struck through in red.

"Yes," I heard myself say. My voice sounded like it belonged to a stranger who'd gotten into my body and hadn't yet learned where I kept my regrets. "I'll guide them."

"Wonderful." Vane smiled, and for a moment the shepherd's face was genuinely warm, and that warmth frightened me more than anything else in the room. "We march in three days. There will be documents to sign, of course. Precision is everything."

"Of course," I said, and the stranger using my voice even managed to sound steady.

* * *

They gave me a cell in the guest wing rather than walking me back to the Scriptorium — "for your convenience," the escort said, which meant *so you don't pass anyone who might ask you questions*. I lay on the cot listening to the building settle and told myself stories. Three days. A road I knew better than my own hands. Soldiers who'd freeze and curse and follow because following was all they knew. And at the end of it, a door, and whatever Vane wanted behind it, and me walking away free with the whole sky to spend.

It was a good story. I told it maybe forty times. By the fortieth telling I noticed I'd stopped mentioning what happened to the child afterward, the way you stop mentioning a stone in your boot because the boot still fits.

Near dawn I gave up on sleep and did what I always did with a problem too big to hold: I went looking for the smallest piece of it I could actually examine. In this case, the child. If I was going to sell a road to a cage, I decided — and yes, I was already using words like *sell*, some part of me filing the truth away for later prosecution — I should at least look at the merchandise. That's what I told myself I was doing. Looking. Due diligence. The vocabulary of complicity arrives early and dresses well.

The children's wing had been repurposed overnight. I knew the corridors, every servant's cut-through in that pile of stone, and I took the one behind the laundry cisterns down toward the old crypt stair, where the interrogations lived. Two guards at the bottom, which was two more than usual. They knew my face — everyone knew the copyist with the ink-stains — and one of them grunted me past with the sublime indifference of men assigned to watch a child and finding it beneath their dignity.

The crypt was cold in the way old stone gets, cold that comes up through your boots and settles in your teeth. Torches at intervals. And at the far end, where the oldest alcoves held the bones of priors nobody visited anymore, someone had built a cage.

Not a cell. A cell would have been almost respectable — walls, a door, the fiction of architecture. This was iron barwork, fitted into the alcove mouth like a kennel, and inside it, on bare stone, was Wren.

The child was smaller than I remembered. That's the trick memory plays — it rounds people up. Wren sat with knees drawn up and arms wrapped around them, forehead resting on the iron, watching me come with an absolute stillness that no child owns naturally. Stillness like that is manufactured. Stillness like that is what you learn when moving brings the wrong kind of attention. Mud had dried in the fine hair at the temples. The wrists had marks. Someone had decided restraint was necessary for a creature that weighed perhaps fifty pounds, and had implemented the decision.

Wren's eyes tracked me all the way across the crypt, and they weren't afraid. That unsettled me more than fear would have. They were *assessing* — the same flat valuation I'd felt from Vane an hour ago, the ledger-look, except that where Vane priced me in marks, this child priced me in something I couldn't read. Old currency. Something from before the tallies.

I crouched down out of lunging range, an instinct I didn't examine too closely, and said, "Hello."

Nothing. The stare continued.

"I'm the one who wrote down your words. The other night. The phrases you kept saying." I tapped my own chest. "Bram."

Wren's head tilted a fraction. And then — quietly, in that language with the bones of my grandmother's songs in it — the child spoke. One phrase. The same one from the sessions, the phrase I'd transcribed phonetically a dozen times without knowing what it meant, the phrase that had followed me out of that room like a smell.

Except this time, alone, in the dark, with no Prior's clerk hovering and no quill in my hand — this time I heard it properly. Heard the cadence. Heard where the stress fell, the way my grandmother's voice used to fall on the second word, mocking, teasing, *eat your bread or the hill-wolf gets it.*

I knew what the phrase meant. God help me, I knew, the way you know a tune you've hated all your life. It wasn't a spell or a warning or a prayer.

It was *who will carry me home?*

A question. A child's question, dressed in a dead king's grammar. Asked at me across iron bars by a kid with restraint-marks on both wrists, in a crypt, under a church, in a country that had spent two hundred years pretending the question had never been asked by anyone.

And I had signed — no, not signed, I'd *said yes* upstairs, and the documents were coming in three days, and my signature was the only thing left between intention and fact — I had agreed to lead soldiers to the door this child had walked out of, and hand the child's answer to the man with the kind eyes and the ledger-heart.

Something in my chest went through the ice I'd built it on. Not dramatically. Ice doesn't shatter in real life; it just quietly stops holding, one hairline at a time, and then one morning there's black water under you and you're already in it.

"I don't—" My voice came out wrecked. I cleared my throat. "I'm working on it," I said, in plain trade-tongue, which was a stupid thing to say to a child who didn't speak it, and possibly the truest thing I'd said in eleven years.

Wren watched me for a long moment. Then the child lifted one small hand — marked wrist, chapped knuckles — and pressed it flat against the iron bars. Between the bars. Toward me.

And the hand was *warm*. I felt it from three feet away, a heat like a hearthstone in deep winter, radiating off that small palm, and beneath the heat something else, a pulse, slow and vast and utterly wrong for a child's body, like standing near a door with a furnace behind it. The torches in the crypt guttered, all together, all one direction, as if the flame itself had leaned toward the child to listen.

Wren said the phrase again. Softer this time. *Who will carry me home.*

The pulse in that small hand beat once, hard, and every torch in the crypt went out.

In the dark, with a bargain cooling in my blood like something swallowed that refuses to go down, I heard myself say:

"Three days is a long time."

The torches caught again, one by one, as though embarrassed. Wren's hand dropped. But the child was smiling — barely, the way frost makes patterns rather than expressions — and I stood up in that cold crypt with twelve marks and three crowns and nine pennies of freedom dissolving off me like breath off glass, and I understood that I had just made my second decision of the night.

The first one damned me.

I was praying the second one wouldn't.
