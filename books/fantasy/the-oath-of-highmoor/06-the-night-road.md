## 6. The Night Road

There is a particular quality to the dark beneath a temple that you do not find anywhere else in the world. I don't mean the ordinary darkness of a cellar or a cave — I've slept in both and can recommend neither. I mean the darkness of a place built by people who believed the dark was holy, who dug their crypts deep on purpose, who wanted you to feel the weight of all that stone between you and the sky so you'd remember your proper size.

The Highmoor crypt was very good at reminding me of my proper size. It had been doing it my whole indentured life.

I came down the servants' stair with a scribe's lamp turned low and a thief's heart going like a drum corps, and I told myself, as I had been telling myself for three days, that this was not yet treason. This was reconnaissance. A man could reconnoitre. A man could walk down a stair with an oil lamp and a coil of smuggler's rope and a bag of bread ends and dried apples and think, quite innocently, about doors.

The lie held until I reached the third landing and heard the singing.

It wasn't singing, really. It was the sound Wren made when the lamplighters passed overhead — a low, cracked keening, the same four notes over and over, like a hinge that wanted oiling. The guards called it the ghost-noise. They drew lots to avoid the crypt shift, which suited my purposes admirably, because it meant the two men on duty tonight were the youngest and the most bored, and bored men will trade anything for entertainment, including their attention.

I'd already traded mine for theirs, if you follow me. Two coppers bought from the temple kitchen, wrapped around a flask of plum brandy that Brother Odo distilled in the herb loft and would have flayed me for borrowing. Cheap at the price. Cheaper than hanging, anyway, which was the alternative invoice.

The child was in the old reliquary cell at the end of the east corridor — a room meant for bones, now holding something with a pulse. When my lamp found the grate, Wren was pressed into the far corner, knees up, mud-colored hair hanging in ropes, watching me with eyes that caught the light wrong. Not glowing. I want to be clear about that, because the Prior's reports had begun using words like *luminous* and *incandescent*, and I'd transcribed enough of them to gag a cathedral. The eyes were just eyes. But they watched the way a hawk watches, without blinking, without any visible interest in anything except the geometry of escape.

"Hallo," I said, which is what I say to everyone, because I've never been able to think of anything better.

Wren said nothing. Wren never said anything in plain speech. What Wren did say — and had been saying, one phrase of it, over and over across nine days of transcription sessions — was that thing. That phrase that had crawled out of my grandmother's lullabies and made my hands shake so badly I'd ruined three pages of fair copy.

*Thal en varren.* Low and slow, the way she used to sing it when she thought I was asleep, when she thought no one who mattered was listening. *Thal en varren*, and I'd spent twenty years thinking it was nonsense, smugglers' gibberish, the kind of song-words mountain women make up to keep children quiet on cold passes. And then a mud-caked child had looked up at me through a cage of iron and said it like it was a sentence. Like it was a *question*.

I crouched by the grate, keeping my lamp low, and did the thing I'd practiced against the wall of my cell until it stopped feeling absurd.

I hummed.

Four notes. Her four notes, the ones that went before the phrase, the tune she'd worn smooth as river stone. I hummed them into the dark of a crypt where the Concord kept its saints' knuckles and its debtors' names, and the child in the cage went absolutely still.

Then Wren uncoiled, crossed the floor without a single sound — I have seen smugglers move; this was better, this was *wrong*, this was a body that had learned silence somewhere feral and permanent — and pressed both palms flat against the grate.

The hands were warm. I remember being surprised by that. Everything else about the child read cold: the stare, the stillness, the smell of wet stone. But the hands on the iron were warm as fresh bread, warm past what skin should be, and where they touched, the grate had gone soft-edged, the bars slightly bowed, like wax that had considered melting and thought better of it.

"Right," I said, mostly to steady myself. "Right. We're doing this."

* * *

Here is what I had learned in nine days of transcribing a child for the Concord, and what I had decided, in the small hours of the fourth night, sitting in my cell with my grandmother's songs running through my head like water finding a crack:

They were going to kill Wren. Not quickly, and not out of cruelty — that was the part that sat in my stomach like a swallowed stone. Prior Aldous Vane did not hate the child. Vane didn't hate anything; hatred required passion, and Vane was a man who filed his passions under correspondence. He was going to use Wren the way you use a key — and when the lock was open, keys get left in doors. There was a page in his private ledger — I copied everything, it's a sickness — where he'd written *the vessel's duration post-extraction: unknown*. Unknown. He'd underlined it twice. Somewhere in that mild scholarly handwriting was a dead child, pre-scheduled, and nobody down here but me had even noticed the ink was wet.

And there was the other thing. The vent above the Prior's study, the one I'd pressed my ear to while pretending to inventory candle stock, and the word that had come through it in Vane's level, reasonable voice: *detonation*. The word *scorched*, applied to mountains. The word *acceptable losses*, applied to — well. Applied to everyone, when you got right down to it. Everyone except Aldous Vane, who intended to be standing somewhere sensible when the fire he'd stolen finished burning.

I am not a brave man. Let's establish that now, while we're being honest, because the ballads — if anyone ever writes ballads, and given how this ended I sincerely hope someone does — will want to make me brave, and I'd like the record corrected early. I am a coward with a good memory and a working knowledge of back stairs. What I did that night, I did because the alternative was to stay in a building with a man who wrote *unknown* and underlined it, and my cowardice, it turns out, has always been more afraid of complicity than of cliffs.

Also — and I admit this freely — there was the song. My grandmother's voice, thirty years dead, humming through a stranger's mouth. You can't leave a thing like that in a cage. Whatever else she lied about, and I've since learned she lied about nearly everything, she didn't sing that tune for it to end underlined in a prior's ledger.

So. Back stairs, borrowed brandy, bribed guards, a coil of rope, and a scribe's lamp with its shutter turned to a thread.

The lock on the reliquary cell was temple work, which is to say it was built to keep out peasants and had never once imagined a man who'd spent his nights letting smugglers through doors that officialdom believed were walls. I had it open in the time it takes to say three Aveils, and I'm not proud of how fast that was, or rather I am, enormously, but it seems tasteless to boast.

Wren came out of the corner slowly, the way animals come out when they've decided you're probably not a trap but haven't ruled it out. Up close, unwashed, unlit by anything but my shuttered lamp, the child was smaller than the transcripts suggested. Nine years old, maybe ten. Thin as a winter branch. And radiating heat like a hearthstone — I felt it a full arm's length away, warmth pressing against my face in a room where the cold came up through the floor from sixty feet of consecrated rock.

"Don't," I whispered, holding up a hand as Wren reached for the lamp. "No light but mine. There are guards, and monks, and one very large dog named Doctrine who has opinions about running footsteps."

Wren stared at me. Then, softly, in that language older than every map it had been erased from: "*Thal en varren?*"

"I know," I said, and heard my own voice go rough. "I know it. Don't ask me how. Ask me when we're somewhere the stones aren't listening."

* * *

The blind trail out of Highmoor is not on any map, because maps are how the Concord owns things, and the first law of smuggling — my grandmother's first law, though she framed it as her second, because her actual first law was *never trust a man who explains his laws* — is that ownership stops at the edge of the known road.

There is a drainage channel behind the tannery sheds that isn't a drainage channel. It runs forty feet, drops through a grate older than the temple itself, and becomes a fissure in the hillside that the masons sealed on paper three hundred years ago and never sealed in fact. Smugglers call it the Gullet. I call it the reason I'm alive to write this, and I have never once been able to find it in the dark without counting steps, so I counted, lips moving, Wren's breath hot against my neck where the child clung to my back like a burr with opinions.

Because cling Wren did. I hadn't expected it. I'd braced for teeth, honestly — I'd heard what happened to the acolyte who tried to change the chamber pot — but the moment we dropped through the Gullet's grate into true darkness, thin arms locked around my throat and did not let go for four hours.

I'd like to tell you I found that moving. Mostly I found it suffocating. Trust is heavy when it arrives all at once and lands on your windpipe.

We went up. That's the trick of the Thornfell foothills that no Concord soldier has ever internalized, because soldiers think in roads and roads go where the empire wants. Smugglers think in goat paths, in sheep-cut ledges, in the spines of ridges where a man on horseback might as well be a man in a painting. By the time the temple bells rang terce — I counted the strokes drifting up from below, faint as memory — we were above the cloud line in the first folds of the hills, and the rain had started, and I had made my first serious tactical error.

Which was stopping.

In my defense, I was carrying a child, a rope, bread, a lamp I couldn't light, and roughly forty years of family guilt, and my legs had submitted their resignation. I found a hollow under a leaning slab of granite — my grandmother would have called it a *kist*, one of her words, another piece of the erased kingdom hiding in plain speech — and I put us both in it and let the rain close over us like a curtain.

That was when Wren bit me.

Not a warning bite. Not a fear bite. A *decision*, delivered to the meat of my forearm with the full sincere commitment of a creature that has eaten worse than me and liked it less. I yelped — a scribe's yelp, undignified, echoing off the hills like a dying goose — and Wren was gone, out of the kist and into the rain and the dark, moving uphill on all fours with a speed that made my eyes report impossible things to my brain.

"Wren!" I hissed into the storm, like an idiot. "Wren, the soldiers, the *dogs*—"

Nothing. Rain. The dark ate the child whole, and I sat in a hole in a mountain with blood running down my arm and forty miles of hostile country ahead, and I laughed. I want that on the record too. I sat there in the freezing wet and I laughed until it turned into something else, because of course. Of course. Nine days of careful transcription, of earned half-trust, of a child pressing warm hands to iron — undone in one night by my presumption that a cage opened makes a companion. Feral means feral. I'd read the word in the Prior's briefings often enough. I'd simply never understood it was a complete sentence.

I bound my arm with a strip of shirt, chewed the last apple dry, and did the only intelligent thing available to me, which was nothing. I stayed in the kist and waited for dawn, because a blind trail in daylight is merely difficult and in the dark is a way to die with dignity intact, and I have never managed dignity even on flat ground.

I did not sleep. Instead I lay with my ruined arm throbbing and my grandmother's voice in my ears, and for the first time in my life I listened to the songs properly. Not as tunes. As *text*.

*Thal en varren* — the door remembers. I knew that now; the child's phrase, the carvings in the deep passes, the thing Brother Odo's forbidden lexicon fragments circled around without ever naming. But there were others. *Kessa mora, kessa dain* — carry the low road, carry the flame. *Venn a thurra, venn a skarn* — hmm. Hmm. That one had always sounded like nonsense about hens. I lay in the dark mouthing syllables my mouth remembered and my mind didn't, and somewhere in the smallest hours I began, very quietly, without deciding to, to hum.

Her tune. The four notes. Then the verse under it, the one I'd carried since I was six, the one about the green country behind the grey door where nobody goes hungry and nobody kneels. I hummed it into the granite like a prayer to a god I didn't believe in, which is, when you think about it, exactly what a prayer is.

* * *

Dawn came grey and rinsed-out, the rain thinned to mist, and the Thornfells stood up around me in layers of blue and slate like something being assembled by a patient god with a poor sense of proportion. I climbed out of the kist, took a bearing off a split crag I'd known since I was fifteen, and turned around.

Wren was sitting six feet away, cross-legged, soaked to the bone, watching me.

I don't know how long. I don't know if the child had come back in the night and sat there in the dark listening to me hum, or arrived with the mist, or had never left at all and simply chosen the moment. Feral children do not explain themselves, and I have since learned that asking them to is like asking weather for references.

We looked at each other. My arm throbbed. The bite mark, I'd see later, was oddly clean — two points, precise, no tearing. A message, maybe. Or a test.

"You ran off," I said.

Wren said nothing.

"It's raining, you're soaked, you're nine years old, and you bit the only person in the world currently on your side." I held up the bandaged arm. "This was unnecessary. I was already committed."

Wren's eyes went to the bandage, then to my face, and something happened in that small mud-streaked countenance that I would spend months learning to read and never fully master: a flicker, a crease, the ghost of an expression that on a courtier would have been called *guilty* and on Wren was closer to *recalculation*. The child had bitten me expecting one kind of creature. Whatever I'd done overnight — lying in a hole humming dead songs instead of running back down to sell the child's location for my debt struck through, which yes, I'll confess, had been on the menu, right up until it wasn't — had not matched the expectation.

"*Thal en varren,*" Wren said. Quietly. Not a question this time. An assessment.

"The door remembers," I answered, in smuggler-gibberish that turned out to be the tongue of an erased kingdom, and the look Wren gave me then nearly knocked me off the ledge. Because I'd said it *right*. I heard it land right, in the way the child's whole body went still and the head tilted, and I understood that in nine days of interrogation no one — not the scholars, not the Prior with his careful transliteration grids — had pronounced those three words the way a grandmother hums them to a child on a cold pass. Accent matters. Love in the accent matters.

Wren got up. Walked over. Took hold of the hem of my coat with one ember-warm hand — gently, this time, the way you take hold of something you've decided not to destroy — and pointed uphill. Toward the deep passes. Toward the country that wasn't on any map.

"No," I said. "Absolutely not. That's a week's climb through country where the Concord's patrols double after tomorrow, and we've got bread for two days, and you bit me."

The hand didn't let go. The pointing finger didn't lower. There is a species of stubbornness that doesn't argue, doesn't plead, doesn't negotiate — it simply *is*, the way stone is — and I stood on a wet ledge arguing with it and losing, and some traitor part of my chest was quietly, treacherously glad.

"We need help," I said finally. "Food, shelter, a route the soldiers won't find. Which means Sera Voss, which means the low passes, which means—"

Wren tilted their head. Waiting. The ember-warm hand still knotted in my coat hem, and I realized with a strange lurch that the child had understood none of the words and all of the meaning: *we go together.*

"Fine," I said, to a nine-year-old, in the rain, with my grandmother's song stuck in my throat like a bone. "Fine. Together. But if you bite me again, I'm teaching you to copy ledgers, and then you'll understand what real suffering is."

And Wren — Wren smiled. Small, crooked, rusty, like a gate opening for the first time in two hundred years. Then the child turned uphill after all, one step, two, and stopped, and looked back at me with that hawk-stare softened into something almost like patience, and waited for me to lead, because apparently even feral children can tell when the grown-up knows the way.

I took one last look down the mountain. Far below, thin as scratches on slate, the road from Highmoor showed a knot of movement. Riders. Four, maybe five, fanning out from the temple's shadow into the foothills with the unhurried confidence of men hunting something that couldn't possibly be fast enough.

They'd found the empty cell already, then. Or they'd found my bribed guards, which amounted to the same thing and would end considerably worse for the guards.

"Right," I said, and turned my back on the life I'd owed for eleven years, and started up the blind trail with a dragon's last heartbeat holding onto my coat hem. "Hold on. It gets steeper."

Behind us, faint on the wind, a horn sounded in the foothills. Long, then short, then long.

The Concord had a name for that signal. So, as it happened, did the smugglers.

It meant the passes were closing.
