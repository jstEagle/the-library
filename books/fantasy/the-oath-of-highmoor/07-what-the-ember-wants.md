## 7. What the Ember Wants

The warm thing is awake again.

It wakes like that — not all at once but in pieces, the way bread rises, the way morning comes through a crack in a wall. First my chest goes hot. Then my hands. Then behind my eyes there are pictures that are not mine, and I have to sit very still or they spill out of me.

I am sitting still now. In a hole under the ground. Under the temple of the ash-smelling men.

I know it's a hole because the air tastes like stone and old water and nothing growing. I don't like holes. But the man with ink on his fingers put me here, and the men with spears were loud outside the door, so I made myself small and quiet, which I am good at, and waited for the warm thing to tell me what to do.

It doesn't tell me things. That's the trouble. It only *shows* me.

* * *

Here is what it shows me this time:

A country where everything is green. Not the green you see in one field, going to seed, half-starved. A whole *country* of it, hills rolling like sleeping animals, rivers bright as pulled wire, trees so tall their tops are lost in cloud. There is a smell that comes with the picture — cut grass, woodsmoke, rain on hot stone — and when the smell comes I cry without deciding to, the way you sneeze.

There is a door in the picture too. Big doors, dark wood banded in iron, set into a mountainside, and carved over them words I cannot read but the warm thing can, because when we get near them in the picture the warm thing makes a sound inside me like a struck bell.

I asked it once — out loud, in the dark, in my own words, the few I have — *what is your name?*

It showed me a wing.

That was its answer. A wing, black at the edge, gold underneath, wide enough to shade a village. And then it showed me the wing folded down and never rising again, and after that it showed me fire going into a small place, a very small place, a place about the size of —

Oh.

About the size of me.

* * *

I did not always have the warm thing. Or maybe I did and I didn't know. It's hard to remember being small. I remember mud. I remember a woman's voice singing, low, in the language with the long words — the language the ink-fingered man knows some of, which surprised me, because nobody is supposed to know it anymore. I remember running. Running is most of what I remember. Running and eating things I found and sleeping in places shaped like bowls of stone, and always, always, the feeling of being followed by something that wasn't a wolf.

The something-that-wasn't-a-wolf was probably the warm thing itself. It hums when I sleep. Wolves don't hum.

* * *

The ash-smelling men came three days ago. Maybe four. Time is slippery underground.

I smelled them before I saw them, the way you smell a fire before you see smoke. They smell wrong. Not dirty-wrong — some of them are clean as new coins — but *wrong*-wrong, like the air after lightning, like a burnt nest. Like ending. Everything they walk past smells a little more dead afterward. I watched a moth land on one of their cloaks and the moth flew away crooked.

They wanted me because of the pictures. The tall one with the white collar and the cold eyes — the Prior, the others call him, the way you say a word you're afraid of — he put his hand flat on my chest, right where the warmth lives, and he smiled, and his smile smelled worse than his robes.

"There you are," he said. Not to me. To the thing inside me. Nobody has ever spoken to the thing inside me before except me, and I didn't like him doing it. My skin crawled and the warm thing flared, and the candle nearest us guttered sideways as if a door had opened somewhere, and the Prior smiled wider.

He thinks the warm thing is a treasure. I can hear it in how he breathes around me. Men breathe differently around treasures. I've seen smugglers breathe that way over a strongbox.

But it isn't a treasure. It's a *person*. Or it was. Or part of one. And it's tired, so tired, and every day the pictures come a little dimmer, the bell-sound a little quieter, and I lie awake in whatever bowl of stone or hole of ground we're hiding in and I press my hand to my own chest and I think: *not yet. Stay. Not yet.*

I don't know who I'm asking. That's the worst part. I'm asking the only thing that's ever been with me, to not leave me, and it can't answer, it can only show me a wing, and a door, and a country so green it hurts.

* * *

The ink-fingered man came down today. Bram. I learned his name from the guards talking above; they said it like a curse, *Halloran*, and spat after it, so I decided I liked him immediately. Anyone the loud men hate is worth at least one chance.

He sat across from my cage — it's a cage, bars, real ones, they don't pretend it's anything else — and he didn't talk much. He had a slate and chalk, and he said words in the long language, slow, watching my mouth.

Most of them he says wrong. His grandmother taught him songs, he told me once before, back when I bit him and ran and came back anyway, and the songs are like my language the way a river in a painting is like a river. Close enough to drink from if you squint.

But one phrase he got right. He always gets this one right.

*Thal en vora,* he said. *Thal en vora.*

And my mouth did it before I decided anything, the way my hand goes to a berry before my head asks if it's poisoned:

*Thal en vora. Thal en vora.*

Give it freely. That's what it means. Give it freely. I know that the way I know which mushrooms kill you — not from learning, from *inside*, like the knowledge grew in me alongside my teeth.

The warm thing stirs when I say it. It leans toward the words like a plant toward light. And when Bram writes it down on his slate, his face does something complicated — hope and fear fighting over the same piece of ground — and the warm thing shows me, quick as a spark, a picture of *him*: the ink-fingered man standing in front of big dark doors, older than he is now, his hand raised, saying words I haven't heard yet.

Then the picture is gone. Pictures are like that. Fish. You grab and they slip.

* * *

Here is a thing I have never told anyone, because there has never been anyone to tell:

I am lonely in a way that has no bottom.

Not hungry-lonely, though I am often that too. Not scared-lonely. Lonely like a cup that keeps being filled and never fills up. Because everything I love is either gone or dying or inside me where I can't hold its hand.

The woman who sang — gone. I looked for her for two winters and found only her song living in my mouth like a tenant who pays no rent.

The green country — gone, or hidden, or maybe never real, though the warm thing insists with such stubbornness that I believe it the way I believe in my own hands.

The dragon — I know that's what it is now, I worked it out from the wing and the size of the shadow in the pictures — the dragon is *dying*, dying in my chest by inches, spending itself to keep me warm in winter and show me beautiful lies about hills, and someday soon the last of it will go out like an ember dropped in snow, and I will be just a girl again, a mud-colored girl who talks in a dead language, and everyone who wants me will stop wanting me, and I don't know whether that will be a rescue or the end of the world.

Probably both. Most big things are both.

* * *

Tonight the Prior came down with two guards and a box lined in grey cloth, and he looked at me the way the smugglers looked at the strongbox, and he said to the guards, "She sleeps eventually. When she sleeps, take the blood. A thimbleful. The scribe's transcription suggests resonance responds to —" and then he used long cold words that meant nothing to me except that they meant *taking*.

Taking.

My whole body understood before my head did. The warm thing surged up my throat and out through my teeth before I could swallow it, and the candles in the crypt all bent toward me like grass in wind, and the grey-cloth box cracked straight down its seam, neat as an egg, and the Prior stepped back and his cold eyes went *hungrier*, which I would not have believed possible, and he said, softly, softly, "Magnificent," and left.

And I curled up on the cage floor with my arms around my knees and my forehead on my shins, and I shook, and the warm thing wrapped itself around the shaking like a blanket, doing the only kind thing it knows, which is heat.

*I won't let them,* I told it. *Nobody takes you. If you go anywhere, you go free. Thal en vora. That's the rule. That's always been the rule.*

It showed me the door again. And this time, for the first time, it showed me the door *open* — open onto green country, open onto sky so wide and blue it looked like the sea had gotten ideas — and standing in the doorway, small against all that light, a shape. Two shapes. One small. One taller, ink-stained, familiar.

Then it faded, and the bell-sound faded, and the warm thing settled low in my chest to wait, patient as stone, patient as winter, and I lay in the dark of the cage and understood, finally, with a child's terrible clarity, what I actually am.

I'm not a treasure. I'm not a key, whatever the Prior thinks keys are for.

I'm a letter. A letter written in fire, sealed for two hundred years, carried by a dying hand from a burning country to — wherever I'm supposed to be delivered. And letters don't get to choose their readers. Letters just get *carried*.

Except.

Except the ink-fingered man writes on his slate in my language and his hand shakes a little when the warm thing flares, and I don't think he wants to read me. I think he wants to *answer* me. There's a difference. Even I can feel the difference, and I'm eight, or nine, or however old mud-children count themselves.

Above me, boots. The door at the top of the stairs opening. Bram's smell cutting through the ash-and-ending smell of the guards — ink, tallow, cold mountain air caught in wool. Coming down the steps fast, too fast for a visit, fast like a man who has heard something at a vent and made a decision his hands haven't confessed to yet.

I press my face to the bars.

"Thal en vora," I whisper to him, before he can speak, before anyone can stop us. "Thal en vora."

His eyes go wide in the dark. And somewhere far above us, faint through the stone, the temple bells begin to ring — wrong, urgent, all of them at once, the alarm-pattern, the run-pattern — and Bram looks up at the sound and then back down at me, and his hand closes around the bars of my cage.
