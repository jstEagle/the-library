## 8. The Smuggler Queen

There are three ways into the Thornfell foothills from the temple lands, and I'd walked all of them in the dark at one time or another, usually with someone else's contraband on my back and my heart going like a debtor's knock. But there was only one way to do what needed doing now, and that way ran through Sera Voss.

I want it noted, for the record of whatever god keeps ledgers on men like me, that I did not want to go. The plan had been simpler: walk north until the mountains swallowed us, live off roots and Wren's uncanny talent for finding things — berries, springs, the one dry hollow in a wet night. A week, maybe two, and then... what? I hadn't gotten that far. Fugitives rarely do. You run first and furnish your future later.

But by the third morning it was raining sideways, Wren hadn't eaten anything but two fistfuls of rowan berries and something they'd dug out of a streambank that I chose not to examine, and the Concord would have dogs on our trail by now if Vane had any sense, which he did, in the way a knife has an edge. Alone, we were dead in a fortnight. Dead slower than a fortnight, actually — dead by inches, cold and hungry and stupid, which is the worst kind.

Sera Voss, then.

Here's what you should know about Sera, since you'll be hearing her name for the rest of this story. She ran the mountain roads the way a spider runs a web — except the spider doesn't charge tolls, and Sera charged everyone. Smugglers paid her a cut to use her passes. Villagers paid her to keep soldiers off their backs. The Concord paid her to look the other way, and she took their coin and looked exactly as far away as suited her and not one inch further. They called her the queen of the mountain roads, half as a joke and half because nobody who said it fully meant it as one. She was forty-some years old, built like a doorframe, missing two fingers on her left hand from a winter I once heard her describe as "educational," and she could out-negotiate a temple prior before breakfast without raising her voice above the level of a woman discussing cheese.

I had run her goods through the passes for six years. She knew me as reliable, discreet, and expensive to replace. She also knew I'd been temple-indentured the whole time, which meant she knew I was a man who could be owned, and Sera Voss did not extend trust to owned men. Owned men got prices. Free men got loyalty, sometimes, if they earned it. I was about to find out which column I fell into now that the temple no longer held my leash — or rather, now that I'd snapped the leash and was running with the temple's most valuable asset under my arm like a stolen ham.

* * *

We came down to her hold at dusk, when the light does the lying for you. It wasn't much to look at: an old waystation wedged into a fold of the hills where three smuggler trails met, stone walls two feet thick, a gate of black oak banded with iron, a yard full of mud and mules and men who stopped talking the moment we stepped into view, because that's the kind of place it was. Conversation resumed only after inspection.

The gate guard was a boy of maybe seventeen with a crossbow he held like he'd read about them somewhere. He looked at me, then at Wren, then back at me.

"Name," he said.

"Halloran. Tell her Bram Halloran wants to buy passage."

"Tell her yourself." He didn't move. Behind him, through the oak, someone laughed at something, low.

"I would, but she'll want to know I came alone and unarmed before she sees my face, and telling you saves her the trouble of being annoyed."

That got a flicker of respect. Boys like that respect nothing so much as a man who talks like the outcome is already settled. He banged on the gate, muttered through a crack, and presently the oak groaned open and there she was.

Sera Voss stood in the gateway with a lamp held at hip height and her weight settled like she owned the ground beneath it, which legally she might have; the paperwork on that particular fold of hill had been lost in a fire decades ago, and Sera had been the only witness. She looked older than my memory of her, or perhaps memory had simply flattered us both. Her eyes went over me the way a customs officer goes over cargo: thorough, unimpressed, itemized.

"Bram Halloran," she said. "Last I heard, you were ink-stained and indentured. Now you're mud-stained and armed. Either is an improvement."

"Sera."

"Don't 'Sera' me. You look like a man about to ask for something, and men about to ask for something always start with familiarity." She lifted the lamp toward Wren, who had positioned themselves behind my left leg with the practiced economy of a creature that had survived by not being seen. "And this?"

"A child I'm responsible for."

"That's new." Something moved across her face — not softness, exactly. Recalculation. "You never struck me as a man who took responsibility for anything he wasn't paid to. Come in before the rain finishes the job. And Bram —" her voice dropped, just for me — "if you're bringing trouble through my gate, you'll pay for it in advance. I don't sell credit anymore. Learned that lesson too."

The yard smelled of mule, woodsmoke, and wet wool. A dozen of her people watched us cross it with the frank appraisal of people who assessed strangers for a living: threat value, purse value, burial cost. Wren pressed closer to me. I put a hand on their shoulder — the first time I'd done that deliberately rather than to steer them out of danger — and felt them allow it, the way you allow weather.

Inside the hall, a fire, trestle tables, the general furniture of a business conducted partly in goods and partly in fear. Sera sat at the head table, waved me into a chair across from her, and waited while bread and hard cheese appeared in front of us. Wren stared at the bread like it might be a trap. I broke off a piece and ate some myself first, and only then did they eat, fast and furtive, watching the room between bites.

"So," Sera said, when the child had food down and I'd had enough to remember I was a negotiator. "Passage. Where, how far, and why should I risk my roads on a temple scribe turned runaway?"

"The high passes. All the way to the deep country. Off every road the Concord watches."

"Off every road I watch, too, then. That's a harder sale." She turned her tankard a quarter-turn on the table, a habit I remembered: it was how she bought time while she counted your tells. "And why?"

"You know why."

"I know rumors. Rumors say the Concord's riders are crawling up every trail from here to Greywater, asking after a scribe and a child. Rumors say the High Prior himself has declared it a matter of faith. When Prior Aldous Vane declares something a matter of faith, Bram, men start dying in quantities that upset my margins." She leaned forward. "So tell me the truth, and tell it plain. What is the child?"

"They're a person."

"They're cargo you're carrying through my mountains, and cargo gets weighed." Her voice stayed level, conversational, which from Sera was more frightening than shouting. "Six years you worked for me. Good work. Quiet work. I'd have vouched for you anywhere on these roads. So vouch for yourself now. Why does the Concord want a child badly enough to burn villages looking?"

I looked at Wren. Wren looked back, mouth greased with cheese, eyes ancient and unreadable, and I understood suddenly that whatever I said next would be heard by both of them, and that lying to either would cost me the other.

"Because the child carries something," I said. "Something old. Something the Concord has wanted for two hundred years, and thinks it can take." I paused. "It can't be taken. That's the part they haven't understood yet."

Sera was quiet for a long moment. Around us the hall noise continued — dice, argument, a mule complaining somewhere outside — but our table had become its own small country with its own weather.

"Carries what?" she asked, quieter.

"I've seen it once. In the crypt, when I got them out. The child's hand was on iron and the iron glowed orange, like a forge, and the child wasn't burned. I've spent six years hauling goods past every strange thing these mountains have, Sera, and I've never seen that. Neither have you. Don't pretend otherwise."

"No," she admitted. "I haven't." And then, because she was Sera: "But I've seen conjurors melt solder. I've seen pilgrims bleed from statues. Wonder is cheap, Bram. It's the most counterfeited coin in the world. Give me something I can't counterfeit."

"I can't," I said. "They're a child, not a trick. What I can give you is this: the Concord isn't chasing a fugitive. They're chasing a weapon they think belongs to them. If they get it, they won't stop at the mountains. You know Vane. You know what the Concord does when it decides something is holy. Your roads will be theirs, your tolls will be tithes, and the word 'queen' will be a joke told in temples."

"And if I refuse? You'll walk out of here and be dead in a month, and my life continues exactly as profitable."

"Yes," I said honestly. "That's the likely arithmetic. I'm not here to threaten your margins. I'm here because you're the only person in these mountains who moves heavy things quietly, and I need you to move us."

She studied me for a long moment, and I braced for the price. It would be steep. Everything Sera sold was steep; that was the point of her. Passage to the deep passes, escort, silence — I'd have signed away years. I'd already served one master to the copper; signing another seemed fitting, and I hated myself a little for how easily my mind reached for the ledger, how naturally slavery arranged itself into columns.

Then Wren reached for the latch.

I saw it happen almost too late. The child had finished eating, and while the adults performed their theater of negotiation, Wren had been exploring the table edge, the bench, the wall — the methodical inventory of a creature mapping territory. At the end of the table stood an iron lantern-hook set into the stone, and beside it the great iron latch-plate of the hall's inner door, polished by generations of hands. Wren touched it the way they touched everything: flat-palmed, deliberate, listening.

The iron began to glow.

Not flicker — glow, steady, the color of a smith's patience, orange sinking toward white at the point of contact. Heat rolled off it in a visible shimmer. The smell of hot metal filled the hall. Wren made no sound at all. Their face wore the expression of someone humming.

Half the hall was on its feet. Dice scattered. Someone swore with real feeling. Sera rose without hurry, which was somehow worse than if she'd shouted, and walked around the table to stand at arm's length from the glowing plate. Heat beat against her face. Her people were reaching for weapons and she stopped them with two raised fingers, never taking her eyes off the child.

"Wren," I said carefully. "Let go."

Wren let go. The glow died slowly, reluctantly, like a sunset deciding whether it meant it. The latch-plate hissed, ticked, and settled — and where five generations of hands had polished it smooth, there was now a shallow depression, a thumbprint of melted metal, cooling from orange to grey to black.

Silence, of the total kind. Even the dice players had stopped.

Wren looked up at Sera Voss, and said — in that language no scribe could read, in the tongue of a kingdom erased from every map — something short and definite. Then they climbed down from the bench, walked to the hearth, and sat down among the dogs as if nothing whatsoever had occurred, and one of the dogs, after due deliberation, put its head in their lap.

Sera stood looking at the latch for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice had changed registers entirely — lower, stripped of the market-floor polish, the voice underneath the voice.

"How long," she said, "have you known about this?"

"Since the crypt. Four days."

"Four days." She laughed once, without humor. "Four days, and you brought it to the one woman in these mountains whose whole trade is knowing what things are worth before anyone else does." She touched the cooled depression in the iron with her remaining fingers, gently, the way you touch a scar. "Do you know what the Concord would pay for proof of this? Do you know what they'd pay for the child?"

"Yes. That's why we're running."

"Mm." She turned and looked at Wren — at the child sitting in the firelight with a dog's head in their lap, eating crumbs off their own sleeve with perfect unconcern, as though the world's most dangerous miracle hadn't just happened at the dinner table. "And what does the child want?"

"They want to not be caught," I said. "Beyond that, I think they want to go home. Somewhere in the deep passes. There's a place they keep walking toward, like a compass needle. I think it's where they came from."

Sera was quiet again. I watched her do the arithmetic — and I mean that literally; I watched her lips move faintly, tallying, the way she tallied everything, risk against return, coin against blood. I had seen her turn away desperate men before. I had seen her sell out a partner once, years ago, a man named Corwin who skimmed from her and got sold to the Concord's magistrates for enough silver to rebuild her eastern storehouse after it burned. Sera Voss was not sentimental. Sentiment, in her trade, was a hole below the waterline.

But she kept looking at the child, and something in her face was doing sums that didn't come out even.

"Corwin," she said abruptly. "Did I ever tell you about Corwin?"

"You sold him."

"I sold him, yes. And for six years I've told myself it was arithmetic." She flexed her mutilated left hand, the one missing two fingers. "He was worth more sold than kept. Clean sum. Except lately I notice I keep the sum and lose the sleep, which means the ledger's wrong somewhere." She turned back to me. "Here's my price, Halloran, and it isn't silver. Passage, escort, my roads, my silence — you'll have all of it. In exchange, the child stays under my protection while they're in my mountains. Not yours. Mine. Whatever they are, whatever they carry, they travel as my people, and anyone — Concord, king, or god — who comes for them comes through me first. That's the price. Take it or walk back into the rain."

"That's not a price," I said. "That's a gift with threats attached."

"It's both. Most good arrangements are." She stuck out her right hand. "Well?"

I hesitated one heartbeat longer — long enough to understand that I was handing over the only precious thing I had to a woman who measured everything, and that this was either the stupidest act of my life or the first smart one. Then I shook it.

Her grip was brief and crushing, a merchant sealing terms. But when she turned away from me and crouched down by the hearth — this queen of the mountain roads, this appraiser of souls — she did it slow, at the child's eye level, hands visible and empty.

"Wren," she said. "Can you say that again? What you said at the table?"

Wren regarded her. Then, gravely, they repeated the phrase from the doorway of a dead kingdom, and Sera nodded as though she'd understood every syllable, and maybe in the way that matters, she had.

"Right," she said, standing, and her voice carried the length of the hall, and every cutthroat and mule-thief in it straightened. "Change of business. This child is under the Voss mark from tonight. Anyone sells them, talks about them, or so much as dreams about them wrong answers to me personally. We move tomorrow, before first light, the high trail past Greywater. And somebody get that latch replaced before the Concord hears the mountains have started making miracles in my front hall."

Laughter, ragged and real, went around the room. The dogs resettled. Wren, wrapped now in a blanket some woman had fetched without being asked, was asleep within minutes, sitting up, chin on chest, dog still pinned under one small hand.

I sat across from Sera at the head table while her people dispersed to their tasks, and the fire cracked, and outside the rain kept up its endless bookkeeping on the roof.

"You know what happens next," she said quietly. "Riders found our ambush site two days south. They'll trace the trail here eventually. Days, not weeks."

"I know."

"And you know Vane won't send constables. He'll send soldiers. Real ones."

"I know that too."

She turned her tankard a quarter-turn and looked into the fire. "Six years I've watched the Concord grow fat on these mountains, Halloran, and I priced my anger like everything else and sold it piecemeal and called it wisdom." A pause. "Tonight I find I've stopped believing my own ledgers. I don't know when that happened. Possibly just now. Possibly four days ago in a crypt I'll never see." She glanced at me sidelong, wry again, the queen reassembled. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we find out what your miracle eats, because it's eating with my household now, and I budget for mouths."

I slept in the straw of the stable with Wren curled against my side, warmer than any child had a right to be, breathing slow and even, and dreamed — I swear this — of green country.

I woke before dawn to shouting in the yard.
