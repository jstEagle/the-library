## 9. Ash on the Wind

The trouble with running from an institution is that institutions have cavalry.

Sera Voss had said something like that on our second morning in the foothills, though she'd phrased it less poetically and with more swearing. She'd also been right, which was the annoying part. Sera was right the way a landslide is right: inevitably, and usually while you were standing underneath it.

We'd made camp in a hollow above the Greywater ford, a smuggler's rest she called the Kettle because the mist off the river boiled up through it at dawn and made everything look like it was being cooked. There were eleven of Sera's people now, not counting me, Wren, or the two mules whose names I never learned because they bit anyone who tried to introduce themselves. We'd spent three days doing what smugglers do when they're waiting for something bad to arrive: nothing, expertly, with knives sharpened.

Wren spent those days learning how to be near people without biting them. Progress was measured in small victories. On day one, Wren sat twelve feet from our fire. By day three, Wren sat six feet away, ate half of a shared loaf instead of snatching all of it, and only hissed once, at Ferren, who'd made the mistake of reaching across the child's plate. I understood the hissing. Ferren reached across my plate once too, back in the temple refectory, and I'd have hissed if it hadn't cost a week's wages in fines.

I'd like to tell you I spent those days composing noble thoughts about freedom and the road ahead. What I actually did was count. It's what scribes do; it's what debt does to you. Eleven fighters against however many riders Vane could scrape together from the garrison at Coldmarch, which was forty at full strength, minus sickness and desertion, call it thirty-two, plus whatever outriders he'd pulled from the toll-houses. I counted the arrows in Sera's quivers. I counted the days since we'd fled Highmoor. I counted the coins left in my purse, which was a short exercise, and then counted them again in case arithmetic had improved overnight.

"You're doing the face," Sera said, dropping onto the log beside me. She had a whetstone and one of her endless collection of knives, this one curved like a question mark.

"What face?"

"The ledger face. The one where you're tallying up all the ways we're going to die." She drew the blade along the stone with a sound like a slow sigh. "Save yourself the ink. There aren't enough numbers."

"Thirty-two," I said. "Riders, give or take."

"Give or take." She smiled, which for Sera meant baring teeth at the world until it apologized. "You always were a cheerful client, Halloran."

"I'm not your client anymore."

"No," she agreed. "Clients pay." But she said it warmly, by Sera's standards, which is to say she didn't stab me, and she passed me a strip of dried venison that tasted mostly of salt and regret. Mountain cuisine.

Wren came and sat down beside me without ceremony, close enough that I could feel the warmth coming off the child like a hearthstone. That was new. The first night, you could stand a spear's length from Wren and still feel it — a faint heat, like sun through a window. Now it was stronger, closer to the skin. I didn't know yet whether that meant the ember was waking or dying, and Ilex wasn't there to ask, and the not-knowing sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

"Song?" Wren asked. Just the one word, in plain speech, learned fast the way children learn anything they want badly enough.

I hummed it. *Down the crooked stair, love, where the ash-tree grows, carry what the mountain gives and leave behind your woes* — my grandmother's nonsense rhyme, except it wasn't nonsense anymore, and every time I sang it I felt the ground listening. Wren closed both eyes and leaned against my arm, and the warmth spread through my sleeve like spilled tea, and for maybe a minute I forgot to count anything at all.

That's when Ferren's whistle cut the dawn. Three notes, low, then silence.

Riders.

* * *

They came down the river road in column of twos, eight visible, which meant more in the trees — Concord doctrine, drilled into every recruit: *never show the whole hand, let the enemy count wrong.* I knew the drill because I'd helped write out the training manual. That's the thing nobody tells you about being owned by an institution: you don't just sweep its floors. You copy its ledgers, transcribe its interrogations, fair-hand its field manuals. Every order that ever crossed Vane's desk had passed under my pen first, neat as scripture.

"They'll want the ford," Sera murmured beside me in the bracken. We lay flat on the ridge, watching through gaps in the ferns. Below, the lead rider raised a fist and the column halted, horses blowing steam into the cold. "And they'll want to be polite about it first. Vane sends talkers ahead of shooters. Cheaper."

The talker rode forward alone: a broad man in a sergeant's half-plate, helm pushed back, hands wide and empty. He stopped at the edge of our campsite — abandoned, deliberately, with a cold fire and a dropped boot — and called out in the carrying voice of a man who has practiced being reasonable.

"People of the Kettle! In the Prior's name! You harbor a fugitive scribe and an unclaimed child. Give them up and ride home rich. Refuse, and we burn the Kettle to the waterline with everyone in it. You have until that smoke clears." He pointed at their own signal fire. A courtesy clock, burning.

"Bastard memorized the speech," Ferren whispered.

"He wrote it," Sera said. "Probably. Look at him."

And I did look, and my stomach went through the floor of the world, because I knew him. Tam Wicker. Big Tam, we'd called him, because there'd been a Little Tam who died of fever the winter I arrived, and after that Big Tam was just Tam, and he'd taught me how to hold a shield in the yard even though scribes weren't required to learn it, because he said a man should know the weight of the thing he writes orders about. He'd carried me on his shoulders during the Founding Day parade when my legs gave out. He'd slipped me honey-cakes when the ration-masters weren't looking, and once — once — he'd looked the other way when he caught me slipping out the postern gate toward the smuggler roads, and said only, "Debts are heavy, little brother. Walk careful."

He was down there offering to burn eleven people alive for a bounty, and he didn't know I was on the ridge, and part of me — the part I'd spend years trying not to examine — wanted to walk down there and fix it. Explain. Say *Tam, it's me, it's Bram, you know me, none of this needs to happen.*

Smoke curled up from the signal fire. The courtesy clock was burning.

"They're fanning out," Sera breathed. Four riders had peeled into the trees on either flank, quiet now, hunting the way you hunt rabbits before the beaters come through. Standard pattern. Sweep the ridgeline, push anyone hiding downhill toward the main line. I knew the pattern because I'd inked its diagram, plate fourteen, figure three.

"We hit the flanks before they link," Sera said. "Ferren, Doss — the east pair. Me, Halloran, and the twins west. Nobody engages the center till the flanks break. And nobody—" she looked at each of us in turn, "—nobody dies stupidly. Clear?"

Nobody answered, which in the mountains means clear.

"And Halloran." Her hand closed on my wrist. "You've thrown a knife at a target. This isn't that. Whatever happens down there, keep moving, keep breathing, and don't watch anything longer than you have to. Understood?"

It wasn't. Nothing about it was ever going to be understood. But I nodded, because the smoke was rising and the flanks were moving and Wren was somewhere behind us with two of Sera's women and a knife they'd given the child over my objection, and the world had stopped asking whether I wanted to be in this story.

We went down into the trees.

* * *

Here is what I remember of my first fight, in the order my memory kept it, which is not chronological but honest:

Fern. Wet fern slapping my face as I ran crouched along the contour of the hill. My heart going like a debtor's knock.

A horse. One of the flanking riders passing so close I could smell its lathered neck, the soldier in the saddle scanning upslope, looking at ferns, seeing ferns.

Sera's knife leaving her hand ahead of me, silent as a fish, taking the second rider's horse through the shoulder — not the man, the horse, because Sera fought like an accountant, spending the least to buy the most. The animal screamed and went down and the rider went with it, and the first rider wheeled toward the sound, and that was when I threw my knife at a human being for the first time in my life.

I would like to report that time slowed, that some ancestral door-warden spirit guided my hand, that I felt nothing or felt everything. None of that. Time moved exactly as fast as it always does, which is horrifyingly fast, and my throw was ugly and lucky and hit the man in the meat of his sword-arm rather than anywhere fatal, and he dropped his sword, and Sera was on him before he finished shouting, and it was handled. That's the word she used later. *Handled.* As if violence were laundry.

Then the third rider came around the alder thicket, and everything got worse.

Because it was Tam.

Not the talker at the ford — Tam had split off with the west flank, of course he had, he was good, he'd always been good, the best of the yard instructors, patient and thorough and kind to the youngest recruits. He came through the trees at a canter, saw Sera standing over a screaming horse and a disarmed man, saw me standing there holding a knife I'd already used, and I watched the recognition cross his face like weather.

"Bram?" he said. "Bram Halloran?"

"Tam." My voice came out small. "Tam, go back. Ride back and tell them we weren't here. Please."

He looked at me for a long moment. Down at the ford, faintly, someone shouted. His jaw worked. And I saw the war happen behind his eyes — the same war I'd been fighting since the crypt, loyalty against knowledge, the uniform against the thing under the uniform — and I thought, for one bright, stupid heartbeat, that kindness would win. That the man who'd carried me on his shoulders would turn his horse around.

"Bram," he said quietly, "you don't know what the Prior will do to the ones who come back empty. You don't know what he did to Captain Rell." He gathered his reins. "Come with me. Bring the child. Your debt dies, you sleep indoors again, and I swear on the Founding I'll see no harm comes to it. To any of you."

It was a good offer. That's what I want written down somewhere, in some honest margin: it was a genuinely good offer, made sincerely, by a good man inside a rotten machine, and for three full seconds I wanted it so badly I could taste the temple bread.

Then, downslope, a scream — one of the twins, or Ferren, I couldn't tell — and Tam's face changed, because duty changed it, and he put his heels to his horse, and the choice was made for both of us by everything we served.

What happened next happened fast, and I've turned it over ten thousand times and it never gets slower. He rode at me — not to kill, I believe that, I have to believe that, his blade angled to disable, a yard-trainer's cut, the kind you use on a student — and I did the thing Sera had drilled into me for three days, the only thing, drop and roll under the line of attack. And I came up inside his guard, inside the reach his longsword needed, and my knife — my ugly, lucky knife — went into him under the arm where the plate gaps, because that's the gap the manual lists, plate fourteen, figure three, the diagram I inked myself.

He made a sound I will not describe here. He stayed in the saddle for a moment, looking down at me with something worse than anger in his face — surprise, just surprise, the pure astonishment of a man discovering the world doesn't work the way the manual promised — and then he fell, and the fall took the knife with him, and I was kneeling in the ferns with empty hands and Tam Wicker was dead on the ground in front of me and the birds had gone quiet.

I don't remember deciding anything. That's the truth I keep arriving at. There was no moment of choice, no moral fork, no dramatic music. There was a body moving the way three days of drilling had built it to move, and then there was a man who used to share his honey-cakes lying still among the ferns, and the difference between those two facts was me.

Sera put her hand on my shoulder. She didn't say anything, which was the kindest thing anyone has ever done for me, because anything she could have said — *he chose his side*, *it was him or you*, *war is war* — all true, all useless as a coin clipped through.

Down at the ford, the signal fire burned itself out. The courtesy clock had run down. From the treeline came shouts, the crash of Sera's people hitting the eastern flank, a horse bolting riderless through the shallows. The battle, such as it was, went the way Sera had drawn it, because Sera always drew things the way they went: the flanks broke, the center lost its nerve, and eight minutes after Tam Wicker fell, the Concord riders were streaming back up the river road with two of their number left behind, one limping and one not.

Eleven smugglers stood in the wreck of the campsite afterward, counting. It's what we do. Two of ours wounded, neither dead — the scream had been Doss taking a slash across the forearm, ugly but clean. Six riders down or fled. And me, sitting apart on a stone, scrubbing at my hands with river water that wasn't cold enough and wouldn't ever be cold enough.

Wren found me there. The child didn't say anything. Wren just sat down in the mud beside me, pressed that impossible warm shoulder against mine, and hummed — badly, tunelessly, with the fierce determination of the tone-deaf — my grandmother's song. *Carry what the mountain gives.* The ember's warmth soaked through my sleeve, and I thought: this is what the fire is for. Not hoards, not doors, not Vane's hungry rituals. This. Sitting with someone in the wreckage and humming until the shaking stops.

"It was him or you," Sera said eventually, squatting beside us. She offered me a flask. I drank. It tasted like paint thinner and I loved it completely.

"I know."

"Do you? Because you've got the face of a man writing himself into the villain's column." She tapped my forehead, none too gently. "Listen to me, scribe. The Concord sent thirty men to drag a child into a cell and burn a mountain open. You want to find the guilty party, start further upstream. Tam Wicker rowed for that ship same as you did. Difference is, you jumped."

"He carried me on his shoulders," I said. "Founding Day. I couldn't stop crying and he carried me the whole route so nobody would see."

Sera was quiet a moment. "Then mourn him," she said. "Mourn him properly, tonight, with a fire and his name spoken out loud, because that's what the mountain does for the dead, even theirs. And tomorrow you get up and you fight again, because the alternative is letting them win, and I promise you, Halloran — I *promise* you — Tam Wicker would rather be mourned by you than avenged by them."

* * *

We spoke his name at the fire that night, all of us, even the mules standing solemn at the edge of the light as if they understood. Sera poured a cup of the paint-thinner brandy into the flames, which nearly killed us all, and the fire leapt green and furious, and Wren laughed for the first time since the temple — a startling, rusty sound, like a hinge remembering its purpose — and then hid the laugh immediately, as if laughter were also something that could be confiscated.

I slept maybe an hour. Near dawn I woke to Wren shaking me, eyes enormous in the ember-light, pointing east through the trees.

At first I saw nothing. Then I saw it: a thin thread of woodsmoke rising from the next valley over, deliberate, steady — not a campfire. A signal. And beneath it, faint on the wind, a bell. The Concord rang bells at their waystations to pass messages faster than any rider: one peal for routine, two for a sighting, three—

Three peals drifted up through the cold air, spaced and unhurried, the sound of a machine changing gear.

"That's Coldmarch's bell," I said. My mouth had gone dry. "Three means they've confirmed us. Means Vane knows exactly which valley we're in. Sera — Sera, wake up — he won't send riders next time. Three means the garrison marches. All of it."

Sera stood looking at the smoke, and for the first time since I'd known her, the queen of the mountain smugglers looked like a woman doing sums she didn't like. "Kettle's blown," she said. "This valley's blown. We need to move, and we need somewhere the Concord's maps don't reach." Her eyes came to me, then narrowed. "You said once there was a man. An archivist. Excommunicated. Lives off every road that exists."

Brother Ilex. The heretic with the forbidden library, the rumor I'd traded on for years and never quite believed. "There's supposed to be a shelter in the high country past the Greywater headwaters," I said slowly. "No trail. Smugglers leave offerings at a cairn and he leaves supplies. If anyone alive knows what Thornfell actually was—"

"If anyone knows what's chasing us," Sera corrected grimly, glancing at where Wren sat warming the dawn like a small coal, "and how to stop it wanting the child back."

Above the far ridge, the smoke rose straight and patient into the brightening sky, and the bell rang three times again, and I picked up my pack, and we went up into the mountains to find the one man in the world who might know why the dead kingdom had chosen my grandmother's songs to survive in.

Behind us, the valley filled with ash on the wind. Ahead of us, Ilex was waiting — though it would be days before I learned that he'd lit that signal fire himself, hours before the Concord ever marched, because Brother Ilex had seen the ember coming in his books, and had been praying for it, and dreading it, for forty years.
