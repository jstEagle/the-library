## 10. The Heretic's Library

The thing nobody tells you about erased kingdoms is how much paperwork the erasing leaves behind.

I'd spent nine years copying ledgers for the Highmoor Concord. I knew what it looked like when an institution decided something had never existed: you didn't burn the records — burning leaves ash, and ash invites questions. You *replaced* them. You wrote so many new documents over the wound that eventually the scar tissue looked like original skin. Two centuries of inventories, tithes, boundary surveys, all of them describing the Thornfells as nothing but mountains. Empty mountains. Mountains that had always been empty, praise be to the Ninefold Light.

So when Sera Voss said she knew a man who'd spent forty years un-erasing them, I expected a lunatic.

I got one. He just happened to be right.

* * *

We came at him from below, because Sera insisted there was no other way to come at anything worth having.

"Brother Ilex doesn't have a door," she said, picking her way up a goat track above the Greywater valley with the ease of a woman who'd been born to vertical ground. "Doors can be kicked in. He has a *chimney*."

"A chimney."

"Technically it's a drainage shaft from an old mine. Technically he lives in the mine. Technically—" she paused to spit over the edge into fog, "—technically he's been dead for eleven years, which suits him fine. Dead men don't get visits from Prior Vane's readers."

Behind me, Wren climbed like the goat the track was named for, silent, barefoot despite the cold, stopping every dozen paces to put one palm flat against the rock as if taking its pulse. The child had barely spoken since the ambush on the ridge road — since the soldier died making that sound I still heard when I closed my eyes. But the ember hadn't gone quiet. I could feel it the way you feel a banked stove through an iron door: a low, patient warmth that had opinions about things.

It had opinions about this mountain, apparently. Twice during the climb Wren stopped, frowned at a stretch of blank cliff face, and pressed both hands to it. Nothing happened that I could see. But the second time, I could have sworn the stone under those small fingers was warmer than the air around it, and for half a heartbeat — half a heartbeat I'd later spend years failing to explain — the blank rock looked less blank. Like text seen through wet paper.

"You saw that too," Sera said. Not a question.

"I saw weather," I said.

"Liar," she said fondly, and kept climbing.

* * *

The chimney was exactly as advertised: a black slot in the hillside breathing warm air, smelling of tallow smoke and old paper and, beneath both, the mineral cold of deep stone. Sera went down it without hesitation, hand over hand on iron rungs older than the Concord, calling out greetings in a dialect I didn't know until a voice floated up from the dark:

"If that's you, Voss, tell your man to mind his boots. If it's the Concord, I've been dead eleven years and I'd thank you to respect that."

"It's Voss, you fossil. And my man is a scribe, so his boots are the least of your worries."

I descended into lamplight.

The mine had become, over four decades, the opposite of everything I understood about libraries. The Concord's scriptorium at Highmoor was white, ordered, silent as a tomb — every page identical, every shelf labeled by decree, knowledge arranged like soldiers on parade. Brother Ilex's library was a warren. A chaos. Books stacked in strata, scrolls in barrels, fragments pinned to timber supports with the reverence usually reserved for saints' relics. Whole walls were covered in rubbings — charcoal impressions of carvings taken from standing stones, door lintels, grave markers, hundreds of them, and every single one covered in script I knew intimately and had never once been allowed to read.

Thornfell. The language of chapter three of my life, the syllables Wren had spoken through the crypt bars while Prior Vane watched through the observation grille like a man watching a coin purse fill.

"Oh," said a small round man, emerging from behind a barrel of scrolls with ink to his elbows and spectacles pushed up into wild grey hair. "Oh, oh, oh." He wasn't looking at me or Sera. He was looking past us both, down the shaft, where Wren stood at the top rung, backlit, hesitating.

"They can smell libraries," Ilex whispered. "Did you know that? Animals can't abide them. Children always find them." He wiped his hands on a robe that had surrendered to ink decades ago and held them out, palms up, the way you'd approach a strange dog. "Hello," he called softly, in Thornfell.

Wren answered.

I will not pretend I understood the exchange. It lasted perhaps twenty seconds — a question, a reply, a longer statement from Wren that made the old man sit down abruptly on a crate as if his legs had been repossessed. When it ended, Wren climbed down into the light, walked past me without a glance, took Ilex's ink-stained hand, and led him to the wall of rubbings like a patron leading a lost scholar to the correct shelf.

"What did they say?" Sera asked.

Ilex had tears running freely into his beard and seemed unaware of them. "They asked if I was the keeper of the words." His voice cracked. "Two hundred years, and they asked if I was the keeper of the words. I said I try to be. They said—" he laughed, wet and wondering, "—they said the stones have missed being read."

Then Wren pointed at one specific rubbing near the center of the wall — a fragment of carved doorway, broken across the middle — and said a word I'd heard before. In the crypt. In my grandmother's third smuggling song, the one about the crooked crown on the crooked hill.

"*Hollow Crown*," I translated, mostly on reflex, then stopped, because saying it out loud made the whole room turn toward me.

"You speak it?" Ilex demanded, rising.

"No. Yes. Badly. My grandmother sang smuggler songs with bits of it in them, and I memorized everything as a child because memorizing is free and I was poor. I don't know what most of it means."

"Show me," he breathed. "Everything. Every song, every rhyme, every nonsense syllable your family ever used to keep children quiet. Do you understand what you might be carrying? Fragments survive in work songs, in lullabies, in *curses* — the Concord never thought to erase what it didn't recognize as language. Your grandmother's songs might hold vocabulary that exists nowhere else on this earth."

And that, more than the ember, more than the escape, more than the dead soldier's face — that was the moment I felt the ground tilt under my whole life. Because I had spent nine years believing my inheritance was a debt ledger. It had apparently been a library, sung into me by a woman who'd never told me why her rhymes didn't rhyme in any tongue I knew.

"My grandmother," I said carefully, "had a great many secrets."

"They all did," Ilex said gently. "That was the point of them."

* * *

Sera and Ilex negotiated shelter terms the way old friends negotiate, which is to say insultingly.

"You'll feed us."

"I'll feed *you*. Your scribe eats like a debt collector."

"He is a debt collector."

"Then he eats last."

But the stew appeared, barley and dried nettle and a heel of salt pork that had achieved a kind of immortality through sheer persistence, and Wren ate three bowls while Ilex watched them eat with the expression of a man watching a comet. Then Sera went up to check the watch-post she maintained on the ridge — "paranoia is a lifestyle, not a phase" — and Ilex spread his life's work across two trestle tables, and we got down to the business of the night.

He talked the way water talks when a dam finally breaks.

"The erasure wasn't one act, Master Halloran. It was a program. Generations of it. First the Concord burned the archives — that took a decade. Then they burned the *teachers* — that took two more. By the fourth generation they didn't need to burn anything, because no one remembered enough to contradict them. They simply wrote the mountains into existence as wilderness and let time do the rest." He tapped a rubbing of a boundary stone. "But stone is patient. Stone waits. There are eleven thousand inscriptions in these ranges, and I have found nine hundred and six. At my current pace I will die around inscription fourteen hundred, and I consider that a triumphant career."

"Why does the Concord care?" I asked. "It's been two hundred years. Whatever Thornfell was, it's dust."

"Dust." Ilex smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. "Master Halloran, may I ask what the Prior told you about the child?"

"That it was feral. That it spoke a dead tongue. That it was to be interrogated and transcribed."

"And nothing about fire?"

My silence answered him.

"Ah," he said softly. "You've heard the vent-speech yourself, then. Good. It saves me the indelicacy." He moved along the table to a section I hadn't examined — not rubbings but original documents, vellum scraps, a page torn from some ancient codex, and at the center a drawing done in faded iron-gall ink: a mountain pass shaped, unmistakably, like a crown viewed edge-on, with a door drawn at its heart. Around the door, in Thornfell script and in a later hand's careful transcription, ran a single repeated line.

"*The door opens for the oath, not the blade.*"

"Every source agrees," Ilex said. "Every fragment, every wayshrine, every corrupted nursery rhyme your grandmother sang — they're all echoes of one document, one law, one warning. The people of Thornfell did not merely hoard power, Master Halloran. They *concentrated* it. Dragon-fire, distilled across centuries into a single vessel — a hoard, yes, but not gold. Fire. Enough fire to remake a country's fortunes, heal blighted land, light cities for a thousand years. Or enough to end them. And because they understood what such a treasure would do to the greedy — and they were surrounded by the greedy; the Concord's founding families were already circling — they sealed it behind a door with one lock and one key."

"The oath," I said.

"The oath. Given freely. Never taken." He looked at me over his spectacles, and for the first time the amiable archivist fell away and I saw the excommunicate underneath — the man the Concord had stripped of rank and name because he would not stop reading. "Do you understand the difference? Anyone can *take*. Take is easy; take requires only force, and the Concord has force to spare. But this door does not answer force. Force makes it a wall. The only thing that opens it is someone standing before it who *chooses* to give — freely, knowingly, without coercion, without bargain. The fire must be offered. That is not poetry. That is mechanism. It is the lock itself."

"So Vane can't open it."

"Vane can march ten thousand men to that door and die of old age in front of it." Ilex's finger came down on the drawing of the crowned pass. "Unless. Unless he has a key that walks. Something that carries the fire. Something that can be made — through fear, through pain, through whatever happens in his interrogation cells — to offer what it holds." His eyes went to the corner where Wren slept curled against a barrel, ember-light faintly gilding the edges of their hair. "The child is not the treasure, Master Halloran. The child is the *hand* that can give it. And Vane has spent his whole career teaching hands to open."

The stew turned to lead in my stomach. I looked at Wren — at the small, feral, stubborn person who had bitten me, fled from me, returned to me at dawn for a smuggler's song — and tried to imagine them in Vane's hands, and found I couldn't finish the thought. Some pictures refuse to develop.

"There's more," Ilex said quietly. "There's always more; history is an onion and every layer makes you cry. But the part you need tonight is this: the sources disagree about what happens if the door is forced anyway. Most say nothing — a wall forever. But three fragments — three, independent, from different regions — say otherwise. They use the same word." He wrote it out for me in Thornfell characters and beneath it his own translation, and I read the translation twice because once refused to be believed.

"*Unbinding.*"

"Meaning?"

"Meaning that a hoard of concentrated dragon-fire, two centuries compressed, released *unwillingly* — released by breaking rather than giving — does not flow. It detonates. The three fragments describe it as 'the green country becoming the red country.' Master Halloran, the Hollow Crown sits above the Greywater valley. Above every village in these foothills. If Vane forces that door, he will not seize a kingdom's treasure. He will scourge the mountains off the map, and this time the erasure will be honest, because there will be nothing left to remember."

The lamp flame bent in a draft from the shaft. Somewhere overhead, wind worried the rocks, and I thought about the Concord riders on the ridge road, and how they'd found our trail faster than luck alone explained, and how Vane did nothing without redundancy.

"We need to move," I said.

"We need to *know* first," Ilex countered. "Knowledge is the only weapon the Concord has never managed to confiscate, and I intend to keep it that way. You want to run — fine, running is wise. But you should know where you're running *to*. And for that—" he gathered his spectacles, his stylus, an armful of rubbings, and the drawing of the crowned pass, and pressed them into a leather satchel with the practiced motions of a man who had packed for flight many times in imagination and was clearly enjoying doing it in fact, "—for that, we go to the wayshrines. There are seven between here and the Hollow Crown. Each holds texts too large to carry. And each will tell us what your grandmother's songs only whisper."

"You're coming with us," Sera said from the shaft mouth, descending again, snow in her hair and grim news in her posture. "Because there's a Concord patrol camped in the valley with a bloodhound and a very good view of this hillside, and I'd rather not learn whether your chimney counts as a door when it's full of soldiers."

Ilex blinked. Then, wonder of wonders, the old man laughed — a bright, cracked-bell laugh, the laugh of a man whose death had just been canceled.

"Forty years," he said, slinging the satchel across his back. "Forty years waiting to see the places I've only ever read. Voss, if you get me killed on the way, I will haunt your ledgers specifically."

"Noted," Sera said. "Up the chimney, everyone. Mind the third rung; it bites."

Wren woke without being woken — the ember-child surfaced instantly, watchful, and fixed their dark eyes first on Ilex, then on the satchel of rubbings, then on me. And spoke. One phrase, clear as a bell in the lamplight, in that language two centuries buried, and this time I caught enough of its shape to feel what it meant, the way you feel the meaning of a tone of voice before you parse the words.

*Take me to the door.*

Ilex went pale. "They said," he translated, voice unsteady, "'The stones are waking. We should not make them wait.'"

Above us, far off down the valley, a hound began to bay.

We went up the chimney into the snow, four heretics and a satchel, carrying the memory of a murdered kingdom toward the door it had locked against the world — and behind us, in the valley dark, torchlight began to move.
