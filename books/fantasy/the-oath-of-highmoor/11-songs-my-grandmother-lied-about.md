## 11. Songs My Grandmother Lied About

I had sung the counting song my whole life without once wondering what I was counting.

*One for the stone and two for the door, three for the fire that burns no more—*

It was a walking song. A keep-your-feet-moving-on-the-night-road song, the kind smugglers hum to stay awake and unafraid in the dark stretches where the passes narrow and the mountain listens back. My grandmother taught it to me over a peat fire in a house that smelled of tallow and wet wool, her hands busy with mending, her voice doing something sly with the melody so that if a Concord man ever heard it through a wall, he'd think it was nothing. Just a woman's nonsense. Just a lullaby.

She told me it was about sheep. She said, *Bram, love, all good songs are about sheep or sorrow, and this one's about both.* And child that I was, I believed her, because why would a grandmother lie?

I have since compiled a list of reasons why a grandmother would lie, and it is longer than you'd think.

* * *

Brother Ilex had turned his library inside out for us.

That's not quite fair — he'd turned it inside out for Wren, mostly, and for the language coming up in the child like spring water through cracked stone. Every morning now there were lessons at the long table: Wren cross-legged on the bench, chin propped on fists, glaring at Ilex's reconstructed alphabets as if they owed him money; Sera pretending to sort her route-books but actually listening with the particular stillness she got when something mattered; Dane sitting by the fire because sitting by fires was still new to her, a luxury she kept testing like ice.

And me. I sat with them because I had nowhere better to be, which is one definition of belonging, and not the worst one.

On the fourth morning, Ilex asked me to sing.

He did it casually, the way scholars do when they've been planning a thing for days. He was laying out rubbing-copies of the pass carvings — the deep ones, the ones from before the erasure, the ones only smugglers and dead men had seen in two hundred years — and he said, "Halloran. You mentioned songs. Your grandmother's. The walking ones." He didn't look up. "Would you give us one?"

"I don't sing for free," I said, which was true, and also a deflection, because I could feel Wren watching me. The child had gone very still in the way that meant the ember was paying attention too. Two listeners for the price of one.

"Put it on your tab," Sera said dryly. "You're good at tabs."

So I sang the counting song. Low, the way Gran sang it, with the sly melody, hands flat on the table like I was holding it down. *One for the stone and two for the door, three for the fire that burns no more, four for the warden at the gate, five for the debt we carry late—*

Halfway through the third verse I noticed the silence had changed quality.

There are kinds of silence. There's the polite kind, the bored kind, the hostile kind. This was none of those. This was the silence of people trying not to breathe, and I stopped singing because of it, and the last line hung in the room unfinished: *and six for the ones who went away.*

Wren said a word.

Not a phrase this time. One word, small and round, and Ilex's hand came down flat on the table hard enough to rattle his ink pots. "Say it again," he breathed, and Wren did, and Ilex looked at me across the wreckage of his papers with an expression I'd only ever seen on men who've just watched a locked box open itself.

"That word," he said. "In your song. What sound does it follow?"

"Follow?" I frowned. "After *fire that burns no more*. Then four for the—" I stopped, because Ilex had spun one of the rubbings around and pushed it at me, and there was the word, carved in stone older than any kingdom I could name, and beside it, in Ilex's careful reconstruction, the meaning he'd spent thirty years guessing at:

*warden.*

"That's not a word," I said stupidly. "That's a line of a children's rhyme."

"It's a word," Ilex said, "in Thornfell speech. It's the word for the keeper of a threshold. The one who holds a door shut — or opens it." He took off his spectacles and polished them on his robe, a fussy motion, buying time, the way he did when the truth was too large to say plainly. "Your grandmother's song is Thornfell, Halloran. It's not corrupted by it. It isn't a smuggler tune with old words worn smooth. It *is* the old words. Sung in the mountains, under the Concord's nose, for two hundred years."

The fire popped. Dane had gone still in a different way — soldier-still, the stillness of someone recalculating a battlefield.

"That's impossible," I said. "My gran was a wool-trader's widow. She mended nets. She lied about the price of everything and she couldn't read past her own name."

"Mm," said Ilex. "Sing another one."

I didn't want to. I want to be honest about that, since honesty seems to be the currency of this whole miserable enterprise. I did not want to open my mouth, because some animal part of me already understood what was coming, the way you understand a storm is coming not from the sky but from the smell.

But Wren was looking at me. Wren, who trusted no one, who had bitten me hard enough to leave a scar I still worried with my thumbnail, was looking at me with an expression of terrible patience, like a person waiting for someone else to remember something important.

So I sang the bridge-crossing song. The one about the troll under the falls who takes a toll of names. And Ilex matched it, line by line, against a rubbing from the Hollow Crown approach — the toll-warden's litany, the formal words spoken to travelers asking leave to enter. A nursery rhyme about a troll, and underneath it, a ritual of hospitality older than the Concord's founding, older than the maps they burned.

Then the lullaby. The one about the star that fell into the mountain and the mother who sang it back to sleep. Ilex laid it beside a fragment he'd recovered from a burned archive — a prayer of sealing, addressed to a sleeping fire, asking it to dream and not wake.

"The mother," Ilex said quietly, "is the kingdom. Singing its power to sleep so no tyrant could wake it and wear it. And the song says—" he touched the fragment, "—it says the mother leaves watchers at the cradle. Wardens. Families charged with keeping the songs alive until the fire chooses to rise again."

"Songs don't need keeping alive by accident," Dane said. Her voice was rough. "Songs get carried on purpose. Someone teaches their children on purpose."

"Yes," Ilex said. "Exactly that. On purpose. For generations." He turned to me, and I wished to every god whose name I'd ever copied in a ledger margin that he wouldn't. "Halloran. Do you know what your family name was, before Halloran?"

"Halloran is my family name."

"It's the name the debt-collectors gave you. After." His voice gentled, which from Ilex was worse than cruelty. "Concord indenture rolls reassign names when they bind a household. It's administrative practice. Erase the lineage, erase the claim. But the songs survived, because songs live below the level where ledgers reach." He spread his hands over the rubbings, the fragments, thirty years of a heretic's obsession. "The wardens of the Hollow Crown were bound to the door by oath and by blood. When Thornfell sealed itself away, the wardens' families were scattered and debt-bonded — the Concord didn't kill them, you understand. Killing leaves martyrs. Indebting leaves servants. They made your ancestors pay, generation after generation, for a door they were sworn to guard, in a kingdom everyone was ordered to forget."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere outside, Sera's people were arguing about rope lengths, and the ordinary sound of it seemed obscene.

"My gran," I said, and my voice came out wrong, thin, like someone else's. "She sang those to me while she mended nets. She said they were about sheep."

"She said that because the alternative was saying what they were, and what they were would have gotten you both taken." Ilex folded his spectacles closed. "She lied because lying was the job. Keeping the songs alive *and* keeping them deniable — that was the warden's whole craft. You weren't raised ignorant, Bram. You were raised hidden. There's a difference."

I stood up. I don't remember deciding to. My legs simply reported for duty without consulting me, and I walked to the window, and I looked out at the gray throat of the valley, and I tried to breathe like a man instead of like a boy at a peat fire being lied to by the only person who had never lied to him about anything that mattered.

Except she had lied about everything that mattered. That was the discovery. That was the wound of it. Not that she'd lied — I understood lying, I was a professional, I'd falsified ledgers with a steady hand and a clear conscience for eleven years. It was that she'd lied *well*, all her life, and the lie had been load-bearing. The lie was the roof over my childhood. Take it out and the whole structure came down, and under the structure was —

Under it was a family that had guarded a dragon's grave for two hundred years and been paid in chains for the service. Under it was a grandmother humming a dead kingdom's prayers while she darned socks, passing the flame down cupped in her hands, disguised as nonsense, praying her grandson would never need it and making sure he had it anyway.

"One for the stone," I said to the window. My breath fogged the glass. "*Two for the door.* She wasn't teaching me arithmetic. She was teaching me the way home."

Behind me, small bare feet slapped the floorboards.

Wren pressed against my side, the way the child did — contact on the child's own terms, brief and fierce, a collision rather than an embrace. And then Wren took my hand, turned it palm-up in both small hands, and studied it with the frown of a scholar, and said something in Thornfell, slow and deliberate, clearly repeating a lesson learned at Ilex's table.

"What did the sprout say?" Sera asked softly.

Ilex answered before I could, and I heard him smile as he did it. "It said: *the song knows its singer.*"

* * *

I walked up the fell that evening alone, above the tree line, to where the wind scrubbed everything clean, because there was a fury in me that needed somewhere to go that wasn't at the people who loved me.

Fury at whom? That's the joke of it. Fury needs an address and mine kept changing. At the Concord, obviously, at the great grinding machine of them, the ledger-keepers and Prior-makers who'd erased a kingdom and enslaved its guardians and called it order. Easy. Comfortable, even. But beneath that, angrier and less speakable: fury at Gran. At the dead woman who'd chosen my ignorance for me, who'd decided I'd live better not knowing what I was, who'd handed me the truth dressed in sheep and expected me never to unwrap it. She hadn't asked. She hadn't left a letter, a confession, a single honest sentence. She'd left me a pack of rhymes and a life built on top of them like a house on a buried foundation, and now the foundation had spoken, and the house was falling, and she wasn't there to catch any of it.

I sat on a cold rock and I sang the counting song to the wind, all the verses, the ones I knew and half-knew, and I let myself hear them properly for the first time. *Four for the warden at the gate.* Me. Apparently. A man who'd sold passes to smugglers and copies to priests and loyalty to whoever paid, a man whose defining talent was getting out from under things.

*Five for the debt we carry late.*

Eleven years I'd thought that verse was about coin.

I laughed, up there, alone in the wind, and it came out with edges on it. Because here was the bitter arithmetic of my whole life, finally done right: my debt to the Concord, the one tallied to the copper, the one Vane dangled over me like bait — that debt was invented. Manufactured. A fiction maintained by men who'd indebted my family in the first place, for the crime of keeping a promise to a country that no longer existed. I hadn't been born owing. I'd been born *owed*. Owed by an institution that had stolen a kingdom's memory, a family's name, and two centuries of wages, and called the theft administration.

And the Concord's own books proved it, if anyone could read them. Ilex wanted to see the indenture rolls. The reassignment records. The paper trail of a two-hundred-year crime, filed neatly in triplicate by clerks who never knew what they were clerking. I almost pitied the archivists who'd have to pull those boxes.

Almost.

When I came down, the fire was banked low and Wren was asleep in a nest of blankets, and Sera was waiting up, sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening. She does that when she's thinking. It saves wear on the conversation.

"Well?" she said.

"I'm a door-keeper," I said. "Hereditary. Unpaid. Two hundred years in arrears."

"You always did have a talent for finding worse employment."

"Sera." I sat down across from her. "Vane offered to strike my debt. For Wren. For the road through the passes." I turned my hands over, palms up, the hands that had copied ledgers, guided strangers, signed whatever was put in front of them. "He thought that was the price of me. He thought freedom was what I wanted most in the world."

"And now?"

"Now I know the debt was theirs, not mine." I looked at the sleeping child, at the faint warmth that came off him even in sleep, like a stone that remembered the sun. "Now I know what I'm actually heir to. It's not a hoard. It's not a throne." I heard myself say it and was surprised at how little it frightened me, how much it felt like setting down a weight I'd mistaken for my own spine. "It's a door. And a promise. And apparently a songbook."

Sera studied me for a long moment, knife stilled. Then she nodded, once, the way she did when a deal finally made sense to her. "Family business," she said. "About time somebody in it worked honest hours."

I smiled. It hurt my face, which is how I knew it was real.

That was when Ilex came hurrying from his workroom with a lamp held high and a piece of paper in his fist, and I knew before he spoke that the night wasn't done with me yet, because Ilex only ran when the news outran his caution.

"Bram." He was breathing hard, eyes bright and terrible behind his spectacles. "The reassignment rolls. I remembered a reference — a marginal citation in a Concord audit from sixty years ago. I went back through my notes." He thrust the paper at me. "The Hallorans weren't scattered randomly after the erasure. They were settled. Placed. Along the old warden roads, one family per pass, generation after generation, close enough to watch, poor enough to control." He swallowed. "There were seven warden lines. Seven doors. Your family kept the Hollow Crown road."

"Past tense," I said.

"No," Ilex said. "Present. The audit lists the households still bound. Six lines have died out or lost the songs — the Concord tracked that, Bram, they *counted*, they counted the songs dying like they count everything." He steadied himself on the table. "One line remains on the rolls. One household still marked active. Still marked *singing*."

The lamp flame bent in a draft from under the door. Out in the dark, the mountain stood the way it always stood, patient and full of buried light, and somewhere in my chest an old rhyme rearranged itself into a sentence I should have understood since childhood.

"Where," I said.

Ilex met my eyes.

"The last warden family on the Concord's rolls," he said, "lives forty miles north. In the village of Ashford-in-the-Vale." He paused, and the pause had teeth. "Bram — it's the village the Concord garrison is marching on tomorrow."
