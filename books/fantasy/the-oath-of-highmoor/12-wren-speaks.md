## 12. Wren Speaks

The man with the ink-stained fingers has a name. Ilex. It tastes like a stone you find in a river, smooth on one side and sharp on the other. He sits across from me every morning at the long table in Brother Ilex's house — which is also a library, which is also a barn, because the roof leaks in three places and he has decided that books matter more than shingles.

He puts things in front of me. That is how he teaches. A cup. He says a word. *Cup.* Then he waits, with his whole face waiting too, the way a dog waits by a door, except dogs are less patient and better company.

I know what a cup is. I knew what a cup was before he was born, probably, though I do not remember being born, so maybe not. The problem is that his word for it is wrong. Not wrong like a lie. Wrong like a coat that fits someone else. His word is *cup* and my word is

and there it goes again. The word opens in my chest like a door opening onto warm air and then the door swings shut before I can walk through it.

I hate that door.

The ember hates it more. I can feel it pressing against the inside of me when that happens, like a bird against a window. *That one,* it seems to say. *That one is ours.* And then the word is gone and the ember sulks, and sulking embers make my fingertips hot enough to scorch the table, which Ilex minds about very much for a man who claims not to mind anything.

"You scorched my table," he said this morning, without looking up from his writing.

"Your table started it."

He looked up then. His eyes went round and wet, the way they go when something surprises him into joy, which happens embarrassingly often for a grown man. "You spoke."

"I speak all the time. You just don't listen in the right—"

I stopped. Because I had heard myself. The words coming out of my mouth were not the words I meant to say. Or they were exactly the words I meant to say, but they were wearing different clothes. They came out in his language, plain as bread, and inside my head they had been dressed in the other one, the old one, the one that lives under my tongue like a coin buried in a floor.

The ember was singing. I could feel it, low and steady, the way it sings when Bram hums that song of his grandmother's. The song is broken. Half its bones are missing. But the ember knows the tune anyway, the way you know your own name even when someone shouts it from far away through a storm.

"Say it again," Ilex whispered.

"No." I pulled my hands into my sleeves. "You'll write it down and then it will be yours instead of mine."

He put down his pen. That, more than anything, frightened me. Grown men only put down their pens when something matters more than the pen, and I did not want to be the thing that mattered. Things that matter get locked up or traded or burned. I have been all three.

"Wren," he said, very gently, the way you talk to a deer you have not yet spooked. "Words aren't owned. They're only borrowed from everyone who ever used them. When I write yours down, I'm not taking them. I'm keeping them safe. There's a difference."

"There isn't," I said. "Safe is just slow taking."

And then he laughed, actually laughed, a short startled bark, and said, "You sound like me at fifteen," and went back to work, and left me alone with the ember and the door inside my chest that kept swinging open onto warm air and swinging shut.

*

Here is what nobody understands about having a dragon's last heartbeat living in you:

It is not quiet.

People think fire is loud, all crack and roar, but this fire is old fire, and old fire is quiet the way snow is quiet. It hums. All day, all night, underneath everything. When I eat, it hums. When Sera swears at her mule, it hums. When Bram is near, it hums louder, and warmer, and I have stopped pretending I don't notice.

It shows me things when I sleep. A country so green it makes green look like a new invention. Rivers the color of bottle glass. A door in a mountain, tall as ten men, carved all over with words — my words, the ones that live under my tongue — and light behind the door like sunrise kept in a jar.

And a voice. Old and enormous and tired, tired the way mountains are tired, which is to say: patiently, forever, until suddenly not.

It does not talk in words. It talks in *wanting*. Mostly it wants to rest. Sometimes, lately, it wants something else, and the wanting feels like standing at the top of stairs in the dark, knowing you have to go down but not remembering why.

Today the wanting had a shape.

Ilex had spread out rubbings on the table — those gray ghost-pictures he makes by laying paper on carved stones and rubbing charcoal over them. Carvings from the deep passes. He does this thing where he talks to himself while he works, low and fast, and mostly I ignore him, but today he said a word and the ember sat up.

Not stood up. *Sat up.* Like a dog hearing its name.

"*Vaelthûn,*" Ilex read, tracing the ghost-letters with a cracked fingernail. "Door-warden. Literally *door-heart-one*. The one whose heart is the hinge."

The word went into me and found its coat. My coat. It fit.

*I know that word,* I wanted to say, and before I could decide whether to say it, Ilex said it again, slower, looking right at me, and the ember pushed, and the door inside my chest swung open, and this time I walked through.

"*Vaelthûn,*" I said. And then, because the door was open and I was afraid it would shut again, I said everything. All of it, a flood, the words tumbling out in two languages at once, braided together, the old one and Ilex's one, and I heard myself doing it and could not stop, like vomiting but with meaning. "It means the one who holds the door with their heart. It doesn't mean guard. Guarding is standing outside with a spear. This is *being* the hinge. If the hinge breaks the door still stands but nothing opens anymore, ever, and that's worse than breaking. The carvings say — the carvings say the door opens for the oath, not the blade. *Draeth mor vael, draeth mor cenn.* The blade wakes the ash. The oath wakes the—"

I stopped, because Ilex had gone very still, and stillness in a grown man is a loud noise.

"The oath wakes the fire," I finished, quietly, in his language, all by itself, no braid. My first whole sentence in it that I hadn't stolen piece by piece. It tasted strange. Like wearing shoes after years of bare feet.

Ilex sat down. Not on the chair. On the floor, right there, among his ghost-rubbings, like his legs had resigned.

"That phrase," he said. "*The door opens for the oath, not the blade.* Do you understand what—" He stopped himself, pressed both hands flat on the floorboards. "Two hundred years. Two hundred years scholars have guessed, fought, died over scraps. And it was a nursery rhyme all along. Your language survived in songs, in children's rhymes, worn smooth like river stones — we thought the meaning wore off with the edges. It didn't. It just went into hiding." He looked up at me, and his eyes were doing the wet shining thing again, and I decided to let him have it, just this once. "Wren. Say it once more. Slowly. In Thornfell."

So I did. *Draeth mor vael, draeth mor cenn.* And the ember sang along, and for the first time since the night the men with ash-smell took everything, I did not feel like a jar with something trapped in it.

I felt like a person holding something. Which is different. Nobody tells you how different it is, holding a thing versus being held inside it.

*

Sera found out within the hour, because Sera always finds out within the hour, because her people tell her things the way rivers tell the sea. She came into the library-barn leaning on the doorframe with her arms crossed, watching Ilex copy out my sentences in three different inks like a man hoarding gold.

"So the little wren talks proper now."

"I talk *both*," I told her. "Proper is your problem."

Bram, who was mending a strap by the fire and pretending not to listen, snorted into his lap. Sera grinned — she has a grin like a knife deciding to be friendly — and crossed the room and crouched down in front of me, which most grown-ups don't bother to do, they prefer talking at you from their height like weather.

"Both languages," she said. "Then tell me true, small fry. That ember in you. What does it want?"

Everyone went quiet. Even the fire in the hearth seemed to lean in, jealous.

I could have lied. I am good at lying; lying kept me alive through two winters alone in the high country. But Sera asks questions the way she buys things: straight, full price, no haggling, and she makes you feel cheap if you haggle back.

"It wants to be given," I said. "Not kept. Not taken. Given." The words came out of me in a rush, in both tongues at once, and the ember flared warm under my skin, agreeing, and I put my hand flat on the table and said, "It keeps showing me the door. And green country. And open sky. And it's *tired*, Sera. It's so tired. It's been holding on for two hundred years waiting for someone to ask it what *it* wants, and nobody asked, they all just planned how to grab it."

Silence. Somewhere outside, the mule said something rude.

"And what about you?" Sera asked, quieter now. "What do *you* want? Not the fire. You."

Nobody had asked me that either. Not Bram, not Ilex, not anyone. They had asked what I knew, what I remembered, what I carried, what I could translate. What I *was* had apparently gone without saying, the way you don't ask a key how it feels about locks.

I looked around the room. At Ilex with his three inks. At Sera with her knife-grin gone soft at the edges. At Bram by the fire, who had stopped pretending not to listen and was now watching me with an expression I didn't have a word for in either language, which annoyed me, so I made one up on the spot and decided never to tell anyone what it meant.

"I want to not be a door," I said. "I want to be a person who knows a door. Those are different things. Everyone keeps treating me like the second kind of first kind." I frowned. That had come out tangled. "Like I'm the treasure chest instead of the thief who gets to spend it."

Bram made a sound. I looked at him. He was rubbing the back of his neck, which he does when something is true and he wishes it weren't.

"She's right," he said, to the room in general, miserably. "We've all been doing it. Me worst of all. Asking her what she remembers instead of what she thinks." He looked at me then, straight on, and his face did the thing it does when he is about to be honest, which he hates, which is why it's worth watching. "I'm sorry, Wren. I've been so busy seeing the kingdom in you that I missed the kid."

"I'm not a kid."

"You're absolutely a kid."

"I bit you."

"Kids bite. Adults negotiate." He held up his hand, the one with the scar where I'd gotten him that first night on the road, back when I trusted nothing and him least of all. "Mostly."

I should explain about Bram, since apparently this is becoming the sort of story where explanations happen.

When I first met him, he smelled like ink and fear and debt, and I judged him for all three. Ink means institutions. Fear means weakness. Debt means owned. I have been owned by things; it is not a smell you forget. But here is what I learned on the road, in the cold, in the running: fear that stays next to danger instead of running from it isn't weakness. It's just courage with its coat still on. And debt — well. Ilex says the old Thornfell wardens were debt-bound too, bound to the door by oath instead of copper. Maybe some debts are chains and some are hinges. Maybe it depends what the debt is *to*.

Bram hums his grandmother's song when he thinks no one can hear. The song is broken. Half its bones are missing. But when he hums it, the ember goes quiet and contented, like a cat in a sunbeam, and I have worked out why, though I haven't told anyone: the song is a lullaby. A door-warden's lullaby, sung to children so the words would survive in hiding. His grandmother wasn't just singing nonsense to him. She was handing him something wrapped in a blanket, and he carried it for twenty years without ever once looking inside.

He is carrying me the same way now. Without looking inside. Without asking what I'm for.

That is why.

*

That evening I couldn't sleep. The ember was restless, humming in a minor key, showing me the door again and again, closer each time, the carvings sharper, the light behind it brighter. It felt like urgency. It felt like a clock somewhere with almost no sand left in it.

So I got up. The house was dark. Ilex slept sitting up among his books like a heron. I padded past him, past Sera's room where she slept with a knife under the pillow and probably one under each foot, and down the hall to where the boys slept — Bram and Dane in the loft above the stable, because Dane still couldn't bear walls that didn't have an exit she'd personally tested, and Bram had moved out there to keep her company, or to keep watch, or because he was Bram.

I climbed the ladder. Bram woke instantly, the way he always does, hand going for the knife under his bedroll before his eyes opened, and when he saw it was me the knife went away and the wariness stayed, softening.

"Nightmare?" he murmured.

"No." I sat down on the edge of his straw tick. He scooted over without being asked. "The fire won't settle. It keeps showing me the door. I think it's scared, Bram. I think it's running out of time and it knows it, the way old people know winter is coming before the leaves turn."

He was quiet a moment. "Ilex thinks weeks," he admitted. "Maybe less. And Vane is marching."

"I know. I hear things. People forget I'm small and not deaf."

"Never forgotten either one." He rubbed his face. "What do you need, Wren?"

There it was again. The question nobody else asked. I lay down next to him, the way you'd lie down next to a banked fire, and stared at the rafters where the swallows nested.

"I need to choose," I said. "Before everyone else finishes choosing for me. Vane chose already — he chose to take. The Concord chose. Even Ilex and Sera, good as they are, they've chosen to protect the vessel. Everybody's chosen except me and you."

"Me?"

"You're the one it hums for." I turned my head and looked at him. In the dark his face was just an outline and two catchlights, but I didn't need more. I reached out and took his hand — the scarred one — and pressed my palm against it, and let the ember warm, just a little, just enough that he'd feel it. He flinched and then didn't pull away.

"What are you doing?" he asked, unsteadily.

"Choosing," I said. "The carvings say the door opens for the oath, not the blade. Ilex thinks that means a warden has to swear it. Fine. But a door takes two, Bram. A hinge and a hand on the handle. I'm not giving this fire to the Concord, or to a kingdom, or to history, or to Ilex's book. I'm putting it in your hand. Yours specifically. Whatever you decide to do with it — give it away, set it free, drop it in a lake — that part's yours. But it goes through you. Because you're the first person in two hundred years who carried the song without trying to use it." I squeezed his ember-warm hand harder, child-grip, the grip that doesn't let go. "You are my door. I choose you. Swear something back or don't, but know that I swore."

For a long time Bram Halloran said nothing at all. I felt his pulse speed up under my palm, and then slow, and settle, like a man making a decision with his whole body.

"If I swear," he said finally, roughly, "it won't be to a kingdom. Or to the fire. It'll be to you. To getting you through this alive and free — *you*, not the ember. Whatever the rite costs, it costs me first. That's the oath. Take it or leave it."

The ember sang. Loud, joyful, a chord I'd never heard it make, and green country bloomed behind my eyes, and the door stood open, and somewhere far away a mountain exhaled two hundred years of held breath.

Outside, far down in the valley, a horn sounded — once, twice, a third time. Sera's watch-calls. Not wolves. Not weather.

Riders.

Bram was on his feet before the third note faded, knife already in hand, and I climbed down the ladder into the dark with my heart hammering and the ember burning bright and certain in my chest, and thought: whatever comes up that road tonight, it is going to find out what a door-warden's oath is worth.
