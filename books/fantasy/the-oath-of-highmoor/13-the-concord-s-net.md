## 13. The Concord's Net

There is a particular pleasure in a well-drafted heresy proclamation. I do not mean the writing of it — though I confess to a craftsman's pride in my own hand — but the moment when the wax seal meets the parchment and something that was merely an opinion becomes, in the eyes of every soul who reads it, a crime.

Halloran had been a scribe. He understood this better than anyone, which is perhaps why his betrayal stung more than it should have. A man who spends ten years copying ledgers learns that paper is the truest army ever fielded. Armies burn villages; paper decides which villages deserve burning.

I sat in my study at the Highmoor temple with the proclamation before me, reading it aloud one final time for the ear of it.

"Let it be known that Bram Halloran, indentured scribe of this Concord, having fled his lawful debt and stolen from the temple's custody a child of unknown provenance bearing signs of profane corruption, is hereby declared apostate and heretic. Let his debt be transferred to his bloodline unto the third generation. Let any soul who shelters him share his sentence. And let the sum of two hundred crowns be paid for his return alive — or fifty for proof sufficient."

The "alive" was important. Dead men make poor keys, and whatever the child was, she was the key. I had watched her through the observation glass for three nights before she vanished from under my nose, and I had seen what the ember did when it dreamed: the candle flames leaning toward her like courtiers, the frost on the crypt window melting in a perfect circle around her cot. Power does not hide itself. Only men hide it, and badly.

My circle assembled at dusk — the six faces I trusted in the way a surgeon trusts his instruments: completely, and only while they are in his hand.

Cantor Merek arrived first, as always, smelling of ink and ambition. Then the Provosts Ferren and Cole, twins in temperament if not in blood, both of whom believed with their whole hearts in nothing whatsoever. Sister Anwen, whose faith was genuine and therefore useful, the way a hammer's weight is useful. And last, Brother Ossian, old and soft-voiced, who had spent forty years making the Concord's cruelties sound like mercy, and who had come to believe his own translations, which made him either the most dangerous or the most honest man in the room.

"You've read it?" I asked.

"We've read it," said Merek.

"Then say what you're all thinking. That two hundred crowns is too much for a scribe."

"It's not the scribe," said Anwen quietly. "It's the precedent. We are paying laymen to hunt within our borders. The mountain folk will notice that we pay better for betrayal than for harvest."

"The mountain folk," I said, "will notice whatever we tell them to notice. That is what fear is *for*, Sister. It is a ledger. You enter a debit — a burned granary, a missing shepherd, a rumor given legs — and the account balances itself in obedience."

Ossian smiled his papery smile. "You make theology sound like bookkeeping, Aldous."

"I make it sound like what it has always been. Sit down. All of you. What follows does not leave this room until it is fait accompli, because I have learned — as Halloran apparently learned before me — that even trusted instruments have ears, and walls in old temples are thin with other people's secrets."

* * *

I told them about Thornfell.

Not everything. Never everything. A secret shared entire is a knife handed hilt-first to your enemy. But I gave them enough: that the erased kingdom had been erased deliberately, by its own hand, rather than surrender its power to the conquerors of that age; that behind its door lay the hoard — the dragon-fire, concentrated, patient, two centuries of stored divinity waiting in the dark like a purse waiting for a thief; and that the child was not a witness to Thornfell's survival but a component of it.

"She is the key," I said. "And Halloran is the hand turning it. He knows the passes. Every smuggler's trail, every blind path through the Thornfells — he sold that knowledge piecemeal for years while copying our ledgers, the clever little traitor. Which means he is the only living map to the door, and we cannot simply kill him and take the child, because without him we will search those mountains until winter eats us."

"And so the bounty," said Ferren.

"And so the net." I rose and went to the great map on the wall — Highmoor and its tributaries, the mountain villages strung along the foothills like beads on a fraying cord. "Look at them. Ashford. Brindle. The Hollows. Nine villages, four thousand souls, and every one of them poor enough to be bought and pious enough to feel righteous about selling themselves. We do not send soldiers to drag information out of them. Soldiers create martyrs, and martyrs create songs, and songs outlive proclamations. No. We send coin, and we send priests, and we send fear in careful doses."

Cole frowned — he was the slower of the two provosts and knew it, and wore his slowness like a monk wears humility. "Fear of what? They haven't done anything."

"Fear of what they might be sheltering," I said. "That is the beauty of it. The child *is* dangerous. The fire in her could level a village; I have no doubt of it, and neither should they. So we tell the truth, or a shape of it. There is a corrupted vessel abroad in the hills, carrying a profane flame, and it must be returned to the temple's keeping before it kindles. Every shepherd who spots smoke where no fire burns, every child who comes home babbling about lights in the high passes — they report to the local provost, and the local provost pays. Not lavishly. A crown here, two there. Enough to keep eyes open and tongues loose."

"And if a village refuses?" asked Anwen.

"Then a village has chosen its side, and we treat it as occupied territory." I let the words sit a moment. She flinched — good. A conscience that never flinches is just decoration. "Understand me, all of you. I take no joy in what may need doing in the foothills. But order is not a gentle thing. Order is a dam, and dams are built of pressure and sacrifice, and if a few fields flood so that the valley lives, then the flooding was not cruelty. It was arithmetic."

Ossian nodded slowly, the way he did when translating scripture into policy. "The old kings called it the shepherd's burden. A flock cannot govern itself. Wolves would eat it; the foolish would wander off cliffs. Someone must hold the staff."

"Someone always must," I agreed. "The difference between us and the wolves is that we count the sheep."

Merek cleared his throat. "There remains the matter of the garrison. If we're blanketing the foothills with informants and provosts, Vane, the riders need staging grounds, supply lines, authority to detain. That requires requisitions, and requisitions require the Chapter's seal, and half the Chapter still believes the Thornfell question is scholarly curiosity, not crusade."

"Then we change what the Chapter believes." I returned to my desk and drew out a folder I had been preparing for a week — testimony transcripts, carefully edited, in which the child's few comprehensible utterances were rendered in their most alarming possible light. *"Ash and ending"* became a prophecy of destruction. Her refusal to speak became demonic silence. The melted latch on Sera Voss's gate — gods, the woman had actually written a report about it, believing she was reporting to friends — became evidence of unchecked pyromantic corruption. "Distribute these to the Chapter elders tonight. By morning, scholarly curiosity will look like negligence."

Anwen turned the pages with hands that were not quite steady. "These are... edited."

"They are *curated*, Sister. Every word in them is true. The arrangement is mine." I met her eyes, and I did not blink, because blinking is how rooms lose their masters. "You may tell yourself I am lying, if it helps you sleep. But you will find no falsehood on any page, and you know it, and that is why your hands shake. Truth needs no defense. It only needs direction."

She looked down. She signed what needed signing. They all did. That is the whole secret of power, if you want it, and I suspect some part of you does: you do not need good men. You need honest ones, shown an honest thing, arranged.

* * *

The proclamations went out by rider within the day — thirty copies, nailed to temple doors and market posts from Ashford to the river towns. I stood in the courtyard to watch the first riders depart, because a leader should watch his orders leave. Orders are like children; if you don't see them off properly, they misbehave on the road.

Provost Ferren joined me, breath steaming in the cold.

"The Ashford priest reports already," he said. "A tinker came through two days back, claims he saw smoke in the high country near the old toll road. No settlement there. No reason for smoke."

"Pay him?"

"A crown. He'll be back with more, or his neighbors will beat him to it."

"That's the whole art, Ferren. Make the finding of things lucrative, and the finding takes care of itself." I watched the riders shrink up the mountain road. "How many soldiers can we field inside a fortnight?"

"Four hundred mounted, another six hundred foot if we strip the river garrisons. Enough to hold every village and search every pass — slowly."

"Slowly is acceptable. Speed is Halloran's advantage; time is ours. He has one child, a dwindling purse, and winter coming down those mountains like a creditor. We have an institution." I smiled, and felt the smile settle into place the way a mask settles — comfortably, familiarly, until you forget it is a mask at all. "He thinks he escaped a cell. He has merely exchanged it for a larger one with worse weather."

Ferren laughed, because subordinates laugh, and went to see to his riders.

I remained in the courtyard awhile. The wind off the Thornfells carried the smell of pine and snow and, faintly, woodsmoke from the temple kitchens — the ordinary smells of a functioning world, an ordered world. Somewhere in those white distances a frightened scribe was teaching a feral child not to bite the hand that fed her, and believing, perhaps, that distance meant safety.

Distance means nothing. Coin travels faster than feet. Fear travels faster than coin.

* * *

That night I visited the crypt.

Not the empty cot where the child had slept — I had ordered that scrubbed, though frost kept reforming on that one window no matter what the servants did, in a perfect ring, and eventually I ordered the window bricked over rather than admit what it implied. No. I went deeper, to the sealed archive beneath the crypt, where the Concord keeps the things it cannot destroy and cannot afford to understand.

The Thornfell fragments. Two centuries of confiscated scraps: a page of grammar copied by a scholar who died mad; a rubbing taken from a waystone by a soldier who deserted the same night; a child's rhyme, transcribed phonetically by some long-dead interrogator, its meaning lost. My predecessors had filed these like curiosities. Curiosities! As if they had not been sitting, generation after generation, on the inventory of the single greatest treasure in the world, unable to read the label.

I held the rubbing to the lamp. The carved characters stared back, angular and patient, older than the Concord, older than every kingdom it had swallowed. *We are coming for you,* I thought at them. *Two hundred years is not so long. We were always coming.*

And beneath the hunger — I will be honest, since honesty costs me nothing here, alone, with no witnesses but stone — beneath the hunger there was certainty, and the certainty was the sweeter of the two. Because I had asked myself the hard question, the one Anwen could not ask without shaking, the one Halloran would ask and let it stop him: *what if the price is high?*

The answer was simple. Everything worth possessing has a price, and the men history remembers are the ones willing to pay it. The dragon-fire would not merely enrich the Concord. It would *complete* it. No more scraping deference from kings. No more debts, ledgers, indentures — the machinery of need itself, retired. One act of seizure, justified by every sermon I had ever preached, and the world would owe *us*.

They will call it theft, some day, if anyone dares call it anything. Let them. The sheep are not consulted about the shearing, and they are warmer for it regardless.

I locked the archive, climbed the stairs past the bricked-over window, and gave the night watchman new instructions: doubled patrols, sealed gates, and word sent to every waystation that descriptions of Halloran and the child were to be posted where travelers could not avoid them.

"Description of the child, Your Holiness?" the watchman asked. "We never took her measure. She was only in custody three days, and mostly she—" he hesitated "—mostly she bit people."

I almost smiled. Almost.

"Describe her as she was found," I said. "Small. Mud-caked. Wild-eyed. Say she speaks no tongue of the lowlands and carries a sickness of fire. Mothers will remember that. Mothers remember everything."

He bowed and went, and I stood a moment longer in the cold corridor, listening to the temple breathe around me — the creak of old timber, the murmur of the night office drifting down from the chapel, the vast sleeping organism of the Concord going about its holy business.

Somewhere above, frost was forming on ordinary windows, in ordinary shapes, and no candle leaned toward anyone, and everything was exactly where it belonged.

By week's end the net would be cast wide: bounties in nine villages, informants seeded along every road, sermons thundering from every pulpit about the corrupted vessel and the traitor who shielded it. By month's end, Halloran would learn what I had learned at forty years of this work — that there is no wilderness remote enough to escape an institution with patience, and that every stranger's kindness has a price tag waiting on it.

And then, from the foothills, the first rider came in ahead of schedule, mud to the thighs, a scrap of paper held high — a tinker's drawing, crude but unmistakable. A small figure by a fire on a ridge above the old toll road. And beside it, tall and stooped, unmistakable to a man who had read his handwriting for ten years:

Bram Halloran.

I sent for Ferren, and for the garrison captain, and for the man who kept the lists of debts.

"Mount up," I said. "Gently, at first. A net is thrown once."
