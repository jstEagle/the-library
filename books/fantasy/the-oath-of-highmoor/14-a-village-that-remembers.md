## 14. A Village That Remembers

The village was called Harrow's Rest, which struck me as a name chosen by someone who had given up on optimism and settled for accuracy. It sat in a fold of the Thornfell foothills like a coin dropped into a pocket — a dozen slate roofs, one crooked shrine to the Concord's Nine, and a smell of woodsmoke and wet sheep that hit me so hard with homesickness I nearly walked straight back down the mountain out of spite.

"We're not stopping here," I said.

"You've said that twice," Sera said. "You'll say it a third time, and then we'll stop anyway, because Wren is swaying on his feet and Dane's arm needs stitching, and you can't stitch anything with pride."

She had me there. Dane had torn her forearm open on a scree slope two days back, and my needlework was adequate for closing ledgers and questionable for closing people. Wren had gone quiet in the way that frightened me most — not sullen, not feral, but *dimmed*, like a lamp running low on oil. He hadn't spoken since the toll bridge. He hadn't even bitten anyone, which for Wren was roughly the equivalent of a healthy man losing his appetite.

"They have a bounty board," I said, third time, as instructed. "I saw it from the ridge. Our faces are on it."

"Your face is on it," Sera corrected. "Mine is a bad likeness. And the child is described as 'a demon of the old country,' which frankly flatters him."

"Ilex?"

The archivist was hunched under his bundle of contraband books, squinting at the village through the drizzle. "Harrow's Rest," he murmured, with the particular reverence he saved for footnotes. "There's a reference to it in the *Marginalia of Saint Corvel*. A village that paid its tithes in wool and never once in faith." He smiled thinly. "Corvel called them stubborn heretics. I call them good prospects."

"That's the spirit," Sera said. "Let's go be good prospects at them."

* * *

The woman who answered the first door we dared knock on was old the way mountain oaks are old — bent, weathered, and clearly planning to outlast everything. She looked at our ragged procession for a long moment. Her eyes went over Dane's blood-crusted bandage, Ilex's excommunicant's collar worn openly as an insult, Sera's knives, and finally down to Wren, half-hidden behind my leg, staring up at her with those ember-lit eyes that made grown men reach for iron.

"Well," she said. "You'll be the ones off the boards, then."

My stomach dropped through the mud into the bedrock of the world. Beside me, Sera's hand drifted toward her hip.

The old woman snorted. "Don't look so stricken. The Prior's criers came through last week offering silver for word of you. Tam Fellows took the silver. Told them you went south toward the lake road." She stepped aside and held the door open. "Tam Fellows would sell his own grandmother for less, and everyone knows it, so nobody believed him. Come in before the whole street sees you standing there like a traveling play."

Her name was Maren Ostrey, though she informed us within the minute that she'd been born Maren of the Ostreys, "and the *of* matters, whatever your temple friends say." She fed us before she asked a single question, which by mountain law and older laws besides made her bound to us and us to her, whether either of us liked it or not.

What she fed us was bread.

Not Concord loaf, gray and dense and blessed thrice. Not smuggler hardtack that could stop a crossbow bolt if thrown with conviction. This was dark rye studded with seeds I didn't recognize, baked in a shape like a knot, and when it came out of her oven the smell filled the room and did something to the air that I still don't have honest words for. It smelled the way my grandmother's kitchen had smelled when I was very small and the world was still mostly song.

Wren went rigid.

He crossed the room without seeming to decide to — one moment behind my chair, the next standing before Maren's table, staring at the bread with an expression I'd only ever seen on his face once before, looking at the carvings above the Hollow Crown pass. His lips moved. What came out was barely a whisper, in the language that had no country:

"*Brannoch sel.*"

Maren Ostrey sat down very slowly.

"My mother called it that too," she said, and her voice had changed entirely — the briskness gone out of it like wind dropping. "Knot-bread. She learned it from hers. We bake it at the turning of the year and we don't tell the priest why, and none of us remember why anymore except that we mustn't stop." She looked up at Wren, and then at me, and her eyes were suddenly bright and terrible. "Boy. Where did that child learn the old tongue?"

"He didn't learn it anywhere," I said. "It learned him."

And then, because she had fed us and the law was the law, I told her. Most of it. Enough of it. The ember, the door, the Prior, the flight from Highmoor. Sera watched me talk with the resigned face of a woman who has long ago stopped being surprised by what comes out of my mouth at the worst possible moments.

Maren listened with her hands flat on the table. When I finished, she rose, cut a piece of the knot-bread, and knelt down in front of Wren — an old woman folding herself down to a child's height, joints cracking, without a flicker of hesitation.

"Eat, little warden," she said.

Wren took the bread. He ate it the way other children eat sweets, slowly, eyes closed, and halfway through the piece he began to cry — silently, the tears just arriving on his face like weather, while the ember under his skin pulsed warm gold and the room filled with a smell like sun on green fields that had no business existing in a slate-roofed kitchen in the rain.

Maren wept too. She didn't make a performance of it. She just knelt there with her hands in her lap and let the tears come, saying, over and over, in a voice too soft for anyone but Wren and me to hear: "We thought we were the last ones who remembered. We thought it was only us, keeping nothing alive for no one."

Later, warmed and fed, we sat by her fire while she told us what the village remembered. Not history — Ilex nearly wept with frustration at this — but habits. Knots tied in a certain way on the lintels. Names for the stars that matched nothing in any Concord almanac but matched, exactly and perfectly, the star-charts Ilex had reconstructed from burned fragments. A harvest song with nonsense syllables that I recognized, note for note, from my grandmother, whose version had different nonsense syllables and a chorus about smuggling brandy past the toll-house.

"The same song," I said. "Two hundred years apart. Two hundred miles apart."

"No," said Maren gently. "The same song, kept alive by people who were never allowed to know they were keeping it." She poked the fire. "The Concord erased the kingdom, lad. They couldn't erase the kitchens."

* * *

We stayed three days. In that time I fixed Maren's leaking roof, because debt makes me itch and gratitude is just debt wearing nicer clothes. Dane's arm healed enough that she stopped pretending it didn't hurt. Ilex sat with Maren every evening with ink and paper, coaxing the village memory out of her like a physician drawing poison, writing down every knot and song and half-remembered blessing until his fingers cramped.

Wren — Wren changed. Fed and warm and *recognized*, he uncurled by degrees, the way something frostbitten thaws, painfully and then all at once. On the second evening he helped Maren braid dough for the knot-bread, his small hands working beside her spotted ones, and neither of them spoke a word, and it was the most fluent conversation I have ever witnessed.

On the third night, Sera found me on the roof I'd mended, watching the road below the village.

"You feel it too," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Feel what?"

"The quiet. Three days of no riders, no criers, no bounty-hungry bastards kicking in doors. Either Vane's lost our scent—" 

"—or someone's found it again," I finished. "Yes. I keep telling myself the roads are just muddy."

"Your face tells me you don't believe that."

"My face has always been a poor liar. It's why I went into forgery instead."

She laughed softly and sat beside me, shoulder against mine, and for a while we watched the dark road together like a pair of crows watching for worms. Down in the village, a dog barked twice and stopped. Somewhere a shutter closed.

"It's Tam Fellows, isn't it," she said.

"Maren said he took the silver once already."

"People who sell once sell twice. It's practically a trade." She was quiet a moment. "We could leave tonight. Slip out the back ways before—"

"And leave Maren to answer for hosting us?" I shook my head. "Vane's criers will ask who sheltered the heretics. You know how these purges work. The village pays for the kindness. It always does."

Sera was silent. Then: "When did you start caring what happens to villages, Halloran? Last year you wouldn't have walked around a burning hamlet to save yourself the smoke."

I considered lying to her, decided it would be wasted effort, and said, "Somewhere between a child biting my wrist and an old woman kneeling on her own kitchen floor. It crept up on me. Like rot. Or faith. Whichever of those you find more flattering."

* * *

They came an hour before dawn, and of course they did; dawn raids are in the Concord's liturgy somewhere, right between hypocrisy and bookkeeping.

I woke to Sera's knife-point against my shoulder blade — her idea of a gentle alarm — and her voice at my ear: "Riders. Six, maybe eight, coming up the lake path. Quietly, for soldiers."

The house went from sleep to war in the space of ten heartbeats. Dane was up with her sword before I'd found both boots. Ilex swept his papers into their oilskin roll with the practiced speed of a man who had fled better houses than this one. And Wren — Wren stood in the middle of the room with his fists clenched, and the ember under his skin was glowing through his shirt like a lantern behind cloth.

"No," he said. Plain speech, cracked and furious. "No take."

"Nobody's taking you," I told him, kneeling to his level. "Listen to me. Nobody. But these men will burn this village to find you, and everyone in it who was kind to you will pay for that kindness. So we leave *now*, quietly, and Maren gets to grow old and stubborn in peace. Can you do quiet?"

His jaw worked. The glow dimmed, grudgingly, banked rather than extinguished. "Quiet," he agreed, in the tone of a treaty signed under protest.

Maren met us in her kitchen, already dressed, pressing a sack into my arms: knot-bread, cheese, a flask of something that smelled like it could strip rust. Her face was set and dry-eyed, and somehow that was worse than weeping.

"There's a drover's track behind the byre," she said. "It looks like nothing. It is nothing, officially — the Concord surveyed these hills forty years ago and their mapmakers put a marsh where that track runs. Walk it north to the split oak, then west along the streambed." She gripped my wrist, hard, old woman's strength like wire cable. "Bram Halloran. Your grandmother's people came from three valleys east of here. I knew her name when you said it. I danced at her sister's wedding, before the tax-men broke up the dancing."

I stared at her. Two hundred years, I'd thought. Two centuries of scattering. But the mountains keep their families closer than maps ever managed, and of course, of *course* —

"She never told me any of it," I said stupidly.

"No. They never did. That was the point of the erasure — it worked best when the erased policed themselves." She pushed me toward the door. "Go. Tell the old country its kitchens held."

Then the front door burst inward, because the world is not sentimental about timing.

* * *

Eight soldiers, as it turned out. A sergeant with a Concord badge and the flat eyes of a man doing arithmetic on our heads — silver apiece, per the boards. He got as far as the threshold, sword drawn, opening his mouth to pronounce whatever formula the Concord used to sanctify breaking into grandmothers' houses at dawn.

Dane hit him like a falling roofbeam.

I have seen knights fight. I copied the battle-ledgers at Highmoor; I know, on paper, what a trained knight is supposed to be. Watching Serah Dane fight in a farmhouse kitchen was a different education entirely. She didn't duel him — dueling is conversation, and she had no interest in talking. One step, a parry that turned his sword into the wall, a pommel-strike to the temple, and the sergeant folded like wet laundry. Three seconds. The other seven were still crowding through the doorway.

"Back door!" Sera shouted, already moving. "Halloran, the boy, GO!"

What followed was not a battle; battles happen between forces. This was a scrum in a narrow house, all elbows and furniture and one overturned stew-pot doing more tactical damage than any blade. Sera fought the way she negotiated — unfairly, cheerfully, and with total commitment to winning. Ilex brained a soldier with the oilskin roll of forbidden books, an act of literary criticism I intend to celebrate for the rest of my life. Dane held the kitchen doorway alone against four men, giving ground by inches, buying seconds the way Maren bought our silence: completely, and knowing the price.

I got Wren out the back and onto the drover's track, and then I did the coward's arithmetic and hated myself for every term of it: *run, get him clear, they need Dane more than Dane needs you*. I ran. Wren ran beside me, and after twenty yards he stopped, spun, and stood there glaring at me with the ember flaring gold through his sleeves.

"Dane," he said.

"She told us to run."

"Dane!" Louder now, and the light was getting brighter, and I understood with a cold lurch that if I made him leave her, I was going to learn what the ember did to farmhouses.

So we went back, because apparently I'm the sort of man who lets a six-year-old out-stubborn him, and we came around the byre in time to see Dane driven back against the hearth-wall, sword nicked, blood sheeting down her healing arm, three soldiers standing and one circling behind her with a weighted net — the Concord's answer to knights they couldn't beat honestly.

Wren screamed.

It wasn't a child's scream. It was the sound the mountain makes when a glacier calves, and the ember answered it. Heat rolled off him in a visible shimmer; the soldier with the net yelped and dropped it, cradling blistered hands; the iron hinge of the kitchen door glowed cherry-red and sagged. Every man in that room felt the same thing I'd felt the first time, in a crypt under Highmoor: the animal certainty of standing near something vast and dying and *awake*.

They broke. Soldiers are brave in formation and human otherwise, and seven of them decided simultaneously that the bounty was not worth whatever lived inside that child. They poured out the front door into the dawn, dragging their unconscious sergeant, and Dane leaned on her sword and laughed — a ragged, incredulous sound — while the kitchen filled with the smell of scorched hinges and Wren stood in the doorway breathing hard, fists still clenched, glaring at the empty room as if daring it to try again.

"You came back," Dane said to me.

"Wren came back. I was luggage."

"Luggage with good instincts." She looked at the sagging door, then at Wren, and something moved across her face that I'd seen on veterans' faces before and never understood until that moment: the look of a person recalculating everything they'd sworn to protect, and why. "Thank you," she said to him gravely, knight to — well. To whatever Wren was becoming.

"*Dane stays,*" Wren announced to the room, in case anyone had missed the policy change.

"Noted," said Sera, appearing at the window with her knives away and her grin firmly in place. "New family rule: nobody gets left in kitchens. Now move, all of you. Those soldiers will find their spines in about ten minutes, and reinforcements ride faster than shame."

* * *

Maren refused to come with us.

We argued. All of us, taking turns, and then in a chorus, and she stood in her ruined doorway with her arms crossed and let us wear ourselves out against her the way rain wears itself out against slate.

"The Concord takes the village regardless," she said at last, when we'd run out of arguments. "If I flee with you, they'll say we all conspired, and they'll burn it for certain. If I stay, I'm a foolish old widow who was robbed in the night by dangerous fugitives." A ghost of a smile. "I can play foolish, boy. I've had practice. My husband played it for thirty years and died rich in a house full of hidden brandy."

"And if they hurt you?" I asked.

"Then I'll hurt back, and I'm meaner than I look." She pressed the food-sack into my arms again, having retrieved it during the fighting with the calm of a woman rescuing laundry. Then she knelt one more time, joints cracking, and took Wren's face in her two spotted hands.

"You carry the knot-bread now," she told him. "Wherever you go. Whatever they call you. You eat it at the turning of the year, and you don't tell the priest why." Her voice caught, just once. "That's how we win, little warden. Not by beating them. By out-*lasting* them. They erase. We remember. Remembering is slower, but it wins."

Wren put his hands over hers and said something long and soft in the old tongue, and whatever it meant, Maren Ostrey wept again, freely this time, and kissed his forehead, and shoved us all out the back before the soldiers' courage returned.

* * *

We walked the phantom track north through the false marsh — dry as a church floor, exactly as unmapped as promised — and at the split oak we stopped to breathe, and Ilex counted heads aloud like a man checking his library against a catalog: "Bram. Wren. Sera. Dane. Myself. All volumes present." Behind us, thin on the wind, came the sound of the village bell ringing the alarm, and then, fainter, voices — many voices, angry ones, and not, I thought, all of them soldiers'.

"They're arguing," Dane said, cocking her head. "Someone's contesting the search."

"Maren," I said.

"Maren," Sera agreed, with deep professional admiration. "Gods preserve obstinate old women. They're the load-bearing walls of this entire country."

We went west along the streambed, cold water over our boots, the bounty boards and the burning hinges and the taste of knot-bread all traveling with us. Wren walked close beside me, and after a mile he reached up without looking and took my hand — the first time he'd done it unprompted, voluntarily, in daylight, as a choice rather than a clinging.

"Kitchen-people," he said suddenly, in plain speech, testing the phrase.

"What?"

"Maren says. Kitchen-people." He frowned, assembling it. "Erasing is fast. Kitchens are slow. Slow wins." He looked up at me, ember-light banked low in his eyes, steady as coals. "We are slow, Bram."

I laughed, and it came out rougher than a laugh should, because my throat was full of an old woman's kitchen and a song about brandy and a sister's wedding two hundred years drowned. "We're slow," I agreed. "Slowest bastards on the mountain."

Behind us the bell kept ringing. Ahead the stream climbed toward country I knew only as rumor — and on the wind, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of hooves. Many. Moving fast. Coming not from behind us, but from the pass ahead.
