## 15. The Disgraced Knight

I've hauled a lot of cargo through the Thornfells in my time. Salt, brandy, unlicensed prayer books, a bishop once — though that's a story involving a goat and I'll thank you not to ask. But never before had the cargo walked up to me on its own two feet, bleeding, and asked to be carried.

That was how Serah Dane arrived at our camp above the Ashen Fork: on foot, in half a suit of Concord plate, with her left arm bound in a sling made from what looked suspiciously like a temple altar cloth, and three bounty riders close enough behind her that I could hear their horses chewing.

"Company," said Pinch, who was supposed to be on watch and was instead teaching Wren to cheat at dice. He has many talents. Vigilance is not among them.

I put down the whetstone, counted to three, and did the arithmetic I always do. Three riders. How much are they worth dead, versus how much trouble is the woman they're chasing worth alive? It's an ugly sum. It is also the only sum that keeps smugglers breathing.

The answer came before I finished it. The riders crested the ridge, saw our cookfire — because of course we had a cookfire; Bram had insisted the child needed warmth like she needs breath — and drew steel.

And the woman in half-plate turned around, drew her own sword one-handed, and planted herself between us and them like a wall with opinions.

"Go," she said. Not to us. To the riders. "This is Concord business. Walk away."

One of them laughed. I'll give him this: he died laughing, which is more than most men get.

* * *

Let me back up, because the fight itself was over faster than my reputation deserves.

Three bounty riders against one exhausted knight with a ruined arm should have been a slaughter. It was — just not the direction you'd expect. Dane fought like someone who had stopped caring whether she lived, which is the most dangerous thing a person can be, and I say that as someone who has been paid to be dangerous for twenty years. She didn't fence them. She broke them. First rider went down with his collarbone stove in by her pommel. Second got a boot to the knee and a sword-flat across the temple, precise as a ledger entry. The third — the smart one — turned his horse and ran, which meant he'd live to report, which meant we had maybe a day before this became everyone's problem.

Dane stood over the unconscious second man, sword tip hovering at his throat, and I watched her decide not to do it. You could see the decision cost her something. Then she swayed, and Bram caught her before the mountain did.

"Well," I said, when she came to by the fire an hour later, propped against a pack with Wren watching her from a wary six feet away — the child's standard distance for all large armored creatures — "you're either very brave or very stupid, and in my experience those are usually the same man."

"Woman," she said.

"That too."

Her name took some getting out of her, but Bram went white when he heard it. *Serah Dane.* Everyone in the foothills knew that name by then; the Concord had spent a month making sure of it. Sermons in every market square. *The Oathbreaker of Millbrook*, they called her. The knight who refused the torch.

"I heard you burned it anyway," I said, watching her face. "Millbrook. That's the story riding around."

"The story is wrong." Her voice was flat as slate. "The village stands. Forty-three roofs, a shrine, a mill. I checked every roof myself before I rode out."

"And the price of checking?"

She pulled the sling aside. Her left arm was burned from wrist to elbow, the skin gone shiny and wrong where the fire had kissed it — her own side's fire, set by her own lieutenant after she'd refused the order. They'd branded her arm with the Concord's sunburst while she was tied to her own saddle. A message, delivered express.

"They gave me a choice," she said. "Light it myself and keep my rank, or refuse and be hunted. I refused slowly. Took me three days to actually say the word. I want that on the record." She looked at her hands. "Three days. The village burned nothing in that time except my illusions about my brothers-in-arms."

Wren edged closer. This was the thing about the child nobody at Highmoor had understood, because nobody at Highmoor had bothered to look: Wren collected hurt people the way other children collect river stones. The feral mud-sprite who'd bitten Bram hard enough to scar — and yes, he still complains, and no, I will never tire of hearing it — had appointed herself sentinel over every damaged soul in our ragged company. She crouched near Dane's boots now, head cocked, listening to something none of us could hear.

"You smell like ash," Wren announced, in her careful new plain-speech, "but not the bad ash."

Dane blinked. "Thank you," she said, with the grave sincerity of a woman deciding this was probably a compliment.

"It is," said Bram quietly. "From her, that's practically a knighthood."

* * *

Here's what I told myself that night, sitting up with the watch I'd assigned myself because I trust nobody, including especially me: *she's a liability. She's Concord-trained, Concord-marked, Concord-hunted, and every one of those things makes her a magnet with legs. Cut her loose. Give her a horse and a head start and wish her luck.*

Here's what actually happened, which is that I gave her my spare bedroll and pretended it was a tactical decision.

In fairness, it partly was. I'd seen her fight. I'd also seen her check on the unconscious bounty rider twice during the night, turning him so he wouldn't choke, taking his knife but leaving his boots. That's not a woman who's given up on the world. That's a woman who's given up on herself and keeps doing the right thing anyway, out of habit, like a clock that's lost its reason to tick but can't stop.

I know that clock. I've been that clock. Which is why, at dawn, I sat down across the fire from her with two cups of Sera's Famous Bitter Brew — the bitterness being famous mostly because I make it — and asked the question.

"Why Millbrook? Why that village, why that order, why'd you finally break after—" I waved a hand, "—however many years of burning on command?"

She wrapped both hands around the cup, the burned one and the whole one, and stared into it like the leaves might tell fortunes.

"There was a boy," she said. "In the village square, when we rode in. Maybe seven. He was drawing in the dirt with a stick. Just — drawing. A house, a bird, I don't know. And Prior Vane's standing there reading the condemnation, and this boy looks up at me — at me specifically, I was the ranking officer, I was the one in the shining armor, and children always look at the armor — and he smiles." Her jaw worked. "He thought we were there to help. Seven years old, and he already believed the lie we tell about ourselves. And I realized I had spent nineteen years making sure that lie stayed believable, and that somewhere in those nineteen years I'd stopped being able to hear the difference between duty and atrocity, because they sound identical when the liturgy's being read properly."

She drank. Grimaced. "Your coffee is a war crime."

"Noted. Continue."

"After I refused, my lieutenant — Harrow, good soldier, decent man, which is the terrifying part — he tied my arm to the pommel and lit the pyre himself. And the boy was watching me the whole time. Watching to see if the armor would save him." She set the cup down. "It didn't. Nothing saved him. I killed two of Harrow's men getting out, and I don't regret it, and that's the part that frightens me most. Not what they made me. What I found out I already was."

Silence by the fire. Somewhere upslope, Ilex was arguing with Bram about etymology, which is how those two say *good morning*.

"You want absolution," I said. "You won't get it here. We're thieves and heretics and one feral child, and none of us is qualified to forgive anything bigger than stealing bread."

"I don't want forgiveness." Something moved behind her eyes, tired and stubborn. "I want to be useful. I spent nineteen years being useful to monsters. Let me spend whatever's left being useful to something else, even if it's just — carrying things. Digging latrines. Whatever you need."

I should have negotiated. A good smuggler queen would have negotiated — terms, shares, an oath with teeth. Instead I heard myself say:

"Can you teach?"

She frowned. "Teach?"

"Bram fights like a librarian, which is insulting to librarians. My people fight like smugglers, which is fine until someone organizes against us, and the Concord is very organized. If Vane comes up this mountain with real numbers — and he will, because the man has the strategic instincts of an avalanche — we need to know how soldiers think. Formations. Signals. Where the soft parts of an army are." I shrugged. "You're the only person in these mountains who ever wore the uniform honestly. Turns out that's worth something."

She looked at me for a long moment, and I watched something crack in her face — not breaking, more like ice going out on a river in spring, the slow groan of a thing letting go.

"Yes," she said. "I can teach."

* * *

She started that same morning, because of course she did. No rest for the useful.

By noon she had my people doing drills in the scree field above camp — pairs, wooden staves, footwork that would have gotten them laughed out of any barracks but might keep them alive. By evening she'd redrawn our watch rotations entirely, pointed out that we'd been posting sentries exactly where any Concord tracker would look first, and identified the gully below the east ridge as "a killing sack you built for yourselves, if anyone competent ever came through."

"She's not wrong," Bram admitted, wincing. "I picked those posts."

"You picked them like a scribe," Dane said, without malice. "Now let's pick them like survivors."

But it was later — after dark, when the drills were done and Wren had fallen asleep against Dane's unburned arm mid-lesson on shield grips, which surprised everyone including apparently Wren — that the real work happened.

I found her sitting apart, cleaning her sword with the mechanical devotion of ritual. I sat down. Neither of us spoke for a while. That's the smuggler's art, really: knowing when silence is the honest thing.

"The branding," I said eventually. "Does it pain you?"

"Every hour." She didn't look up. "It's meant to. That's the design."

"My grandmother had a brand too." I hadn't meant to say it. It came out anyway, the way true things do around fires. "Smuggler's mark, cut into her shoulder when she was sixteen. She used to say the Concord gave her two gifts: the scar, and the reason to hate them clearly for the rest of her life. Said she'd have paid for the second one."

Dane almost smiled. "Did she believe that?"

"No. But she said it loudly enough that eventually it became true, which is how most truths get made in my family." I nodded at Wren, asleep, her small hand still curled in the fabric of Dane's sleeve. "That one doesn't hate anyone yet. I'd take bets on whether we manage to keep it that way."

"We won't," Dane said softly. "Vane will see to it. Men like him make haters the way rivers make mud." She paused. "But we can decide what the hatred builds. That's the only choice in it, I think. The one I missed for nineteen years."

Above us, on the ridge, the watch changed. Below us, the valley lay black and quiet, full of villages that would open their doors to Concord coin or Concord fear depending on which arrived first. Somewhere south, an army was being pointed at this mountain by a man who believed fire was a tool and people were fuel.

"You know what you are, Dane?" I said, standing, brushing off my knees.

"A disgrace to the order."

"A limb," I said. "Broken off one tree, growing onto another. Hurts like hell at the graft. Takes. Eventually." I offered her a hand up, and after a moment — a moment long enough to matter — she took it.

* * *

We woke before dawn to Pinch shaking my shoulder, and I knew before he spoke, because Pinch only wakes me gently when the news is terrible.

"Riders," he whispered. "South road. Six — no, eight. Flying the sunburst. And they've got a prisoner cart."

Bram was already up, already pale. "How do you know it's—"

"Because," Pinch said, and his voice cracked in a way I'd never heard, "the prisoner in the cart is wearing your colors, Bram. And the sergeant leading them is carrying a proclamation. Bounty notice. Your face is on it." He swallowed. "So is hers. And Wren's. And mine, apparently, which — flattering, honestly, I've never been wanted for anything before—"

"*Pinch.*"

"—and the notice says the Concord garrison at Cold Ford is holding someone. Someone important. Someone they're questioning." He looked at me. "Sera, they're saying it's a knight. One of ours, now. They're saying they caught her trying to rejoin us."

The camp came awake around us in pieces — Ilex lighting a candle with shaking hands, Wren standing silent at the tent flap with her ember-hand glowing faintly through her fingers like a lamp behind skin, Dane strapping on her half-plate with movements that were very calm and very fast.

"They don't have me," Dane said. "I'm here. Whoever's in that cart—"

"It's bait," I said flatly. "Vane knows we rescued you once. So he dangles another uniform in front of us and waits to see if we're sentimental." I spat into the fire. "Which, obviously, we are, because I recruit exclusively broken-hearted people with hero complexes."

"Sera," Bram said quietly. He was looking at Dane. Dane was looking at the south road, where the first grey light was coming up, and where a cart with a figure in it crawled toward Cold Ford like a fly dragging its wings.

"How long," Dane said, "to raise everyone you trust?"

I did the sums. Riders, roads, garrison walls, hours. Ugly sums again. But I'd made a graft last night, and grafts take.

"Get the others," I said. "We ride in an hour. And somebody wake the archivist — if we're doing something this stupid, I want it written down that it was my idea, so history blames the correct person."
