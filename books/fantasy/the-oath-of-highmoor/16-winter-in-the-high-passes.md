## 16. Winter in the High Passes

The storm came down on us like a debt collector — sudden, personal, and unreasonable about terms.

We'd meant to make the shepherd's hut at Cael's Rest by nightfall. Instead the sky closed at midday, the wind turned from merely hostile to actively murderous, and Sera Voss — who claimed to know every stone in these mountains by its first name — looked at the whiteout, looked at Wren shivering in my arms, and said the word I'd been dreading: "Cave."

Not even a good cave. A limestone gash in the mountainside above a frozen waterfall, wide enough for four people and a mule if nobody had ambitions, deep enough that we wouldn't all be found as one frozen sculpture come spring. The mule, I should note, was not consulted and did not consent. Her name is Buttercup, she hates all of us equally, and she has been the most consistent presence in this entire enterprise.

* * *

The first three days were almost festive, in the way that drowning is almost swimming.

Sera got a fire going with wood we'd scavenged below the snowline and some smuggler's trick involving pine pitch and blasphemy. Dane — Serah Dane, disgraced knight, current occupation: human wall — cleared the back of the cave of rubble and organized our supplies into neat rows, because apparently you can take the knight out of the order but not the order out of the knight. Ilex sat against the wall with his precious recovered fragments wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like a nursing mother, translating Thornfell glyphs by firelight and occasionally announcing things to no one.

"The wayshrine inscriptions use a dual-case system," he said, on the second evening. "Fascinating. The formal register marks sacred contexts, but here—" he tapped a scrap of vellum "—the same root appears in what must be a market ledger. Which means the sacred and the commercial shared vocabulary. A people who didn't separate their gods from their grocery bills."

"Wonderful," said Sera, without looking up from her knife, which she was sharpening for the fourth time that day. "When we're eating our boot leather next week, I'll remember they prayed over it first."

"We are not going to eat boot leather."

"Brother, I have eaten boot leather. It was this boot." She held it up. "Different mountain, same winter."

Wren sat between me and the fire, wrapped in two cloaks and one of Dane's spare shirts, watching everything with those ember-colored eyes. The child ate when food appeared, slept when sleep was possible, and otherwise simply observed us the way you might observe weather — attentively, without judgment, waiting to see what we would do next.

On the third day the wind found a new voice in the rocks above us, a low moaning whistle that made Wren flinch every time it rose. On the fourth day, the food situation stopped being theoretical.

* * *

Let me give you an accounting, since I was for six years a scribe and accounting is the only prayer I know.

We had: half a sack of barley, eight strips of smoked goat, a wheel of cheese gone hard enough to use as a weapon, a jar of Sera's honey — guarded with her life, and I mean that; I once saw her draw steel over less — dried apples, salt, and Buttercup, who was not on the menu and knew it, chewing smugly at her hay.

Four adults eat roughly twice what four adults want to eat in a cave with nothing to do but think about eating. Wren ate like a bird, which worried me more than if the child had eaten like a wolf. Birds, in my experience, are either fine or dead, with very little middle ground.

By the sixth day we were down to barley porridge, thin as regret, twice daily. By the eighth day, Dane gave up her portion to Wren and pretended she hadn't, which fooled no one, least of all Wren, who pushed half of it back onto her bowl with the grave solemnity of a small emperor returning tribute.

"I don't need it," Dane said.

"You do," said Wren.

It was, I think, the longest sentence any of us had heard from the child in days. We all went quiet. Dane looked at the bowl, then at Wren, and something moved across her face — that terrible knight's expression, duty and grief fighting for the same ground.

"Eat," Dane said finally, gruff as gravel. "Growing children. That's an order."

"You're not my commander."

"No," Dane agreed, and there was the ghost of a smile under the frostbite-chapped lips. "Thank the gods."

* * *

Boredom is the true test of a company. Hunger tests the body; boredom tests whether you can stand each other, and by the tenth day we were failing spectacularly.

It began with Ilex correcting Sera's geography.

"If we'd taken the eastern spur," he said, "we'd be at Cael's Rest now, eating mutton."

"If we'd taken the eastern spur," Sera replied, with the patience of a woman explaining water to a fish, "we'd be at the bottom of a ravine, being eaten by whatever eats things at the bottom of ravines. The eastern spur doesn't exist anymore, Brother. It exists in your books. Books don't have to walk on it."

"My maps—"

"Your maps are two hundred years old. The mountain has had time to change its mind."

Ilex drew himself up. He's a small man, excommunicated, half-blind in one eye, with ink permanently stained into his knuckles, and when he draws himself up he achieves the height of a slightly offended fence post. "My maps," he said, "are reconstructed from primary sources."

"Your maps are gossip written down by dead men."

That was the moment Dane, who had been silently re-wrapping her foot bandages in the corner, said, "In the Order we settled disputes with the quarterstaff."

"We don't have a quarterstaff," said Sera.

"There's a stalactite."

"Dane," I said, "no."

And Wren laughed.

It was such a small sound, and so unexpected, that all four of us turned like compass needles. Wren had both hands over their mouth, eyes crinkled, shoulders shaking — laughing the way someone laughs who hasn't practiced, rusty and startled at their own noise.

"What?" said Dane, wounded.

"You," Wren managed. "Stalactite."

Then Sera started, and then I did, and for a full minute the five of us — counting Buttercup, who made a noise I choose to interpret as laughter — howled in a freezing cave over absolutely nothing, while the storm screamed outside like it wanted in on the joke.

That was the night the stories started. Not because anyone planned it. Because after you laugh together in a place like that, silence gets too heavy to carry alone.

* * *

Sera went first, naturally. She told the story of how she got her gate — the iron gate at Hollowbridge with the latch that Wren's ember had melted months ago, the gate that had bought her loyalty. She'd stolen it, it emerged. From a tax collector. Who had stolen it from a bridge guild. Who had stolen it from a temple.

"So it's come home," Ilex said piously. "All iron returns to the Concord eventually."

"All iron returns to *me* eventually," Sera corrected. "The Concord just holds it awhile." She fed a stick to the fire. "First job I ever ran, I was fourteen. My mother drove the cart. We were moving salt past the Highmoor tolls, and a toll-warden decided to inspect us personally, which meant trouble, because Mother had hidden the salt under a false floor and stood on it in her good boots the whole conversation. And this warden — fat little man, smelled of lamp oil — kept looking at her feet. Kept looking. And finally he says, 'Madam, your boots are smoking.'"

We waited.

"And Mother looks down, and sure enough, the lantern she'd set on the false floor has burned through the canvas, and the salt's catching, and smoke is pouring up through her bootlaces. And she looks him dead in the eye and says, 'Aye. Saint Brigid's blessing. They're holy boots.' And the warden — this is the part I love — the warden *apologized* for doubting her, tipped his hat, and waved us through. We lost forty pounds of salt and gained a legend." She shrugged. "Everything I know about this trade, I learned watching that woman lie to a man whose own boots were more honest than she was."

Dane surprised us all by snorting. Then, pressed — and Dane folds under social pressure faster than under sword pressure — she told a story about her training years, about a drill-sergeant named Corwin who could sleep standing up mid-sentence and once reviewed a battle formation while dreaming aloud, loudly, about his grandmother's geese.

"He promoted a scarecrow," Dane said. "Full honors. We saluted it for a week before anyone told him."

"Did he punish you?" I asked.

"He promoted the whole line. Said we'd shown initiative." She shook her head slowly. "He was a good man. Most of them were." And the warmth went out of her voice on the last word, and none of us pushed, because we all knew where that road led — to villages that wouldn't burn, to orders refused, to a name struck from the rolls. Some doors you don't knock on in winter.

Ilex told a story that wasn't funny at all, but we let him, because it was his turn and because it mattered. About how he'd been a young archivist in the Concord's own library, cataloging confiscated materials, and had found a single page — a child's copybook, Thornfell script, practice letters — misfiled among tax records. Someone had erased a kingdom so thoroughly that a child's homework survived only by accident, filed under *miscellaneous*.

"A whole country," he said, "reduced to miscellaneous." He looked at the fire. "I've spent thirty years making them legible again. Every song, every carving, every name. Because erasure isn't killing people. Killing happens once. Erasure is deciding they never happened at all, forever, on purpose. And the only counter to it is somebody stubborn enough to write it back down."

Nobody spoke for a while after that. Even the storm seemed to listen.

Then Wren tugged my sleeve and said, "Yours."

* * *

Mine. Right. The scribe's story. What was I going to tell them — about copying ledgers until my fingers bled? About tallying a debt to the copper? About guiding smugglers through passes I'd sworn never to speak of?

I told them about my grandmother instead. About the songs.

"She sang while she cooked. Always the same ones, and she always sang them wrong, or so I thought. Words that didn't fit together. *Stone remembers what river forgets.* I asked her once what language it was and she said, 'Ours,' and boxed my ear for asking twice." I fed the fire a knot of pine. "I thought she meant family nonsense. Grandmother nonsense. Turns out —" I glanced at Ilex, who nodded encouragingly, the way scholars do when someone else is about to say the thing they've been dying to say. "Turns out they're nursery rhymes. Thornfell nursery rhymes. Corrupted, worn down, two hundred years of mouths mangling them, but Ilex can trace half of them back to carvings in the deep passes. My family sang a dead kingdom's lullabies and never told me why."

"Do you hate her for it?" Dane asked quietly. "For keeping it secret?"

I thought about it honestly, which is more than I usually manage. "No. I think she was doing what door-wardens do. Keeping something safe by keeping it quiet. She just never told me I was the something." I looked at Wren, curled against my side, ember-light faintly pulsing under the skin of their small hands when they slept. "Or maybe the something hadn't arrived yet."

Wren reached up and touched my jaw, briefly, the way you touch a doorframe for luck. Then held out a hand toward the fire.

* * *

Here is what I understood only later, and too late, and I'll say it plainly now because shame does no one any good:

Wren had been warming us for days.

The cave stayed livable when it had no right to. Our hands and feet, numb in the evenings, came back by morning. The chill that should have crept into our bones and stayed there never quite arrived. I noticed it the way you notice a headache ending — not at all, until someone points it out.

Ilex pointed it out, on the twelfth day, in the flat voice he used for findings he hated.

"The temperature differential in this cave is impossible," he said. He'd been measuring, of course. He'd been wetting a finger and holding it toward Wren at intervals like some kind of heretic dowsing rod. "There's a source of heat in this room that isn't the fire. And it's been running continuously." He took off his spectacles and cleaned them, which he did when the news was bad. "The ember isn't a flame, Bram. Flames rest. This is a heart. And hearts that beat harder than they should—"

"Gutter faster," I said. "You said weeks. In the autumn you said weeks."

"I said weeks based on a resting rate." He put the spectacles back on. "This is not a resting rate."

We all looked at Wren. Wren looked back, unblinking, and then — with the terrible practicality of a child who has survived by reading rooms — said, "Cold kills you. Fire is mine. Fair trade."

"It is not a fair trade," I said, sharper than I meant to.

"It is the only trade." Wren pulled the cloak tighter and lay down facing the wall, conversation over, a small spine of stubbornness under the wool.

Sera caught my eye across the fire and shook her head minutely: *not now*. Dane quietly added her blanket to Wren's pile. Ilex wrote something in his notebook and crossed it out and wrote it again.

That night I couldn't sleep. I sat up watching the child breathe and counted the pulse of light in their cupped hands — visible now even through closed fingers, a slow amber throb like a lantern behind skin. When we'd found Wren in the autumn, the light had flashed only in fear or anger. Now it burned steady, all night, every night, a hearth that never banked itself.

I thought of my grandmother again. *Stone remembers what river forgets.* I thought: what else did the songs say? What did the wardens know about carrying fire that nobody had lived long enough to write down?

In the morning, Wren was slower to wake. Slower to eat. The light in their hands dimmed for the first time when they slept, guttering like a candle in a draft — and Ilex watched it happen and went gray around the mouth, and started recalculating, muttering numbers, and whatever number he arrived at, he didn't share it.

Which meant it was bad.

* * *

On the fifteenth day the storm broke. Just like that — the wind died in the afternoon, the clouds tore open, and the sun came down on the snowfields so bright and clean it felt like an apology. We crawled out of the cave blinking, four half-starved ghosts and one small ember, and stood in a world of blinding white silence.

"Cael's Rest is two days east," Sera said, squinting at the ridgeline. "Mutton. Real bread. A roof with a chimney." She clapped Ilex on the shoulder hard enough to stagger him. "And your eastern spur, Brother, may it rest in peace."

But I wasn't listening. I was crouched by Wren, who had walked ten steps out of the cave and stopped, swaying, one hand pressed flat against a boulder as though steadying themselves against the whole turning world.

Under their palm, where the stone showed through the snow, frost was melting outward in a slow circle. And beyond it — I had to look twice, had to make myself believe it — the snow for twenty yards around us was softening, slumping, sinking, as if spring had laid one warm finger on the mountainside just for us.

Wren looked up at me. Their face was pale as the snowfield, their lips cracked, their eyes still burning that impossible amber — brighter than ever now, spending itself like a spendthrift with nothing left to save.

"Bram," they said. Their voice was hoarse from disuse and cold. "How far?"

"Two days. Then food, and a fire that isn't you, and rest."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a general reviewing terrain. Then they straightened, took my hand, and began to walk.

Behind us, Ilex murmured to Sera, low and urgent, and I caught only the end of it — caught the word *days*, not *weeks*, and the way Sera's jaw set when she heard it.

Two days to Cael's Rest. I tightened my grip on Wren's cold little hand and started walking faster.
