## 17. The Ember's Price

I have spent forty years reconstructing a country that everyone else agreed to forget, and I will tell you what that teaches a man: patience, humility, and a profound suspicion of anyone who claims certainty. Certainty burned the libraries of Thornfell. Certainty excommunicated me. So when I tell you that I sat up three nights in Brother Ilex's hermitage — my hermitage, though I share it with four fugitives and a child who radiates heat like a hearth with opinions — running calculations I would stake my soul on, understand what it costs me to say it plainly.

The ember is dying. And if Aldous Vane takes it at the door, he will not open Thornfell.

He will detonate it.

* * *

Let me begin with the arithmetic, because arithmetic is the only prayer I trust.

I had asked Wren for permission before I touched him — an old habit from my archivist days, when the difference between scholarship and theft was precisely the asking. He had regarded me with those ember-colored eyes and said nothing, which in Wren's grammar meant *yes, but be quick about it.* The boy consents the way rivers consent to bridges.

I took his pulse at the wrist, at the throat, at the hollow behind the ear where the skin glowed faintest gold when the fire stirred. I measured the intervals between flares. I recorded the temperature of his palms against a candle flame, counting seconds. And on the third night, by lamplight, with Sera Voss asleep sitting upright in the corner because she claimed beds were for people with something to lose, I did the division.

Here is what two centuries of erased records taught me about dragon-fire: it is not fuel. Fuel sits and waits to be burned. Dragon-fire is more like a heartbeat — it spends itself in every beat, and its whole nature is expenditure. The dragons did not hoard fire the way the Concord imagines, out of greed. They hoarded it the way hearts hoard blood: because stopping was death.

Wren carries the last heartbeat of the last dragon. A heart that has been beating alone in a child's chest for years, with no body left to sustain, no purpose left to serve, spending and spending into the dark.

My figures gave it six weeks. Seven if we coddled it. Four if we pushed hard through winter passes, which we would, because the alternative was waiting for spring in territory crawling with Concord riders.

Six weeks. I wrote the number down and then crossed it out, as though ink had any authority over the truth. Then I wrote it again, smaller, as though shame might shrink it.

Sera woke — she wakes whenever a quill scratches, I suspect she sleeps with one eye on the exits even now — and read the number upside down across the table.

"That's soon," she said.

"That is very soon."

"Can you make it wrong?"

"I can make it *unwelcome*," I said. "Wrongness requires effort I do not possess."

She grunted, which from Sera is a full paragraph of sympathy, and went back to sleeping upright with her knife loose in her belt. It occurs to me, writing this, that I have described my colleagues as a woman who sleeps armed, a scribe who hums smuggler songs like penance, a disgraced knight who polishes armor no order will ever let her wear again, and a feral child who is also, functionally, a countdown. I did not choose them. They chose each other, and I was simply standing near the choosing when it happened. This is how all the best things in my life have arrived. Also several of the worst.

* * *

But the dying ember is only half of it. The other half is worse, and the other half is why I could not sleep, why I sat hunched over my notes until the candle drowned in its own fat, why I am writing this now with hands that want very much to shake and are being denied.

Vane's ritual will work. That is the horror. Not the way he intends — nothing ever works the way fanatics intend, providence having a sense of humor — but it will *function*. And the function is annihilation.

Let me explain, because I have earned the right to explain; I have spent forty years being told I explain too much by people who then died ignorant.

The Concord's doctrine of power is acquisitive. Everything holy, everything potent, everything old — it must be seized, contained, held. Their theology is a ledger, and I mean that without affection; I lived under their roofs long enough to know they believe grace can be inventoried. So when their oldest sealed records speak of Thornfell's door and the fire behind it, they conceive of it as a vault. You open the vault. You take the treasure. The treasure becomes yours.

And there is a rite — I have fragments of it, enough to reconstruct the spine of it — that purports to do exactly this. To seize the fire through force of office and ritual authority, to bind it to the bearer. The Prior has spent generations of Concord treasure preparing for it. He believes Wren is the key that fits his lock.

But here is what the erasure hid, here is what the door-wardens understood and the Concord never has: dragon-fire given freely multiplies. Dragon-fire taken by force *detonates*.

I found the passage eleven years ago, in a fragment sewn into the binding of a psalter some terrified monk had hidden in a border priory. The handwriting deteriorates halfway through; whoever copied it knew what they were copying and their hand shook doing it. It reads — I have translated it perhaps a thousand times since:

*That which is offered passes through and becomes the world's. That which is grasped breaks upon the holder, for the fire knows no master, only a door or a pyre.*

A door or a pyre. I used to think that was poetry. Archivists are sentimental that way; we spend our lives among dead men's metaphors and start believing they're furniture.

It is not poetry. It is physics — the older physics, the kind the world practiced before men like Vane decided reality required their permission. The fire is a heartbeat, I said. Consider what a heart does when you grip it. Consider what happens when you attempt to *own* a thing whose entire nature is giving itself away.

Vane will stand at the Hollow Crown with his ritual and his authority and his certainty, and he will grasp. And six hundred years of stored dawn — every sunrise the last dragon banked against this exact theft — will answer him the only way a clenched fist answers: by breaking everything around it.

I ran the numbers on that too. I wish I hadn't. The scorch-line runs from the Hollow Crown down both faces of the range. Every valley. Every village that has sheltered us. The passes Bram knows so well he navigates them blindfolded — gone, or glass. The snowpack would melt in an hour and flood the lowlands for a season. The Concord host Vane is assembling would not survive to learn what killed it, which is the only mercy in the projection, and it is a mercy aimed at the wrong people.

They call me a heretic. Fine. But I want it recorded, in whatever hand survives me, that the orthodox position — the Concord's own doctrine — is the one that ends in burning the world. Heresy is merely accuracy arriving early.

* * *

The morning after my third sleepless night, I gathered them in the hermitage's single warm room and told them. All of it. The six weeks. The detonation. The choice.

Dane stood by the window with her arms crossed, wearing that expression knights perfect — the one that says *I am listening and also calculating how many men I'd need to hold that doorway.* Sera sat on the table itself, because chairs are for people with something to lose. Bram held Wren against his side, and I watched the boy's small hand open and close against Bram's coat, and I hated myself a little for what I had to say next.

"So we run," Dane said. "We go somewhere the Concord can't follow and we let it gutter out quietly. Natural death. No ritual, no detonation."

"You cannot outrun a clock," I said gently. "And Vane does not require our cooperation. If the ember dies unfelt, unclaimed, he loses his prize — but he will tear apart every mountain and every family between here and the sea looking for the body. And there is a third outcome none of us wants: the fire dies inside Wren."

Silence. Bram's arm tightened.

"It wouldn't kill him," I said quickly. "I don't think. The fire has wrapped itself around his life so thoroughly that I cannot find the seam. But I genuinely do not know what remains when you remove a heartbeat from someone who has never known another. Whatever is left might be a person. It might be a wound wearing a person's face. I will not pretend to knowledge I lack."

"You always pretend to knowledge you lack," Sera said. "You just label it properly first." She looked at Wren, who was watching us all with that terrible stillness children achieve when they understand more than their vocabulary allows. "So what's the option, priest? You didn't sit up three nights just to tell us we're doomed. Doom comes free."

"The option," I said, "is the one Thornfell chose."

I told them about the wayshrine record — the self-erasure, the sealing. A kingdom that looked at tyranny coming over its borders and declined, not to fight, but to *be worth conquering*. They took their fire and locked it behind a door that opens only for a free gift. They made their own power unusable to thieves. Every tyrant since has stood before that door with a crowbar, and the crowbar is exactly the wrong tool, and the crowbar-wielders have all assumed the problem was the size of the crowbar.

"The fire must be given," I said. "Freely, knowingly, by a warden's oath, at the threshold. Given, it passes out of every hand forever — into the land, into the roots of things, into weather and wheat and the ordinary miracle of morning. No one holds it. Everyone has it. And Vane arrives at a door that is already open onto an empty room."

"And the cost?" Dane asked. She asks about cost the way other people ask the time. I respect it enormously.

I looked at my notes. I looked at Wren.

"The rite demands a willing heart," I said. "The old texts are... ambiguous on whether 'willing' means willing to give the fire or willing to be given with it. The wardens' line ended two centuries ago. There may be a reason."

Bram went very still. I saw him arrive somewhere ahead of my words, the way he always does — a man who learned to read danger in ledgers before he learned to read words. His grandmother's songs. The rhymes Ilex-the-heretic had spent decades collecting from mountain grandmothers who sang corrupted nursery verses without knowing they were holding a kingdom's ashes in their mouths.

His family. The door-wardens' scattered, debt-bound descendants. Which would make —

"Don't," Bram said quietly.

"I haven't said anything."

"You were going to say my name like it's an answer."

I am an honest man. It is my single virtue and it has cost me a career, a faith, and the comfortable company of the certain. So I said: "Bram, the oath requires an heir of the wardens. If the texts are right, and I believe they are, that heir is you."

* * *

He walked out. Of course he walked out. I would have walked out too, if anyone informed me that my whole cynical, debt-counting, keep-your-head-down existence had been a twenty-year rehearsal for martyrdom. Hope is a burden; inherited destiny is a debt with compound interest, and Bram Halloran has spent his entire adult life suffocating under debts.

Sera followed him — I heard her boots, unhurried, and I did not envy either of them the conversation.

Which left me with Wren, and Dane, and the arithmetic.

The boy slid off the bench and came to my table and put both small hands flat on my notes, covering the numbers, the way you cover a birdcage to quiet it.

"Six weeks," he said. He has been learning our speech from Ilex — from me — a handful of words a day, spoken in that careful, ember-warmed voice that makes ordinary vocabulary sound like liturgy. "That word. Six weeks. It means the fire stops."

"Yes."

"And then the bad man takes nothing. Or the bad man takes it and everything burns." He frowned, working it through, and I realized he had followed the whole argument faster than most adults manage, because children have not yet learned to look away from hard truths. "But there's a third thing. Under your paper. The thing you didn't say out loud."

My mouth went dry. "And what is that?"

He looked up at me, and for a moment the glow behind his eyes brightened — the dragon looking through, perhaps, or perhaps only a frightened child borrowing courage from something vast.

"You know what the fire wants," Wren said. "You've known since before you met me. That's why you hid in these mountains with your books instead of telling them. Because knowing makes it yours too."

Out of the mouths of babes and sucklings. I have read that verse a hundred times in Concord psalters and never once believed it until a seven-year-old convicted me of complicity in a snowbound hermitage.

Because he was right. I have known about the door for thirty years. I reconstructed the rite, the warning, the detonation — I pieced together a dead kingdom's final testament and then I *filed it*, catalogued it, kept it safe the way cowards keep truths, telling myself that knowledge without a vessel was harmless. I waited for a warden to appear. I waited for the world to send someone else.

The world sent a scribe who hums his grandmother's songs and a child who smells of woodsmoke. Providence has a sense of humor, and it is not a kind one.

"Wren," I said, "what do you know about burdens?"

"They get heavy," he said. "And then you put them down. Bram showed me. With the pack. You don't carry it alone or you fall off the mountain."

I laughed. I am not ashamed to record that I laughed until my eyes stung, in a cold room, at the edge of a deadline written in fire. Somewhere outside, Bram's voice rose and fell in argument with Sera, and Dane's whetstone whispered against steel, and the wind tested the shutters like a creditor.

Six weeks. I have spent forty years making the erased legible, and now the ledger has come due in the worst possible currency: urgency. Every hour we spend hiding is an hour off the ember. Every mile toward the Hollow Crown spends the same coin. We must move soon, fast, through winter country, with the Concord's net tightening below us — and at the end of the road stands a door that will ask my friend Bram Halloran for everything he has, and possibly everything he is.

Knowledge is supposed to be power. That is what the Concord teaches, what every academy and seminary teaches. It is a lie. Knowledge is weight. Power is what you manage to lift anyway.

I closed my notes. Outside, Bram had stopped arguing, and Sera came back in alone, wearing the expression of a woman who has won an argument and regrets it.

"He wants to see you," she said to me. "He's got questions. And Ilex—" she paused, and for once the smuggler queen sounded almost gentle, "—he's got your book already. Started reading. The Thornfell one. He says if he's got to die for a kingdom, he intends to know its name properly first."

I reached for my coat, and my hands, which had wanted to shake for three days, were perfectly still.

The clock had started. Heaven help us all, we would race it.
