## 18. Betrayal at the Toll

I've been robbed by kings, cheated by priests, and once held at knifepoint by a customs man in Ilvaren who had the decency to be embarrassed about it. None of it prepared me for the particular flavor of a knife that comes from inside your own hand.

His name was Doss Marlowe. Remember it, because I intend to make sure somebody writes it down eventually, and I'd rather it be spelled right.

He'd been with me nine years. Nine years of cold passes and warm firesides, of splitting takes down the middle and lies down the other middle. He taught two of my younger runners to read river ice. He carried old Maretta on his back for six miles when she broke her ankle above Kettle Gap, and he never once mentioned it after, which is the mark of either a good man or a very patient one waiting to be paid.

Turns out it was the second one. The payment was a pardon.

* * *

I should have seen it earlier. That's what I keep chewing on, nights now, like a bone with no meat left on it. There were signs. There are always signs; hindsight just has better eyes than foresight ever will.

Doss had gone quiet over the winter. Not sullen-quiet — I know sullen, I invented half its variations — but settled-quiet. A man who's made up his mind stops arguing, because arguments are how you find out if you're wrong. Doss stopped finding out if he was wrong sometime around the first hard frost.

And there was the matter of the Concord rider we took at Wolford's Mill back in autumn, the one Doss questioned alone before anyone else got a look at him. At the time I'd thought nothing of it. Doss was my interrogator. It was his work. Now I think about the hour he spent in that barn, and about how a pardon gets negotiated — slowly, carefully, with terms — and I want to put my fist through a wall, except we were sleeping in caves then, and the walls were mostly mountain, and the mountain didn't deserve it.

The others trusted him too. That's the part that stings worst. The knight Dane had sparred with him. The archivist Ilex had argued theology with him until dawn, delightedly, the way scholars do when they think they've found someone who'll actually fight back. And Wren —

Well. Wren is a child, and children trust whoever feeds them, and Doss had made a point of being the one who fed them. I understand that trick. I've used it myself. It works because it's not even a trick, really. It's just kindness wearing a workman's coat. You don't suspect kindness until it hands you to someone with chains.

* * *

We came down out of the high country in the last week of thaw, eleven of us all told: me, Bram Halloran and his feral little ember-bearer, the disgraced Dane, Brother Ilex with his satchel of forbidden paper, and six of my people including Doss. We were headed for the toll bridge at Carrow Ford, because Carrow Ford was the only crossing over the Greywater that wouldn't be watched — or so said the map in my head, drawn from twenty years of knowing that river the way you know a scar.

Bram had argued for the ford itself, wading. I'd laughed at him.

"The Greywater in thaw," I told him, "is less a river than a grudge with water in it. It'll take a grown man off his feet and give him back downstream somewhere around midsummer. We take the bridge."

"Then why isn't it watched?"

"Because the Concord thinks in ledgers," I said. "They guard what's written down. Carrow Ford's bridge isn't written anywhere but here." I tapped my temple, feeling very smug about my head.

You can see where this is going. I've relived it enough times that I could narrate it in my sleep, and lately I have.

* * *

We came down to the bridge at grey dawn, mist on the water thick as wool. The bridge at Carrow Ford is an old thing, older than Highmoor's maps — stone pylons, timber deck, sagging a little in the middle like an old dog settling. On a clear day you can see a mile up the road in each direction. In mist, you can't see your own boots.

Doss volunteered to go across first and check the far side. Another sign, filed under loyal instead of damning. He crossed into the white and was swallowed, and I remember thinking: good man, steady man, and turning to say something to Dane about spacing the crossings —

And the mist coughed up soldiers.

Not from the far side. From *under*. They'd been lying in the cut beneath the bridge approach, thirty of them at least, maybe more, wrapped in grey cloaks that ate the fog, and they rose out of the ground the way nightmares do — no shouting, no challenge, just crossbows already leveled and a voice saying, calm as a tithe-collector:

"Sera Voss. You're detained under writ of the Highmoor Concord. Your companions will be taken for questioning."

I did what any sensible smuggler does when ambushed at a bridge in fog: I started inventing.

"Questioning about what?" I called back, cheerful, buying seconds. "My taxes? Because I can promise you the arithmetic won't hold."

"About the child."

There it is, I thought. There's the real writ underneath the writ.

Bram had gone white. Dane had stepped forward without being asked, putting herself between the crossbows and the rest of us, which is either bravery or habit; with her I've come to believe it's both wearing the same coat. Ilex was clutching his satchel like a mother with a babe. And Wren —

Wren was looking at Doss.

The boy — I use "boy" loosely; Wren is maybe ten years old and older than that in ways I still can't name — Wren had followed Doss to the near rail and stood there watching the mist where he'd vanished, and there was something in that small face I recognized from every ledger I'd ever cooked: the look of someone doing sums they don't want the answer to.

"Doss?" Wren called into the fog. Just the name. No accusation. Children always ask first.

The mist answered with silence, and the silence answered everything.

"He sold us." Bram said it flatly, the way you say a diagnosis. "That's why the timing. That's why the route. Sera — he knew the route. You only told—"

"I only told my own people." My mouth had gone dry. "Six people. Four of them are standing next to me."

Which meant two things at once, and my brain, being a merchant's brain, tallied them instantly. One: Doss had sold the route. Two: either Renna and Cott had sold with him, or — worse, so much worse — they hadn't known, and were about to learn what their lieutenant had done with nine years of their trust at exactly the same moment I was learning it, while standing in a killzone.

Renna's face told me before her mouth did. She was staring at the crossbows with the expression of a woman doing the same terrible sums Wren was. Then she looked at Doss's empty place in our line, and I watched nine years die in her eyes.

"Cott," I said quietly. "Renna. With me. Everyone else — Dane, the bridge."

Because here's the ugly arithmetic of an ambush: thirty crossbows on an open approach means everyone dies politely in order unless somebody makes the mathematics messy. Dane was the messiest object we had. She was already moving, sword out, walking toward the crossbow line with the unhurried gait of a woman who had drilled beside men like these for fifteen years and knew precisely how much a charging knight costs them in nerve.

"Hold!" the officer shouted — a captain by his voice, young, uncertain, the kind the Concord promotes for paperwork. "Loose and you're all under writ of—"

Dane reached the first rank and the mathematics got very messy indeed.

* * *

I won't dress up what happened next, because dressing it up would insult the people who bled. Dane hit the Concord line like weather. Not gracefully — grace is for tournaments — but with the brutal economy of a soldier who has decided exactly how many people she needs to stop and how few she needs to hurt doing it. Flat of the blade. Pommel. A boot to a knee that folded with a sound I felt in my teeth. She fought the way Ilex says Thornfell's wardens were sworn to fight: as if violence were a debt you pay down, not a fortune you spend.

"GO!" she roared back at us, parrying a spear. "Bridge! NOW!"

We went. All smugglers are cowards in the professional sense — we survive by knowing when a position is lost — and the position was lost, and the only asset worth saving was standing four foot nothing at the bridge rail with firelight starting to gutter in his cupped hands.

"No," I said, and grabbed Wren's shoulder, and the shoulder was hot as a hearthstone through his coat. "No fire. Not yet. If you burn them now, the whole Concord knows what you are and every village between here and Highmoor becomes a cage. You run. Running is the weapon today."

He looked at me with those ember-colored eyes — I swear they've gotten brighter since winter, or maybe I've just gotten more afraid — and he *ran*, and I ran behind him, and behind me Dane held the line the way a dam holds a river, which is to say magnificently and temporarily.

Renna went down at the bridge's midpoint. Crossbow bolt through the meat of her thigh, bad angle, screaming but alive. Cott hauled her up over his shoulders without breaking stride, which tells you everything about Cott and nothing new. Ilex stumbled; Bram caught him; the satchel of heretical paper nearly went into the Greywater and Ilex made a noise I have only otherwise heard from women whose children fall down wells.

And Doss—

Doss was on the far side of the bridge. Standing among the soldiers. Not fighting them. Not bound. Standing with his hands loose at his sides and a Concord cloak already over his shoulders against the morning damp, and when our eyes met across forty feet of sagging timber, he didn't look away, and he didn't apologize, and somehow that was the wound of the whole day. A man who betrays you and flinches has at least admitted the crime. Doss looked at me the way you look at a business partner across a concluded deal: *nothing personal; the terms were fair.*

"VANE'S TERMS WERE FAIR?" I screamed it, which was undignified and useless and absolutely necessary. "NINE YEARS, DOSS!"

"They offered me my name back," he called, and his voice didn't even shake. "You can't match that, Sera. Nobody can. They own it. They've owned it since the day I signed."

And that — gods help me — that landed, because I understood it. That's the filthiest trick the Concord ever learned: not buying loyalty but selling back identity. Every soul in Highmoor's ledgers is a debt wearing a person. Doss hadn't sold us out for gold. He'd sold us out for the fiction of being free, and the Concord would keep that fiction on loan to him for the rest of his life, interest accruing, forever. I'd seen it a hundred times in smaller rooms. I just never thought I'd watch it walk out of my own company wearing my own cloak.

Wren stopped running. Turned around. Looked at Doss across the bridge with the fog streaming between them, and raised one small hand — not in fury, not in curse. In something like farewell. And the air between them shimmered, just faintly, heat-haze in cold mist, and every soldier on that span flinched back a step without knowing why, and Doss's face finally cracked. Just for a heartbeat. Just enough to show me the man underneath the deal.

Then Dane's voice cracked open the morning: "BRIDGE IS LOST! GO, ALL OF YOU, *GO*!"

She'd been driven back to the near pylon. Blood on her face — hers, mostly. Three soldiers down around her and the captain screaming for restraint because apparently even Vane's writ wanted the knight alive, which was the only reason she was still breathing. She caught my eye across the chaos and gave me the smallest nod, the kind officers give when the order is *leave me*, and I hated it, and I obeyed it, because obeying it was the only way her sacrifice stayed a sacrifice instead of a waste.

We went into the trees on the south bank, all seven of us — me, Bram, Wren, Ilex, Cott carrying Renna, and Tammy, who'd been with me three years and cried the whole climb and I loved her for it. Behind us the sounds of the bridge: orders, boots, the creak of timber taking weight it wasn't asked to take.

Then a crash, and shouting, and silence where Dane had been.

* * *

We walked until noon without speaking. That's smuggler discipline: grief keeps until camp. But when we finally stopped — a hollow under a rock shelf, cold spring, no smoke — the grief all arrived at once and sat down among us like an extra passenger.

Cott laid Renna out and pulled the bolt. She bit down on a strap and didn't scream, which cost her more than screaming would have. Ilex worked on her with the same hands that handle thousand-year-old manuscripts, gentle and precise, murmuring apologies to her thigh as though the thigh were a text he was annotating.

Tammy cried. Bram sat apart, staring at his hands, doing whatever Bram does with guilt — I've noticed he treats guilt the way other men treat laundry, constantly, privately, and with visible results. Wren sat next to him and put one ember-warm hand on his knee, and Bram flinched and then didn't move away.

Me, I stood at the edge of the hollow looking back the way we'd come, toward a bridge I could no longer see, and I made myself do the tally. Because that's my job. Somebody has to count.

One: Doss Marlowe, traitor, nine years, sold for a pardon he will never actually receive, because pardons from the Concord are promissory notes drawn on your continued usefulness. Two: Renna, wounded, will live if the leg stays clean. Three: Tammy, broken-hearted, mendable. Four: Cott, steady as bedrock, God preserve him. Five: Ilex, intact, terrified, already writing — I could see him scratching notes by the springlight, because to that man everything is a record, even this. Six: Bram and Wren, alive, which is the entire mission, which means by the coldest possible accounting the day was a success.

Seven: Serah Dane. Captured. Taken alive, deliberately, because Vane wants her — wants her broken publicly, wants the spectacle of the oath-breaker recanting, wants to turn her shame into a lesson for every other knight with a soft heart and a working conscience.

And eight, the entry I couldn't make balance: the route is burned. Carrow Ford, the high trails, the blind paths Ilex and Bram had pieced together from songs and carvings — Doss knew them all, because I told him, because he was mine, because trust is just intelligence you hand to someone and pray they hold it sacred.

Every road to the Hollow Crown now runs through the Concord's fingers.

Except.

I turned back to the hollow. Bram was watching me. He'd learned to read my silences somewhere on the winter trail, damn him.

"You're counting," he said.

"I'm always counting."

"What's the number?"

I crouched by the spring and splashed my face, because the alternative was letting them see what the counting had cost. "The number is that we've lost our map and gained a prisoner problem. Vane has Dane, and Dane knows the pass approaches — the ones we showed her. He also knows—" I stopped. Looked at Wren. Looked at Bram. Said it anyway, because they deserved the whole ledger, ugly entries included. "He knows what she knows. And Vane is many things, but patient with locks isn't one of them. He'll open her however he has to."

"He won't talk her," Bram said. "You've seen her. She'd eat her own tongue first."

"Everyone talks eventually, Halloran. I've made my living on that fact." I wiped my face. "The only question is whether they talk because they're weak or because they're clever. Dane's clever. Clever prisoners give up things that aren't true, or things that are true and useless, or—" I hesitated again. Hated hesitating. Finished it. "Or things that are true and fatal, if they believe trading them buys time for someone they love. She's spent a month learning to love this disaster of a family. That's leverage. Vane doesn't need her to break. He just needs her to *hope*."

Silence in the hollow. Even the spring seemed quieter.

"So we go back," Cott said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't a plan, it was just a fact wearing a question's clothes. "For the knight."

"We're seven," Tammy whispered. "They're the Concord."

"Aye." I stood, and my knees announced their opinion, and I overruled them. "And we were one smuggler, one debtor, one heretic, one child, and one broken knight when we started, and look at us — still walking. Here's what Doss never understood, because he was busy negotiating his own ransom: you don't get to choose your family's flaws. You just choose whether you leave over them."

I looked at each of them. Renna pale and furious. Cott granite. Tammy sniffling. Ilex already, gods love him, sketching the garrison layout from memory. Wren with fire banked in his fists like a boy holding two stolen apples. Bram watching me with that hungry, frightened, decent face.

"Dane held a bridge so we could run. Smuggler's rule: you don't leave cargo behind. And she's not cargo. She's crew." I rolled my shoulders. "So we rest tonight. Tomorrow we start learning everything there is to know about the Concord garrison at Wolford's Mill — guards, shifts, drains, weaknesses, and how many men it takes to embarrass the Highmoor Concord so thoroughly they'll be telling stories about it for a hundred years."

"And if it can't be done?" Bram asked.

I smiled at him, and it was not a nice smile. It was the smile I wear when the take looks impossible and the risk looks worse and some fool customer asks me if I'm sure.

"Halloran," I said, "I've been robbed by kings. This is just another Tuesday."

But that night, when the others slept, I sat up with the spring talking to itself in the dark, and I let the wry face drop, and I did the honest tally, the one nobody else gets to see. Nine years. One bridge. One friend in a cell, and one traitor in a borrowed cloak, sleeping warm tonight under the roof of everything we're trying to burn down.

Trust, I thought, is just intelligence you hand to someone and pray they hold it sacred.

From now on, I'd hand it out like poison — measured, labeled, and only to the ones who'd already proven they could carry it.

Starting, tomorrow, with the ones who'd prove it by helping me steal a knight back from the Concord's own kitchen.
