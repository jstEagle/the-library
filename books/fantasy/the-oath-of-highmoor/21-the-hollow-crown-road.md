## 21. The Hollow Crown Road

The mountain had been waiting for us. I want to say that plainly, because everything else about that climb was so tangled up in fear and exhaustion and Sera's cooking — which is to say, the absence of it — that I nearly missed the obvious truth: the Hollow Crown Road knew we were coming.

It started small. A spring that shouldn't have been running in late autumn, chuckling over stones like a child told to be quiet and failing at it. Then a rowan tree, heavy with berries out of season, standing exactly where my grandmother's song said the *warden's welcome* would stand. I'd sung that verse ten thousand times as nonsense. *Under the rowan, wet or dry, the road remembers you went by.* I'd assumed it was smugglers' cant for a cache point. It was not a cache point.

"That's Thornfell speech carved into it," Ilex said, crouching by the trunk with his cracked spectacles pushed up into what remained of his hair. He traced letters older than any script I'd been taught to copy, letters the Concord had spent two centuries pretending never existed. "It says: *All who walk in sorrow are counted home.*"

"Charming," said Sera Voss, who trusted nothing that spoke without a price attached. "Is there an ambush in 'counted home'? Because I've been counted before, and it always ends with someone taking inventory of my goods."

"It's a blessing," Ilex said.

"In my experience," Sera said, "blessings are ambushes that got religion."

I laughed. I shouldn't have — Wren was walking badly, pale as tallow, the ember inside them guttering low enough now that even I could feel the cold coming off the child like a draught under a door — but laughter's the body's way of refusing to believe bad news, and I was doing a great deal of refusing that week.

* * *

We climbed for three days through country that grew stranger with every switchback. I'd guided smugglers through these passes since I was fifteen, and I will tell you honestly: I had never seen this valley. Oh, I'd walked its length a dozen times — scree and dead pine and a stream the color of dishwater — but that's not what I saw now. Now the pines were enormous and silver-barked, planted in rows along a road that hadn't existed yesterday, and the stream ran clear as glass over paving stones cut so finely you couldn't fit a knife-blade between them.

"The erasure didn't destroy Thornfell," Ilex murmured, walking with his head tipped back like a man drunk on architecture. "It *veiled* it. The kingdom sealed itself away from sight and memory both. And we're walking back behind the veil."

"Why now?" Sera asked. "Why's it showing itself to us?"

Ilex looked at Wren, who was staring at a ruined archway with an expression I couldn't read — grief, maybe, or recognition. "Because something in this country can feel the ember," he said. "And it's leaning in to watch."

That night we camped beneath the archway, and I dreamed of a green country I'd never visited, and woke with tears on my face that weren't mine. I'm a scribe; I know whose handwriting is whose, even on my own skin.

* * *

The ghost-lights began on the fourth day.

Sera spotted them first — she sees everything first, it's insufferable — small blue flames drifting in the dusk above a terraced hillside where orchards had once grown. Not fire. Fire eats. These lights *lingered*, patient as moths, and when Wren raised one hand they drifted close, circled the child twice, and settled into the grass like tired birds.

"What are they?" Wren asked. Plain speech, mostly, though the accent still bent words the old way. Weeks of Ilex's teaching, weeks of the ember resonating in the high air, had pulled the language up out of the mud where the Concord had buried it.

"Lamps," Ilex said softly. "Or the memory of lamps. In the histories I reconstructed, Thornfell kept festival lights burning on the hillsides all winter, so travelers could find their way home in the dark." He took off his spectacles and polished them on his robe, which he does when he's about to say something that frightens him. "They're lighting the way for us. Or — no. For the fire. They think the dragon is coming home."

Wren looked down at their own chest, at the place where the ember lived, and said nothing. But I saw the child's hand press flat against it, the way you'd hold a wound, or hold a friend.

I know I keep calling Wren *the child*. Habit. By then Wren had opinions about soup, strong ones, and had learned to cheat at cards from watching Sera, and had asked me once, very seriously, whether the Concord men who smelled like ash would die someday too. I'd said yes. Wren had nodded, satisfied, and gone back to whittling. That's a person, whatever else they are. But some habits of the heart are cut deeper than sense, and part of me still saw a mud-caked figure at a postern gate in a storm, speaking a dead tongue, trusting no one. Part of me always will.

* * *

The names were the worst part. Or the best. I haven't decided, and I don't suppose I ever will.

They started appearing on the third day: letters surfacing on gateposts and milestone-stones like drowned things rising, slow and inevitable. Ilex read each one aloud with a voice that shook worse every time. *Vael Harrowen. The Weavers' Stair. Coldmarket. Saint Brigid-of-the-Snows.* Names that had been struck from every map, every ledger, every tongue the Concord permitted — and here the stones simply refused to forget.

I copied them. Of course I copied them. I'm indentured to the most bureaucratic institution on the continent; when I am moved beyond words, I transcribe. I filled eleven pages of my notebook with names, and somewhere around page nine I realized I was crying again, and around page twelve I realized why.

"Halloran," Ilex said quietly. He'd been watching me write. "Do you know what your family name means in Old Thornish?"

"I assumed it meant debt," I said. "It's done well enough in that meaning for three generations."

"*Hale-oran*," he said. "*Keeper of the door-light.*"

The wind came down the pass just then, and every ghost-light on the hillside flared at once, and I swear to you — I, who falsified ledgers for the Concord for six years and therefore know precisely how much a written record is worth — I swear the mountain said my name.

* * *

We found the village on the fifth day, if you can call it finding. It stood in a fold of the valley where my maps — the real ones, the smuggler's maps inked on my grandmother's skin of memory — showed only a rockslide. Two hundred houses, or the bones of them, roofed in slate gone green with moss, streets swept clean by no one. A market square with a well at its center, and carved around the well-mouth, in letters deep enough to read by touch: *Thirst and be welcomed.*

"No bodies," Sera said, doing her rounds with a knife already loose in her fist. "No bones either. Whole town, nobody home. I hate it."

"They didn't die here," Ilex said. He stood in the square turning slowly, reading the town the way I read a ledger — for what had been subtracted. "When Thornfell erased itself, the people went with it. Through the door, or into the sealing. This isn't a graveyard. It's a house with everyone out at prayer."

"Creepier," said Sera. "But fine."

Wren walked the streets alone while we made camp in a tavern that still had benches and, miracle of miracles, a chimney that drew. The child went door to door, touching each lintel, and at every seventh door — I counted, because counting is how I pray — Wren stopped and hummed three notes. My grandmother's song. The lullaby that had called a feral child back out of the dark on our first night running.

At the last house, the largest, set apart behind a yew hedge, Wren stopped humming. Stood very still. Then came back to the tavern, sat down beside me, leaned against my arm, and slept.

"What happened at the big house?" I asked Ilex later, keeping my voice low so it wouldn't reach the sleeping weight against my shoulder.

"The warden's residence," Ilex said. "It would have belonged to the family sworn to keep the door." He looked at me over his spectacles with an expression I recognized, because I wore it myself whenever the Concord's accounts balanced too neatly: the look of a man who has checked the sum four times and gotten the same terrible answer. "Bram. The doors of those houses. Have you noticed the marks?"

I had. Carved into every lintel, worn but legible: a circle crossed by a line, with a small flame above it. I'd been copying the symbol into my notebook for two days without letting myself understand it, because I'd seen it before — branded into the cover of my grandmother's songbook, sewn into the lining of her coat, scratched into the beam above my mother's childhood bed in a house the Concord burned for unpaid taxes.

"The Hallorans were door-wardens," I said. It came out flat. Facts are flat; that's the only mercy they offer.

"The Hallorans were the door-wardens," Ilex said. "The last of them. Your family held the Hollow Crown for six hundred years, until the erasure scattered them south with their songs broken into pieces and their oath reduced to nursery rhymes." He paused. "Until you."

Against my shoulder, Wren breathed in and out, ember-warm even in sleep, and somewhere above us in the black pass the ghost-lights burned on, patient as a congregation, waiting for the service to begin.

* * *

Dawn brought the cold, and the cold brought the argument.

"We go back." Sera had her boots on, her pack cinched, her knife doing that thing where it appears in her hand without passing through any visible stages. "Look at the child. Look at them, Halloran. Honestly look."

I did. Wren slept sitting up, chin on chest, and the skin around their eyes had gone grey as old pewter. The ember had been guttering since the winter caves — Ilex had told us weeks ago, in his gentle scholar's way, that the fire was spending itself faster than any text predicted — but here, climbing toward the thing it had waited two hundred years to return to, it was burning itself down like a candle in a draft. Every flare of warmth that lit a ruin, that raised a ghost-light, that woke a name in a stone, cost the child something. Wren was paying for the country's memories out of their own purse.

"If we turn back," Ilex said, "the ember dies anyway. Slower, colder, in some ditch between here and nowhere. At least at the door there's a rite. A choice. Maybe—"

"Maybe," Sera repeated. "Wonderful word. I've buried three crews on *maybe*." She turned on me. "You're the warden's heir, apparently. Congratulations on the inheritance. So act like it. Wardens protect people. That's the whole job description, as far as I can make out from your grandmother's songs, which — incidentally — I've heard you humming since the day I met you, you great sentimental ox."

"I know," I said.

"You know?"

"I've known since the well." I looked at my hands, scribe's hands, ink-stained and trembling slightly, and thought about the fact that everything I owned in the world fit in a satchel and none of it was mine. "The songs. The symbol. The way the road opens ahead of us and closes behind. The mountain isn't showing us these things because we're clever, Sera. It's showing them because it knows me. Because some clerk in a temple two hundred years ago wrote my family's name in a book, and the book remembers, even if the Concord burned every copy." I swallowed. "Wren chose me. Back in the crypt, back at the toll, back at every crossroads since. I used to think it was because I sang the right song. Now I think it's because the child's fire recognizes the door it belongs to. And I'm asking you — I'm begging you — to trust that I will not let that fire be wasted. On Vane. On a detonation that scorches these peaks down to slag. Or on nothing."

Silence, except the wind and the well-water talking to itself in the square.

"And if giving the fire freely kills the giver?" Sera said at last, quiet as a blade going into a sheath. "Ilex won't say it outright. He hides behind his verb tenses. So I'll say it. What if the price is you?"

"Then it's me," I said. "I'm the cheapest thing on this expedition. You're worth more than the cargo you've ever carried, Ilex has spent forty years building the only honest library left on this side of the mountains, Dane is rotting in a Concord cell because of us, and Wren—" My voice did something embarrassing, so I steadied it. "Wren hasn't even gotten to be a person yet. If the ledger has to balance, let it balance against the column with the least written in it."

"That's not how ledgers work," Sera said. "Believe me. I cook mine."

"It's how this one works."

She stared at me for a long moment. Then she spat, which among the mountain clans is a signature on a contract, shouldered her pack, and said, "Then we move fast, before you get any nobler. Nobility walks slowly. It stops to monologue."

* * *

We climbed out of the dead village into the teeth of the pass, and the country leaned in closer with every step.

I don't mean mist. I don't mean wind. I mean that the air grew thick with attention, the way a courtroom feels when the accused stands. Ruins warmed under our hands — actual warmth, blood-heat, rising out of stone that should have been frozen since before my grandmother's grandmother. Ghost-lights gathered along the ridgelines in their hundreds now, a congregation of small blue fires, silent, watching. Once, crossing a bridge over a gorge full of cloud, I heard singing far below: dozens of voices, wordless, in harmonies no living throat remembered. Wren sang back. The gorge went quiet, listening. Then, softly, it answered.

"The dead kingdom is leaning in to watch," Ilex had said, days ago, and he'd been wrong only in number. It wasn't leaning. It had risen from its chair. It was pressing its face against the window of the world, and everything it had hidden — its roads, its names, its lamps, its songs — was spilling back into being around us like water finding its level.

By midafternoon we could see it: the Hollow Crown itself, a saddle of black rock between two peaks, crowned with a ring of standing stones, and in the center of the ring—

"There's nothing there," Sera said. "Tell me there's supposed to be something there."

"There's supposed to be a door," Ilex whispered.

There was a door. It simply declined, for the moment, to be visible — or rather, it showed itself only sideways, a shimmer in the air like heat off summer stone, there when you looked away and gone when you looked at it. Wren, however, walked straight toward it without hesitation, the way you'd walk toward a lit window in your own house, and pressed one small palm flat against the empty air.

The shimmer bloomed.

For one heartbeat — I will swear this on any ledger you put in front of me — I saw through it. Green country. Open sky. Rivers running silver through valleys that no blight had ever touched. And a presence, vast and old and terribly, gently tired, that regarded the four of us standing in the snow with something I can only call *recognition*, before the vision folded shut like a book closed by careful hands.

Wren turned around. The child's face was wet, and the ember under their ribs flared once, bright as a struck match, and guttered again — lower now. Much lower.

"It says come home," Wren said. In plain speech, and then again in Thornish, and the whole mountainside of ghost-lights flared in answer, and far below us, down the long road we had climbed, dust rose against the grey sky.

Not dust. Columns. Banners. The disciplined glitter of a host on the march, moving fast, too fast, with all the patience of a man who believes the world owes him its secrets.

Sera got her spyglass up, looked for a long moment, and lowered it with the expression she saved for prices she considered insulting.

"Well," she said. "Vane's early. Everyone grab something sharp. The sermon's about to start."
