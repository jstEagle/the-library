## 23. The Rescue

There are three ways to get into a Concord garrison, and I've used all of them at least twice. You can buy your way in — expensive, and the price goes up every time someone in Highmoor decides smuggling is a sin again. You can sneak your way in — which works fine until someone sneezes, and then you're running downhill with a crossbow bolt in your thigh, speaking from experience. Or you can walk in through the front gate like you belong there, because the single greatest weakness of any soldier is that he cannot imagine anyone being stupid enough to do exactly what you're doing.

I chose door number three. Mostly because it's the cheapest, and partly because Bram looked at me when I suggested it like I'd grown a second head, and I've found that when Bram Halloran looks at me like that, I'm usually about to have an excellent time.

"You want to *walk in*," he said.

"I want to walk in," I confirmed.

"With six people."

"Seven. Ilex insisted on coming, and I've learned not to argue with men who know more dead languages than I know living ones."

"And you're going to get Dane out of a garrison holding forty soldiers by *walking in*."

"Forty-one," I said. "There was a reinforcement detail last week. Ilex counted their cookfires." I clapped him on the shoulder, which he endured the way a fencepost endures weather. "Bram. Sweet, doomed scribe. The Concord has never once imagined that we'd come to them. They think we're running. Everything they've built down there — the palisade, the watchtowers, the little signal beacon they light every night so Vane can feel important — all of it points outward. Nobody builds defenses against the thing walking up the road in broad daylight."

"And what exactly will be walking up the road?"

"A tithe cart," I said, and smiled the smile my mother taught me, the one that had gotten her out of three arrests and into one marriage. "Full of grain for the garrison. Which we are, technically. It's real grain. We paid for it with real coin, which hurt more than you can possibly imagine."

* * *

Let me tell you how a smuggler plans a job, since the ballads always get it wrong. The ballads say we're bold. We are not bold. Bold smugglers die young and rich only in the imaginations of the people who bury them.

We planned for nine days. Nine days of Ilex drawing maps on the floor of a shepherd's hut with charcoal, mapping the garrison at Greyford Mill from memory of its construction — because it turned out, in one of those jokes the gods apparently find hilarious, that Brother Ilex had helped survey the site twenty years ago as a young archivist cataloguing old millstones, before the Concord decided he was a heretic and he decided they were right to worry. Nine days of Wren sitting quietly in the corner, listening, and occasionally saying something like *"the tall one sleeps badly"* in that flat way children have of stating horrors as facts. Nine days of me arguing with Bram about whether the plan was insane, and him arguing back that it was merely reckless, and both of us pretending this was a disagreement rather than a courtship conducted entirely through risk assessment.

The plan, such as it was:

The garrison at Greyford Mill sat in the bend of the river, a fortified millhouse with a palisade, a watergate, and a front gate that opened twice daily for supply carts. Forty-one soldiers. A captain named Odo Rennick, whom Dane would later describe to me in terms I won't repeat around children, though she did use the word "competent," which from Dane is roughly equivalent to a love poem. One prisoner, held in the old millstone cellar: Serah Dane, knight of the Concord, traitor to the Concord, our broken limb, awaiting transport to Highmoor in four days' time for trial and, given Prior Aldous Vane's current mood, a very short trial indeed.

Four days. So we went on day five minus one, if you follow me, which is to say we went now.

The tithe cart rolled up the valley road at midmorning, drawn by two mules and driven by a farmer named Colm who owed Sera Voss more money than he owed the Concord, which in the Thornfell foothills is the only loyalty worth the name. In the cart, under three feet of barley, lay me, Bram, and two of my best — Ferro, who could pick a lock with his teeth if his hands were otherwise occupied, and a wiry girl called Mouse whose entire personality was the ability to be forgotten by everyone who looked at her. Ilex walked beside the cart in a monk's habit that fit him badly, carrying papers that were almost convincing forgeries of tithe manifests, because Ilex could not forge documents without correcting the grammar of the originals, and the original grammar had been atrocious.

Wren stayed behind. That had been the longest argument, longer even than the insanity debate. Wren wanted to come; Wren's ember could melt iron like butter; Wren pointed out — in plain speech now, words coming easier every week, which made arguments harder to win, a trend I noted with alarm — that the last time we left family behind, we got her captured.

"The ember stays away from Vane," I told them. "That's not negotiable. If this goes wrong, you're the reason it can't go worse."

Wren looked at me for a long moment with those too-old eyes, and said, "Bring her back."

Not please. Not a question. A child handing you a task the way a king hands you a banner.

"Bring her back," I agreed. "Both halves. She'll be annoyed if I bring back less than all of her."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about lying under grain in a cart while a Concord sergeant pokes through your cargo: it is boring. Terror is boring. It's long minutes of barley dust in your nose and the sound of boots on the cart rail and a voice above saying, "Manifest says twelve sacks. I count fourteen."

"It says twelve *and* two of meal," Ilex said above me, in the mild tone of a man correcting a student. "The scribe at the abbey writes his twelves carelessly. I've complained about it for years. No one listens to archivists."

A pause. Somewhere above, the sergeant deciding whether a fussy monk was more suspicious or less.

"Ferro's in the front sack," Mouse had whispered to me beforehand, meaning: if this goes wrong, kill the sergeant first. Ferro, in reply, had said nothing, because Ferro communicates primarily through the quality of his silences, and that silence had been very high quality.

"Fine," the sergeant said finally. "Through the gate. Unload by the east storehouse. And you—" this to Ilex, "—the monk signs the receipt, not the farmer."

"Naturally," said Ilex, who had already forged the receipt.

The cart lurched forward. Under the barley, I exhaled about a third of the air I'd been holding and saved the rest for later, because that's how you do it — rationed, like everything.

We rolled across the yard. Through the slats of the cart bed I could see it upside down and sideways: the millhouse rising gray and wet against the rain-hung sky, the palisade stakes, the well, the rack of spears by the armory door, and — there, set into the foundation below the millrace — the cellar grate, black iron, behind which somewhere was Dane.

And there, hanging limp in the drizzle off a pole above the gatehouse, was the Concord standard. White tower on gray field. I stared at it upside down and thought, with the particular clarity that comes to me in moments of mortal stupidity: *we're going to steal that too.* I hadn't planned to. It just arrived in my head fully formed, the way good ideas do, usually about ninety seconds after they've become unavoidable.

* * *

The trick with unloading a cart is that unloading takes time, and time is the enemy of every plan ever made. But it's also your friend, if you understand that soldiers on garrison duty have been bored past the point of thought. Forty-one men, weeks in a wet valley, watching a river. Their attention had died of natural causes long ago.

So we unloaded slowly. We dropped sacks. We argued about where the meal went. Ilex signed receipts with excruciating care. Mouse charmed two soldiers into showing her the millwheel, which sounds impossible until you've seen her do it; within ten minutes she knew their watch rotation, which of them had dice debts to the other, and that the cellar key hung on a board in the captain's office because Captain Rennick didn't trust the guardroom with anything.

Ferro and I drifted toward the woodpile at the palisade's corner, hauling an armload each like the laborers we were so obviously not, and while we stacked wood badly on purpose, Ferro slipped the bar off the postern gate with two fingers and a piece of wire, casual as breathing. That was our exit. Every plan needs an exit, and mine was now officially open, and I felt better about existence generally.

Then the bell rang.

Not the alarm bell. The chapel bell — the garrison kept a chaplain, poor devil — ringing noon, and forty-odd men began moving toward the mess hall with the herd-instinct of the professionally fed, and I realized two things simultaneously: the yard was about to empty, which was good; and our window was now, which was bad, because windows that arrive early are how plans die.

"Now," I breathed, and we went.

* * *

The cellar stairs smelled of wet stone and old flour and, beneath it, the particular stink of a place where someone has been kept a long time. I went down first with a shuttered lantern, Ferro behind me, and the dark swallowed the noise of the garrison above until all I could hear was water — the millrace thundering through the channel beside us, close enough that the wall sweated.

She was in the second cell, and I knew it before the lantern light reached her because the first cell was empty except for straw, and because the second cell's occupant was doing push-ups.

Push-ups. On a dirt floor. In chains. Serah Dane, disgraced knight, traitor, our Dane, counting under her breath — "*twenty-nine, thirty*" — with the grim rhythm of a woman who had decided that if the Concord was going to hold her, it was going to have to hold her *fit.*

I leaned against the bars and waited until thirty-five, because interrupting Serah Dane mid-count is one of the few genuinely dangerous things a person can do.

"Thirty-six," she said, standing, turning, and there she was: thinner, bruised along the jaw, hair hacked short by someone careless with a knife, eyes the same flinty gray as the day she'd refused to burn a village full of people and set her whole life on fire instead. She looked at me. At Ferro. At the lantern. Back at me.

"You're late," she said.

"Traffic," I said. "Turn around. Ferro?"

Ferro was already working the lock, and here is where I'll confess something: my hands shook. Not during the gate, not under the barley, not creeping down the stairs. But seeing her — seeing what they'd done to the jaw of a woman who broke bread with us, who taught Wren to hold a practice sword, who carried our retreat on her back at the toll bridge so the rest of us could live — my hands shook, and I hated it, and I let them shake, because Sera Voss does not lie to herself about what things cost. That's the whole trade secret, really. Everyone thinks smuggling is about nerve. It's about accounting. You count everything, including grief, and you pay it, and you keep moving.

The lock clicked. Dane stepped out, rolled her shoulders, flexed her chained hands, and said, "Keys are on the board in the office. Mouse knows."

"Mouse knows," I confirmed. "Dane. Before we run. Are you—"

"I'm fine."

"You're lying."

"I'm a knight," she said. "Lying is the one part of the training I failed. I'm angry, I'm half-starved, and I have been listening to that millrace for eleven days planning how many ways I could kill Captain Rennick with it. Put a weapon in my hand and I'll be fine."

Ferro handed her a knife. She weighed it, tested the edge with her thumb, and something in her face settled, like a house finding its foundation again. Then she looked at the stairs, at the lantern, at me, and asked the question I'd been dreading:

"How many between us and the gate?"

"None right now. Chapel bell emptied the yard. We've got maybe four minutes before the mess hall finishes feeding them."

"Then why are we standing here talking?"

"Because," I said, "there's one more thing, and you're going to think it's stupid, and I need you to not argue."

* * *

It should have gone clean. In my experience, it never goes clean, but it *should* have, and the fact that it didn't was not my fault, mostly.

We came up out of the cellar into a yard gone quiet as held breath. Mouse met us at the woodpile, wide-eyed, and reported in a whisper: the soldiers were eating, the captain was eating, the gate was unbarred on our side thanks to Ferro's earlier courtesy, and everything was terrible because everything being perfect made her nervous. I respect that in a person.

We crossed the yard in a loose knot — laborers escorting a prisoner, that was the shape of it, and if anyone glanced, we were moving a troublemaker to the wagon for transfer. Ten steps. Twenty. The mule cart waited just outside the postern, Ilex holding the reins, wearing the expression of a monk praying very specifically for us.

Thirty steps from the gate, Captain Odo Rennick came out of the mess hall wiping his mouth.

Later, Dane would tell me he'd had a habit of leaving meals early to walk the yard — discipline, or indigestion, no one would ever know. What I knew was that he stood there in the drizzle with forty men at his back through a doorway, and looked at us, and looked at Dane's cropped hair and chained wrists, and his face went through recognition like a lamp going up room by room.

"Hold!" he shouted. "Hold, that's the prisoner—"

And Serah Dane, our Dane, did the most Dane thing I have ever witnessed in a life crowded with remarkable foolishness.

She turned around. She walked *toward* him. And she said, in a clear carrying voice pitched for the mess hall doorway and every ear in it:

"Captain Rennick. Order your men to stand down, and no one dies today for Prior Vane's ambition."

Because she'd spent eleven days in a cellar learning the garrison's rhythms, and what she'd learned — Mouse confirmed it later, gleeful — was that Rennick's own sergeants thought the order to hold a knight without trial stank, and that at least six of the forty had served under Dane before her disgrace and still called her *Serah* when the officers weren't listening. She wasn't fighting her way out. She was giving forty men permission to look elsewhere.

Rennick drew his sword. Of course he did. Men like Rennick would draw their swords on the tide.

What happened next took maybe eight seconds and I saw all of it, because I'd stopped pretending I was going to help and started doing my actual job, which was getting my people out. Dane closed the distance inside his swing, caught his wrist, put the knife — flat, always flat, I saw it plain — against the join of his armor, and threw him. Not killed him. Threw him, into the mud, and stood over him with the yard's whole attention, and said something I only caught the edge of, something like *"This isn't your fight, and you know it isn't,"* and Rennick, flat on his back in the rain with his own sergeant conspicuously not helping him up, understood — you could see him understand — that his garrison's heart had already changed sides and simply hadn't filed the paperwork.

We went through the postern. All of us. Dane last, walking backward, knife low, covering us with the unhurried competence of a woman closing up shop. And as she passed under the gatehouse pole, she stopped, reached up, and pulled down the Concord standard — white tower on gray field, Vane's pretty flag — off its hook, and wrapped it around her shoulders like a cloak.

"Sera," she said, catching up at a jog. "Drive faster."

* * *

They sounded the alarm, naturally, forty seconds too late and gaining nothing. We had mules, a river, and a blind trail through alder marsh that exists on no map because I invented it, and by dusk the alarm bells of Greyford Mill were a rumor behind three ridgelines.

We made camp in a hollow under a fallen beech, cold camp, no fire, and passed around hard cheese and the good bread Ilex had hidden in the false bottom of his document case, because of course he had a false bottom, the man forged documents the way other men knit. Dane ate like someone relearning pleasure. Wren — who had ignored every instruction to stay at the safehouse and instead appeared at our campsite at moonrise, having followed the blind trail markers with the eerie certainty of a child who belonged to these mountains more than any of us ever would — sat pressed against Dane's side, checking her bruises with small fierce hands, and Dane let her, and looked over the kid's head at me with an expression I pretended not to see because it was dangerously close to gratitude and neither of us could have survived that conversation.

"So," I said, to break it before it became a moment. "The standard."

Dane looked down at the folded gray-and-white bundle beside her, Vane's tower printed on the cloth, mud-stained now, dragged through marsh water, stolen.

"It seemed wasteful," she said, "to leave it hanging there doing nothing." She turned it over in her hands. "You know what this is, don't you? It's not cloth. It's the claim. Wherever this flies, Vane's law flies. His writ, his ledgers, his indentures." Her thumb moved over the embroidered tower. "Every village that sees me carry it out of a garrison under my arm is going to ask itself what else of his can be carried off."

Bram laughed — short, surprised out of him — and said, "You stole a kingdom."

"No," said Dane, and there was something new in her voice, something that hadn't been there before the cellar, before the throwing-not-killing, before the walk backward through the gate. Something that sounded, God help us all, like hope wearing armor. "I stole the *idea* that they're untouchable. Kingdoms we build ourselves."

Nobody spoke for a moment. The fireless dark, the mountain wind, Wren asleep against Dane's shoulder with one fist closed in the stolen standard like a child with a blanket.

Then Ilex cleared his throat, in the specific register he used when about to ruin everything with information.

"There is," he said, "a complication."

Of course there was. There is always a complication. I took another piece of cheese and braced.

"Vane's response to the raid will not be pursuit," Ilex said. "Pursuit is for thieves. This was an humiliation — a knight freed from a fortified post by smugglers, his standard taken. He will stop hunting you and start *marching*. Full host. And he'll conscript as he comes, village by village, because a man like Aldous Vane cannot tolerate the existence of a thing that got away." He paused, delicately. "Also, the route he must take passes directly through the Hollow Crown approach. He is going to reach the door at nearly the same time we do."

I chewed. I swallowed. I looked around the circle — Bram pale in the starlight, Dane already mentally reviewing terrain, Mouse grinning because Mouse loves a disaster the way other girls love a fair, Wren awake now and watching us all with those old, old eyes.

"Well," I said, standing, brushing off my knees. "Then we'd better get there first. Everybody up. We've got a dead kingdom to visit and a prior to disappoint."

Somewhere behind us, far off down the valleys, a beacon flared on a hilltop — then another, and another, signal fires passing the news north like a lit fuse burning toward the mountains.

Dane stood, slung the Concord standard over her shoulder like a trophy and a threat, and fell into step beside me without being asked.

The family was whole again. Now we just had to stay alive long enough to be dangerous.

The fuse burned north. We marched ahead of it.
