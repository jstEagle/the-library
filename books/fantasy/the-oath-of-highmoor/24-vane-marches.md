## 24. Vane Marches

I have been called many things in my fifty-three years — *ambitious*, mostly behind doors; *ruthless*, by people who had never had to balance a ledger of souls; and once, memorably, by a dying heretic whose name I have taken care to forget, *a man who mistakes his appetite for his conscience*. That last one stung for a week. Then I burned the man's library and felt better.

But no one has ever called me a fool, and I intend to keep that record intact, which is why what happened at the toll bridge made me so very angry.

Not at the failure itself. Failures happen. Empires are not built by men who never lose; they are built by men who lose expensively and learn. No — what made me angry was the *shape* of the failure. A lieutenant bought with a pardon. A bridge ambush scattered by smugglers. Serah Dane — Dane, who I personally raised from a raw-boned girl who apologized to her horse — walking out of my garrison carrying our own standard like a festival banner.

It was not a defeat. It was an embarrassment, and embarrassments, unlike defeats, multiply.

* * *

I stood in the map room of Highmoor until the candles guttered, and I will confess to you what I told no one else: I laughed.

My aide-de-camp, Brother Cassian, a young man with the unfortunate habit of piety, stood at the door looking as though he wished to be elsewhere. "Prior?"

"Four hundred years," I said, tapping the map where the Thornfells rose in their smug grey spine. "Four centuries of priors before me, each one cleverer than the last, and every one of them played this game the same way. Quietly. Carefully. A purchase here, an archive sealed there, an indenture signed in the dark. Subtlety upon subtlety, laid down like sediment." I traced the mountain passes with one finger. "And do you know what all that subtlety purchased us, Cassian? A mud-caked child in a cage and a scribe with a smuggler's tongue running through the hills laughing at us."

"The host is not yet assembled, Prior. The villages along the western road—"

"I know what is assembled. I signed the requisitions myself." I turned to him and watched him flinch, which I confess I enjoyed more than it should have. "That was always the mistake. Not ours — theirs. Every prior before me believed the fire could be *approached*. Crept up on. Negotiated with, courted, stolen like a widow's silver. As if power were a thing you pickpocketed." I put both hands flat on the map, on the mountains, on the door hidden somewhere inside them. "Power is not pickedpocketed, Cassian. Power is *taken*. Openly. Overwhelmingly. So completely that the taking looks inevitable, and inevitability is the only legitimacy anyone has ever needed."

He swallowed. "You mean to march."

"I mean to march."

The word sat in the room between us like a body. The Concord had not marched in force since my grandfather's day, and then only against bandits, and even that had been spoken of afterward in the embarrassed tones men use for family scandals. We were shepherds. We kept order. We held ledgers, and ledgers, I had learned at my father's knee, are the sharpest instruments ever forged — but there are moments when the ledger must be backed by something with an edge.

"You will want to consult the Conclave," Cassian said carefully.

The Conclave. Twelve old men in twelve cold rooms, each of whom had spent thirty years ensuring that nothing was ever decided, because a decision might make someone an enemy. I thought of them the way one thinks of a fence around a field one has outgrown.

"The Conclave," I said pleasantly, "will receive my dispatches after the fact, and will find, upon reflection, that they agree with everything I have done. They always do. Agreement is much easier to locate retroactively."

"That is not—"

"Cassian." I smiled at him, and I mean the smile sincerely; I have never been cruel without purpose, only ever cruel *with* it. "You have served me well for six years. You may serve me well for forty more, or you may write your objections in a letter tonight and discover tomorrow how quickly a pious man can be reassigned to the fen missions. Choose whichever future you prefer. I will respect either."

He chose, as nearly everyone chooses, the longer future. I have found this is the great secret of institutions: not loyalty, not belief, merely the arithmetic of comfort performed quickly enough to look like conviction.

* * *

We marched on the third morning of the thaw, two thousand spears and four hundred crossbows and the baggage train of a small city: siege engineers, forge-wagons, priests of the third rank to sanctify whatever we found, and clerks — clerks most importantly — because I did not intend to seize the dragon-fire only to misplace it in some administrative confusion afterward. If history remembers one thing about Aldous Vane, let it be that he was *thorough*.

I rode at the head of the column on a grey mare named Certainty, which my stable-master thought was whimsy and which I knew was doctrine. Behind me the banners of the Concord unfurled — the open book, the balanced scale, the flame within the circle — and I looked at them for a long while as the road unspooled, because I wanted to be certain, absolutely certain, of what I would tell the first village.

Here is what I told them, standing in the market square of Millbrook with two thousand soldiers fanned across the surrounding fields like a hand closing:

"People of Millbrook. For two hundred years the Concord has kept its peace, and you have kept yours, and between us there has been order. But order is not a wall. Order is a garden, and gardens must be defended. There is a fire sleeping in these mountains — an old fire, a wicked fire, a fire that once burned kingdoms whole. There are those who would wake it and loose it upon your homes, your children, your graves. We march against them. We ask nothing of you but your sons, your carts, and your faith."

And here is the remarkable thing, the thing I want recorded somewhere, anywhere, in whatever ledger survives me: *they believed me.*

Not all of them. The miller's wife looked at me with the flat animal hatred of a woman watching wolves count her flock, and an old man spat, and I had him whipped, moderately, as instruction. But the rest — the rest came forward with their sons. Mothers kissed boys on the cheek and pushed them toward the wagons. A farmer shook my hand. A girl no older than fourteen asked if she might carry water for the column, and when I asked why, she said, "Because the Prior says the fire will burn us all," and crossed herself.

I gave her a place among the camp followers and I did not sleep well that night, which surprised me. I had expected to feel nothing at all. Deception, in my experience, sits in the stomach like any other rich food, and one grows accustomed. But there was something about the girl's face — the pure, unguarded relief of it, the way fear left her body when I spoke — that lodged somewhere beneath my ribs.

I examined the sensation honestly, alone in my tent, because self-examination is a discipline and I am nothing if not disciplined. And what I found there was this: I was not lying to her. That was the discovery that unsettled me. When I said the fire would burn her village, I meant the fire *unmastered* — the fire in the hands of Halloran and his rabble of smugglers and heretics and broken knights. Everything I told her was true. It simply was not the whole truth, and the difference between a true thing and the whole truth is precisely the width of the world.

Every tyrant in history, I suspect, went to sleep telling himself exactly this. I take no comfort in the suspicion. Comfort is for people with smaller work.

* * *

By Ashford we had conscripted three hundred more. By Cold Harbour, five hundred, plus carts, plus grain, plus a tithe of iron that stripped the smithies bare. The villages gave because the alternative to giving was refusing in front of soldiers, and refusal is a lesson villages learn quickly and teach their children slowly.

I will not pretend it was gentle. At Cold Harbour the reeve locked the granary and quoted charters at me — actual charters, parchment, seals, the pathetic talismans by which small men ward off large ones. I had the granary opened and the reeve given a choice: join the column as a guide into the foothills, or stand hostage for his town's compliance. He chose the granary's dignity over his own freedom, which I confess I respected, and which changed nothing whatsoever.

"We will be remembered for this," Cassian said to me that night. He had stopped saying things like *the Conclave will hear of this* sometime around Ashford. Progress of a kind.

"Yes," I said. "We will."

"And you are content with that?"

I looked out over the camp — ten thousand fires now, or near enough, the smoke rising into the dark like a forest of grey trees, the sound of two thousand conscripted boys singing bawdy songs badly because singing keeps fear in its cage. Somewhere in those mountains a child carried the last heartbeat of a dragon, and somewhere ahead of that child a door waited that had defeated four hundred years of my predecessors' patience.

"Content is a word for farmers, Cassian," I said. "I am *certain*. Contentment is wanting what you have. Certainty is knowing what you will have. Only one of them is useful."

"And if the fire refuses you?"

I had wondered when he would ask the real question. I made him wait while I poured the wine, because answers taste better when they cost the listener something.

"The fire does not refuse," I said. "Fire is not a person. Fire is a fact. The stories — the ones the archivists burned and the ones they failed to burn — say the door opens to an oath, freely given. Very poetic. Very Thornfell." I drank. "But an oath requires an oath-taker, and every oath-taker they have is a hunted fugitive, and I have two thousand men and the patience of a man who has already outlasted everyone who told him to wait. If the door wants willingness, I will bring it so much necessity that willingness becomes a formality. People give freely what they cannot help giving, Cassian. I learned that running an indenture office."

He looked at me for a long moment, and in his face I saw — clearly, finally, uselessly — the exact moment his faith curdled. Not into rebellion; Cassian would never rebel. Into something quieter and more serviceable: understanding. He understood me now, wholly, and understanding a man is the death of believing in him, and believing in a man is the only fuel obedience burns when the orders turn dark.

He bowed and left, and I finished the wine alone, and I will admit to you — to this page, to whatever future reads it — that I sat a while with the taste of it.

Because here is the truth I keep in the innermost drawer of myself, under lock, under the ledgers, under the certainty: I do not want the fire for the Concord. The Concord is a vessel, and vessels are replaceable. I want it because I have spent my entire life moving other people's power from one column to another — debts, tithes, souls, grain — and I am *tired*, tired in the marrow, of being the clerk of other men's kingdoms. The dragon-fire will not make me rich. Rich is a number, and numbers I can make already. It will make me *first*. First cause instead of final copyist. Shepherd, not sheepdog. When I hold that fire, no one will ever again be able to look at me the way that dying heretic looked at me, and see appetite wearing a collar.

They call it corruption, what I am doing. I have read the histories; I know the word they will reach for. But corruption implies a deviation from some original purity, and I have audited my own soul line by line, Prior's privilege, and found no purity to deviate from. There was only ever the climb. The mask of salvation fits my face because I carved it to fit. If the world insists on being saved by monsters, the world should at least be grateful that this one keeps excellent records.

* * *

On the ninth day we reached the last of the lowland villages, a huddle of roofs called Fernhollow, and there the character of the road changed. Ahead the Thornfells shouldered up out of the morning mist, white-capped, indifferent, and every scout I sent into the foothills came back reporting the same impossible things.

Ruins that should have been cold stone were warm to the touch. Ghost-lights in the high passes, blue-white, moving with purpose. And stones — boundary markers, wayshrines, a bridge abutment — bearing letters that no living mason had cut, letters in a dead script, fresh-cut, as though the country itself were remembering how to spell.

I stood before one such marker for the better part of an hour while the column wound past me, two thousand men craning their necks at a rock. The script was Thornfell's. The carving was new. My chief archivist, a dried plum of a woman named Sister Ondine whom I had plucked from the censorship office precisely because she feared nothing, ran her fingers over the grooves and said, quietly, in a voice I had never heard her use:

"Prior, this says *welcome home*."

"To whom?" I asked.

She did not answer, which was answer enough, and I felt — for the second time in this campaign, and I disliked it more than the first — the cold finger of something older than strategy touch the back of my neck.

Because I had planned for everything a rational world could offer. Soldiers, supplies, weather, treachery, the pass defenses, the smugglers' tricks, even Dane's knowledge of my own dispositions — all of it mapped, weighed, countered. I had planned for the child. I had planned for Halloran's guilt and Sera Voss's greed and the heretic's scholarship.

What I had not planned for was the mountain itself taking sides.

"Order the column closed up," I said at last. "Double the outriders. And Sister — if the country starts talking to us again, I want to know *what it says* before anyone else hears it."

She nodded and moved off, and I stood alone before the marker a moment longer, reading the words I could not read, feeling the warmth coming off the stone like breath.

Somewhere above me, in the high white silence, a door was waiting. It had waited two hundred years. It could wait another week.

But I no longer believed it was waiting for me.
