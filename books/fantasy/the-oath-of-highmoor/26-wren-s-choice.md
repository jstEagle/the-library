## 26. Wren's Choice

The ember talks in pictures. It has never once used words, which is unfair, because everyone else in the world uses words at me all the time, all day long, like rain that won't stop. Ilex uses words like he is building a wall out of them, one careful stone after another. Sera uses words like knives she keeps very sharp and mostly points away from you. Bram uses words soft, like he is afraid of waking something up.

The ember does not use any of it. The ember just *shows* me things, the way you show a dog a bone instead of explaining the bone to the dog. I have decided this is rude of it. I have decided other things too, lately, now that the words are coming loose inside my head like teeth that were never properly stuck in.

Here is what I know, in order:

One. There is a fire living under my ribs on the left side, where Bram says his heart is, which means either the fire is my heart or my heart is somewhere else, and I have not decided which answer I like less.

Two. The fire is old. Older than the temple with the cold stone rooms. Older than the mountains, maybe, though Ilex says nothing is older than the mountains and then makes a face like he is not sure he believes himself.

Three. The fire is dying. I did not need Ilex's sums for this one. I can feel it the way you feel a fire going out in a hearth — not all at once, but in the spaces between the warm parts getting longer and longer, until one space will be the last space and there will be no next warm part at all.

Four. And this is the new one, the one that came to me last night while everyone slept and the cave breathed its slow ice-breath around us: the fire is not a *thing*.

It is a *someone*.

* * *

I woke up knowing it, the way you wake up knowing you dreamed something important and then spend all morning chasing the tail of it around the edges of your skull. Except this wasn't a dream. This was the ember, finally, after all these years of showing me pictures, showing me the biggest picture of all — itself.

A heartbeat.

Not a heart beating. A heartbeat. Singular. One beat, stretched out over two hundred years like honey off a spoon, so slow and so long that nobody — not the dragon it belonged to, not the wardens who caught it when the kingdom sealed itself shut, not Wren-the-child who was given it before she could say her own name — nobody had ever thought to ask what it actually was.

It was the last heartbeat of the last dragon. Held. Not ended. *Held*, the way you hold a door, the way you hold your breath, the way my hands held Bram's sleeve for three whole days after the night road because letting go seemed like the kind of thing that couldn't be undone.

Someone had caught the dragon's dying and refused to finish it. And then they had put it in a child, because children are small and portable and don't get asked questions, and I know exactly how that works because I have been that child.

I lay in the dark of the cave listening to Sera snore like a landslide arguing with itself, and I understood something else, which was that I was furious. Not the hot kind of furious, the kind that makes you bite people. A cold kind. The kind the mountains have.

They had asked the fire nothing. Two hundred years of keepers, of wardens, of smugglers passing it hand to hand like a stolen candle, and every single one of them had carried it without ever once turning around and saying: *what do you want?*

Well. I am small and portable, but I am done not being asked things. And if nobody else was going to ask, I would.

* * *

I waited until morning, because even angry, I know better than to talk to dragons in the dark. Also because Bram wakes first and makes the terrible tea, and if the fire was going to answer me, I wanted to be holding something warm in my hands that wasn't the end of the world.

Bram hummed while he made it. Grandmother-song, the low one, the one about the green country behind the door. He thinks I don't notice him doing it. He thinks a lot of things about what I don't notice. He is wrong about nearly all of them.

"Sleep all right?" he asked, which is a Bram question, meaning it isn't really a question, it's just a way of putting warm air into the morning.

"I'm going to talk to it," I said.

His hands stopped. "Talk to what?"

"You know what." Everyone always says *the ember* like it's a coal, like it's a thing you'd carry in a box. "The dragon."

"Wren." He crouched down so his eyes were level with mine, which he does when he is scared for me and trying to make it look like patience. "Ilex said—"

"Ilex said weeks. Ilex said the ritual. Ilex said give it freely, never take it, Ilex said a hundred words that all mean *be careful*. Nobody said *ask it*." I took the terrible tea because refusing it would take longer than drinking it. "You all keep deciding things about it. Vane wants to take it. Ilex wants to free it. Dane wants to protect it. You want—" I stopped, because I didn't actually know what Bram wanted, and his face went strange when I paused, like a door opening onto a room he hadn't swept.

"What do you want?" he asked quietly.

"That's the whole point," I said. "Nobody knows. So I'm going to find out."

He didn't say no. That is one of the good things about Bram. When he is scared, he doesn't grab; he just gets very still and watches you the way you watch a bird land on a branch, hoping it stays, knowing it might not. He watched me walk out of the cave mouth into the white morning, and he let me go, and later Ilex would tell him he should have stopped me, and Bram would say, "She'd have bitten me," and everyone would laugh, and it would even be true.

But mostly it was that he trusted the bird to come back.

* * *

There is a shelf of stone above the cave, a little ledge like the mountain made it on purpose for sitting, and I climbed up there and folded my legs the way Ilex folds his when he reads, because it seemed respectful. You should be polite when you're asking someone the biggest question of their life.

"Hello," I said. Out loud, first, because starting out loud felt honest. My breath came out in a cloud. "I know you don't use words. But I'm going to use some anyway, and you can show me back. Deal?"

Under my ribs, on the left, the warmth shifted. Not answering yet. Listening. I know the difference now — listening is still, like a rabbit deciding whether you're a predator; answering moves, like a rabbit running. The warmth was very still.

"Here's what happened to you," I said. "Somebody caught your last heartbeat. They were probably trying to save it. Maybe they were trying to steal it. Maybe it was both, because people are usually both, I've met enough of them now to know. And then the kingdom shut itself away, and the heartbeat got passed down and down and down, and eventually it got passed to me, and nobody told me anything, they just gave me to it like I was a box."

The warmth flickered. An image came up through my chest like water rising in a well: a hall of stone, tall people in grey robes, a small bundle being carried on a cushion. Me. Smaller than me. Being handed over like bread.

"Yeah," I said. "That one. We agree on that one."

I hugged my knees. The cold bit through my coat. Somewhere below, Sera was swearing at a frozen boot, which is the most normal sound in the world, and I held on to it like a rope.

"So here's my question. The real one." My voice came out smaller than I wanted, so I made it bigger on purpose. "Do you want to live? Or do you want to rest?"

Silence. Even the mountain seemed quiet. Even Sera's swearing had paused, as if the boot had apologized.

And then the ember showed me everything.

* * *

This is how it went. I am going to write it all down because Ilex taught me letters and Bram gave me a stub of charcoal and Dane said a thing worth knowing gets written twice, and this is the most worth-knowing thing there has ever been, so.

First it showed me the dragon. Not the way the stories show dragons — not the terror, not the gold, not the burning. It showed me the dragon from *inside*, which is a strange thing to see, like being a house and looking out all your windows at once. The dragon was old and tired and full of a country. That is the only way I can say it. Full of a country. Every green valley, every river, every sheep-field and orchard and high summer meadow, all of it lived inside the dragon the way the dragon now lives inside me. The dragon didn't own Thornfell. Thornfell owned the dragon, or maybe neither of those, maybe they just belonged to each other the way a song belongs to a singer and a singer belongs to a song.

Then it showed me the tyrant coming. I didn't see his face. Fires don't remember faces, only heat. But I saw the heat of him, the wanting, the reaching, and I understood why the kingdom chose to erase itself rather than be eaten. Ilex told us that story at the wayshrine, but hearing a story and being shown it from inside the heart that made the choice are different animals entirely. This wasn't defeat. This was a door being closed by someone strong enough to close it.

Then it showed me the catching. The last heartbeat, falling, and hands — many hands, generation after generation of hands — catching it, holding it, passing it. Two hundred years of people keeping a promise they'd half forgotten the reason for. Some of them loved it. Some of them feared it. One of them — a woman with smoke-grey braids, and I knew her, oh, I knew her, because Bram hums her songs — one of them sang to it, smuggler songs, corrupted little rhymes, and the heartbeat kept time with them, and for a moment I was so homesick for a grandmother who died before I was born that I had to stop and press my fist against my mouth.

Then it showed me the waiting. And this was the worst part, worse than the tyrant, worse than the catching. Two hundred years of waiting, held inside one stretched-out beat, and underneath all of it, the same wish repeating like water dripping in the same place on the same stone:

*Don't let it end in a cage.*

*Don't let it end in a cage.*

*Don't let it end in a—*

"Okay," I said out loud, wet-faced and freezing. "Okay. I heard you. But that's not my question. Everybody wants to not end in a cage. Vane wants to open a cage. Ilex wants to open a different cage. I asked you something specific, and I'm a person, and you're going to answer me specifically."

And the warmth under my ribs did the thing it does when it's amused, which is tickle, which is completely unfair during an argument.

Then it answered.

* * *

It showed me green country. Not memory this time — I could tell the difference now, the memories have dust on them, these were clean. Green country and open sky. Grass moving in wind that smelled like rain and growth and growing things. Rivers running bright. Valleys that had been blighted and sick for two hundred years, and I watched them — in the picture, in the wish — I watched them heal. Color coming back into them like blood into a numb hand.

And I understood.

It doesn't want to live. Living means staying. Staying means me, forever, a fire in a girl, a girl as a lantern, both of us kept. Kept by Bram, kept by the family, kept safe, kept warm — and kept is just a kinder word for caged, and we would both grow old inside the kindness, and the valleys would stay grey, and the sky would stay shut.

It wants to be *given*.

Given to the land. Poured out. Finished. The last heartbeat finally allowed to complete itself, not snuffed out and not hoarded, but spent — spent the way you spend everything that matters, all at once, gloriously, on something bigger than yourself. All that saved-up life, two hundred years of a single held note, released into the soil and the rivers and the roots of things. The dragon's last act wouldn't be dying and wouldn't be enduring. It would be a gift so large it would look, from close up, exactly like an ending.

Open sky. That was the last image. Open sky, and beneath it, green country going on forever, belonging to no one, needing no keeper.

I sat on my stone shelf and cried, but it was the good crying, the kind that rinses instead of rots.

Because here is the thing that shames me even writing it, the thing I almost didn't put down: for one moment — one small, mean, human moment — I had hoped it would say *live*. Live, and stay, and be mine, and I would never be alone again. There is a fire in my chest that is older than kingdoms, and some greedy little corner of me wanted to keep it the way Vane wants to keep it, just with better reasons and softer hands.

And the ember knew. Of course it knew. It has been living with a child for years; it knows every corner of me, including the ugly ones. And it loved me anyway, the way the country loved the dragon, the way you love a song you've heard ten thousand times.

It wasn't asking to leave me. It was asking me to help it finish. And helping someone finish is the opposite of losing them. Ilex said that once about a book whose last page had burned — he said, "We don't mourn the missing page, we honor the story by telling it whole." I didn't understand him then. I understand him now.

I climbed down off the shelf.

Everyone was pretending not to have been watching the cave mouth for the last hour. Sera had gotten her boot on, apparently by threatening it. Dane stood with her arms crossed doing her statue impression. Ilex had ink on three fingers and a fourth expression queued up. Bram just looked at me, and his face was doing the thing where it tries to read mine and pretends it isn't.

"Well?" he said.

"It answered," I said.

Every adult in that cave leaned in. Four grown people, a knight and a queen and a scholar and a smuggler-scribe, all of them cleverer than me on paper, all of them having argued for days about taking and freeing and protecting and seizing — and every one of their grand plans suddenly looking like what it was: people fighting over a box without asking what was inside it whether it wanted out.

"It doesn't want to live in me forever," I said. "And it doesn't want to be taken, and it doesn't want to be protected, and it doesn't want to be freed like a prisoner. It wants to be *given*. Given to the land. All of it, all at once. The valleys come back green. The sky comes back open. And then it's done, and it's glad, and nobody owns it, not ever again."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on your shoulders.

"Not taken," Ilex whispered, and his voice cracked in the middle like a boy's, and I realized he had spent his whole life reconstructing a dead country and had just heard its heart speak. "Never taken. Given. Oh, you brilliant, impossible child—"

"It also showed me its insides," I added, because fairness matters. "Which were extremely green. And it likes Bram's humming. The smoke-braid woman's songs. It kept time."

Bram made a sound like someone had hit him gently in the sternum. He sat down on a rock without appearing to decide to.

Dane spoke last, slowly, the way she speaks when the words cost something. "Giving it all at once. What does 'all at once' cost the bearer?"

And that was the question, wasn't it. The one underneath. I felt the ember go still against my ribs — not hiding, exactly. Waiting to see what I'd say. Trusting me to say it.

"I don't know yet," I said honestly. "But I know this much. It's been holding its last heartbeat for two hundred years so it could give it away right. The least we can do is find out what giving costs before we're standing at the door with our hands full."

Ilex was already reaching for his satchel, already muttering about wayshrine records and warden rites. Sera was already talking routes and timing and *who holds the pass mouth*. Dane was already counting soldiers in her head; I could see her lips move.

Only Bram looked at me. Only Bram had gone the color of the snow.

"Bram," I said, and took his hand, and it was cold, so I warmed it, which made him flinch and then not-flinch. "It's going to be all right. It told me the ending. Green country, open sky. That's not a sad ending. That's the best ending there is."

He smiled at me the way you smile when you believe a child to make the child feel better, which is the wrong direction, and I let him have it, because adults need that sometimes.

Below us, far down the valley, a thin line of black was crawling along the white road like ink spilled across a page.

Concord banners.

Sera saw them first, because of course she did. "Company strength," she said, all the softness gone out of her voice. "Maybe more. Moving fast." She turned, and her eyes found mine, and then Bram's. "Whatever your fire just decided, little bird — it looks like we're going to have to argue it at the door."
