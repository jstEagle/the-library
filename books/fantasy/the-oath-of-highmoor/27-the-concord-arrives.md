## 27. The Concord Arrives

Dawn came up over the Hollow Crown like a blade being drawn slowly out of a sheath, and I remember thinking — with the particular clarity that arrives only when everything is about to go wrong — that I had never seen the mountains so beautiful, and I would likely never see them again.

The pass mouth was a black notch in the eastern wall, half a mile below the door. Between us and it lay a shelf of scree and old paving, Thornfell's road surfacing from under two centuries of stone like a whale's spine breaking water. And beyond the notch, down in the valley where the mist was burning off, there were fires.

Hundreds of them.

"They came faster than Ilex said they would," Sera said beside me. She had her spyglass up, elbow propped on a boulder, entirely too calm about it. "Vane must have pushed his vanguard through the night. That's what I'd have done."

"You've never pushed anything through the night in your life except a bottle."

"I've pushed plenty through the night, love. You were usually one of them." She lowered the glass and passed it to me. "Look at the left flank. Tell me what you see."

I looked. The Concord host spilled across the valley floor like ink poured from an overturned pot — ranks of white-and-grey, the sun-catch of pikes, the slow patient geometry of men who believed God and the ledger were both on their side. But at the left flank, where the ground narrowed toward the smugglers' trail I knew better than anyone alive, the formation was wrong. Too dense. Too eager.

"They know about the lower trail," I said.

"Someone sold it." Sera shrugged, and something in the shrug told me she already knew who, and that the knowing had cost her more than she'd ever admit. "Doesn't matter now. What matters is arithmetic. Count their banners for me."

I counted until my mouth went dry. "Forty banners. Maybe six thousand on the field. More coming up the valley behind them."

"And how many of us?"

I didn't answer, because the answer was insulting. Dane stood at the center of our line with perhaps ninety souls behind her — Sera's smugglers, a scattering of villagers who'd walked away from their hearths to be here, a handful of excommunicated temple clerks carrying kitchen knives and Ilex's borrowed conviction. Dane herself was in her old Concord plate, scrubbed of insignia, which was either a defiant joke or armor, depending on how the morning went. She was checking straps on a shield like a woman preparing breakfast.

"Ninety against six thousand," I said.

"Eighty-seven. Marta broke her wrist falling out of the supply cave last night." Sera took the spyglass back. "But we don't need to beat them. We need to bore them. Every minute they spend trying to dig us out of this pass mouth is a minute you and the little ember aren't spending climbing to that door." She glanced at me, and beneath all the iron in her face there was something almost gentle. "So buy me time, Halloran. Go climb."

* * *

Wren was waiting at the shrine above the pass mouth, sitting cross-legged on a fallen pillar as if waiting for a lesson, because that's what Wren had been doing every morning for three weeks — lessons with Ilex, words coming back into the child's mouth like water finding an old channel. The ember showed faintly through the skin of both hands now, a glow like coals under ash. It had been getting brighter as we climbed, and brighter meant closer to gone.

"Is it time?" Wren asked. Plain speech, mostly. Wren still slipped into Thornfell when frightened or tired or happy, but plain speech when it mattered. "Are they here?"

"They're here."

"Then why are you looking at me like that?"

"Like what?"

"Like Grandmother Ilex looks at the broken pages." Wren hopped off the pillar. At eleven years old — or whatever age a feral child carried out of a crypt actually was — Wren had mastered the art of making me feel transparent. "You think I can't do it."

"I think nobody should have to do it."

"The dragon didn't ask anybody either." Wren started up the stair without waiting for me, small boots finding the worn hollows that two-hundred-year-old feet had worn into the stone. Then paused, and looked back, and said the thing that has kept me upright ever since: "But it asked me. Last night. In the dream. It said *are you willing*, and I said yes, and it went quiet after that, like it was crying, or resting. So don't look at me like that, Bram. Look at me like I'm walking somewhere."

I looked at Wren like that. It was the best I could manage with my throat closed.

We climbed. Behind us and below us, faint as weather, the first horns of the Concord began to sound.

* * *

I won't pretend I heard much of the battle. Sound carries strangely in the Hollow Crown; the walls are shaped like a cupped hand, and everything that happened at the pass mouth reached me as a distant percussion — horns, then drums, then the deep rolling concussion of many men running uphill in armor, then the smaller sounds, screams and steel, thinned by distance into something almost abstract.

Almost. Not quite. Because every scream had a name attached to it in my head, and most of those names I knew.

What I know of the stand at the pass mouth I pieced together afterward, from Dane and Sera and the survivors, and I set it down here because it deserves setting down, even if no ledger will ever hold it.

They held the notch itself, where the road pinched between two walls of living rock barely wide enough for four abreast. Dane put her shield-men there — twenty of her steadiest — and did the thing that made every soldier who survived that day tell the story for the rest of their lives: she stepped out alone, in front of her own line, into bowshot, and raised her visor so the Concord could see her face.

Serah Dane. Knight-Captain of the Third Vigil, stripped of rank, declared traitor, hunted for a year — standing in the dawn light in front of the army she'd once served, arms open.

"She shouted something," Marta told me later, her splinted wrist cradled against her chest. "Couldn't catch it all. Something like — 'I am Serah Dane, and I burned nothing.' Over and over. Like a bell. And the front ranks just... stopped. You could see them not wanting to believe it."

Vane wasn't with the vanguard — he was a half-day behind with the main body, which was the only mercy that morning — but his vanguard commander was a man named Corvel, and Corvel had no patience for bells. He ordered the advance.

And then Sera's people did what Sera's people had been doing for thirty years, which was make the mountain itself disagree with the Concord's plans. The scree slopes above the approach had been seeded overnight with every trick in the smuggler's book — cut holds, rolled boulders on hair-trigger chocks, oil and pitch on the switchbacks, and in one inspired touch, a hundred beehives from a village apiary positioned above the second bend. When the first company hit the pinch point and Dane's shields locked, the mountain began shedding stones, smoke, and furious bees, and the neat geometry of six thousand men turned into six thousand individual arguments with gravity.

It bought minutes. That's all any of it bought. Minutes measured out in blood and bruises and the lives of people whose names I am writing into this account wherever I can find room for them, because minutes are the currency of last stands and someone paid full price for every one of mine.

* * *

Halfway up the door-stair, Wren stopped so suddenly I nearly walked into the child's back.

"What is it?"

"They're afraid." Wren's hands were pressed flat against the rock wall, and the stone under those small palms was glowing — a soft gold spreading outward from each finger like warmth through frost. "The mountain is telling me. Down there—" a nod backward, toward the sound of the battle, "—so many of them are afraid, and some of them are dying, and the mountain can taste it."

"Can you do anything?"

"No. Can you?" There was no accusation in it. Just the terrible honesty of children.

"No."

"Then we keep walking. That's what Dane would say." Wren lifted both hands off the stone, and where they'd rested, the rock stayed warm-gold for a moment before fading. "She says it when she's most scared. I can smell it on her. *Keep walking.*"

So we kept walking.

* * *

The door, when we reached it, was exactly as Ilex had described and nothing like I had imagined. All my life I'd pictured the hidden door of Thornfell as a door — hinges, timber, iron, something you could knock on. What stood at the head of the Hollow Crown was a cliff face forty feet high and sixty wide, dressed stone fitted so finely that a knife blade couldn't slip between the blocks, carved over its whole surface with writing so old the letters had worn round as river stones. And in the center, at the height of a tall man's heart, a single uncarved square of darker stone, smooth as still water.

The oath-stone. The place where a warden's hand was meant to rest.

"It's beautiful," Wren whispered.

"It's a wall."

"It's a door. Doors are just walls that haven't been asked properly yet." Wren walked up to it and laid both glowing hands flat against the dark square, and the whole cliff face shivered — not moved, exactly, but *noticed*, the way a sleeping animal notices a familiar footstep. The carved letters along the edges kindled faintly gold, one after another, like lamps lit down a long street.

And then the light guttered. Flickered. Dimmed to embers, literally, the glow retreating into Wren's hands.

"I'm sorry," Wren said, to the door, to me, to the dying voice inside. "There isn't enough of me left to hold it open. We need the words now. Right now."

I pulled Ilex's transcription from my coat — three weeks of the old archivist's labor, copied in my own hand because Ilex's eyes were failing and mine weren't yet. The warden's rite. The Door-Keeper's Oath. Words my grandmother had sung to me as nonsense rhymes while she carded wool, corrupted across generations into smuggler-songs, and here they were, whole, in stone older than every kingdom I'd ever owed money to.

"Bram." Wren tugged my sleeve. "Listen."

I listened. Below us, the percussion of the battle had changed character — fewer horns, more horns, a new rhythm underneath, and I recognized it because I'd drilled to it in the temple yards a decade ago: the Concord's marching cadence. A second column. They'd found another way around, or simply thrown enough men at the scree to bury the traps under bodies.

"How long do we have?" I asked.

Wren cocked their head, listening to a mountain I couldn't hear. "Not enough. But some. Enough for the words, if you're fast."

I unfolded the page. My hands were steady, which surprised me. Somewhere below, eighty-odd people were buying these next few minutes with their bodies, and it seemed the least I could do to hold my hands still for them.

I put my palm against the dark square of the oath-stone.

And the stone drank my warmth and gave nothing back, and the carved letters waited, and Wren stood beside me glowing like a banked fire, and far below, in the cupped hand of the Hollow Crown, the Concord began to sing its marching song.

I opened my mouth to swear.

That's when the ember screamed — not in Wren's voice, and not in the dragon's, but in both at once, a sound like a struck bell cracking — and the light died in Wren's hands completely, and the child swayed, and caught my arm, and said, in a whisper gone thin as paper:

"He's here. Vane. He's already in the pass."
