## 28. The Battle of the Pass

I had killed my first man at nineteen, in the mud outside Callow Ford, and I remembered it the way you remember a bad tooth: precisely, uselessly, and against your will. I had thought, in the years since, that I had made peace with killing. That was one of the lies I told myself along with *the Concord serves* and *my armor still fits.*

Standing at the mouth of the Hollow Crown pass with two hundred smugglers, forty villagers, and a cheese-maker armed with a hay hook, I finally understood that I had never once fought for something. I had only ever fought for an order that came down from someone else's ambition. The difference between those two things turned out to be the entire width of my life.

"Knight," Sera Voss said beside me, cracking her knuckles like a woman settling in for cards rather than slaughter, "you're doing the face again."

"What face?"

"The one where you look like you're composing your own eulogy and finding it tedious."

"It's a good eulogy," I said. "Short. Accurate about my failings."

She snorted. Below us, filling the valley floor from treeline to riverbank like spilled ink, the Concord host was making camp. Ten thousand men, give or take the ones who'd already deserted, which Vane's officers counted as casualties of morale rather than honesty. Banners of white and gold. Cook fires in neat rows. Everything orderly, everything obedient, everything wrong.

Ten thousand. We had two hundred and change, a narrow pass, and the stubbornness of people who had been told all their lives what they were not allowed to be.

"You could still walk away," Sera said, not looking at me. "Nobody would think less. Well. I would, but I'm petty."

"I spent eleven years walking toward things I didn't believe in," I said. "I'd like to try the other direction for once."

"Mm." She drew her knife and began checking its edge by feel, the way she did everything — as if sight were a courtesy she extended only when convenient. "Then here's how we ruin their morning."

* * *

The plan was Sera's, and it was beautiful in the way smuggler plans are beautiful: it treated the Concord's virtues as its wounds. Order, discipline, obedience to the chain of command — these meant ten thousand men would move exactly as their officers expected them to move, into exactly the ground we wanted.

The pass mouth narrowed to forty paces wide between two shoulders of rock the locals called the Old Man's Knees. Beyond it the road climbed through switchbacks to the Hollow Crown itself, where Bram and Wren were even now climbing toward a door I half believed in and wholly intended to defend. Nothing got through the Knees. Not today.

The first wave came at dawn with drums, because Vane loved drums; he thought they sounded like destiny. They sounded, to me, like the funeral marches I'd stood through for men whose names I'd forgotten before the dirt settled.

We let them come up the first switchback. We let them crowd into the narrowing throat, shields locked, confident, singing their marching song about the light of Highmoor that never fails. I knew that song. I had sung it. My voice had been one thread in that fabric, and there is no describing what it does to you to hear it coming up a hillside to kill your friends.

Then Sera's people dropped the scree.

Not a rockslide — nothing so honest. She'd had her crews spend three days loosening the slope above the second bend, and when the signal came, two hundred tons of mountain simply decided to lie down on top of the Concord's front ranks. No screaming. There wasn't time. Just a sound like the world clearing its throat, and then a hundred yards of road became a hundred yards of grave.

I have heard men say war is chaos. It isn't. Chaos is fair. War is arithmetic performed by frightened people, and the answer is always the same number wearing different faces.

"They'll regroup," I said.

"They'll regroup," Sera agreed cheerfully, "and send the next lot up angrier, and we'll do it again, and eventually they'll stop being soldiers and start being a mob, and mobs are my favorite thing in the world because I've been robbing them since I was nine."

* * *

They sent the second wave at midmorning, without drums this time, which told me Vane's commanders had learned something, or lost the nerve for music. This one came up the goat tracks flanking the main road, the way I would have done it, the way I had taught recruits to do it a hundred drills ago. I recognized the maneuver the way you recognize handwriting on an envelope that brings bad news.

That was the worst part, that morning. Not the dying. The *recognizing.* Every trick they tried, I had helped invent, refine, or drill into some sergeant who now shouted it up a mountainside at me. Every counter we used, they had trained me in first. We were fighting with each other's hands.

"Hold the left," I told the villagers at the second switchback, and forty farmers with hunting bows and one hay hook looked at me with the particular trust of people who had decided I knew what I was doing, and I hated every ounce of it. "Loose when I whistle. Not before. Arrows are promises — don't make ones you can't keep."

An old woman with a crossbow — Maren, who fed Wren bread and wept over it, who had lost a grandson to a Concord tithe-collector's enthusiasm — squinted up at me. "You've done this before, knight."

"Too much," I said.

"Good," she said. "Do it again."

They came around the flank at noon, and we broke them on the switchbacks, and it was ugly and close and nothing like the clean arithmetic of the morning. A boy of perhaps sixteen got past our line and I put him down with the flat of my blade and a boot in his chest, and he looked up at me with such pure bewildered hatred that I nearly apologized. I didn't. Apology would have been worse than the blow. Instead I took his sword and pointed him downhill and he went, running, alive, carrying the news that the pass was held.

Alive. I found myself counting the ones I'd left alive like coins in a purse, and understanding, with a clarity that felt almost like fever, that this counting was the whole of whatever remained of my soul.

* * *

By late afternoon the arithmetic had stopped favoring either side, which meant it had started favoring us, because they needed to win by dark and we only needed to exist. And then the drums started again, deep and slow, and the host parted on the valley floor like water around a prow, and I saw the banner I had served under for eleven years come up the road.

Commander Halvard Rennick. My teacher. The man who had pulled me out of a burning farmhouse in the Border Reaches and told me, coughing, that I had the makings of a real soldier. The man whose signature was on my commission. He rode at the head of his household guard, three hundred veterans in blackened plate, and he reined in below the Knees and looked up the pass with field glasses, and I swear on whatever's left of my honor that I felt him find me. Distance doesn't matter. Some looks carry.

"He's coming himself," Sera said.

"Yes."

"Is that bad?"

I watched Rennick hand the glasses to his standard-bearer and dismount. Watch him buckle his helm under his arm — a courtesy, an offer, the old way, champion against champion to spare blood. He hadn't changed. That was the unbearable thing. The Concord had eaten whole provinces and I had let it happen in slow increments I could explain away one by one, and Rennick had stayed exactly the honorable man he always was, serving it faithfully, because he had never once asked what he was serving.

"It's bad," I said, "in the way that matters least right now."

I went down alone. Of course I went down alone. There are scenes you are given in life, and you can refuse them, but you will pay interest forever.

* * *

The ground between the lines went quiet the way snowfall is quiet. Two armies watching. Somewhere above us, hidden in the high rocks, Bram and Wren were climbing toward the door, and every minute I bought here was a minute they breathed, and I held that thought like a coal in both hands.

Rennick waited until I was twenty paces off, then set his helm on a stone and stood waiting, bareheaded, gray at the temples now, the same scar splitting his left eyebrow that had been there the day he fished me out of the fire.

"Dane." His voice carried the way it always had, pitched to fill a parade ground. "You look terrible."

"Hello, Commander. You brought ten thousand men to a pass held by shepherds."

"I brought order to a province eating itself with heresy and rumor." He said it without heat. That was the horror of him — no cruelty in it, none at all, just the clean machinery of belief. "Come home, Serah. The Prior has offered full amnesty. Your record restored. Whatever madness brought you here, it can be — "

"Did you burn Marrowdale?"

Something moved behind his eyes, small as a fish under ice. "The village refused lawful quartering."

"Did you burn it?"

"I followed orders," he said, and then, because he was honest underneath everything, the way a well-built house is sound underneath rotting wallpaper: "Yes."

"And you're standing there telling me the fire was order."

"I'm telling you the alternative is *this*—" he gestured at the pass, at the dead of the morning scattered down the switchbacks, ours and theirs together, indistinguishable now in their armor of dust and blood, "—ten thousand against two hundred, Dane. You cannot win. You know the arithmetic better than anyone I ever taught."

"That's true," I said. "You taught me the arithmetic. You just never taught me what the numbers were."

He sighed, and I saw the moment he gave up on saving me, and I loved him for grieving it, and I despised myself for loving anything about this. "Then I'm sorry, Serah. Yield or die. Those are the terms I have."

"You always did hate improvising."

I drew.

* * *

I will tell you what actually happened in that fight, because songs will get it wrong — indeed, Ilex has already gotten it wrong twice in draft, and I have crossed out both versions.

It lasted ninety seconds. It should have lasted ten. Rennick was the finest swordsman I ever stood across from, and he was fifty-three years old and tired, and I knew every habit in his body because he'd built mine from the same timber. When he cut high-right I was already moving, because he'd drilled that cut into me four thousand times. When I feinted low he didn't bite, because he'd seen that feint from me a thousand times. We were two people reading a book we'd each written half of.

And the whole time, the drums kept up behind his lines, and the wounded cried on the rocks above, and I understood that whichever of us fell, the other would carry it, and that this — not the steel, not the orders — was the actual war. Everything else was logistics.

He beat me back twice. Once my foot found the edge of a dead man's shield and I went down hard and saw his blade rise, and I remember thinking, with absurd calm: *so this is the shape of it after all.* And then I saw his wrist turn — a fraction, a mercy, the flat coming around instead of the edge — because he was Rennick, and even now, ordered to kill a traitor, some buried warden in him flinched from murder.

I rolled, came up inside his reach, and did the thing no Concord duelist does, the thing they train out of you in your first year because it isn't *soldierly*: I dropped my sword, caught his blade arm in both hands, and broke his elbow over my knee.

He screamed. I have heard that scream in my sleep since. I put him on the ground and pinned him with a knee and took his own dagger and held it at his throat, and the whole pass went silent, ten thousand men and two hundred thieves all breathing at once.

"Yield," I said.

"Kill me," he said. "It's cleaner. You'll be executed within the year regardless, Dane. The Prior doesn't forgive. He *uses.*"

"I know," I said. "I know exactly what he uses. That's why you're going to live, you stubborn old bastard. Someone has to survive this to tell the truth of it, and it clearly can't be me — I'm far too busy."

I took his sword and broke it on a stone. Then I hauled him up, propped him against the rock where his own lines could see him — disarmed, defeated, alive — and I turned to the host and did the last thing I ever expected to do with my voice. I shouted.

"Concord! Look at your commander! Alive! Every man who walks away from this pass today walks away *alive*, because that's the terms here — not the Prior's terms, *ours*. Go home. Tell them the pass held. Tell them a disgraced knight beat the best swordsman you had and couldn't be bothered to kill him, because we're not here for your blood. We're here for the door, and the door isn't yours!"

Silence. Long enough that I heard wind in the scree, and someone in the Concord ranks begin, very quietly, to sob.

Then a sergeant — a woman I'd trained at Callow Ford, Tamsin, who'd held the line beside me through two winters of border raids — threw down her spear. Just threw it down, deliberately, in front of everyone, and sat on a rock.

Others followed. Not many. A dozen. Thirty. But a crack in a dam doesn't need to be large. It needs only to be *true.*

* * *

What saved us was not my speech — speeches are for people with armies — nor even Rennick broken and living on the rock like a warning carved in flesh. What saved us was dusk, and the shape of the pass, and ten thousand men discovering simultaneously that nobody wanted to be the first column up the Knees after dark.

Vane's marshals formed them for a night assault. Twice. Both times the formation dissolved into arguments, into officers shouting, into the particular mutiny of soldiers who have watched their comrades die all day on a hillside for a door most of them couldn't name the purpose of. By full dark the host had drawn back beyond bowshot and was burning its dead, and the smoke of it rose straight up in the windless air like the valley itself praying.

Sera found me sitting on the Old Man's Knee, hands shaking now that shaking was permitted, laughing at nothing.

"Well," she said, sitting down beside me and passing a flask. "You broke your commander's arm and converted thirty of his men with a speech. On behalf of the criminal classes, welcome home. We've been trying to recruit you for months."

"He taught me everything," I said.

"Then you gave him a fine education in return." She drank, and passed it again. "How's the arm? Yours, I mean. Not his."

"Broken somewhere in there too, probably."

"Good. Means you fought honestly." She nodded uphill, toward the dark heights where the switchbacks climbed away toward the Hollow Crown, toward a door of green light that Bram swore was real and I was only now beginning to believe in. "Your scribe and the little one are at the third shrine. Ilex sent a runner. They're close, Dane. Door-close."

I looked up at the mountain. Above the smoke, above the army, above everything, the peaks of the Thornfells caught the last light, and for one breath I could have sworn the stone itself was glowing — faintly, warmly, the way a hearth glows through a shuttered window in a house where someone is waiting up for you.

"How long?" I asked.

"Until Vane gets his mob moving again? Dawn. Maybe less." Sera stood and offered me her hand, and I took it, and my arm screamed, and I didn't care. "So rest your sword arm, knight. Whatever happens up there tonight — we hold this pass till sunrise. After that, it's out of our hands."

Above us, deep in the dark of the Hollow Crown, a light kindled that was not any fire I knew — green-gold, patient, ancient — and the whole valley saw it, ten thousand men and two hundred thieves, and every drum on the Concord side went silent at once.

Dawn was coming. From both directions.
