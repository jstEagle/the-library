## 30. Dragon-Fire, Given

I want to tell you that I understood what was happening to me.

I didn't. Nobody understands the inside of a miracle. You understand it the way a riverbed understands water — by being shaped by it while it goes through you and is gone.

But let me start where it actually started, which was with my hands shaking so badly I couldn't tie the last knot of the warden's cord, and Wren reaching up — small fingers, ember-warm even now, even at the end — and tying it for me.

"There," Wren said. "Now you look like somebody's grandmother."

"Your grandmother," I said, "was a liar with excellent taste in songs."

"My grandmother was a goat."

"You don't *have* a grandmother. You were found in a turnip cart."

"And yet somehow I know she was a goat." Wren's mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling, one corner lifting like a door deciding whether to open. Behind us, down the switchbacks of the Hollow Crown, Sera's people were dying on their feet to buy us minutes. Dane's voice carried up the pass in scraps between the wind — battle-cadence, Concord drill-tongue, telling grown men twice her size where they could put their pikes. Every scrap of it cost something. We were spending other people's blood to climb forty steps of ancient stone, and there was no honest way to make that feel noble. It felt like theft. It felt like being the reason.

So I climbed the steps.

* * *

The door of Thornfell was not what two centuries of erased maps would have you imagine. No gold. No titanic relief of dragons rampant. It was a slab of grey mountain granite, maybe nine feet tall, set into the cliff face above the pass like a headstone someone had leaned against the world and forgotten to bury under it. The carvings on it were worn nearly smooth — Ilex had spent thirty years reconstructing them from rubbings and fragments, and here they all were in one piece, shallow as old scars: a tree with roots like hands. A door with no handle. A line of small figures, and every figure holding something out, palms up, giving.

Giving. Not taking. Two hundred years of Concord scholars had read every surviving syllable of Thornfell looking for the trick, the lock mechanism, the lever that would yield up the hoard. They never once considered that the whole rite might be exactly what it looked like. That the kingdom hadn't hidden its treasure. It had *buried* it, the way you bury a beloved thing, so that no one could make it serve them — and left instructions for the only kind of hand that could ever take it back out again: an empty one.

The stone was warm. I pressed my palm flat against it and it was warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, warm the way bread is warm when you pull it from the oven too soon, and underneath the warmth something moved. Slow. Enormous. Patient past all reckoning.

A heartbeat. Two hundred years between beats, and I'd arrived just in time for one.

"It knows you," Wren said quietly. "It's been waiting so long it forgot how to wait, and then it heard your grandmother's song coming up the mountain, off-key, and it remembered."

"That song is a smuggler's counting rhyme about contraband brandy."

"It's a nursery rhyme about the door," Wren said. "You've been singing the oath your whole life, Bram. You just thought it was about brandy."

Down in the pass, something exploded. Orange light, then screaming, then Sera's voice cutting through it all like a whip-crack — *"Fall back to the narrows! Make them come single file, the arrogant bastards!"* Minutes. She was buying minutes. Ilex stood behind me with his satchel clutched to his chest like a shield, and his face was wet, and he wasn't wiping it.

"The words," he said. "Bram. The words, before my nerve goes, because my nerve is going."

I looked at Wren.

Here is what I had decided on the climb, and I will not pretend it was a clean decision or a brave one, because it was neither. The rite demanded a willing heart given freely. Ilex believed — had spent the whole night believing, hunched over his translations by ember-light — that it demanded a life. A warden's life, poured out with the fire, the door drinking its keeper dry as the price of opening. And Wren had already worked this out too, because children always work out the terrible arithmetic first, and Wren had stepped forward on the climb, calm as Sunday, and said, "I'm the vessel. It should be me. It came in me; it can go out me."

And I had said no. Not heroically. Selfishly, probably. With the full ugly knowledge that I was choosing my own heart over the child's, that some cold clerk in the temple of my soul was tallying it up — *one life against one life, scribe, which do you owe more?*

Wren looked at me now, and the ember-light behind those eyes was very old and very tired, and Wren said: "Ask it."

"Ask it what?"

"What it wants. That's the whole rite, you idiot. Palms up. *Asking.* Everyone for two hundred years tried to take, and the answer was always no, and nobody ever once asked." Wren took my scarred hand — the ink-stains, the rope-burns from the temple ledgers, the new burn where the cord had seared me — and turned it over, palm up, and held it against the warm stone. "Ask."

So I asked.

I don't have words for how you ask a mountain a question. You open your hand and you mean it, that's all. You hold out everything you are — the debts, the smuggler roads, the grandmother's songs, the shame of the cage, the child whose hand you're holding — and you say, without sound, in the language of an open palm: *what do you want?*

The mountain answered.

* * *

Later, trying to write it down for Ilex — because of course I wrote it down, I'm a scribe, it's the only prayer I know — I filled eleven pages and burned ten of them. Here is the eleventh, abridged:

It showed me green country.

Not Thornfell-as-it-was. Not ruins, not glory days, not the hoard the Concord dreamed of — no vault of concentrated fire, no weapon, no treasury. Just country. Valleys running with clear water where blighted streams had been. Orchards heavy on hillsides that in my lifetime had been scab and stone. Fields of barley outside villages whose names I knew only as smudges in erased records. And everywhere, everywhere, the fire going out of itself and into the ground, into the roots, into the water table, into the marrow of the mountains — not spent, not hoarded, but *planted*. Sown. A dragon's entire hoard of two hundred years of gathered power, and the use it wanted to put it to was making things grow.

It had been keeping all of that. Holding it through the erasure, through the burning of the records, through the Concord's founding and the indenture ledgers and every copper tallied against every desperate name — holding it the way you hold your breath — waiting for one person to ask it what it wanted instead of what it was worth.

And then, beneath the green, the second image, and this is the one I burned pages trying to avoid:

It wanted to be given. All of it. To the last spark. Not kept, not shared, not rationed out to worthy causes. *Gone.* Because power that stays becomes a throne, and a throne becomes a Concord, and a Concord becomes ledgers and cages and children taken from turnip carts. The last dragon had watched what happened to the world when fire was owned. Its final act of will — older and stronger than any ritual Vane could butcher — was to make sure it could never happen again. It would pour itself out until there was nothing left to seize, and then it would rest, and the door would close forever, and the age of dragons' hoards would end not with a conquest but with a gift.

The price was written into the pouring. What comes through the door passes through a keeper. I understood that with a clarity that had nothing to do with language: the vessel of the giving would carry the whole of it, and no mortal frame carries the whole of a dragon and remains unmarked.

Remains. Full stop. That part was less clear.

"I'll do it," I said aloud, and my voice cracked in the middle like a boy's. "Me. Not Wren. Ask it — it'll take a warden. It said heir of the wardens. I'm the heir. Wren's just — Wren's a child who got lost in the wrong weather."

"Bram—"

"It's done. It's decided. Don't argue with a man who's about to be a conduit for divine arson."

Wren didn't argue. Wren did something worse. Wren reached up and put both small hot hands on either side of my face, and looked at me with eyes full of two hundred years of patience, and said, "Nobody's going to die today. I asked it weeks ago. You weren't listening."

* * *

What happens next happened fast and slow at once, the way avalanches do.

The door didn't swing open. Doors that swing open lead into rooms, and there is no room big enough for what was behind that granite. Instead the stone *remembered itself*. The worn carvings deepened. The tree grew roots of light down into the mountain. The little line of figures with their open palms began, very faintly, very terribly, to sing — and it was my grandmother's song, note for note, the counting rhyme about contraband brandy, except every verse was longer than I'd ever learned, and the true words rose up under the corrupted ones like bedrock under topsoil.

*One for the asking, two for the free,*
*three for the hand that holds nothing, see —*
*copper and crown and covenant chain,*
*the door knows the difference, again, again.*

The heat came next. Not burning — I need you to understand that, because everyone asks, and because Vane's historians later wrote that the heretic Halloran was consumed, and they were wrong in the specific way liars are always wrong, technically accurate and wholly false. It didn't burn. It *passed*. Everything the dragon had hoarded across two centuries of patient dark came up through the mountain, through the door, through the singing stone, and through me — and I was not destroyed any more than a riverbed is destroyed by the river. But I was shaped. Oh, I was shaped.

I felt valleys I had never seen fill with water. I felt scab-land split and green. I felt barley push up through rock in a single impossible season, felt orchards blossom out of order, felt the blight lift from the low country like a fever breaking — and each valley it healed, something went out of me to pay the passage. Not blood. Not years, quite. More like... names. I could feel names leaving. For a long time after, I couldn't remember the name of the street I'd been born on. I still can't remember the temple clerk who first tallied my debt — his face is gone, and good riddance, but it frightens me sometimes, how much of a man can be toll.

Wren stayed with me the whole time. Hands on mine, on the stone, forehead pressed to the granite, humming the song — the true version, the whole verses, learned in a week what my family had spent generations corrupting. The ember in Wren was guttering as the great fire poured past it, the last coal giving itself into the general conflagration, and I could feel that too, a small grief inside the enormous one: the dragon's heartbeat slowing toward its final rest, content, *relieved*, like a sentry finally allowed to sit down after two hundred years at post.

The dawn came up over the eastern peaks while the fire was still pouring, and the light hit the released power mid-air, and the whole sky over the Thornfells turned the color of the inside of a pearl. Down in the pass, the fighting stopped. Both sides. Men who had been trying to kill each other thirty seconds earlier stood in the orange-and-pearl light with their weapons hanging, watching blighted land heal faster than the eye could follow, watching rivers run clean, watching a dead kingdom's curse of ash lift off the valleys like a sheet pulled off furniture in an empty house.

And then the fire was done. There was no tapering, no fade. It simply finished, the way a breath finishes, and the silence afterward was the largest thing I have ever stood inside.

The door was stone again. Cool now, for the first time in two centuries. Warm only where my hand still rested, cooling by degrees, like a cheek after weeping.

On the ground at my feet lay a small drift of fine grey ash, and in the middle of it, unburnt, a single scale the size of my thumbnail, iridescent, dulling even as I watched — the last physical evidence that a dragon had ever kept faith with the world. Wren picked it up and closed it in both fists and sat down right there on the threshold of a vanished country and cried the way children cry, entirely, without management, and I sat down beside Wren and did the same, and neither of us said anything, because there was nothing left to say that the mountain hadn't heard.

* * *

That was when Prior Aldous Vane came up the last forty steps.

He came alone — his vanguard had broken at the narrows when the sky changed color, or perhaps simply knelt; accounts differ and I was crying at the time. He climbed slowly, robes scorched at the hem, face streaked with soot, and he was not running. That's the detail I keep. Even ruined, the man climbed like a procession. He reached the threshold and stopped, and looked at the door — cool, shut, ordinary granite — and at the drift of ash, and at me sitting in it with a sobbing child in my arms.

"Where is it," he said.

"Gone," I said. "All of it. It's in the valleys now. In the water. In the barley. It's in everything and it belongs to no one, which means, Prior, by the arithmetic you've spent your whole life learning — it belongs to *everyone*, and you may consider that your bill."

Something crossed his face that I mistook, in that moment, for grief. It took me much longer — another chapter of my life, honestly — to understand it was worse than grief. It was *emptiness*. The look of a man standing in a vault that has just been revealed to contain nothing, discovering that everything he was had been built around the contents.

He reached past me. Past Wren. Past the scale-shaped absence where a hoard should have been. His hand came down on the cool stone of the door, fingers spread, and he pushed, the way you push a door that has always opened for you, because you were told since childhood that doors open for men like you.

The stone did not open. The stone did not so much as warm.

"No," Vane said, conversationally, to the mountain. "No, this is incorrect. The ritual requires—" and then he stopped, because even he could hear it, the flaw in the sentence. The ritual required a willing heart, palms up, asking. He had brought an army, a doctrine, and a hunger two centuries deep, and the sum total of what the door would give a man like that was exactly what it gave him: nothing.

"Get away from it," he whispered, and now the emptiness had edges on it, sharp ones. "Whatever you did, undo it. The fire can't be gone. Fire isn't *gone*, it transfers, it obeys conservation, it—" He rounded on me, and his composure, that beautiful institutional composure, came apart like wet ledger paper. "You were a *scribe*. Do you know what I built? Do you know what I gave up? There was a woman, once, and a life, and I set them aside because I was promised — I was *promised*—"

Behind him, up the pass, came the sound of boots. Many boots, disordered, and Sera's voice riding ahead of them like a banner: *"Don't let him near the door!"* — and Dane's answer, hoarse but upright: *"Too late. Doesn't matter. Look at it. LOOK at it, Aldous. It's a wall. It was always going to be a wall for you."*

Vane looked at the wall. Then at the ash on my knees. And I watched the last thing die in him — not dramatically, not with thunder. The way a lamp dies when the oil's out. He straightened his scorched robes with two precise tugs, a man reasserting procedure over apocalypse, and said, in the level voice of the pulpit:

"This changes nothing. The Concord endures. The ledgers endure. You have made yourself a heretic before witnesses, Halloran, and heresy has a process." He turned to go, then paused, and without looking back added, almost gently, in the voice he must have used on novices: "Ash is still ash. Whatever it touched. Remember that, when you look in the mirror."

Then he walked down into the pass, past his kneeling host, and the boots behind Sera surged forward, and I understood — sitting in dragon-ash with a scale in a child's fist and a mountain cooling at my back — that the story was not over. Miracles end wars in songs. In life, they just change what the war is about.

Sera reached the threshold first, took one look at the healed valleys below, one look at me, and said, "Right. Someone had better start talking, because in about an hour every survivor in that army is going to have a different story, and I intend for ours to be the one with the wine in it."

Dane arrived leaning on Ilex, bloody, grinning, half-dead and entirely herself. "Halloran," she said. "You look terrible."

"I've been a riverbed," I said.

She nodded as though this explained everything, and perhaps it did.

Below us, in the pearl-light fading off the green new country, Prior Aldous Vane stood alone among his host, and raised his voice, and began — I could see it, even at that distance, the familiar architecture of it — to preach.
