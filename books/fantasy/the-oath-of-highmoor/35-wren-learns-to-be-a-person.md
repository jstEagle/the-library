## 35. Wren Learns to Be a Person

The letters have too many corners.

That is the first thing I will tell you about learning to be a person. Letters are sharp. They sit on the page like little stone walls, and Brother Ilex draws them one at a time in the dirt with a stick, and I am supposed to make my hand do what his hand does, and my hand has never done anything except grab food and bite Bram and hold fire.

"This is an A," Ilex says. "It is the oldest letter we have. In Thornfell script it looked like this—" and he scratches something that looks like a bird's footprint, "—but the meaning survives."

"A," I say.

"Excellent. Now B."

B looks like two bellies pushed together. I tell him this and he laughs so hard he has to put the stick down. Ilex laughs easily now, which Sera says is unnatural for an archivist, that they are supposed to be dry as old paper, but Ilex says he spent thirty years being dry and it got him excommunicated, so he is trying wetness instead.

I copy A until my A looks less like a mountain that fell over. Then B. Then C, which is a moon with a bite out of it, which I like, because I know about moons with bites taken from them; the ember used to show me that sometimes, before—

Before.

The ember is gone. I write that sentence now the way you touch a bruise to check if it still hurts. It does. But differently. When the ember lived in me, everything was loud — colors had tastes, stones had moods, the whole world hummed against my skin like bees under wool. Now the world is just the world. Quiet. Ordinary. Dirt is only dirt.

Except when it isn't. Except when I press my palm flat against the ground of the pass and feel something faint and warm underneath, like a hearth in a house where everyone has gone to bed. The dragon-fire went into the land, Ilex says. Into roots and rivers and the deep bones of the mountains. So the warmth isn't nothing. It's just not *mine* anymore.

I am learning what mine means. It is harder than letters.

* * *

Sera teaches me cards because she says every person should know how to lose honestly.

"You're going to be very good at it," she says, dealing.

She is right. I lose constantly. I lose at Rooks and Crowns, I lose at Knives, I lose at a game she invented called Voss's Ruin which I am fairly sure exists only so she can win it. She takes my buttons, my pretty stones, my one good feather, and twice she has taken my dessert, and when I accuse her of cheating she puts her hand over her heart and says, "Wren. I would never," with her eyes doing the thing where they are laughing at you sideways.

"She cheats," Dane tells me later, sharpening her sword by the fire. She does this even though there is no one left to fight, which Sera teases her about, and which Dane ignores with the dignity of a woman who once held a pass against five hundred men and therefore does not have to justify her hobbies.

"I knew it," I say.

"Of course she cheats. But here is the lesson." Dane points the whetstone at me. "You keep playing anyway. That's the whole trick of it. You play with people who cheat and people who don't, and you learn to tell the difference, and you keep your temper either way. That's most of being alive."

This is the kind of thing Dane says. Everything with her comes back around to being alive, as if she is still surprised by it, as if every ordinary evening by the fire is a small victory she has to note down somewhere. Sometimes she catches herself saying these things and goes quiet and rubs the scar on her wrist, the one from the Concord cell, and then Sera deals another hand and nobody mentions anything.

The night I finally beat her — really beat her, fair, with a Flush of Bells she never saw coming — Sera stares at my cards for a long moment and then says, very seriously, "Right. That's it. You're banned."

"Banned?"

"Banned from winning. A queen can't be seen losing to a nine-year-old. It undermines the mystique."

"I'm not nine. I think. We don't know how old I am."

"Even worse." She sweeps the deck up. "A person of indeterminate age defeating the terror of the Thornfell roads. The songs would be merciless." And then, quieter, ruffling my hair in the way I pretend to hate: "Good hand, sprout."

Good hand. I turn those words over all night in my bedroll. Good hand. Not good vessel, not good key, not good little ember-bearer, please hold still while we decide the fate of nations with your body. Just: good hand. Something I did. Something that was mine.

* * *

I argue with Ilex about history on Tuesdays, because Tuesdays are lesson days and arguing is also a lesson, he says, though I think he mostly enjoys it.

Today's argument is about whether the last dragon was lonely.

"It was a dragon," Ilex says. "Dragons were vast. It thought thoughts the size of weather fronts. Loneliness may not even have been available to it as a concept."

"It showed me green country," I say. "Every time. Over and over. Fields and sky and a door standing open. If it wasn't lonely, why did it keep showing me the door?"

"Perhaps it was showing you hope."

"Hope for what?"

Ilex opens his mouth. Closes it. He does this sometimes, when I ask a question that turns out to be bigger than it looked, like pulling one thread and finding the whole sleeve attached. He takes off his spectacles, which he does when he is buying time, and polishes them on his robe.

"When I was reconstructing Thornfell," he says at last, "I worked alone for nineteen years. Every archive that fired me, every colleague who crossed the street when they saw me coming, every night copying fragments by candlelight knowing no one would ever read them. And some nights I asked myself why. Why preserve a country that chose to erase itself?" He puts the spectacles back on. "And I think the answer is what your dragon knew. Things given freely don't die. They wait. A history in a locked box is a corpse. A history handed from hand to hand is a seed."

"So it wasn't lonely. It was waiting."

"Or both," Ilex says. "Both is usually the answer. Loneliness and hope share a wall, the way grief and love do. Anyone who tells you otherwise hasn't felt either one recently."

I consider this. I am getting better at considering things instead of just feeling them all the way through immediately, which is another part of being a person, apparently — the ember felt everything at full volume, all the time, and look where that got everyone.

"Write that down," I tell him. "In the true history. 'Loneliness and hope share a wall.' Attribute it to me."

He writes it down. I checked later. He actually wrote it down, with my name spelled properly, W-R-E-N, three letters I learned early because Bram said I should know how to sign for things.

* * *

Bram teaches me the parts nobody else can. He teaches me which mushrooms lie about being safe, and how to read weather off the way birds sit on fences, and how to whistle through cupped hands loud enough to reach the far pasture. He teaches me the smuggler songs, but different now — Ilex has un-corrupted them, untangled the Concord's deliberate wrongness from the old shapes underneath, and when Bram sings them the new way his voice does something complicated in the middle verses, like the song is walking him past a grave.

"That one," I say one evening, pointing. "Sing that one again."

"'The Warden Counts Her Sheep'?"

"If those are sheep," I say, "then I'm a bishop."

Bram laughs — full laugh, head back, the kind he didn't used to do back when the temple owned his nights. "It's a counting rhyme," he says. "Wardens counted the watch-fires along the pass and called them sheep. My grandmother sang it to get me to sleep. I counted dragons and called them sheep my entire childhood and never once wondered why a smuggler needed to count to twenty in an extinct language."

"Do you miss her? Your grandmother?"

He thinks about it honestly, which is one of the best things about Bram; he never gives you the fast answer, the polite one.

"I miss who I thought she was," he says. "A woman who lied to me about lullabies. But the woman who actually was — who kept the counting alive through two hundred years without knowing why, who made sure I'd know the sounds of a dead kingdom in my mouth before anyone could take them away—" He shakes his head. "No. I don't miss her. I'm trying to become someone worthy of her."

"You already are," I say, because it is true and because someone should say it to him, since he will never say it to himself.

He looks at me for a moment with the expression he gets — the soft, wrecked one, the door expression — and then clears his throat and says, "Your whistling needs work," which is Bram for *thank you*.

* * *

We plant the garden on a morning so clear the peaks look cut out of paper.

It is my idea, or half mine. For weeks I had been going to the same spot below the eastern wall of the holding, where the ground stays warm even at night, where snow melts first, and pressing my hands against it and listening. Not hearing, exactly. Remembering, maybe. The way you remember a song you heard as a baby.

"There," I told Sera. "Plant there."

"What are we planting?"

"Everything. Whatever grows."

So on planting morning the whole strange family of us kneels in the dirt: Sera with her sleeves rolled up swearing at a hoe like it owes her money; Dane turning soil with soldierly precision, rows straight enough to march; Ilex consulting a fragment of Thornfell agricultural record and announcing, delighted, that we are planting their barley, *their actual barley*, saved in a sealed jar for two centuries, which he handles like a relic and plants like a farmer; Bram carrying water up from the stream, two buckets at a time, refusing help, in the way of men who spent years paying off debts and are still learning that some work is just work.

And me. I kneel in the middle and make the holes with my fingers, because my fingers know the ground now, a little. Not the way they used to. Enough.

"The record says," Ilex reads out, "that Thornfell farmers sang to the seed. Germination verses. There's a whole genre — planting songs, harvest songs, a famous one about beans that scholars have fought over for generations—"

"Sing the bean song," Sera demands.

"I absolutely will not, it's undignified—"

"Sing. The bean song. Or I'll sing it, and you'll have to live with my version forever."

Ilex sings the bean song. It is short and silly and rhymes in a language that used to be erased, and by the second verse Sera is humming it, and by the third Dane is keeping the rhythm with the flat of her spade, and Bram sets down his buckets and joins in on the low part, the harmony line his grandmother's grandmother must have sung over seeds in this same soil, and I sit in the middle of it with dirt under my nails and no fire in my chest and think: *this*.

This is what the ember showed me. Green country. Open sky. Not a hoard, not a power, not a kingdom restored to its throne. This. People kneeling in dirt singing to seeds in a language that came back from the dead because a child carried it in her teeth and a scribe couldn't stop humming.

The dragon gave everything away and this is where it went. Into barley. Into bad card games. Into a bean song.

I press one seed into its hole and cover it, gently, the way you'd tuck in someone sleeping, and I whisper the old words to it — the ones that mean *grow, little one, the wall is down, the light is yours now* — and I swear the ground breathes back.

Probably it's just the wind.

But probably isn't nothing. Ilex taught me that.

* * *

At supper that night — barley porridge, obviously, which Sera declares "thematically appropriate and objectively tragic" — Bram comes in late from the road with snow on his shoulders and a letter in his hand, and I know before he speaks that something is wrong, because Bram carries good news loosely and bad news like a hot coal.

"From the valley settlements," he says. "Three villages asking to join the holding. That's fine, that's good, we expected that. But there's a fourth letter." He sets it down by his bowl without eating. "From a man calling himself the Voice of the Concord Reborn. He's gathered some of the factions that wouldn't take Dane's amnesty. He's saying the fire wasn't destroyed — that it sleeps in the pass, and that the holding is sitting on it, and that any true believer has a duty to come and take it."

Dane is already reading over his shoulder. "How many men can he actually field?"

"Fewer than he thinks. More than zero."

"He wants the door," I say.

Everyone looks at me.

"The door is shut," I say. "I can feel it. Stone now, warm, asleep. But he doesn't want the door. He wants what people think the door was." I push my bowl away, angry in the slow way I've learned to be angry, the kind you can aim. "Vane grasped at fire that had already chosen, and he burned, and they watched him burn, and they still didn't learn. They think the power is a *thing*. A treasure. Sitting under our barley like coins."

"And it isn't," Sera says.

"It never was." I look around the table — Sera, Dane, Ilex, Bram, my impossible family, gathered out of debts and disgraces and desertions into one warm room — and I understand suddenly, completely, the way you understand things at supper that you couldn't understand at the door of a dead kingdom: the fire was never under the mountain. The fire was this. It always could have been this. That's what it wanted to be given *for*.

"They'll come anyway," Dane says quietly. "Men like that always come. The question is what they find when they get here."

Ilex takes off his spectacles and polishes them, buying time, and then he smiles — the small, dangerous smile I have only ever seen him wear when someone mistakes an archive for an empty room.

"Then let's make sure," he says, "that they find a country that remembers how to defend itself. Wren — Tuesday's lesson may need to move up."

I grin at him around my spoon.

Letters have too many corners. But I am learning all of them.
