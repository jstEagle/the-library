## 36. Letters to the Erased

I have been excommunicated for eleven years, and in all that time no one has asked me to write anything down.

This is not quite a complaint. Excommunication, I have found, is an excellent arrangement for a scholar. The Concord stripped me of my orders, my stipend, and my right to touch their archives, and in exchange I received something infinitely more valuable: permission to be right without consequence. A man with nothing to lose can follow a footnote to its bitter end. A man with a pension cannot.

But there was always a loneliness to it. Eleven years of reconstructing a country that everyone agreed had never existed — eleven years of writing letters to the erased and receiving no reply — and now, at last, the letters were answering back.

It began, as most of my life's important events have begun, with someone else's handwriting. Sera Voss put a sheet of paper in front of me one evening in the pass holding, along with a cup of wine so cheap it could only charitably be called grape-adjacent, and said: "You keep saying you're writing a book. Write it."

"I am writing it," I said. "I have written four pages."

"Four pages."

"Five, if you count the title."

She looked at me over the rim of her cup with the particular patience of a woman who has smuggled contraband past armed checkpoints and finds a slow archivist genuinely baffling. "You've got a whole valley full of people down there whose grandmothers sang songs about Thornfell before anyone knew what Thornfell was. You've got him—" she jerked her chin toward Bram, who was pretending very hard to be absorbed in mending a fishing net by the fire, "—walking around with an entire nursery rhyme cycle in his head that he thought was about goats. And you've written *five pages*?"

"The title," I said with dignity, "is very good."

The title was *A History of Thornfell, Being an Account of the Kingdom That Was Not*. Sera was correct, which she would remind me of frequently enough over the following months that I eventually had the phrase stitched into a cushion. The book would not be written from archives alone, because the archives were ash — Vane had burned what remained of them when he marched, in one final act of institutional spite, as though erasure twice performed might finally stick. No. If Thornfell was to be given back its name, the name would have to come up out of the ground itself. Out of kitchens. Out of lullabies. Out of the mouths of mountain folk who had been singing a dead kingdom's songs for two hundred years without knowing why the tunes made them homesick.

So we went down into the valleys with paper, ink, and a list of questions, and I did the thing scholars are worst at: I listened.

* * *

The first interview was with a woman named Marta Cullen, who kept bees above the village of Lowmere and who greeted our questions with the flat suspicion of someone who assumed we'd come to tax her hives. It took Wren ten minutes to dismantle this suspicion entirely — children are the universal solvent of peasant distrust, and Wren, who had spent the better part of a year being hunted by an army, had developed a talent for looking harmless that I suspect they will carry into old age and weaponize shamelessly.

Marta's grandmother, she told us, used to sing while working the wax, and the song was nonsense — or had always been presented as such. *"Down where the river forgets its name, the nine bells sleep in honey."* Nonsense. Except that the river above Lowmere had three names in living memory, and the ninth bell of Hollow Crown — Ilex, don't gloat — had been cast, according to the wayshrine fragments I recovered two years ago, from bronze alloyed with mountain honey-wax to keep the tone sweet in cold air.

"The nine bells sleep in honey," I repeated, and my hands shook so badly I blotted the page.

Marta looked at me the way one looks at a man who has become emotional over beekeeping. "That's just how gran sang it."

"That," I said, "is a technical specification."

We collected forty-one songs that autumn. I will not catalogue them here — the book does that, at length, with the apparatus a proper historian owes his dead — but I will say what the collecting taught me, because it taught me something I did not expect.

Erasure is not as thorough as its perpetrators believe. The Concord burned the records, razed the towns, salted the maps, and forbade the name on pain of the lash, and they believed they had killed a country. But a country does not live in its records. A country lives in the way a grandmother rocks when she sings, in the shape of a loaf scored with a crown-and-thorn pattern nobody could explain, in a curse (*"Thornfell take your boots"* — which every smuggler I ever met used, and which turns out to invoke an actual legal penalty, because Thornfell law held that theft of another traveler's boots in winter was a capital offense, the mountains being indifferent to jurisprudence), in a hundred small habits that outlived every edict. The Concord killed the body and buried it under a new name, but the hands of the dead kept moving through the hands of the living, kneading bread, humming tunes, scoring patterns into doorposts for luck, and the living never knew whose hands were moving theirs.

Memory, it turns out, is smuggled the same way everything else gets over the passes: in small quantities, concealed in ordinary goods, passed hand to hand for generations, until someone comes along who knows what to look for.

I know what to look for. It is the only thing I have ever known how to do.

* * *

Not every interview was easy. I should say that plainly, because the book will make the work sound like a harvest, and harvests are never only abundance.

In the village of Stonebeck an old man named Alder threw us out. He stood in his doorway, a big ruined fist of a man, and said: "I'm not saying those words out loud. My father wouldn't say them. His father had his tongue cut for less." And then, as we turned to go, quieter: "You really think they're gone? The Concord?"

Sera answered that one. "Gone enough."

He watched us a long moment, and then he said, without opening the door further: "My grandfather used to hum something when he sharpened his scythe. Never words. Just the tune." And he hummed it — four bars, halting, half-remembered — and shut the door before I could thank him.

I transcribed those four bars by moonlight with tears on the page, and if you ask me why, I will tell you it was the ink smudging, and you will know I am lying. Four bars of a scythe-sharpening song, sung by a man afraid of his own mouth, was the whole of my life's argument made audible. They could cut out tongues. They could not stop a tune traveling from a father's throat to a son's. Erasure is a war against memory, and memory fights dirty, and wins slowly, and wins.

Wren was our best instrument, though neither of us planned it. Children in those villages would not talk to me — I have a face like a disapproving owl and a manner to match — but they would talk to Wren, who was still young enough to be one of them and strange enough to be interesting. It was Wren who learned that the game children played with knucklebones in three separate valleys had the same scoring rhyme, and that the rhyme counted in Old Thornic: *cat, gate, star, door.* One, two, three, four. The numbers survived inside a counting-out game, hidden in plain sight for two centuries, passed from child to child exactly as the ember had been passed from keeper to keeper — because children are the vaults a country builds when its libraries burn.

When Wren told me this, sitting on a wall with their legs swinging, they added: "They didn't hide it in grown-up things. They hid it in us. Because grown-ups break."

I wrote that sentence down verbatim, and it is in the book, unattributed, because some sentences belong to everyone.

* * *

And then there was Bram's grandmother.

Bram came to the work reluctantly, which surprised no one who knew him. He had spent his whole life treating those rhymes as smuggler's superstition — road-songs, luck-chants, the sort of thing you mumble over a lockpick — and discovering in the cave at Wintershollow that they were door-warden liturgy, that his family had been Thornfell's last keepers, had rewritten him in ways he was in no hurry to examine. He gave me fragments. Fragments, always. A line here, a verse there, offered sideways while doing something else with his hands.

"They're wrong, anyway," he said once. "Gran changed them. Or her mother did. Half of them don't scan, and the words don't match between verses."

"They don't scan in Common," I said, "because they're not Common songs. Give me everything. All of it. The versions that contradict each other especially."

"Why especially the broken ones?"

I set down my pen and made him look at me, which took some doing; Bram Halloran avoids eye contact the way other men avoid debt-collectors. "Because corruption is evidence," I said. "When a text is transmitted illegally for two hundred years, every change tells you about the people who carried it. Your grandmother didn't ruin those songs, Bram. She *edited* them — she flattened the Thornic sounds into Common so the Concord's listeners would hear nonsense, she swapped the sacred words for goat-words, she built a code that could survive in a smuggler's mouth in a taproom full of informants. She wasn't careless. She was the last warden of a country, hiding it in a lullaby, and she did it perfectly, because here you stand."

He was quiet a long time. Then he said, "She lied about them, though. When I asked, she said they were just songs."

"She lied about everything," I agreed. "Lying was the job. Now do yours. Sing."

So he sang. It took six evenings. He would give me a verse, and I would set it beside the wayshrine inscriptions, beside the knucklebone counts, beside the bell-song of Marta Cullen and the scythe-tune of Alder of Stonebeck, and piece by piece the corrupted rhymes came home. Words he'd sung all his life as *"the grey mare walks the hollow lane"* resolved into Thornic: *grá-maire*, the grey door-warden — the office, the oath, the keeper of the threshold. *"Count your coins and count them twice"* was a warding formula; the door answered only to a numbered oath, seven names spoken in order, and the "coins" were the names themselves, worn smooth by two centuries of transmission into something about money, because money is the one subject no informant bothers to report.

The night we restored the seventh verse complete — the oath-verse, the one he had sworn at the door with Wren's hand in his — Bram stopped mid-line and sat down on the floor of my workroom and stayed there a while.

"It's her," he said finally. "The voice in it. Gran. She's — the meter's a walking rhythm. It's meant to be sung on the road to the door. She was teaching me the route my whole childhood and I thought she just liked walking."

"Yes," I said.

"And the last line—"

"The last line is your name," I said. "*Halloran*, from *hall-ór-an*: keeper-of-the-gold-that-is-not-gold. Hereditary. It's in the genealogies I reconstructed from the parish fragments. Your family held the Hollow Crown wardenship for eleven generations."

He laughed then, or something near it — a cracked, wet sound. "Eleven generations of smugglers."

"Of *wardens*," I corrected, "who were forced to smuggle. There is a difference, and the difference is the entire content of my book."

He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, briskly, the way men do when they have decided crying is a task to be completed rather than felt. "Put her name in it," he said. "Gran's. Maren Halloran. Whatever else you write, put her name in."

"Maren Halloran," I said, "will appear in the first chapter, the last chapter, and several footnotes."

"Heaven help the reader."

* * *

Winter came down off the peaks early that year, and we worked through it — the interviews moved indoors, into kitchens, and the kitchens changed the work again, softened it. People who would not speak across a table spoke across a stew. Old women argued with each other about variant lyrics with a ferocity that would have shamed any convocation of theologians, and they were usually both right, because oral tradition forks like a river and every branch carries true water.

By thaw, I had two hundred and six songs, forty-three interviews, eleven complete texts restored from Bram's cycle, the knucklebone numerals, three bread-stamping patterns decoded (they are sigils of the warden houses; the "lucky knot" every baker in the high valleys uses is the mark of the Threshold Keepers, and I have already sent word asking bakers to please not stop using it now that it's famous), and five pages grown to a manuscript the size of a paving stone.

I wrote the dedication last, because dedications are the hardest prose there is. In the end it was short:

*To the erased, who were not destroyed, only dispersed — and who kept themselves alive in bread and songs and children's games, awaiting readers. You are legible. You were always legible. Forgive us the delay.*

Bram read it over my shoulder and said nothing for a moment, and then: "Vane burned the archives because he thought the past was a threat you could kill."

"The past isn't a threat," I said. "It's a debt. And debts—" I glanced at him, and he snorted, because of course he did, "—always find their bearers."

The manuscript went to the copyists in spring — Sera's people, who proved alarmingly good at clandestine distribution of a book, having spent decades distributing everything else — and I confess I expected my work then to be finished. History recovered, names restored, the erased given back their letters. A scholar's ending. Tidy.

It was Dane who brought the complication, riding up from the lowlands with Concord mud on her boots and a folder of documents she had extracted from the peace negotiations by means I have elected not to document. She dropped it on my worktable and said, "You'll want to see this before your book goes out. The Concord's remnant didn't burn *all* their records after all. Their Order of Inquiry kept copies of everything the Prior's faction destroyed — including the original erasure orders." Her jaw tightened. "Signed two years *before* the war anyone remembers. They didn't erase Thornfell because it threatened them. They erased it because they'd already decided to come back for the hoard someday — and they wanted the deed clean."

I looked down at the folder. Two hundred years of careful forgetting, and beneath it, the receipt.

"Well," I said, reaching for a fresh pen with a hand that was not entirely steady, "it appears the second edition will require a preface."

Dane almost smiled. Almost. "There's something else. One of the signatories' families is still on the Concord's ruling council. Still holding a seat. Still voting." She tapped the oldest page. "Hereditary. Like a certain warden's office I could mention."

Outside, Wren's laughter carried up from the garden — sharp and bright and entirely alive — and I dipped my pen, and began again.
