## 39. The Oath of Highmoor

There are two kinds of oaths in this world, I've decided. The kind that binds you, and the kind that frees you. The Concord never learned the difference. They thought an oath was a chain — swear it and be owned. My grandmother's people knew better. An oath is a door you open on purpose.

I know what you're thinking. Bram Halloran, giving a speech about doors. The scribe who spent nine years copying ledgers about grain tithes and debt-interest has developed opinions. Well. Nine years inside a cage teaches you the shape of the lock, if nothing else. And I've had a year now — a year of mornings that didn't start with tallying someone's misery, a year of ink I chose myself, a year of Wren's laugh echoing down the pass like water over stones. A man can learn things, given time and enough bread.

It was Wren who started it. Of course it was. Everything worth doing in my life since the storm at the postern gate has been Wren's doing, though I'll deny that under questioning.

We were mending the archive roof — third shingle from the left corner, which Sera had declared cursed after it fell on her head twice in one week — when Wren stopped hammering and looked out over the holding with that particular stillness they get. The look of someone listening to something very far away, or very old.

"You're going to make them swear," Wren said.

"Make who swear what?" I asked, because asking questions buys time, and time is the only currency I've ever been rich in.

"The wardens' oath. You keep almost saying it. When Dane drills the watch. When Ilex catalogs another crate. When Sera argues about tolls." Wren turned and looked at me with those ember-colored eyes that no longer glowed but remembered glowing. "You keep almost saying it, and then you stop."

I hammered the next shingle down harder than it needed.

Because Wren was right, damn them. All winter I'd been carrying the oath-words around like stones in my boot. *The road remembers its walkers. The door keeps its keeper. Fire given is fire living.* Ilex had restored them whole from three different sources — a wayshrine rubbing, a villager's song, and my grandmother's rhymes, which apparently were never nursery nonsense at all but liturgy wearing a disguise. The last door-wardens had sworn them. And I had sworn a version of them at the threshold of a dead country while dragon-fire poured through me and out into the world, so you'd think the words would feel used up. Spent. Like a coin passed through too many hands.

But they didn't. They felt like they were waiting for something.

* * *

The trouble with founding something — and I say this as a man whose only previous institutional experience was being owned by one — is that everyone expects it to have rules. The pass holding had grown the way weeds grow: without permission and against all advice. What began as Sera's smugglers camping under the ruined archway had become forty roofs, then sixty, then a market day that drew folk from three valleys. Ilex's archive had swallowed two buildings and was eyeing a third with acquisitive intent. Dane trained a watch that wasn't an army, exactly, because Dane would rather die than command an army again, but was certainly something with drills and shifts and a bell.

And people came. That was the part that kept surprising me. Not just smugglers and exiles, though we had those in abundance — we had farmers fleeing Concord tax-collectors, scholars fleeing Concord book-burners, a former Concord choir-singer who could make grown men weep with a hymn and refused to sing anything the Concord had touched, which limited her repertoire considerably. We had a family of tanners from the lowlands who'd heard the fire had healed their valley's blight and wanted to live where such things happened. We even had, God help us, a Concord defector or two, vetted by Dane with a thoroughness that made hardened smugglers wince.

They came because there was no crown here. No creed. No ledger with their names in it. And every few weeks somebody would ask me, with the particular politeness people use when they're about to make a problem, *so who's in charge?*

The answer, officially, was nobody, and the holding ran on arguments — Sera's council of route-masters, Dane's watch rotations, Ilex's archive rules, and a general assembly that met in the old gatehouse and achieved decisions the way a river achieves a canyon: slowly, and by wearing everything down. It worked. It worked ugly and loud and it worked. But underneath all the arguing ran a question nobody said aloud, because saying it might break whatever spell kept us alive:

What holds a thing together when nothing owns it?

I'd been avoiding the answer for a year. Wren caught me hammering shingles and wouldn't let me avoid it anymore.

* * *

I went to each of them separately, because I'm a coward and also a strategist, and those are frequently the same profession.

Sera I found in her counting room, which she insisted on calling an office, doing accounts in a cipher of her own invention that Ilex called "an abomination" and she called "job security."

"If you're here about the toll schedule," she said without looking up, "the answer is no, the lowland merchants can afford it, and yes, I'll be insufferable about it at assembly."

"I want to swear the wardens' oath," I said. "The real one. Ilex finished restoring it. And I want—" Here my eloquence, so dependable on the page, deserted me. "I want it to not be just mine."

That got her attention. She set down her pen — a rare event, roughly as common as snow in high summer — and looked at me for a long moment.

"You want to swear it to the holding," she said. "To the people."

"To the road," I said. "To whoever walks it. To each other, if they'll have it. Not to a kingdom. Thornfell's dead, Sera, and good riddance to crowns, even dead ones. But the oath itself — the *giving* of it — that was never the kingdom's. That was older. It belonged to the wardens before there was ever a throne, and it survived the throne. It can survive anything else we build and lose."

She leaned back and put her boots on the table, which meant she was thinking hard and hiding it behind insolence.

"You understand what you're proposing," she said. "An oath that binds nobody. That anyone can walk away from. That has no punishment for breaking except shame, and half these people came here specifically because they don't believe in shame anymore."

"That's exactly why," I said. "A chain-oath, you break the first chance freedom offers. This kind — you keep it because you chose it. Every morning. The way belonging works when it isn't inherited."

Sera was quiet for a while. Then: "Dane will cry."

"Dane will not."

"Bram. I have seen that woman fight twenty men and come away bored. I have also seen her weep at a christening. She will absolutely cry."

* * *

Dane did not cry. She stood very straight in the training yard with her arms folded and listened to my whole halting explanation, and when I finished she was silent long enough that I started composing apologies.

Then she said, "I took an oath once. At nineteen. Kneeling in the white hall at Highmoor with Vane himself presiding, feeling like the most important person alive." Her jaw worked. "It took me fifteen years to understand that oath was a leash dressed as a crown. And it took me one village burning to finally take it off." She looked at me. "If yours is what you say it is — if walking away carries no penalty — then yes. Gladly. Because the only oath worth swearing is one you could refuse."

Ilex, naturally, had opinions. Ilex always had opinions; it was how he loved people. He received my request in his archive, surrounded by crates of rescued books, and delivered a lecture on the historical evolution of the warden's rite that lasted until I threatened to leave, at which point he cut to the heart of it with startling gentleness.

"Do you understand what you're recovering?" he asked. "Not the words. The *institution*. The old wardens weren't servants of Thornfell's kings — the records are clear on this. The kings served *them*, in a sense. Wardens were chosen, never born to it, and they could set the office down whenever they wished. It was power held the way you hold water: openly, carefully, knowing it would slip through your fingers regardless." He smiled, that dry smile that had survived excommunication intact. "Every institution since has tried to hold power the way you hold a fistful of sand. Squeeze, and lose it all. Your grandmother's people understood something we've spent two centuries unlearning, Bram. If you manage to rebuild it, even in miniature, you'll have done something no kingdom ever managed."

"No pressure, then," I said.

"Immense pressure," Ilex agreed cheerfully. "I'll draft the liturgy."

* * *

We set the day for midsummer, because Sera insisted that any ceremony worth attending deserved decent weather, and because midsummer was the day the fire had poured through me at the door, and it seemed right to answer that anniversary with something given instead of something lost.

We held it at the old archway at the mouth of the pass — the Hollow Crown itself, though the name had softened in the telling until most folk just called it the Gate. Ilex had argued for the archive hall; Dane had argued for the training yard; Sera had won by pointing out that the Gate belonged to everyone who'd ever walked through it, including everyone yet to come, which was rather the point.

They gathered at dawn. Not just our four — though Wren came barefoot and solemn in a new shirt embroidered with thorn-patterns that Sera's seamstress had copied from Ilex's rubbings, and Dane wore her knight's sword with the Concord insignia filed off and a mountain-thorn sprig wired in its place, and Sera arrived looking like she'd dressed deliberately badly to prove she couldn't be made ceremonial, which fooled no one because she'd clearly spent an hour on her hair. But the whole holding came. Sixty families, maybe more. Farmers and smugglers and scholars and that choir-singer, who sang the old gathering-song as the sun came up, the one my grandmother used to hum, and hearing it sung properly — whole, restored, with all its verses — I had to stand very still and breathe for a while.

Ilex read the rite. He'd rendered it in plain speech alongside the old tongue, because, as he said, an oath you don't understand is just a trap with nice acoustics.

"*I am the keeper of the door, not its owner. The road remembers its walkers, and I remember the road. Fire given is fire living; fire taken is ash. I hold what passes through me loosely, and pass it on whole. I ask no crown, keep no key, owe no king. What I bind myself to, I bind freely, and free I remain.*"

Simple words. Old words. Words that fit in your mouth like well-worn stones.

I swore them alone first — the warden's part, mine by inheritance and by choice, both together now, neither canceling the other. My voice cracked on *freely*, and I heard Wren make a small sound, and Dane did not cry, she absolutely did not, there was just something in her eye that was the wind off the pass, obviously.

Then the others came forward. One by one, and freely, as the rite demanded — Sera with her hand flat on the ancient stone of the arch, swearing to keep the road open and honest; Dane swearing to guard the walkers and lay down her sword the day guarding became ruling; Ilex swearing to keep memory alive and legible, and to burn no book and bow to no censor. And Wren —

Wren came last, small and grave, and pressed one hand against the stone beside mine.

"I was the key," Wren said, in the old tongue first and then in ours, and the whole holding went quiet to hear it. "Now I am the walker. I swear the road my feet, and my growing, and my green country." A pause. Then, with the devastating directness that is entirely Wren: "You are my door. I choose you back."

Sixty people watched a feral child who once bit me on the wrist formally adopt me in front of a two-hundred-year-old archway, and I am not ashamed to say my composure did not survive it.

After us, others came. Not all — the rite required willingness, and some folk hung back, watching, unconvinced or simply unhurried, and that was right too. An oath you're pressured into is just the Concord with better manners. But maybe thirty came forward through that bright morning — Sera's route-masters, Dane's watch, the tanners, the choir-singer, a dozen others — laying hands on the stone and binding themselves to each other and the road, freely, with the door standing open behind them the whole time.

No kingdom. No creed. No ledger. Just people, choosing.

* * *

There was feasting afterward, naturally. Sera had imported a truly irresponsible quantity of lowland wine "for diplomatic purposes," and the choir-singer taught everyone the rest of the gathering-song, and Dane danced twice, which I mention only because it required three witnesses to be believed later.

I found Wren at dusk, sitting up on the archway's shoulder where the kids weren't supposed to climb, watching the lights of the holding come on below like earthbound stars.

"They'll ask you to lead it someday," Wren said. "The holding. Maybe soon. You know that."

"I know," I said. "And I'll refuse, and they'll ask again, and eventually we'll work out whatever this thing actually is. Something that doesn't need a head, I hope. Something that grows sideways."

Wren considered that. "Like ivy."

"Like ivy. Kings are oak trees — impressive, and the lightning finds them first. Ivy just keeps going."

Wren laughed, and the sound went down the pass the way water goes down a streambed, finding all the old channels. Then Wren grew quiet, and I knew the other thing was coming, because I'd felt it building all day like weather.

"There were words," Wren said softly. "Under the arch. When everyone swore. The stone was listening — really listening, the way it did at the door. And at the end, when you said *free I remain*..." Wren looked at me, eyes catching the last light. "Bram, the stone answered. Just once. Just a breath. But it said a word I haven't heard since the door."

My skin prickled. "What word?"

Wren spoke it in the old tongue, and Ilex would later render it three ways and argue with himself about all of them, but the sense of it, the shape of it, was unmistakable:

*Remembered.*

The dead country, two centuries erased, had leaned close to our little rite in our little pass, and spoken one word into it — not resurrection, not command, just acknowledgment. The road remembering its walkers.

Below us the holding glowed and laughed and sang. Behind us the mountains kept their counsel. Somewhere far to the south, I knew, the Concord's remnant factions were still circling each other like wolves deciding whether the pack had dissolved, and Dane would tell me tomorrow that vigilance was peace's rent, due daily.

But tonight the door was warm under my hand, and my people were choosing each other in the firelight, and somewhere in the stone an old voice had said *remembered*, and meant us.

"Come on," I told Wren. "Your family's waiting, and Sera's guarding the good wine with a crossbow."

We climbed down together into the lamplight — warden and walker, scribe and ember, the last of an erased line and the first of something with no name yet — and went home.
