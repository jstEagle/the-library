## 40. Green Country

The valley used to be called the Scouring, which tells you everything you need to know about what the Concord thought of it. Now the children call it the Greening, because children are honest about things and adults are only clever.

I know this because I am one of the children. Officially. Sera says I am a child of unusual opinions, which is her way of saying I argue too much. Ilex says I am a primary source, which is his way of saying the same thing but with more footnotes. Dane says I'm a menace with a slingshot. All three are true. I have learned that people can be several true things at once. It took me most of a year to learn it about myself.

This morning I walked down out of the pass holding with six of them behind me — Pip and Marta and the twins, Olla and Sen, little Corvin who still sucks his thumb when he thinks no one is looking, and Tam, who is nine and already convinced he knows more words than I do. He does not. But I let him think he is close, because a rival keeps you sharp.

"Say it again," Tam demanded, for the fourth time since the switchbacks. "The morning word."

I stopped where the path crested, where you can see the whole valley laid out below like something spilled and spreading — green going on green going on green, all the way to where the lowlands haze into gold. Two years ago there was no green here. Two years ago there was grey ash over grey soil under a grey sky, and the Concord's surveyors had written *sterile — value: nil* in their ledgers, in Bram's handwriting, though he didn't remember writing that particular line and swears he would have written *value: unknown* if he'd been thinking properly. He was indentured then. You don't get to think properly when you're owned. I know that too, now, from the inside.

"*Ael-dorra*," I said. "Morning-word."

"Ael-dorra," they repeated, mangling it in six different directions. Pip turned the r into a growl. Marta swallowed the second syllable entirely. Corvin said something that was not even pretending to be the word, but he said it with such conviction that I nodded at him anyway. That is how teaching works, I have decided. You plant the seed crooked and the tree grows straight anyway, or it grows crooked, and either way it is a tree and it is yours.

"It means the first light coming through a door," I told them. "Or through leaves. Or through anything you've been waiting behind."

"Waiting for what?" asked Olla, who is five and asks the questions that matter.

I looked at the valley. The dragon-fire went through here first, before anywhere else — Ilex has the maps, and the burn-pattern of the healing follows the old riverbeds like water running backward. Where the fire passed, the blight lifted. The ash turned to soil. The dead springs woke up coughing and then laughing. There is grass here now that my grandmother's grandmother never saw, because there was no grandmother's grandmother in a place like this; the Concord kept its granaries full by keeping its valleys empty.

"Waiting for someone to open it," I said.

Olla considered this with the gravity of a judge. "And did they?"

"Yeah," I said. "Eventually."

* * *

Here is what I remember of the ember, and Ilex says my remembering matters more than any of his books, which made him sad and glad at the same time, the way true things often do.

It was warm. Not fire-warm. Hearth-warm. The difference is everything. Fire wants things — wants wood, wants air, wants to eat its way outward and up. The ember wanted nothing. It only offered. For as long as I carried it — my whole life, from wherever it found me, in whatever burned village or broken year I came from, I don't remember, I've decided not to mind — it sat behind my ribs like a second heart that had already done all its wanting and arrived at something gentler.

It showed me pictures. A green country. A door standing open in a hillside with light coming through it the color of *ael-dorra*. Sky with no ceiling on it. I used to think those were memories of somewhere else, someplace the dragon had been happy. It took me years — well, months, but I was small, so it counts as years — to understand they were not memories at all.

They were instructions.

The dragon's last heartbeat spent two hundred years sealed behind a stone door in the Hollow Crown pass, and every minute of those two hundred years it held the same picture in front of itself the way you hold a song in your mouth on a long dark road. Not *I want out.* Not *avenge me.* Not even *free me,* exactly. Just: *this. This is what I am for. Find hands willing to give it.*

Vane couldn't hear it. Vane stood in front of the door with an army and a ritual and two centuries of the Concord's hunger, and he heard nothing, because the ember doesn't speak to taking. It speaks one language and it is the language of giving, and if you come to it with your fists closed you might as well be deaf. I watched him learn that. Everyone did. It wasn't dramatic, what happened to him. Ilex wrote four pages about it and got it wrong in all four. It was simple. He reached for a thing that had already been given away, and his hands closed on nothing, and nothing is heavier than anything, if you carry enough of it. The door shut. His host stood in the pass-mouth looking at their empty Prior and then at each other, and armies break not when they lose but when they realize what they were following, and then they go home.

Some of them went home. Some of them stayed, and learned words, and are now on the winter roster for the pass holding's kitchen rotation, which Dane negotiated personally and with visible enjoyment.

But that's the old story. Bram says I should tell the new story, because the old story is his to tell and he tells it badly, with too much of himself in the middle of it. He's wrong about the middle part. He's exactly the right amount of himself. But he gets embarrassed, so I let him have it.

* * *

We came down into the Greening proper around midmorning, when the light does the thing the old word describes — comes through the birches in long gold bars, laying stripes across the path like someone braiding sun into shadow. The children scattered the way children do, which is to say instantly and in six directions, and I let them, because the whole point of this country is that nothing in it needs guarding anymore.

There are orchards here now. That still stops me, every time. Apples. Actual apples, small and sour and stubborn, growing on trees that were seeds two winters ago. Sera runs them the way she ran her smuggling routes — ruthlessly efficient schedules, bribes paid in cider, and a strict rule that anyone caught hoarding fruit owes the kitchen double. She calls herself the Apple Queen sometimes. Nobody crowned her. She crowned herself, which Sera would tell you is the only legitimate method of coronation, and she'd be right, and she'd also be insufferable about it, both at once, which is how Sera contains multitudes.

I found Tam crouched at the edge of the old spring — the first one that woke, the one we call the Coughing Spring because of the sound it made coming back. The water runs clear over stones that two hundred years of ash had buried. Under the ash, the stones had carvings.

"Is this one real?" Tam asked. He knows some of the marks are real and some are new, cut by villagers who wanted their wells and lintels to speak the old language again even if they only knew it secondhand, through Ilex's primer, through my mouth.

I knelt beside him. The carving was worn nearly smooth, older than the restoration, older than the erasure maybe. A spiral, opening outward. A door-mark.

"Real," I said. "Very old. Older than the bad years."

"What's it say?"

Ilex would give him the etymology. Three roots, the warden-clause, the grammatical argument about whether the final glyph modifies the door or the one who opens it. I love Ilex for knowing that. But Tam is eight, and eight-year-olds deserve the truth before they deserve the footnote.

"It says *welcome*," I said.

He touched it with one finger, careful, the way you touch something asleep. Then he stood up and yelled "*Ael-dorra*" at the spring, at the valley, at the entire concept of morning, and went to catch frogs.

That is the whole religion, if you're wondering. That's what we ended up with after everything. A word shouted at water and a mark on a stone that means welcome. Ilex is writing it all down anyway. He says a faith without footnotes is just an opinion with better posture. But he also cried the first time he heard Corvin say *thank-you-words* wrong, and I know why, and I'll tell you why: because Brother Ilex spent forty years reconstructing a dead country out of fragments, alone, excommunicated, laughed at by every living archivist, and the country came back not as a kingdom or a hoard or a vindication of his life's work but as a small boy mispronouncing it at a frog pond. And that was better. He knew it was better. That's the part that made him cry.

* * *

At noon we ate on the flat rock above the orchards, bread and hard cheese and early apples, and I taught them the counting rhyme.

My grandmother's rhymes came back to us whole, in the end. That was Ilex's doing and Bram's and mine together — I'd say half a line and Ilex would find its bones in a wayshrine rubbing, and Bram would dig up the next half from some corner of his childhood where it had been waiting disguised as nonsense, and between the three of us and a dozen villages whose songs had been carrying pieces for generations, we stitched the nursery canon of Thornfell back together. One, two, the warden's shoe. Three, four, the open door. It scans badly. Ilex insists the original meter was different and the smugglers ruined it, and Bram says the smugglers improved it, and they have this argument at least monthly and will until they die, and I hope they do, because an argument that lasts that long is just love wearing work clothes.

"One, two," I called, and six ragged voices answered, "the warden's shoe."

"Three, four."

"The open door!"

"Five, six—"

"The fire gives!"

Seven, eight, the gate don't hate — no, that one's wrong, Tam always puts *hate* in because it rhymes better in his version and honestly his version is winning, the way living versions do. Nine, ten, begin again. That line used to frighten me, before. Begin again sounded like a threat, like the wheel of it, the debt of it, the Concord's ledgers tallying you down to the copper and starting fresh each morning so you could owe them anew. Bram explained once what the line meant in the original tongue, and it isn't *begin again* at all. It's closer to *the morning forgives the night.* The counting doesn't restart because you failed. It restarts because mornings do that. Because that's what mornings are for.

After lunch, Marta asked me the question. Someone asks it every time, and I answer it every time, and the answering is part of the teaching, though Ilex would say it's part of the liturgy and Bram would say it's part of the penance and I say it's just what you do when a kid looks at you with that particular hungry look.

"Were you scared?" Marta asked. "When you carried it?"

We were walking the long row of young apple trees, checking the trunk-guards against winter voles. My hands remembered the work even while the rest of me listened to her.

"Yes," I said. "Not of it. Of what people would do to get it."

"Was it heavy?"

"No. That was the strange part. The worst things I've ever carried were all light." I handed her a fallen branch for the wood-sledge. "Cages are light. Debts are light. Words like *property* — you can fit a whole person inside that word and it weighs less than an apple."

Marta chewed on this. She is seven and processes ideas the way goats process shrubbery: thoroughly and from all angles. Then she said, "So the fire was easy to carry because it wasn't trying to own you."

I stopped walking.

Out of the mouths of babes, my grandmother would have said, except my grandmother is a set of rhymes now and a grave in the pass holding and a voice that comes out of Bram's humming sometimes when he thinks he's alone. Out of the mouths of seven-year-olds, then. Yes. That is precisely it. The dragon spent two centuries being coveted by everyone with power, and the moment it was given away freely — poured through a willing warden into the dawn, into the rivers, into the roots of the waking valleys — nobody could own it, and so everybody got to live in it. That's the whole secret. That's what the Concord never understood and what Vane died not understanding: the fire was never a hoard. It was always a passing. You keep it exactly as long as it takes to hand it on.

"You're going to be trouble," I told Marta, "when you're older."

"I'm already trouble," she said. "Tam said so."

* * *

We went up to the door in the late afternoon, because we always do, on the days I take the children down into the Greening. It's not far — an hour's climb from the orchards, the path switching back through birch and rowan and the first stands of real forest, trees tall enough now that a person could hide in them, which Sera notes with professional approval every single time we walk it.

The door is stone now. Warm to the touch, always, even in frost — Bram checked it through a whole winter once, obsessively, and pronounced it *geothermally consistent,* and Dane said *it's warm because it likes us,* and honestly Dane is right and Bram knows it. The light-marks on the stone have faded to ordinary carvings. Whatever moved in the mountain when the fire passed through has finished moving. Thornfell is not coming back. Ilex says that plainly now, in his history, in the book he's calling *Letters to the Erased,* except he changed the title last month to *Letters from the Erased,* because, he says, they answered.

Bram carved the oath-words fresh below the door two summers ago, deep, in the old script, where the weather will take decades to soften them:

*I do not keep. I keep watch.*

*What was given, I give.*

*The door is not mine. It is the morning's.*

Children run their fingers along it the way you'd pet an old dog. Some of them memorize it. None of them are afraid of it, which Ilex says proves the restoration worked better than any archive, because fear was the Concord's whole curriculum.

Today I sat on the threshold — it's a good sitting stone, wide and warm — and the children spread around me in the grass, and for a while nobody talked, which is the other lesson I teach, the hardest one, the one the adults are still failing at. You don't have to fill the quiet. The quiet is not a debt. Nothing is tallying.

Corvin fell asleep against my side, thumb in mouth, one hand fisted in my sleeve. Tam pretended not to watch the treeline for hawks and then watched it for hawks. Down the valley the smoke of the pass holding rose thin and domestic, and somewhere in it Sera was probably extorting someone affectionately, and Dane was drilling the watch with a patience that would have astonished her old commander, and Ilex was arguing with a footnote, and Bram —

Bram was coming up the path.

I saw him before the children did, the way I see weather now. He walks differently than he did. The scribe's stoop is mostly gone; the smuggler's soft step remains, because some things you keep. Ember-scars climb his left arm like ivy written in silver, and on cold nights he says he can hear the land settling, the roots drinking, the springs talking in their sleep. Half-hearing, he calls it. I think it's more than half. I think the fire left a little of itself in him the way a song leaves itself in you after it ends, and I have never told him this, because he would make a self-deprecating joke and then go sit alone with it for an hour, and he's earned better than that. I'll tell him someday. When he's old. When he can't escape.

He reached the clearing and stood a moment looking at the door, the way he always does — the grave-visit look, the talk-to-the-stone look — and then at the children strewn across the grass like laundry, and then at me.

"Well?" he said. "Did they learn anything?"

"Tam's version of the counting rhyme is winning."

"That's because Tam's version is better."

"Ilex will fight you."

"Ilex fights me before breakfast. It's practically a meal." He sat down heavily beside me, careful of Corvin, and leaned back on his palms, and looked out at the green country going on and on below us, gold at the edges where the light was lowering, and for a long time he didn't say anything, and I let him, because I'd just been teaching the lesson.

"They're asking me to write the school charter," he said finally. "The valley folk. Formal schooling, letters, numbers, the old language as a subject. They want my name on it."

"Will you put it?"

He was quiet a while. Below us, *ael-dorra* light came through the birches in long slow bars, striping the grass, striping the sleeping children, striping the warm stone of a door that had given away everything it had and was resting now, the way you rest after work that mattered.

"I keep almost signing 'former scribe,'" he said. "Old habit. Ledger-thinking. Who I was to whom, what I owed." He rubbed the silver scars on his arm absently, the way you rub an old ache that's become a companion. "But that's not what a charter needs. A charter needs a name that means *forward.*" He glanced at me, and there was something in his face I'd seen only a few times — the look he gets when the past and the future shake hands. "Warden Halloran," he said, trying it out. "Not Highmoor's warden. Not Thornfell's. The road's. The holding's. Theirs." He looked down the valley, where the smoke rose and the orchards grew and the springs ran over welcome-stones. "Do you think a name can belong to a place that belongs to nobody?"

I thought about it. I thought about the ember, and the picture it held for two hundred years in the dark — green country, open sky — and how it was never a memory and never a dream but an instruction, and how the instruction was finished now, completed by a scribe who owed everything and gave it anyway, and how the only thing left of the last dragon in all the world was this: warmth in a stone, words in children's mouths, and a valley so green it looked like the inside of the vision itself.

"Yeah," I said. "I think names are doors."

He laughed — really laughed, the rare kind — and pulled the charter draft from his coat, and smoothed it on the warm stone, and I watched over his shoulder as he struck through *former scribe of the Highmoor Concord* with one clean line, the way you strike a debt, the way you close a ledger, the way you finish a sentence you've been carrying a long time.

Below us the light came through everything it could find — leaves, clouds, the open door of the sky going amber over the ridgeline — and the children slept in it, unowned, untallied, unbought, and Tam murmured the counting rhyme in his sleep and got it slightly wrong, and it was better that way.

Green country. Open sky. And the door, warm under our hands, unlocked — not for the worthy, not for the rightful, not for whoever holds the biggest army or the oldest claim, but for the willing.

Anyone. Everyone. Whoever comes walking up the path.

*Ael-dorra.* Morning-word. Begin again.
