## 1. The Rat Who Knew My Name

The rats of Ironhollow knew Oscar Eriksen before he knew himself.

They came to him at dusk the way secrets come to priests — unasked for, inevitable, smelling faintly of the things they'd witnessed. He sat on his windowsill in the crooked attic above the tannery on Cobbler's Row, three flights up where the city's smoke thinned and the moon had room to breathe, and he waited. He'd learned patience from them, actually. Rats were patient creatures. They had to be, living as they did in a city that drowned them in boiling oil by the cartload.

"You're late," he murmured.

A brown female with a notched ear emerged from the gutter-spout and regarded him with eyes like wet garnets. *The baker on Millstone Street,* she said — though *said* was wrong; there were no words, only meanings that bloomed in Oscar's mind like ink dropped in water. *The baker on Millstone Street beats his wife on Thursdays. The wife has been saving coins in a flour jar. Six silver. She means to run.*

"Thursdays," Oscar repeated softly. "Of course it's Thursdays."

This was how he ate. Not by commanding the rats — never commanding; command left a taste in the air that certain people could smell — but by *asking*, by listening, by trading crumbs and warmth for gossip no human would sell cheap. The baker's wife wanted to disappear. Oscar could arrange that, for a fee, using pigeons to carry messages to safehouses whose owners never asked questions. It was small magic. It was deniable magic. A man talking to himself on a windowsill was mad, not criminal. And madness wasn't a hanging offense.

Magic was.

He fed the rat a rind of cheese and felt her pleasure buzz against the edges of his mind, simple and clean as a struck bell. Three years he'd been in Ironhollow now, three years since he'd fled the northern provinces where they'd burned his teacher alive in the market square of Halden's Cross. Three years of keeping himself *small*. Small enough to slip through the witch-hunters' nets, which were woven from more than rope — they were woven from suspicion, from neighbors' glances, from the particular silence a man made when he was hiding something.

*Wanting things gets people hanged.* That was the first law of survival, older than any statute. Want a friend? A friend could betray you. Want a lover? A lover could be tortured until they gave up your name. Want anything at all, and wanting became a thread some hunter could follow back to your throat.

So Oscar wanted quietly, or told himself he didn't want at all.

*The harbor rats say a ship came in,* the notch-eared female offered, washing her whiskers. *From the south. They say the cargo smelled strange. Old iron and paper.*

"Everything in this city smells strange," Oscar said. But he filed it away. Harbor gossip was coin too, if you knew the right buyers.

* * *

The night market on Vesper Lane opened when the curfew bells went soft — that liminal hour when the watch changed and the lantern-lighters hadn't yet finished their rounds, when Ironhollow belonged briefly to no one. Stalls bloomed out of doorways like mushrooms after rain: a woman selling bottled fog from the fens, a man with caged finches who didn't know Oscar could feel the birds' despair pressing against his skull like a headache, a spice-merchant whose turmeric stained everything it touched including, allegedly, the truth.

Oscar moved through it all with his collar up and his hands in his pockets, buying nothing he couldn't eat, speaking to no one longer than necessary. This was his ritual. Anonymity, practiced nightly like scales on a fiddle.

He was haggling over a heel of dark bread when the pigeons screamed.

Not aloud — pigeons rarely wasted breath — but in that other language, the one that lived behind his eyes. *Danger-danger-danger,* they cried from the eaves, a chorus of gray alarm. *Iron-woman-walks. Iron-woman-comes.*

He turned, bread forgotten, every nerve pulled tight as a snare-wire.

She was standing at the stall across the lane, examining bottled fog with an expression of scholarly skepticism, and Oscar understood immediately why the birds had lost their minds. There was nothing outwardly alarming about her — a young woman in a patched traveling coat, dark hair escaping its braid, ink stains on her fingers like she'd been wrestling a squid made of midnight. Pretty, even, in the sharp way of people who read too much and slept too little.

But the rats. The rats had gone utterly silent, and silence, from rats, was louder than screaming.

The notch-eared female was on his shoulder before he realized she'd followed him down — she did that sometimes, when she judged him insufficiently careful — and her voice trembled against his mind like a leaf in wind. *Old iron. She smells of old iron. Of deep places. Of danger, Oscar. Danger wearing a kind face.*

"I can see that," he whispered.

"You're talking to your rat," the woman said.

Oscar's heart performed a maneuver that would have killed a lesser man. She was looking at him now, one eyebrow arched, amusement and something sharper underneath — assessment, calculation, the look of someone cataloguing him for future reference.

"He's conversational," Oscar managed.

"Mm." She set down the bottle of fog. "Most people talk *at* their animals. You talk *with* them. I watched your mouth move and the rat answer with its ears. That's not training. That's conversation."

Every instinct Oscar possessed — three years' worth, hard-won, paid for in blood and burned names — screamed at him to walk away. Leave the bread. Leave the market. Find a new attic, a new city, a new name. This stranger had looked at him for thirty seconds and seen the thing he'd buried deepest, and people who saw things got people hanged.

But she had also said *conversation*, and the word landed in him like a coal in dry straw.

"Do I know you?" he asked, which was stupid, because obviously he didn't, because if he'd known her he would have remembered the way her eyes held lamplight like a cup holds wine.

"No," she said. "But I think I might be looking for you. Wren Calloway." She extended an ink-stained hand.

*Don't take it,* shrieked every survival instinct he owned. *Wanting things gets people hanged.*

Oscar took it.

Her grip was warm and firm, and beneath the smells of travel and lamp-oil there was, faintly, unmistakably, exactly what the rat had promised: old iron. The smell of a sword kept sheathed a hundred years. The smell, though he had no way of knowing it yet, of a crown.

"Oscar," he heard himself say. "Eriksen."

"Oscar Eriksen." She repeated it slowly, tasting each syllable, and her expression shifted — amusement draining away into something graver, something almost apologetic. "I need to tell you something, and you're going to think I'm mad."

"Madness isn't a hanging offense," Oscar said. "I've checked."

Something flickered across her face at that — recognition, maybe, or grief — and her hand tightened on his.

"There's a book," Wren Calloway said, "that my family has guarded for a hundred years. And last night, Oscar Eriksen, I found your name in it."

Above them, unnoticed, the pigeons had stopped screaming. In the terrible quiet, every bird on Vesper Lane had turned its head toward the two of them, watching with small round eyes, as if they already knew what was coming — as if the future had already been written, and they, unlike Oscar, had been permitted to read it.
