## 2. A Century of Waiting Ink

Oscar had kissed her once before the world turned inside out, and he would spend the rest of his life measuring time against that moment — the small hours of the night market, the taste of spiced wine on Wren Calloway's mouth, the rats in the eaves going suddenly, reverently silent.

Now he stood in her family's house above the tannery row, dripping rain onto a floor older than the city charter, and tried to understand what she was showing him.

"It's been in my family for six generations," Wren said. "We don't read it. That's the first rule. We keep it dry, we keep it hidden, and we never read it."

The book lay open on a table lit by three candles and one shaking hand. It was bound in leather gone the color of dried blood, its pages soft as cloth, and the ink — the ink was wrong. Not faded, as a century-old ink should be. It sat black and wet-looking on the page, as if written that morning by someone whose hand had not yet dried.

*The rat-speaker comes to the house above the tannery row,* Oscar read, and his stomach dropped through the floorboards. *He will stand where you are standing. He will read these words with rain still on his shoulders.*

"That's—" His voice came out thin. "That's tonight. That's now."

"I know." Wren's jaw was set, but her hands weren't steady. She was a tall woman built like someone who'd spent her life hauling nets, with a scar splitting one eyebrow and eyes the gray-green of harbor water, and right now those eyes were fixed on him like he might dissolve. "I found this page three days ago. After the market. I told myself it was coincidence. Old books say strange things."

"Old books don't say *my name.*"

The page said more than his name. It described the night market, the rat who had warned him — *the grey one with the torn ear, who will tell him she smells of old iron* — the kiss under the eaves. It described Wren's laugh, which Oscar had heard exactly once and had thought about every hour since. And beneath all of it, in the same patient black script:

*Tomorrow, at dawn, they will come for her. He will run with her into the dark places, and the book will go with them, because the book has always intended to go with them.*

Oscar looked up. The candles guttered. Somewhere below them, in the tannery yards, a dog began to bark — not the ordinary complaint of a dog at cats, but the flat, continuous alarm of an animal that could smell strangers.

"Wren," he said quietly. "How many people know you have this?"

"Nobody. Nobody living."

"Then why does the page know I'm standing here?" He touched the ink. It was dry under his finger, though it looked wet. Warm, too, faintly, like skin. "Why does it know about tomorrow?"

She didn't answer, because the front door came off its hinges.

* * *

Later — much later, in the dark under the city — Oscar would try to remember the raid in order and fail. Memory gave it to him in pieces: the door splintering inward; lantern-light full of men in the crown's gray-and-gold; the witch-hunters' iron badges shaped like open manacles; Wren's hand closing around his wrist; the codex vanishing into a satchel she'd apparently kept packed beside it for six generations, because her family had always known this night might come.

And the voice. A hunter with a face like a closed ledger, standing in the ruined doorway, saying in a tone of terrible mildness: *"Wren Calloway. By order of the Crown, for consorting with forbidden texts and harboring a mage. You may come quietly, or we may add your name to the list of those who didn't."*

They went out the back window, over the tannery vats, their boots slipping on wet planks while shouts rose behind them. Oscar did the only magic he'd ever dared in daylight: he flung out a thread of himself to the yard dogs, three big mastiffs straining at their chains, and asked them — begged them — to sing.

They sang. The street filled with belling, frenzied sound, hunters cursing and covering their ears, and Oscar and Wren dropped into the mouth of the old cistern tunnel behind the rendering shed, into cold and absolute dark.

They ran. Down stairs that hadn't been stairs since the founders' time, along channels where water moved with a slow underground patience, past brickwork furred white with mineral bloom. Oscar spoke softly as they went — not spells, just greetings — and the dark answered him. Rats poured along the walls beside them, guiding them past collapsed passages. Bats overhead shifted their flight to mark the safe turns. Once, something vast and blind in a flooded gallery brushed against his mind like a whale passing a rowboat, considered him, and let them pass.

"You're talking to them," Wren panted, when they finally stopped in a vaulted chamber where an ancient fountain dripped into a pool. "The whole way down. I can hear it — not words, just... a hum. Like a hive."

"Does it frighten you?"

"Everything frightens me right now. That's not the same question." She set the satchel down and looked at him across the pool, her face half-lit by the phosphorescent fungus crusting the fountain's basin. "Three days ago I didn't know you. Tonight I've kissed you, and fled my home with you, and there's a book downstairs — or there *was* — that says we do all of this before we do it. Do you understand how that feels? Like I'm reading about someone wearing my life."

"Yes," Oscar said. "That's exactly what it feels like."

Because it did. He had spent three years making himself small — charming pigeons to carry letters for coin, asking rats after lost rings and runaway apprentices, keeping his gift folded up tight as a moth in winter. Wanting things got people hanged. He'd watched it happen to a hedge-witch in Copperlane when he was nineteen, watched her swing while the crowd looked away from her out of self-preservation, and he had built his whole life on the lesson: want nothing, claim nothing, belong to no one.

And then a woman at a night market had laughed at his terrible joke about pigeon post rates, and some locked room in his chest had swung open, and he had wanted — and the wanting had felt like fate, because it *was*, because a hundred years ago some hand had written his wanting down in wet black ink and left it to wait for him.

"Here's what I keep snagging on," Wren said. She pulled the codex from the satchel and set it on the fountain's rim, not touching it further, the way one handles a snake. "My family guarded this thing for six generations without ever opening it. Why? What were we guarding it *for*? Books don't need protecting unless someone wants them, and no one wants a book nobody reads." Her voice roughened. "Unless the book wants something. Unless it's been waiting for the right pair of idiots to carry it somewhere."

"The page said the book intends to travel with us."

"I know what it said. I read it too, Oscar. That's rather the problem." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. When she lowered them, she was looking at him with an expression he couldn't name — grief and fury and something tender underneath, all fighting for the same ground. "I don't want to be a character. I don't want to fall for a man because a dead monk wrote it down. I want it to be mine. Do you understand? I want to choose it."

The words landed in him like a stone in still water. He thought of the kiss at the market — how he'd chosen it, or believed he had, how it had felt like the single most voluntary act of his careful, hidden life. And how the book had already known.

"Then we choose it anyway," he said. "Whatever's written. We do it because we decide to, not because the ink says so. Maybe that's the only freedom anyone gets — doing the inevitable thing on purpose."

Wren stared at him. Then, slowly, she laughed — the laugh from the market, the one the book had predicted, and this time it sounded like defiance instead of surrender. "You're either very wise or very stupid, rat-speaker."

"Both, usually."

She reached across the pool and took his hand, deliberately, watching herself do it, claiming the act. And that was when the codex moved.

No wind reached the chamber. No hand touched it. But the great soft pages turned themselves, whispering, whispering, and stopped.

Oscar leaned over the fountain's rim and read the fresh black line, dated in the corner with tomorrow's dawn, and felt the last warm thing in the night drain out of him.

*At dawn they will reach the sealed hall, and there the iron remembers the king.*

Below the line, smaller, almost an afterthought, in the same patient hand:

*One of them will bleed for the other. The book is sorry.*

Wren read it too. Neither of them spoke. Above, muffled through a hundred feet of stone and earth, the hunt for them went on — and somewhere far above that, in a palace of iron and old bargains, a king who was not entirely a king waited for dawn.
