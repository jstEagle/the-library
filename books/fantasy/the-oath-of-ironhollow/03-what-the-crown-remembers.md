## 3. What the Crown Remembers

The deep tunnels smelled of rust and old water, and the rats down here were wrong.

Oscar felt it before he heard it — the way a man feels a draft from a door he thought was shut. The beasts of Ironhollow's undercity spoke in short, greedy syllables, all hunger and territory. But these things that scuttled along the seams of the walls spoke in long vowels, in words worn smooth like river stones. *Down-down-down*, they said. *The King below. The King below.*

"They're singing," Wren whispered beside him, her lantern held low so its light wouldn't crawl up their faces. "Rats don't sing."

"City rats gossip. These ones pray." He kept his voice flat, though his skin prickled. Three years of keeping his gift small and secret, and now he was walking toward the one place in Ironhollow where magic pooled like standing water — drawn by a book, followed by vermin, wanted by hunters. Every instinct he had honed screamed at him to turn around, to go back to charming pigeons for copper and being no one.

But Wren walked on, and his feet betrayed him by following.

The codex rode against her chest in a sling of oilcloth, and even wrapped it seemed to hum. She'd stopped consulting it hours ago; she simply walked as if she already knew the way, and perhaps she did. The pages had been written before either of them was born. Somewhere below them, Oscar knew, there was ink describing this exact tunnel, this exact stumble over a rotted beam, and probably — his stomach turned at the thought — some exact and terrible thing waiting at the end of it.

*The King below,* sang the rats. *The King who is not a king.*

* * *

Wren had stopped reading the codex aloud two days ago, after the passage about the bridge.

She had read it to herself instead, lips moving silently while Oscar pretended not to watch, and what she found there she folded away behind her ribs like a letter she couldn't bear to open. Because the codex did not merely describe what would happen. It described how it would feel. *And the iron-tongued girl shall bleed upon the threshold stones,* said the ink, *and her blood shall be the key that turns the lock.* It named the wound. It named the place. It even, with a scribe's cruel thoroughness, named the hour: dawn, on the third day.

Which was tomorrow.

So she walked with her hand pressed to her side as if she could hold the future closed like a door, and she let the beasts guide them, because the beasts knew the undercity better than any living map. A blind salamander led them through a flooded gallery where drowned chandeliers hung like jellyfish. A colony of pale bats read the air currents and shrieked warnings before each collapse. And always the rats sang their slow hymn, growing louder as they descended, until Wren began to understand the words herself — not through Oscar's gift but through proximity, the way you learn a song stuck in a companion's head.

*He bargained,* the rats sang. *He bargained, and the bargain ate him whole.*

"Tell me again why we're doing this," she said, not because she didn't know, but because saying it aloud made it hers and not the codex's.

Oscar glanced back at her. In the lantern light he looked young and exhausted and stubbornly kind, which was the combination she had fallen for against every ounce of her family's training. Calloways did not fall for anyone. Calloways kept books and kept distance and died old in a house full of unread prophecy.

"Because Aldric's been dead for ten years," Oscar said, "and nobody told him. Because whatever's wearing his face signed death warrants with his hand. And because—" he hesitated, and she watched him decide to tell the truth, watched it cost him, "—because if I'm the one the book says unbinds him, then maybe I can rewrite the ending. Maybe listening works better than commanding."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we run. That's the plan. There's always a run."

There wasn't. She knew there wasn't, because the ink said so. But she loved him a little for pretending, and hated the codex a great deal more for making even love feel like something she'd been assigned.

* * *

They came up out of the tunnels beneath the palace itself, through a seam in the foundations that no architect had drawn, guided by a final rat who sat on a broken step and would go no further. Its whiskers trembled. *Here the song ends,* it said. *Above lives the note the song is about.* Then it fled into the dark, and Oscar stood very still for a moment, shaken, as if he'd been handed something heavy.

"The throne room," he said quietly. "It's directly above us."

The stair beyond the seam was iron — actual iron, not stone — and it should not have existed. Ironhollow's founders had built in granite and oak precisely because iron held magic the way a sponge holds blood. Yet here was a staircase of forged black metal, and as they climbed it Oscar's breath went ragged and Wren saw frost forming on her own eyelashes despite the heat rising from below.

The doors at the top were sealed with a king's oath. They could see it in the way the metal had fused — no lock, no bar, just ten years of a promise holding the doors shut from inside.

"Aldric sealed himself in," Wren breathed. "After the crown took him. Some part of him locked the doors."

"Some part of him is still in there," Oscar said. "Listening. Waiting." He set his palm flat against the iron, and Wren saw him do the thing no mage would ever do — he didn't push, didn't command, didn't whisper a word of power. He simply touched it the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. *We've come to help,* he said, without sound. *May we come in?*

The doors opened.

* * *

The throne room was a wound of amber light. Every window had been bricked over except one high slit facing east, through which the first grey hint of coming dawn fell like a blade. Dust hung motionless in the air. And on the throne of black granite sat King Aldric the Ninth — or what remained of the arrangement between a dying king and a hungry crown.

He was mummified upright, hands on the armrests, spine held straight by the thing circling his skull. The crown of Ironhollow was not gold. It was forged iron, plain and brutal, older than the dynasty, and it had grown — roots of it threaded down through his scalp, his jaw, his shoulders, visible where the flesh had wasted to leather and bone. It had kept him alive exactly as long as the bargain required. Not one day longer than necessary. Not one day less.

And when they entered, the dead king's eyes moved. Dry lids scraped open over eyes gone white as milk, and a voice came out of him like wind through a chimney — two voices, actually, braided: a tired human rasp and something vast and metallic underneath, speaking in unison.

"You are the rat-charmer," it said. "You are the ink-girl. We dreamed you both. Dreams are the last luxury of the consumed."

"What did he bargain?" Wren asked, and her voice cracked on the question.

The braid of voices sighed. "Plague. Famine. The city dying street by street. He knelt before us — we were only a crown then, an instrument, waiting to be worn properly — and he asked for his people's salvation. We asked the price. He said, *anything*. Kings say that word too easily. We took the anything. Ten years we have worn him, ruling well enough, feeding the city, and all the while drinking him drop by drop. He has been aware the entire time. Trapped behind his own eyes, watching his hands sign, watching his mouth order hangings. He begged us, once a year, to let him die." The white eyes turned to Oscar, and something almost gentle moved in the metal voice. "He begs now."

"Why tell us this?" Oscar whispered.

"Because you did not command. You knocked." The crown's roots flexed, and iron groaned against bone. "No mage in a century has knocked."

* * *

They came out of the dark with crossbows and witch-irons, six of them in grey-and-crimson, filling the doorway like spilled ink. Their captain's voice rang off the brick-sealed windows: "By writ of the Regency Council — the beast-speaker and the codex-thief, dead or bound!"

Later, Oscar would understand that everything happened in the space of three heartbeats. The captain raising his hand. The bolts loosing — not at Wren, though she stood nearer, but at him, because the Council feared a beast-speaker's tongue more than a librarian's book. Him frozen, too slow, three years of hiding having taught him to freeze. And Wren moving — not away, not behind cover, but *into* it, shoulder-first, arms wide, putting her body between the iron quarrels and the man she had been fated to love and refused to be scripted about.

One bolt took her high in the side, below the ribs, punching through with a sound like a nail driven into green wood.

The world narrowed. Oscar caught her as she folded, and her blood was hot through his fingers, and somewhere above them dawn was beginning — the hour the ink had named, the threshold stones beneath them slick and red. *Her blood shall be the key.* Fury rose in him hotter than anything he had ever felt, fury at the hunters, at the codex, at the smug certainty of written fate, and beneath the fury, cold and clear as well-water, one thought:

*Unless I break it first.*

The crown on the dead king's head turned toward him, patient as geology.

"Well, little listener," it said. "Dawn is here. Choose quickly."
