## 4. The Unbinding

The throne room at dawn was the color of old bruises.

Grey light bled through the clerestory windows in long shafts, catching dust that hung in the air like a held breath. Oscar stood where the hunters had left him — they had not left him far; three crossbows still tracked Wren's heartbeat from the gallery above — and looked up at King Aldric.

The king had been handsome once. Oscar could see it beneath everything else, beneath the grey of a face worn too long by something that was not grief and not age. Ten years on the iron throne had hollowed him the way water hollows stone: patiently, without malice, until what remained was mostly absence. The crown sat low on his brow, dark as a nail driven into the world, and it did not gleam even in the sunrise. Iron drank light. Everyone knew that.

What no one knew — what Oscar was about to bet both their lives on — was why.

"Speak your command, beast-speaker," said the captain of the witch-hunters behind him, voice flat with disgust. "Command the crown to release him, as the prophecy demands. Or we finish this here."

*As the prophecy demands.* The codex lay open on the flagstones where Wren had dropped it, its century-old ink still wet-looking in the dawn, the last page showing a line drawing of this exact moment: a small man before a high throne, a king like a candle burnt to its socket, and above them all a crown rendered in careful strokes — and beneath the crown, in letters that had appeared only an hour ago, the word *unbound*, and beneath that, smaller, crueler: *and the girl's blood waters the stone.*

Wren was pale against the wall where they'd propped her, her hand pressed over the wound in her side. She caught his eye and shook her head once. *Don't.* As if she would rather die than be the reason he did anything.

He loved her. He had known it for weeks now, and hated knowing it, because the book had known it first, had written his love down before he'd felt it, and every tender thing between them had felt slightly secondhand ever since. But standing there with the crossbows trained on her breathing, he understood something the book had never managed to say: fate could script the meeting, but it couldn't script the choosing. That part was still his.

"Your Majesty," Oscar said, and his voice cracked on the title, so he tried again. "King Aldric. I'm not going to command anyone."

The captain made a sound of contempt. Aldric's eyes — clouded, distant, the eyes of a man watching himself from very far away — flickered. Focused. For one moment, something human looked out through them like a drowning man through ice.

"Boy," the king whispered. "Whatever you mean to do. Do it quickly. I am so tired of being *worn*."

* * *

Here is what Oscar knew about speaking to beasts, knowledge bought with three years of rats in his cupped hands and pigeons on his windowsill: you do not begin with words. You begin with listening. A rat does not care what you want. A rat cares that you have noticed it is afraid.

Every mage who had ever attempted the unbinding — and the codex recorded eleven attempts across a hundred years — had come to the crown with commands. *Release him. Let go. Be broken.* They had brought fire, holy water, counter-charms, armies. They had treated the crown as an enemy to be defeated or a lock to be picked.

Oscar crouched slowly, deliberately, in full view of the crossbows, until he was small against the floor of the throne room. Small, the way he made himself small for frightened animals. Then he opened whatever door it was inside him that he had spent his whole life keeping barely ajar, and he listened.

The sound that came through nearly knocked him flat.

It was not a roar. It was not cold, or ancient, or triumphant. It was *lonely* — a vast, grinding loneliness like a ship's anchor chain dragging across a seafloor where nothing lived, had never lived, would never live. The crown had been forged to hold a kingdom together, and it had done so, and in doing so it had eaten the only mind it would ever touch, and now there was nothing left in all the world that spoke to it except the man it was devouring, and soon there would not even be that.

It wasn't a tyrant. It was the loneliest thing in Ironhollow, and it was holding on because holding on was the only thing it knew how to do.

"Oh," Oscar said aloud, and his eyes were stinging. "Oh, you poor thing."

The whole room went still. Even the hunters.

"You're not evil," he said softly, to the air, to the crown, to the dark iron drinking the morning light. "You were made to keep a city from falling apart, and you've been doing it alone for two hundred years, and nobody ever asked if *you* wanted anything." He swallowed. "I know what that's like. Wanting things gets you hanged, so you stop wanting, and then someone shows you a night market and a girl who laughs at your terrible jokes, and you remember you've been dead inside for years." His voice shook. "I let go anyway. Last week. Terrifying thing I've ever done."

Aldric was crying without any expression at all, tears sliding down a face that had forgotten how to move.

"I'm not going to order you to release him," Oscar said. "I'm going to ask. And I'm going to tell you what happens after, because I think nobody's ever told you that either. When you let go, the kingdom will shake for a while. People will be scared. And then — " he glanced back at Wren, at her bloodied hand, her steady eyes — "and then people will take care of each other. That's what people do, when they're allowed. You don't have to hold them anymore. You never had to hold them alone. That was the lie they forged into you."

He reached out — not touching, never touching, hands open and empty the way you showed a wild thing you meant no harm.

"Let him rest. Please. And when you're ready to stop being a crown — the scrap-yards are full of iron that became ploughs, and bells, and cradles. Nothing ends. It just stops hurting."

The silence went on for ten heartbeats. Twenty.

Then the crown *sighed* — a sound like a furnace going cold, like a rope finally slacking — and lifted.

* * *

It rose perhaps a hand's width off the king's brow and came apart into rust-red flakes, drifting down through the dawn light like the world's saddest snowfall, and King Aldric slumped forward and would have fallen from the throne if Oscar hadn't caught him.

Up close the king weighed almost nothing. Up close his eyes were just an old man's eyes, watery and blue and astonished.

"I can't feel it," Aldric whispered. "Ten years. I can't feel it. What is your name, boy?"

"Oscar Eriksen."

"Oscar Eriksen." The king smiled, and it transformed him, made him briefly the man he must have been at his coronation. "They'll write songs about you and get every word wrong. Tell my daughter—" A cough. Blood on his lips, but his face was peaceful, unclenched for the first time in a decade. "Tell her the winter she was born, I bargained so she'd never know hunger. Tell her it was worth it. Tell her I'm sorry I wasn't there to say it myself."

He died at sunrise, human, held by a rat-charmer in an empty throne room, and it was the gentlest death Oscar had ever witnessed.

Around them, the codex began to scream.

Not literally — books don't scream — but the pages fluttered wildly, ink crawling and dissolving, the prophecy eating itself line by line as the future it described stopped existing. The drawing of the throne room blurred. *And the girl's blood waters the stone* faded to nothing. Wren made a small sound, and when Oscar looked over, she had pulled her hand from her wound and was staring at it: the bleeding had slowed, then stopped, then the skin was simply closed, smooth, as though the wound had been unwritten.

"It needed me dead," she whispered. "The ending needed me dead, and you broke the ending."

"The ending was a suggestion," Oscar said, and laughed, wetly, helplessly. "Everything in that book was a suggestion. Nobody told us we could decline."

* * *

They gave him the pardon at noon, in a room that smelled of lamp oil and fear. The captain of the witch-hunters herself delivered it, standing stiff as a pike, a document heavy with seals and ribbons.

"Full pardon," she read. "In exchange for a binding oath of silence. No magic spoken, no gift used, no word of what occurred in the throne room, for the term of your natural life. The kingdom requires stability, Eriksen. Stability requires that no one learn a street-rat unmade what archmages could not."

Oscar thought of three years of smallness. Of rats and pigeons and coin scraped together, of teaching himself not to want. Of a life built like a hiding place.

He thought of Wren waiting in the corridor, alive, impatient, already packing.

"No," he said.

The captain blinked. "You understand this offer expires—"

"I understand perfectly. Keep your pardon." He stood, and it felt strange and wonderful to stand at full height in this city. "But here's what I'll give you free of charge: I'm leaving. Today. Exile, self-imposed, no need to hunt me. And someday — not soon, but someday — someone's going to figure out that magic isn't a hanging offense, it's a conversation, and this kingdom is going to need people who know how to listen. When that day comes, send word to wherever the road takes us. We'll come back."

"*We*," the captain repeated.

Oscar grinned, and it was Wren's grin now, borrowed and kept. "We."

She was waiting at the west gate with two packs, the ruined codex bundled under her arm — some pages blank now, but others, older pages, still full of futures nobody had gotten around to breaking — and the road beyond the walls ran green and gold into country neither of them had ever seen.

"Took you long enough," she said.

"Fate wrote me leaving at dusk. I like disappointing fate."

They walked out of Ironhollow together, not because a book said they would, but because they chose to, every step of it theirs — and if, miles on, when the city had shrunk to a smudge behind them, a fox paused at the treeline and regarded Oscar with bright, curious eyes, and Oscar tipped his hat and said *good morning* like an introduction between equals —

Well. Some conversations were only beginning.
