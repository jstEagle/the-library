## 1. The Ledger Opens Both Ways

The Vellum Guild kept its night inventory the way a mortician keeps a body: with respect, with distance, and with an exact accounting of everything that had gone wrong.

Emrys Almeida walked the long aisle of the vault at the hour the candles were changed, her ledger open across her left forearm, her pen scratching in the small merciless hand that had made her famous among people who would never admit to knowing her. The vault beneath the guildhouse on Candlemas Row was not a cellar so much as a throat—low-ceilinged, warm-breathed, lined with shelves of grey stone that sweated in summer and rang faintly in winter, like something swallowing. The air tasted of tallow and old rain and, underneath it all, the particular ozone tang of things that were not entirely lawful.

She stopped at Shelf Nine and lifted the first manifest card.

*One storm, bottled. Provenance: the drowned coast of Serevan. Contents verified by shaking; contents survived verification. Condition: restless.*

She held the bottle up to the candlelight. The glass was sea-green and older than the guild, corked with wax stamped by a chandler three generations dead, and inside it a small grey weather turned over and over like a bird that had forgotten it was a bird. When she tilted it, the storm pressed its whole weight against the near side of the glass and looked at her. She had been doing this work for eleven years and she still could not decide whether bottled storms *looked* at you or whether she merely preferred to believe they did. Preference, she had learned, was a line item no ledger could balance.

*Condition: restless,* she wrote again, because the card said so and because it was true, and then, after a pause that no one would ever audit, she added nothing else. The god did not speak. The god had not spoken in ten years, two months, and nineteen days, and Emrys had learned to keep that figure the way other mercenaries kept their scars—not displayed, not denied, simply carried where the weight of it could do the least damage.

Her oath had been sworn at seventeen, knee-down in a flooded shrine with water to her chin and a dying priest's hand on her crown, and the words of it had gone into her the way a hook goes into a fish: cleanly, and all the way through. *I will keep your ledgers true. I will carry your silence as faithfully as I would have carried your voice.* She had thought, at seventeen, that the second clause was poetry. She knew better now. It was terms. It had always been terms, and somewhere along the way she had become the kind of merchant who honored contracts even when the counterparty had stopped answering letters.

She set the storm down gently. Storms remembered gentleness; that was the trouble with them.

* * *

The rest of the inventory went the way inventory always went: in order, without incident, and with a low continuous hum of wrongness that Emrys had long since folded into the background noise of her life, the way a harbor-dweller stops hearing gulls.

Shelf Eleven: *three years, borrowed, unclaimed by their original owners.* They sat in stoppered phials the color of weak tea, and when she uncorked one to check the seal—a formality, but the guild was nothing if not a church of formalities—the smell of someone else's childhood came out: cut grass, bread, a dog barking far away. She stoppered it quickly. Borrowed years were the guild's most honest commodity and its cruelest. People sold the years they could not bear to live through, and the guild resold them to people who wanted to feel what it was like to have survived something. Nobody came out ahead. The guild, of course, did.

Shelf Fourteen: *one name, never given.* It lay in a lead box the size of a prayer book, and she did not open it. The last clerk who had opened it had spent six weeks answering to a name that wasn't his, cheerfully, before anyone noticed, and by then the man he had been was a rumor with excellent handwriting.

Shelf Sixteen: *seven promises, defaulted, held in escrow.* Broken oaths congealed into a substance the guild called dross—grey, brittle, faintly sweet-smelling, like old icing—and sold by weight to alchemists who claimed it made a passable flux. Emrys privately believed the alchemists were lying about the flux and buying something else entirely, but that belief was not auditable, so she recorded the weights and moved on.

At each shelf she paused—one breath, no more—and listened inward, to the place behind her sternum where the oath lived. A decade ago there had been warmth there, a hearth-glow of attention, the sense of being *read*. Now there was only the oath itself, patient and cold as a nail in wood. Ten years, two months, nineteen days. Her junior clerks believed the god was testing the faithful. Her fellow factors believed the god had died, though none of them said so above a whisper, because a guild whose charter was sworn to a god could not afford to find out. Emrys believed in figures. Figures said the god was silent. What the silence meant was not a question arithmetic could answer, and she had stopped asking it aloud years ago, which was its own kind of answer.

She finished Shelf Twenty-Two at the turn of the third bell and signed the night inventory with the small flourish that meant *complete and true*, and it was then, closing the great ledger, that she heard the footsteps on the stairs.

They were bad footsteps—young ones, hurrying ones, the steps of someone who had rehearsed this descent twice and abandoned the rehearsal halfway. Emrys did not turn around. She finished blotting her signature, squared the ledger, and said, "Tamsin. You're supposed to be asleep."

Junior Clerk Tamsin Ferrow stood at the foot of the stairs holding a book against her chest the way soldiers hold wounded children, which was to say too tightly and with a total misunderstanding of what would help. She was nineteen, freckled, chronically overworked, and possessed of the single most dangerous quality a guild clerk could have: she read things.

"Factor Almeida." Tamsin's voice cracked and she pushed through it. "I know the hour. I know I'm not supposed to—I catalogued it myself, three days ago, Lot 114, acquisition from the Marrow estate, and I put it on Shelf Nineteen with the miscellany because I couldn't classify it, and I've been trying not to look at it, I *have*—" She ran out of air. "You should look at it instead."

Emrys turned. In eleven years of night inventories she had been interrupted by drunk factors, weeping clients, one escaped bottled fog (a long story, involving most of the kitchen staff), and Guildmaster Corvane himself, once, standing in the dark among the shelves like a man visiting graves. She ranked Tamsin's interruption somewhere below the fog and slightly above Corvane, which was generous of her.

"Classify it," Emrys said. "In the morning. With the proper forms."

"It's a prophecy book," Tamsin said. "And the newest entry is dated tonight."

* * *

The vault went very quiet in the specific way rooms go quiet when they have been listening all along and want you to know it.

Emrys crossed to Shelf Nineteen without hurrying. Hurrying was a tell; she taught that to every clerk she trained. Lot 114 sat where Tamsin had left it, wedged between a taxonomist's study of extinct angels and a jar of preserved regret (small, amber, harmless-looking), and it was, on first inspection, an unremarkable thing: a quarto bound in vellum gone the color of old teeth, its boards scuffed, its spine re-sewn at least twice by hands of differing skill.

"Provenance," Emrys said, opening it. "Quickly."

"The Marrow estate, south bank. The family kept it for four generations as a curiosity. The last Marrow sold the house and everything in it and took ship east. The auctioneer's list called it *family devotional, author unknown*." Tamsin swallowed. "It isn't devotional. Or if it is, it's devotion to something with very good timing."

Emrys turned the pages. The early entries were in a fine, slanted hand, brown-black ink gone bronze with age: dates from a century past, and beside each date a few lines of prophecy written after the manner of the old seers—all mist and metaphor, mountains walking, crowns of salt, a bride of glass. Standard fare. She had appraised forty books like it. Most were frauds committed after the fact by clever forgers writing history backward. A few were genuine, and those were worse, because genuine prophecy had a texture to it that forgery could not fake: the ink seemed to arrive on the page rather than be put there.

"Authentication rites," she said. "All of them. Fetch the kit."

Tamsin fetched the kit.

They worked through the rites in the order the guild mandated, and with each one Emrys's professional composure held, and under the composure something began, very quietly, to come apart.

The silver-wire test: no reaction. The book was not warded, glamoured, or ensorcelled—or rather, whatever enchantment it carried was so old and so settled that it no longer registered as magic at all, the way a river doesn't register as moving if you stand in it long enough. The blood-ink test: negative; the text was ordinary gall-and-oakgall, mixed by someone who knew their trade. The provenance trace, which read the memory of paper: and here Emrys stopped, and made herself repeat the reading twice more, and got the same impossible result both times.

"The paper is ninety-one years old," she said slowly. "The binding is eighty-eight. The *text*—" She looked up. "The text predates its own paper, Tamsin. The newest entry has been in this book longer than the book has existed."

"That's not possible."

"No," Emrys agreed. "It isn't. Write that in the margin. *Not possible.* Initial it."

But it was the final entry that made her sit down on the iron stool by Shelf Nineteen, because the final entry was not mist and metaphor at all. It was plain as a bill of sale. And it was dated—in the same confident bronze ink as the century-old pages—with tonight's date.

*On this night the auditor comes to the burning hall, and stands in the smoke with the ledger open, and swears an oath she does not mean, and means it anyway. Her name is written here because it was always written here:*

***Emrys Almeida.***

The candle flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft. Somewhere above them the guildhouse settled its bones, floorboard by floorboard, the sound of a large building pretending to sleep.

"Factor?" Tamsin whispered. "What does it mean, *the burning hall*?"

"It means nothing," Emrys said. "It means a hundred years ago somebody wrote a piece of theater and got lucky with a name. Almeida isn't rare in the southern provinces. Neither is Emrys." She heard her own voice delivering the assessment flatly, correctly, exactly as she would deliver it to a client, and heard beneath it the small cold voice that did her real thinking, which said: *You tested it four ways. It's genuine. It's genuine and it knows your name and tonight is tonight.*

She closed the book. Her hands were steady. She noted this, clinically, the way she noted all data: hands steady, pulse elevated, mouth dry. Fear was just the body running an inventory of its own, and she was, whatever else, excellent at inventory.

"Tamsin. Who else has seen this?"

"No one. I catalogued it at night. I didn't file the classification slip yet, I—" Tamsin's freckles stood out pale against skin going white. "I brought it straight to you."

"Good." Emrys weighed it in her hands. A book should weigh about what a book weighs. This one weighed slightly less, as if some portion of it had already gone elsewhere, or had not fully arrived. "It goes to Guildmaster Corvane in the morning, sealed, with a full report. Until then it stays on my desk, not on the shelf, and you don't touch it, and you don't speak of it."

"Yes, Factor."

"And Tamsin." Emrys waited until the girl met her eyes. "Whatever you think you saw when you read that page—whatever you think it promises—it's a book. Books are goods. Goods are inventory. We do not let inventory tell us who we are."

Tamsin nodded, unconvinced, and climbed back up the stairs to her cot with the careful tread of someone who now knew the dark had furniture in it.

Emrys stood alone among the shelves with the storm looking at her and the borrowed years breathing softly in their phials, and she told herself the sensible things, all of them, in order, the way she balanced a column: coincidence of names; the credulity of tired clerks; the guild's long experience with forged wonders. She told herself these things while carrying the book up to her office, while setting it on her desk beneath the lamp, while looking at it for a long time without touching it.

Then, because she was Emrys Almeida—because she had built her whole self out of the habit of keeping records no one else would keep—she took a fresh sheet of guild paper, dipped her pen, and copied the final entry out entire, word for word, date for date, name for name. If Corvane ordered the book burned or locked away, the record would survive. Records were the only loyalty she had never regretted.

She blotted the copy, folded it into her coat, next to her heart, where merchants kept the documents that mattered and fools kept love letters, and blew out the lamp.

In the dark of her office, Lot 114 lay closed on the desk, patient as an unpaid debt.

Outside, over the rooftops of the free cities, the moon was rising.
