## 2. Ink That Remembers

The Vellum Guild kept its authentication rites the way other institutions kept their sins: in a locked drawer, in a careful hand, consulted rarely and never spoken of above a whisper.

Emrys Almeida laid them all out on her desk that night like a surgeon laying out knives.

There were nine of them. Nine rites for nine kinds of lie, because the guild had learned over four centuries that impossibility came in flavors. The candle of true wax, which guttered blue in the presence of forgery. The salt circle of provenance, which no object could cross without leaving its history written in the grains. The mirror test, the needle test, the weighing against a feather cut from a dead angel's wing—each one expensive, each one slow, each one requiring permissions Emrys technically did not have and was taking anyway.

She had told herself she was being thorough. That was the word she'd chosen: *thorough*. A senior auditor, presented with an anomaly in inventory, would naturally want to verify before escalating. It was due diligence. It was procedure.

It was not, she admitted somewhere around the second hour, procedure at all. Procedure would have been to seal the book in lead and wake Corvane immediately. Procedure would have involved witnesses.

Instead she sat alone in the audit room with a book that knew her name, and she worked.

* * *

The book itself was unremarkable to look at, which was its first lie. Grey-green boards scuffed soft at the corners, spine cracked from a century of handling, brass corner-fittings gone dull as old teeth. It smelled of cedar shavings and something fainter underneath—smoke, maybe, or the memory of smoke. The kind of smell you couldn't name until you'd stood too close to a burning building, and then never forgot.

Its title page read, in a hand so plain it was almost aggressive: *A True Account of Things Not Yet Done.*

The guild's cataloguers had logged it forty years ago as a curiosity. Minor prophetic text, regional origin unknown, no significant predictions verified. It had spent those forty years in the deep stacks, third sublevel, shelf 118, wedged between a treatise on the migratory habits of debt-spirits and a jar containing what the label optimistically called *one (1) honest man's regret.*

And then a junior clerk named Pip Wrenn—seventeen years old, ink-stained to the elbow, with the particular hunger of someone who reads everything shelved near him instead of working—had pulled it out on a slow evening and opened it to the newest page.

Emrys had seen the entry now perhaps fifty times. She could recite it. She recited it anyway, silently, as she set the candle of true wax beside the book and watched the flame stand perfectly still, neither guttering nor bending, as if it were afraid.

*On the night of the twin moons' crossing,* the entry read, in ink that had not been there forty years ago, or ten years ago, or last week when Pip had first shown it to her trembling, *a woman stands in a hall of burning paper. Her hair is bound back for work. She swears an oath she does not mean, and the oath is accepted, and this acceptance is the hinge on which every door after swings.*

Below it, smaller, in the same hand:

*Her name is entered in the guild rolls as E. Almeida. Auditor. Third rank. The ledger opens both ways.*

Tonight was the night of the twin moons' crossing. She could see them through the audit room's one high window, two pale discs riding close together like conspirators, and the sight of them made her jaw ache from clenching.

"Right," she said aloud, to the empty room, because saying things aloud helped, and because the alternative was admitting how much her hands wanted to shake. "Let's find out what you are."

* * *

She began with the candle, since it was already lit. The flame of true wax burned steady in the presence of honesty and guttered blue before forgery—a copied signature, a faked provenance, a soul pretending to be something it wasn't. She held it over the open book, over that final page, close enough that the heat pressed against her knuckles.

The flame went white.

Not blue. White—the color it turned, according to the rite's marginalia, only once before, in Year 211, when a merchant had attempted to sell the guild a bottle containing not a captured storm but a captured *god*, and the ensuing litigation had taken eleven years and cost three lives. Emrys stared at that white flame for a long moment, then pinched it out with fingers that were steadier than she had any right to expect.

"Noted," she said, and wrote it down in her own working notebook, because whatever else happened, she was still an auditor, and auditors wrote things down. *Candle: anomalous. Not forgery. Worse.*

The salt circle next. She swept a clean ring on the floorboards, chalked the four cardinal marks, and set the book in the center. The salt of provenance worked slowly; you had to give it a full hour, during which any object inside the circle would gradually reveal its history in the arrangement of the grains—where it was made, who had touched it, what it had witnessed. Most objects produced a modest record. A spoon might show its smith, its buyer, its decades in a drawer. The salt was patient. The salt remembered everything the object remembered, no more.

At the end of the hour, Emrys knelt and read the ring.

The grains had arranged themselves into lines. Lines into columns. Columns into—she felt her breath go thin—*script*. The salt hadn't shown her where the book was made. The salt had transcribed a passage of it, a passage she hadn't read yet, in handwriting that looped and slanted in a way that raised the hairs on her arms, because she knew that hand. She saw it every day, scratched into her own working notes, into guild requisitions, into the margins of ledgers she'd been correcting since she was twenty-two years old.

It was her handwriting. Aged, somehow—looser, wearier—but hers.

The passage read: *I have counted the days of the god's silence the way I count everything, in columns, as though numbers could hold grief in place. Ten years. The column does not balance. Nothing balances.*

She swept the salt away with the side of her hand, harder than necessary, scattering a decade of her own private arithmetic across the floorboards.

* * *

The mirror test came next, and the mirror test was where her professional composure finally began to crack along its fault lines.

The rite was simple in principle. You placed the object before a mirror ground from volcanic glass, treated with silver and with the ash of burned contracts, and the mirror showed not the object but its *origin*—the moment of its making, the truth of its first cause. Forgeries showed the forger's hand. Cursed items showed the curse being laid. Ordinary books showed a scribe at a desk, an inkpot, lamplight.

Emrys positioned the glass and looked.

She saw a woman at a desk. Lamplight. An inkpot. But the desk was hers—the audit desk, with its worn leather inset and the notch she'd carved in the edge seven years ago to hang her sealing-wax spoon—and the woman wore her own grey coat, her own hair bound back for work, exactly as the prophecy described. And the woman was writing, writing fast, pages piling up around her while behind her, through the doorway of the room, orange light flickered. Paper burning. A whole hall of paper burning.

The woman in the mirror paused. Looked up.

Looked—at her.

Emrys did not remember deciding to cover the mirror. She remembered the cold of the cloth in her hand, and the silence afterward, and the sound of her own breathing, which took longer than it should have to come back under control. She stood with her palm flat against the draped glass for a full minute, feeling her pulse knock against the volcanic black beneath.

Every rite said the same thing, in its own language. The candle said *true*. The salt said *yours*. The mirror said *coming*. The feather-weighing, which she performed last, with the dead angel's plume balanced on the book's cover, said nothing at all—the feather simply rose, gently, as though relieved of weight, and drifted to the ceiling, and stayed there, pinned by nothing, until she left the room.

Nine rites. Nine confirmations. The book was authentic. The book was authentic *backward*: the analysis of its paper dated the stock to roughly a century ago, and the analysis of its ink dated the final entry to tonight, and both of these things were true simultaneously, which meant the book predated its own materials, which meant—

Which meant the guild's entire taxonomy of impossible things needed a new category, and Emrys was too tired, and too frightened, and too angry to invent one just then.

* * *

Guildmaster Corvane arrived before dawn.

He always moved quietly for a large man; he seemed to take a craftsman's pride in it, the way his bulk negotiated doorways without announcing itself. He stood in the doorway of the audit room now, taking in the scene—the dead candle, the scattered salt, the covered mirror, the book sitting in its circle of chalk with the feather still pinned to the ceiling above it like a small white accusation—and Emrys watched him assemble his understanding of it all in about four seconds flat.

"You ran the rites." His voice was level. Corvane's voice was always level; it was one of the reasons he'd risen to Guildmaster, and one of the reasons people trusted him with their secrets and then regretted it. "All nine."

"All nine."

"I gave no authorization for the rites."

"No," Emrys agreed. "You gave an order to seal it and sent me to bed. I'm the senior auditor on nights, Guildmaster. Authentication falls within my remit." She kept her voice in the same register as his—flat, professional, the voice of two colleagues discussing freight tariffs. Inside, something else was pacing. "You'll want the results."

"I'll want your report filed through channels."

"My report is that the book is genuine. Every rite confirms. It isn't forged, enchanted, cursed, or conjured. By every measure this guild owns, that final entry is accurate prophecy, dated tonight, bearing my name." She paused. "And the mirror shows a woman at my desk, in a burning hall, swearing an oath."

For the first time in the six years she had served under him, Emrys saw Corvane blink twice in quick succession—a tell she'd catalogued years ago and never once seen him display. It lasted less than a heartbeat. Then his face closed back over like water.

"The book goes into the lead box," he said. "Triple-sealed, warded, logged under restricted acquisition. You will file your findings through channels, in full, and then you will take a fortnight's leave."

"A fortnight's—"

"Paid," Corvane said, which was worse, because the guild did not pay for leave unless it wanted something gone quiet. "Effective immediately. Merrick will cover your audits."

"Merrick can't read half the provenance marks."

"Then Merrick will learn." Corvane stepped forward and lifted the book from the chalk circle with both hands, carefully, the way one handles a sleeping animal that has bitten before. Up close, Emrys saw that his thumbs were pressing hard against the boards, whitening at the nail beds. He was afraid of it. Corvane, who had personally appraised a bottled plague and haggled down a demon's asking price, was afraid of a century-old diary.

"You've seen this before," she said. It wasn't a question. She hadn't planned to say it; the words simply left, the way words do when the thing holding them gets tired. "Haven't you. You knew what it was when it came up from the stacks."

Corvane set the book down on her desk, between them, and regarded her for a long moment. In the grey pre-dawn light through the high window, with the twin moons fading behind the glass, his face looked older than it had an hour ago. Or perhaps she was only now looking properly.

"I knew what the *catalogue* said it was," he said at last. "Whether that's the same thing remains to be seen. File your report, Almeida. Take your leave. And—" the level voice dipped, almost gentle, which from Corvane was more alarming than shouting, "—do not copy that page."

Emrys went very still.

"I haven't copied it," she said.

"No," Corvane agreed. "Not yet." He gathered the book up again, tucked it under his arm like a magistrate carrying evidence, and paused at the doorway without turning around. "You're the best auditor this guild has produced in thirty years, Emrys. That's precisely why I know what you're going to do the moment that door closes. Don't make me be right."

His footsteps faded down the corridor, unhurried, quiet, and then the outer door booted shut, and the lock spoke its iron syllable, and she was alone with the feather still drifting at the ceiling and the smell of cedar and old smoke.

* * *

Here is what Emrys knew about herself, and had known since she was nineteen years old and had sold her first oath to the guild: obedience was a tool, and tools were for using, and the moment a tool began to use *you*, you put it down.

She also knew that Corvane would have watchers on her by midday. That her lodgings would acquire a quiet neighbor. That her fortnight's paid leave was a cage with cushions in it. All of this was fine. All of this was expected. She had grown up in the free cities, where the powerful watched the useful, and the useful learned to be interesting only on their own schedule.

So she did the obedient things, and did them well, and let herself be seen doing them. She filed her report through channels—full, meticulous, professionally devastating in its implications and scrupulously dry in its language. She surrendered her night keys. She packed a bag in her lodgings, visibly, inefficiently, leaving the window unlatched in a way that no competent burglar would miss and no incompetent watcher could resist reporting.

And then, in the hour before moonset, with the watcher outside counting her lit windows, Emrys sat at her kitchen table with her working notebook open and reproduced, from perfect memory, the entirety of the final page of *A True Account of Things Not Yet Done.*

Her pen moved steadily. She did not hurry. Hurrying made errors, and errors in transcription were sins against the trade, and besides—if she was right about even half of what the rites had told her, then this page was going to matter very much in the days to come, and it deserved a careful hand.

*On the night of the twin moons' crossing, a woman stands in a hall of burning paper.*

The ink went down black and wet and ordinary. Just ink. Just her hand. Whatever the original page had been, the copy was only a copy—except that as she wrote the final line, her own line, the one the salt had shown her, the one about counting the days of the god's silence in columns, her pen hesitated over the paper, and she understood suddenly, completely, the way one understands a fall only once it has begun, why Corvane had known exactly what she would do.

Because he would have done it too. Because anyone who had spent their life learning to read ledgers would look at a prophecy naming them and do the same arithmetic she was doing now: *if the book is true, then the oath comes. And if the oath comes, then everything depends on whether I swear it knowing, or swear it blind.*

The ledger opens both ways.

Emrys blotted the page, folded it twice, and sewed it into the lining of her left boot, where she had kept important things since she was nineteen years old. Then she banked her fire, latched the window she'd left open for the watcher's benefit, and lay down on top of the blankets fully dressed to wait for morning.

Outside, the twin moons touched, held, and began their long slide apart.

Somewhere across the city, in a lead box in a vault three floors underground, a book waited for its date with the patience of paper—which is to say, forever—and Emrys fell asleep reciting the entry one last time, like a prayer, like a warning, like the first line of a contract she had not yet signed:

*A woman stands in a hall of burning paper. She swears an oath she does not mean.*

"And the oath is accepted," she murmured, into the dark. "And this acceptance is the hinge on which every door after swings."

She dreamed of smoke that smelled like cedar, and of a god whose silence had a shape, and the shape was a door, and the door was opening.
