## 3. The Date Arrives

Moonrise came to Vellum Hall the way debt comes to a debtor: slowly, then all at once.

Emrys had not gone home. She told herself it was diligence—the night inventory still had four crates of bottled weather uncounted, a jar of borrowed thunder that needed turning so it wouldn't settle, and a shelf of names-that-were-never-given that required someone with steady hands to dust them without speaking aloud. That was the official reason she gave the junior clerk who found her at her desk an hour before moonrise, the copied page folded into her coat like a knife.

The true reason sat in the pit of her stomach and did not have a ledger entry.

She had tested the prophecy book against everything the guild owned. She had weighed its paper against paper from its own century—impossible, because the fibers were older than the sheet they made. She had held it over candleflame and watched the fire refuse it, curling around the edges like a dog that knows better than to bite its master. She had read the newest entry forty times until the words stopped being words and became a door.

*And on the night the moon rises whole, the hall shall burn, and the woman Emrys Almeida, who swore falsely, shall be carried through smoke to the seat of the false-crowned king, and there the accounting begins.*

Her name. In a book older than her grandmother's grandmother. Written in ink that remembered being wet.

"You should eat something," Pell said. He was twenty, freckled, terrified, and holding a plate of bread he'd clearly forgotten he was carrying. "Guildmaster Corvane says you're to be watched, but he didn't say you couldn't be fed."

"Corvane can watch me starve," Emrys said, but she took the bread, because terror is no excuse for poor resource management, and her mother had raised her on that particular catechism. She tore it and ate mechanically, tasting nothing, while her eyes went to the high windows of the ledger hall, where the moon was climbing through a sky scoured clean by wind.

Whole. The moon would be whole tonight for the first time in three months.

"Pell." She swallowed. "If something happens down here—"

"Nothing's going to happen."

"If something happens, you take the master ledger, the second copy, and you go to the salt cellar under the counting house. Not the vault. The *salt cellar*. Do you understand?"

He looked at her the way juniors looked at her when she explained compound interest: as if she were mad but mathematically correct. "Yes, mistress Almeida."

"Good." She checked the wicks in the reading lamps. She checked the fire buckets, full, positioned exactly where regulation demanded. Then, because the prophecy had specified *burning*, she moved two of the buckets closer anyway, and hated herself for the superstition, and moved them back, and then left one where she'd moved it, because if the world insisted on following a hundred-year-old script, the least she could do was improvise a stagehand's intervention.

The moon cleared the eastern towers at the turn of the hour bell.

Emrys felt it before she saw it. It came up through the floor first—a vibration in the soles of her boots, like the hall itself clearing its throat. The lamps guttered, all nine of them, though no window stood open. The air pressure changed; her ears popped. And in the reading room behind her, the prophecy book—which Corvane had ordered chained inside an iron reliquary case, triple-locked, warded with sigils that cost more per character than Pell earned in a year—began, distinctly, to hum.

"Pell," she said calmly. "Salt cellar."

He ran. Good boy.

Emrys stayed. Later she would examine this decision with the same cold arithmetic she applied to everything, and she would find the sum honest: she could not run from a contract with her name signed in it. Every mercenary knew what happened to those who defaulted. The collection was always worse than the debt.

The humming became a chord. The chord became a voice—not sound, precisely, but meaning pressing directly against the inside of her skull, the way her god's silences used to feel, except this was the opposite of silence. This was language with mass.

*The hall shall burn.*

"No," Emrys said, out loud, to a century-old book. "Absolutely not. I've audited this building. I know where the fire wards are buried."

The lamp nearest the west stacks flared green, then white, then simply *left*, its flame leaping sideways onto a shelf of bottled storms as if called by name. And the storms answered.

What happened next happened in the order written, which was somehow worse than chaos. The glass jars cracked in sequence, oldest first, releasing a decade of hoarded weather into a room built of paper. Wind hit the shelves. A lightning-strike-in-a-bottle, guild inventory number 4471, acquired from a dying storm-mage who had sworn it harmless, discharged itself across the rafters and caught the resin sealant in the ceiling joints—the same sealant, Emrys realized with the distant, horrified clarity of an auditor watching fraud unfold, that had been installed ninety-nine years ago. Almost as if someone had planned the materials a century in advance.

Flame walked along the ceiling like a hand opening finger by finger.

Emrys did not scream. Screaming was for people whose ledgers balanced. She grabbed the fire bucket she'd moved—her small rebellion, now looking less like superstition and more like a joke the universe had allowed her to play on herself—and threw it, and the water turned to steam before it reached the flames, and the steam turned to smoke, and the smoke did not rise.

It *gathered*. It gathered the way ink gathers in water, the way debts gather interest: patiently, totally, with intent. It poured down from the burning ceiling and coiled around her ankles, her wrists, her throat, warm as breath and smelling of old vellum and something sweeter beneath—myrrh, maybe, or the memory of myrrh. The heat fell away from her skin as though she had been marked exempt.

*Carried through smoke,* the book had promised.

"I know," Emrys said, coughing. "I *read* it."

The smoke pulled. There was no other word for it. The floor tilted—or she tilted, or the world reclassified her position—and the ledger hall, her hall, with its ten thousand shelves and its lamplight and its careful cold arithmetic, folded away like a page being turned. The last thing she saw was the prophecy book, unchained, its iron reliquary split open like a hatched egg, its pages fanning themselves in the firelight, turning toward the entry that described her, as if checking her off.

Then: falling. Then: not.

* * *

She landed the way a sentence lands when the speaker is interrupted—mid-word, mid-breath, mid-*sentence*, because that was the truth of it, she understood immediately: she had been inserted into the prophecy at whatever point its text had reached, and the text, apparently, had been in the middle of describing her arrival.

Stone flags, cold through her boots. Incense thick enough to chew. Her knees hit marble; her palms followed; the copied page spilled from her coat and skated across the polished floor to stop against the toe of a boot.

A guard's boot. Polished black leather, silver buckle stamped with a crowned tower.

Around her, drawn steel sang out of a dozen scabbards at once.

Emrys lifted her head. The throne room of Castle Veyra opened above and around her like the interior of a frozen wave—vaulted, gilded, hung with banners of a kingdom she had only ever handled as collateral: the free cities' neighbor, Aldric's realm, the shield between the free cities and whatever gnawed at the world's eastern edge. Torchlight ran along walls carved with a dynasty's worth of kings, each face chiseled serene, each serenity now shadowed by soot-dark streaks of age. Candles burned in their hundreds and none of them flickered, which her professional instincts flagged instantly as wrong. Flame always flickered. Air always moved. Unless the air itself had been instructed otherwise.

"Down! Weapons down—" A sergeant's bark. "She appeared from *nothing*, I'm telling you, I was watching the doors—"

"She came out of the smoke, look at the smoke—"

There was smoke here too. Thin threads of it, rising from nothing, from the exact place where she knelt, dissipating like the signature on a finished letter. Twelve guards formed a crescent around her, swords leveled, faces doing their best to be stone and mostly failing. Young men. Two women. One of them, near the end of the crescent, a tall guard with grey eyes and a jaw set hard enough to crack walnuts, was not looking at Emrys at all.

She was looking at the throne. And her sword arm was trembling.

Emrys got to her feet slowly, hands open and empty, the universal grammar of *I am not worth the steel*. Her mind, traitorously calm, began cataloguing: exits, four visible; guards, twelve plus the sergeant, thirteen; wards in the stonework, faintly luminous seams running beneath the flagstones in patterns she recognized from guild contracts—protection lattice, oath-bound, expensive, and recently *re-tuned*, the sigil-work slightly fresher than the stone around it.

Someone had renovated this room's defenses within the last year. Someone with money and fear.

"In the name of King Aldric," the sergeant said, "you are under arrest for sorcery, trespass, and—by the gods, what *is* that smell—"

"Vellum," Emrys said. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Burning vellum. You'll want to have the tapestries checked; smoke clings to silk." She straightened her coat. Somewhere in her chest her heart was performing percussion, but her face had been trained by thirty years of telling wealthy people bad news about their investments, and her face did not betray her. "Gentlemen. Ladies. Before anyone does anything regrettable, I suggest you send for whoever manages your king's contracts. I believe I'm expected."

The word *expected* landed in the room like a dropped coin. Heads turned—not toward her. Toward the throne.

Emrys turned too, and the terror she had been holding off with procedure and inventory finally arrived, all at once, the way the moonrise had.

King Aldric of Veyra sat above the dais steps on a throne of black oak and silver, and he was the most beautiful ruin she had ever seen. Even from across the hall she could see it: the hollowed cheeks, the skin with the translucency of good parchment held to light, the hands resting on the armrests with the perfect, terrible stillness of a man who has decided blinking is optional. He wore the crown well, in the sense that a nail wears a picture frame. His eyes were open. His eyes were fixed on her.

And behind his eyes, something else was looking back.

She had dealt with possessed things before—every senior guild member had, eventually; impossible trade attracts impossible clients. Possession had a texture. It had a *crowding*, a friction, two wills rubbing in one skull. This was not that. This was worse. This was smoothness. Whatever sat behind King Aldric's gaze fit there so perfectly, so seamlessly, that a stranger would see only a tired king, perhaps ill, perhaps grieving, and pity him.

But Emrys had spent a decade keeping books for a god who never answered, and she had learned to hear the shape of silence. Aldric's silence had a shape. It was the shape of a mouth held shut from the inside.

"Emrys Almeida," the king said.

His voice was his own—warm, resonant, a voice built for coronation oaths—and that was the worst part, hearing how beautiful the instrument was while knowing the player. The guards lowered their swords a fraction, reassured by mere familiarity. The grey-eyed guard lowered hers not at all.

"You know my name," Emrys said.

"I know everything in the book." Aldric smiled. It reached every part of his face except anywhere that mattered. "You are punctual. The text said moonrise whole, and the moon rose whole, and here you are. I do admire punctuality in a contractor. So few of your profession respect the terms." He rose, unhurried, robes settling around him like poured glass. "Kneel, if you would. Or stand. It makes no difference to me. Formality is for the living."

Behind him, unnoticed by any guard, the candles nearest the throne had begun to burn with a faint inner gleam, a light like light passing through crystal. As Aldric descended the dais steps, Emrys saw it catch in the skin of his hand—a glint, refractive, structural. Glass spreading beneath the surface like frost claiming a window, hairline by hairline, patient as interest.

*Something wearing the crown.* The copied page in her pocket felt suddenly hot.

"Your Majesty appears unwell," she said.

"His Majesty is extremely well." The smile again, wider now, using muscles the king perhaps had never used, arranged with an apprentice's understanding of how smiles worked. "That is rather the point. For one hundred years I have been well. I have been *patient*. And now the book has delivered me its next chapter, right on schedule, and she has excellent handwriting, and she keeps her oaths badly, which is the most useful quality in the world." He stopped three steps above her, close enough that she could see the crown's reflection doubled in his pupils—one reflection where a pupil's reflection should be, and beside it another, sharper, colder, like the glint off a blade. "You are wondering what I am."

"I'm wondering what you're offering," Emrys said. "Everything offers something. That's the first law of trade."

For one instant—less than an instant, a flicker, a single dropped frame—the thing in the king's face went still with something that looked almost like delight. Almost like recognition. Behind her, she heard the grey-eyed guard's breath catch.

"Ohh," Aldric breathed. "*There* she is. The auditor. Yes. Yes, we will do business, you and I." He extended one glass-flecked hand, palm up, courteous as any merchant. "But negotiations are tiresome with an audience, and the hour grows short, and the moon is already descending from its whole. Come. Walk with me. We have terms to discuss—your guild's silence, your god's silence, and the small matter of what you are owed."

He turned and swept back toward the shadows beyond the throne, and the guards parted for him like water, and Emrys stood alone on the flagstones with a dozen blades pointed loosely at her back and a choice assembling itself in her chest out of terror and fury and something harder than either.

Every instinct screamed *run*. But she had learned tonight that the prophecy did not offer exits, only entrances.

Emrys Almeida smoothed her coat, lifted her chin, and walked after the thing that wore her neighbor-king's face—past the guards, past the frozen candles, past the tall grey-eyed soldier whose sword had not lowered and whose lips, barely moving, shaped two silent words meant only for her:

*Not alone.*

The Regent paused at the shadowed archway, half-turned, glass catching torchlight along its jaw.

"Now then," it said, in Aldric's lovely ruined voice. "Let us speak of price."
