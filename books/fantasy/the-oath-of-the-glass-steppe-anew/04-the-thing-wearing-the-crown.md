## 4. The Thing Wearing the Crown

The throne room of Castle Veyra smelled of cold stone and older lies.

Emrys arrived the way smoke leaves a fire—backward, unwillingly, dragged through a seam in the air that closed behind her with a sound like a ledger being slammed shut. She came out of nothing into a wash of torchlight, and her boots met marble so polished it showed her reflection twice: once below her, and once, faintly, in the vast glass panes that lined the eastern wall like a wall of frozen water.

She did not scream. Screaming was for people who had not spent ten years cataloguing bottled storms. What she did instead was take inventory.

Marble floor, black and white in a checkerboard worn soft at the edges by a century of processions. Banners of Veyra's blue stag hanging motionless in air that should have moved them. Braziers burning without smoke—warded flame, guild-made if she was any judge; she'd inventoried enough of them to know the particular amber of a Vellum contract burning steady. Two dozen guards in the blue-and-silver of the royal house, pikes at rest. And at the far end of the hall, raised on seven steps of white stone, a throne of antlered iron with a king sitting in it.

King Aldric. She knew his face from the guild's portrait ledgers—the way she knew every face that mattered to the free cities' trade: as a line item. A young king, crowned young, widowed younger. The portraits showed him with kind eyes and a jaw set like a man used to carrying weight he hadn't chosen.

The man on the throne had the face but not the eyes. His eyes were the color of weathered glass, green-grey and faintly cloudy, and they did not blink when he should have blinked. They did not move when his head turned. It was like watching someone wear a face the way a hand wears a glove—competently, from the inside out, with all the warmth pressed flat.

*There you are,* Emrys thought, and hated how calm the thought was. *There is the thing my book wrote about.*

"Emrys Almeida," said the king—or said something using the king's mouth, because the voice was wrong too. Aldric's voice, by every account, was low and warm. This voice came out warm the way a hearth is warm after the house has burned down: technically true, entirely hollow. "Keeper of Ledgers. Auditor of Impossibilities. You are one hundred years early."

"I'm told I'm exactly on time," Emrys said. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. Somewhere beneath the terror—which was real, which sat in her stomach like swallowed river-stone—was the mercenary's oldest reflex: *when you cannot fight and cannot flee, negotiate.* "Your Majesty. Or whoever keeps your hours."

A ripple went through the guards. Not fear exactly. Something more uncomfortable than that—the discomfort of men who had learned not to hear their king speak strangely, and were now hearing it anyway because a stranger had made it impossible to unhear.

One guard flinched. Emrys marked her the way she'd mark an error in an inventory: automatically, permanently. A woman, captain's knots on her shoulder cord, dark hair cropped short beneath her helm, standing at the throne's right hand. Everyone else in the hall had arranged their faces into practiced blankness. This woman's face had cracked—one heartbeat of naked horror before she sealed it again—and in that heartbeat her eyes had gone to Aldric with something that was not loyalty and not fear but grief.

Grief. For the king. As if somewhere under all that glass, she still believed there was someone to grieve for.

Interesting. Emrys filed her away and turned back to the thing on the throne.

"You appeared from nothing," the Regent said through Aldric's mouth. It tilted the king's head, and the motion had a quality Emrys had seen only once before—in a puppet show in Salt Harbor, where the puppeteer was very good and slightly bored. "My guards find this alarming. I find it *informative*. The guild taught its keepers passage-rites once. I had thought that art lost."

"The guild teaches many things it doesn't advertise." Emrys let her gaze wander as she spoke, casual as a buyer browsing. There—a hairline seam of light along the baseboards, running the length of the hall: a ward-lattice, old and dense, threaded through the stone itself. And there, where the light thickened around the throne steps, seven anchors, one per step, feeding upward into the seat itself. The Regent wasn't just wearing the crown. The whole throne room was built around it like a harness. "I'm told the guild also taught things it has since forgotten it knows."

"Yes," the Regent said softly. "It has. That is rather the point."

It rose. The motion was Aldric's body moving correctly—every joint bending as joints bend—but assembled wrong, the way a sentence is wrong when every word in it is real. It descended the seven steps slowly, and the guards parted without being ordered, and Emrys stood her ground because running would tell it everything about her that she wanted hidden.

Up close, the wrongness had a smell. Glass heated by sun. Ozone, faintly, like the air after lightning. And underneath both, something almost sweet—the smell of a candle shop, of tallow and honey, the smell she had come to associate over ten silent years with prayers that went nowhere.

"Do you know what I am?" it asked.

"A parasite," Emrys said. "Glass and memory. Bound once, badly, and loose again." She had decided on the walk across the marble—three seconds, no more—to play it straight. Mercenaries survived by knowing their market value and quoting it without flinching. "And I know what you want, because everything wants the same two things. To be fed, and to be left alone. So let's talk terms."

Something flickered behind Aldric's glassy eyes. Surprise, perhaps. Or appetite. With this creature she suspected the two were neighbors.

"Terms," it repeated, tasting the word.

"I appeared in your throne room in front of two dozen witnesses," Emrys said. "You can't explain me, and explanations breed questions, and questions breed priests, and priests are the one flavor you can't digest—am I right? Faith sits in your mouth like ash. That's why you emptied the temples first, wherever you've been before here. That's why the shrines in the lower city have been quietly decommissioned these past three years, and everyone blamed budget cuts." She had read those budget reports. She read everything. It had never once felt, until tonight, like reading the menu of a predator.

"You have done your homework," the Regent said.

"I keep ledgers. Homework is my trade." She folded her hands, a merchant's posture, unhurried. "Here are mine. One: the guild records nothing of what it saw tonight. No entry, no rumor, no whisper sold downriver to the information brokers in Coriel. Silence, certified and stamped. Two: safe conduct out of Veyra whenever I choose, no tail, no accidents on the road. Three—" and here she let herself smile, thin and professional, "—payment. Because silence from the Vellum Guild is never free, and you know it, or you wouldn't be negotiating."

"And what price does silence carry tonight?"

"Names," Emrys said. "Yours. Your history. What you were before you were a crown-shaped appetite. Where you've been since Saint Verity broke you—and yes, I know that name, so don't waste the king's lovely face pretending surprise."

The pause that followed lasted four full seconds. In it, Emrys heard her own heart counting, and heard too—faintly, impossibly—from somewhere very deep inside the thing wearing the king, a sound like knuckles dragging against the inside of a coffin lid. She chose to believe she imagined it. She was almost certain she did not.

"Verity," the Regent said at last, and the name came out of Aldric's throat like a splinter coming out. "The saint. The oathbreaker. The woman who taught me what hunger costs." Its head tilted again, that puppeteer's tilt. "You bargain well, Keeper. Better than the last one who stood where you stand. But you are negotiating from a single assumption—that you will leave this room alive to spend whatever I pay you."

"Everyone negotiates from that assumption," Emrys said. "Most just don't say it aloud. It's bad form."

Somewhere among the guards, someone made a small involuntary sound—half laugh, half cough, strangled instantly. The captain with the grief in her eyes did not make a sound at all, but her shoulders had changed. Emrys tracked it without turning her head. Shoulders drop a quarter inch when a person decides, secretly, that they are on your side.

"You are either very brave," the Regent said, "or very expendable."

"I'm very well-insured," Emrys said. "Which looks identical from the outside."

It studied her then, truly studied her, and she felt the attention like a lamp held close to paper—like something reading her, leaf by leaf. She kept her face open and her thoughts shut, and behind her shut thoughts she counted ward-anchors: seven at the dais, four in the pillars, one humming beneath the floor directly under her feet, and a lattice-seam running up the eastern glass wall to a keystone above the throne where all the lines converged. Every fortress has a knot. You just have to find where the string is tied.

"You will have your silence," the Regent said finally. "Certified. Stamped. The guild respects stamps; I remember when it invented them." It smiled with Aldric's mouth, and the smile fit the face beautifully, and that was the worst thing yet. "And I will pay in names, as you ask. Here is the first, freely given: the god you pray to, Keeper—the quiet one, the one who has not answered in ten years—it did not go silent."

The floor did not tilt. The braziers did not gutter. Nothing in the room changed at all except everything.

"It was silenced," the Regent said, savoring each syllable like a man drawing out a fine wine he intends to spit out. "And I know who holds the cork. Bring me what I ask—not names, Keeper, something better. Bring me the guild's founding charter, the original, the one written in blood-ink on vellum that predates its own paper. You've seen it. I can smell it on you. Bring it to me within nine days, and I will give you back your god's voice."

Nine days. The number dropped into her mind and rang there like a coin on marble.

"You're lying," she said, because lying was cheaper than letting her voice shake.

"I am many things," the Regent said, "but I have never once lied about gods. Gods are the only honest currency left." It turned, ascending the seven steps, settling back onto the throne with that terrible correct-wrong grace. "Nine days, Emrys Almeida. The charter, in exchange for a voice. Consider it. Consider what a decade of unanswered prayer weighs, and what you would pay to end it."

Then, to the hall at large, in a different register—warm, kingly, hollow in the way a bell is hollow—"Captain Dain. See our guest to the gate. Gently."

* * *

Sera Dain walked her out through corridors that got colder as they got farther from the throne room, though Emrys suspected the cold was not in the stones.

They walked six turns in silence. Sera's gauntlets creaked softly at her sides, and her breathing had the deliberate evenness of a woman counting her way past something. At the seventh turn—a narrow gallery hung with portraits of dead Veyran kings, all of whom looked, Emrys thought, like men who had died tired—Sera stopped.

"Say it quickly," Emrys said, not turning around. "Whatever it is. Hallways in this castle listen. I counted four listening-wards between the throne room and here, and I'm only good at counting, not at silencing."

Sera exhaled through her nose. "You're going to die here," she said quietly. "In nine days, or sooner. Everyone who bargains with it dies, and worse—they *agree* first. They agree, and agreeing is how it gets its hooks in. Every deal it makes is a thread, and it weaves." Her voice stayed level, soldier-flat, but underneath it ran something raw. "So don't agree."

"Too late. I agreed to consider."

"Then unconsider it fast." Sera stepped around to face her, and up close her eyes were grey-brown and exhausted and fiercely awake—the eyes of someone who had been watching a horror alone for years and had just discovered the horror could be spoken of. "Ten years I've served in this castle. Four of them under *that*. I've watched it eat councilors' certainties, chaplains' faith, a whole embassy's memory of ever having crossed our border. And every night it takes pieces of me too—little ones. My mother's face went first. Then the name of my home village. Then why I took this post at all." Her hand went, briefly, to her breastplate, where something lay hidden beneath—flat, book-shaped. "So I write everything down. Every night. Everything I can prove happened. Because the journal remembers when I can't."

Emrys went very still.

A journal. Kept against nightly erasure. By a woman sworn to protect the very king the eraser wore.

"You feel it rewriting you," Emrys said slowly, "because oaths resist. Yours is an oath to protect the king—and it's trying to eat your loyalty to him, and the oath fights back, and the fight is what you feel as loss."

Sera stared at her. "Yes," she said. "That's—I've never been able to put words to it. That's exactly what it feels like. Like being robbed by someone who has to reach past a dog to do it."

"The Regent feeds on sworn loyalty," Emrys said. The thought assembled itself as she spoke, ledger-cold, ledger-clear. "Oaths are its food. It eats them out of people, and what's left is obedience without love. Which means every sworn soldier in this castle is a meal on a schedule." She met Sera's eyes. "Including you. Especially you—you've got a strong oath and a strong spine. You're not a snack, Captain. You're a feast it's saving."

For a moment neither of them moved. Torchlight guttered in the gallery, throwing the dead kings' faces into soft motion, as if they stirred in their frames at the conversation.

"I signaled you," Sera said at last. "Back there. I don't know if you saw. When it smiled with him—with *his* mouth—I couldn't stand it anymore, four years of standing it, and I thought: if this stranger sees it too, then I'm not mad, and if I'm not mad then maybe there's a way through this that isn't just enduring until it finishes eating me." She drew a slow breath. "So. Are you going to bring it its charter, Keeper?"

"No," Emrys said.

"Are you going to tell me why?"

"Because I think I already know what happens in nine days, and it isn't a delivery." She glanced down the gallery, at the faint seam of ward-light threading the wainscoting, and lowered her voice to almost nothing. "But I need to see your journal, Captain. Tonight, if you can manage it. And I'll show you mine."

Sera's brow creased. "Your what?"

"My ledger," Emrys said. "Where I wrote down, word for word, what a hundred-year-old prophecy book says happens next." She allowed herself a grim, thin smile—the first since she'd fallen out of the sky and into this glass-walled kingdom. "You keep records to stay yourself. So do I. Let's compare accounts, Captain, and see whether the numbers agree."

Above them, faint through the vaulted stone, the great bells of Castle Veyra began to toll the midnight hour—and on the ninth stroke, Emrys would swear afterward, the sound bent, just slightly, as if something enormous had leaned against the world to listen.
