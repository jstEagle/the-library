## 5. Sera's Oath, Broken Smaller

The barracks smelled of tallow and wet stone, the way all of Castle Veyra smelled after dark—as though the mountain itself were breathing out through its walls, slow and mineral and old. Sera Dain sat on her cot with a candle stub cupped between her boots and a book open on her knees that no one in the castle knew existed.

It was not a fine book. It was a soldier's ledger, actually—salvaged from a dead quartermaster's effects three years ago, its columns half-filled with grain tallies she'd never bothered to finish. The rest of it was hers. Every night, before sleep took her, she wrote down what had happened that day. Not what she remembered. What she *wrote*.

There was a difference now. There had been a difference for eleven months.

She wrote: *The king held court today. He smiled at Lady Vessane twice. The second smile came too late, after she'd already finished speaking. He laughed at nothing during the petition of the millers' guild.*

Then she stopped, because the pen had begun to shake.

*That is wrong,* she added beneath it, pressing hard enough to score the page. *I know it is wrong. I remember knowing it was wrong this morning. By tomorrow morning I will have written this and I will believe it.*

She blew out the candle and lay back and did not sleep so much as fall into it like a woman lowering herself into cold water, inch by inch, hating every degree.

* * *

They came for her an hour past midnight.

Not with torches—not with the clangor of arrest—but with a soft knock, three beats, then a pause too long to be official. Sera was on her feet with a knife before the second knock finished, because a captain of the Veyran guard learned early that the dangerous hours were the quiet ones, and because for eleven months she had been waiting for exactly this knock.

"Captain Dain." A voice through the door, low, pitched to carry only as far as the wood. "You flinched yesterday. In the throne room. When the king spoke."

Sera said nothing. Her heart was going like a drum in a retreat.

"You looked at me," the voice went on, "and you looked away, and you made a decision about me in under a second, and you were right to make it. I'm coming in or I'm not. But if I'm not, we're both going to keep being alone in this castle, and I've done eleven months of alone, and I can tell from your face that you've done less than a week of it and already hate it more."

A long silence. Then, quietly, almost against her own judgment, Sera unbarred the door.

Emrys Almeida slipped inside like smoke given errands. She still wore the traveling leathers she'd arrived in—arrived in *out of nothing*, mid-sentence, in front of the whole court—and she moved through the dark barracks room the way Sera imagined a cat moves through a room full of sleeping dogs: entirely aware of every one of them, entirely unbothered by the odds. Her eyes went to the ledger on the cot, and stayed there.

"You write things down," Emrys said. It wasn't a question.

Sera's hand drifted toward the knife again. "How—"

"The way you checked your memory against the wall when the Regent spoke. You weren't listening. You were *verifying*. I do that with ledgers." She nodded at the book. "May I?"

"No," Sera said, and then, because eleven months of solitude is a weight that breaks hinges, "Yes. Sit down. Touch nothing else."

Emrys sat. Sera lit the candle again and hated how much light suddenly felt like a risk.

"I swore an oath," Sera said, before she could decide not to say it. The words came out of her like water through a cracked dam—eleven months behind them, pushing. "When Aldric was crowned. Seven years ago. Hand on the crown itself, before the assembled court, before the gods' alcove. To protect the king. Body, mind—" her jaw tightened— "*and memory.* That was the wording. Nobody thought about the wording. Why would they? It was ceremonial. It was *pretty.*"

"And now?" Emrys asked.

"Now something edits me." Sera heard her own voice go flat, which was how she kept it from going anywhere worse. "Every night. Or every time it matters. I'll stand guard and I'll see something—the king's shadow moving wrong, or his voice coming from a throat that isn't moving—and by the time I finish my watch I remember deciding it was nothing. I remember *deciding*. There's a whole little story in my head about why I decided it, complete and reasonable, like someone wrote it for me while I wasn't looking." She tapped the ledger. "Except I write everything down first. Before sleep. Because whatever it is, it works on the remembering, not the writing. Paper keeps. Paper doesn't get edited."

Something moved across Emrys Almeida's face—not sympathy, exactly. Recognition. The particular expression of a professional encountering another professional's work and finding it sound.

"How far gone are you?" Emrys asked.

"I don't know." Sera laughed, and it came out ugly. "That's the joke, isn't it? I can't audit my own head. I read last month's entries sometimes and I don't remember writing them. I read them and it's like reading a stranger's letters. A stranger who was scared of exactly what I'm scared of, who noticed exactly what I notice, and who I have to take on faith."

She turned pages without wanting to. Showed Emrys, because showing someone was itself a kind of relief, the first in nearly a year.

*March 4th. The king wept at supper. Real tears. For four seconds he was in there, behind the glass, and he mouthed something at Captain Oren. Oren reported the next day that the king had been "in excellent spirits." Oren has served twelve years. Oren does not lie.*

*April 19th. I dreamed of the coronation. In the dream the crown was warm. Crowns aren't warm. I woke up fond of the Regent. I woke up FOND of it. Burned that feeling off with drill until noon.*

*June 2nd. Found myself standing outside the king's chambers at 3 a.m. with no memory of leaving my bed. My hand was raised to knock. Written here so tomorrow-me believes tonight-me: DO NOT KNOCK. WHATEVER YOU THINK YOU HEARD IN THERE CALLING YOUR NAME, IT WAS NOT THE KING.*

Emrys read without touching the pages. When she reached the June entry she went very still, the stillness of a person doing arithmetic they don't want finished.

"Captain Oren," she said. "Twelve years of service. Does he still report the king's moods?"

"He reports whatever the Regent wants reported." Sera closed the book. "Oren isn't compromised the way I am. He doesn't fight it, so he doesn't feel it. It takes the loyal ones slowly, and the truly loyal ones—" her throat worked— "the truly loyal ones it eats fastest. I figured that out around April. I got fond of it once. Do you understand what that means? It *drank* something out of me and left fondness in the glass."

The candle guttered. Neither of them moved to fix it.

"It eats oaths," Sera said. "That's what I think. That's what the journal says if you read it right. It doesn't feed on fear or flesh or any of the things monsters are supposed to want. It feeds on sworn loyalty. On the thing that binds a guard to a king, a priest to a god, a soldier to a banner. It drinks the binding itself. And what's left over—" she spread her hands— "is a man like Oren. Compliant. Content. Empty where the oath used to be, and grateful for the emptiness."

* * *

Emrys Almeida pulled a ledger from her coat.

Of course she did. Sera would think about that later—the absurdity of it, the mercenary sitting in a guarded barracks at one in the morning producing a bound book of accounts the way other people produced a knife, and the two objects serving the same purpose exactly.

"This is mine," Emrys said. "Eleven years of contracts. Every oath I've taken as guild business, recorded, priced, and witnessed." She opened it flat beside Sera's journal. "If yours says what I think it says, mine should say it too. From the other side. If it eats oaths, then somewhere in the record there should be a pattern. Consumption. Intake. Something a merchant could read."

Sera stared at her. "You're going to *audit* it."

"I'm going to audit it," Emrys agreed, with the first thing resembling warmth she'd shown since arriving out of thin air. "Everything can be audited. That's either the most comforting truth in the world or the most horrifying one, depending on which side of the ledger you're standing on."

They worked until the candle burned down to a coin of wax. Sera gave her dates; Emrys matched them against court records she'd memorized in two days of predatory observation—public ceremonies, feasts, oaths of fealty renewed by the city garrison each season, the swearing-in of new guards. And the pattern, when it surfaced, surfaced the way patterns do: all at once, horribly obvious, undeniable as arithmetic.

Every major ceremony of loyalty preceded a change in the Regent's behavior. After the garrison's seasonal oath, the king's glassiness deepened for days. After the knighting of six young officers in February, the Regent had spoken through Aldric with a fluency it hadn't managed in weeks prior—Sera remembered the court whispering that His Majesty seemed *rested*. After the priests of the Alcove renewed their vows at midwinter, the thing behind the king's eyes had walked the halls unblinking for three nights, and three guards had requested transfer and couldn't say why.

And the guards who changed—Oren, the chamberlain, half the inner circle—they'd all sworn directly to the crown. The deeper the oath, the faster the hollowing.

"It's not random," Sera said, and her voice sounded strange to her own ears, small in the stone room. "It's never been random. It's a feeding schedule."

"It's a *harvest calendar*," Emrys said quietly. "And someone built it."

Sera looked at her. "You knew something like this. Before you came here. That's why you appeared in the throne room like a struck match. That prophecy—"

"I know what tonight's date says in a hundred-year-old book," Emrys said. "I know it names me. I don't yet know why, and I don't trust anything I don't understand, including my own arrival." She closed her ledger. "But Captain—Sera—here's what I trust. Two independent records agreeing. Your journal and my books tell the same story from opposite ends. That's corroboration. In my trade, corroboration is the closest thing to truth that paper offers."

Sera felt something crack open in her chest—a hairline fracture, painless, terrifying. Eleven months she had been the only sane woman in a madhouse, keeping a dead man's ledger alive by candlelight, unable to tell anyone, unable even to trust the self who woke each morning. And now a stranger who fell out of a burning hall was reading her private terror like a merchant reads freight, nodding, *corroborating*, treating the unthinkable as a line item to be verified.

"You believe me," Sera said. It came out rougher than she meant. "You've known me four hours and you believe me."

"I believe your handwriting," Emrys said. "It's consistent across eleven months. Pressure, slant, letterforms—all stable. People whose memories are rewritten write differently when the rewriting is fresh. Yours isn't fresh. Which means whatever it's doing to you, it hasn't gotten to the part of you that holds a pen." She stood, tucking the ledger away. "That part of you is the oath it can't digest yet. Remember that. However much it eats, there's a kernel it's choking on."

"Why?"

"Because a true oath isn't just loyalty," Emrys said, at the door, in the grey before dawn. "It's a debt. And debts have two ends. Whoever holds the other end of yours—" her eyes flicked upward, toward the royal wing, toward the thing wearing her king— "still has a claim on you. It can drink the loyalty. It can't erase the creditor."

She paused with her hand on the bar.

"One more thing. Tomorrow, when it speaks through him—it watches me when it wears him. It knows I know. So from now on, we're strangers. You report anything odd about me, promptly and loudly. Make it convincing. You've seen me talking to servants I shouldn't. You suspect my contracts."

Sera stared. "You want me to *betray* you."

"I want you to stay *employed*," Emrys said. "An oathbreaker on the inside is worth ten martyrs at the gate. Besides—" and there was something almost gentle in the merchant's flat voice— "you've already broken smaller, haven't you? Every night, when you write the truth instead of the edited version. Every morning, when you choose to believe the page over your own head. You've been breaking its rewrite of you for eleven months, one candle at a time." She slid the bar free. "Keep breaking it small, Captain. Small breaks don't show. And when the big one comes—we'll do it together."

The door closed. The candle died. Sera Dain sat alone in the grey light with her ledger open on her knees, and for the first time in eleven months, she was not alone with it.

She picked up the pen. Her hand was steady.

*Met the mercenary tonight. She believes the page over the memory, same as me. We compared books. It eats oaths—it eats the loving part and leaves the obeying part. It has a harvest calendar. And the mercenary says my oath has two ends, and the other end is still mine.*

*Tomorrow I denounce her to the court. She asked me to. Written here so tomorrow-me trusts tonight-me: it's a play. Whatever you feel when you say the words, the words are a shield, not a wound. Break it small. Keep breaking it small.*

She signed and dated it, blotted the page, and was reaching for the bar to check it twice—habit now, holy habit—when she heard it.

From the direction of the royal wing, faint but unmistakable, carried up through the mountain's breathing stone: singing. One voice. Aldric's voice—or the throat that wore him—singing a coronation hymn at three in the morning, sweet and slow and perfectly, terribly on-key.

And beneath it, so low she might have invented it, a second voice answering. Thin. Frayed. Coming from somewhere deep under the floors, under the chapel, under the roots of the castle itself.

Counting. Someone down in the dark was counting.

Sera barred the door, sat back down, and added one final line to the entry, pressing so hard the nib tore through:

*It is not the only thing imprisoned in this castle.*
