## 6. What the God Swallowed

Prayer, for Emrys Almeida, had become a form of bookkeeping.

She did not kneel—kneeling was for the devout, and she had long ago stopped pretending to be devout. She did it the way she did everything: standing at her desk in the narrow room the guild had given her at Castle Veyra's guest wing, a single candle burning, the borrowed inkwell squared to the desk's edge, and the day's accounts balanced before she allowed herself anything so extravagant as speech.

*Ledger of prayers outstanding,* she would write in the small private codex she kept beneath the floorboard. *Day 3,641. No reply. Interest accruing.*

Ten years of that. Ten years of addressing a god the way one addresses a business partner who has stopped answering correspondence—not with anger, at least not anymore, but with the flat professional courtesy of someone who has learned that fury changes nothing on the balance sheet.

The god had a name once. She knew it the way every oath-sworn mercenary of the Vellum Guild knew it: as a weight behind the sternum, a warmth like a coin held too long in a closed fist, a presence that used to murmur along the edges of her thoughts when she signed contracts, warning her which clauses were rotten. The guild called it the Underwriter. Its name, spoken aloud, tasted like rain on old vellum, and Emrys had not tasted it since she was nineteen years old and swearing her founding oath with Corvane's hand heavy on her shoulder.

*You will keep the ledgers true,* the god had said—or rather, its voice had moved through the oath itself, through the words as they left her mouth, the way light moves through glass. *And the ledgers will keep you.*

Then silence. Not absence—the warmth behind her sternum remained, dull and banked like a fire raked over with ash—but silence. A decade of it.

So tonight, after Sera Dain had slipped away to her barracks with her hidden journal pressed against her ribs like a second heart, after Emrys had copied out three more pages of the Regent's consumption patterns by candlelight until her eyes ached, she did what she always did. She squared the inkwell. She trimmed the wick. She opened the small codex to a fresh line.

And she prayed out of habit, the way a sailor taps a hull, the way a merchant touches iron.

"Underwriter," she said quietly, to the candle, to the ash-banked warmth behind her breastbone. "Emrys Almeida, account holder. Reporting status. The thing wearing King Aldric eats oaths. I have arithmetic to prove it. I am currently resident inside its mouth, in case your silence is due to simple ignorance rather than contempt."

Nothing. Of course nothing. The candle flame leaned in the draft from the ill-fitted window and righted itself, indifferent.

She wrote: *Day 3,642. Still no reply. Requesting guidance re: parasite of glass and memory currently enthroned above the free cities. Any response appreciated. —E.A.*

She was capping the ink when it happened.

The warmth behind her sternum moved.

Not stirred—*moved*, the way a prisoner shifts against chains, sudden and desperate, and for one vertiginous moment Emrys felt the whole shape of it: something vast folded impossibly small, something that had been screaming into a sealed room for ten years and had just heard, through ten years of stone, the tap-tap-tap of someone knocking.

Then a word.

It did not come as sound. It came the way an entry comes into a ledger—simply present, where a moment before there had been blank space, written in a hand she recognized the way you recognize your own mother's handwriting on an envelope:

***RUN.***

The candle went out.

Emrys sat in the dark with her heart slamming against her ribs and the word sitting in her mind like a coin too hot to hold. Her first instinct—the mercenary's instinct, the survivor's—was to obey. Ten years of silence and the first word her god gave her was an alarm. Gods did not say *run* about nothing.

Her second instinct was the clerk's instinct, and it was stronger.

*Interrogate the source,* Corvane had taught her, back when she'd been young enough to believe his lessons were gifts. *Every document has an author, every author an interest, every interest a price. Never accept a word at face value—audit it.*

So she did not run. She struck flint to steel with hands that were not quite steady, relit the candle, pulled the codex close, and began, in her coldest hand, to interrogate.

*Query one:* Why now?

She thought about it the way she thought about any anomalous entry—a payment arriving years late, a debt surfacing after its debtor's death. A word could arrive now only if two conditions held. First: the sender had just become able to send. Second: the sender had just become aware of something urgent enough to spend whatever it cost to send it.

What had changed? She had changed. Three days ago she'd been pulled through smoke into a throne room; two nights ago she'd found Sera's journal and the terrible symmetry between them; last night she'd lain awake doing arithmetic on the Regent's feeding schedule. And today—

Today she had stood in the gallery above the council chamber and watched the Regent speak through Aldric's mouth, and she had noticed something she hadn't let herself think about directly, because noticing it fully felt like madness.

When the Regent spoke, the warmth behind Emrys's sternum had flinched.

*Query two:* What does the flinch mean?

She wrote it down and stared at it. A god flinching at a parasite's voice. There were only so many reasons a creature flinched at another creature's voice. Fear. Recognition. Or—

Or the flinch of a body remembering the thing that hurt it.

Emrys set down the pen. Her hand, she observed distantly, was shaking.

For ten years she had built a theology out of silence, because silence was the only scripture available. The god had abandoned its sworn. The god had never cared, or had died, or had been a story the guild told to leash its clerks. She had grieved it the way you grieve a parent who walked out—angry, then numb, then done. She had kept praying out of habit and spite, and she had told herself the habit meant nothing.

But a god who had abandoned her could not flinch.

A dead god could not send a word.

Only a *caged* god could do both—only something imprisoned, straining against walls it could not break, hearing her nightly reports for ten years the way a drowning man hears rain on the surface, unable to answer, unable to answer, unable to answer—and then, tonight, finding some hairline crack in those walls wide enough for exactly one word.

*RUN.*

Which raised the question she did not want to ask, so she asked it, because she was a ledger-keeper and ledger-keepers ask the questions that ruin them.

*Query three:* Who built the cage?

She knew the Regent ate oaths. She had the arithmetic—Sera's journal cross-checked against the temple rolls, the thinning loyalties of the sworn soldiers, the rate of consumption plotted against the Regent's visible strength. It fed on sworn loyalty the way a lamp fed on oil. That was established.

Now consider, said the cold clerk-voice in her head, what a creature like that would make of a god. Not a man's oath, not a soldier's fealty—small candles, all of those. But a god? A god was an ocean of sworn loyalty, ten thousand mercenaries bound by founding oaths, centuries of accumulated faith banked like grain in a tower. To a thing that ate promises, a god was not an enemy.

A god was a *larder*.

Emrys stood up so fast her chair went over backward. She didn't notice. She was pacing the narrow room, candle in hand, shadows swinging across the walls, thinking it through because if she stopped thinking it through she would have to feel it.

The timeline fit. The Regent had been bound once before—Sera had said the chapel archives hinted at it, records from a century ago, a binding bought with a sacrifice. A century ago the Regent had been shattered and buried. And ten years ago—ten years ago, not a century, *ten*—the god had gone silent.

Unless the Regent had not been as buried as the histories claimed. Unless something had cracked it loose early. Unless it had spent a decade doing what it did best: swallowing, slowly, patiently, the way a serpent swallows—an egg first, then the bird.

A god did not go silent. Emrys saw it now with terrible clarity, saw it the way she saw a falsified column the moment her eye crossed it. A god did not go silent the way a man does not stop breathing while his heart still beats. Silence was not the god's choice. Silence was a *symptom*. Something had put its hand over the god's mouth. Something had wrapped it in glass and memory and swallowed it whole, and for ten years it had been screaming inside that translucent prison, and every prayer Emrys had logged—3,642 of them, dated, filed, interest accruing—had been landing against the inside of its cage like moths against a window.

It had heard everything. It had been able to say *nothing*.

Until tonight. Until the Regent grew hungry enough, stretched enough, thin enough in some seam of itself, that a crack opened—and the god, feeling Emrys's mind brush past that crack the way she'd brushed past it every night for a decade, had spent everything it had on a single word.

*Run.*

Because the god knew what she had only just deduced. Because the god understood better than anyone alive what the Regent did to things it caught. Because somewhere in the dark of its prison, the Underwriter had felt the Regent's attention turn—had felt the parasite taste the air around Castle Veyra and find, threaded through it, the flavor of a rival larder, a captive god, and beyond the god, reachable through the god, a sworn mercenary with prophecy written in her name—

Run, the god had said. Flee. Get out. I have been eaten and I cannot protect you and it knows your name.

Emrys stopped pacing.

The candle had burned down nearly to the plate. Somewhere below, the castle settled and ticked, full of sleeping soldiers and one sleeping king who was not alone behind his eyes. On the desk lay the codex with its interrogation laid out neat as an autopsy: *query one, query two, query three*, each answered, each answer worse than the last.

Grief came first. It came strangely, sideways—grief not for the silence but for the *misreading* of it. Ten years she had spent resenting a prisoner for not writing. Ten years of cold entries, of *no reply*, of filing her abandonment under *character of correspondent* when it belonged under *crime of third party*. Every bitter prayer had been a letter delivered to a cell. She pressed both fists against her sternum, against the banked warmth there, and felt it press faintly back, faint as a pulse through wool, and something in her chest broke and re-set at a new angle.

And then, rising through the grief like silt settling into mud, colder and heavier: betrayal.

Because grief was for the god. Betrayal was for everyone else.

Corvane. Corvane, who had taught her to interrogate sources, whose lessons had just handed her the truth. The guild traded in impossible things—bottled storms, borrowed years—and the guild had gone on trading, fat and serene, through the exact decade its god had gone missing. Merchants notice when a warehouse burns. Merchants notice when their patron stops signing. Either Corvane had never wondered why the Underwriter went silent—inconceivable; Corvone audited *sunrise*—or he had wondered, and looked into it, and looked away, or worse: he had looked into it and *filed the answer*, and continued business.

Had the guild sold the god? Was that even possible—could you draft a contract for the swallowing of your own divine guarantor, route it through shells and proxies, book the profit under impossible goods? Emrys thought of the deep vaults, the sealed annexes, the acquisitions no junior clerk was cleared to inventory. She thought of how quickly Corvane had ordered the prophecy book destroyed-or-sealed, how fast he'd moved, how *practiced* the motion had been.

Practiced. Like a man executing a contingency drafted long ago.

And underneath the betrayal of Corvane, a colder question coiled, the kind of question that ended careers and dynasties: if the Regent had swallowed her god, and the Regent now sat on the throne of the free cities, and the guild had known about the Regent since at least the moment it started grooming *her*—

Then the silence had never been an absence at all. It had been a cover-up. Her whole decade of faithful, furious, unanswered devotion had been conducted in front of an open grave that her own employers had dug, or paid to have dug, or at very least swept clean and marked *nothing here*.

Emrys picked up the chair. She set it straight. She sat, because this was how she handled the end of the world: she sat down and made an entry.

*Conclusion:* The god did not go silent. The god was silenced. Suspected agent: the entity designated the Regent, current occupant of the crown of Castle Veyra. Possible accomplices: unknown. Suspect list begins with the Vellum Guild's senior partnership.

She looked at that last clause for a long time. Then she wrote beneath it, smaller:

*Note: If the god can be swallowed, it can perhaps be recovered. A swallowed thing sits inside the swallower. Everything I need may already be inside the thing I mean to kill.*

The warmth behind her sternum pulsed—weak, unmistakable, deliberate. Twice. Like a hand squeezing hers. Like *yes*, or like *careful*, or like a prisoner pressing its palm to the wall of its cell because someone outside had finally, finally said *I know you're in there*.

"I'm coming," Emrys whispered—to the god, to the candle, to the honest dark. "I don't run. You should know that by now. You wrote my contract."

The warmth flickered. And within the flicker, faint as a watermark, she felt something else arrive—not a word this time, not even language, but an *image*, pressed against the crack in the walls like a face against frosted glass: a chapel. Stone, old, older than the castle around it. And beneath it, beneath the chapel floor, a downward sense, a weight of sealed air and older paper, a door that no one had opened in a hundred years.

Then the crack closed. The warmth sank back to its banked ember-glow, spent, and Emrys was alone again with her candle and her codex and her new understanding of the world.

But not with her new understanding of herself. That part stayed lit.

She blew out the candle, took up her pen one last time, and wrote tomorrow's first entry by moonlight through the window:

*Day 3,643, anticipated. Objective: locate chapel. Locate door. Interrogate the century. And find out what my masters buried alongside their god.*

Outside, far off toward the horizon, torches were moving in the dark where no torches should be—too many, too orderly, too patient.

Mavros was coming. And Emrys Almeida, mercenary, clerk, oathbreaker-in-waiting, had nine days to dig up a truth her own guild had killed a god to keep buried.

* * *

She was at the chapel doors before dawn, Sera's note in her pocket and frost on the stones, when the warmth behind her sternum pulsed once more—urgent now, rhythmic, almost like a heartbeat counting down—and she understood, with a clerk's terrible clarity, that the god was not saying *run* anymore.

The god was saying *dig*.
