## 7. The Usurper's Vanguard

Councilor Aldous Ives had spent forty-one years learning that the worst news never arrives loudly.

It came, as a rule, in small containers: a sealed tube of vellum carried by a rider whose horse had been lathered white; a junior clerk's hesitation before knocking; the particular quality of silence in a corridor that should have been full of footsteps. The worst news came dressed in procedure, because procedure was how men like him survived it—procedure gave terror a filing system.

So when the horn sounded from the eastern watchtower at half past the third bell of morning, and the sound did not stop—not the three notes of a supply train, not the five of a diplomatic escort, but the long unbroken bray that every councilor learned to dread in his first year—Ives set down his pen, capped his ink, and squared the papers on his desk so that their edges aligned. He allowed himself ten seconds to be afraid. He had found that fear given a fixed allotment behaved better than fear left to wander.

Then he rose, took his staff of office from its stand, and went to tell the king's body that war was coming.

* * *

The council chamber of Castle Veyra had been built to hold argument the way a cistern holds water: deep, cool-walled, and with an acoustics that returned every voice to its speaker slightly changed. Ives had always found it unsettling—the way your own words came back to you sounding older, or more afraid, or more certain than you remembered making them. Today the chamber was already full when he arrived, and full in the wrong way. Councilors stood at their places rather than sitting. No one had lit the brazier. Someone had opened the shutters and the autumn light fell across the long table like something spilled.

"Tell me," Ives said, taking his seat at last, because someone had to sit, and if no one sat then the room would remain a held breath indefinitely.

The Master of Birds, a narrow woman named Osseline whose hands were never still, pushed a message cylinder down the table toward him. It had traveled hard. The wax seal—Mavros's personal sigil, a boar spear driven through a broken coronet—had been stamped over the Veyran seal, deliberately, the way one stamps over a face.

Ives broke it and read aloud, because the chamber needed to hear it in his old steady voice rather than pass it hand to hand like a contagion:

*"To the Council of Regency, Castle Veyra. Let it be known that King Aldric Veyra, son of Marren, is dead."*

He stopped there. Not for effect—he would have given a great deal for it to be effect—but because the sentence required something of him he could not immediately name. Across the table, Councilor Brann's eyes flicked toward the high seat at the end of the room, where the king sat.

King Aldric sat as he always sat these days: upright, hands folded on the armrests of the throne's lesser chair—for he did not take the throne itself except at ceremony—and perfectly, terribly still. His skin had taken on a quality Ives could only describe as lacquered, a faint glassy sheen along the cheekbones and knuckles that the candlelight used to find even now, in daylight. His eyes were open. They were aimed at the middle distance, at nothing, and Ives had long ago stopped meeting them, because once—only once, months ago—he had looked into them and had the distinct sensation of being read the way an auditor reads a ledger: thoroughly, coldly, line by line, looking for discrepancies.

He made himself continue reading.

*"Let it be known that the man who wears his crown in Veyra is not Aldric Veyra, but some thing that has eaten him. I have seen it. I have spoken with survivors of the palace guard who fled. And let it be known further that I, Cassian Mavros, General of the Eastern Marches and blood-kin to the royal line, do hereby claim the regency of all the free cities, until such time as this usurper is burned out of the king's stolen flesh.*

*"My armies march. Eleven thousand sworn. We will arrive within the month. Open your gates and surrender the creature wearing Aldric's face, and the cities will be spared. Refuse, and I will take them gate by gate, street by street, and every soldier who dies defending that thing will have died for a monster.*

*"This is my only offer. — C.M."*

Silence followed, the kind with weight, the kind that pressed on the ears. Then everyone spoke at once.

"—he can't possibly believe—"

"—eleven thousand, gods, the eastern levies were only four thousand strong when—"

"—he's claiming Aldric is *dead*, he's saying it openly, that's treason of the—"

"—and what do we say? That the king is alive? Look at him! *Look* at him!"

That last voice was Brann's, and it cracked in the middle, and Ives watched the words land in the chamber like a dropped glass. Every head turned toward the king. Toward the thing behind the king's eyes, which had not moved, had not blinked, and which now—slowly, with the smoothness of oil finding level—turned its gaze upon the assembled council.

"Thank you, Councilor Ives."

The voice was Aldric's voice. It had always been Aldric's voice, warm and a little rough, the voice of a man who liked bad wine and good arguments. But it came out flattened now, each word placed with a precision no living speaker used, like stones laid without mortar.

"The council thanks you," the Regent said through him. "The message has been received. The council may deliberate."

And the eyes went distant again, and the body resumed its stillness, and forty years of statesmanship told Ives, with sick certainty, that they had just been dismissed from their own council chamber by the furniture.

* * *

They deliberated anyway. What else was there? Procedure was the raft they clung to.

"The gates hold," Brann was saying, pacing. "Veyra's walls held against the Kessarine League with two thousand defenders. We have the garrison, we have the temples' sworn orders—"

"The temples are emptying." This from Osseline, quiet, her fingers working at each other. "You know they're emptying. The Order of the Vigil reported a tenth of their sworn brothers reporting... doubts. Dreams. A feeling that their oaths had gone thin. Thin—that's the word they used. An oath feeling *thin*. What does that even mean?"

"It means whatever sits on that chair is drinking," said Councilor Hesk, who was old enough to say such things and too ill to care about consequences. "It drinks the sworn loyalty around it the way a taproot drinks a well. Mavros isn't wrong, you understand. That's the horror of it. Read the letter again. *Some thing that has eaten him.* He's right. He is entirely right, and he is marching eleven thousand men here to save us from the truth, and we cannot even admit the truth in our own chamber."

"We don't *know*—" Brann began.

"Ives." Hesk turned his clouded eyes on Ives. "You've audited the treasury accounts since the change began. You've seen the requisitions. Tell them."

Ives kept his hands flat on the table. "The palace granary requisitions doubled in the first month after the king's illness," he said. "Not because consumption rose. Because everything the household swallows is brought twice—once to the kitchens, and once to the king's apartments, where it is... presented. Offered. The ledgers record it as *tribute to the crown's health.* I signed off on it because the alternative was asking questions I was not prepared to hear answered."

"You signed off," Osseline repeated softly.

"I filed it," Ives said, and heard his own words come back to him from the cistern walls sounding exactly like what they were: the confession of a man who had chosen his ledger over his king. "I am telling you now so that none of you can later claim ignorance. Whatever we decide in this room, we decide knowing."

The light through the shutters had gone the color of pewter. Somewhere below them, in the city, a temple bell rang noon, and Ives counted the strokes out of habit—seven, eight, nine—and realized with a small cold jolt that the bells had been ringing nine at noon for weeks. The bell-sworn had thinned too. Their count drifted like a clock running down.

"The treaty," said a new voice.

Everyone turned. Councilor Perrin Dane had not spoken all morning; he rarely spoke at all anymore, and Ives had privately begun to wonder whether Perrin still fully inhabited his own skull or had become another piece of well-kept furniture. But he spoke now, and his voice had that same flattened precision Ives had learned to dread.

"There will be terms offered," Perrin said. "When Mavros arrives. Terms from inside. We should discuss them before they are given to us."

"What terms?" Brann asked. "Surrender terms?"

"No." Perrin's eyes were wet and calm. "Peace terms."

* * *

The Regent summoned them to the throne room at dusk.

Ives had attended perhaps two hundred formal audiences in that hall over his career, and he knew its grammar the way he knew the grammar of contracts: where the petitioners stood, where the banners hung, how long the herald's staff struck the floor. He knew, therefore, exactly how wrong the hall was now. The banners had been taken down—or rather, they hung as they always hung, but their colors had drained to a uniform pale grey, as though the dye had been drunk out of them. The great windows were shuttered, though dusk needed no shutting-out. Torches burned in brackets that had stood empty for a century. The air tasted of hot glass.

Aldric sat on the throne itself.

He had not done that since the change. Ives understood, in the wordless way one understands certain things, that this was a statement, and that the statement was being made for the benefit of witnesses—though which witnesses, and to what end, he could not say. The king's body wore full regalia: crown, mantle, scepter laid across his knees. The glassy sheen had spread. It climbed his throat now in fine branching lines, like frost on a window, like cracks in ice that had frozen mid-shatter. When he moved his fingers, Ives could see the light catch and refract beneath the skin of his knuckles.

Behind the throne, where the royal standard should have flown, hung a banner Ives had never seen: a field of white bearing a single upright shard, rendered in silver thread. It had not been there yesterday. He was almost sure it had not been there yesterday.

"Herald," the Regent said, and its own voice served.

There was no herald. The Regent simply waited, and then continued, and Ives understood that it had been announcing itself to a court that existed only in its memory—or its appetite.

"Let it be entered into record," the Regent said, "that the Council of Regency is convened in full, in the presence of the crown, to hear and consider terms of peace."

"*Terms of peace,*" Osseline whispered beside him. "Gods preserve us, it's actually—"

"General Cassian Mavros advances upon the free cities with eleven thousand soldiers bound to him by oath of fealty," the Regent continued, in Aldric's ruined-warm voice, each word laid like masonry. "These soldiers swore their oaths freely, in temples, before witnesses, with full hearts. Their loyalty is the strongest wall between Mavros and reason. It is also," and here the Regent paused, and something moved behind the king's eyes like a fish under lake ice, "nourishment."

No one breathed.

"I propose the following treaty," the Regent said. "One: the free cities shall formally acknowledge the crown's authority over all oaths sworn within their bounds—all of them, civic and sacred alike, past and future. Two: the temples shall transfer custody of their vow-registers to the crown. Three: in exchange, the cities shall be defended. Not by armies. By me. Mavros will not reach the walls. His soldiers' oaths will turn from him before his vanguard crests the eastern ridge, and they will kneel where they stand, and they will be *mine*, and the free cities will know a peace such as they have never known—every citizen bound to every other citizen in perfect concord, perfect loyalty, perfect obedience." The king's mouth smiled. The smile did not involve anything above the cheekbones, where the frost-lines branched. "No more war. No more betrayal. No more councils arguing in cold rooms. Only peace. Only love, of a kind. Only belonging."

The hall was silent except for torch-hiss.

"And the cost," Ives heard himself say.

His own voice surprised him—steady, dry, the auditor's voice, the voice he used when a merchant tried to bury a clause in subtext. He felt every eye in the hall swing to him, including, with a physical weight like a hand on the back of his neck, the eyes of the thing on the throne.

"The cost," the Regent agreed, pleasantly. "Always the cost, with accountants. The cost is consent, Councilor Ives. The citizens will swear. Freely. At a ceremony—a grand one, in the square, with music and bread and their children on their shoulders. They will renew their fealty to their king and to each other, and their oaths will be honored, treasured, *kept*, forever, in perfect health, in the crown's keeping." A pause. "They will not feel a thing. That is my guarantee. I keep what I take. Ask anyone I have ever kept."

*Ives thought of the grey banners. Of the thin bells. Of Sera Dain, whom he had seen in the corridors lately moving like a woman carrying something breakable, checking doorways, counting exits.*

"The alternative," the Regent said, "is General Mavros. Who will burn the lower quarters when the garrison retreats behind the inner wall, because that is what he did at Harrow Ford, and your archives will confirm it if you ask. Who will execute every temple-sworn soldier he captures as an oath-traitor, because his army feeds on captured oaths—it is how he has grown four thousand into eleven thousand in a single season, Councilor; ask yourself where the other seven thousand came from, and what happened to the men who originally held those loyalties. Who will rule you afterward with a spear and a grievance." The Regent leaned forward, and the frost-lines on the king's throat caught the torchlight and threw it back in tiny rainbows, beautiful and obscene. "Two monsters stand at your gates, Councilor. One offers peace. Choose."

*Ives looked at the king's face—the real face beneath, if any of it remained, the face of a man who had once fallen asleep during Ives's budget presentation and apologized afterward with such genuine, boyish embarrassment that Ives had kept the memory like a coin—and he understood, with total clarity, that he was going to have to choose, and that both choices ended with the free cities swallowed.*

"One day," Ives said. "The council requires one day to consider terms of this magnitude. That is procedural law. You wrote the procedural law."

Something flickered across the king's face—there and gone, fast as a bird's shadow. If Ives had not spent forty years reading faces across negotiating tables he would have missed it entirely. It was not the Regent's expression. It was too human, too pained, too brief.

*Aldric,* Ives thought, and his heart slammed once against his ribs. *Whatever is left. Are you in there?*

"One day," the Regent said, settling back, and the human thing was gone as if it had never been. "Granted. Deliberate well. Peace is patient. I am patient. Mavros is neither."

* * *

They gathered afterward in the small map room, the six of them who still trusted one another—which was to say, the six who had not yet noticed themselves becoming furniture—and Ives closed the door and stood with his back against it and finally let the fear out of its allotment.

"Nine days," Osseline said. She had her fingers pressed flat against the map table as if steadying herself against a ship's deck. "Mavros's vanguard could move faster than the letter claims. And the ceremony—if the council consents—the preparations alone—"

"We won't consent," Brann said. "We can't. Feeding every oath in the free cities into that *thing*—"

"And the alternative is eleven thousand men at the gates, and Mavros burning the quarters, and—" Hesk coughed, long and wet, and waved off Osseline's reaching hand. "Two monsters. He said it himself. Two monsters, and us in between, holding the map."

Ives stared down at the map spread beneath his hands: the free cities in careful ink, the eastern marches in fading brown, the little painted soldiers his predecessors had moved across this table for three generations. His predecessors had never had to choose which army of the damned arrived first.

"There's a third party," he said slowly. "At the table. Whether it knows it or not."

Brann frowned. "Who?"

"The guild." Ives straightened. "The mercenary woman—the one who appeared in the throne room out of smoke, the one the prophecy books name. Almeida. She carries the Vellum Guild's ledger. She has spent days walking these halls with an accountant's eyes, same as mine, and I have watched her watch the wards." He pushed away from the door. "The Regent offered her contracts for her silence. Which means the Regent fears what she knows. And Mavros marches on oaths, which means somewhere in his baggage train there are ledgers too—conscription rolls, vow-registers, paper. Paper cuts deeper than swords, gentlemen. I built my career on it."

"You want to put our city's fate in the hands of a mercenary clerk," Hesk said. It was not quite a question.

"I want to put it in the hands of the only person in this castle who reads the fine print," Ives said, "before either monster makes us sign."

Outside the map room's narrow window, the dusk had thickened, and far to the east—so faint it might have been imagination—there was a smudge on the horizon that might have been dust raised by marching feet, or might have been the first smoke of fires yet to come. And beneath it, in the throne room, a king made of glass sat waiting to be fed, while a general made of grievance marched to kill him, and between the two of them walked a woman with a ledger and a god that did not answer, carrying, as yet unknown to herself, the only terms worth signing.

Ives blew out the lamp and went to find her.
