## 8. Ledger Arithmetic

The lamp burned low over Emrys's borrowed desk in the east wing of Castle Veyra, and she did what she always did when the world grew unbearable: she opened a ledger.

The castle had given her a guest chamber that was less a room than an apology—high-ceilinged, drafty, hung with tapestries of hunts that had happened before her grandmother's grandmother drew breath. The desk was walnut, older than the guild, its surface scarred by a hundred years of pens. Someone had left ink, paper, sand for blotting, as if the castle itself expected her to work. She supposed it did. Everyone wanted something accounted.

She laid out three documents side by side.

First: her own notes from the audience with the Regent, taken in merchant's shorthand so tight that only she could read them back—the cadence of the thing's speech, the way Aldric's mouth moved half a beat behind his words like a puppet whose strings had stretched, the exact phrasing of the contracts it offered. *Favorable terms,* it had said through the king's lips, smiling with teeth that belonged to no one. *You are a woman who understands favorable terms.*

Second: Sera Dain's journal. The captain had surrendered it two nights past with hands that shook and eyes that did not—a soldier's contradiction, terror held in formation. It was a small book, oilcloth-bound, the kind of thing a woman could hide in a boot. Inside, in cramped, disciplined script, were entries stretching back four years. Four years of a woman writing herself into existence each morning because something else wrote her out each night.

Third: a fresh page of Emrys's own, ruled into columns, because grief and fear meant nothing until they were made to stand still long enough to be counted.

She dipped her pen. She began.

* * *

The first column was dates. That was easy. Sera's journal began 14th of Thawmonth, four years ago, with an entry so brief it read like a confession whispered into a well: *I don't remember swearing to him but I remember the feeling of having sworn. Is this what faith is?*

Emrys read forward. The early entries were long—whole pages, dense with names and duties and the small textures of a life: which stable boy stole apples, which councilor watered his wine, the way the king laughed at his own jokes before he finished telling them. The king laughed. Emrys marked that. Whatever wore Aldric now, four years ago there had still been laughter enough to record.

Then the entries began to shorten.

Not gradually. In steps. Like a stairway descending into dark water, each landing deeper than the last. By the second year the daily entries had become every-other-day entries, then weekly, then—Emrys turned pages with increasing care—monthly. And the content changed too. The early journal recorded the world outside Sera. The later journal recorded only the self inside her, as if the world had grown too large to hold and the captain had contracted down to the minimum footprint of a soul: *My name is Sera Dain. I swore to protect the king. I do not remember when. My mother's name is—* and here, once, a blank space, and beneath it in different ink, added later, smaller: *found it again. Halden. Her name is Halden.*

Emrys sat back. Outside the narrow window, the moon was rising over the free cities' rooftops, silvering the slate the way frost silvers a field. Somewhere below, the castle guard was changing shifts. Somewhere beyond the walls, General Mavros was doing whatever usurpers did at night—sharpening, praying, counting soldiers the way she counted debts.

She returned to the columns.

The third column she headed *Consumption.* This was the heart of it. If the Regent fed on oaths—if it ate sworn loyalty the way a tapeworm eats a meal—it must eat on some schedule. Nothing fed continuously. Even a fire banked itself between loads of coal. And if Sera's memory-loss came in waves rather than a steady erosion, the waves would have a period. A rhythm. A rate.

She went through the journal entry by entry and marked every instance where Sera described the sensation directly. The vocabulary varied but the substance never did: *hollow morning. Felt scraped. Like waking after someone else slept in my skull. Empty where the oath should sit.* Emrys tallied them, converted irregular dates to days-elapsed, and built a sequence:

Forty-one days between losses. Then thirty-eight. Then thirty-six. Then twenty-nine. Then twenty-two. Then nineteen. Then fifteen. Then twelve.

She stared at the numbers for a long time. The lamp guttered; she trimmed the wick without looking away from the page.

The intervals were shrinking. Not randomly—they were shrinking along a curve she recognized. Any merchant who had ever extended credit knew it. Any ledger-keeper who had watched a debtor slide toward ruin knew it. Interest compounding against a balance that could not grow. The Regent was spending its stolen sustenance faster than it replenished it, and to cover the shortfall it was drawing on Sera—and whoever else it fed upon—more frequently, taking deeper cuts each time, the way a drowning man breathes.

Emrys did the arithmetic that merchants dread and cannot avoid. She extrapolated the curve.

Nine days.

Give or take one, depending on whether the curve bent gently or sharply at the tail—and curves like this bent sharply, they always did, the last collapse was always steeper than the model promised. Nine days, perhaps eight, perhaps seven if the thing had other hosts besides Sera whose depletion accelerated the draw. Nine days until the interval between feedings approached zero. Until the Regent either found a feast vast enough to reset its ledger entirely—

—or starved.

She wrote the two outcomes at the bottom of the page, underlined each once, and looked at them the way she had once looked at a shipping contract that would either make the guild's year or sink it.

Either it feeds enormously, or it weakens past the point of holding its host.

* * *

Sera found her at dawn, because of course she did. Soldiers woke with the light even when they'd been up half the night walking their post twice, checking corners, unlearning whatever the night had tried to write over them.

"You haven't slept." The captain stood in the doorway with bread and hard cheese and a look of professional assessment, as though exhaustion were a tactical weakness to be reported up the chain.

"Sleep is a debt," Emrys said, not looking up from her columns. "I'll pay it later. Sit down. I need your mornings."

"My mornings?"

"The ones you can remember. All of them." Emrys turned the journal toward her. "Your journal is the most valuable document in this castle, Captain, and I don't think you know why."

Sera sat. She ate mechanically, watching Emrys the way people watch surgeons. "Corvane's man asked me for it yesterday. Your guildmaster."

"I know. I told him it was sealed as evidence pending my audit."

"You can do that?"

"I can do anything with paperwork. That is the entire secret of power, Captain—everyone believes the person holding the ledger." Emrys tapped the page. "Now. Tell me about the hollow mornings. Don't think. Just tell me the first one that comes."

Sera's jaw worked. "Three days ago. Woke up and couldn't remember the gate codes. Stood at the north post for an hour knowing I used to know them, feeling the hole where they'd been." She stopped. Her hand had gone, unconsciously, to the journal, fingers resting on its oilcloth cover like a woman touching a wound to confirm it was still hers. "It's faster now. You've seen the dates."

"I've seen more than the dates." Emrys slid her ruled page across. "Look at the intervals. Forty-one days. Then thirty-eight. Thirty-six. Down to twelve. Do you understand what this curve is?"

"A sickness getting worse."

"It's a loan being called in." Emrys kept her voice flat, professional, because flatness was a mercy—she had learned that years ago, delivering bad numbers to men whose fortunes hung on them. "Whatever this creature is, it doesn't feed steadily. It feeds in draws. And each draw is larger than the one before, because it's spending more than it takes. It's running a deficit against your soul, Captain, and compounding. At the current rate—" she touched the figure at the bottom of the column, ink still faintly wet— "it must feed again within nine days. Massively. Or it drops below the threshold needed to hold King Aldric."

Sera went very still. "Below the threshold. You mean it might just... let go? Leave him?"

"I mean it becomes unbindable from the inside. Weak enough that a binding could take. There's precedent—I'll need to find it, but the guild's archives reference a binding done a century ago, and bindings leave records the way wounds leave scars." Emrys folded her hands. "That's the good news."

"There's bad news. There's always bad news. Bad news travels with good news like a shadow."

"The bad news is the feast." Emrys rose and walked to the window. Below, the city was waking—smoke from ten thousand chimneys standing straight in the windless cold, bells ringing the hour from temples whose congregations, if the whispers were true, were thinning week by week as sworn soldiers felt their loyalties go strange and soft in their chests. "A deficit this size doesn't get covered by sipping from one captain's memories. To reset its ledger, it needs volume. Hundreds of oaths renewed at once. Thousands. Sworn aloud, together, in a single ceremony, witnessed and bound and—" she heard her own voice tighten and forced it level— "consumed."

Sera stood slowly. "An oath ceremony. Renewal of fealty. They do one every—" She stopped. Emrys watched her arrive at it, watched the color leave her face in stages, like a tide going out.

"Every ten years," Emrys finished quietly. "And the last one was nine years ago."

"The anniversary." Sera's voice had gone hoarse. "The tenth-year renewal falls within your nine days. Within the window. It's not a coincidence, is it. It's been steering toward it. The whole court, the whole calendar—it's been keeping the appointment the way a spider keeps—"

"The way any careful creditor keeps a collection date." Emrys turned from the window. The morning light caught her face and gave nothing away; she'd spent thirty years making sure of that. But inside, beneath the composure, something was moving—something cold and clear and growing harder by the hour. "Ten thousand citizens will walk into the temple square in nine days and swear their loyalty aloud. To the crown. Which means to the thing wearing it. And it will eat all of them at once—every oath, every memory of why they swore, every scrap of self that lived inside the promise. They won't die, Captain. They'll continue. Happily. Empty. Loyal forever to nothing at all."

Silence. The bread sat untouched on the table between them, going stale in the cold air.

"And if we stop the ceremony?" Sera asked. "Cancel it. Postpone it."

"Then it starves on schedule and we bind it while it's weak. But you and I both know it won't permit cancellation. It controls the king's voice, the council's fears, half the guard's orders. It will dress the ceremony in whatever clothes it needs—patriotism, piety, panic about Mavros. 'Renew your fealty in the face of the usurper.' It will make refusal feel like treason." Emrys picked up her pen. "Which means our true deadline isn't nine days. It's however long it takes us to find the old binding and prepare it, minus however long it takes the court to organize ten thousand people into a square. We are racing a ceremony, Captain, and ceremonies move fast when kings command them."

"Then we start tonight."

"We started yesterday." Emrys was already pulling a fresh sheet toward her, ruling new columns, her pen moving with the swift economy of a woman who had spent her life turning catastrophe into line items. "I need everything the castle archive holds on the last century. I need the chapel records—I don't know why yet, but the Regent's wards bend around the chapel like water around a stone, and things bend around stones for reasons. And I need you to keep writing in that journal, every day, twice a day. You're not just a witness anymore, Captain Dain. You're the instrument. Your forgetting is the gauge by which we measure our enemy."

Sera looked at her for a long moment. Something passed across the captain's face—not trust, trust took longer—but recognition. The recognition of one professional meeting another across a table strewn with terrible arithmetic.

"My mother used to say accountants were the coldest people alive," Sera said.

"Your mother never met an accountant defending something she loved." Emrys didn't look up. "We're the warmest people alive, Captain. Coldness is just what warmth looks like when it's working."

When Sera had gone, Emrys sat alone in the grey-gold light with her columns spread before her, and let herself feel, for exactly sixty seconds by the water-clock, the full weight of what the numbers meant. Ten thousand souls on one side of a ledger. One silent god, one imprisoned king, one exhausted captain, and herself—a mercenary with a decade of unanswered prayers and a name written in a dead saint's book—on the other.

Then the sixty seconds ended. She closed her eyes out of habit older than thought, the way she had every night for ten years, and prayed to the god that did not answer. *If you can hear me. If anything of you remains anywhere in this glass-ridden dark. I have nine days and a plan with more holes than a moth-eaten coat. I am asking for the truth of what happened the night you went quiet. Show me where to dig.*

Nothing. The silence she knew like her own heartbeat. She exhaled, opened her eyes, reached for the archive request forms—

And froze.

On the blank sheet before her, in ink that gleamed wet and black as a beetle's back, in a hand that was not hers, a single word had written itself:

**DIG.**
