## 9. The Archive Beneath the Chapel

The word came at dawn, and it came the way all of the god's words had come for ten years: not as a voice, but as a weight.

Emrys had been awake, as she was every morning now, with Sera's journal open on her knees, cross-referencing dates. The arithmetic sat in her head like a stone swallowed whole—nine days had become seven, seven would become three, and somewhere in that narrowing corridor of time the Regent would feed again. She had been staring at nothing when it happened: a pressure behind her sternum, brief and total, like a hand pressing flat against her breastbone from the inside.

*DIG.*

One word. Her second in a decade. The first had been *RUN*, and she had obeyed it only in the way a merchant obeys a market shift—by turning it over, weighing it, refusing to be stampeded. This one she did not weigh. She stood up so fast the journal slid from her lap and hit the floor with a slap that sounded, in the grey silence of the guest chamber, like a door closing.

Sera was already in the doorway, sword half-drawn, hair bound back for the night watch she'd just left. "What is it?"

"It spoke again." Emrys heard her own voice and did not recognize it. It was too steady. Steadiness was what happened to her when the ground opened under her feet; she had learned long ago that terror and bookkeeping used the same muscles. "One word. *Dig.*"

"Dig where?"

"That," Emrys said, "is the question I intend to bill someone for."

* * *

They started in the chapel because there was nowhere else to start. Castle Veyra had a thousand floors and Emrys knew perhaps forty of them, but the god had spoken to her in the chapel before—ten years ago, the last time—and gods, whatever else they were, were creatures of habit. Besides, Sera said, the chapel was the one place in the castle the Regent never entered. Aldric's glass-eyed body attended services because attendance was expected, but the thing behind his eyes sat through them the way a man sits through a language he despises, still and contemptuous, its attention fixed on the stained glass as though the saints depicted there owed it money.

"They're odd saints," Sera murmured as they slipped inside. Morning light fell through the windows in long colored blades—amber, blood-red, a green like pond water—and lay across the flagstones in broken pieces. "No one knows who they're meant to be anymore. The faces wore away a century ago. There are records of records about them, if you follow me, and then nothing."

Emrys followed her along the nave, boots quiet on stone worn into shallow valleys by generations of kneeling. She counted without meaning to—it was the tic of her trade, the ledger running always beneath the skin. Seven windows. Twelve pillars. One altar of black granite that predated everything else in the room by centuries, if the tooling marks were anything to judge by.

She stopped at the altar.

It was not centered. Everyone assumed it was; the candles flanked it symmetrically, the rail curved around it evenly, and the eye forgave it because the eye wanted symmetry. But Emrys had spent twenty years measuring things that lied about themselves. The altar stood four fingers' width off the chapel's true axis. Deliberately. And directly beneath it, if you dropped a plumb line from the apex of the vaulted roof—the roof's axis, not the altar's—you would find—

"The floor," she said. "Here. Look at the joints."

Sera knelt. The flagstones around the altar were old, but these four were older, and they had been reset at least once; the mortar lines were too clean, too recent, a scar dressed to look like skin. No dust in the seams. Dust settled everywhere in Veyra—the Regent's court kept a hundred servants busy polishing history—but here the gaps between stones had been swept within the season.

"Someone maintains this," Sera said slowly. "Someone has been maintaining this for a hundred years."

"Not someone." Emrys ran her thumb along a seam and felt, faintly, under the stone's cold surface, something else. A hum. Not sound exactly—a resonance, the way a struck bell keeps ringing below hearing. The same feeling she got, sometimes, praying. The feeling of speaking into a well and hearing no splash. Except now the well was answering. "A covenant. Someone bound by oath, maintaining a seal. That's how oaths work—they outlive the people who swear them. They need hands."

"Whose hands?"

"Whoever's been lighting these." She nodded at the candles. Two of them, at the altar's base, burned with a steadiness no draft should have allowed. Their wax pooled in shapes that, if you looked too long, resembled letters. Emrys made herself stop looking. "There'll be a sacristan. There's always a sacristan."

There was. He found them rather than the other way around—an old man named Brother Anselm, small and round-shouldered, who came up from some lower stair with a broom in one hand and an expression of profound, weary recognition on his face.

"So," he said. "You've found it, then."

"You knew we would?" Sera's hand had gone to her sword hilt. Old habit. Anselm looked like a man who had never lifted anything heavier than a hymnal.

"I knew *someone* would. Eventually. It's in the terms." He set the broom against a pillar with the care of a man laying down a tool he had carried for fifty years. "I swore an oath when I took this post. To keep the floor swept, the candles lit, and to admit, without hindrance, whoever came asking about the archive—if they asked on a day when the seal permitted asking. Most days it doesn't. The stone stays shut. People knock and hear nothing and go away and tell themselves they imagined the door." He looked at Emrys with eyes gone pale and watery with age, and something in them that was not watery at all. "You knocked this morning. The seal answered. So either the terms are ending, or you are the one they were waiting for. Both possibilities keep me awake at night, and I am very old and sleep poorly regardless."

"What archive?" Emrys asked. "What's beneath us?"

Anselm smiled, and it was a terrible smile, gentle and grieved. "Ask your god. It dug the hole."

* * *

The stair went down farther than the castle had depth. That was Emrys's first coherent thought as they descended, and her second thought was that the thought itself was evidence of how far past ordinary architecture they had traveled. The steps were cut from black granite matching the altar—no, she corrected herself, the altar matched the *steps*—and each one bore a single carved word at its edge, so worn by feet and time that only fragments remained. She read them going down: *KEPT. WITNESSED. UNBROKEN. KEPT. WITNESSED.* A liturgy in stone, trodden nearly to erasure by a century of one old man's broom and candle.

The air changed as they descended. It grew colder, then warmer, then settled into a stillness that had temperature but no movement—as though the air down here had been holding its breath since before either of them was born. The god's presence pressed against her awareness like a tide against a sea wall. Closer now. Louder, if a silence could be loud. She realized, somewhere around the two-hundredth step, that the hum she'd felt through the altar floor was not coming from the walls or the stone.

It was coming from her own chest.

Her prayers—every prayer she had ever banked in ten years of unanswered devotion—were resonating down here, the way a tuning fork answers a note played in another room. Her god had been in this castle once. Her god had *worked* here. Whatever waited at the bottom of this stair, it knew the shape of her faith, and her faith knew it back.

"Emrys." Sera's voice came low behind her. "Your eyes."

"What about them?"

"There's light in them. From underneath. Like standing over water at night."

Emrys touched her own face and found her cheekbones lit faintly silver, as if moonlight were leaking out of her skull instead of falling into it. She filed it away—*note: symptoms, cause unknown, investigate later*—because filing things away was how she stayed sane, and sanity was going to matter more than explanations before this day was done.

The stair ended at a door.

Not a grand door. A plain one, iron-banded oak, unremarkable except that it bore no lock, no handle, and no hinges she could find. Instead, across its face, a seal had been laid in lead and wax and something darker—she leaned close and did not let herself name what the darker thing was—and inscribed around the rim in a script she almost recognized. It was guild-script. Old guild-script, the ceremonial hand they used for founding charters, the kind Corvane could write in his sleep.

And across the center of the seal, in the same hand, a single line:

*I gave my name to buy this door. Do not give yours cheaply.*

Emrys stood before it for a long moment. Then, because she was who she was—a woman whose entire life was the reading of other people's fine print—she read the line aloud.

The seal dissolved. Not broke—*dissolved*, the lead running like butter, the dark substance sublimating into a smell she refused to identify, the oak swinging inward on air that hadn't moved in a hundred years. Warmth rolled out over them, carrying dust and lamp oil and paper and, beneath all of it, a scent she knew from the guild's deepest vaults: the smell of ink that remembered.

"Oh," said Brother Anselm, from the top of the stairs, where he had followed no farther than his oath required. "That line was meant for the *next* one. Saint Verity wrote it for the next one. It was supposed to make you hesitate."

"It worked on everyone else for a century," Emrys said, and stepped through.

* * *

The archive was a sphere.

Not truly—her eye insisted it was a sphere, but her merchant's mind overruled the eye and measured instead: a domed chamber, maybe forty feet across, shelved in concentric rings that climbed the curving walls, every shelf packed with books, folios, scroll-cases, sealed jars of the kind the guild used for bottled storms and borrowed years. At the chamber's heart stood a reading table, and on the table, centered with the precision of a shrine, lay a book.

Emrys knew that book. She had authenticated it three nights ago in the guildhall of Vellum, under lamplight, with trembling hands she'd pretended were steady. The prophecy book. The diary. The volume whose newest entry bore tonight's date and her name.

Except the book on the table was not quite the same. Its twin, yes—same binding, same paper, same impossible age—but this copy was thicker. Whole gatherings of pages the guild's copy lacked had been razored out of the one she knew. Here they remained, intact, waiting.

"Its twin," she whispered. "The guild's copy is a censored edition."

"Then the guild," Sera said, drawing her sword at last—not against any enemy, Emrys noticed, but out of sheer need to hold something honest—"*censored a prophet*."

Emrys approached the table the way she'd approach a contract worth more than her life. Which, she reflected, this was. She did not touch the book. First, discipline: she circled the room, reading spines. Records of harvests. Rosters of a royal guard sworn a century ago. Jars labeled in Verity's handwriting—she recognized it now, the same hand as the seal—labeled *YEARS, SPENT, 1 EA.* and *OATHS, BROKEN, ASSORTED* and one jar, smaller than the others, simply labeled *ME*. And along one whole curve of shelving, ledgers. Hundreds of ledgers, all bearing the Vellum Guild's colophon.

Guild ledgers, a century old, hidden beneath a chapel.

She pulled one at random and opened it, and her blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the archive's preserved air.

It was an accounting of the Regent. Feeding records. Consumption rates. Dates, quantities, sources—oaths harvested from soldiers, from priests, from a king. Entries in a neat professional hand, annotated and audited, with corrections in the margins. The Vellum Guild had been keeping books on the thing wearing Aldric's crown since before Aldric's grandfather was born. Not fighting it. Not warning anyone. *Auditing it.*

"We didn't stumble into this," Emrys heard herself say. Her voice came out flat, the flatness of a woman reading numbers she cannot refute. "We were sent. Corvane ordered the book sealed and me watched—he *knew*. The guild has known for a hundred years. We're not investigators, Sera. We're inventory."

"Emrys—"

"Look at the marginalia. Look at the *handwriting*." She turned pages faster. "Same hand, decade after decade after decade. Different clerks, same hand. Either the guild teaches its masters one script, or—" She stopped. Closed the ledger. Set it down with exaggerated care, the way one sets down a thing that might detonate. "Later. Grief later. Arithmetic first. That's the rule."

Arithmetic first. She turned to the book on the table at last, and opened it to the pages the guild had razored out.

Verity's hand, hurried now, the tidy script of the early entries degrading page by page into something scraped and desperate:

*The binding holds, but the terms are crueler than I told them. It cannot be killed. Glass does not die; it breaks, and breaking is only a change of shape. What can bind it is a vacuum where an oath used to be. Every chain has two ends, and the Regent grips its end so tightly that only the other end can be moved—which means an oathbreaker. Not a traitor; traitors break oaths for gain, and gain fills the vacuum again. It must be someone who breaks faith *at cost*, deliberately, knowing the price, leaving nothing in the space where the promise was. The Regent feeds on sworn loyalty. Feed it a hollow, and it starves trying to swallow the absence.*

*They ask me who will do this, when I am gone. I tell them: the door will choose. I have written myself into the book so that the choosing will look like fate to those who need it to be fate, and like a summons to those who need it to be a summons. I am sorry for the deception. I am sorrier for the truth, which is that I could not bear to leave the choice to chance, and could not bear to make it openly, because openly-chosen sacrifices run away.*

*The price, so no one can claim they weren't warned: the binder must be willing to be forgotten. Not dead. Forgotten. Every record of her erased, every memory of her dissolved, every name she ever signed unsigned. The god who seals the rupture spends itself to do this, and the last act of its spending is to take the binder out of the world's remembering. That is why no one remembers Saint Verity. That is why the saints in the windows have no faces. She is up there. All of them are her. I have checked the iconography twice.*

Emrys stopped reading. Her hands were shaking, and she was distantly surprised to find that she was not afraid. What she felt was worse than fear: it was *recognition*. The cold clarity of a woman reading a contract and finding her own signature already at the bottom.

"Sera," she said quietly. "Read this."

Sera read. Emrys watched her face do what faces did—confusion, horror, denial arriving in orderly procession—and then watched something worse arrive: understanding. Sera looked up at her, and in the archive's strange underwater light her eyes were bright and furious and wet.

"No," Sera said. "No. Find another clause. You're the best contract-reader I've ever met—find another clause."

"The clause is the point. That's what makes it binding." Emrys closed the book gently, the way she closed every book, even books that had just sentenced her. "It needs someone the Regent trusts enough to feed on. It needs someone with a god's mark on them to carry the sealing. It needs someone whose breaking will leave a true hollow. Sera, I am not being chosen by destiny. I am being chosen by *specification*. Somewhere, a hundred years ago, a dying saint wrote a job description, and I match every line item."

"And the price—"

"—is that no one remembers me. Including me. The god spends its last breath unmaking my name, and I don't get to object afterward, because afterward there is no I." She laughed, briefly, and hated the sound of it. "Do you know what the worst part is? Professionally? It's a *clean* transaction. Full disclosure. Terms stated. Consent implied by the door opening. Verity even apologized for the deception. She was thorough. She was *ethical*. I hate her a little for it, and I understand her completely, and I think she'd have liked me, and none of that changes a single number in the ledger."

Sera crossed the chamber and gripped her by both shoulders, hard, gauntlet-cold even through Emrys's sleeves. "Listen to me. I swore an oath to protect the king, and that thing eats it nightly, and I write myself back into existence every morning with ink and stubbornness. If forgetting is the enemy, then *memory is the weapon*, and I know something about holding onto yourself when everything is built to erase you." Her voice cracked and steadied. "Whatever the terms say, we will find the loophole. That's what you do. That's what you're *for*. So you are not allowed to decide tonight that the contract is final."

For a moment Emrys let herself lean into the grip, into the fury of it, into the outrageous idea that terms could be renegotiated with a century-old saint and a starving god. Then she straightened, and did what she always did with unbearable things.

She reached for the ledger.

"One condition," she said. "If it comes to it—if there's no loophole—I want the terms amended. Before I break anything, I write myself into a new book. Names, deeds, debts forgiven. Copies hidden in three places the Regent and the guild both missed. If the price is forgetting, then the price includes a fight, and the fight starts now, with ink."

Sera's grip loosened into something gentler. "Now that," she said, "sounds like an oath I could stand behind."

Above them, muffled through a hundred feet of stone and a swept floor and a faceless window, the chapel bell began to ring—and kept ringing, urgent and wrong, the alarm pattern, the pattern Sera had taught her meant only one thing.

Something had happened in the castle above.

And down the stair, unhurried, familiar footsteps—old man's footsteps, a broom's rhythm—came descending toward the open door, though Brother Anselm had sworn his oath admitted visitors and forbade him to follow.

"Saint's bones," Sera breathed, hand going to her sword. "He's coming down. He's *never* allowed to come down."

The footsteps paused at the threshold. And Anselm's voice, when it came, was not entirely an old man's voice—it had another voice underneath it, patient and vast and terribly tired, the voice of something that had been holding a door shut with its body for a hundred years.

"Clerk Almeida," it said. "Your god asks me to tell you the third word. It says you'll want to sit down."
