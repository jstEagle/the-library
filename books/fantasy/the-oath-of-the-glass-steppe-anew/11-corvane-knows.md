## 11. Corvane Knows

Emrys climbed the last of the stairs with the word *ASK* still ringing in the hollow where her god's voice used to live, and found the great doors of Castle Veyra already open.

Not forced. Not breached. Open, as if the castle had been expecting someone. The torches along the entry hall burned a shade too steadily, and the draft that came through those doors smelled of tallow and old paper and the particular dry sweetness of vellum cured in a hurry—the smell of the Vellum Guild's counting rooms, the smell of home, arriving at the worst possible hour in the worst possible shape.

Guildmaster Corvane stood in the center of the hall like a man inspecting a warehouse he suspected of shorting him.

He had not changed in the ten years she had served under his seal. That was the first obscene thing she noticed. He was a long, gray man, all edges folded inward, dressed in traveling wool the color of ash, and his face had the settled quality of a ledger that balanced to the copper—nothing wasted, nothing surprising. His satchel was open at his hip. His boots were dusty from the mountain road. And he stood in the throne room's antechamber without permission, without escort, without the trembling deference every other visitor to Castle Veyra wore like a required garment, and the guards flanking the doors did not stop him.

They could not see him, Emrys realized. Or worse—they saw him perfectly, and something in them refused to register him as worth reporting.

"You're supposed to be in Vell," she said.

"I am where I am needed." Corvane did not turn. He was looking up at the vaulted ceiling as though reading it, and perhaps he was; Emrys had learned enough about wards in nine days to know the ceiling was inscribed floor to apex with binding script, a lattice of oaths older than the kingdom. "You've been busy, Almeida. Sabotaging ward-lattices. Befriending captains. Praying." He said the last word the way other men said *smuggling*. "I taught you better tradecraft than that."

"You taught me inventory," Emrys said. Her voice came out level. She was proud of how level it came out, in the way one is proud of a wall holding against a flood one knows will take it eventually. "The rest I taught myself."

"Mm." He turned then, and looked at her, and for a moment—one long, merchant's heartbeat—he simply appraised her. She knew that look. She had given that look to goods on the night shelf: bottled storms, borrowed years, names never given. It was the look of a man establishing condition, provenance, and price.

She had been on the receiving end of it before. She understood that now. She had been on the receiving end of it since she was eleven years old and starving on the Vell docks, and a gray man had knelt down, opened a contract, and said, *Sign here, child, and you'll never be hungry again.*

"Come," Corvane said. "Walk with me. There are things you should hear sitting down, but there is no time for sitting."

* * *

He led her through corridors she had spent nine days mapping by stealth, and he navigated them as though he owned them. Perhaps he did. At each turn he spoke, in the mild cadence of a guildmaster dictating correspondence, and what he said dismantled her life plank by plank while she walked on legs that felt borrowed.

"The Vellum Guild was founded one hundred and six years ago," he said. "You know our charter. You recited it at your induction. *To record faithfully, to hold in trust, to trade only in truth.* Do you remember who dictated that charter?"

"A founder," Emrys said. "The histories say—"

"The histories say what we wrote into them. We write histories, Almeida. It is half our trade." He paused at a window overlooking the lower city, where the temples were dark and the oath-ceremony scaffolds were rising in the square like ribs. "The charter was dictated by a god. The god whose silence you have been auditing for ten years with such admirable precision. The founding oath of the Vellum Guild is a leash, and the other end of it has been held in a glass fist for a century."

Emrys stopped walking.

Corvane continued three paces before noticing, turned back, and regarded her with something almost like patience. "Yes," he said. "That look. Every one of you makes that look, sooner or later. Though most of you make it too late to be useful."

"Every one of *who*?" Emrys asked. And because she was who she was—an auditor, an accountant, a woman who could not leave a column unsummed even while drowning—she answered herself. "Contingency stock. You said it just now, or you meant to. Say it plainly, Guildmaster. I want it in your words. I intend to quote you."

Something flickered across his face—not guilt. She searched hard for guilt and found none. What she found was satisfaction, thin and cold as frost on a ledger page.

"Very well. In my words." He clasped his hands behind his back. "One hundred years ago, Saint Verity shattered the Regent and bound its fragments beneath this castle, at the cost of her own name, her own memory, her own existence in the record. Before she went, she left instructions. Prophecies, if you like—she called them forecasts. She told us the Regent would regrow itself inside a royal host. She told us approximately when. And she told us what would be required to break it a second time: an oathbreaker. A person sworn so thoroughly, to so many things, that when they broke, they would break *completely*, and the vacuum of their unbinding would tear the parasite loose from whatever it clung to."

"So the guild has spent a century—"

"—raising candidates. Yes." Corvane said it the way he announced quarterly earnings. "Orphaned young. Sworn early and often—to the guild, to the god, to dead parents' memories, to personal creeds they mistake for choices. We keep their contracts deliberately contradictory. Loyalty to the guild versus loyalty to truth. Obedience versus conscience. We feed them just enough autonomy to let them believe they are choosing, and then we wait for the prophecy to name one. Yours named you nine days ago. Efficient. Verity's arithmetic was always sound."

The corridor seemed very far away suddenly. Emrys heard her own breathing, slow and deliberate, the breathing she used over disputed accounts.

"My contract," she said. "My mercenary contract. Clause eleven. The one that lets me refuse any job that violates my conscience."

"Written by me," Corvane agreed. "Specifically so that you would one day refuse a job on grounds of conscience, thereby breaking clause eleven's companion clause, which requires you to accept any assignment designated *vital*—which tonight's assignment is. Your escape hatch was engineered to become your trap. You were built to break, Almeida. Beautifully. Completely. On schedule."

"And my loyalty to you," she said. "Twenty years. Ten of them keeping the ledgers. All those nights."

"Was the strongest thread in the weave," Corvane said, "and therefore the one we wove tightest. A candidate who loves her masters cannot be bought away from them. We made certain you loved us." He tilted his head. "Did you? I've often wondered whether that part took."

The question was so casual, so genuinely bookkeeping-mild, that it did more damage than cruelty could have. Emrys felt twenty years rearrange themselves around her like furniture in a darkened room—every kindness reclassified, every promotion reread, every time Corvane had praised her precision now legible as inspection of an asset ripening toward harvest.

"The night the god went silent," she said. "Ten years ago. The ledgers recorded it as an event. An anomaly. We audited it annually."

"We caused it," Corvane said. "Or rather—we permitted it. The Regent needed feeding at its rebirth, and the nearest meal was a god. The guild's charter binds us to that god, so the guild's leadership negotiated terms. Neutrality, in exchange for continuity. We sold the silence, Almeida. We booked it as a loss and moved on." He smiled faintly. "You'd have done the same. It's why we trained you."

And there it was. Not rage yet—rage would come, she could feel it gathering somewhere below her sternum like floodwater behind a door—but underneath it, colder and more terrible, the auditor's certainty: *the books balance.* Everything he said fit. Every strange kindness, every sealed archive, every year of her carefully cultivated conscience—it all summed. She had spent her life trusting arithmetic, and the arithmetic said Corvane was telling the truth.

"You're here," she said, "to collect me. To deliver me to the ceremony as the contingency. The oathbreaker. The sacrifice."

"I'm here," Corvane said, "to inform you of your terms. Whether you accept them is—" and here he allowed himself the ghost of a shrug, "—consistent with design either way."

* * *

They returned to the antechamber, and Emrys understood why he had wanted her walking: a body in motion does not stop to strike. Now, standing, facing him across ten feet of polished stone, she let the floodwater through—not all of it, but a controlled release, the way she had once cracked a sluice gate to drown a rival caravan's road.

"You groomed children," she said. "For a century. You starved them, swore them, leashed them, and filed them."

"We preserved them," Corvane said. "The alternative was letting the Regent wake unopposed. Sentiment is a cost, Almeida. Someone must pay it, and better it be paid by the few than defaulted upon by all."

"Do you know what the god said to me?" Emrys asked. "After ten years of your negotiated silence? Three words. RUN. DIG. ASK." She stepped closer. "It is still screaming inside that thing wearing the king. Still caged. Still *ours*, by charter, by oath, by every contract your guild ever notarized—and you sold its voice to buy yourselves a quiet century. And then you grew a crop of children to spend on the repair."

"Yes," Corvane said.

No defense. No justification beyond the ones already offered. Just the word, clean as a posted price, and somehow that was the worst of it—that he did not consider himself a monster, because monsters do not balance books, and his books balanced.

Emrys's hands were shaking. She looked down at them, at the ink stains in the creases of her fingers, twenty years deep, and thought: *these hands kept his ledgers. These hands audited his silences.*

Then the shaking stopped, because fury crystallized the way she did everything else: into structure.

"You said the charter binds the guild to the god," she said quietly. "Dictated by the god. One hundred and six years ago."

"I did."

"And a charter," Emrys said, "is an oath. Recorded. Witnessed. Executed under seal." She watched his face very carefully as she laid the next card down, the one she had found not in the archive but in the architecture of everything he had just confessed. "An oath to a god who is not silent, Guildmaster. Who is imprisoned. Who is owed. And the guild—you, personally, as its executing officer—are bound to render assistance to your creditor upon demand."

Corvane went very still. It was the stillness of a man who has heard, in his opponent's voice, the sound of his own figures being read back to him.

"That interpretation has never been tested," he said.

"No," Emrys agreed. "But you taught me that an untested clause is not a void clause. It's an opportunity." She straightened. Somewhere above them, in the throne room, the Regent sat behind Aldric's eyes, and in two days it would stand before ten thousand people and begin to eat. "You came here to deliver my terms. Consider them delivered. Here are yours: the guild's records of the binding, complete. The original charter. Verity's full archive. And your personal testimony, witnessed and sealed, to everything you've confessed tonight."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I invoke the charter," Emrys said, "before witnesses, before the court, before the god itself if it can spare another word—and the Vellum Guild dissolves. Every contract voided. Every impossible thing in your vaults released. A century of careful accounting, unwritten in an afternoon." She smiled, and knew it was not a kind smile. "I'm told you booked my god's silence as a loss, Guildmaster. Let me show you a real one."

For the first time in twenty years, Corvane looked at her without appraisal. He looked at her the way a man looks at weather.

"You understand," he said slowly, "that this changes nothing about what you are. The prophecy named you. The breaking will require you. Whatever leverage you take from me, the terms remain."

"I know what I am," Emrys said. "I've read the entry. But you made one error in a century of flawless accounting, and I want you to know that I found it."

"Which was?"

"You assumed a leash works because the dog doesn't know it's there." She picked up her satchel—the one holding Sera's journal, Verity's diary, the copied prophecy page, the whole ragged evidence of nine days. "Leashes work until the animal reads its own contract. Then they're just rope. And rope, Guildmaster—" she slung the satchel across her shoulder, "—cuts both ways."

Above them, muffled through floors of stone, the bells of Castle Veyra began to ring the hour, and with them came a sound that stopped them both: horns. Many horns. From the east walls, from the watchtowers, from every direction at once—a sound Emrys had hoped not to hear for six more days.

Mavros's army had arrived early.

Corvane closed his eyes briefly, as if consulting some inner ledger, and reopened them resigned. "Three days become one," he said. "The Regent will accelerate. Which means, Almeida, that your leverage and my cooperation just became equally worthless unless we move within hours." He held out his hand—not for hers, but palm-up, a merchant's gesture, ancient as the guild itself. "Shall we do business after all?"

Emrys looked at the hand. Twenty years of looking at that hand.

"Not business," she said. "Terms. And you'll sign them in blood-wax, under the god's seal, where I can audit them forever."

Behind his eyes, she saw the exact moment Corvane recalculated—saw the century-old machine of the Vellum Guild encounter, for the first time, a clause it had never written.

Far off, past the windows, the first fires of Mavros's vanguard lit the horizon, and the glass crown above the empty throne began, very softly, to sing.
