## 12. Nine Days of Glass

The horns came an hour before dawn.

Sera Dain was on the wall when the first note broke over the sleeping city—low and cracked, the sound of an instrument played by a man with frozen fingers—and she knew before the watch-captain beside her had finished his yawn that something was wrong. Mavros's army was supposed to be nine days out. Nine days of margin, nine days to prepare, nine days in which the arithmetic Emrys Almeida had scratched onto scraps of paper might be made to work. The horns said otherwise.

She counted the fires on the eastern ridge while the sky went from black to bruise. Four hundred. Five hundred. More than the scouts had promised, more than the council had budgeted for, spread across the hills like a constellation that had fallen and refused to go out. Below each fire, she knew, were soldiers who had sworn oaths they did not remember swearing—conscription ledgers signed in their names, loyalty harvested like wheat they'd never planted.

"They're not stopping to camp properly," said Brannic, the watch-captain, squinting through his glass. "They're just... pausing. Like they don't need rest."

"Like something else is carrying them," Sera said, and felt the words settle into her stomach like swallowed stones.

Because she knew what carried armies like that. She had seen it every night for months, in the king's eyes, in the slow creep of glass along the edges of Aldric's jaw where skin used to be. And she had seen it in her own journal, in her own handwriting growing subtly stranger page by page—the letters leaning left when she wrote them leaning right, words she didn't remember choosing.

The Regent was hungry. The Regent was in a hurry. And hurry, Sera was learning, was the most frightening thing about it.

* * *

By midmorning the castle was a hive kicked open. Runners on every stair, councilors emerging from their chambers with sleep-creased faces and voices pitched too high, servants pressing themselves flat against walls as men in armor passed at speeds armor was not made for. And above it all, beneath it all, threading through everything: the voice of the king.

Except it was not the king's voice. Sera had spent eleven years learning the difference between Aldric's speech and everyone else's—the way he paused before hard decisions, the small apologetic cough he made when he contradicted someone he liked, the Veyran lilt on the word *honor* that no tutor had ever managed to grind out of him. The thing that stood at the council table now spoke in perfect, placeless diction. Every word arrived fully formed, like coins struck from a single mold.

"The ceremony will proceed," it said. "In three days."

"Three days?" Councilor Ives's voice cracked on the second word. He was a round man built for comfort, and comfort had abandoned him weeks ago; Sera watched him clutch his chain of office the way a drowning man clutches driftwood. "Your Majesty—I beg your pardon—but the rites require nine days minimum. The purification fasts alone—"

"The rites," said the Regent, "will proceed in three days." It turned Aldric's head, and the light caught the crown and slid down the glass at his temple like water down a windowpane. "General Mavros is at the walls in three days. Would you prefer he arrive to find your loyalties unorganized?"

Sera stood at the chamber door, one of twelve guards, and watched the councilors look at one another. None of them would meet the king's eyes. That was the thing nobody said aloud: they had all stopped looking at him. Eleven years of fealty, and the men who governed Veyra could not bear to hold their sovereign's gaze, because somewhere behind those eyes something looked back that should not know how.

Ives bowed. They all bowed. The Regent smiled with Aldric's mouth, which was the worst part—it smiled *correctly*, warmly, the way Aldric had once smiled at Sera when she'd taken a knife meant for him in the market at Lowbridge, and the correctness of it made her skin crawl worse than any snarl could have.

"You are dismissed," it said to the guards, and then, as they filed out, its gaze snagged on hers.

One heartbeat. Two. Sera kept her face blank, her stride even, her hands loose at her sides. Inside her chest, her heart performed a drumroll against her ribs.

It knows, she thought. Or it suspects. Either way—

Either way, tonight she would burn another journal and start another. She was on her fourth since winter began.

* * *

The temples began emptying on the second day.

Sera noticed it first because she walked past them. That was her habit now, born of necessity: every morning, before her shift, she took the long route through the city, past the Chapel of the Ninefold Dawn, past the shrine of Saint Verity with its worn stone feet, past the little river-temple where the tide-priests kept their lamps lit against the dark hours. She told herself it was patrol discipline. She knew it was a census. She was counting the faithful, the same way Emrys Almeida counted inventory, and watching the numbers fall.

The Chapel of the Ninefold Dawn held services at dawn and dusk. On Tuesday there had been forty worshippers at dawn. Wednesday: twenty-six. Thursday: eleven, most of them elderly, huddled together in the front pews as if for warmth. By Friday morning there were six, and the priest—a stooped woman named Ondile whom Sera had known since girlhood—stood alone at the altar lighting candles that no one had come to see.

Sera slipped inside. Incense and old wax and cold stone. Her boots sounded very loud.

"Captain Dain." Ondile didn't turn from her candles. "Come to arrest me? I hear the palace considers empty pews a form of treason now."

"What happened to your congregation?"

Now the priest turned, and her face was gray with a tiredness that went deeper than sleep. "They stopped coming. Not gradually, Captain—all at once, like birds leaving a tree before a storm. And do you know what they say, the few who still come?" She lowered her voice, though the chapel was empty except for God and the two of them. "They say they woke up and couldn't remember why they believed. Not doubted—*doubted* I could forgive. Doubt is honest. But this—" she pressed her hand flat to her sternum "—this is a hole. They reach for their faith and there's nothing there to reach with. Like a hand missing fingers."

Sera's mouth had gone dry. "Soldiers too?"

"Especially soldiers. The garrison chaplain says half his men can't finish the Litany anymore. They get to the fourth line—the line about whom you serve and why—and the words just... stop. In their mouths. Like the oath itself has been scooped out of them." Ondile studied her. "You've gone pale, Captain."

"I'm fine."

"You're not. And you're not here by accident either, are you? You've been walking past my chapel every morning for a month. Counting." The old woman's eyes were sharp under all that weariness. "Whatever's happening in that castle—you know. Don't tell me. I don't want to know, not yet. But take this." She pressed a candle stub into Sera's hand, cheap tallow, worth nothing. "Saint Verity's own rite. When you can't remember why you believe, light this and say her name. Memory is a candle, child. Even a guttering one shows you the shape of the room."

Sera carried the candle stub out into the gray morning and held it the whole way back to the castle, and could not have said why her hand wouldn't unclench around it.

* * *

That night she checked the hidden journal.

She kept it behind the loose stone in her quarters, wrapped in oilcloth, written in a cipher of her own devising that she changed monthly. The ritual had become the spine of her days: write everything, date everything, read yesterday's entry aloud each morning so the words entered her ears and not just her eyes—because her eyes could lie to her now, or rather could be made to lie, could be shown a page that said one thing and remember it saying another. Reading aloud, hearing her own voice claim her own history, was the last authentication rite she trusted.

She unwrapped the oilcloth. Opened to today's page.

Her handwriting was already there.

She stood very still. The stone was cold against her back; the candle she'd brought for light trembled slightly in its holder, though her hands, mercifully, did not. She was trained for this. Eleven years of guard duty taught you to stand still while everything in you screamed.

The entry was dated today. The ink was dry. And it was not her hand.

It was close—that was the horror of it, how *close* it was. Someone had learned her letters the way a forger learns a coin: patiently, lovingly, flaw by flaw. But the pressure was wrong, heavier, the downstrokes gouged slightly deep, as if the writer pressed too hard because writing was new to it, because hands were new to it.

She forced herself to read.

*Sera keeps her little book. Sera counts her mornings. How diligent. How touching. I let her keep it, you understand—not out of cruelty, but because her resistance flavors the loyalty. Spoiled meat is still meat, but loyal meat that fights is a feast.*

Below that, in the same gouged hand:

*Three days. Tell the captain with the candle that I know about the candle. Tell her that her king is still in here, watching. Tell her that he weeps so beautifully.*

The journal fell from her fingers.

Sera did not scream. She wanted to; her whole body was a closed fist around the urge. Instead she did what she had trained herself to do, what Emrys had helped her build these past weeks into something almost like armor: she observed. She picked up the journal. She noted the time. She noted the position of the loose stone—undisturbed, which meant the thing hadn't needed to open the stone, hadn't needed hands at all, had simply reached through whatever thread of rewritten memory connected Sera to her own secrets and written *from the inside*. She noted that the entry called her *the captain with the candle*, and understood, with a lurch, that the Regent knew about Ondile, knew about her morning walks, knew about the counting.

And she noted the other thing. The thing buried in the middle, almost casual, almost kind.

*He weeps so beautifully.*

It wanted her to know Aldric was alive. Why? To torment her? Perhaps. But Emrys had taught her to read documents the way a merchant reads contracts—for the interest underneath the words. Everything the Regent did served its hunger. And if showing her that Aldric lived served the hunger somehow...

Then maybe Aldric alive was leverage it intended to use. Maybe it planned to make her trade something for him.

Or maybe—her breath caught—it was afraid of something, and Aldric's survival was the crack in its armor it couldn't help showing her.

She rewrapped the journal. She did not put it back behind the stone. Instead she tucked it inside her breastplate, against her heart, where she could feel its weight all day, a small rectangular promise: *you are still you, you are still you, you are still you.* Then she blew out her candle, sat down on her bunk in full darkness, and allowed herself exactly sixty seconds of shaking before she began planning.

* * *

Emrys found her before dawn, as Emrys always seemed to—appearing out of the corridor shadows with the unhurried step of a woman who had already accounted for every variable including Sera's insomnia.

"You got one too," Sera said, without preamble.

Emrys's face did something complicated and controlled. "The prophecy book. A new entry appeared overnight. In my handwriting, describing tomorrow's ceremony in detail I haven't been given." She paused. "Yours?"

Sera handed over the journal without explanation. Watched Emrys read. Watched the ledger-keeper's composure develop hairline fractures—there in the jaw, there in the whitening knuckles—and thought, not for the first time, that she trusted those fractures more than she'd ever trusted anyone's calm.

"It's showing off," Emrys said finally. "Or testing us. Both. It wants us to know it's inside our defenses." Her finger stopped on the page. "*Loyal meat that fights is a feast.*"

"That's the part I keep reading."

"And this." Emrys tapped the other line. "*He weeps so beautifully.*" She looked up, and in the dark her eyes had the flat shine of someone doing arithmetic at speed. "Sera. Why would it tell us he's alive?"

"Because it's cruel."

"Cruelty is expenditure. This thing doesn't spend anything it doesn't recoup." Emrys folded the journal closed with strange gentleness, as if it were a wounded bird. "It's telling us because it needs us to try to save him. Because a rescue attempt—ours, specifically, at the ceremony, at the moment of maximum feeding—is something it has planned for. We're not breaking into its trap, Sera. We're being *invited*."

The words landed like cold water. Sera sat with them. Outside, the eastern horizon was going the color of old scars, and somewhere beyond the walls five hundred fires burned in ranks, and somewhere below the city ten thousand citizens were preparing to swear away the only thing they had left.

"Then we change the invitation," Sera said.

Emrys smiled, thin and fierce. "Now you're thinking like a guildswoman." She rose. "Get me every name you trust. Not the ones you hope you can trust—the ones you'd stake your journal on. If we're going to walk into a feast, I want to arrive as the knives."

Sera started listing names in her head—Brannic, young Pell who still wept at the Litany's fourth line and therefore still had a fourth line, old Marta at the gate—and stopped.

"Emrys. There's something else." Her throat was tight. "The temples are emptying. The soldiers can't finish the Litany. Whatever feeding it's been doing quietly, all winter—it's accelerating. My congregation, my garrison, my *city*—" She heard her own voice crack and hated it and let it. "How much time do we actually have?"

Emrys was quiet a moment. When she answered, her voice was the voice she used for reading sums aloud at funerals: gentle, exact, merciless.

"Less than three days. And Sera—whatever happens at that ceremony, whatever it has planned for us—" she touched the journal in Sera's breastplate, briefly, like a benediction, "—remember this. It writes in your book. But you're the one who decides what's true. That's the one account it can't forge."

She slipped out into the graying corridor. Sera sat alone in the dark, listening to the horns on the far ridge begin again, thinner now, closer, like something breathing down the city's neck.

Against her heart, faint but unmistakable, she felt the journal move.

Not opened. Not removed. Just—turned, slowly, from within her breastplate, like a sleeper shifting toward wakefulness. Like a hand turning a fresh page.

And in the silence of her quarters, soft as candleflame finding wick, she heard the scratch of a pen beginning to write.
