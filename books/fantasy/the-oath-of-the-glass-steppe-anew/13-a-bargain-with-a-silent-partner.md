## 13. A Bargain with a Silent Partner

The chapel archive still smelled of the century that had sealed it—dust and candle-tallow and the faint mineral sweetness of old glass. Emrys sat on the floor with her back against the altar's stone foot, the twin prophecy book open across her knees, and did what she always did when the world grew too large to hold: she made an inventory.

*One god,* she wrote in the margin of a page Verity had left blank, *silent ten years. One parasite of glass and memory, feeding on oaths. One king, alive beneath it, if the archive is honest. One oathbreaker, willing to be forgotten.* She underlined nothing. Underlining was for conclusions, and she had none yet.

Sera Dain stood at the top of the narrow stair, keeping watch out of habit more than necessity. The captain had not slept properly in three days—the Regent rewrote her each night, and each morning Sera woke holding her hidden journal like a drowning woman clutching driftwood—but her eyes were steady now, steadier than they'd been since Emrys had first seen her flinch at the king's wrongness.

"You're writing," Sera said. "At a time like this, you're *writing*."

"Ledgers don't panic. That's their whole virtue." Emrys blew on the ink. "When I can't trust my memory or my courage or my guild, I can trust arithmetic."

"And what does your arithmetic say?"

Emrys looked down at the column of figures she'd been carrying in her head since the archive opened, the numbers she hadn't yet dared set down because setting them down would make them real.

"It says I'm going to make a very bad trade," she said.

* * *

She waited until the castle slept—or performed sleeping, which was the same thing these days, since whatever watched through Aldric's eyes never entirely closed them—and went alone to the chapel above the archive. Not to pray. She told herself it was not to pray. It was to conduct a transaction, and transactions required only two parties and terms both could accept.

The chapel was small and old, older than the castle around it, built by hands that had believed the god of the steppe might want a roof in the rain. The altar was bare. No idol, no icon—the Vellum Guild's faith had never tolerated graven things, on the principle that anything you could carve was smaller than what you owed. There was only a window of thick green glass through which the moon came down like light through deep water, and a floor worn concave by generations of kneeling.

Emrys stood instead of kneeling. It felt important to stand, tonight. Equals negotiated standing up.

"All right," she said aloud, and her voice fell flat in the empty nave, swallowed by stone. "I know you can't answer. You haven't answered in ten years. I've kept count—I keep count of everything, you know that about me. Four thousand three hundred and eleven days." She had counted them the way a creditor counts arrears: without heat, without forgiveness, because heat and forgiveness were luxuries the ledger couldn't afford. "Four thousand mornings I said the dawn words. Four thousand nights I closed the day's accounts in your name. And four thousand times, silence."

The silence now was no different from any other silence. Of course it wasn't. Silence was just absence wearing its usual face.

"But here's the thing I finally understand," she went on, pacing now, her boots whispering on stone worn smooth by other people's desperation. "Prayer isn't speech. I learned that in the guild. A prayer is an entry in a ledger. Every time I prayed to you and you didn't answer, something was recorded. Faith extended. Debt incurred. Ten years of unanswered petitions, all of it accruing interest in some account between us that neither of us has touched." She stopped at the altar and laid both palms flat on the cold stone, the way she'd lay hands on a contract to seal it. "So I'm calling in the debt. All of it. The whole decade. Every prayer I ever banked with you—I'm withdrawing the principal."

The moonlight lay green and cold across the floor. Somewhere in the walls, wind found a gap and moaned.

"I offer it as fuel," she said. "Not as worship. Fuel. Burn every scrap of faith I've stored up in you, drain the account to zero, spend me down to the last coin—and in exchange, put me where I need to be. Inside. Past the glass, past the lattice, past everything that wears his crown and eats her memories. Let me reach Aldric. Let me speak to the man underneath before the ceremony feeds that thing enough strength to swallow this city whole." Her voice cracked on the last words, and she hated it, and let it crack anyway. "That's the trade. My decade of devotion for one conversation. I know it's a bad price. Take it anyway. You were a merchant's god once—you of all powers should appreciate a buyer who knows she's being robbed."

Nothing. The candles she'd lit guttered in a draft and righted themselves. The moon moved a finger's width along the floor.

Emrys exhaled slowly through her nose. "Of course," she murmured. "Ten years of silence, and I expected—" She laughed, short and ugly. "*Expecting* is how they get you. Corvane taught me that too."

She turned to go.

And the world turned with her, inside out.

* * *

It began with the ledger. Not hers—a vast one, bound in something dark and veined like muscle, its pages turning without wind, and she understood with the certainty of dreams that this was the account between herself and her god, the true book behind all her careful mortal columns. She saw the entries. Thousands of them, tens of thousands, each one a prayer she'd spoken into silence: *keep Sera's watch unbroken; let the baker's son live; give me the numbers; give me the numbers; please, give me the numbers.*

Every entry was marked *received*. None were marked *answered*.

*I heard all of it,* said a voice that was not a voice, that arrived the way pressure arrives in deep water, from every direction at once. *Every word. I have been listening for ten years the way a man buried alive listens—to everything, unable to return even a knock.*

The ledger hall of the vision dissolved, and Emrys stood somewhere else.

She stood inside the Regent.

She knew it immediately, knew it the way one knows a room one has catalogued for weeks—the lattice-wards she'd mapped from outside were here around her, but seen from within, and they were not architecture at all. They were *bars*. Pillars of glass rose around her into a darkness that had no ceiling, and each pillar was packed with shimmering sediment: fragments of oaths, thousands upon thousands of sworn promises ground fine as sand, the stolen loyalty of soldiers and servants and kings compressed into glittering strata. The Regent's strength, she realized. Its whole terrible weight. It was built of other people's faithfulness, stacked like grain in a hoard.

And at the center—

"Oh," Emrys said. Her voice broke apart in the glass air. "Oh, *no*."

At the center, wrapped in chains that were also pillars, that were also the bars of the thing's own body, hung a shape of light.

It had been beautiful once. She knew that the way you know a face from a portrait ruined by water—the proportions were there, the grace, the suggestion of wings folded not in rest but in exhaustion. But the light was guttering. It flickered like a candle in a dying draft, dimming and rallying and dimming again, and around it the glass drank at its edges with slow, patient hunger, the way lichen drinks at stone.

Her god looked up at her.

She had imagined this reunion for ten years. In her bitterest imaginings—in the ones she'd never confessed to anyone—she had pictured anger. Recrimination. *Why did you leave me? Why did you go silent and leave me alone in a guild full of men who traded in souls by the pound?* She had rehearsed the argument so many times it had gone smooth as riverstone.

None of the rehearsal survived the actual moment.

Because the god was screaming. Had been screaming, she understood suddenly, with a horror that went through her like a spear through wet paper—for ten years, for longer, perhaps since the night Verity shattered the Regent a century ago and the binding had caught something it was never meant to catch. The god of the steppe had not abandoned her. It had been *swallowed*. Imprisoned in the belly of the usurper, its voice sealed inside glass, screaming wordlessly into the dark while its faithful counted the days of its silence and called it abandonment.

Four thousand three hundred and eleven days of unanswered prayer, and the answer had been there all along, a few miles north of her ledger desk, screaming behind glass.

Emrys dropped to her knees on a floor that was not a floor. The grief that came out of her was not elegant. It was not the composed sorrow of a woman who keeps ledgers. It was raw and animal and ten years deep, and it tore out of her in sounds she didn't recognize as her own.

*I could not tell you,* the god said, and its voice was ragged, worn thin as a psalter read nightly for a century. *A word spent is strength lost. I have been rationing myself for a hundred years—one word, then another, only when the bars thinned enough to pass them through. RUN. DIG. Each syllable cost me a season of fading. Do you understand what I am telling you, little clerk? When you withdraw your debt—when you burn your decade of prayers to force your way inside—it will not be fuel you are spending.*

Emrys lifted her head. Her face was soaked. She hadn't noticed crying until now.

"It will be you," she whispered. "I'll be burning *you*."

*A portion of me. The last portion that can act.* The light rallied, brightened, and she saw that it was doing it deliberately—squaring itself, the way Sera squared her shoulders before walking into a council chamber full of people who wanted her silent. *Hear the terms clearly, because I know you will insist on clarity. If you spend the debt, I can carry you through the glass to Aldric. I can hold the bridge open long enough for one conversation, perhaps two. And then there will be almost nothing left of me. Enough to see. Enough to witness what my faithful do. Perhaps—perhaps—not enough to survive the final breaking, when it comes.*

"When it comes," Emrys repeated. Not *if*. They both knew the arithmetic. Nine days, then three. The ceremony. The breaking.

*I have been imprisoned a hundred years,* the god said. *Do you know what I dreamed of, all that time? Not freedom. Freedom was too large a dream for the rations I allowed myself. I dreamed of being* useful. *Once. One act that mattered, at the end, chosen rather than extracted.* A pause, and when the voice came again it was gentler, and infinitely tired. *You are offering me exactly that, Emrys Almeida. You think you are robbing me. You are the first person in a century to ask me for anything instead of taking.*

"That's not—" Emrys pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "That's not a comfort. That should be a comfort and it isn't."

*Truth rarely is. That is why so few trade in it.* The light drifted closer, close enough that its failing glow warmed her tear-wet face, and for one moment—one unbearable, borrowed moment—she felt what she had felt as a girl on the steppe, the first time she'd prayed and been answered, the enormous particular attention of being known. *Ask me the question you came to ask.*

She lowered her hands. "Is Aldric alive? Truly alive? Not a husk, not a shell the Regent hollowed?"

*Alive. Stubbornly, magnificently alive. He keeps himself by naming his failures, one each hour, like a monk keeping his office.* The god's light flickered—amusement, or something adjacent to it, surviving even here. *He has been reciting the names of everyone he believes he has failed for three years. He has not once named himself among them. It is the most infuriating act of humility I have ever witnessed, and I once watched Saint Verity argue with her own prophecy.*

Emrys laughed—a broken, wet sound, but a laugh. "Then we'll get along."

*One warning,* the god said, and its brightness contracted, serious now, urgent. *The Regent will feel the bridge the moment I open it. It feeds on sworn loyalty, and a soul reaching toward a king through divine fire is a banquet it cannot ignore. You will have minutes. Perhaps fewer. And it knows your name already—it has read the same prophecy you have. When it sees you coming, it will not send guards.*

"What will it send?"

The god did not answer directly. Instead the vision shifted, tilted, and Emrys saw—as though looking down through the god's fading eyes—the throne room as it existed beneath the surface: the great glass lattice webbed through stone and timber and human minds, and at its heart the Regent, vast and patient, coiled around its food stores like a spider around an egg case. And coiled nearest of all, cradled closest, wrapped in the finest and densest strands—

Memories. Bottled years. A hoard of other people's yesterdays, and among them, glinting with a familiarity that stopped her breath, entries in a hand she knew better than her own.

Guild script. Guild seals. Entries dating back further than Corvane's tenure, further than any master she had ever served.

The guild hadn't merely known about the Regent for a century. The guild had been *feeding* it.

*Now you see why I chose this moment to break my rationing,* the god said quietly. *You needed to know what stands behind your masters before you stand before the king. Go home, Emrys. Sleep if you can. Tomorrow night, when the moon is in the same quarter it held the night you were pulled through the smoke—come to the chapel, kneel at this altar, and say the dawn words backward.*

"And you'll burn the debt."

*I will burn everything,* the god said, *and gladly. It has been a hundred years since anyone offered me the dignity of being spent on purpose.*

* * *

She came back to herself on the chapel floor, cheek against cold stone, the green moonlight lying across her like a sheet drawn over a body. Her ledger was still open beside her, the ink dry, her marginal inventory unchanged. *One god, silent ten years.* She took up the pen with a hand that trembled and struck through *silent*, and wrote above it, in letters small and fierce:

*Silenced. Imprisoned. Screaming. Forgive me.*

Then, beneath the list, she added a new line item, because she was who she was, because arithmetic was the only prayer she trusted:

*Terms accepted. Principal: 4,311 days of faith, plus accrued interest. Purchased: one bridge, one conversation, one chance. Cost to counterparty: everything remaining.*

She stared at that last phrase for a long time. Then she added a second line, the kind of entry no honest accountant makes and no honest accountant can resist making:

*To be repaid in full. However long that takes. Whatever it costs. This debt runs the other way now.*

Outside, the moon was setting. One day remained before the bridge could open. Two before the ceremony began.

And far below her feet, beneath the archive, beneath the chapel, beneath a century of buried truth, something vast and patient turned over in its sleep—and in a guarded cell in the castle's deepest level, Guildmaster Corvane smiled at the wall and said, to no one visible, "She spoke to it tonight. I *felt* the account move."

He turned his head, and the candlelight slid off his eyes like rain off glass.

"Prepare the third-day rites early. We're running out of stock."
