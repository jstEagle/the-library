## 14. The King Underneath

The names were the only thing that kept the walls of him standing.

Aldric Veyra had counted them so many times now that they had worn smooth as river stones, and still he turned each one over in the dark behind his own eyes. *Marta of the Lowbridge tanneries*, whose son he had sent to die holding a ford no one needed held. *Old Fenwick*, who had taught him falconry when his father was alive, and whom he had pensioned off with less than a cook's wage because the treasury was thin and the council was proud. *The crew of the *Kestrel*, eleven names, all drowned on a crossing he had ordered in winter to make a point about tariffs.*

He said them in order, every night, or what passed for night inside the glass. It had been four hundred and— he had lost count once, early, and the losing of it had frightened him more than the thing that wore his face ever could —so he started again. Four hundred and some nights. He recited the failures like a rosary, and the reciting was the prayer, and there was no one to hear it.

That was the trick of it, he had learned. The Regent fed on oaths, on loyalty, on the bright clean weight of devotion sworn aloud. But grief was not loyalty. Failure was not faith. The Regent drank from the wellspring of what men gave willingly, and Aldric had made certain that what he gave willingly, alone, in the dark, was nothing but his own guilt—and guilt, it turned out, tasted like ash to the thing behind his eyes. Every name he spoke was a stone he swallowed so the Regent couldn't. His kingdom had shrunk to the size of a penance, and he guarded it with everything left of him.

Tonight—or what he thought was tonight; time ran strange under the glass, like light through water—the names would not come easily. Something was different. The pressure at the edge of his awareness had changed its shape.

For ten years he had felt the Regent the way a man feels a coat: always there, always fitted to him, sometimes so close he forgot where his skin ended and it began. It sat behind his eyes and looked out through them. When he slept, it walked in his body through marble corridors and spoke treaties in his voice and smiled with his mouth at people who had known him since boyhood, and none of them flinched, and that flinching-that-never-came was the loneliest fact of his captivity. Either they could not tell, or they would not let themselves.

But tonight the glass was thinner. He could feel it the way you feel weather coming in an old scar. Somewhere far above his prison, something had cracked.

He gathered himself—not his body, which was a memory of a body, but the dense small knot of attention he had learned to be instead—and pressed upward against the inner surface of his own skull. Ten years of pressing. Ten years of silence answered by the vast cold patience of the parasite, which knew he pushed and found his pushing amusing, the way a man finds it amusing when a moth tests a window.

Tonight, the window was warm.

He pressed harder. And through the warmth came—

Light. Not the pale diffusion that passed for daylight inside the Regent's domain but true light, angled and golden, carrying smells with it: candle smoke, old vellum, ink, the iron tang of blood recently shed, and underneath all of it something he hadn't smelled since before his crowning—rain on hot stone, the smell of the courtyard where he'd played as a boy. A vision-bridge, though he didn't have the word for it yet. All he had was the sensation of a door opening in a wall he had stopped believing had a far side.

And a voice. A woman's voice, roughened by smoke and fury, saying words he caught in fragments:

*"—hear me? I know you're in there. The god showed me—I traded ten years of prayers for this, so you had better be worth it—"*

*Worth it.* Aldric almost laughed. The sound died in the glass. *No one is worth ten years of anything,* he thought at her, as hard as he could. *Turn back while the door holds.*

The vision steadied. He saw her then, or the idea of her, seen from every side at once the way one sees things in dreams: a woman in a mercenary's oilskin with ledger-squares of ink stained into her fingertips, dark hair escaping its braid, eyes that did arithmetic even while they wept. She stood somewhere real—a chapel, he thought, or the bones of one—and she had her palms pressed flat against a page of writing that glowed faintly gold, and her lips were moving through a prayer with the cadence of someone reading terms of contract aloud, because apparently that was how she prayed.

She couldn't see him. Not properly. He understood that with the sinking certainty of a man shouting up through ice. She was looking *through* him, at whatever the bridge showed her, and he was a voice at the bottom of a well.

So he did the only thing he had practiced for ten years in the dark. He made himself precise.

*I am here,* he pushed outward, packing every scrap of meaning into the smallest possible space, the way he had once compressed state correspondence into single lines to spite verbose ambassadors. *I am Aldric Veyra. I am alive. I am sane. Ask me anything.*

The woman went very still. Even across the bridge, even through two layers of reality, he felt her stillness land like a dropped anchor.

"Say your own name again," she said slowly, "and tell me the name of the man who taught you to read."

He didn't hesitate. *Brother Calloway of the Temple of the Quiet Hand. He rapped my knuckles with a ruler until I learned that mercy and rigor are the same discipline. He is dead now. I failed to protect the temple lands during the flood year. That failure is eleventh on my list.*

"Your list?" Her brow furrowed. "What list?"

And oh, how long it had been since anyone asked him a question meant only for him. He poured the answer out before the glass could thicken again, before the Regent noticed the crack in its window and came to seal it with its cold hands.

*The list of everyone I have failed. I say it every night. Marta of the Lowbridge tanneries. Old Fenwick. The crew of the Kestrel—Tomas Egan, Piet Aldous, the twins Sarel and Dannel Ovey—* he gave her all eleven, fast, before he lost his nerve. *It is the only thing the Regent cannot take. It eats oaths. It drinks loyalty. But shame given freely to no god at all—it has never learned the taste.*

The woman's expression did something complicated. Pity and assessment fighting for the same ground, and assessment winning, because she was the kind of person whose survival depended on winning that fight.

"The ledgers call it the Regent," she said. "I've been doing its accounting. Do you want to know what I've found?"

*Tell me everything,* Aldric said. *I have ten years of listening stored up and nowhere to spend it.*

She told him. She talked the way she must have done everything—efficiently, precisely, without ornament, and somehow the plainness made it worse. The feeding schedule. The oath ceremonies. General Mavros marching on cities that believed their king had abandoned them. Captain Sera Dain and her journal of eroding selfhood. The archive beneath the chapel. Saint Verity, a century dead, who had shattered this same creature once before by breaking every promise she'd ever made and letting herself be forgotten so thoroughly that not even history remembered her face. And Emrys Almeida's own name, waiting in Verity's diary a hundred years before she was born, patient as a trap.

"You understand what I'm telling you," she finished. Her jaw was set. "There's a version of the next nine days where I do exactly what Verity did. Where I break my oath, become the sacrifice, shatter the thing wearing you, and no one remembers my name by spring. Your guild masters planned it that way before I could walk. You're welcome."

*Aldric felt horror rise in him like floodwater.*

"No," he said aloud—his first word spoken with his own throat in ten years, and it came out rusted, ruined, barely a whisper, and it was the sweetest pain he had ever felt. "No. Listen to me."

She heard. Whatever the bridge was, it carried his voice now, thin and cracked, the voice of a man who hadn't used it except in dreams.

"I have spent ten years being eaten," Aldric said, and the whisper gained strength with every word, as if the muscle of speech remembered itself through sheer indignation. "Ten years watching my body sign things, smile at things, bless things I would burn rather than bless. And in all that time the one thought I refused to surrender—the one stone I kept in my chest when it pried at everything else—was that my suffering had a *price*, and that the price was paid by other people. Always other people. Marta's son. The Kestrel's crew. The soldiers Mavros feeds on. If your freedom costs another life spent in my place, then I am not a king rescued. I am just a larger hostage, traded for a smaller one, and the trade profits the same cruelty either way."

"That's very noble," Emrys said flatly, "and it's also arithmetic I reject. I don't rescue hostages on their own terms. I'm a mercenary. I set my price."

"Then name it."

"Both." The word came out of her like a blade drawn. "You and the city. Both walk out of this or neither does. I'm not taking your bargain, Your Majesty, and I'm not taking Verity's, and I'm certainly not taking the Regent's. I've spent my whole life keeping books for people who think every solution is a sacrifice, and I'm telling you the math doesn't require it. The Regent has to feed publicly within days or weaken past binding—that's a deadline, not a doom. Deadlines can be attacked. So here are *my* terms, and you'll note I'm offering them to a man trapped in his own skull, which tells you something about how desperate I am: I need you to fight from the inside. When I pour borrowed years into you—at the ceremony, at the moment of maximum strain—you will have strength that isn't yours and hasn't been for a decade. It will hurt. It may unmake parts of you that the glass grew around. Can you do that? Can you take back a body that hates you?"

Aldric was quiet for a moment. Inside the glass, he reached—tentatively, the way a man touches a wound he's afraid to look at—for the edges of his own flesh. He felt the lattice there, filaments of crystal threaded through muscle and marrow, grown comfortable, grown *architectural*. To tear them out would be to tear out pieces of himself. He might stand again and be half a man. He might speak again with a voice like broken crockery. He might remember his mother's face and never again.

"I have spent ten years reciting the names of my failures," he said, "because I believed that remembering them was the last duty left to me. You're offering me the chance to earn forgiveness instead of merely memorizing my debts. There is no version of that offer I refuse."

"Good." Something eased in her shoulders, though she tried to hide it. "Because I should warn you—when the god showed me this bridge, it wasn't gentle. It's been screaming in there with you, Aldric. Ten years. Did you know?"

He closed his eyes. Behind them, the glass glittered, patient, listening. He chose his words with a king's care, knowing the enemy might taste them.

"I suspected," he said. "Some nights the dark behind me wasn't empty. Some nights it *grieved*. I thought I was imagining company." His voice roughened. "Tell it—tell your god—that its prisoner hears it. Tell it that its screams kept me human when the names weren't enough. Two voices in the dark make a country, however small. We were a country of two, my god and I, and we governed ourselves with more honor than most nations manage."

Emrys Almeida, mercenary, auditor of impossible things, blinked rapidly and pretended she hadn't. "I'll pass along your compliments to the management," she managed. Then, quieter: "Three days. The ceremony accelerates. Day three, mid-rite, when it's drinking deepest—that's when its grip loosens. Sera will hold the guards. I'll hold the years. You hold yourself. Can you hold yourself for three more days?"

"I have held myself for ten years," Aldric said, "against a thing that eats devotion. Three days is nothing. Three days is a holiday."

Despite everything, despite the ruin of his voice and the crystal creeping visibly along the backs of his hands where they gripped the throne's arm, he smiled. And across the bridge, in a ruined chapel lit by banked gold, Emrys smiled back at a man she'd never met, and for a moment the vision held both of them whole.

Then the light began to gutter.

"What—" she started.

The cold came first. Aldric felt it the way he always felt the Regent's attention: a drop in temperature behind his eyes, a stillness in the air of his prison, the particular silence of a predator that has stopped moving because it has finally heard the mouse. The crack in the window was closing. He could feel the parasite pouring itself into the fissure like freezing water, sealing it, smoothing it, humming with something that was almost—but not quite—pleasure.

*Interesting,* said a voice that was not a voice, everywhere in him at once. *A door. Someone has built a door into my house.*

*Emrys,* Aldric said, fast, spending his final seconds like a bankrupt king spends his last coin. *It knows. Whatever comes next, it knows about you now—*

"It's fine," she said, though her hands were already moving, already snatching the glowing page away from the altar. "Doors lock from both sides. Get ready, Your Majesty. Three days."

The bridge snapped shut like a book closing.

Aldric Veyra sat alone in the dark behind his own eyes, in the country of two that was suddenly a country of one again, and waited for the cold to finish its work.

But the Regent did not seal the crack entirely. He felt it leave a sliver open—a thread, a hairline, deliberate as a spider's web left across a doorway. Through the sliver he heard it speaking with his mouth, to someone in the throne room above, in a tone of gracious amusement he knew too well:

"Send word to Guildmaster Corvane. Tell him his asset has gone... conversational. And tell him if she cannot be recalled, then the contingency proceeds early. We will simply have to find a new vessel."

A pause. The sound of parchment. Then, softer, addressed down into the dark directly at him, with terrible intimacy:

"Sleep well, little king. You have such interesting visitors lately. I do wonder who else knows you're home."

Far away, in a ruined chapel, Emrys Almeida blew out the candles and began, in a ledger with fresh pages, to write three columns: *what we have, what we owe, and what we will take back.*

Above her, in the hills, the first watchfires of Mavros's vanguard kindled against the night—two nights ahead of any projection, burning in a pattern she recognized from a century-old diary.

They had run out of time faster than anyone had calculated.
