## 15. The Ceremony of Chains

Dawn came to Veyra the color of a ledger's first page—blank, waiting, faintly gray at the edges where yesterday's ink had bled through.

Emrys stood at a window in the guest quarters Sera had arranged for her, watching scaffolds rise in the great square below. Not gallows. Worse. The guild built gallows; Emrys had seen enough of them to know their geometry by heart. These were altars. Seven of them, arranged in a ring around a central dais, and between them ran channels cut into the flagstones—shallow grooves filled with something that caught the early light and held it too long, too brightly.

Glass. The channels were lined with glass.

"Ten thousand expected," Sera said behind her. She was already armored, already armed, already wearing the particular blankness she put on like a second helmet when things went badly. "Every temple in the city has been ordered closed for the day. Every sworn soldier, every guildsworn clerk, every marriage-vowed couple—the Regent wants *everyone* who carries an oath inside them standing in that square."

"It's not a ceremony," Emrys said. "It's a harvest."

Below, workmen were unrolling a carpet the length of the square, crimson as an open wound. Somewhere a bell began to toll—not the alarm bell, not the hour bell, but a third tone Emrys hadn't heard before, low and sweet and wrong, like a note played on glass instead of bronze.

She had spent the night doing what she always did when the world tilted: arithmetic. Nine days, her calculations had given the Regent. It had taken three. Which meant either her numbers had been wrong—and Emrys Almeida did not often suffer wrong numbers—or the Regent was starving faster than its own consumption should have allowed. Something had been drawing on it. Something caged and screaming, spending itself in whispers.

*RUN*, the god had said. Then *DIG*. Two words in ten years. Emrys had begun, very quietly, to think of them as installments on a debt.

"Where will you be?" Sera asked.

"Where the wards are thickest." Emrys turned from the window. In her satchel: chalk of three colors, a vial of null-salt, Corvane's own authentication seals lifted from his traveling case two nights ago, and forty pages copied from Verity's diary in a hand so small it could pass for decoration. "The lattice that feeds the ceremony runs under the whole square. Seven anchor points, one convergence node beneath the dais. If I sever even two anchors, the harvest goes thin. Thin feeding means weak Regent."

"And if you're caught?"

Emrys smiled the smile she used across negotiating tables, the one that had closed contracts worth more than most baronies. "Then I'll be caught doing inventory."

* * *

The square filled like a cup fills—slowly, then all at once.

From the shadowed colonnade along the north wall, Emrys watched them come. Bakers still flour-white. Soldiers in half-armor, ordered to attend unarmed, which sat on them like a yoke. Old women holding the hands of children too young to have sworn anything yet, brought anyway because the summons had said *all loyal subjects*, and in Veyra, these days, absence read as treason. Ten thousand people shuffling into a stone bowl to pour ten thousand oaths down a throat made of glass.

The bell tolled its sweet wrong note again, and the crowd quieted the way animals quiet when a predator moves through the grass.

Aldric came out onto the dais at mid-morning.

He walked well. That was the horror of it. His spine was straight, his robes immaculate, his face composed into the mild benevolence of a king greeting his people. But Emrys had seen him through the vision-bridge, had heard him reciting names in the dark—every failure, every fallen soldier, every broken promise kept like coins against starvation—and she could see now what the crowd could not: the way he paused a half-beat too long at the top of the steps. The way his left hand stayed curled slightly, always, as though gripping something only he could feel. The way the light hit his cheekbone and *stuck*, just for a moment, refracting.

Glass, spreading up from the collar line like frost climbing a window. By evening it would reach his jaw.

Behind him, above him, *through* him, the Regent watched the crowd with eyes that were no longer entirely Aldric's color.

"My people." The voice carried impossibly far, riding the ward-lattice like a ship rides current. "For too long we have been divided. Today, we renew ourselves. Today, every voice swears, and every oath is *heard*."

The crowd murmured assent. What else did it have?

Emrys slipped away from the colonnade while the first procession formed, moving along the maintenance galleries beneath the stands—a warren of service tunnels built a century ago for festival logistics, and mapped in exquisite detail in a folio she'd borrowed from the castle archive and not returned. Her chalk marked the first anchor point thirty feet down the eastern gallery: a sigil burned into the stone, glass-grown over it like scar tissue, humming with power drawn from somewhere deeper.

Verity's diary had described this lattice. A century old, and the architecture unchanged. The Regent did not innovate. It repeated. It was a creature of ritual and recurrence, of oaths renewed in the same words in the same places until meaning wore smooth as river stone.

It also, Emrys noted with professional appreciation, had excellent bookkeeping. The lattice was balanced to the copper. Which meant it would notice imbalance immediately—but it would attribute that imbalance to *itself*, to hunger, to the strain of feeding. No system suspects sabotage when it can blame appetite.

She drew the null-salt vial, uncorked it with her teeth, and poured a thin line across the anchor sigil.

The effect was immediate and silent. The hum dropped a quarter-tone. Above, muffled through stone, the first ranks of citizens knelt, and the Regent's voice rang out the formula of fealty, and ten thousand voices began to answer—and the answering sound came down through the floor like water through a drain.

Emrys felt it pass beneath her knees. A pressure, a pull, a vast slow swallowing. Oath after oath after oath, each one a small bright thread of loyalty and habit and love-of-kingdom, unraveling out of ten thousand chests and running down glass-lined grooves toward the dais.

She moved for the second anchor.

* * *

By the third anchor, the square had changed.

Emrys saw it from a grate in the western gallery, looking down through iron slats. The kneeling crowds nearest the dais had gone still in a way that stillness should not go—too complete, breathing in unison. And Aldric—

Aldric was *glowing*.

Not metaphor. Light moved under his skin like lamplight behind vellum, following the paths of veins, and where it passed, skin became glass and stayed glass. The transformation had reached his throat. When he spoke the closing words of the morning rite—*and my will is the realm's will, now and always*—his voice came out doubled, harmonized with something vast and cold beneath it, and half the crowd sighed with pleasure at the beauty of the sound, and the other half wept without knowing why.

The glass climbed past his jaw.

*Hold on*, Emrys thought, pressing her palm flat to the cold grate. *You recited nine hundred names in the dark. You can hold for two more days.*

The fourth anchor was guarded.

That was new—that was *today* new—and Emrys flattened herself into an alcove and took stock. Two soldiers at the gallery mouth, but not palace guard; their armor bore the black-and-copper of Mavros's vanguard, an advance company arrived ahead of the general's army. Oath-fed men. Their eyes had the fixed lacquered look of soldiers whose loyalty lived outside their bodies, in a ledger they'd never read.

They were also, she noticed, standing exactly where the anchor sigil was.

Which meant the Regent knew its lattice could be touched, and had chosen to defend the anchors with men whose minds it already owned.

Emrys considered her options with a merchant's patience. Force was out; she was good, but two oath-sworn killers in a narrow gallery was arithmetic she lost. Stealth might work, but the null-salt needed direct application, and those two were close enough to smell it.

So she did neither. She opened her satchel, took out Corvane's authentication seal, and pressed it into a patch of soft mortar beside the anchor with a small written slip beneath it: *Inventory audit, Vellum Guild, item 4471, condition: compromised.* Let the Regent chew on that. A false trail pointing at the guild, at paperwork, at bureaucracy—the one predator it might not recognize as one.

Then she circled back toward the fifth anchor through the chapel undercroft, where the archive's entrance waited behind her broken seal like a held breath, and where DIG had led her and Sera into the dark.

The fifth anchor fell to null-salt without incident. The sixth was in the crypts, cold as a banker's heart, and she severed it kneeling among the bones of kings who had ruled before the Regent ever crossed the sea of memory to find a crown.

Six of seven. One anchor left, directly beneath the dais. The convergence node.

And as she rose from her knees in the dark, wiping chalk dust from her fingers, the bell began to toll again—and the tone had changed. The sweetness was gone. What remained was a sound like a wineglass developing a crack, note by widening note, and every hair on Emrys's arms stood up because she knew that sound.

Something in the lattice had noticed after all.

* * *

The seventh anchor lay beneath the dais in a chamber accessed by a stair hidden behind the royal standard. Emrys reached it as the afternoon rite began, and she heard the crowd before she saw the node: ten thousand voices speaking the oath in perfect unison, no longer called but *drawn*, the words pulled out of them like thread from a spindle.

The convergence chamber was beautiful. That was the worst discovery of the day. Glass had grown through the vaulted ceiling in branching fractals, catching torchlight and splitting it, and at the center, suspended in a lattice of crystal threads, hung a single drop of liquid glass the size of a heart, pulsing slowly.

Every oath in the square ran into that drop. She could see them—actually see them, faint gold filaments raining down through the stone, thousands upon thousands, each one labeled in her mind's eye the way she labeled everything: *marriage vow, medium quality; enlistment oath, degraded; coronation-era civic pledge, pristine, rare.*

If she destroyed the node mid-ceremony, ten thousand oaths snapped back to their owners at once. Some would break under the recoil. Hearts, minds, perhaps lives. She stood with her vial of null-salt and did the cruelest arithmetic of her career, and chose the smaller number.

"You always did prefer the books to the battlefield."

The voice came from the stair behind her, and Emrys's blood went cold in a way the crypt never managed.

Guildmaster Corvane stepped down into the glass light, and he was not dressed for travel. He was dressed for court—silver-gray, guild chains at his throat, and behind him came four guild enforcers and, worse, worse than all of it, a herald in royal livery.

"How," Emrys said quietly, "did you find me."

"The same way I found you twenty years ago, girl. You leave a paper trail because you believe paper is honest." Corvane's face was composed, regretful, the face he wore when foreclosing on a friend. "The null-salt requisition. Your access to the archive. And this—" he held up a page, and even upside-down Emrys recognized her own handwriting, copied from Verity's diary, "*—in your hand, quoting a text sealed by guild charter. You've been busy. I'll grant you that. You've been thorough. You were designed to be thorough.*"

"Designed," Emrys repeated. Later she would have time to be furious about that word. Right now she had seconds. "Corvane. Whatever you think you're doing—"

"What I'm doing," he said, "is damage control. The guild spent a century preparing for the Regent's return. We groomed oathbreakers, we seeded agents, we wrote contingencies in triplicate. And you—" his voice tightened, and for one moment the mask slipped and she saw not the master but the accountant, appalled at a ledger out of balance—"*you* are supposed to be our contingency. Not freelancing through the undercrofts cutting anchor lines. The prophecy requires an oathbreaker at the ceremony, yes. On the *third day*. At the appointed hour. Not—"

"Not on your schedule," Emrys finished. "On mine."

She threw the null-salt.

Not at the node—at the crystal threads suspending it. The vial burst, salt flashed white, and the suspension lattice shrieked like a saw hitting bone. The great drop of glass fell, struck the floor, and cracked into a spiderweb of dead facets, and above them the unison chant stumbled, faltered, broke into ten thousand separate confused voices gasping their own names as if remembering them.

And in that instant of chaos, Corvane pointed at her and said, clear and carrying, to the herald:

"There stands the assassin. Marked by prophecy. Guild-certified."

The chamber door above banged open. Light. Boots. Sera's voice shouting orders that were drowned almost immediately by other voices, and then hands on Emrys's arms—four pairs, six pairs, enforcers and guardsmen together, and she did not fight, because fighting here meant dying here, and dying here meant the seventh day belonged to the Regent.

But as they dragged her up the stair, through the ringing halls, out onto the high terrace overlooking the square, she looked back once at the dais.

Aldric stood at the railing, glass to his temples now, and the thing behind his eyes looked directly at her across two hundred feet of screaming crowd—and smiled with his mouth.

*Found you*, the smile said. *Item four-four-seven-one. Condition: acquired.*

They bound her wrists in guild manacles—her own guild's manacles, iron stamped with the Vellum seal—and the herald's voice rang out over the square, proclaiming a prophecy-marked assassin foiled in her attempt on the king's sacred person, and the crowd roared its fear and relief in the same breath, and Emrys Almeida stood in public ruin with her chin level and her heartbeat slow.

Let them look. Let them write it down.

She had six anchors' worth of sabotage in the lattice, a diary full of a saint's instructions, a king reciting names in the dark, and three days.

The arithmetic was not finished. It never was.
