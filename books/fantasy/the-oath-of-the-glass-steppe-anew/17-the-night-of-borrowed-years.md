## 17. The Night of Borrowed Years

The guild's vault door was made of a single slab of black oak older than the castle above it, and it opened for Emrys the way a dog opens for its master—with reluctance and with love.

Sera stood in the torchlight of the under-hall and watched the lock think. That was the only word for it. The mechanism had no keyhole she could see, only a brass plate inscribed with columns of figures, and as Emrys laid her palm against them the numbers crawled like ants, rearranging themselves into sums that balanced, and when they balanced, the oak sighed and swung inward on hinges that had not screamed in a hundred years.

"Ledger-locked," Emrys said, by way of explanation. "The vault opens to whoever owes it nothing."

"And you owe it nothing?"

"I owed it everything." Emrys's face did something complicated in the torchlight—a flinch folded inside a smile. "Until two nights ago, when I unmade the founding charter. Now the guild's claim on me is ash. Ash opens doors too. Come on."

The air beyond the threshold was cold in a way Sera had never felt cold before—not the cold of winter but the cold of *kept things*, of cellars where wine slept for centuries and woke unchanged. The stairs went down farther than the castle had height. Her boots rang on stone, and each echo came back a half-beat late, as if the dark were considering whether to return it.

"You shouldn't be here," Sera said quietly. It wasn't a warning; it was arithmetic. "If Corvane's people find you down here—"

"Corvane released me because his own paperwork cornered him, not because his heart grew three sizes. He'll spend today drafting new contracts that bind me some other way. Which means tonight is the only night I own." She glanced back, and in the gloom her eyes caught the light like coins at the bottom of a well. "Besides. You're my witness now, Captain Dain. Everything I take from this place goes in your journal. Two hands remember better than one."

Sera touched the leather-bound book at her hip without meaning to. Ten months of nightly entries, ten months of writing herself back into existence every dawn while the Regent sanded her down and rewrote her loyalties like a clerk correcting an error. She had thought, until a week ago, that she was alone in the war against forgetting.

Now she walked through a vault full of forgotten things, and every shelf was a mirror.

* * *

The bottled years were kept in the deepest gallery, in racks like the wine cellars of some vast and patient house.

Each bottle was no bigger than a fist, blown in glass so old it had gone faintly violet, and each held a color Sera had no name for—a slow amber swirl that moved against gravity, rising when it should have fallen, settling when disturbed. Labels hung from their necks on waxed cord, written in the Vellum Guild's meticulous hand.

She lifted the nearest one to the lamp and read:

*Borrowed from: Tomas Wren, cooper, age thirty-one. Term: nine years. Purpose: to outlive a debt-collection he could not survive awake. Repayment: never collected. Status: spent.*

"He paid nine years of his life to hide from moneylenders," Sera said. "And then?"

"And then the guild sold the years to someone who wanted to be younger, and Tomas got them back old and empty, and died at forty with the body of a fifty-year-old." Emrys moved down the rack, reading labels, pulling bottles with a speed that looked casual and wasn't. "This is what we do, Sera. This is what I did, for eleven years. I audited the inventory of people's lives and called it commerce."

"You're stealing them now."

"I'm *liberating* them." A ghost of the merchant's humor. "There's a difference, and the difference is what happens next. Watch."

She carried six bottles to a workbench at the gallery's end, produced a stopper of plain cork, and drew the first one free.

The sound was small—the soft gasp of a seal breaking—and then the year came out. Not poured; *exhaled*. Amber light unfurled in the cold air like a banner, and for one heartbeat Sera felt warmth on her face, smelled sawdust and river water and hot iron, heard distantly a man laughing in a cooperage—and then Emrys spoke a word in a language that predated the guild, and the light gathered itself obediently around her wrist and sank into her skin like water into drought-cracked earth.

Sera's breath fogged the lamplight. "What did you just do?"

"Armed myself with other people's discarded promises." Emrys flexed her fingers; faint gold traceries showed along her knuckles and faded. "Bottled years are time someone gave up. Spent oaths are loyalty someone threw away. Both are fuel, if you're willing to carry them. The Regent feeds on oaths freely given and renewed. So we're going to feed it a banquet of the opposite—oaths abandoned, years squandered, faith withdrawn. Poison dressed as a feast."

"How many do you need?"

"As many as the vault holds." Emrys was already moving down the racks again, faster now, her ledger-mind visibly working behind her eyes. "Day three is the moment. The ceremony runs three days because the Regent can't gorge all at once—it has to digest. By the third day it will have swallowed ten thousand renewals of fealty, and its grip will be iron. But iron glows before it bends. There's a window, Sera. The moment between the last swallow and the settling of the meal, when it reaches for the *next* thing and hasn't closed its mouth yet. We hit it there."

"And if we miss the window?"

Emrys pulled another bottle, broke it, let the year sink home. "Then Aldric wears glass forever, the free cities sign the treaty, and every oath sworn east of the mountains becomes food. So we don't miss."

* * *

They surfaced from the vault after midnight, laden like mule trains, and found the still-unsworn waiting in the guild's counting hall.

That had been Sera's work, and it was the work she understood best—not bottles, but bodies; not promises, but people. All day she had moved through Castle Veyra and the lower city with a list in her head and a question in her mouth, asked low, asked sideways, asked only of those whose eyes were still their own: *Have you been summoned to the Renewal? Have you felt your loyalty thinning like cheap wine? Do you remember your mother's name without checking?*

Forty-two had answered true. Forty-two guards, gatekeepers, kitchen-knights, and one deaf archivist who read lips like other men read letters. They filled the counting hall now, standing among the guild's great ledgistered desks, and they watched Emrys Almeida set her stolen cargo on a table with the care of a woman defusing something.

Sera climbed onto a desk so they could see her. She had never been good at speeches. She was good at the truth, delivered flatly, and she'd learned this month that flat truth traveled further than eloquence.

"Three days from tomorrow," she said, "the Regent holds its final ceremony. Ten thousand citizens, sworn aloud, renewed fealty. You've seen what happens to the sworn. Garrick, show them your hand."

Garrick of the West Gate stepped forward and turned his palm up. Fine lines of glass glittered beneath the skin of his wrist, catching the lamplight, delicate as frost ferns. He'd sworn the first day's renewal before anyone knew what it cost. He would not swear again, but what was in him stayed in him.

"That's one day's feeding," Sera said. "Two more days, and the glass reaches the heart. And it isn't only the ones who swear. Every soldier in Mavros's army outside those walls is oath-fed too—bound by conscription contracts most of them never read. If the Regent finishes its meal, it doesn't matter who wins any battle afterward. There won't be a *who* left to win it."

Silence. Somewhere above them the castle creaked, settling, or listening.

"So here's the plan," Sera went on. "We can't fight ten thousand. We can't even fight the palace guard, half of whom are already glass to the elbow. What we can do is interrupt. Day three, mid-ceremony, at the moment of maximum feeding, the Regent's grip loosens—one breath, maybe two, while it swallows. In that breath, Emrys pours a century of stolen years into the king, and we break the ward-lattice holding the throne room. Our job isn't glory. Our job is *seconds*. We hold the doors, we hold the aisles, we hold the line long enough for two people to do an impossible thing. Some of us—" she didn't soften it; they deserved the arithmetic, "—some of us won't walk out."

A voice from the back: the deaf archivist, signing while her granddaughter spoke for her, both of them steady. "Why us? Why not the whole city? Ten thousand people hate that thing."

"Because hatred is loud and the Regent hears loud things," Sera said. "And because the city has already begun to forget why it hates. Ask anyone on the street whether the king seemed strange lately. Half of them will say he seemed *better*. Calmer. That's the rewriting, working downward. No—we go small, quiet, and tonight, because by tomorrow night none of us may remember why we volunteered."

She looked out at the forty-two faces. Lamplight moved on them. And what struck her—not for the first time since the vault, not for the last time tonight—was how little they looked like soldiers and how much they looked like family. Strangers bound by nothing but a shared refusal to be eaten. No god behind them, no charter above them, no oath holding them together except the ones they chose in this moment, freely, knowing the price.

Found things, she thought. Found family. The kind you dig out of rubble.

"Those who stay," she said, "take a mark. Ink, not glass. On the left wrist, where Garrick's glass shows on his right. So we know our own in the dark."

Emrys rose from the table without being asked, uncapped the ledger-ink, and began, one by one, to draw a small symbol on forty-two wrists: a circle broken by a straight line. Sera recognized it from the archive beneath the chapel—from Saint Verity's diary, from the margin of the page where Verity had written, a century ago, *I break my chains so others need not wear them.*

An oathbreaker's mark. Worn proudly, for once.

They lined up to receive it like communicants, and Sera stood beside Emrys and watched her city arm itself with ink and stolen time, and for the first time since the night she'd caught the king's eyes shining wrong behind his crown, she was afraid and steady in the same breath, which she was beginning to understand was simply what courage felt like when it grew up.

* * *

Near dawn they worked by sections in the counting hall, pushing aside ledgistered desks to lay out the map of the throne room drawn from Sera's memory and Emrys's ward-sight, arguing positions in whispers.

"The east aisle," said Garrick, tapping the parchment. "The sworn enter from the east. If the lattice breaks while they're mid-feast, they'll panic, and panicked glass-bearers are weapons."

"Which is why the east is mine," Sera said. "Six of the steadiest with me. We don't fight the crowd—we *sing* to it."

They stared at her.

"There's a hymn," she said. "Old one. My grandmother sang it; half the city knows it without knowing it knows. *The Keeper's Lull.* It's about a shepherd counting sheep home through weather. It does something to people—settles them, gathers them. The Regent feeds on oaths shouted in fear and fervor alike, but it can't eat a lullaby. There's no oath in it. Just counting. Just names." She met Emrys's eyes across the table. "You told me once the Regent eats loyalty. Well. Grandmothers found the countermeasure centuries ago and never knew it. You get the crowd singing about sheep, and there's nothing in their mouths for it to swallow."

Emrys laughed—a short, startled sound, quickly smothered, but real. "Paper cuts deeper than swords," she murmured, "and lullabies cut deeper than paper. Fine. East aisle sings. West aisle holds. I take the dais with the bottled years. Sera—"

"I know."

"When it loosens, you'll feel it. Everyone will. Like a great intake of breath."

"Then we move on its exhale."

They bent over the map again, and the candles burned down, and outside the windows the sky went from black to iron to the pale bruise-gray of a morning that would be the second-to-last day. Somewhere beyond the walls, Mavros's campfires ringed the city like a lit fuse. Somewhere above them, in a throne room already half glass, the Regent sat wearing a king and counting its meals.

Sera rolled up the map, tied it, and wrote the date in her journal in a hand that only shook a little.

*Tomorrow,* she wrote. *Whatever happens tomorrow, remember: we chose this. Nobody fed it to us. We picked it up with both hands.*

Below that, without letting herself think about it, she added the names of the forty-two. All of them. Even the ones she barely knew. Especially those.

Someone had to keep the count. That was the whole war, she was starting to understand—someone had to keep the count, or the thing that ate memory ate everything.

She closed the journal, and the knock came at the counting-hall doors: three blows, urgent, wrong-rhythmed. Garrick opened them onto a messenger boy gray-faced and mud-spattered, one of theirs, sent up from the walls.

"They've come early," the boy gasped. "Mavros—they're not waiting for the treaty. Banners up, ladders out—they're hitting the gates at first light." He swallowed. "And the heralds are riding ahead of them, captain. Crying the proclamation in Mavros's name at every corner. He's declared King Aldric dead. He's declared himself the true heir." The boy's voice dropped, and the horror in it was worse than the news. "And the Regent—through the king—it's agreed. The crown has acknowledged him. Court says the succession is lawful, the ceremony's been moved forward—"

"How far forward?" Sera asked, though she already knew. She could feel it in her teeth, the way you feel weather turning.

"Day three is tomorrow," the boy said. "It's all happening tomorrow."
