## 18. Day Three: The Breaking of the Host

The bells of Castle Veyra rang wrong.

Emrys had spent three days learning the city's sounds the way she'd once learned a ledger's columns—by repetition, by rhythm, by the small deviations that meant someone was cheating. The morning bells should have rung nine times for the third day of the Ceremony of Chains. They rang eleven, and the last two notes came out flat, as if the bronze itself had been drinking something that dulled it. Glass did that to metal. She had watched it happen to Sera's sword hilt, to the chapel door hinges, to the eyes of ten thousand citizens who had already sworn twice and would swear again today until nothing of them remained unowned.

*Everything wagered at once,* she thought, standing in the shadow of the aqueduct with Sera Dain's hand on her shoulder and a century of stolen time in the satchel against her ribs.

"You're certain about the arithmetic," Sera said. It wasn't a question. Sera asked questions like that when she needed to hear the answer aloud one more time, the way soldiers recite their own names to remember whose body they're wearing.

"The Regent feeds on the ninth hour. Mid-ceremony, mid-swear, when the lattice is widest open." Emrys adjusted the satchel strap until it bit her collarbone. "For perhaps forty heartbeats after the feeding begins, its grip loosens everywhere else. That's when Aldric can move. That's when I can cut."

"And if your count is off by even a minute—"

"Then I'll be glass by sundown and you'll be next year's journal entry." She smiled, and knew it looked like Corvane's smile, all ledger-line and no warmth. "I've read my own obituary in a prophecy book, Captain. Whatever happens today, I've already made peace with the ink."

Sera didn't smile back. She squeezed Emrys's shoulder once, hard enough to leave a bruise she'd be grateful for later—a real pain, an owned pain—and said, "The unsworn guards hold the west gate. Forty-one men and women who never knelt. When the glass cracks, we crack it wider."

"When it cracks," Emrys corrected. Not *if*. A merchant never sold a certainty she didn't believe in.

Below them, the Ceremonial Steppe—the vast marble apron before the castle gates—was filling. They came in river-lines from every quarter of Veyra: bakers still floured to the elbow, dockworkers with tar under their nails, temple orphans in borrowed white, the old and the young and the hollow-eyed middle-aged who had already given too much and knew it and came anyway because the alternative was the army at the walls, Mavros's banners black against the eastern sky like a wound that wouldn't clot.

Ten thousand throats. One throat, soon, if the Regent had its way.

Emrys touched the satchel again. Inside, wrapped in guild-wax and prayer-cloth, were one hundred and twelve vials of bottled years—the vault's whole hoard, raided three nights past, each one a life somebody had traded away or sold or surrendered. Some held decades. One, she knew from the manifest, held sixty-one years bought from a dying woman who'd wanted her granddaughter to outlive her debts. Emrys had read the entry twice before sealing it into the satchel. *Borrowed things must be returned,* her first guildmaster had taught her. *Even if you return them broken.*

Today she would return a century to a king.

"Nine hours," Sera said.

"Nine hours." Emrys pulled her hood up and went down into the crowd, alone, the way oaths are always finally kept.

* * *

The ceremony floor smelled of hot stone and lilies.

They had carpeted the Steppe in white—white cloth, white petals, white-robed acolytes of the state temple chanting the Litany of Fealty in rounds so interlocked that no single voice could stop without breaking the pattern. The Regent loved patterns. Patterns were cages with pretty bars.

Emrys moved through the crowd with a courier's satchel and a servant's downcast eyes, and the wards let her pass because she carried, in her pocket, the guild charter itself—the founding document, the oath older than the guild, the paper on which Corvane had been cornered three days ago until he tore his own seal rather than break his own law. The charter hummed against her hip like a caged bird. Somewhere beneath the castle, in the archive beneath the chapel, its twin purpose slept: it had always been an oath to her god, signed by dead men who hadn't known what they were signing. Paper cuts deeper than swords. She intended to prove it one last time.

The dais rose ahead. Upon it, two thrones—one empty, draped in mourning cloth since the day General Mavros declared Aldric dead; one occupied.

Aldric sat beneath the crown like a man sitting beneath a glacier. His skin, where it showed at wrist and throat, had gone translucent, veins visible as frost-ferns, and behind his eyes the Regent looked out with the patience of geology. It wore his face the way a signet wears a crest. As Emrys drew near the front ranks, the thing turned his head—slowly, luxuriously, a connoisseur savoring the vintage—and found her in the crowd of thousands.

It smiled with his mouth.

*You brought me such a gift,* it did not say, but she heard it anyway, felt it pressed flat against the inside of her skull like a flower kept in a book. *A hundred years of faith, walking to me on its own legs.*

She kept her eyes down and her counting steady. Ninth bell in eleven minutes. The lattice—she could see it now, having learned to see it through the vision-bridge her god had opened—spread from the throne like frost across a window, threads of pale light running down through the dais, through the marble, into the roots of the castle and out along the roads the crowds had walked. Every thread ended in a person. Every thread pulsed faintly, hungry, waiting for today's swallowing.

Her god was in there somewhere. Caged in the Regent's center, screaming for a decade, spending its strength to whisper single words to her—*RUN*, *DIG*, and last night, through the bridge, a full sentence at last, ragged as a torn banner:

*When it feeds, it must open its mouth.*

Eleven minutes. Emrys took her place among the functionaries at the dais's base, between an incense-bearer and a woman whose job was apparently to hold a silver ewer of consecrated water nobody drank. Nobody questioned her. Power this old stops checking credentials; it assumes everything has already been bought.

The ninth bell rang.

And the Regent began to feed.

* * *

It was worse than the vision had shown her.

The Litany reached its closing round, and ten thousand voices spoke the words of fealty together—and the words *left* them. Emrys saw it happen, saw the sound become substance, a golden-grey tide rising off the crowd like heat-shimmer and flowing up the lattice threads toward the throne. People swayed as it went. A baker dropped his cap. An old soldier fell to his knees with an expression not of pain but of *loss*, groping at his chest as though checking whether something had been picked from his pocket.

At the throne, the glass spread. It climbed Aldric's neck in branching sheets; it silvered his cheeks; it took his hands where they lay on the armrests, turning knuckles to crystal, and inside the crystallizing cage of him, Emrys knew, the man himself was reciting names—the failed, the fallen, everyone he'd ever let down—holding himself together with guilt because guilt was the one thing the Regent couldn't digest.

Forty heartbeats. The grip loosening. *Now.*

Emrys stepped out of the line of functionaries, broke the ewer-bearer's concentration with an apologetic murmur, and walked up onto the dais as if she belonged there—as if she were the next item on the liturgy. The ward-lattice parted around her because the charter in her pocket was an older authority than any ward: an oath to a god, and gods outrank parasites.

The Regent was drinking. Its attention was a funnel aimed at ten thousand souls, and for those few precious seconds it could not spare more than a sliver of itself for the woman climbing its own stairs.

She reached the throne. She looked into Aldric's glassing face, and she said—not loudly, but clearly, the way you read terms into a contract—"King Aldric of Veyra. I have a delivery."

She opened the satchel.

One hundred and twelve vials caught the morning sun and threw it back fractured, a constellation in wax and glass. She took the first one—the dying grandmother's sixty-one years—and pressed it to the frost-fenned back of his hand.

"Bottled years," she said, "borrowed long ago. Returned now, with interest, to a man who'll use them better than their sellers feared."

She broke the seal.

The years came out like light coming out of a struck match—sudden, total, a bloom of gold that sank into Aldric's skin and *spread*. Emrys broke the second seal, and the third, and then stopped counting, working down the satchel with both hands, pressing vial after vial to whatever part of him was still flesh, pouring a century of other people's time into the king the way you'd pour water into a cracked pot and pray the cracks closed faster than the level fell.

Glass retreated. Frost-ferns faded. Color came back into his lips like dawn coming back over a ridge. And deep in his eyes, behind the parasite's geological stare, something *moved*—a man waking, a man fighting, sixty-one years and ninety-nine others' worth of borrowed vigor flooding muscles that hadn't obeyed their owner in months.

The Regent noticed.

It noticed the way an avalanche notices a snowflake—it turned the whole weight of its attention from the crowd onto her, and the pressure of that regard was physical, a hand the size of the sky pressing her flat. The lattice threads snapped taut around her wrists, her throat, her ribs. The crowd screamed. On the walls, horns began to sound.

*Little clerk,* the Regent said, and now it said it aloud, through Aldric's half-glassed mouth, a voice like a chandelier falling in slow motion. *Little ledger-keeper. You brought me a century and you'll watch me drink it.*

"No," Emrys said. Her knees were buckling. The threads were tightening. She tasted blood and realized she was smiling anyway, because she'd counted, and the count was good. "You brought *yourself* a century. I brought the delivery boy."

She put her hand flat on the frozen armrest, on the lattice's root, and she prayed—not silently, not habitually, not the way she'd prayed for ten unanswered years—but aloud, with her whole banked decade of faith behind it, every prayer she'd ever stacked like coin in an account no one honored:

*"You told me to come. Now TALK."*

* * *

The god spoke through her.

There is no honest way to make it small. It came up through her spine like a bell struck underwater, through her lungs, her throat, her jaw, and out of her mouth came a sound that was not her voice and had not been heard in the world for ten years—a voice like the first word ever said, like the last word anyone will say, a voice made of the memory of every oath ever kept. It rolled across the Steppe and the white petals lifted off the ground. The litany-chanters faltered and wept. The lattice threads around Emrys's throat went slack as cut rope.

And the sound *cracked the glass*.

Not only the glass on the throne—everywhere. The silvering on the castle walls split into spiderwebs. The Regent's frost-lattice shivered along its thousand thousand threads, and in the crowd, people gasped and clutched themselves as bonds they hadn't known they wore suddenly pulled tight and *creaked*. The Regent shrieked through Aldric's mouth, a sound like a mirror breaking in a locked room, and for the first time Emrys saw it clearly—not as the thing behind the king's eyes but as what it truly was: a lattice, a web, a contract written in glass, and contracts, she knew better than anything alive, have terms.

Through her, the god said one word more, and the word was not RUN or DIG but a name—

*Aldric.*

Inside the glass, the king moved.

Later, witnesses would disagree on what they saw. Some said light. Some said a man standing up inside a statue. Sera Dain, arriving at a run with forty-one unsworn guards and her journal clutched like a weapon, said simply: "He *stood*." Sixty-one years and ninety-nine others' worth of borrowed life surged through him, and King Aldric—glass to the elbows, crown askew, tears freezing on crystal cheeks—rose out of the throne that had eaten him and drove both transfigured fists down onto the armrest, onto the lattice's root, onto the contract itself.

The root cracked.

The Regent screamed with ten thousand stolen voices at once—the baker's, the old soldier's, voices going back a century, Verity's among them, *Verity's* above all—and every thread in the lattice whipped taut, and Emrys, still pinned at its center, still channeling a god with her mortal throat, felt the whole structure begin to come apart around her like a burning building deciding which way to fall.

She had wagered everything. The dice were in the air.

The lattice, dying, chose its direction—and came down *on her*.
