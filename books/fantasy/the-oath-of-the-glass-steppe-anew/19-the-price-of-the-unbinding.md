## 19. The Price of the Unbinding

The glass was singing.

That was the first thing Emrys understood in the terrible quiet after the god's voice cracked the lattice—understanding arriving not as thought but as sensation, the way a merchant knows a bad coin by its weight. The Regent's throne room had become a cathedral of broken light. Shards hung in the air where the ward-lattice had been, suspended like rain deciding whether to fall, and each shard hummed at a pitch just below hearing, so that Emrys felt the song in her teeth, in the hollows of her wrists, in the ledger-scarred pads of her fingers.

Aldric lay crumpled on the dais steps, breathing, alive—the century of bottled years she had poured into him still flickering under his skin like banked embers. Sera was shouting something from the doors, her voice arriving warped and slow, as though the whole room were submerged. Ten thousand voices beyond the walls, mid-ceremony, mid-oath, held in the Regent's grip.

And above it all, on a throne of black stone that had once been a king's seat and was now something older, the Regent was dying.

Emrys saw it plainly, the way she could read a forged signature. The thing behind Aldric's stolen eyes had worn its host for a hundred years, feeding on sworn loyalty, growing fat and faceted and beautiful the way ice is beautiful—and now the god's voice had run through it like frost through mortar, and it was coming apart. Cracks laddered across its form: not flesh, never flesh, but something like cut crystal given ambition. Light leaked from the fissures. Where the light fell, memories bloomed in the air—a child's hand in an adult's, a soldier's oath spoken at dawn, a queen's name spoken wrong at a funeral—all of them meals it had eaten and never digested, spilling out of it now like coin from a split purse.

It turned to look at her with Aldric's face, and Aldric's face was kind, and that was the cruelest thing about it.

*Emrys Almeida,* it said, and its voice was every voice that had ever knelt. Her guildmaster's. Her mother's. Her own, younger, swearing her founding oath with ink still wet on the contract. *You have done what Saint Verity did. You have cracked me open. Do you know what I kept for a hundred years? Everything. No one is forgotten while they are inside me.*

"I know," Emrys said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "I audited you."

Something moved behind the cracks—amusement, or hunger, or both; with the Regent the distinction had always been academic.

*Then audit this.* It extended one crystalline hand, and in its palm, cupped like water, there was a voice. She heard it before she understood what she was hearing: a low resonance she had spent ten years listening for in every silence, in every prayer that fell unanswered into the dark. A voice like a struck bell wrapped in wool. Like her name said by someone who had known her before she was a ledger-keeper, before she was a blade for hire, when she had been only a girl who believed that somewhere, something vast was watching over her and found her worth watching.

Her god's voice.

*It lives,* the Regent said gently. *Imprisoned in me these ten years—I swallowed it the way I swallow everything, and it has screamed in my belly ever since. But it is not dead. Break nothing. Kneel instead. Swear yourself to me, body and breath and memory, and I will make you my vessel as I made your king my crown—and the first gift I pour through you will be its voice restored. Every prayer answered. Every silence filled. You will hear it again, Emrys Almeida, for the rest of a very long life.*

The voice in its palm sang on, and it was singing her name.

Emrys stood very still.

She thought of Verity's diary, the pages she had read by candlelight in the archive beneath the chapel while the world outside emptied itself of oaths. *I broke every chain I carried,* Verity had written, in a hand that grew shakier with each entry, *and I let memory take me in payment, and I do not regret it, but oh, I am so tired, and no one remembers my face, and the book remembers only because books are stubborn.* She had thought, reading it, that she was learning how to win. She understood now that she had been learning how to lose well.

Because this was how it worked. This was always how it worked. The Regent did not conquer; it *contracted*. It offered, and the offering was real—that was the horror of it, the offer would be honored to the letter—and the price was written in terms too fine to read until you were already bound. Verity's successor. Another host. Another century of glass wearing a willing face. And her god freed of one cage only to be handed to another keeper, its voice restored and immediately owned again, screaming in a new belly forever.

She looked at the voice cupped in the crystal palm. It sounded so tired. Ten years she had prayed into silence and called it abandonment, called it faithlessness, built her cold mercenary composure on the foundation of *it left me first*. All that time it had been in here. Screaming. Hearing every word she prayed and unable to answer, the way a man behind glass hears everything and touches nothing.

*I know what you want,* the Regent murmured, softening, the way Corvane softened right before he asked her to falsify a column. *You want it back. That is not weakness. That is love, and I honor love. Only say the words.*

"The words," Emrys repeated.

*Any oath. Your choice of terms. I am generous in my dying.*

"Generous." She almost laughed. Around her the hanging shards trembled, and beyond the walls ten thousand throats waited mid-vow, and Sera was shouting at the doors, and Aldric breathed in ember-flickers on the steps, and somewhere inside this dying thing her god went on singing her name like a lighthouse that had forgotten the sea had frozen over.

She had spent her whole life reading contracts. It was time, finally, to break one.

"No," Emrys said.

The shards stopped trembling.

"You're offering me my god's voice in exchange for an oath. But here's what your own ledgers show—" and she lifted the copied page, Verity's diary, the prophecy book's twin, holding it up between them like a writ of execution— "you don't eat gold. You don't eat blood. You eat *sworn things*. Oaths are your food and your chains and your architecture. Every binding you've ever laid was built out of someone's promise."

*Yes,* the Regent said, and there was something new in its voice now. Something that had not been there a moment ago. She took a savage pleasure in identifying it: uncertainty.

"So I'm going to give you nothing to build with."

She reached inward, to the place where her founding oath lived—the first contract she had ever signed, the one Corvane had engineered, the leash disguised as a livelihood, *I swear service to the Vellum Guild until debt or death releases me*, sworn over a ledger, sealed in guild-wax, fed for fifteen years with her loyalty, her labor, her belief that she belonged somewhere. She felt it there, warm as a coal, familiar as her own pulse.

An oath was a two-ended chain. Corvane had taught her that without meaning to, cornered by his own charter in a cell below the chapel: *every binding binds the binder too*. And a chain, if you knew where the links were joined, could be undone from either end.

Verity had broken every oath she held and let memory consume her. Scattered herself into a hundred small unbindings until there was nothing left to hold, nothing left to remember her. That was one way. The desperate way, the way of a woman alone with no witness and no arithmetic.

But Emrys had witnesses. Emrys had arithmetic. And Emrys needed only to break *one*.

"You want a vessel," she said aloud, and her voice rang strangely in the singing air, and she realized the god's voice in the Regent's palm had gone silent, listening. "You need sworn material. Fine. Here is the largest oath in this room."

She spread her arms. The guild-mark burned on her inner wrist where Corvane had inked it fifteen years ago—not a tattoo, a *term of contract*, alive with borrowed authority.

"My founding oath. Fifteen years of service, sworn and sealed and renewed in spirit every single day I kept their ledgers. Thousands of hours of loyalty. Decades of accrued faith—you've tasted it already, haven't you? You told me so, the night I arrived. You said I came pre-seasoned." She smiled, and knew it was not a kind smile. "Come and take it."

*Emrys—* the Regent began, and its voice had lost all its borrowed warmth, and underneath she heard, very faintly, fear.

"Too late." She spoke the old words backward. Not reversed—*unmade*: the formula every Vellum clerk learned and swore never to use, the dissolution clause, the unwinding, the sentence that unraveled a contract from the signature-end. She had copied it out as an apprentice because her hand was neat and Corvane valued thoroughness. She had never dreamed she'd be its instrument.

"*What was sworn is unsaid. What was bound is released. Let the debt return to the debtor and the oath to the air that heard it. I withdraw my word. I break my chain. Witness me.*"

Sera witnessed—from the doors, sword drawn, shouting her name. The city witnessed, ten thousand strong. And the god witnessed, wherever it cowered inside its enemy's dying body, and Emrys felt its attention land on her like sunlight through a window after years underground.

Then she snapped the oath.

There was no sound. There was a *shape* to the absence of sound—a implosion, a place where noise refused to go. The guild-mark on her wrist flared white and then went dark, dead ink, ordinary skin. Fifteen years of sworn loyalty tore loose from her all at once, and Emrys cried out, because it did not feel like freedom yet; it felt like a tooth pulled from the jaw, a hemorrhage of the self, fifteen years of who she had been pouring out of the wound.

And the Regent—starving, cracking, desperate—lunged for the feast.

That was the trap. That was the whole of it, the gambit she had built out of Verity's diary and Sera's journals and nine days of cold arithmetic: the Regent could not *not* feed. It was dying, and here was a century-grade oath torn free and blazing, offered like a lamb to a wolf too starved to check the lamb for knives. It seized the unbinding with everything it had left, every remaining facet, every shard in the hanging air converging—

—on an oath that no longer existed.

It swallowed a vacuum.

For one heartbeat nothing happened. Then the Regent's whole body bowed inward, folding around the emptiness it had eaten, and the cracks that the god's voice had opened began to widen with a sound like a glacier calving, like a cathedral bell dropped from orbit, like every promise in the world being torn in half along its perforation. Because oaths held things together—it was the nature of oaths to hold—and it had just devoured an unbinding, and the unbinding went on unbinding, chewing outward through everything the creature was, through a hundred years of consumed vows and stolen crowns and swallowed gods, following the grain of every chain to its weakest link.

The Regent screamed with Aldric's mouth, and then with a hundred mouths, and then with no mouth at all.

"It eats oaths," Emrys rasped, to Sera, to the room, to herself, needing to say it aloud to make it true. "So I fed it the one meal that undoes eating."

The light burst outward.

* *

Afterward, she would remember it in fragments, the way one remembers any catastrophe survived: the shards falling all at once like the sky surrendering; the spilled memories rising off the dying thing in a storm—a century of stolen moments returning to the air, seeking their owners beyond the walls, and the great collective gasp of ten thousand people remembering something precious all at the same instant; the throne splitting down the middle like a loaf; Sera catching her around the shoulders before her knees gave out.

And in the heart of the collapse, one shape that did not scatter.

The god rose out of the ruin of the Regent the way smoke rises off a snuffed candle—vast and dim and ragged at the edges, thinner than it should have been, a lantern burned down to its last inch of oil. It had been imprisoned for a decade and half-digested besides, and it was dying, and it knew it, and it did not flee. Instead it turned what served it for a face toward Emrys, and the voice that had been silent for ten years spoke one last time—not a word, not RUN, not DIG, but her name, whole, unhurried, pronounced with the tenderness of someone who has rehearsed an apology for years and knows there is no longer time to deliver it.

*You heard me,* it said. *Every prayer. I heard every one. I could not answer. Forgive the silence—it was never yours.*

"I know," Emrys whispered. "I know. I'm sorry it took me so long."

*You broke your chain to free me.* The great dim shape bent closer, and she felt its regard like warmth on winter skin. *No one commanded it. No prophecy forced it. You chose. Do you understand what a rare thing that is? A sacrifice freely chosen is the only key that fits my lock. Verity was taken by her destiny. You walked in on your own feet.*

"Don't spend yourself on speeches," Emrys said, and her voice cracked at last, here, now, when she had held it together through everything else. "Seal the rupture. Go."

*One thing more.* It reached—not with hands, but somehow—and touched her forehead, and for a single instant Emrys stood inside her god's dwindling light and saw what it saw: the free cities lit against the dark like ledgers full of entries worth keeping; Sera's fierce loyal heart; Aldric reciting names in his ruined sanctuary of self; and herself, a small bright figure with ink-stained fingers, marked in the great account not as a servant, not as a vessel, but as *friend*. *The guild's charter was sworn to me. With me spent, it dissolves—unless someone rewrites it. Rewrite it, Emrys. Make it honest. Make it small. Debts forgiven, not owed. Promise me.*

"That's an order," she managed, wet-eyed and furious at her own tears.

*A request,* it corrected, with something that had once been humor. *From a friend. The only kind I accept.*

Then it poured itself out.

Not into her—she braced for it, ready to fight even this—but *into the wound*, into the vacuum where the Regent had been, filling the tear in the world with its final substance the way a man stops a breach with his own body. The rupture shrieked and closed. The singing in the air died mid-note. And the presence that had watched over her since childhood, that she had raged against and prayed to and mourned, thinned, and softened, and settled over the throne room like snowfall, like ash, like a blessing too gentle to be anything but final.

Gone. Not silenced. Spent.

The silence that followed was the truest silence Emrys had ever stood inside. It did not abandon her. It simply *was*—the silence of a finished thing, a ledger balanced to the last copper, an account closed in good standing. She knelt in the drifting motes of what had been a god and a monster and a king's long captivity, and she grieved, openly, hugely, the way she had never permitted herself to grieve in ten careful years.

Sera knelt beside her and said nothing, because Sera understood that some silences deserve company.

On the dais steps, King Aldric opened his eyes—his own eyes, glassless, terrified, alive—and looked up at the rafters where the light had been, and then at Emrys, and his first act as a free man was to whisper a name from his list of failures. Hers wasn't on it. He added it anyway, she was sure, out of sheer contrariness, and she found she didn't mind.

Outside, the ceremony of chains had become ten thousand people standing in a plaza with their oaths returned to them, raw and new and theirs, staring at their own hands. Beyond the walls, General Mavros's army waited with orders signed in a seal that no longer existed, soldiers whose conscription contracts had just lost their enforcing power—and Emrys, kneeling in the wreckage with her god's last warmth fading off her skin, felt her merchant's mind already turning, cold and clear and grief-shot, toward the satchel of captured ledgers Sera had smuggled from the archive.

Mavros had brought forty thousand men to these walls. He had made a fatal error: he'd brought their paperwork too.

Emrys wiped her face, stood, and picked up the satchel. Paper cuts deeper than swords. It was time to read the enemy his own terms.
