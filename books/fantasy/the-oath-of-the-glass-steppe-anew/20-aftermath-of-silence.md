## 20. Aftermath of Silence

Aldric woke on the floor of his own throne room, and the first thing he understood was that he had not been asleep.

Sleep was a country he remembered—soft borders, kind weather—but this had been something else. Ten years of standing very still inside a house made of glass while something else walked through the rooms of him, rearranging furniture, hanging its own pictures over his memories, letting visitors in through doors he could no longer find. He lay on cold marble scattered with the glittering wreckage of the Regent's lattice, and each shard caught the dawn light and threw it back at him, and every shard held a reflection of a face he barely recognized: thinner than he remembered, older, the eyes ringed with a decade of someone else's sleeplessness.

He tried to speak and found his throat full of dust.

*Say your name,* a voice in him advised—the voice of a habit ten years old, the discipline that had kept him sane when sanity had nothing else to feed it. *Say your name and count what you have failed.*

"Aldric Veyra," he rasped, to no one, to everyone, to the shattered glass and the smoke-stained vaults above. "King of the Free Cities, keeper of the Oathmoot, son of Maravel." His voice cracked like river ice. "I have failed my councilors, who begged me for answers I could not give. I have failed Sera Dain, whom I watched forget herself one night at a time and could not tell her so. I have failed ten thousand citizens who swore fealty to a thing wearing my face."

The recitation steadied him the way it always had. The names were ballast. As long as he kept naming his failures, he kept being someone who *had* failures—and a thing that failed was a thing that lived, that chose wrongly, that suffered consequence. The Regent had never once failed at anything. It had only consumed.

He pushed himself up onto his elbows. Across the hall, near the broken doors, two figures stood silhouetted against the gray-gold light: a woman in mercenary leathers, streaked with ash and glass-dust, swaying slightly as though the ground had become an argument she was losing; and beside her, holding her upright by one arm, Captain Sera Dain—Sera, whole-eyed, present, looking at him with an expression he had never once seen directed at himself in ten years of public life.

She looked at him like he was real.

"Your Majesty," Sera said, and her voice did the strange double-thing it must have been doing for years without his knowledge—relief and grief in the same syllable. She started toward him, then stopped, uncertain, as if approaching a man might be more dangerous than approaching the monster had been.

"It's me," Aldric said. And because he knew how those words sounded from a mouth that had lied for a decade, he added, "Ask me anything. Ask me the thing you asked me the night before it took me. Ask me about the harbor."

Sera went very still.

"You said," she answered slowly, "that you'd rather lose the harbor taxes for a year than see one fisherman's boy pressed into the wall-guard. You said a crown should be heavy in the hand but never on anyone else's neck. Then you apologized for keeping me past my watch, and you—" Her jaw worked. "You remembered my mother's name. Liria Dain. You'd only met her once."

"Liria Dain," Aldric repeated, and the name came up from somewhere deep and unspoiled, a coin from the bottom of a well, still bright. "She told me I talked too much like a ledger and not enough like a king."

Something broke open in Sera's face—not tears exactly, but the place where tears would live if she let them. She crossed the rest of the distance and helped him stand, and his legs shook beneath him like a foal's, ten years of stillness having taught his body nothing about motion.

Beside them, Emrys Almeida had lowered herself onto the bottom step of the dais. She sat the way a soldier sits after battle: all at once, gracelessly, as if her bones had filed their resignation. In her hands she held a ledger book, closed, both palms pressed flat against its cover as if it were a wound she was staunching.

And around her—Aldric felt it before he understood it—there was a silence with a shape to it.

He had spent ten years trapped behind the Regent's glass, and in all that time he had heard one other presence in the world besides his captor and himself: a vast, muffled, furious sound, far off, like the sea heard through a mountain. He had thought it was madness at first. Later he had thought it was hope. Only in these last days, through Emrys's borrowed bridge of prayer, had he learned its name—a god, caged in the same prison as him, screaming where no one could hear.

Now the silence where that sound had been was total.

"The god?" he asked quietly.

Emrys didn't look up. "Spent," she said. The word came out flat, merchant-precise, an entry in a ledger. "Not silenced. Spent. There's a difference. A silenced thing can be found again." Her thumb moved along the ledger's edge, back and forth, back and forth. "It gave everything it had left to seal the rupture. To keep whatever was on the other side of the Regent from pouring through into us." At last she raised her head, and Aldric saw that her eyes were dry in the terrible way of eyes that have decided crying is a debt they cannot yet afford. "I heard it say my name. Once. Right at the end. That was the whole inheritance."

"I'm sorry," Aldric said, and meant it with the whole ruined weight of his chest.

"Don't be." She stood, using the dais rail, and when she turned to face him there was something new in her bearing—not softer, exactly, but *unlocked*. "Be sorry for yourself instead. You've got ten years of a city to convince you're not still wearing it."

That, Aldric thought, was the true battlefield now. Not the walls. Not Mavros's host camped beyond the river like a second dusk. This room, and the thousand rooms like it in every mind in Veyra, where a king's face had become synonymous with a parasite's hunger.

He looked down at his hands. Glass still glinted faintly in the creases of his knuckles, fine as sugar. When he flexed his fingers, they ached from wrist to fingertip, and in the ache was a memory of the feeding—the ceremony three days ago, ten thousand voices swearing themselves upward, and the sweetness of it, gods forgive him, the *sweetness*, because the Regent had let him taste it too, had made him complicit even in captivity, savoring through his nerve-endings the loyalty it drank. He would carry that sweetness in his memory until he died. That was part of the price no one had warned him about: rescue did not unmake what the prisoner had been made to feel.

"Sera," he said. "How bad is it out there?"

Sera's mouth twisted. "Bad in ways we don't have words for yet. Half the temples are empty—the sworn soldiers woke up yesterday feeling like they'd lost a limb, and half of them think the loss is *you*. They can't feel their oaths anymore, and an oath-sworn man without an oath is a man falling forever. Some are deserting north to Mavros because he promises them something to hold." She hesitated. "Some are just sitting in the squares, waiting for orders that feel like theirs again."

"And the council?"

"Divided. Councilor Ives wants to declare you recovered and move on. Two others want a rite of verification—they've sent for the guild's authenticators." Her eyes flicked, briefly, to Emrys. "Which is its own problem, given what came out at the ceremony about the guildmaster."

"Corvane," Emrys said, and the name had iron filings in it. "Is in custody. Charming cells, actually. I inspected them personally during my recent incarceration." She said it lightly, and none of it was light.

Aldric straightened as much as his body allowed. Ten years of watching through glass had taught him one skill above all others: reading rooms. He read this one now—three exhausted people in a wrecked throne room, an army at the walls, a city holding its breath—and he understood that the hardest campaign of his reign was about to begin, and it would be fought without a single sword.

"Then we start," he said, "with the truth. All of it. Before anyone else can sell the city a version with the inconvenient parts removed."

* * *

They carried him out onto the eastern balcony at noon, and the city of Veyra rose below him like a question.

Smoke still feathered up from the ward-lattice towers where Emrys's sabotage had burned. The great squares were packed—not with celebrants but with *waiters*, thousands of faces tilted upward, and the quality of their waiting made his skin prickle. It was not the waiting of a crowd hoping for good news. It was the waiting of people who had already been lied to magnificently for a decade and had learned, at cost, to keep their hope in a locked box.

Below the balcony, arranged in a wary crescent, stood the remnant of the palace guard—perhaps four hundred of them, the unsworn and the newly-unbound, Sera's people, holding pikes with white knuckles because they did not know yet whether they were protecting their king or facing a shapeshifter wearing his skin.

Aldric stepped to the balustrade alone.

His legs wanted to fail him. He let them know, privately, what would happen to their careers if they tried.

"People of Veyra," he began, and his voice—thin, rusted, a hinge unused for ten years—carried poorly. He stopped. Started again. Stopped again. Below, he saw the crowd begin to murmur, saw the murmuring take the shape he dreaded: *it sounds wrong, it sounds weak, the thing spoke better than that.* Of course it had. The Regent had used his voice like a virtuoso; Aldric had spent a decade unable to use it at all.

So he did the thing that no usurper, no parasite, no clever impostor would ever think to do.

He stopped performing.

"My voice is broken," he said simply, and let them hear it break. "Ten years of disuse will do that. So will screaming, which I have done a great deal of, though none of you could hear it. I am not going to stand here and give you the speech you deserve, because I cannot give it well, and you have earned better than a badly delivered comfort." He gripped the stone. "Instead I am going to do the only thing I have left. I am going to tell you what happened to you, and let you decide whether I'm worth believing."

Silence, vast and hostile, from the square.

"For ten years, you have sworn oaths to a king who could not hear them. Every renewal of fealty, every temple vow, every soldier's enlistment—you gave your loyalty into the keeping of my face, and something else drank it. You felt yourselves grow... quieter. More obedient. You told yourselves it was peace. It was feeding." A ripple moved through the crowd, ugly, alive. "Three days ago, many of you swore again at the Ceremony of Chains, and some of you have woken since feeling hollowed out. That hollowness is real. Your loyalty was taken. I cannot give it back—I don't know how, and I won't pretend. What I can tell you is that the thing that took it is dead. Shattered. By a woman named Emrys Almeida, who paid more for your freedom than any of us will ever fully reckon."

He turned his head. Emrys stood back from the balustrade, arms folded, visibly refusing the moment. He spoke her name anyway. Let the city have someone to thank who wasn't suspect.

"And now," he continued, "the hard part. You look up here and you see the face that presided over your slow theft. You have every reason to doubt me. Doubt me, then. Test me. Send your priests, your guild authenticators, your grandmothers who remember my father. I will submit to any scrutiny you devise, publicly, repeatedly, for as long as it takes—because a king who fears examination has already confessed to something." His voice found a fraction of strength, or perhaps the crowd lent it to him. "I spent ten years memorizing the names of everyone I failed, because names were all I could keep. I will spend the next ten years earning back the right to hear them spoken kindly. That work starts today, whether you believe me or not."

For a long moment, nothing.

Then, from somewhere deep in the square, a single voice—an old woman's voice, cracked and carrying—called out: *"Liria Dain's boy used to feed the gulls off the Tanners' Walk. Do you remember what she called them?"*

Aldric closed his eyes. The memory surfaced whole, uncorroded, from a stratum of himself the Regent had never bothered to excavate because it was too small, too human, too worthless to a thing that ate only oaths.

"She called them 'the lords,'" he said. "Because they squabbled over everything and produced nothing, and she fed them anyway. She said it was good practice for court."

The laugh that went up from the square was ragged, half-sobbed, disbelieving—and it was the first sound in ten years that Aldric had heard aimed at *him*, and it undid him more thoroughly than the Regent ever had. He stood at the balustrade while the sound washed over him, gripping the stone, letting it hurt.

Behind him, Sera murmured, "They'll come round. Slowly. Belief is a crop, not a switch."

"I know," Aldric said. "I've got time. It's the only thing I've got plenty of."

* * *

Evening brought the war back.

He received the report in the small map room where his father had once planned harvest festivals, because the great war room still smelled of glass-smoke. Sera stood over the table with a courier's dispatch in her fist, and Councilor Ives hovered at the door wearing the expression of a man who has survived one monster and suspects the world keeps them in stock.

"Mavros hasn't moved," Sera said. "That's what worries me. Forty thousand soldiers camped on the far bank of the Serle, and for two days—nothing. No siege lines, no probing attacks, no parley. Just..." She searched for the word. "*Waiting.* Like a dog that's lost its master and doesn't know whose whistle to answer."

"Because they have," Aldric said quietly.

Ives frowned. "Majesty?"

"The oath-fed soldiers. Their loyalty wasn't to Mavros—it was routed through him, from the Regent. The Regent's command-seal fed them; we saw the taint in his signet at the ceremony." Aldric traced the river on the map with a finger that still trembled. "Whatever bond held forty thousand men in marching order died on the dais three days ago. Mavros is commanding an army of sleepwalkers who have begun, one by one, to wake up. And waking men ask questions. *Why am I here? Who promised me what? What exactly did I swear?*" He looked up. "An army in that condition doesn't attack. It *fractures*. And Mavros knows it. Which means he needs a victory—or a massacre, or a miracle—before the fracture reaches him. Desperate men with armies do desperate arithmetic."

"Then we fortify," Ives began. "We can hold the walls if—"

"No." Aldric said it gently, and watched the councilor stop as if he'd struck a bell. "If we fight them, we kill men who were stolen before they were defeated. Fishermen's boys, Ives. Pressed into service under oaths they never understood, fed upon by the same thing that fed on me, and now we'd put steel through them for the crime of being enslaved badly." He straightened, and for a moment the trembling left his hands. "I spent ten years listening to that thing plan battles. I know exactly how many of us die holding these walls against forty thousand, even weakened ones. I also know something it never learned, because it couldn't: paper cuts deeper than swords, in the right hands. And I happen to know where the best hands in the free cities are currently standing."

He turned. Through the map room's narrow window, across the courtyard, lamplight burned in the Vellum Guild's temporary offices, where a woman with a merchant's cold precision and a mercenary's stubborn heart was, at that hour, almost certainly doing what she did best: reading other people's ledgers.

Somewhere in Mavros's baggage train, Aldric would wager his fragile new credibility, marched the conscription rolls. Every forced signature. Every oath extracted under false terms. Forty thousand contracts, each one containing, in some clause or codicil, the truth of what those soldiers had actually agreed to—and what had been done to them beyond it.

"Send for Almeida," he said. "Tell her I need an auditor. Tell her the account in question is an army."

Sera was already moving for the door. But at the threshold she paused, and looked back at him with an expression he was learning to bear—that raw, unguarded belief, still so new it chafed like a new boot.

"There's one more thing," she said. "The watch on the river reports torches moving in Mavros's camp tonight. Hundreds of them. Not battle formations—" she hesitated— "gatherings. Soldiers clustering around fires, and officers riding fire to fire like they're trying to put out a blaze nobody can see." Her voice dropped. "Whatever's happening in that army, Majesty, it's happening fast. Whatever she reads in those ledgers—we'll need it by dawn."

Beyond the window, across the darkening river, the enemy's fires multiplied along the horizon like a fever breaking out across skin—and Aldric, king twice over now, once by blood and once by survival, felt the old fear rise in him and set it, deliberately, on the shelf beside his named failures, where it belonged.

"Then pray she works quickly," he said. "And this time, when we pray—"

He stopped. The habit of a lifetime, addressing a god who had finally, truly gone silent, and no one left to hear it but the three of them and the city.

"This time," he finished quietly, "we'll have to make do with each other."
