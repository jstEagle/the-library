## 21. The Battle of the Free Cities

The army arrived at dawn on the fourth day, and it did not come like a storm.

That was the first thing Emrys noticed from the eastern wall, her breath fogging in the cold gold light: storms announce themselves. Storms have thunder before lightning, wind before rain, a pressure in the ears that makes animals restless and old soldiers superstitious. Mavros's host came instead like a tide chart made flesh—orderly, silent, inevitable. Forty thousand men in ranks so straight they might have been ruled onto the plain with a guildsman's stylus. Their banners hung dead in the still air. Not one of them sang, or jeered, or beat spear against shield, as armies had done since armies were invented.

They simply stood, and waited to be spent.

"They're not soldiers," Sera said beside her. Her voice was hoarse from three days of command, from pulling half-drowned guardsmen out of the Regent's undertow, from shouting Aldric's name at crowds who needed convincing. "Look at their eyes. I've seen it before—in the ones who'd been sworn twice. They're not here because they chose to be. They're here because someone holds the other end of them."

Emrys held up the ledger she had carried out of Mavros's abandoned field camp—the conscription rolls his quartermasters had left behind in their haste, along with everything else that could burn. She had spent the night reading them. All of them. Four hundred pages of small, careful handwriting, and beneath each soldier's name, in ink so pale most men would never notice it, the terms.

*In consideration of bread, lodging, and protection of kin, the undersigned grants unto General Mavros Veyra the full term of his obedience, such term to endure until lawfully discharged or death, whichever comes first.*

*Death,* Emrys thought, looking down at the sea of waiting faces, *whichever comes first.*

"Paper cuts deeper than swords," she said. It was the first lesson Corvane had ever taught her, back when she was nineteen and hungry and grateful for every cruel kindness the guild paid her. She had hated him for it once. She was about to make it a weapon.

Sera looked at her. "You want to *read to them.*"

"I want to read *their own signatures* to them," Emrys said. "There's a difference. Swords tell a man he is beaten. Paper tells him he was never asked."

---

By midmorning the walls of Castle Veyra's lower city were crowded—not with archers but with readers.

Emrys had requisitioned every crier the city employed, every temple bell-ringer with a voice trained to carry, every schoolmaster whose lungs had survived thirty years of lecture halls. Sera had found her twelve veterans of the guard whose diction was good enough to make a drunkard weep. They stood at intervals along the battlements with copies of the conscription ledger, and below them, on wooden platforms hastily nailed together in the market square, stood the amplifying horns the guild had once used to auction bottled storms—impossible things repurposed, Emrys noted with some private satisfaction, into honest ones.

Aldric stood at the center of the wall, wrapped in a plain grey cloak, no crown. He had insisted on being there. His hands still trembled sometimes; there were hours when glass showed faintly at the edge of his jaw, a shimmer like frost on a window, and he would press his palm flat against stone until it faded. But his voice, when he spoke, was his own, and it was steady.

"You're certain?" he asked Emrys. "Once you begin, they'll hear all of it. Some of those terms will wound more than any arrow. Men will learn what price their families paid for their enlistment."

"That's rather the point, Your Majesty."

"And if it fails? If they march anyway?"

Emrys looked out over the plain. Forty thousand men. The free cities behind her held perhaps ninety thousand souls, half of them children and grandmothers and candlemakers, and the garrison could not field two thousand swords. Steel would bleed this city dry in an afternoon, and even victory would look like a graveyard.

"Then we lose," she said, "and I will stand on this wall and read the casualty lists afterward, too, so that no one gets to pretend they didn't know the cost. That's what ledgers are for, Your Majesty. Not comfort. Record." She flexed her fingers around the book's spine. "But it won't fail."

"How can you know?"

"Because I've been a mercenary for eleven years," Emrys said. "And I know what it does to a man, finding out the fine print. He doesn't get angry at the enemy. He gets angry at himself for not reading. And a man angry at himself will do almost anything to prove he isn't a fool." She turned to Aldric, and something in her chest—some old, scarred-over place where the god's voice used to live—ached suddenly, unexpectedly. "My king. Trust me. I am very good at making people feel foolish."

Aldric studied her for a long moment. Then he nodded once and stepped back, giving her the wall.

Emrys climbed the platform. The morning air was cold and smelled of woodsmoke and thawing earth and, faintly, of the river. Below her the army waited, forty thousand pairs of eyes fixed forward at nothing. Above the enemy host, on a ridge a mile out, Mavros's command tent flew his personal standard—a black gauntlet on white—and Emrys knew that somewhere inside it, the general was watching, waiting for the parley that never came, the charge that never came, wondering what the city was doing with its silence.

She opened the ledger.

"My name is Emrys Almeida," she said, and the amplification horns took her voice and threw it across the plain like a thrown stone skips across water. "I keep accounts. That is my whole trade, and it is a dull one, and I apologize in advance for the tedium of what follows. But your general has left his books behind him, and by the law of every free city—including the ones he claims to have conquered—abandoned property belongs to whoever finds it. So I found these. And I am going to read you your own contracts."

A ripple went through the ranks. Small, uncertain. Men turning to look at men.

"Article One," Emrys read, and her voice did not waver, because she had rehearsed it in the dark before dawn, because she had learned from a silent god that words spoken plainly are worth more than words shouted. "*In consideration of bread, lodging, and protection of kin, the undersigned grants unto General Mavros Veyra the full term of his obedience.*

"Bread," she repeated. "Lodging. Protection of kin. Let us check the accounting, shall we? Article Nine: rations may be reduced during campaign at the quartermaster's discretion. Article Eleven: lodging consists of shared tent canvas, one blanket per two men. Article Fifteen—" and here she paused, and let the pause do its work, the way a good auditor always lets the numbers breathe— "*the undersigned acknowledges that protection of kin extends only to kin residing within territory controlled by General Mavros, and shall lapse entirely upon the undersigned's death, desertion, or capture.*"

Silence on the plain. The kind of silence that has weight.

"If you die," Emrys said gently, "your family loses the protection you signed away your life for. Read it yourselves—I'm holding the page up now, and our scribes have posted copies at every gate, and any man who walks to the walls unarmed will be given one freely, to take home, to show his wife, to argue over by firelight. We are not asking you to believe us. We are asking you to *read.*"

Somewhere far off on the ridge, a horn blew—a single sharp note of alarm, or anger. Emrys ignored it.

---

She read for four hours.

She read Article Twenty-Two, which permitted Mavros to transfer a soldier's oath to "any party designated by the general, without further consent required"—and watched ten thousand men understand, all at once, in the same terrible instant, why they had felt hollow for months, why their loyalties thinned like watered wine, why they had marched toward a city they had no grievance against. The Regent was gone, shattered, sealed—but its taint lived on in the machinery it had built, in the transfer clauses, in the pale ink. The oaths were still moving. Still owned. Still *eatable.*

She read Article Thirty-One: *the undersigned waives all right to lawful discharge except upon presentation of written release bearing the general's seal, which seal the undersigned acknowledges may be applied without his presence or knowledge.*

"There it is," Emrys said, and her voice cracked just slightly, and she let it, because a little humanity carries farther than perfect diction. "Your discharge exists. It's real. It's in this book, in the appendix, pages three hundred and six through four hundred and twelve—every man in this army has a lawful discharge already prepared, dated and stamped, waiting only for a seal. Your general has been holding your freedom in a drawer for months. He was going to use it as a bargaining chip. Or spend it. You know what spending looks like by now. You've felt it."

And then—the part she had saved, the part she had argued with Sera about until midnight, because Sera wanted it first and Emrys insisted last things should land hardest—she read them the command seal.

Mavros's own seal, pressed into every order, every banner-oath, every article of the conscription charter. The guild's authentication rites had taken apart the impression in wax and found what lay underneath, layered into the matrix itself like a worm in fruit: a lattice of glass-fine script, Regent-work, feeding. Every order the general signed renewed the oaths of everyone who obeyed it. Every battle flag raised in his name poured a little more of his soldiers' loyalty into the channel the parasite had dug before Emrys broke it—and the channel, empty of its master, ran now to whoever held the seal.

"Mavros thinks he commands this army," Emrys said. "Gentlemen. The seal commands *him.* Ask yourselves why a man who claims to mourn his king has never once removed his gauntlet. Ask what he agreed to, and when, and whether he remembers agreeing. We do not ask you to attack him. We ask you to look at him. Truly look. You are oath-sworn men; you know better than anyone alive how to see the shape of another man's chains."

She closed the book.

"That is all," she said. "No ultimatum. No deadline. Just the record. The gates will remain open until sundown. Any man who walks through them unarmed will be fed—bread, lodging, protection of kin, exactly as promised, honored to the letter, because unlike your general, we keep our contracts. And any man who stays on that plain tonight—" her voice softened, and the horns carried the softness tenderly, like a hand laid on a shoulder— "we will read to you again tomorrow. And the day after. As long as it takes. Truth keeps, gentlemen. It's the one commodity in my trade that never spoils."

She stepped down from the platform. Her legs shook. Sera caught her elbow.

"Well?"

Emrys exhaled. "Now we wait. And we pray—" she stopped, corrected herself, the old reflex dying hard— "we *hope*."

---

The desertions began at noon.

Not as a flood—as a seep. A single soldier near the right flank set down his spear, picked up his kit, and walked westward with the deliberate calm of a man going to market. No one stopped him. Then three more. Then a file. Then a whole company turned as one body, wheeled, and marched, and the officers who tried to bar their way found themselves staring into the faces of men who had finally *read*, and there is nothing on earth more immovable than a man who has checked the arithmetic.

By midafternoon the plain looked like ice breaking up in spring—great drifting floes of men separating from the mass, the formation rotting outward from its edges. The ridge erupted into chaos Emrys could only partially see: riders galloping between units, Mavros's elite guard forming a ring around the command tent, banners dropping one by one like leaves letting go.

What happened next, she learned later, from the mouths of the deserters themselves, from sergeants who walked through the gates with their hands raised and their voices shaking.

Mavros had come out of his tent. He had stood before his shattering army and drawn his sword and called them cowards and traitors, and his voice had carried, and a veteran named Hask—Hask Ellory, fourth company, fourteen years' service—had walked forward alone, unarmed, carrying a copy of the conscription ledger that the city's scribes had passed hand to hand along the front lines all afternoon. Hask had held it up. And he had said, loudly enough for a thousand men to hear:

*"General. Show us your hand."*

And Mavros—who had been told, who must have known, who had perhaps told himself a hundred comfortable lies about necessity and legacy—had hesitated.

Fourteen years of service buys a man a moment of authority. The whole front rank went quiet. And in that quiet, Hask said the thing Emrys would write down later that night, verbatim, in the new ledger, under debts forgiven:

*"You always wore the gauntlet. Even at your son's funeral. We thought it was grief. Was it grief, General? Or was it chains?"*

Mavros had struck him down. Of course he had—there was no other move left on his board, and men who hold tainted seals are not their own masters when cornered; the lattice tightens. But forty thousand witnesses saw a defenseless old soldier die for asking to see a hand, and they saw the gauntlet fly off in the struggle, and they saw what the hand beneath had become: glass to the wrist, veined with pale script, the Regent's architecture still living in it like roots in a dead tree's stump, reaching, hungry, ownerless.

The army did not turn on Mavros in fury. It was quieter than fury, and worse. They simply stopped obeying. Whole regiments grounded arms. The guard around the tent dissolved as men remembered—reading the articles in their heads, Emrys supposed, checking the arithmetic of their own hearts—that their oaths ran to a seal, and the seal was rotten, and therefore the oaths were void, and therefore they were, for the first time in months, free men standing in a field.

Mavros was taken by his own honor guard. Alive. The glass hand they bound in lead and salt, carefully, the way one handles evidence—for it was evidence, Emrys knew, the finest exhibit she would ever enter into a record.

---

At sundown the last of them came through the gates.

They came in columns and clots and single files, forty thousand men diminished by desertion and defection to perhaps thirty-one thousand, tramping through the western gate with their weapons stacked by their own sergeants outside the walls, passing under torchlight into a city that fed them—bread, actual bread, ovens running since dawn, the bakers' guild having presented Emrys with a bill she signed without blinking, because some costs are joys. Protection of kin: clerks at long tables taking down names of wives and mothers and sons in villages still held by Mavros's rump forces, dispatches going out by rider that night under Aldric's own seal guaranteeing their safety. Lodging: barracks, temples, barns, the great hall floor.

Emrys stood at the gate table past midnight, stamping discharges. Page after page. Name after name. Each man received his paper—the lawful release, properly sealed, the thing that had sat in a drawer while he marched—and each man looked at it as though it were a holy relic, and several wept, and one kissed her stamp-hand, and she said, every time, the same words:

"Don't thank me. Thank yourself. You read."

Near the end, when the line had thinned to stragglers and the torches burned low, Sera appeared at her shoulder with two cups of something hot and medicinal.

"Eleven hundred discharged by my count tonight," Sera said. "Rest tomorrow. It'll take days."

"Days," Emrys agreed. She looked out through the gate at the plain, dark now, littered with abandoned tents and dropped banners and the scuffed earth of forty thousand feet. Somewhere out there, in a lead-lined cell beneath the guardhouse, Mavros sat with his ruined hand and whatever was left of his mind, and tomorrow she would sit across from him with a lamp and a ledger and take his deposition, gently, thoroughly, the way one audits anything that has been eaten.

"Emrys." Sera's voice was odd. "You should come to the wall. The north wall. Now."

Something in her tone. Emrys set down the stamp.

They walked fast through streets that smelled of woodsmoke and fresh bread, up the stairs, out onto the north battlements where sentries stood pointing at the horizon—and Emrys followed their fingers and felt the ground tilt beneath her.

To the north, beyond the hills, beyond the dark, the sky above the distant mountain passes was glowing. Not sunrise-glow. Not firelight. A cold, glassy luminescence, pale as the veins in a dead man's hand, pulsing slow and rhythmic, like breathing.

Like something vast, buried a century ago, turning over in its sleep.

"The rupture," Emrys whispered. The god's final sealing—it had been a bandage over a wound, not a cure. She had known that. She had chosen it anyway. "It's not holding."

Beside her, in the cold wind, Sera said quietly: "The watch reports it started an hour ago. Right after the last of Mavros's banners came down." A pause. "As if it was waiting to see how the battle ended."

Emrys stared north at the pulsing light, and her hand found the ledger at her belt—her new ledger, three weeks old, full of forgiven debts—and held it the way a believer holds a talisman.

"It didn't wait to see," she said. "It *felt* it. Every oath voided tonight was a chain loosened somewhere else." She turned toward the stairs, already moving. "Wake the council. Wake Aldric. And send for Verity's diary—we're going to need the rest of it."

Above the northern passes, the glass-light pulsed again, patient as an opening eye.
