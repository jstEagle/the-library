## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The rain came to the Glass Steppe the way debt collectors came: without warning, without mercy, and with every intention of staying until it had taken something.

Wren Okonkwo heard it first against the guild's high windows—a sound like sifted gravel, like the steppe itself being slowly sanded down—and did not look up from the ledger. Looking up was a habit they had unlearned decades ago. Invisibility was not a gift; it was a discipline, practiced hourly, worn smooth as an old coin.

They dipped the quill and continued.

*Tuesday. One bottle, sealed glass, containing thunder as caught over the Kettle Mountains in the last hour of a dying storm. Purchased from a weather-widow for three crowns and a promise. Sold before noon to a lady who would not give her name and paid in a coin that has not been minted since the Sundering. The coin is warm. The coin is still warm.*

That was the trouble with the Steppe Guild's books. A counting house, people called it, and mostly they were right—there were columns, there were sums, there was the soft rustle of clerks doing honest arithmetic in the long gallery below Wren's scriptorium. But the guild traded in impossible things, and impossible things did not sit quietly in columns. They leaked. The bottled thunder had hummed against its shelf all morning, and now the shelf hummed too, and now Wren's teeth hummed faintly, and by evening some clerk would complain of a headache and Odile would raise one eyebrow and say nothing, because Odile said nothing about anything unless saying something cost you.

*Wednesday. A debt of forty years, discharged in full by payment of six years, tendered by the debtor's daughter, witnessed and accepted. The debtor will wake tomorrow remembering nothing but gratitude. Note: the years were good ones. They tasted of plums when I counted them.*

Wren paused at that line, though pausing was also a habit they tried to unlearn. They had held the girl's years in their cupped hands while the scale settled. Six years of plums and summer light, and she had handed them over without weeping, which told Wren everything about what those years had been worth to her, or everything about what the debt had been worth to someone else. The ledger did not ask. That was the mercy of the ledger. It recorded; it did not judge. Wren had built a whole life inside that mercy, brick by brick, entry by entry.

*Thursday. Nothing sold. A man came asking whether we bought memories. Told him we were a counting house. He looked at me too long. Logged his face anyway.*

They had no name that anyone spoke aloud. That was the terms of it—the oath, the silence, the arrangement older than most of the clerks' grandmothers. Swordspeakers swore themselves out of the world: no name given, no blade drawn, no word of the First Tongue passed their lips on pain of unmaking. In exchange, the world forgot them. Not gradually, not politely—*forgot*, the way water forgets a stone pulled from a river. Wren had walked into guildhouses where they'd worked for years and watched masters squint at them like strangers. It stung less each time. That was the worst part. It stung less.

Below, through the floorboards, came the ordinary music of the guild: the scratch of pens, the clink of the strongbox, a junior clerk laughing at something and then catching herself, because even joy kept quiet hours here. Wren listened to it the way other people listened to a hearth. It was not love. It was close enough that they had stopped checking the difference.

*Friday. Sold one door that opens onto yesterday. Buyer unknown, intermediary known, intermediary now regretful. Note to self: stop selling doors. We do not know where they go when they are opened twice.*

Outside, the rain thickened into something with intent. Lightning walked the horizon in slow strides, and the light through the windows went the color of old pewter. Wren trimmed the lamp, though the day was barely spent, and watched the storm come up the steppe like an army that had all the time in the world.

It was near dusk when the knock came—not a knock, really. A knocking-shaped collapse. Someone small hitting the guild door with the flat of both hands until the door won.

Wren was the only one in the entry hall, because Wren was always the only one anywhere, that being the point of them. They crossed to the door, put their eye to the iron viewer, and saw mud. Mud in the shape of a child, maybe nine years old, soaked past any argument with the weather, hair plastered flat, eyes enormous and lit from behind by lightning.

Guild policy was clear: no trade after dark, no charity ever, no exceptions that could be entered into a ledger. Wren's hand was already moving to the bolt to turn it when the child spoke through the wood.

The words were not words Wren knew, and were words Wren knew better than their own heartbeat.

The First Tongue. Three syllables of it, cracked and childish and terrified, and Wren's body reacted before their mind could forbid it—the spine straightening, the breath dropping low into the belly, the old forms rising in the blood like water finding an old channel. Across the hall, on its wall peg, Wren's sword began to hum.

Not rattle. Hum. A low, sweet, resonant note, like a struck bell deciding whether to be a bell at all. The blade had hung silent for twenty-two years, oiled and wrapped and sworn to sleep. Now it sang one note in the dark, and the note matched the child's voice, and Wren stood between them feeling the oath tighten around their throat like wire drawn through flesh.

*No,* Wren thought, uselessly, the way one thinks *no* at a flood. *No, not here. Not me. Not again.*

The child knocked again, weaker, and said the syllables once more, pleading this time, and Wren understood them. That was the horror of it—they *understood*. *Open. Please open. They are coming.* And beneath the words, woven into the grammar itself in a way no child could fake, was the structure of the old tongue, the language that had once commanded blades across battlefields and started the Sundering War with a single badly-sworn sentence. Dead for a generation. Buried with the orders that spoke it. Buried, Wren had believed, with everyone who spoke it—including, nearly, a child who had stood in a burning courtyard and been made to swear silence in exchange for being overlooked.

The sword hummed. The rain hammered. Somewhere above, Odile's office light burned, and Odile missed nothing that happened under her roof, which meant the ledger of Wren's anonymity had just been opened to a new page whether they willed it or not.

Wren unbolted the door.

The child fell across the threshold more than stepped, and up close they were thinner than mud suggested, with a burn scar along one forearm shaped like a brand half-finished. They looked up at Wren—at the gray clerk's robe, the ink-stained fingers, the face the world had agreed to forget—and said something else in the First Tongue, soft, wondering.

Wren knew that phrase too. Every swordspeaker knew it. It was what novices said on the day of their swearing, the oldest sentence in the order:

*I found you. I was sent to find you.*

"I don't know what you're saying," Wren lied, in Trade-Speech, in a voice trained flat as ledger paper. "You're bleeding on the guild floor."

But the sword on the wall was still humming, louder now, and the child's eyes had gone to it, and gone wide, and gone knowing—and outside, far off across the black steppe, Wren saw riders' lanterns strung out along the road like a rosary being counted. Many lanterns. Coming on through the rain without hurry.

Invisibility, Wren thought, reaching back to lift the humming blade from its peg—it ended the moment someone speaks your dead language at your door. It ends the moment you answer.

The child reached up and took Wren's ink-stained hand without asking.

"They're coming," the child said—in Trade-Speech this time, accented, exhausted, unmistakable. "I ran so fast. I ran for days. Are you the one who keeps the book?"

Behind them, at the top of the stairs, a floorboard creaked. Odile stood in her doorway with a single candle, watching, her face giving away precisely nothing, her eyes giving away everything.

"Yes," Wren heard themself say, and the word felt like the first entry in a new ledger. "I keep the book."
