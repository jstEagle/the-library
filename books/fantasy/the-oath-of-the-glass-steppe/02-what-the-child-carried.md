## 2. What the Child Carried

The child slept in the copying room, curled in the space between a shelf of debt-bonds and the cold hearth, and Wren spent the night pretending not to watch them.

This was a lie they told with their whole body. Wren sat at the long clerk's table with three ledgers open before them and entered not one figure. The lamp burned low. Rain still worked at the shutters in slow, patient fingers. And on its peg by the door—Wren's blade, unclaimed for twenty-two years, wrapped in oilcloth like something dead—hummed.

Not loudly. Not enough to wake anyone. A thread of sound, the way a struck tuning fork remembers itself long after the ear gives up on it. Wren had learned, over two decades of silence, to stop hearing it. Tonight it was all they could hear.

*Vesh-ka,* the child had said, standing in the doorway with rain running off them in ropes, mud to the elbows, eyes too old for the small face above them. *Vesh-ka ren.* *I am come to the counting.* Or: *I am come to be counted.* The First Tongue was generous that way, generous the way a knife is generous—it cut where you needed and where you didn't.

Wren had not heard it spoken since the night the Chapterhouse burned. They had heard screams in eleven languages that night, and then silence, and then twenty-two years of numbers, which never lied and never commanded anything.

The child stirred. Wren looked down at the ledger, wrote a number, crossed it out, wrote the true one.

* *

Morning came gray and businesslike, and the Glass Steppe Guild did what it always did with impossible things: filed them.

Sef—that was the name, offered in trade-speech with the flat caution of someone who'd learned names could be taken—was folded into the clerks' routine before the second bell. Odile's orders, delivered without explanation: *the mud-child works the copy bench until we know what mud it carries.* So Sef sat between Pellam, who inked contracts, and Maro, who was half-deaf and therefore safe, and copied figures from rough slips into fair hand. The child's letters were terrible. The child's arithmetic was perfect.

"They don't teach column-carrying in ditches," Pellam observed, peering over Sef's shoulder on the second morning.

"Maybe I wasn't in a ditch," Sef said.

"Where were you, then?"

Sef's pen paused. Wren, three desks away and ostensibly reconciling a shipment of bottled thunder—forty-one jars, six cracked, the cracks *singing*, which would need to be reported as spoilage or sold as music—watched the pause stretch.

"Nowhere," Sef said finally, and copied another line.

Wren knew every flavor of *nowhere*. They had lived in one for twenty-two years. It was a country with excellent borders and no interior, and children only learned to live there if someone had taught them the language.

At midday Wren broke their first rule. Small. Almost nothing. The guild rationed bread by the ledger-entry; Wren recorded Sef's allotment twice and ate half their own loaf so the arithmetic of the pantry stayed honest. They told themselves it was housekeeping. They were very good at telling themselves things; they'd had practice.

The second rule broke itself. The rain had turned cold, and Sef's coat—a scrap of waxed canvas that had once belonged to a much larger corpse—let water through like a sieve. There was an old gray wool coat in the lost-property chest, unclaimed nine years, which by guild law should have been auctioned. Wren recorded it as *damaged, unsellable* and hung it over Sef's chair while the child was at the pump. When Sef came back and found it, they went very still, the way small animals go still, and looked around the room until their eyes found Wren's.

Wren looked back down at the ledgers. Numbers never lied. People just lied near them.

"Thank you," Sef said quietly, in trade-speech. Then, softer, in the other tongue—the one that made the blade on the wall hum like a held breath: *Vesh-ka som.*

*I see you.* Or: *I am seen by you.* The grammar refused to decide, and Wren's hands went cold on the pen.

They were seen. That was the whole catastrophe of it. Twenty-two years of being no one, of the oath wearing their name smooth the way water wears stone, and this child had walked through the door and begun, without meaning to, to read them aloud.

* * *

Guildmaster Odile received her visitors the way she received everything: seated, unhurried, with tea she never drank. Her office smelled of cedar and old paper and, faintly, of the iron filings she kept in a dish because she liked knowing where sharp things were.

"The Compact came through Redford Ferry four days ago," she said. "Three riders, ash-grey cloaks, broken-spear sigil. They bought horses and asked questions. The ferryman sells questions cheaper than horses." She turned the dish of filings a quarter-turn. "Their sigil is thirty years out of fashion. Their questions were about a child."

Wren stood across the desk and said nothing. Silence was the oldest clause of their contract, and Odile had never once held it against them—which was its own kind of pressure, like a debt accruing silently.

"You took stock of our new clerk," Odile went on. "You've been feeding them double. Don't look surprised; I read the pantry ledger the way I read everything, including your face, which you keep like a locked room but which has recently acquired a window." She set down the dish. "Here is what I know about the Ashen Compact, Wren, because I was trading when you were still being sworn to whatever it is you're sworn to. They were the ones who hunted your order. Not the mob, not the kings—the Compact. Professional men. Methodical. They believed the First Tongue was a weapon that ought to have exactly one owner."

The word *weapon* landed in the quiet room and lay there.

"And now," Odile continued, "they want a child who appeared at my door speaking words that made your sword sing loud enough that Maro heard it, and Maro is deaf as a post. So." She folded her hands. "Shelter costs. My price is the truth—not the guild truth, not the ledger truth. Yours. What are you, swordspeaker? What did you swear, and to whom, and why does a man who keeps books like a monk flinch when a child speaks?"

Wren's throat worked around twenty-two years of nothing.

"I can't," they managed.

"I know," Odile said, almost gently. "That's rather the point of an oath, isn't it. But understand me, Wren: I will shelter that child while it suits the guild, and the moment it stops suiting—if the Compact offers me something better than trouble—I will weigh it the way I weigh everything." Her eyes, pale and patient as winter light. "Unless you give me a reason to weigh otherwise. Truth is heavy. It tips scales."

She let them go without an answer. But as Wren reached the door:

"One more thing. The riders asked their questions in trade-speech. Except once. The tall one corrected the short one's pronunciation of a word." She pronounced it herself, badly, carefully: *"Veshka."* "I thought you'd want to know they speak it too."

* * *

That evening Wren sat with Sef at the copy bench while the clerks' benches emptied, and asked, in the flattest trade-speech they could manage, "Who taught you to talk like that? Like the old way?"

Sef's pen stopped. The child studied the page as though the figures might rearrange themselves into an escape route.

"My mother. And—" A breath. "Other people. Before."

"Before what?"

"Before the grey-cloaks came." Sef said it simply, the way children report weather. "They wore a spear with no point. They made everyone stand in rows. They asked who could speak the old words, and people who couldn't answer fast enough—" The child's hand closed around the pen. "I ran. Mother said run to the Steppe, find the counting-house, say *vesh-ka ren*. She said someone there would—" Sef stopped. Looked up. "Someone did, didn't they."

Wren's silence stretched long past denial.

"There's a sword on that wall," Sef said, very quietly. "It sings when I talk. Did you know that? It sang the first night. I thought maybe it was singing for you."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the guild's great lock turned for the night, and Wren felt the oath move inside them like wire drawn tight—not the comfortable wire of habit, but the kind that binds, the kind that bites when you test it.

"It's late," Wren said. "Sleep."

But at the door Sef spoke once more, barely a whisper, in the First Tongue—the old words falling into the room like coins into a well: *Som-ren vesh. Vesh-ka il.*

And Wren, who had not answered to their own name in twenty-two years because they no longer owned one, understood perfectly. *Watch over me. You are mine now.*

Down in the dark of the copying room, the blade hummed agreement.

And far off along the steppe road, past the last guild lantern, Wren saw—or thought they saw, or dreaded seeing—three points of light moving steadily closer through the rain, riding at the pace of men who had already sent their demand ahead of them.

By morning, it would be nailed to the guild door.
