## 3. The Price of the Oath

The Compact came at dawn, as I had known they would. War-hungry men keep the hours of butchers.

I was in the counting room when the horn sounded from the eastern palisade—three long notes, then silence, which was worse than a fourth. Every clerk in the guild knew what three-and-silence meant: *visitors who do not wish to be counted.*

Sef was two floors above, mending ledgers with the other junior clerks, their small hands smudged with ink they were learning to love. I felt the child's presence the way one feels weather coming—a pressure behind the teeth. The blade on my wall peg hummed its low, glassy note, and for the first time in thirty years I did not press my palm against it to make it stop.

"Master Okonkwo." Odile stood in the doorway of my office, and I saw that she had dressed for negotiation: grey wool, no jewelry, her guildmaster's chain worn plain over her breastbone like an apology. "They're asking for the child by description. Not by name."

"They don't know the name."

"No," she said. "But they know the tongue. And tongues are rarer than names." She let the silence sit between us, and I understood that she had already begun to count me among the things she might have to spend.

* * *

They gathered in the long hall beneath the guild's great seal—the scales and the open book, carved in stone so old no one remembered which came first. There were seven of them from the Ashen Compact, ash-grey cloaks gone brown with steppe dust, and at their center a woman with a burn-scarred jaw who introduced herself as Vess Corrane, *Speaker-Remnant of the Compact*, as though the title were a wound she wore openly.

"The child speaks the First Tongue," Corrane said. "You are harboring it. We are asking—politely—for its return."

"Its," Odile repeated, mild as milk. "The child has a name."

"The child has a weapon in its throat." Corrane's eyes moved across the assembled clerks, unhurried, cataloguing. They passed over me without pause, and I kept my face the blank ledger-page my oath had trained into it. Thirty years of being no one. It had never cost so much to maintain. "You were not alive for the Sundering, guildmaster. I was. I watched a boy of nine speak a sentence and unmake a city wall, and the wall took four hundred people with it. What we do, we do so that it does not happen again."

"And your method?"

Corrane smiled, and the scar pulled tight. "Containment. Study. And where study fails—" she spread her hands, "—mercy."

Behind her, her six companions stood too still. Swordspeakers, or what remained of them. I could see it in how they held their weight, in the way their hands hung loose and ready. Once, there had been hundreds of us. Then the war, then the purges, then the oaths—and now this: six hollowed survivors hunting a child across the steppe because the child could do what we had sworn away.

Odile said, "I will consider your request. You will be given quarters and hospitality while I do."

"You will give us the child," Corrane said, "or you will explain to your guild why a counting house chose to die for a debt it never owed."

* * *

I found Odile that evening in the archive, standing before the sealed cabinet where the guild kept its oldest contracts. She had not lit a lamp. In the dark she looked older, and tired in a way I had never seen.

"You heard," she said. It wasn't a question. The archive carried sound like water carries silt.

"I heard."

"I built this place from nothing, Wren. A mudflat and a lie—that we were only counters. Forty years pretending to be dull so the world would leave us alone." Her hand rested on the cabinet's iron lock. "Seven hundred souls under this roof. Cooks. Children of clerks. Sef."

"And?"

She turned. In the dark her eyes were wet and furious. "And I am going to ask you to tell me what you know about that child, and you are going to refuse, because of your oath, and I am going to have to decide whether the guild survives on the back of a bargain I cannot stomach. So spare us both the hour and answer me now: what is Sef to you?"

The wire of the oath drew tight around my ribs. I felt every word I wanted to say press against it and fail. *A child who said goodnight to me in a dead language. A child who laughs when the ink blots. A child I would—*

"I can't," I whispered.

"No," Odile said softly. "You can't. That's the trouble with oaths. They only ever protect the person who swore them." She struck a lamp alight, and the flame threw her shadow tall against the contract shelves. "I have until tomorrow's noon bell. Pray your silence is worth more than mine."

* * *

I did not sleep. I went down to the vault below the counting room, where the guild stored the trades too strange to shelve—bottled thunder sulking in its jar, a coffin of frozen years—and I sat on the cold floor with my blade across my knees and finally, finally let myself remember.

Because here is what I had told myself for thirty years: that my order, the Swordspeakers of the Vow, had been destroyed in the purges; that I alone survived because I was clever and quiet; that my oath of silence was a shield, a way to keep the First Tongue locked behind my teeth so it could never again command steel to kill.

It was a comfortable story. Comfortable stories are the most expensive lies a person can carry.

The memory came the way floodwater comes—through cracks I had spent decades plastering. Not the purges. Before them. A valley called Heshen's Rest, green once, before the war taught the land to be ashamed of green. I was nine years old. My mother was a swordspeaker, and she had brought me to the valley because it was supposed to be safe, because the fighting hadn't reached it yet.

Then the Compact came—not these seven, but their grandmothers' generation, wearing the same ash-grey—and they had not come to fight. They had come to end the speaking forever. They gathered every swordspeaker in the valley into the meeting hall. My mother put me in a root cellar beneath the floor and told me, in the First Tongue, *be still, be silent, be nothing,* and then she closed the trapdoor, and through the seams between the boards I watched them burn the hall with forty speakers inside it.

Forty voices. My mother's among them. And I lay in the dark with my hand pressed over my own mouth, nine years old, keeping the last vow anyone would ever ask of me: *be silent.* When the soldiers found me days later, half-starved and mute, an oldCompact officer looked at me and decided a child who wouldn't speak was a child who couldn't. He let me live. He thought he was showing mercy.

So the oath was never mine. It was never a shield. It was a grave marker—I had been carrying a massacre inside my mouth for thirty years, calling it discipline, calling it peace, and all the while the Compact that dug that grave was still walking the world, still burning halls, and now they had come for a child who reminded them of everything they feared.

My mother's last word to me had been *silent*. But the first word—the word she used every morning, the word that woke me, the word that meant *rise, beloved, the day needs your voice*—that word rose in me now like a blade coming out of a scabbard after thirty years of rust.

I stood. I took the humming sword from my knees, and it sang against my palms like it recognized me, like it had been waiting in the dark of the vault for exactly this.

Above me, the noon bell began to ring.

* * *

I crossed the long hall while the bell was still sounding, and the whole guild was gathering because bells at noon mean judgment, and everyone comes to watch judgment. Odile stood at one end, grey-faced. Corrane and her six stood at the other, and between them, flanked by two clerks who didn't know they were guards, stood Sef—small, ink-stained, chin up, terrified and refusing to show it.

"Wren," Odile breathed, seeing the blade. "Wren, don't."

Corrane saw it too. I watched recognition move across her scarred face like frost across a window. "A swordspeaker," she said, almost wonderingly. "Here. All this time."

I walked to the center of the hall, to the space directly beneath the carved seal, and I planted my feet the way my mother taught me, weight low, breath even, the old stance rising through my body like water finding an old channel. Seven Compact veterans reached for their weapons. Clerks screamed. Sef's eyes went wide and wet and fixed on me.

And I opened a mouth that had been shut for thirty years, and I spoke.

Not Common. Not the guild's careful trade-cant. The First Tongue—the language that wakes steel, the language they burned a valley to bury—filled the hall, and every blade in the room answered it. Six Compact swords leapt halfway from their sheaths, trembling, weeping metal. My own blade rose and hung in the air beside me, singing, and the sound of it cracked a wineglass on the far table and made the great seal's stone scales tremble on their carven arms.

"*Hear me,*" I said in the old words, and my voice broke on them, rusted and reborn. "*I am Wren Okonkwo, daughter of Amara of Heshen's Rest, and I was there. I saw what you did in the valley. I have been silent thirty years, and my silence ends today.*"

Corrane's hand was shaking on her half-drawn hilt, fighting the song of her own steel. "You'll start the war again," she hissed.

"No," I said, and turned to Sef, and held out my free hand. "*Run, little one. Run, and don't look back. I will hold the door.*"

Sef ran. Behind me, Corrane shouted the order, and seven blades came free of their sheaths all at once, and the last thing I heard before the hall erupted was Odile's voice, ringing clear as the noon bell:

"*Seal the doors!*"
