## 4. A Name Rewritten

The guild hall had never held so many people. Wren counted them the way they counted everything—out of habit, out of fear—two hundred clerks pressed along the walls, the counting desks shoved aside, and at the center of the floor the delegation of the Ashen Compact: seven figures in ash-grey cloaks, their sigil stitched in thread the color of burnt bone.

Odile stood between them and the stairs to the vaults, her ledger closed under one arm like a shield she had no intention of drawing. She looked tired in a way Wren had never seen on her—not the tiredness of long hours, but of a woman who had run the arithmetic of survival all night and hated every answer it gave her.

"The child is not for sale," Odile said. "Not for coin, not for favor, not for the continued health of this guild. I have said so. You keep asking."

"We ask," said the Compact's leader, a woman with a voice like gravel settling, "because you have not yet understood what you are refusing. That child speaks the First Tongue. Every year we leave her untrained, unbound, the world grows closer to another Sundering. We are not your enemy, guildmaster. We are the only ones who remember how to prevent the last war from happening twice."

"You remember how to start it," Wren said.

The word came out before they decided to speak, and once spoken, there was no taking it back. Two hundred heads turned. The Compact leader turned slowest of all, and when her eyes found Wren, they narrowed with something worse than anger—recognition.

"There," she said softly. "The swordspeaker. We wondered which counting house had swallowed you."

Wren stepped forward. Their blade was not on their hip; it hung on the wall of their narrow room three floors up, where it had hummed for eleven days like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing. They did not need it. Not yet. What they needed was already in their throat, and it had been waiting thirty years.

"I am going to say some words," Wren told the hall. "Most of you will not understand them. That is the point. The Compact will."

* * *

They had spent the night not sleeping, but reading—their own oath, finally, after decades of reciting it without comprehension. The words had been given to them at nine years old by men with ash on their sleeves, and they had repeated them every dawn since, a lock turned daily until they forgot there was ever a door.

But oaths were contracts, and Wren Okonkwo kept ledgers. And every contract, however old, however cruel, had terms. Buried in the archaic grammar of the swearing, in clauses designed never to be read aloud by the bound, they had found it at the third hour past midnight: *this oath holds while its purpose stands.* The purpose, written plainly, was *the silence of witnesses.*

A witness who renounces witnessing—who steps forward and gives testimony freely—held an oath with nothing left to grip.

And so, in the grey before dawn, Wren had climbed to the wall peg, taken down the blade that had sung to Sef's voice, and carried it into the hall like a bride carried flowers.

Now they stood in the center of the floor, and the Compact leader's hand had drifted, very casually, toward her cloak.

"Before I speak," Wren said, "let the record show what this oath has cost. I was nine years old when the Compact burned the village of Tarnholt. I watched forty-one people die. I was made to swear silence over their ashes, and the silence became my name—or rather, the absence of one. For thirty years I have been *clerk*. I have been *the quiet one*. I have been a line item in someone else's ledger." They drew a breath. "I am done being entered. I am doing the entering now."

They spoke the First Tongue.

It came out rough, rusted, half-remembered—but language does not forget itself, and neither, it turned out, did the blade. On Wren's shoulder, wrapped in cloth, the sword began to sing, a low harmonic that made the windows buzz and two hundred clerks flinch as one. The Compact leader's hand stopped moving toward her cloak. Her seven companions went very still, the stillness of soldiers who have heard artillery and know exactly what it means.

Wren spoke the renunciation clause by clause. *I release the silence. I claim the witnessing. Let my testimony stand in open air, and let the oath that bound it find no purchase.* With each phrase the singing deepened, and with each phrase Wren felt something unwind in their chest—a wire pulled taut for thirty years, slackening link by link, until the final word fell away and they were standing in a hall full of strangers, unbound, terrified, and more present in their own body than they had ever been.

The Compact leader exhaled slowly. "You understand what you've done. There are others of us. Others who survived. If you will not be silent, you could be—"

"A weapon? A teacher of weapons?" Wren shook their head. "You had thirty years to ask me. You spent them hunting a child instead."

"She is a danger to—"

"She is a girl," Wren said, "who learned her grandmother's words and ran when men in ash-grey came for her. The danger in this hall is the same danger that burned Tarnholt, and it is wearing your sigil." They lifted the blade, unwrapped, bare steel catching lamplight—and then, deliberately, drove it point-down into the floorboards between themselves and the Compact, and left it standing there, released, its song fading to nothing. "I am a swordspeaker reborn, as you say. Remember what that meant the first time. Then decide whether you want to be remembered the same way."

Silence. Long enough that Wren heard the storm-drain dripping somewhere below, heard Sef's breath catch in the crowd near the stairs, where Odile had insisted the child stand surrounded by the largest clerks the guild employed.

Then the Compact leader bowed—shallow, grudging, but a bow. "The Compact withdraws. For now." Her eyes flicked to the blade in the floor, then to Wren's face. "Names, swordspeaker. You'll want one. Silence suits no one free."

She turned, and her six followed, and the doors closed behind them with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence.

* * *

What came after was smaller, and stranger, and better.

Odile crossed the floor, took the ledger from under her arm, and set it in Wren's hands. "Your accounts," she said. "All of them. A swordspeaker can't hold rank in a counting house—it would draw every vulture on the Steppe to my door within the month. So as of this morning, you're dismissed."

"Dismissed."

"Fired. Disgracefully. I'll tell everyone you embezzled." Her mouth twitched. "It's the kindest lie I've ever told. But the ledgers stay yours—you kept them honestly, and honest books are rarer than magic. Take them. Take the coat you stole. Take the child."

Sef came down the stairs slowly, mud still faintly ghosting her cuffs though it had been days since the rain. She stopped in front of Wren and looked up with the expression of someone about to attempt something dangerous.

"Wren," she said. Carefully, in trade-tongue, tasting it. "That's your name?"

"It was. Before."

"It's yours again," Odile said. "I witnessed the whole thing. Consider yourself re-entered into the world's accounts, Wren Okonkwo. In my own hand."

Around them the clerks were drifting back to their desks, but not before—Wren noticed with a strange tightness in the throat—more than a few paused to nod. One pressed a heel of bread into Sef's hand. Another, the old man Tibbet who had complained for years about Wren's silence, clapped them once on the shoulder and said, "Knew you'd talk eventually. Nobody keeps books that neatly without something to prove."

* * *

They walked out at dawn, because it seemed right that a new name should meet its first morning.

The Glass Steppe stretched east, gold and green and endless, the frost burning off the grasses in threads of silver smoke. Sef walked between Wren and the borrowed coat, chattering—in trade-tongue, mostly, but every few sentences slipping into the First Tongue just to feel it, and each time the blade on Wren's back answered with a hum soft as a cat's purr.

"You'll teach me to speak it safely," Sef said. Not a question. It hadn't been a question since the second night, when she had fallen asleep against Wren's shoulder mid-sentence.

"Safely," Wren agreed. "Slowly. And loudly enough that no one ever gets to pretend they didn't hear us coming."

Ahead, the road bent toward the river towns, toward markets where impossible things changed hands and someone, somewhere, would eventually hear rumors of a clerk with a singing sword and a child who spoke the oldest language alive. Wren found they didn't mind. Ledgers were for keeping track of what mattered, and for the first time in thirty years, everything that mattered was walking beside them, arguing cheerfully about breakfast.

Behind them, the guild bell rang the opening hour. Ahead, the sun cleared the horizon and set the whole steppe alight, glass-bright, like a page waiting to be written on.

Wren Okonkwo—named, witnessed, free—turned east, and began.
