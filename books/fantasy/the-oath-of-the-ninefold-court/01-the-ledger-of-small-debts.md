## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The candle on Jonah Kavanagh's desk had burned down to a stub the color of old teeth, and still they copied.

*Ward against night-walking, third revision.* The letters went down in the careful temple hand they'd been drilled into over twelve years — each stroke a small act of obedience, each line a small theft. By daylight, this same page would sit under the seal of Saint Verre's Scriptorium, blessed and catalogued and sold at lawful price to those who could afford it: merchants, magistrates, the wives of guild-masters who wanted their doorways kept clean of everything that crept. But this was not daylight. This was the hour when Jonah worked the second ledger.

They set down the last stroke, blew across the wet ink, and held the page up to the candlelight. The ward-script caught the glow and held it — faintly, the way a cat's eye holds a lantern — and Jonah felt the small satisfaction that never quite curdled into guilt, no matter how many times they told it to. The copy was good. Better than good. The temple's own masters couldn't have matched the line-weight, and Jonah knew it, and knew also that knowing it was its own kind of sin, one of the seven or eight they'd stopped bothering to confess.

From the drawer beneath the desk they took the other book. Not the ledger of debts — the *real* ledger, the one bound in grey calf-hide with no title and no temple mark, the one that lived under a floorboard by day and came out only now, when the tower slept. They opened it to the current page and read down the column in the light of the dying stub:

*Marta of Tanner's Row — hearth-ward, renewed quarterly — paid in bread and mending.*
*The ferryman Oss — river-charm against the drowned ones — two years' installments outstanding, forgiven.*
*Aldwin Cooper — fever-knot for his daughter, Nell — PAID IN FULL, God keep her.*

Twelve years of names. Twelve years of small transactions conducted through the wicket-gate of the scriptorium's herb-door, in whispers, in barter, in coins warm from pockets that didn't have many. Illegal charms, the law called them. Unlicensed workings. The kind of thing that got a scribe defrocked if they were lucky and given to the Crown's questioners if they weren't. The kind of thing Jonah had been doing since they were nineteen years old and had discovered that the poor of Vhalen could not afford the temple's prices and were dying anyway, quietly, of things the temple's wards could have prevented.

And beneath all of it, governing all of it, the debt.

*Owed to the Temple of Saint Verre: twelve years' service, begun the 3rd of Embermonth, in payment of—*

In payment of what, the ledger never said. That was the cruelty of it, or the mercy; Jonah had never decided which. They had been brought to Saint Verre as a child of nine, delivered by an uncle who wept at the gate and was gone before the bells finished ringing, and the High Cantor had looked down at them with eyes like wells and said, *You will work until the account is settled,* and that was all anyone had ever said about it. Twelve years of copying wards by day. And somewhere in the middle years, without any decision being made, the nights had become something else entirely.

Jonah ran their thumb along the ledger's edge and did the arithmetic they did every night, though the number never changed. Fourteen months. Fourteen months and the account closed, and they could walk out of Saint Verre's gate with nothing owed to anyone, free to be nobody in particular in a city full of somebodies. Fourteen months. They'd survived eleven years and ten. They could survive fourteen months more.

The thought should have been comforting. It sat in their chest instead like a stone in a shoe — present, irritating, impossible to ignore. Something tonight was wrong with the arithmetic. Not the numbers themselves but the feeling underneath them, the sense that they were counting down toward a door that might not open.

Outside the scriptorium's high window, Vhalen lay spread below like embers raked flat: ten thousand rooftops, the grey thread of the Greywater, the distant smudge of Lowcandle where the smugglers and charm-hawkers and desperate folk lived by their own unwritten law. Above it all, the stars. Jonah glanced at them out of habit — ward-scribes learned to read the sky the way farmers read soil — and found them as they always were, the Ninebright arrayed in their ancient ring around the pole, steady as scripture.

*Steady as scripture.* They almost laughed. Nothing about tonight felt steady. All evening there had been a hum in the tower, so faint Jonah kept deciding they'd imagined it — a vibration in the floor joists, in the inkwell's surface, in their own back teeth. The charms hanging from the beam above the desk — their own private stock, the pieces too risky to sell — had been swinging. Not moving, exactly. Swinging. A slow, metronome drift, all eight of them together, like weeds leaning in a current Jonah couldn't see.

They got up, crossed the cold stones, and stood beneath them. The fever-knot turned first. Then the road-blessing. Then all the rest, in sequence, clockwise, a slow wheel of leather and twine and pressed herbs and hand-lettered vellum.

"That's not possible," Jonah told them, because talking to one's inventory was a habit even the temple hadn't managed to break.

The charms kept turning. And beneath the hum, so faint it might have been the wind in the tower's throat, something else — a sound like a very large bell struck once, very far away, and still ringing.

* *

The knock came at the herb-door at half past the midnight bell, which was late even for the night trade.

Jonah knew all their customers' rhythms the way a tide-pool knows its visitors. Marta came at dusk, always, bread still warm in her basket. Old Oss came when he was drunk enough to admit fear and sober enough to count coin. The marked ones — the ones with birth-signs, the ones the temples wouldn't touch — came between the third and fourth bells, hooded, hurrying. Nobody came at half past midnight unless something had gone badly wrong, and when Jonah lifted the wicket-latch and saw who stood in the herb-garden fog, they understood that something had.

"Tam." They kept their voice low. "Tam, you look like you've run here."

"I did." Tam Voss was a knife-grinder off Cutler's Lane, forty years old, built like his own grindstone, and currently breathing like a man who'd outrun something worse than distance. He pushed past the wicket without waiting for invitation — another first — and stood in the little anteroom among the drying bundles of vervain and saltwort, working his cap in both fists.

"Someone following you?"

"No. Yes. I don't—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I need the deep ward, Jonah. The one you said you didn't have. The one I asked after at Midwinter and you said nobody sells."

Jonah leaned against the doorframe and let the cold from the garden seep through their robe. Every instinct they owned — and eleven years of night-trade had grown them a fine set — was saying *turn him away, come back tomorrow, take the easy sale.* But Tam Voss didn't frighten easy, and frightened men made mistakes, and mistakes got scribes given to the questioners.

"There is no deep ward," Jonah said. "I told you true. There's household wards, road wards, water wards. What you're asking for doesn't exist."

"It exists." Tam's voice dropped until it was barely above the fog. "My wife's grandmother had one. From before. She died in the spring and we found it sewn in her mattress, and my Nana says it's older than the temple, older than the *city*, and she says—" His hands stopped working the cap. They were shaking. "She says the stars are going to go out, Jonah. She says that's what the ward was *for*. And three nights running I've dreamed of a sound like glass breaking inside a wall, and I can't sleep, and I'll pay anything—"

"Tam." Jonah stepped forward and put a hand on the man's forearm, feeling the tremor in it. "Sit down. Breathe. Dreams are just dreams, and grandmothers' mattresses are just mattresses. Let me make you a sleep-charm, a good one, and you'll laugh at yourself by morning—"

That was when Tam pulled out the coin.

He did it the way men do things they don't understand — quickly, half-defensively, as if producing proof. "I know what I'm asking. I know how it sounds. But I've got this, see, it came to me in the week's takings, and Nana took one look at it and went white and told me to bring it to whoever sold me the fever-knot for Nell, because she says the old blood knows the old marks—"

The coin lay in his palm, dull silver gone dark with age, and Jonah looked down at it and forgot, entirely, how to breathe.

It was stamped with nine interlocked rings.

Not a design — Jonah had copied ten thousand designs and knew a pattern when they saw one. This was a *seal*, a sigil, the rings woven through one another in a way that hurt the eye to follow, each ring passing over and under its neighbors in an order that refused to resolve, that seemed almost to shift when looked at directly. Around the rim ran lettering in a script Jonah had never seen and yet somehow recognized, the way you recognize a face in a crowd from twenty years gone.

And every charm hanging in the tower above them began to ring.

Not swing. Ring — a thin, clear note like wet crystal touched with a fingernail, all eight at once, harmonics sliding over one another, and the sound went through Jonah's chest and down their arms and into the hand that was reaching, without any decision on their part, for the coin.

"Don't," Tam said — or maybe Jonah said it, or maybe nobody said it and the word simply happened. Their fingers closed on the silver.

Cold. Cold like well-water in Embermonth, cold like the underside of a gravestone — and then, beneath the cold, a hum answering the charms' ringing, a resonance that climbed up through Jonah's wrist bones and settled behind their sternum and *knew* them. There was no better word. The coin knew them, the way a lock knows its key, the way a wound knows salt, and somewhere in the deepest part of the night a bell that had been ringing since before Jonah was born changed its tone by a single note, and every hair on their body stood up and paid attention.

"Where," Jonah heard themselves say, in a voice that sounded borrowed, "did you get this?"

"A man on the quay. Foreign-looking, or old-looking, or — I don't know, Jonah, I gave him four coppers for it because it was pretty, that's all, that's *all*—" Tam was backing toward the door now, cap crushed in his fists, eyes wide at the ringing charms, at Jonah's face, at whatever Jonah's face was doing. "Keep it. Keep the cursed thing. I don't want it. Nana was wrong, the old blood doesn't want to know the old marks, I take it back—"

The door banged. Fog swirled in and settled. The footsteps faded down the herb-path, quick and quicker, and then there was only the garden breathing, and the candle guttering, and the coin sitting in Jonah's open palm like a small cold moon.

The ringing faded slowly, note by note, the way a congregation's singing fades after the benediction. But the humming didn't stop. The coin lay quiet and dark and patient against their skin, and beneath their sternum that single changed note went on sounding, soft and endless, like something tuning itself to them — or them to it.

Jonah stood in the anteroom for a long time. Long enough for the candle stub to drown in its own wax. Long enough for the fog to creep fully in and bead on the stone floor. They thought about setting the coin on the step and leaving it for the rats. They thought about taking it to the High Cantor in the morning, laying it on that vast desk, and saying *explain this to me, explain the last twelve years, explain why your tower rings when this comes through my door.*

They thought about the ledger, and the fourteen months, and the door they were counting down toward.

Then they closed their hand around the coin, carried it back up the stairs, and did the most honest thing they'd done in twelve years: they wrote it into the grey ledger. *Received in trade, one coin, unknown mint, nine interlocked rings. Provenance: Tam Voss, Cutler's Lane. Value: —*

Their pen hovered over the value column for a long moment.

Then, because the truth had hold of their wrist the same way the coin had, they wrote: *Value: unknown. Possibly everything.*

* *

Sleep, when it finally came near dawn, was shallow and strange, and the dreams were all of bells.

Jonah woke once, certain they'd heard the temple's great bourdon tolling though no service was scheduled, certain the floor was vibrating, certain that when they pressed their palm flat against the boards they could feel the whole tower humming like a struck string that hadn't finished its note. Through the window the sky was going grey at the edges, and the Ninebright were fading into it one by one, the way stars do, the way they always had, the way they would tomorrow and every tomorrow after.

Except.

Jonah knelt at the window with the blankets pooled around them and counted, and counted again, and told themselves they'd lost track, that exhaustion was playing tricks, that the ninth star of the ring had always been dimmer, harder to find, a test for novices—

But they hadn't lost track. They were a ward-scribe; counting was the first thing they'd ever learned to do right. Eight stars burned in the ancient ring. Where the ninth had hung since before the city was founded, since before the temple, since before any ledger recorded any debt, there was only darkness — not cloud-dark, not mist, but a hole in the sky shaped exactly like a missing star.

Behind them, on the desk, the coin hummed in the grey silence, patient as a debt coming due.

Jonah Kavanagh sat in the cold window-frame and watched the dark place where the ninth star used to be, and understood — the way you understand a crack in a foundation, slowly and then all at once — that the life they had built out of small transgressions was resting on something much larger than they were.

And that something had just moved.
