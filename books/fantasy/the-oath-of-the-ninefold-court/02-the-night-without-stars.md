## 2. The Night Without Stars

The bell of Saint Verre rang midnight, and Jonah Kavanagh was counting.

Not coins — though there were coins, forty-one coppers and a clipped silver half hidden in the false bottom of the ink cabinet, twelve years of small thefts and smaller sales pressed flat as autumn leaves. Jonah was counting stars. It was a habit begun in childhood on the fens outside Vhalen, where the sky hung so low and thick with light that you could name them like neighbors: the Shepherd's Lamp, the Sisters Three, old Vael's Lantern burning red at the hem of heaven. From the scriptorium tower the city stole most of them, but enough survived — a dozen, fifteen on clear nights — and Jonah had learned long ago that a person who could not sleep might as well count something older than their debts.

Twelve years, four months, and eleven days remained on the temple ledger. Jonah knew it to the day. The debt had been inherited at fourteen, when their mother died owing Saint Verre for her own funeral — a sum so modest it was almost an insult, and so absolute that no labor short of a lifetime could discharge it. The Cantors called it *the gentle bondage*. Debt-slaves like Jonah were fed, housed, taught letters and law, given meaningful work copying wards and prayers onto parchment and vellum. In return they gave the temple everything they would ever earn, and in exchange for nothing they sold charms in the dark to people the temple pretended not to see.

Jonah had gotten good at both lives. By day: the steady scratch of the reed pen, forty lines before the noon bell, illumination work on feast days. By night: the sliding panel behind the reliquary case, the customers who came smelling of tallow smoke and river mud — a fishwife wanting safe passage past the toll-wards, a midwife needing a charm against childbed fever, a boy with a branded hand who wanted, only wanted, to stop shaking when soldiers passed. Jonah charged what they could pay, which was rarely much, and told himself every night that the transgression was small, that the wards they copied by day and the wards they sold by night were the same words anyway, that God or whatever lived above the ninth heaven surely kept better ledgers than the Cantors did and would find the balance acceptable.

That was the thought in Jonah's head when the first star went out.

It went out the way a candle goes out when pinched between wet fingers — no flare, no fall, no slow dimming. One moment Vael's Lantern burned red above the eastern hills; the next, the place where it had been was simply an absence, a hole cut from the cloth of night. Jonah's pen stopped. A bead of iron-gall ink swelled at the nib and dropped onto the page, blooming black across a ward-line meant to hold back hunger from a granary in some village whose name Jonah would never learn.

*Cloud,* they thought. *A cloud.*

But the sky over Vhalen was clear as glass, cold and windless, the kind of late-autumn night where the air itself seems to be holding its breath. And the hole where the star had been was not filling with cloud. It was spreading.

The Shepherd's Lamp went. Then the Sisters Three, all together, like three windows shuttered by one hand. Jonah rose from the desk without deciding to, walked to the tower window on legs that felt borrowed, and watched the darkness come.

It came like a tide. That was the only word for it, and even that word failed, because tides have edges you can watch, foam-lines creeping up sand, while this was an edge made of nothing devouring something — a blackness rolling across the sky from the north, from the borders, from the direction of places Jonah had only ever seen as names on maps copied by lamplight. Star after star after star went under it. The moon itself began to dim, its silver thinning to pewter, to lead, to a ghost of a coin held behind smoked glass.

And then the sound came.

It came up through the stone.

Everyone in Vhalen heard it that night, though afterward no two of them could agree on what it had been. Some said thunder with no storm. Some said the bells of every church ringing at once without hands. But Jonah, standing barefoot on the tower floor with the cold climbing through the soles of their feet, knew it for exactly what it was, because they had spent twelve years writing these words and selling these words and whispering these words into the ears of the desperate:

It was breaking glass. Breaking glass heard through stone. Enormous, distant, layered — a thousand panes going at once, then ten thousand, somewhere far below the world, and the stone of the tower carried the shivering of it up through the mortar and into Jonah's teeth until their jaw ached with sympathy.

*The border wards are falling.*

The thought arrived whole and terrible, and Jonah did not know how they knew it, only that they did — the way one knows a mother's footsteps on a stair. The great wards of Vhalen, the ones the temple maintained with public ceremony twice a year, the ones every child learned to thank in evening prayers — the wards along the Greywater and the Cinderfell passes and the fen-marches where Jonah had been born — were coming down all at once, all along the rim of the realm, like a crown slipping from a dying head.

Below, the city woke.

At first it was only dogs, a rising chorus of barking from the tanneries district, from the riverside slums, from the noble gardens where hounds were kept fat and stupid and still knew something was wrong. Then lights — candles blooming in windows like a rash, shutters banging open, voices calling street to street across the gaps. A woman's scream from the direction of the Fish Gate, high and sustained, the scream of someone who has looked up and understood. Somewhere a temple bell began to ring the alarm pattern, frantic, arrhythmic, and was joined by another, and another, until the whole city was clanging and no one could have said what any of it commanded.

Jonah stayed at the window.

They should have gone down — to the scriptorium, to the High Cantor, to whatever duty bound a debt-scribe when the sky itself defaulted. Some part of Jonah understood this distantly, the way one understands a summons read aloud in a dream. But their feet would not move, because the last of the stars had gone out, all of them, everywhere, and the sky above Vhalen was now a single seamless black from horizon to horizon, deeper than any night Jonah had ever seen, a night with no texture, no depth, no distance. It did not look empty. It looked *closed*. Like a lid. Like an eye shut by a hand that meant never to open it again.

In the total dark, the city became its own constellation — thousands of candle-flames trembling in windows, torches being lit in the streets below the tower, the great beacon on the Temple of Verre's spire catching with a roar of pitch. People poured into the squares. Even from this height Jonah could hear them now, a vast murmuring broken by cries: *What is it? What's happened? Are the heathens come? Is it plague? Is it the end?*

An old man in the square below fell to his knees and began to pray loudly enough to be heard over the bells, and others knelt around him like iron filings around a lodestone, and Jonah felt something twist in their chest — because Jonah knew the prayers those people were praying. Had written them. Had illuminated them in gold leaf for feast-day copies. *Keeper of the Ninefold Light, guard us through the dark watches.* They were prayers to the Court. Everyone in Vhalen prayed to the Ninefold Court without knowing it; the names had been worn smooth into ordinary devotion, nine saints, nine keepers, nine interlocked rings of protection around the realm.

Nine rings.

The coin.

Jonah's hand went to their pocket without any decision being made anywhere in their body. The coin had come to them only three nights ago — payment from a fen-woman buying a charm against nightmares, an old piece of currency nobody minted anymore, stamped not with a king's profile but with nine interlocked rings, worn nearly smooth. Jonah had almost refused it, had almost named it worthless, but something had made them bite it, weigh it, close their fist around it and say *this will do.* And ever since, the charms in the hidden cabinet — the ones awaiting sale, the little folded papers and knotted cords and wax-sealed wafers, each one a copy of a copy of a fragment of some ward-line older than the temple — had been humming.

Not singing. Not ringing. *Humming*, low and continuous, felt more than heard, the way a struck tuning fork is felt in the bones of the hand. Jonah had told themselves it was imagination, exhaustion, the damp getting into the wax seals. Three nights of telling themselves that.

Now, standing in a darkness with no stars in it, listening to the last vibrations of the wards' dying shudder die away through the stone, Jonah drew out the coin.

It burned cold.

There was no other way to say it. Not warm — cold, a biting, living cold, the cold of deep well water in summer, of a key left overnight in frost. It sat in Jonah's palm and drank the heat from their skin and gave back nothing, and across its worn face the nine interlocked rings seemed to hold more light than anything else in the world — a faint gray shimmer, like foxfire, like the memory of silver. And beneath Jonah's feet, fainter than breath, the hum answered. Every charm in the cabinet below. Every charm sold these twelve years, out in the city, in the villages, hanging at the necks of fishwives and midwives and branded boys — if the hum traveled as it seemed to travel, then all of it, everywhere, was answering this one small dead coin in the dark.

*What did we sell them?* Jonah thought, and the thought had teeth. *What did I sell them, all those years?*

Because the charms had worked. That was the thing Jonah had never let themselves examine too closely. Cheap copies, degraded fragments, wards copied by a debt-bound scribe with a tired hand — and yet the fen-woman's nightmares had stopped. The midwife's mothers had lived. The boy with the brand had slept whole nights. Small miracles at small prices, and Jonah had pocketed the difference and called it survival. But if the wards of the realm were one great working — if the border wards and the temple wards and the little paper charms sold in alleys were all threads of a single garment —

Then tonight that garment had torn from hem to collar, and every scrap of it was crying out.

The door of the tower room banged open. Brother Anselm stood there, old and stooped, holding a lamp with a shaking hand, his face the color of tallow. "Jonah. Jonah, come away from the window." His voice cracked in the middle. "The High Cantor commands all scribes to the undercroft. Now. There are—" He swallowed. "There are things being said. Come away from the window."

Jonah did not move. "Brother Anselm. The wards. The border wards — they're fallen, aren't they."

"How could you possibly—"

"The sound. Through the stone." Jonah closed their fist around the burning-cold coin. "I know what breaking wards sound like. I've sold enough of them."

Something passed over the old brother's face — fear, yes, but beneath it a flash of something worse: recognition. As if Jonah had said a password they should not have known. "Come down," he said again, quieter. "Please."

Down in the streets, the crowd in the square had begun to move, a slow millennial drift toward the temple doors, toward light, toward authority, toward anyone who would tell them the sky had not ended. Torches streamed through the lanes like embers blown on a wind. And above it all the seamless dark pressed down, patient and total, waiting.

Jonah took one last look at the starless vault, at the place where Vael's Lantern used to burn, and slid the coin back into their pocket, where it lay against their hip like a splinter of the vanished sky.

Whatever was coming through the broken borders — whatever had eaten the stars — Jonah understood, with the calm certainty of a person stepping off a ledge, that the small life they had built out of small crimes was over. Twelve years of debts and secret sales had just been called due, all at once, by something with the reach of heaven.

They turned from the window and followed Brother Anselm down into the dark of the temple — where, though neither of them knew it yet, the High Cantor was already waiting with the truth, and the truth was heavier than any star.

* * *

The undercroft of Saint Verre smelled of cold incense and older stone. Forty scribes crowded it by lamplight, and above their heads, muffled by floors of rock, the panic of Vhalen went on like weather.

Jonah stood at the edge of the crowd with their hand still closed around the coin, and watched the High Cantor descend the far stair — watched her pause at the bottom, look out across the assembled faces, and find Jonah's eyes across the room with a precision that made the cold coin seem suddenly warm by comparison.

She had a ledger in her hands. Not the debt-ledgers. Something older, bound in gray leather, stamped with nine interlocked rings.

"Jonah Kavanagh," the High Cantor said, and her voice carried strangely in the stone, as if the room itself were leaning in. "Your debt has come due. All of it. Tonight."

The lamps guttered. And in Jonah's pocket, the coin began to hum.
