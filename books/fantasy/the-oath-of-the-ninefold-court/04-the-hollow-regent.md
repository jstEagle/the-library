## 4. The Hollow Regent

The wax was the first wrong thing.

Sera Ilvane had knelt on the cold mosaic of the Vhalen throne room perhaps four hundred times in her eleven years of service — as a squire, as a knight-errant of the border wards, and finally, for the last two years, as nothing at all: a ward-knight without a post, summoned to audiences because her name still carried weight she no longer deserved. She knew the rituals of the court the way a blind woman knows her own hallway. She knew that the royal seal pressed into green wax smelled of cedar and hot iron. She knew the herald's cadence, the rustle of petitioners shuffling forward, the particular creak of the Iron Throne — not iron at all, but dragonbone lacquered to look like it, a vanity of the first king Aldous that historians had long since stopped correcting.

She knew all of it so well that when the wax wept black, her body understood before her mind did.

* * *

It began, as most terrible mornings do, with perfect ordinary light.

Dawn came through the high clerestory windows in long amber blades, laying themselves across the floor like swords laid down for a parley. The audience chamber smelled of beeswax and lilies — the lilies renewed every third day, an old superstition against plague. Petitioners lined the eastern wall in their rough finery: a wool merchant from the Greywater marches whose warehouses had flooded; a widow from Candle Row whose sons had been conscripted; a temple clerk with ink-stained fingers clutching a ledger to his chest like a shield.

Sera stood at her assigned place among the honor guard, six paces left of the dais, her sword peace-bonded and her posture immaculate. Disgrace had taught her this much: how to be present and invisible at once. How to stand so straight that no one thought to look too closely at her face. Two years ago she had returned from the border fortress of Kellin's Watch with her command dissolved and her honor forfeit — *the wards failed under your watch, Knight Ilvane*, the tribunal had said, as though she had personally unknotted the spells that kept the marauding things of the wild dark away from Vhalen's throat. As though she had not spent three days and three nights feeding her own blood into the ward-stones while they drank and drank and would not be filled.

She had sworn oaths at Kellin's Watch. That was the thing no tribunal had wanted to hear spoken aloud. Ward-knights swore the old words — the ones the temples called decorative, archaic, a pretty inheritance from the Ninefold Court's vanished age. *By ring and root and rendered breath.* She had said them with her hand in the ward-stone's hollow, and the stone had taken something from her that she had never gotten back, and in exchange the borders had held for nine more years.

Until the night they didn't.

She did not think about that night. She had become very skilled at not thinking about that night.

"Petitioners approach," the herald cried, and the doors opened, and King Aldric the Third of that name entered to hold his morning justice.

* * *

He looked well. That was what she noticed first, and what she would keep noticing, helplessly, for the rest of the hour: he looked *well*. Better than well. The king had been ill through the winter — everyone knew it, though no one said it above a whisper. A wasting sickness, the physicians called it, a cooling of the blood. In the autumn he had been gray-faced and thin, his hands trembling so badly he'd needed a page to steady the scepter. Now he walked the length of the chamber with a rolling, unhurried stride, and his color was the deep healthy bronze of a man who has labored in summer fields, and there was something in the set of his shoulders —

Sera searched for the word and found it and disliked it immediately.

*Well-fed.*

Aldric settled onto the dragonbone throne, and the lilies by the dais stirred though there was no draft. "Bring me the first petitioner," he said, and his voice was his voice, warm and a little hoarse, the same voice that had once, years ago, thanked her father for thirty years of ward service at a midwinter feast. Everything about him was exactly right.

That should have been reassuring. Instead, Sera felt the fine hairs on her forearms rise beneath her gauntlets, one by one, like soldiers standing to attention.

The wool merchant came forward, sank to his knees, and stammered out his grief — flooded warehouses, ruined stock, a tax assessment due on goods now floating in the Greywater. It was a small sorrow, the ordinary grain of a kingdom's life. The old king — the real one, the tired one — would have waved his steward over, granted a remission, made some dry joke about the weather to soften the man's humiliation.

This Aldric listened with total, motionless attention. His head tilted. Not the tilt of a man thinking. The tilt of something listening *through* him.

"The water took what the water was owed," the king said gently. "But you are not the water, are you. You are only the man standing where it wished to pass."

The merchant blinked. "...I — I don't understand, Your Majesty."

"No," the king agreed, kindly. "Very few do. Steward — remit his assessment. And take his name." He smiled, and it was Aldric's smile, worn like a favorite coat. "All of their names. We will remember who came to us in the season of rising water."

Relief broke over the merchant's face like sunlight. He kissed the hem of the king's robe and withdrew weeping with gratitude, and the chamber murmured its approval — generosity, mercy, the king restored to himself. Only Sera saw the steward write the name in a ledger bound in leather so dark it was nearly black, and only Sera noticed that the ledger had no lock and needed none, because its pages were already full.

*We will remember.*

Somewhere far below her fear, below even her training, an older instinct spoke in the language of oaths: *that is not how remembrance works. Remembrance costs the rememberer. This costs nothing. Nothing that costs nothing is safe.*

* * *

The widow came next, then a guildmaster, then a land dispute between two cousins that had festered for six years. The king resolved each case with unsettling speed and unsettling precision — never wrongly, Sera realized with growing horror, but never *humanly*. He gave each petitioner exactly what they had come for and something extra besides, a small uncanny bonus, a kindness slightly too tailored, as if he had reached inside each grievance and found the precise shape of the wanting. And every judgment ended the same way.

"And your name?"

"Marla Tenneck, Majesty."

"We will remember you, Marla Tenneck."

By the ninth repetition, Sera had stopped hearing it as courtesy. It sounded like a tally. Like a ledger closing softly, entry after entry after entry. The chamber adored him. She could feel the love rolling off the crowd in waves — here at last was a king who *saw* them, who remembered the small people — and the love itself frightened her more than anything else, because she recognized its texture. It was the devotion of things being gathered in. Of sheep counting themselves toward a gate.

Her hand had drifted, without her permission, to the old scar at her collarbone — the mark the ward-stone had left at Kellin's Watch, a pale seam like a closed eye. Beneath her gauntlet, the scar had begun to ache. It had not ached in two years.

*By ring and root and rendered breath,* she had sworn, in a stone hollow, with her blood running. The oath had gone somewhere. Oaths always went somewhere; that was the whole secret architecture of the world, the thing the temples had let fall into bedtime story. Her oath lived in the border wards, and the border wards were failing, and now the scar woke like a dog lifting its head before the earthquake comes.

The tenth petitioner was the temple clerk with the ledger.

He was young, round-shouldered, terrified in the way of minor officials bearing bad arithmetic. "Your Majesty," he began, "the — the tithe rolls from the northern temples. There is a discrepancy. The ward-charms distributed these past years — the accounting shows—"

"Yes," the king said.

Just that. *Yes.* Softly, with enormous patience, the way one speaks to a child explaining that rain is wet.

The clerk faltered. "Majesty?"

"There is a discrepancy." The king rose from the throne.

Sera had seen kings rise a hundred times. It was a production — the rustle of councilors, the shifting of guards. This rise was different. It was smooth the way water is smooth, continuous, without any of the small honest jerks of joints and effort, and it seemed to go on longer than a body should allow, unfolding upward like something surfacing from a depth. The lilies bowed. Every candle flame in the chamber leaned toward him at once, a silent congregation bending east.

And Sera looked up — she could not have stopped herself any more than she could stop her heart — and met the king's eyes.

Behind the eyes.

There was a moment — later she would measure it in heartbeats, five, perhaps six — when the mask of Aldric thinned like fog over glass, and she saw what wore him.

Not a demon. Demons, she had been taught, were fire and appetite, all roar and hunger, and you met them with steel and prayer and sometimes you even won. What she saw behind the king's grey eyes was worse than fire. It was *patient*. Vast the way the sea is vast — not large but *deep*, layered, going down past all light. It looked back at her through her sovereign's face with an attention so complete and so old that her knees nearly buckled, and in that attention there was no hatred at all, which was the most terrifying part. Hatred you could answer. This regarded her the way a farmer regards a field in early spring: noting what had come up, deciding what would be permitted to grow.

And beneath that regard, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, she felt something else. Something muffled and human and drowning, pressing outward from very far inside — the sensation of a man's fists against the inside of his own skin.

Then the fog closed. Aldric blinked, and he was only a tired king again, smiling down at the clerk, saying warmly, "The tithe rolls are the least of our concerns, son. Go home to your scriptorium. Tell them the crown is satisfied."

"But the wards—" the clerk whispered.

"*Are satisfied,*" said the king, and every candle flame stood upright again, and the chamber exhaled.

The clerk fled. Sera marked his face — young, honest, doomed-looking — and filed it away in the place where she kept the few things she was still ashamed of not doing.

* * *

The audience ended. The court dissolved into its bright murmur. And Sera stood at her post, sweating inside her armor, doing the mathematics of survival.

No one else had seen. Or if they had, they had seen and chosen blindness, which amounted to the same thing. If she spoke — *Majesty is possessed, Majesty is a glove with something inside it* — she would be taken to the physicians, or worse, to whatever had replaced the physicians. She was a disgraced knight. Her word was worth precisely the paper of her forfeiture. And the thing behind those eyes had looked at her, of all the assembled court, had looked at her *longer*.

Because of the scar. Because of the oath. Because whatever it was, it knew ward-work when it tasted it in the air, and it knew her blood had been poured into the failing stones at Kellin's Watch, and it knew — oh, it knew with that bottomless patience — that she was one of the reasons the door had opened wide enough for it to walk through.

The guilt arrived then, fully formed, like a blade driven hilt-deep. She had sworn the old words. They had all sworn the old words, dozens of knights along the border, feeding the stones year after year because the stones asked and asking why was not a knight's business. The wards had drunk and drunk and would not be filled, and none of them had wondered where the wine went. None of them had asked what covenant they were actually keeping alive, or starving, or feeding to something else.

*By ring and root and rendered breath.* She had thought she was holding the line. She might have been holding the door.

"Sera Ilvane."

She started. The chamberlain stood beside her, mild and correct. "His Majesty requests a word before you go. He remembers you."

Four words. *He remembers you.* They went through her like cold wire.

"I am honored," she heard herself say.

* * *

The privy chamber off the audience hall was small and golden, hung with the hunting trophies of four generations. A table, two chairs, a window with light falling through it in a clean square. The king sat alone at the table, no guards, no stewards, and gestured her to the chair across from him with a hand that trembled — trembled! — exactly as the sick king's hand had trembled all last winter.

"You look at me strangely, Knight Ilvane," Aldric said. His voice was gentle. His eyes were only eyes. Perhaps it had been the light. Perhaps the fasting. Perhaps she was mad, disgraced and mad, seeing monsters in her sovereign because shame needs a monster to feed on.

"Forgive me, Majesty. I am — I have been poorly."

"Yes," he said. "You have. Since Kellin's Watch." He turned his hand palm-up on the table, and in it lay the great seal of Vhalen — the signet ring of the first kings, worn into audience only for the sealing of instruments of state. "I have a task for you. A small errand, for old service. There is a charter to be dispatched to the northern garrisons. New orders for the ward-knights." His mouth curved. "The borders must be tended, don't you find? Things drift, when no one tends them."

He held the ring out to her. "Press the seal yourself. I find I trust your hands."

Every instinct she owned screamed the same single syllable: *no.* But there was no graceful refusal from a disgraced knight to her king's face, and refusing would put her name in the black ledger with all the others, remembered. So she took the ring. It was heavier than it should have been, the way wet rope is heavy, and warm — warm as a living thing, though it had been sitting on bare wood.

She pressed it into the wax the chamberlain had readied, and lifted it away, and the charter bore the crowned stag of Vhalen in perfect relief.

Except that the wax was black.

Not shadowed, not darkened — black, a blackness with depth to it, and as she watched, a bead welled at the edge of the impression the way resin wells from cut pine, swelled, and ran down the parchment in a slow glistening line. The seal was weeping. The royal seal of Vhalen was weeping black wax, and the drop fell to the table and did not spread, and did not dry, and sat there like an eye.

"Beautiful, isn't it," the king said softly. "The old ways coming back. Ring and root and rendered breath." He said the words of her secret oath — words no king had spoken in a century, words that existed only in ward-stone hollows and the mouths of dying knights — and he said them the way a man quotes a favorite poem.

Sera's vision narrowed to a tunnel. She heard herself say, from very far away, "Majesty should rest."

"Yes," he agreed, pleasant as Sunday. "Soon. There's so much still to gather first." He extended his hand for the ring, and she placed it in his palm and watched his fingers close around it, and the fingers did not close quite like fingers.

"You may go, Knight Ilvane. And do give my regards to the border." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "You'll want to see it before it's gone."

* * *

She walked out of the palace through corridors that had housed her whole adult life and felt every portrait's eyes track her passage. At the gate, the guards saluted her — *Knight Ilvane* — and she returned it with a steady arm while her mind ran ahead of her like a horse with the bit in its teeth.

Proof. She needed proof, something that would survive her death, because she understood now with total clarity that she would die if she stayed, and probably soon, and possibly messily. She went to her rooms — she was given lodgings in the outer ward, a disgrace's modest cell — and she did not pack. Packing told watchers what they needed to know. She took three things only: her sword, her father's knife, and the charter.

The charter with the weeping seal.

She wrapped it in oilcloth against her ribs, beneath her breastplate, where the black bead had soaked a small cold stain through her shirt that would not wash off and did not hurt and *waited*.

At the postern gate the sentry asked her business, and she showed him the token that any ward-knight still carried the right to show: passage on the king's service. It was even true, in its way. She had been given an errand. She was simply choosing to run it in entirely the wrong direction.

The city of Vhalen swallowed her, market-crowded and sunlit and utterly unaware, and Sera walked fast through the middle of it with a dead king's orders against her heart, trying to think. Who believed disgraced knights? No one. Who might believe evidence? The temples — but the temple clerk had looked half-dead of his own knowledge already, and the High Cantor's silence over the years suddenly read very differently. The border garrisons — but the charter she carried was addressed to them, new orders, and she would bet her other lung that those orders were the reason the seal had needed pressing at all. Something was going out to the ward-knights in the king's name, wearing the king's seal, weeping its black signature, and whatever it ordered, it would be the end of what remained of the old protections.

Which meant someone had to warn them. Which meant she had to get north, past the checkpoints, alone, disgraced, hunted the moment her absence was noted.

Or — not alone. There were others who still said the old words. Others whose scars ached. Others who had fed the stones and never asked where the wine went. If any of them had begun, in these last strange weeks, to wonder —

She reached the south road as the bells rang noon, and paused at the milestone where the pilgrim path branched toward the river country, and made herself say it plainly, once, in her own head:

*Aldric is dead. Whatever sits on the throne is not him. And I helped open the door.*

The words did not destroy her, which surprised her. They clarified her. Grief and guilt, she discovered, could be load-bearing if you stacked them properly.

North, then. Toward the river, the ford, the roads that led out of everything she had known. Behind her the palace towers caught the afternoon sun and blazed gold and innocent, and high in the clear sky the stars' daytime absence felt, for the first time in her life, less like distance than like *holding its breath*.

Two miles out, at the shrine of Saint Verre where travelers paused for blessing, she learned from a carter's gossip that the temple district had been sealed at dawn by royal inquisitors — fires reported, arrests, the scriptorium of Saint Verre itself in flames.

Someone else, then. Someone had gotten out ahead of her. Someone carrying the same arithmetic she carried, running the same direction.

Sera adjusted her sword-belt, put the burning temple at her back, and walked into the gathering dusk with a weeping seal against her ribs and one purpose narrowing her whole horizon to a point: *find whoever survived Saint Verre, and swear them — freely, this time, with eyes open — to stand against the thing wearing the crown.*
