## 6. The Charm-Seller's Market

Lowcandle did not appear so much as accumulate.

One moment the road was a rutted scar between blackened fields, and the next there were lanterns — dozens of them, then hundreds, strung on wires and nailed to eaves and hung from the ribs of derelict barges that had been beached so long they had become architecture. The warren had grown outward from an old coal landing like mold from a damp wall: lean-tos stacked on lean-tos, bridges of rope and scrap iron sagging between rooftops, staircases that went up and kept going up until Jonah could not tell which level was the ground. The air smelled of tallow smoke, fried onions, river mud, and under it all something older and sweeter — burnt sugar and old paper, the smell of charms being made.

"You've been here before," Sera said. It was not quite a question.

"I worked here." Jonah pulled their hood lower as two children darted past with a stolen wheel of cheese and a pursuer's curses behind them. "Twelve years. Every market day, every festival, every night the temple gates were slow to close. If you were poor in Vhalen and the wards failed your door, you came to Lowcandle. And if you came to Lowcandle, eventually you found me."

"And sold them what, exactly?"

Jonah heard the edge in her voice and did not flinch from it. They deserved the edge. "Protection. Small protections. A knot-charm against fever. A threshold-braid to keep nightmares out. Charms against the marked sickness, against bad luck, against the things that come through when the border wards thin." They paused at the head of the first stair, looking down into the lantern-lit throat of the market. "I told myself I was selling comfort. That the poor would buy false hope anyway, from someone worse than me."

"And was that true?"

"I'm about to find out."

* * *

The market proper began where the stairs ended, in a cavernous space that had once been a grain warehouse and was now a city unto itself — stalls and cook-fires and sleeping hammocks layered three deep, noise pressing down like weather. Jonah moved through it the way water moves through familiar stone, finding channels no stranger could see. Nod here. A coin pressed into a child's palm there. Once, a low whistle answered by another whistle three stalls away, and the crowd ahead of them parted just enough.

"They remember you," Sera murmured.

"They remember what I sold them." Jonah stopped before a stall hung with dried herbs and fish-hooks, where an old woman with cloud-white eyes sat sorting beans by touch. "Mother Cass. It's Jonah."

The sorting hands went still. "Kavanagh." The name came out flat as a dropped plate. "Temple scribe. Charm-seller." She turned her blind face toward them, and Jonah saw that her eyes had been stitched shut long ago, the lids cross-hatched with fine silver thread — ward-work, they realized with a lurch, ward-work done *into flesh*. "Half of Lowcandle owes you money and the other half swears you saved their lives. Both are wrong, aren't they? Both are right?"

"The charms were real, Cass. More real than I knew. They're pieces of something old — pieces of the covenant that holds the realm together. The stars going out—"

"I know why the stars went out." Her voice cracked like a whip. "My granddaughter bought a fever-knot off you four years ago. Paid six coppers she didn't have. Last month it started humming. Humming, Kavanagh, like a struck bell that wouldn't stop, and then two nights ago it sang one note so loud the neighbors came running, and my granddaughter stood in the middle of the floor with light coming out of her hands and weeping because she thought she'd been cursed." The bean-scoop trembled in her grip. "What did you put in my girl?"

"Nothing. That's what I'm trying to tell you. I copied those knots from a template I wasn't supposed to see. I thought they were wards. They were fragments — shards of an oath sworn by nine seats of a court older than the kingdom. The humming means the fragments are waking up now the covenant is breaking. Your granddaughter isn't cursed. She's *carrying*."

Silence spread around them like spilled oil. Nearby conversations had gone quiet, one by one, the way they do when grief enters a room.

"Carrying," Mother Cass repeated. She set down the scoop. From beneath her stall she drew a small cedar box, worn smooth by handling, and opened it. Inside lay a braid of gray thread knotted seven times, and the braid was glowing — faintly, patiently, like a coal remembering fire. "Her father's. He wore it against the marked sickness and it held. When he died I kept it. Now take it, since it seems it was never ours to keep." She pushed the box across the stall. "But you'll look at her first. You'll tell her herself what she carries, not me secondhand. That's my price. That's the debt *you'll* owe *me*."

Jonah took the box with both hands. "I swear it."

"Swearing's cheap this week," Cass said bitterly, but her hand brushed theirs across the stall, brief and human. "Go. There are others humming. The whole warren sounds like a bell tower with the bells muffled. People are frightened, Kavanagh. Frightened people with burning charms is a fire waiting for a spark."

* * *

They found others.

A ferryman named Brakk who had bought a storm-knot for his boat and surrendered it willingly, almost gratefully — "it started whispering to the river, I don't hold with things that whisper" — handing over a fragment shaped like a coiled wave of braided copper. Twin sisters, laundresses, who between them owned a single charm bought years apart without either knowing, and whose fragments clicked together in Jonah's palm with a sound like a lock finding its key. A beggar with no legs who refused payment for his fragment and made Jonah laugh, badly, for the first time in days: "About time something I own mattered. Take it. Mind you come back and tell me if I'm famous."

Some gave freely. Some gave after argument. One woman gave after weeping, because the charm had been her mother's and giving it up felt like burying her again, and Jonah stood through the weeping and did not rush her, because rushing her would have been a theft on top of a theft.

By the time the market lamps had burned down to their third refilling, Jonah carried eleven fragments wrapped in oilcloth against their chest, and each one hummed at a different pitch, and together, faintly, they were beginning to harmonize.

It was Sera who said it, quietly, as they paused to eat standing at a noodle stall neither of them tasted. "You knew all of them. Their names, their troubles, what they'd paid and what they couldn't afford." She was watching Jonah over the rim of her bowl. "Twelve years of small crimes, and every crime has a face."

"That's what makes them crimes," Jonah said. "If the people had been strangers, it would only have been business."

* * *

They found Tam Voss near midnight, in the deepest tier of the warren, where the lanterns thinned and the river seeped audibly through the walls.

He had been Jonah's customer for nine years. A dredger who worked the sunken quarter of old Vhalen, pulling salvage from streets the river had claimed; broad-shouldered, slow-speaking, careful with money the way men are who have never had enough of it. His wife had died of the marked sickness while pregnant with their daughter, and the child, Liss, had been born marked anyway — a birthmark like a splash of ink across half her face, which the ignorant called curse-touched and which the charms, at least, seemed to quiet. Jonah had sold him threshold-braids and calming knots, always at cost, sometimes below it, telling themselves the discount was virtue rather than investment.

Tam's door was already open when they knocked. That was the first wrong thing.

The second wrong thing was Tam himself, standing in the middle of the single room with his back to the wall, holding a dredging hook, and behind him — small, dark-eyed, eight years old, the ink-stain covering her cheek like a shadow of a wing — Liss, clutching something to her chest.

"Temple man," Tam said. His voice was steady but his knuckles were white on the hook. "I heard you were back. Heard you were asking after the charms. Heard the ones that hum are worth gold to certain buyers." He swallowed. "I'm asking you to leave, Jonah. Please. I don't want trouble. I just want what's mine."

"Tam." Jonah kept their hands visible and their voice low. "No one's buying. I'm not selling either. Listen to me — the charms, all of them, they're pieces of something breaking. Something that's breaking the sky along with it. The fragment you have — Liss's calming knot — it might matter. It might matter more than anything either of us ever understood. I'm asking, not taking."

"Everyone asks." Tam's eyes were wet and furious. "The king's men asked last week. Polite as priests. Wanted to know who'd sold marked folk charms, wanted lists, names. I didn't give names." He lifted the hook higher. "Then the inquisitors came tonight. Followed you here, maybe, or maybe they've been sweeping Lowcandle since the stars died. They took Old Marta two doors down for a humming charm. Took her *screaming*." His voice broke. "I watched my wife die. I will not watch them take my daughter's—"

The window exploded inward.

Later, Jonah would piece it together: a grappling line, black-clad figures, the Regent's questioners in their ash-gray hoods — not many, four perhaps, but trained, silent, moving like spilled mercury. Later, Jonah would understand that the inquisitors had not followed them at all; they had been working the lower tiers all night, door to door, harvesting humming charms and the people attached to them. Tonight had simply been the night the harvest reached Tam Voss's door, and Jonah's knock had come at almost the same hour.

But in the moment there was only motion and lamplight and Sera shouting, drawing steel, meeting the first figure in the doorway with a crash that shook the room. Jonah grabbed for Liss. Tam swung the hook. A questioner caught the shaft and wrenched, and Tam — strong from twenty years of dredging, strong as an ox, strong enough — wrenched back and drove the man into the wall hard enough to crack plaster.

For a moment, one bright suspended moment, it seemed they might hold. Sera had dropped one and backed a second into the corner; Jonah had Liss by the wrist, pulling her toward the back hatch that stank of river; Tam stood over them both with the hook raised like a banner.

Then the third questioner simply walked past all of it — walked through the fight the way a knife walks through water — and put his hand flat on Liss's chest, over the charm, and spoke one word in a language Jonah's bones recognized and their mind did not.

The charm flared white. Liss screamed — not in pain, in something worse, in *recognition*, as if the word had reached inside her and read her aloud — and the questioner smiled behind his hood and pulled.

And Tam Voss, dredger, widower, father, did the arithmetic of love that has no other solution. He dropped the hook. He wrapped both arms around the questioner from behind, around the man, around the reaching hand, around the word still in the air — and he heaved backward with everything his life had built into him, toward the window, toward the dark, toward the river-smell and the drop.

"Run," he said to Jonah. Calmly. Almost gently. "Take her. Run."

They went out the window together, Jonah and the screaming child, and the last thing Jonah saw of that room was Tam Voss and the questioner going through the frame in a tangle of limbs and gray cloth, falling together into the black water below the lowest tier, and the splash that followed sounded, horribly, like a door closing.

Sera came out after them bleeding from a scalp wound, having left two questioners dead or dying in the doorway, and did not ask questions. She ran. Jonah ran, carrying Liss, carrying the child's grief like a second weight, through dripping tunnels and up rope ladders while the warren woke around them in shouts and lantern-light.

* * *

They stopped at dawn in the loft of a rope-walk above the fish-market, a long dim space smelling of tar and hemp, safe for now because its owner owed Jonah six years of unpaid charm-debt and paid it, wordlessly, with blankets and silence.

Liss had stopped screaming. That was the mercy. She sat wrapped in a blanket with her ink-dark cheek against Jonah's shoulder, holding the calming knot — untouched, unharmed, still humming its small stubborn note — and she did not cry, which was worse than crying. Eight-year-olds should cry.

"He held onto him," she said at last, to nobody, to the tar-dark rafters. "Papa doesn't let go of anything. He says that's what dredging teaches you." Her small hand tightened on the knot. "Is Papa coming back?"

Jonah had lied to customers for twelve years. Comforting lies, profitable lies, lies worn smooth as river stones. They opened their mouth and found that every lie they owned was useless here.

"No," they said. "I don't think so."

Liss nodded slowly, the way children nod at terrible truths, accepting them whole because no one has yet taught them to break things into manageable pieces. Then she held up the charm. "This was Mama's pattern," she said. "Papa said so. It's all I have of both of them now." She looked up, and in the gray pre-dawn light her marked face was solemn as an icon. "You can borrow it. For your important thing. But you have to give it back."

"I will," Jonah said, and took the fragment with hands that were not steady, and understood, finally and completely, what twelve years of small sales had actually purchased: not comfort, not protection, but this — a shelf of other people's most precious things, stored in strangers' bodies, called due on a night like this one, paid for in blood like Tam Voss's blood, still cooling somewhere in the black water under the lowest tier.

Sera sat down beside them, cleaning her blade with mechanical care. She glanced at the child, asleep now against Jonah's side, and then at the oilcloth bundle of fragments, and then out the loft window, east, where the sky remained starless and would remain starless.

"The temple registers," she said quietly. "You sold hundreds. Maybe thousands. Each one a fragment, each one a person."

"Yes."

"And the Regent's people are collecting too. Door to door. Faster than us, better armed, and they know exactly what they're looking for." She sheathed the blade. "We can't visit them all one by one, Jonah. We'll lose."

Jonah looked down at the sleeping child, at the humming knot in the bundle, at their own hands that had written every one of those charms in a fair copying hand, and felt something shift in them — not resolve exactly, nothing so clean. More like a debt ledger turning itself to a new page.

"Then we stop visiting," they said. "Lowcandle knows me. Twelve years, market day, every festival. Tomorrow I stand where I always stood, and I don't sell — I call in. Every soul who ever bought a charm from Jonah Kavanagh comes to the old stall, and hears why, and chooses." They looked at Sera. "Freely given or not at all. That's the only way any of this works. I think—" they laughed, hollow, "—I think that's the whole lesson, and I haven't even learned it properly yet."

Sera studied them for a long moment. "And if the Regent's collectors come to your stall tomorrow?"

Jonah touched the cold coin in their pocket, and beyond it the bundle of fragments, eleven small voices beginning, very softly, to sing together.

"Then they'll find out," they said, "what a market full of debtors looks like when the debts are finally theirs to collect instead."

Far below, across the black water of the harbor, a temple bell that no one had rung began tolling — slow, deliberate, counting — and did not stop.
