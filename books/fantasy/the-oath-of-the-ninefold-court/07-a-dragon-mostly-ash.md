## 7. A Dragon, Mostly Ash

The Cinderfells did not look like mountains so much as the memory of them — a spine of black ridges running north from the Greywater, their slopes glassed in places where old fires had melted stone to mirror. Sera had ridden past their foothills twice in her ward-knight years and both times her horse had refused to go further, planting its hooves and trembling as though it could smell something she couldn't.

This time she was on foot, leading a borrowed mule that had no such sense, or no such dignity. Jonah walked beside her with the coin and the fragments wrapped against their chest, and behind them the road fell away toward Vhalen, where every bell had been ringing wrong for three days.

"You're certain this is where we're going," Jonah said. It wasn't quite a question. It hadn't been a question since the second night out of the temple's ashes, when the coin had grown cold enough to frost the inside of its wrapping and pulled at them like a fish on a line whenever they drifted east.

"I'm certain the seal wants to go here," Sera said. She kept it in an iron box at her belt — the royal seal that wept black wax, the thing she'd stolen off the Regent's own desk while the thing wearing the king looked at her with eyes like wells. "And I'm certain the seal is older than the crown. Whatever made it was made here first."

"And you learned this where? In your knight lessons?"

"In my disgrace," Sera said. "There's more reading time, after."

Jonah almost smiled. It was a small thing, that almost, but she'd begun to collect them. Three days on the road with a temple scribe who sold illegal charms to debtors, and Sera Ilvane — who had once held the Oath of the Veil with her whole throat, who had spoken the words that let the Hollow thing into her sovereign — had started collecting almost-smiles the way a drowning woman collects air.

The mountain took them at dusk.

There was no gate, no cave mouth visible until they were nearly inside it. One moment they were climbing a slope of slag and ash-lichen; the next the rock simply opened, a seam widening into a corridor tall enough for a chapel and warm as a held breath. The warmth was the first wrong thing. Cinderfell nights froze hard enough to crack iron, and here the air came up from below like something exhaling in its sleep.

Sera unhooked the lantern, then thought better of it. Far down the corridor, deep in the dark, something glowed.

Not firelight. Not lamp-glow. It was smaller than either and older than both — a color she had no name for, gold trying to be red trying to be remembered. It pulsed. Slowly. Like a heart that had forgotten what hurry was.

"Vhalen's histories say the dragons died at Sundering Ford," Jonah whispered. "Four hundred years ago. All of them, in one battle."

"Histories are written by people who need wars to end cleanly," Sera said.

They walked. The corridor widened, narrowed, opened into galleries where the walls were carved — not decorated, *carved* — with rows upon rows of names in a script that predated the Ninefold Court and probably predated the language Sera dreamed in. She caught fragments by lantern-light as they passed: *keeper of the eastern wind*. *She who carried the law in her blood.* *He chose the burning.* Thousands of names. Tens of thousands. The walls went back farther than the lantern reached.

"It's a ledger," Jonah said softly, and their scribe's voice cracked on the word. "It's a debt-ledger. Every name owed to someone."

"Or owed by," Sera said. "With dragons I've stopped assuming we're the creditors."

The gallery ended at a lip of black glass, and beyond it the mountain opened into a hollow vast enough to swallow the Scriptorium of Saint Verre entire, dome and all. Sera stood at the edge and felt her understanding of the world quietly rearrange itself around what she saw.

At the bottom of the hollow lay a dragon.

*Mostly* lay. That was the honest word, and some instinct told her honesty would matter here. What remained of Vaskarath was a hill of ash-grey scale stretched over a frame like felled masts, ribs showing through the hide along his flanks where flesh had burned away or simply been spent. One wing folded against the wall was more hole than membrane, laced with scar tissue like a moth-eaten banner. His tail ran out into rubble. His head alone was the size of the mule cart, horned and heavy-jawed, resting on a bed of cinders he had perhaps shaped himself, perhaps simply collapsed into four centuries ago.

And in the hollow of his chest, beneath scales worn thin as vellum, burned the light.

A heart-flame no bigger than a candle.

That was the horror of it, Sera thought. Not the ruin — ruins were honest, ruins were over. The horror was the flame, guttering in that cavernous dark, still burning after everything, still *insisting*, the way a man lost in snow keeps walking because stopping is death. She had seen siege cities hold out longer than they should have. She had never seen anything hold out this long on this little.

The eye opened.

It was larger than her shield. It moved with the slow gravity of something that had long ago stopped being impressed by urgency, and it fixed on them across two hundred feet of dark air, and Sera felt her knees inform her they were considering resignation.

"Two oath-breakers," said a voice like a landslide deciding to be patient about it, "come to my tomb."

Sera's hand found nothing to grip. There was no sword big enough; there had never been a sword big enough; the knights who'd written the manuals had known that and written them anyway. "You know us."

"I know the taste of oaths." The great head shifted; cinders slid from it like sand off a dune. "Yours broke loudest, veil-knight. I heard it in the wards the night the stars went out. A promise tearing is a very particular sound." The eye slid to Jonah. "Yours is quieter. You never swore loudly enough to break anything. You just... leaked. Twelve years of small betrayals sold at market stalls." Something moved in the ruined face that might have been a smile, if ruin could smile. "I find yours more interesting."

"We've come to ask for help," Jonah said. Their voice shook and held anyway, which Sera was learning was simply what Jonah did. "The Court's broken. The Regent—"

"I know what sits on the throne." The word came out with heat, actual heat, a breath of air hot enough to make Sera's eyes sting. "I felt it take the king. Do you know how that felt, little debtor? Four hundred years I have lain here keeping the last of the covenant's fire alive — *keeping it alive*, feeding it what I am, year by year, scale by scale, becoming this—" the wing-hole flexed, "—so that when the oath failed there would be one ember left to relight it. And the night the wards fell, I felt the crown eat the law and I could do *nothing*. I cannot fly. I cannot burn. I can barely *remember*." The flame in his chest guttered hard, and for one terrible instant Sera saw it shrink, saw the darkness lean in—

—and steady again. Barely.

"The covenant isn't dead," Jonah said. "We have fragments. Charms — the ones I sold. They were pieces of it, weren't they? Scattered on purpose?"

"Scattered like seed," Vaskarath said. "By fools who hoped. By me, at the end, when there was no one left to carry them properly. The poor keep what the great discard. I put the oath into cheap charms and sold the idea to a temple that liked the tithes, and twelve years of debtors and widows carried the Ninefold Court in their pockets without knowing it." The eye closed partway. "It was either that or let it die with me. I chose indignity. I recommend it over death. It has better conversation."

Sera stepped forward before her fear could vote. "Then take the fragments. Take them and relight it yourself. You're the keeper of the flame —"

"*Look at me.*"

She looked. It cost something to obey, and she obeyed anyway, and that was the whole of her life lately: paying costs for looking at things other people had the wisdom to avoid.

"There is less of me than there was of the king when the crown finished with him," Vaskarath said. "If I gathered every fragment in creation into my chest tonight, I could make the flame flare bright enough to read by. For an hour. Then it goes out, and the covenant goes with it, and there is no scattering seed a second time because there will be no me to scatter it." He drew a breath, and the whole hollow breathed with him, warm and sulfurous and sad. "The flame must go to the First Hearth. The place it was kindled. Only there can it drink deep enough to burn on its own again. And a flame this size cannot walk."

"Someone carries it," Jonah said quietly.

"Someone carries it." The eye opened fully again, and Sera understood, suddenly, why the histories called dragons *the honest race*. Everything else in her life had lied to her gently — the Order, the crown, the pretty ceremonies of the oath. This creature simply told her the truth at landslide volume and let her stand in it. "The flame lives on vow. It always has. Fire that remembers promises needs a mouth to speak them and a body to mean them, and so a carrier takes it into themselves — holds it under the breastbone, speaks the covenant line by line along the road, and feeds it their own warmth when it gutters. And when they reach the Hearth—"

"They give it back," Jonah said. "All of it."

"The flame does not distinguish between the warmth offered freely and the body it was borrowed from," Vaskarath said. "Carriers always burn. That is not a price I set. It is not a price anyone set. It is simply what fire *is*, and I will not dress it in ceremony so you can pretend otherwise. Every carrier in the ledgers outside—" his gaze, vast and tired, flicked toward the corridor of names, "—burned. Some slowly. Some in a single afternoon. All of them knew, and all of them went, and I have spent four centuries deciding whether that makes them saints or fools, and I have concluded it makes them *people*, which is worse and better than either."

Silence filled the hollow. The candle-heart pulsed. Somewhere above them, through miles of stone, Sera imagined she could feel Vhalen's wrong-rung bells.

"How long," Jonah asked, "does a carrier last?"

"Long enough," said Vaskarath, "if they start soon."

"That's not an answer."

"It is the only answer that has ever been true." The great head lowered until one ancient eye was level with them, close enough that Sera could see herself reflected in it, small and warped and unable to look away. "I will tell you what I told the last order of knights who climbed down here, ninety years ago, asking me to burn their enemies. I told them: I do not sell hope. I lend it. And my interest is terrible."

*

They camped that night in the gallery of names, because the mountain's warmth held even there and neither of them could have found the way down in full dark anyway. Sera built a small fire out of ash-lichen and driftwood from the mule's packs, mostly for the light of it, and sat watching the carved walls instead of sleeping.

*Tens of thousands of names.* She traced one with a fingertip — *she chose the burning* — and thought about Dain, her oath-brother, who had stood beside her in the coronation hall nine years ago and repeated the Veil-oath with her, syllable for syllable, and who was presumably still standing somewhere in Vhalen serving the thing they'd both helped enthrone. She thought about how easy it had been to believe that swearing was the same as doing. That saying the words with a straight back settled the account.

Her whole knighthood, she realized, had been a charm bought from a stall. Something that hummed convincingly and bound nothing.

"You should sleep," Jonah said, sitting down across the fire with the wrapped fragments in their lap. They were turning the old coin over and over in their fingers. "You took first watch on the road."

"So did you."

"I'm not the one who fought three smugglers in Lowcandle harbor."

"You *paid* those smugglers."

"Which is why it counts as fighting." But there was no humor in it tonight. Jonah stared into the flames, and Sera watched the firelight move across a face that looked, for once, exactly as young as it was. "Ninety thousand names on these walls, maybe more. Every one a person who carried fire and burned. And tomorrow — today, now — I'm supposed to decide whether to add mine." They laughed, short and awful. "Twelve years I counted down a debt thinking it was money. Silver to the temple, day by day, and freedom at the end of it. I used to dream about the last payment. I'd buy a shop. A real shop, with a license." Their thumb stilled on the coin. "And it turns out the debt was this. It was always this. The temple wasn't holding my money, Sera. It was holding my *name* until I was ready, and nobody ever asks the collateral if it's ready."

"Do you want my counsel?" Sera said. "As a disgraced knight?"

"As the only other person awake."

"Don't decide tonight." She fed the fire another stick. "Decide tomorrow, in front of him, when the price is standing right there breathing on you. Decisions made at midnight always feel noble. Midnight lies."

Jonah huffed — almost another smile, but not quite — and was quiet a while. When they spoke again their voice had dropped, gone careful, the way voices go near wounds.

"When he said carriers burn. Did you see it? In his face?"

"Yes."

"He's watched every single one of them go. Ninety thousand times, he's stood where we stood and named the price, and waited to see if they'd flinch." Jonah looked up at the dark ceiling of the gallery, at the names they couldn't read in the firelight but could feel, the way you feel weather coming. "And he keeps hoping they won't flinch. Even now. Even bitter as he is. That's the worst part, isn't it. He *wants* someone to say yes."

"That's not the worst part," Sera said.

"No?"

"The worst part is that I understand him." She turned her hands to the fire, palms open, and looked at the scars across her knuckles from the palace gates, from the audience chamber door she'd broken getting out, from every small violence of her disgrace. "I spent nine years wanting someone to come and unmake the oath I regretted. Just — arrive, and fix it, so I wouldn't have to be the one who paid to undo it. And now here's the dragon offering exactly that. An unmaking. A way back. And the toll is everything." Her laugh came out smoke-thin. "Midnight lies, Jonah. But sometimes it tells the truth too. The truth is I'm not afraid of burning. I'm afraid of being asked."

Jonah didn't answer that. They sat together while the fire ate down to coals, and above them the names kept their long vigil, and far below them in the dark the last heart in the Cinderfells pulsed on — small, stubborn, waiting.

*

Dawn, if dawn could be said to exist underground, came as a faint greyening of the corridor air. They went down again to the hollow, and Vaskarath's eye was already open, already watching, as though he had never slept at all — as perhaps he hadn't, in four hundred years.

"Well?" The word rolled through the hollow like distant thunder taking attendance.

Jonah stepped forward, and Sera saw them square their shoulders the way the debtors did at her father's counting-house, back when she was a girl — the way people stand when they've decided to hear the number.

"Before I answer," Jonah said, "tell me one thing. The flame. When it burns a carrier — does it take them, or do they give it?"

The silence that followed was long enough that Sera's heart began to count.

"Four hundred years," Vaskarath said at last, slowly, "and no one has ever asked me that question." The vast eye narrowed — not in anger. In something older and stranger, something that in a human face Sera might have called *wonder*. "Every knight demanded to know how long they'd last. Every priest asked whether the death would count as martyrdom. You ask who *holds the choice*." A plume of breath, hot and shaking, stirred their hair. "Little debtor. Come closer. There is something I have not shown a living soul since the Sundering — and I think, before you decide, you had better see what the flame does when it is *given* rather than taken."

The scales over the dragon's chest parted — painfully, Sera thought, like old scar tissue remembering how to open — and the candle-flame rose up out of the hollow of his heart into the air between them, tiny and impossible, casting its unnamed color across all three of their faces.

And in its light, Sera saw the shape of the thing that would change everything: the flame was not alone.
