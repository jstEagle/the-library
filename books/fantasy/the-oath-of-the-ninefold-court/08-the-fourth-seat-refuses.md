## 8. The Fourth Seat Refuses

The University of Corveth rose out of the morning mist like a spine of pale stone, its towers stacked one upon another in the manner of a man standing on his own shoulders to see further. Jonah had never seen it before. Twelve years in the Scriptorium of Saint Verre had taught them the architecture of cloisters and bell-towers, of walls built to keep the world politely outside. This was different. The university had been built to look *down*.

Sera walked ahead with her hood up and her hand resting on the pommel of a sword she was no longer permitted to carry openly. A disgraced ward-knight in a city that still believed its wards held — though the stars had gone dark a fortnight past, and everyone had felt the wards fall like glass breaking through stone — drew looks. She ignored them with the practiced ease of someone who had spent years being looked at and found wanting.

"The Fourth Seat is Magister Aldous Ondrey," she said, without turning. "Chair of Covenant Law. He held the seat for thirty years before the Court went quiet. He'll have heard we're coming."

"How?"

"Because he's the only one of the nine who chose to be found." She glanced back, and there was something complicated in her face — respect and resentment sharing the same small space. "The others hid. He built a tower and put a lamp in the window."

They climbed. The stair to Ondrey's study was worn into shallow bowls by generations of student feet, and the walls were lined with the names of graduates carved so finely they seemed more like whispers than inscriptions. Jonah read them as they climbed because reading was what their hands did when their mind was afraid. *Maren Voss. Tey of the Ford. Ilvane, S.* They stopped. Sera's own name, carved twenty years ago, the stone around it darkened where countless thumbs had touched it in passing. She didn't touch it now. She walked past it the way you walk past a grave you're not ready to visit.

Jonah said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't be a wound dressed as comfort.

* * *

Magister Ondrey received them in a round room at the top of the eastern tower, a room that was three-quarters window and one-quarter books, as if the architect had understood exactly what kind of man would live here. He was old in the way of well-kept things: silver at the temples, a back still straight from habit rather than vanity, hands folded on a desk that bore a single sheet of paper covered in a tight, beautiful hand. He wore no ring. Jonah noticed this immediately, because they had spent two weeks learning to notice it — every oath-keeper they'd found carried some token of the Ninefold Court, and Ondrey's hands were conspicuously, deliberately bare.

"You're the debtor," he said. His voice was dry and pleasant, the voice of a man who had spent decades explaining things to people slower than himself. "Kavanagh. Ninth Seat. I felt the covenant stir when the coin changed hands in Saint Verre's market — a little tug, like a fish testing a line. I wondered how long it would take you to climb my stairs."

"You knew about me," Jonah said. "Before the temple fell."

"I knew there *was* a you. The Ninth's heir was always going to surface eventually; blood-debts are patient that way." He gestured to chairs. Neither of them sat. "You've come to ask me to take up my seat again. To help you relight the dragon, reforge the Court, speak the vow. I can see the whole speech assembled behind your eyes like scaffolding. Shall I save us both an hour?"

"Yes," said Sera.

"No," said Jonah, and then, because honesty had become a discipline they could no longer afford to skip: "I mean — yes, that's why we came. But I'd rather hear what you have to say first. Everyone else has told me what I am. You're the first person I've met who might tell me what I'm asking."

Something moved behind Ondrey's eyes — not warmth, exactly. Recognition, perhaps. He unfolded his hands.

"Then sit, and I will tell you why the answer is no."

* * *

He told it plainly, which Jonah came to understand was the cruelest way he knew how to tell anything.

Forty years ago, Ondrey had been a young advocate in the Court's circuit courts, the Fourth Seat's junior, arguing land-rights for a river village called Brassen's Hollow. The case was simple by the standards of law: a downstream lord had diverted the water, and the village's fields were dying. The Court's justice was famous for its fairness. Ondrey won. The writ came down sealed in nine rings, ordering the lord to restore the flow.

"And he did," Ondrey said. "For eleven days. Then he paid a man to burn the village granary and blame the villagers themselves, and under the old statutes, a community that destroyed its own means of livelihood forfeited its claims. The Court reviewed the case. I stood before the Nine and argued until my voice gave out. And the Court ruled against the village — not because the evidence failed, but because the *form* was satisfied. The lord had followed the letter of a law written three hundred years before either of them was born. Justice, as the Court understood it, was a machine. You fed it correctly prepared grievances, and it dispensed correctly measured outcomes. It did not care that children starved. Caring was not among its functions."

He turned the single sheet of paper on his desk so they could see it. It was a list — names, dates, small sums. A ledger.

"After the ruling, I went to Brassen's Hollow myself. Out of guilt, if you want the word. I expected to find rage. Instead I found arithmetic. Families dividing what remained. An old woman paying a charm-seller her last coin for a ward against bad luck, because the law had failed her and charms were all that was left." His eyes came up and rested on Jonah with terrible gentleness. "I kept her ledger. I have kept ledgers ever since — every case the Court's form defeated, every person who bought a scrap of magic because the great machine of justice had no slot for their grief. Forty years of names. Do you know what I learned, Kavanagh?"

Jonah's throat was very tight. "That the charms worked."

"That the charms *were the work*." For the first time, Ondrey's voice lost its polish. "Not the Court. Not the writs, not the seals, not the nine interlocked rings. A grandmother in a burned village buying a knotted thread to keep fever from her grandson's bed — that was the covenant doing what it was actually for. Not thrones. Not seats. Small mercies, traded hand to hand between people who owed each other nothing but attention. And when the Court sat in its high hall dispensing justice by formula, it was strangling that — slowly, respectfully, with excellent calligraphy."

He stood and went to the window. Below, the university's courtyards were full of students crossing in the weak gray light, and beyond the walls the city of Corveth lay under its starless sky like a patient waiting for a diagnosis.

"So when the Court began to fail — when the Regent started drinking the dragon-fire and the rings went dark one by one — I did not mourn. I let my seat go quiet and I stayed here, teaching law to children, most of whom will grow up to serve machines just like the one that broke me. And now you arrive, heir of the Ninth, flame-bearer, and you ask me to help rebuild it." He turned back. "No."

* * *

"We don't want to rebuild it the old way," Jonah said. The words came out faster than they meant them to. "The Regent unmade the law itself — the wards are down, the borders are open, the Hollow are conscripting whole villages. Whatever the Court's failures were, what's coming is worse. People are dying *without* law now, not because of it."

"Yes," Ondrey agreed pleasantly. "And your solution is to restore the thing that failed them, with yourself on the ninth chair, and hope it fails more gently this time."

"It isn't—"

"Isn't it? Tell me what happens if you succeed. Speak the vow, relight the flame, the Court stands again. Who sits the other eight seats? Sworn keepers, bound by blood and covenant, answerable to an oath they didn't choose — an oath older than they are, written by dead hands, enforced by a fire that burns anyone who breaks it. You've met Vaskarath. You know what the price of the flame is." He let that sit. "You are proposing to chain nine people to a duty they inherited, for the good of millions who will never know their names. That is precisely the arrangement that produced the Hollow Regent. A king crowned into hunger, wearing a duty he never chose until the duty ate the man. And you would build it again, but smaller, and call it salvation."

"The alternative is the Regent," Sera said flatly.

"The alternative," Ondrey said, "is whatever you build *after* you stop him. Which is the actual question, and you haven't asked it yet, because you're still thinking in thrones."

Jonah felt heat rise in their chest — frustration, yes, but underneath it something worse, something that had been gathering since the High Cantor had opened the hidden ledger beneath Saint Verre and shown Jonah twelve years of their life laid out in another man's handwriting. "You talk about cages," they said. "Imposed oaths, inherited duties, chains. Fine. But you're sitting in a tower while villages burn. Your refusal is also a choice, Magister, and it has a body count too. You don't get to be clean by staying out of it."

"No," Ondrey said simply. "I don't. That is quite true, and I have made my peace with it, or something near enough to peace to sleep at night. But notice what you just did, Kavanagh." He came closer, and up close his eyes were the pale gray of paper left too long in the sun. "You told me my refusal kills people, therefore I must accept the seat. By that same logic, your blood-debt obligated you to serve the temple for twelve years, therefore you served. The logic of the cage. *Someone must, therefore you must.* It is the oldest argument in covenant law and it has justified every throne, every levy, every forced vow since the first ring was cast. I refuse it. I refuse it even now, with the sky dark and the Hollow marching. Perhaps especially now — because now is when it sounds most reasonable."

"You'd let the realm fall over a principle."

"I would let the realm *choose*," Ondrey said, "over a principle. Yes. That is exactly what I would do, and I will tell you the last thing I learned in the Court, the thing that sent me up this tower and closed the door." He paused, and for a moment the dry pleasantness fell away entirely, and Jonah saw the young advocate who had stood before the Nine until his voice gave out. "The day the Court ruled against Brassen's Hollow, I swore the renewal of my seat's oath with my whole heart, because I believed in it, and the oath took, and I was bound — truly bound, fire-bound, unable to act against the Court's judgment no matter what my conscience screamed. When the ruling came down wrong, I *knew* it was wrong, and I could do nothing. My own sworn word held my arms while children starved. That is what an imposed oath is, heir of the Ninth. Not a bond. A gag. And you are carrying fragments of that gag across the country in your pocket, planning to weld it back together around nine throats including your own."

Silence filled the round room. Below, a bell rang the third hour, and the sound came up through the floor like something rising through water.

Jonah thought of the debt-ledger under Saint Verre. Twelve years of copied wards, sold charms, counted coins — all of it assigned, all of it written in a book they had never signed. They had told themselves they chose that life. Even after learning the truth, some stubborn part of them had clung to it: *I stayed because I chose to stay.* Standing in Ondrey's tower, hearing their own justification spoken back in an old man's dry voice, they felt the floor of that story give way.

Because hadn't they done the same thing at the market in Lowcandle? Taken the fragments from frightened people who owed them nothing? Asked the widow Marette to surrender her daughter's fever-charm because the covenant needed it? *Someone must,* they had said, gently, kindly, with the best intentions in the world, *and you hold the piece.*

Oaths imposed are cages. And Jonah had been both the bird and the keeper of the cage, sometimes in the same hour.

"I'm not him," Jonah said quietly. It wasn't clear even to them whether they meant the Regent, or Ondrey, or themselves-from-yesterday. "I don't want to bind anyone."

"Don't you?" Ondrey asked, not cruelly. "Sit with that question on the road. Sit with it properly, the way you'd sit with a difficult text — not looking for the passage that agrees with you." He returned to his desk and, from a drawer, took a slim volume wrapped in oilcloth, which he held out. "The Third Seat fragment. It came to me fifteen years ago, left in a bequest by a dying charm-seller who didn't know what she had. I've kept it because destroying it felt like a decision I had no right to make. I still feel that way. So I'm giving it to you — not to the Court, not to the covenant. To you, Jonah Kavanagh, person, who owes me nothing and is owed nothing by me. Consider the difference. It's the entire lesson."

Jonah took the bundle. It was lighter than they expected, warm as a held hand. Through the oilcloth they could feel the faint hum of the fragment answering the others — nine rings wanting to be whole again, hungry for restoration, for the old shape, for the cage rebuilt and gilded.

"Will you come with us?" Sera asked. "If not for the Court — for the people?"

Ondrey looked out at his students crossing the courtyard, at the city under its blind sky, and for a long moment Jonah thought the answer might change.

"No," he said again, softer. "But I'll tell you what I told the last person who climbed these stairs asking for help, forty years ago, in a burned village: I keep ledgers. Send me names. When this is over — whatever 'over' means — send me the names of everyone who chose, freely, to stand. Every settlement that swears itself, every stranger who takes up a fragment without being ordered to. I want to know whether it can be done. Whether belonging can be built without binding." His mouth curved, dry as dust. "Prove me wrong, Kavanagh. It's the only gift I have left to want."

They went down the worn stairs, past Sera's carved name, out into the gray morning. At the tower's base Jonah stopped and unwrapped the oilcloth just enough to see the Third Seat fragment: a ring of old gold, one of nine interlocked, dark with long disuse.

It hummed against the others in their pack like a voice calling across a distance. *Come home. Be whole. Be bound.*

Jonah closed their hand around it and did not answer.

Behind them, far to the south, the horizon had begun to glow — not with dawn, which had come hours ago, but with a dull red stain spreading along the edge of the world like wax weeping from a seal. Sera saw it too. Her hand found her sword-hilt.

"That's Cinderfell burning," she said. "Or the road to it. Either way — we're out of time to sit with questions."

They ran for the stables, the fragment humming its hungry song, and behind them in the round room at the top of the tower, a lamp burned steadily in the window of the only man in Corveth who had refused them — watching, keeping his ledger, waiting to be proven wrong.
