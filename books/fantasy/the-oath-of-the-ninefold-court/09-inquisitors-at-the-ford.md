## 9. Inquisitors at the Ford

The Greywater came down out of the hills the color of old pewter, and it did not so much flow as slide, slow and muscular, carrying silt from a hundred burned farms toward a sea that no longer had stars over it. Sera Ilvane reined in at the top of the bank and looked at the ford with the particular attention of a woman who had once been paid, in oaths and silver, to notice things like fords.

It was a good ford. That was the problem.

The water spread wide there, shallow over a spine of pale stone, the kind of crossing an army would use — or a column of riders moving fast under a black banner. There was no bridge within two days' ride. There was no other way across. And on the far bank, half-hidden by willows going gray and bare, she could see the mud churned dark where hooves had passed. Many hooves. Recently.

"They know we're coming," she said.

Jonah pulled up beside her, the mule they'd bought in Corveth blowing hard beneath them. The scribe looked hollow-eyed; three days of hard riding would do that, and so would learning your whole life was a lie assigned to you by men who kept ledgers of souls. But Jonah's hands were steady when they opened the oilcloth bundle across their knees, just enough to check the fragments again. Nine pieces of carved bone-and-iron now — eight gathered from Lowcandle's desperate, one from Ondrey's tower left behind unclaimed — and each one hummed against the others like struck tuning forks remembering a chord.

"The charm-seller's market," Sera said. "Half of Lowcandle knows you sold them those things. Half of *them* would sell you to save their own skins."

"I don't believe that," Jonah said.

"You believed wards were wards for twelve years." She hadn't meant it as cruelly as it landed. She watched it land anyway. "I'm sorry. I'm saying we assume pursuit, because assuming anything else is how people die at fords."

Jonah looked at the water a long moment. "Then what?"

"We cross separately. I go first, fast and loud, straight down the middle. You go downstream, quiet, through the reeds, with the fragments and the mule. If anyone's waiting, they'll look at me." She said it the way she'd once said *hold this line* to green soldiers outside Harrowgate — flat, certain, already grieving it a little. "You can't fight. Don't pretend. But you can walk, and walk, and keep walking until Vaskarath's mountain is between us and them. That's the whole of the strategy. It isn't clever. It doesn't have to be."

"Sera—"

"Don't." She turned her horse toward the water. "I broke an oath once because I thought surviving was the same as keeping faith. I let the Regent's men walk past me into the palace cellars because my oath-brother told me it was procedure, and I wanted so badly to believe him that I didn't read the seals on his orders. Everything since has been interest paid on that debt. Let me pay some of it here, where it buys something."

She kicked the horse forward before Jonah could answer, because if she waited for an answer she might not do it at all.

The water took her to mid-thigh, cold enough to make her gasp, then shallower as the stone spine rose beneath the current. The horse found its footing. On the far bank the willows hung still. Too still. Birds should have been arguing in them. She counted the shadows between trunks the way she'd been taught — nine years a ward-knight, four years a fugitive, and the counting never stopped — and found the place where shadow had weight, and edges, and buckles.

*There.*

"Inquisitors," she called back over her shoulder, loud and clear, giving Jonah the warning with her voice because it might be the last thing she gave anyone. "Ride!"

They came out of the willows as she said it — six of them first, then more behind, gray-cloaked, faceless behind smooth steel masks with no eyeholes she could find, only a seam of dull light where eyes should be. The Questioning House colors. The Regent's own. Their horses were wrong too, too quiet, hooves making no sound at all in the mud, as though sound itself had been confiscated from them.

Sera drew her sword and put herself squarely in the middle of the ford, where the stone spine narrowed and the water ran fastest, and did the arithmetic every soldier does and hates: how long can one person be a wall.

Long enough, she decided. Long enough.

"By oath and office," she shouted, because the old forms were all she had left and she meant to spend them, "none of you pass this water while breath is in me."

One of the masked riders raised a hand, almost courteously, and the six split — three for her, three swinging downstream after Jonah. So much for being looked at. She wheeled the horse, splashing, cursing, and got between the flanking riders and the reed-banks just as the first of them reached her. Steel met steel with a shock that went up both arms to the shoulder. The masked thing on the other end did not grunt, did not strain, fought like something reading instructions about fighting. She killed its horse instead — a mercy, maybe, for a creature made to carry such a rider — and as the animal fell she cut the rider from the saddle and felt the blade go in with no resistance at all, like cutting water, like cutting a coat hung on nothing.

Where the blood should have been, ash puffed out and drifted on the wind.

*Hollow,* she thought. Not men. Not anymore. The Regent had been emptying his soldiers the way he emptied reflections, pouring the person out and leaving the shape.

Downstream, a scream — the mule, not Jonah — and then Jonah's voice cracking across the water: *"Sera!"*

"HOLD THE REEDS AND RUN!" she roared, and turned back into the press of blades, and stopped thinking in words for a while.

* * *

Fighting is memory. That was the secret no drillmaster ever admitted. Every parry is a lesson someone bled to teach you; every footwork pattern is a dead sergeant's voice still counting. Sera fought the ford with eleven years of other people's sacrifices stacked in her arms, and for a time it was enough. Two more of the hollow ones came apart into drifting ash under her blade. A third caught her across the thigh with a hooked falchion and she felt the wound go cold immediately, colder than the river, a cold that *climbed*, that started looking for the warm places in her—

She remembered the weeping seal. Black wax, weeping. What touches the Regent's servants stays touched.

So she did the unforgivable practical thing: she cut off her own awareness of it. Tore the sleeve, bound the leg above the wound with her sword-belt cinched cruelly tight, and kept moving, because downstream Jonah was still visible, a small figure dragging a stumbling mule through the reeds with a bundle clutched to their chest like a child, and three riders were closing, unhurried, inevitable.

"No," she said, out loud, to no one. "No, you don't."

She charged. It was not skill by then; it was fury wearing skill's clothing. She took the first rider off his horse with a vaulting cut she'd have been flogged for attempting at the academy. The second turned to face her and she saw, close up, the mask's blank steel, and reflected in it her own face — and realized with a lurch that the mask showed her reflection but the visor behind it showed *nothing*, a darkness with no Sera in it at all, and something in her gut understood finally, fully, what was hunting them, and she screamed and hit it until it came apart into ash that the river carried away in gray ribbons.

The third rider did not attack.

He sat his horse at the edge of the shallows, and he lifted off his mask, and he had a human face. Younger than she remembered and older too, a scar through one brow she'd watched heal years ago, brown eyes she had trusted with her life and her oath and, once, with the truth about the cellar doors.

"Dain," she said. The name fell out of her like a dropped stone.

"Sera." His voice was careful. Flat the way voices get when the man behind them is holding himself together with procedure. "Serah Ilvane. Traitor to the Crown, deserter of the Ward-Knights, bearer of stolen regalia." He recited it like a warrant. Maybe he needed it to be a warrant. "In the name of the Regent, surrender."

"Look at your men," she said. Her leg was screaming; the cold had reached her knee. "Look at what he makes of them. Ash in uniform, Dain. You've seen it. Tell me you haven't seen it."

"I've seen order," he said. "I've seen a realm that isn't burning, which is more than your friends in Lowcandle can claim, more than whatever cult of broken seats you've joined—"

"I swore the same oath you did." She stepped toward him through the shallows, sword point dropping, because she could not hold it up and say this at the same time. "We stood in the Rotunda with wet ink on our thumbs and swore by the Nine Rings to guard the law against all hunger, *all* hunger, Dain, and then we walked the thing that wears the king right past our own post because the orders had the right seal, and I have spent four years knowing it was my fault—"

"It wasn't the seal that failed," Dain said, and his voice cracked on it, the flatness splitting down the middle. "It was us. I know. I *know*, Sera, I've had four years of knowing, and the Regent — the Regent says a debt acknowledged is a debt discharged. He says if we serve faithfully, the realm survives, and what we did gets *carried*, not punished—"

"That's what hunger always says." She was close enough now to touch his stirrup. Close enough to see that under the gray cloak he wore the old oath-knot at his throat, the one from the Rotunda, frayed but not removed. He'd never taken it off either. "Dain. Brother. Give me your hand and come away from him, or give me your sword and finish it, but don't stand in this water pretending you're on anyone's side but your own guilt. Guilt isn't a side. It's just a cage with better furniture."

For a moment — she would remember this moment for the rest of her life, however much of it remained — his hand actually moved. Twitched toward hers across the cold air above the running water.

Then the horn sounded from the willows. Deep, patient, wrong — a note that the river itself seemed to lean away from. More riders, ranks of them, filing silently out onto the bank. And Dain's face closed like a door.

"Downstream," he said quietly, urgently, the warrant-gone, the brother flickering through. "Sera. They'll take you to the Questioning House. Whatever happens — don't tell them where the scribe goes. They can't read the fragments without a living oath-sworn tongue, do you understand? Your silence is worth more than your—"

The horn again, and this time the masks began to move, all of them, unhurried as tide.

Sera looked at Dain one last time. Then she threw her sword into the deep channel — she would not hand it to them, and she could not run on the ruined leg — and stood up straight in the middle of the Greywater with the cold climbing her thigh and her hands empty and open.

"I surrender," she said formally, loudly, in the words of the old law, the real law, the one the crown-thing was eating bite by bite. "By the Nine Rings, witness me."

And somewhere upstream, impossibly far and impossibly near, she felt — or imagined, or hoped — a faint answering hum, like eight small bones singing in a scribe's arms, already growing distant, already safe, already gone.

* * *

They bound her wrists with cord that drank the light and put her on a horse that smelled of nothing, and rode north through the willow country while the day died without any stars coming out to bury it.

She counted, because counting was all she had. Forty-one riders. Three hours to the high road. Somewhere south of them, a scribe with borrowed courage and a mule and eight pieces of a broken covenant, getting further away with every mile, and her silence standing between him and the masks like the wall she'd promised to be.

At dusk they halted in a hollow, and Dain came and crouched beside her, and for a moment neither spoke.

"You should have ridden on," he said finally. "When I hesitated. Why didn't you run?"

"Because you'd have had to choose," she said. "Right there, in front of them. And I've seen what choosing costs him—" she nodded at the nearest mask, sitting its silent horse, watching with its seam of light, "—and I couldn't make you pay it in front of witnesses. That's not mercy, Dain. Don't mistake it. It's just that I've broken enough oaths for both of us already."

His jaw worked. Then, quickly, glancing once at the masked rider, he pressed something into her bound hands — folded small, hidden in the shelter of her sleeves. Wax. A broken piece of black wax, hardened around a scrap of parchment, torn from a royal dispatch.

"If they question you," he murmured, "they'll ask about the scribe. Refuse. Refuse everything. The longer you refuse, the more of their strength they waste on you, and the further he gets." His voice dropped to almost nothing. "And Sera — the fragment you carry, the seal — the Regent fears it. Not the Court. *It.* When they brought news of you, the whole palace went quiet, like a house where something is listening. Whatever you do at the Questioning House — make them afraid of you. Fear makes them careless."

He rose to go. She caught his sleeve.

"Dain. When this ends — whichever way — the oath we broke in the cellars. We settle it. One way or another. Swear it."

He looked down at her for a long moment, and the scar through his brow caught the last gray light, and he said, "Sworn," in the old form, the Rotunda form, and she felt the word land in her chest like a second heartbeat.

Then the masks closed around her, the column moved north into the starless dark, and Sera Ilvane sat her borrowed horse with her hands bound and a dead man's seal hidden in her sleeves, riding toward the Questioning House — where, she had begun to suspect, the Regent's greatest fear and her only hope were the very same thing:

A scribe who owed debts, walking east with a song of nine rings, and a tongue that would not tell.
