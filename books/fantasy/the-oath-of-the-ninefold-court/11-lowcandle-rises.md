## 11. Lowcandle Rises

The proclamation went up at dawn, nailed to the customs post at the mouth of the warren in letters tall enough to read from across the canal: *By decree of the Regent's Grace, all practitioners of unlicensed arts are declared hollow-touched. Shelter them and be judged with them. Bring them forth and be rewarded.*

Jonah read it three times while the ink was still tacky enough to smudge.

*Hollow-touched.* A word that had meant nothing a month ago and now meant a pyre. The paper bore the royal seal at its foot — and Jonah's stomach turned over, because even printed, even flattened into black wax on cheap vellum, they could see what Sera had shown them: the way the seal seemed to weep if you looked too long, dark threads bleeding down through the fibers like roots.

"They'll come tonight," said Wren Marsh, who kept the toll-gate and had kept it for forty years and missed nothing that passed through it, including, for twelve of those years, a scribe with a satchel full of illegal charms. She spat over the edge of her walkway into the grey water. "They always come at night. Soldiers like the dark. It does their thinking for them."

Jonah looked up at the sky — still starless, still wrong, a ceiling of blank iron over Lowcandle's crooked rooftops — and felt the fragments shift against their ribs where they were sewn into the lining of a coat that had never been warm before and now felt heavy as armor.

"We have until tonight," Jonah said. "Maybe less."

"Then I'd best wake the warren," Wren said, and pulled the rope that rang the low bell, the one that wasn't supposed to mean anything except that fish had come in cheap.

In Lowcandle, everyone knew what the low bell meant.

* * *

They came out of their doorways and stairwells and cellar rooms the way water comes out of stone — slowly, then everywhere at once. Jonah stood on the steps of the old net-menders' hall and watched them gather, and something in their chest ached so badly they had to press a hand flat against it to keep it from showing.

Because Jonah knew every face.

There was Maren Ostrey, the washerwoman whose hands cracked and bled every winter until she'd bought a salve-charm from a nervous young scribe and wept over it in the alley because it was the first thing in years that hadn't hurt. There was Tam Broadstairs, who'd paid for his daughter's fever-charm with a coin he'd sworn was his last and turned out not to be, because he always found one more, because fathers did. There was the girl called Sixpence who had no other name and a birthmark like spilled ink across half her face, and the ferryman Dole who talked to drowned men, and the whole row of chimney-climbers who wore their luck-knots under their collars and would rather hang than give them up.

The poor. The desperate. The marked. Jonah had sold to them all, night after night, twelve years of small transgressions counted down against a debt they hadn't understood — and now here they stood in the cold morning light, holding the pieces of a broken covenant in their cracked palms, looking at Jonah as though Jonah might know what to do.

Which was the worst part. Because Jonah didn't. Jonah had spent a lifetime hiding — behind ledgers, behind counters, behind the careful gray anonymity of a temple scribe — and every instinct they owned was screaming *run, scatter, take the fragments and go, save yourself, save the oath, let these people fend for themselves the way they always have.*

But Sera wasn't there to say it first, so Jonah said it themselves.

"The soldiers will come tonight," Jonah told them. "They have lists. Some of those lists have your names on them, because I wrote some of them." A ripple went through the crowd — not anger, or not only anger; there was something worse underneath it, something like hurt. "I sold charms to this whole warren for twelve years. I took your coins and I never told you what you were carrying. You deserve the truth now, late as it is: what you bought from me isn't trinket-work. It's older than the temple. It's older than the crown. It's pieces of an oath that used to hold this realm together, and right now you are the only people in Vhalen carrying them."

Silence. Somewhere a gull screamed at the empty stars.

"And?" said Maren Ostrey. Her voice was dry as lye. "What do we do?"

"I should tell you to run," Jonah said. "Hide them, hide yourselves. That's what I'd have done a month ago. That's what I've done my whole life." They made themselves look at the faces, each one, the way you look at a wound you've caused. "But running hasn't saved anyone yet. The Regent doesn't want the charms. The Regent wants what happens when people stop believing they belong to anything but fear. So I'm asking instead: stand. Not because you owe me. Because you owe each other. Because whatever else breaks tonight, it doesn't get to break *this*."

Tam Broadstairs cleared his throat. "The fever-charm," he said. "The one for my Lissa. It's hers. I bought it for her."

"It is hers," Jonah agreed carefully, "and it's also—"

"It's hers," Tam repeated, "and she sleeps better with it under her pillow, and if a man in a red coat thinks he's taking it off my daughter, he can climb through me to reach it." He crossed his arms, and he was not a large man, but he had the look of a door that had been barred.

Sixpence laughed — a short, startled sound, quickly swallowed. Then Maren laughed too, harsher, and then it moved through the crowd like fire through dry grass, and Jonah stood on the steps of the net-menders' hall and understood, with a clarity that nearly buckled their knees, that they had just become someone other people followed.

It was the most frightening thing that had ever happened to them. It was also, quietly, the first thing in twelve years that had ever felt chosen.

* * *

They worked all day.

Wren organized the approaches — the canal gates, the stair-alleys, the three ways into the warren that weren't ways at all unless you'd grown up falling down them. The chimney-climbers mapped sightlines from the rooftops and strung signal-lamps between the towers, colored glass scavenged from a shuttered pleasure-house, green for watch and red for trouble. Dole ferried the oldest and smallest among them across the water to the reed-flats, grumbling the entire time about how he'd never signed on to be a grandmother, and came back having refused to leave himself.

And Jonah sat at a trestle table in the net-hall with the fragments laid out before them, teaching.

That was the strangest part. For twelve years Jonah had sold charms finished, wrapped, anonymous — take this, pay me, don't ask questions. Now there was no time for finishing and no coin changing hands, so Jonah taught raw: how to hold a fragment without flinching when it hummed, how to speak toward it instead of at it, how to feed it a true sentence — *my name is, my hands are mine, I am not alone* — and feel it brighten like an ember breathed upon.

"Don't try to make it work," Jonah told them, again and again. "It already works. It's been waiting. All it needs is for you to mean something while you're holding it."

Maren's salve-charm glowed steady gold in her palm within the hour, and she stared at it as though it had betrayed her by being beautiful. Sixpence's luck-knot turned in her fingers and threw light like a spinning coin. Even old Wren, who claimed she'd never bought so much as a blessing in her life, produced from somewhere inside her coat a worn copper ring stamped with a single interlocking circle — "Toll-token, they said. Paid extra for it. Always wondered why" — and when she held it up it sang, faint and clear, in harmony with everything else on the table.

Nine rings. Eight seats scattered. And here in a smugglers' warren, dozens of small bright shards answering one another across a plank table, weaving themselves into something that had no name yet but had weight, had *pull*, had the gravity of a thing becoming true.

Sera arrived at dusk, mud to the knees, with a cut on her brow she wouldn't explain and news she would: the Regent's column had left Vhalen at midday. Two hundred soldiers. Inquisitors in gray. And riding at their head, she said quietly, a herald wearing white gloves, which meant the Regent itself considered Lowcandle worth a personal instrument.

"Two hundred," said Wren. "Against how many? Forty? Fifty?"

"Fifty-one," said Tam, counting heads with a carpenter's habit.

"Fifty-one," Jonah echoed, and heard how it sounded, and watched everyone else hear it too. Nobody left. That was the miracle nobody remarked on. The reed-flats had taken the children and the frail, and every remaining soul stayed standing in the net-hall's lamplight, and when Jonah looked around at them they saw not a militia — there was nothing martial in these bent backs and cracked knuckles — but a congregation. A parish with no god except one another.

"Then we don't fight them like fifty-one," Jonah said, and the plan came out of them almost fully formed, as though the fragments themselves were dictating. "We fight them like a warren. Like Lowcandle. Nobody meets two hundred in a square. We meet them in the places they don't fit — the stair-alleys, the walkways, the dark under the eaves. And when they're lost in the dark—" they touched the coat where the fragments slept "—we show them the light."

* * *

The soldiers came at the second hour past midnight, exactly as Wren had promised, boots and lanterns pouring through the customs gate in disciplined silence. The herald walked at their center in white gloves, and where the herald passed, the lanterns burned lower, and men who had swaggered going in began walking closer together.

Lowcandle let them in.

That was the trick of it — the hardest thing Jonah had ever asked of frightened people, harder than any charm: to stay hidden, stay silent, stay *home*, while the enemy filled your streets. The warren held its breath. Chimney-climbers lay flat on cold slates. Wren doused the gate-lamp and sat in the dark of her toll-house with her copper ring closed in her fist like a prayer.

The column reached the net-square, empty and lamplit and innocent, and the herald raised one gloved hand, and that was when the lamps in the tower windows changed from green to red.

Doors opened. Not many. Just enough. Maren Ostrey stepped out of her wash-house with her salve-charm blazing in her bleeding hand like a captured sunrise. Tam Broadstairs stood in his doorway with his daughter's fever-charm glowing soft blue against his chest. Dole lit his ferry-lamp with a shard that burned the color of deep water. Sixpence spun her luck-knot overhead and it threw rings of light across the cobbles, nine rings, ten, twenty, overlapping, interlocking, and every fragment in the warren answered — Jonah felt it through the coat lining like a chorus tuning, like a vow remembering itself — and the square filled with ordinary people holding ordinary light that was not ordinary at all.

"Hold!" the herald cried, and its voice was wrong, layered, a voice with more throats than one. "In the Regent's name, surrender the hollow-touched!"

"In whose name?" Jonah called back, stepping into the square at last, into the open, done with hiding. Their voice shook and they let it. "Say the name plainly. Whose?"

And the herald faltered — actually faltered, white gloves twitching — because it was a creature built on borrowed names, and the square around it blazed with fifty-one small lights that belonged entirely, freely, stubbornly, to the people holding them.

A soldier broke rank. Then three. They were conscripts, Sera had warned — hollow-eyed, their reflections dimmed — but dimmed reflections still squinted, and light that remembered its oaths was a hard thing to march into. The line wavered like water.

Then the herald screamed, a sound like tearing parchment, and the lanterns along the canal guttered all at once—

—and stopped guttering, because Wren Marsh rang the low bell, and every window in Lowcandle slammed open at once, and the whole warren roared down into the square, pots and pans and voices and fury, the sound of a thousand people declaring that these fifty-one were *theirs*, and the fragments flared together into a single wall of woven light that stood between the soldiers and the warren like a drawn breath that would not be exhaled.

The column broke. Not defeated — Jonah knew the difference, watching them stream back out through the customs gate with the herald shrieking curses in its layered throat — but broken, for now, unable to swallow what it had been sent to eat.

The cheering started before the last boot faded. Jonah stood in the middle of the net-square shaking, surrounded by people laughing and weeping and gripping one another's arms, and Maren Ostrey caught their shoulder and shouted over the noise:

"They'll come back with more!"

"They will!" Jonah shouted back, and found, to their own astonishment, that they were smiling. "And next time we won't just be fifty-one!"

Because even now, even in the ringing aftermath, word was moving — runners on the roof-paths, signals flashing tower to tower — the warrens east of the canal asking what had happened, the river-settlements, the quarry camps, every place Jonah had ever sold a charm in secret, every hand that had ever carried a piece of the covenant without knowing its name.

And far to the south, Sera had said, riding hard with the news: the First Hearth. The dragon. The flame that waited for a carrier.

The wall of light sank gently back into fifty-one small glows, and the warren of Lowcandle stood in the dark under the starless sky, unbowed, belonging to itself — and Jonah looked south, toward the mountains, and felt the fragments pull like a compass needle finding north.
