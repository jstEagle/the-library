## 12. Rescue at the Questioning House

The cell had no window, but Sera Ilvane knew the hour by the bells of Vhalen's lower quarters — a cracked bronze thing that struck late and off-key, the way everything in Lowcandle ran crooked. When it tolled, she counted. Nine strikes meant the middle watch. Nine rings, nine strikes. She laughed at that once, on her first night, until laughing pulled at the bruises across her ribs and turned into something wetter she refused to name.

They had taken her boots. They had taken the weeping seal from inside her coat within an hour of the ford, and she had watched a questioner turn it over in gloved fingers with the reverence of a man handling a relic, black wax beading along its edge like sweat on a corpse. Then they took her name too, in a sense. In the Questioning House she was not Sera Ilvane, ward-knight, disgraced. She was *subject four*, held pending the Regent's pleasure.

Her hands were bound in front of her with iron-thread cord — expensive, that; the Regent's people did not waste iron-thread on commoners — and she sat against the wall because sitting was less humiliating than lying, though only slightly.

She thought about Jonah Kavanagh on the far bank of the Greywater, small and soaked and clutching the satchel of charm-fragments like it was a child. She thought about the look on their face when she'd turned back toward the inquisitors: not gratitude, not grief, just the flat stunned blankness of someone who has never been protected before and does not know where to put it.

*Good,* she told herself. *That was worth this.* She was not entirely sure she believed it.

* * *

They came for her on the fourth day.

She heard him before she saw him — the particular cadence of boots in the corridor outside, unhurried, measured, each step placed rather than fallen. She had marched beside those boots for six years. She had polished their buckles as a joke during winter quarters and gotten punched for it, laughing.

The door opened. Torchlight from the hall spilled in and cut his shadow long across the floor, and Dain Corvess stood in it exactly as she remembered him, except for the eyes.

He was still handsome in the blunt way he'd always been, jaw like a fence post, hair cropped to regulation. He wore the grey-and-silver of the Regent's questioners now instead of a knight's white, and the change of color should have been nothing — cloth was cloth — but it landed on her like a slap. The oath-brothers of the Wardens wore white so that anyone in any village could see them coming and know they came honestly. Grey was the color you wore when honesty had stopped being the point.

"Sera." Her name in his mouth was careful, the way a man handles a blade he knows has already cut him once.

"Dain." Her voice came out rougher than she intended, four days of disuse and bad water. "You've been promoted."

"I've been *assigned*." He crossed the cell and set the torch in the bracket by the door, then lowered himself onto the stool bolted to the floor opposite her — the interrogator's seat, positioned so the subject must look up. He didn't make her look up. He sat forward, elbows on knees, close enough that the pretense of procedure fell away between them like a dropped cloak. "Gods below, Sera. What happened to your face?"

"You should see the other three of them."

His mouth twitched — the ghost of the old grin — and died. He glanced at the door, then back. "I requested this assignment. You need to understand that. There were others who wanted you. Magister Veyle's people wanted you first, and what Veyle's people do—" He stopped. His knuckles whitened on his knees. "You're going to talk to me instead. That's the arrangement I bought."

"Generous."

"It's the only kindness left in this building, and I'm spending it on you, so don't—"

"Don't what?" She leaned forward as far as the cord allowed, and the iron-thread bit into her wrists, and she welcomed the pain because it steadied her. "Don't waste it? Dain, look at where we are. Look at what you're wearing. You spent your kindness the day you traded white for grey."

Something moved behind his face — hurt, quickly buried, but she saw it land, and hated herself a little for aiming there and hated the world more for making the aim true.

"They said you went over," he said quietly. "At the ford. That you fought for a charm-thief and a bag of illegal trinkets against your own oath-brothers. Brakken's dead, Sera. You put Halvard's eye out. They showed me the reports."

"The reports." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "Did the reports mention what we were holding the ford *against*? Did they mention what's wearing the king?"

The silence that followed was long and cold and full of the dripping somewhere in the walls, water finding its slow way through stone, patient as rot.

"You're tired," Dain said at last. "Four days in the dark. Confusion. It happens to everyone down here. The mind starts—"

"Dain." She made her voice gentle, which cost her more than shouting would have. "Look at my wrists."

"What?"

"My wrists. Under the cord. Do it."

He hesitated — procedure, suspicion, the trained wariness of a questioner whose subject is trying something — and then he did it, because beneath all of it he was still the boy who had checked her bandages after the Skirmish of the Weir without being asked. He unwound the iron-thread carefully, wincing at what it had done to her skin, and then his hands stopped moving entirely.

The mark was on her left wrist, low, where a pulse lived. A ring. One perfect circle, ink-dark, sunk into the flesh not like a tattoo but like a scar that had always been waiting to exist. Around its rim, faint as breath on glass, ran a thread of script older than the alphabet the temple taught children.

"That's not possible," Dain said. His voice had gone very quiet. "That's oath-script. That's *Warden* script. It hasn't been written since—"

"Since the Court fell. Since the wards failed. Since the stars went out." She flexed her fingers. The ring pulsed with her pulse. "It appeared two nights after the Night Without Stars. I didn't ask for it. I didn't earn it. It's just *there*, Dain, and I think — I think it means the oath isn't dead. I think it means the Court was never properly dissolved, only starved, and something in me is still sworn whether I like it or not."

"And you believe that makes you righteous?" He stood abruptly, retreating behind procedure like a man pulling a visor down. "A scar? Sera, I have seen a hundred fanatics with marks and visions. Half of them carved their own. The Regent's law exists because magic rots judgment—"

"*Whose* law, Dain?" She rose with him, unsteady, wrists raw, and got in front of him before he could reach the door. Close. Close enough to smell the lamp-oil on his collar and beneath it the horse and leather smell of the man she'd trusted with her life for six years. "*Who* gave the order that magic-holders are outlawed? Who sits the throne? Have you seen him? Not from the back of a hall — *seen* him. Up close. Have you looked into his eyes?"

"I serve the crown."

"The crown is a hat!" The words tore out of her louder than she meant, echoing off stone. "The man under it is gone. I stood three feet from him, Dain, in the audience chamber, and I looked into his eyes, and there was nothing human looking back. Something vast and patient and *hungry* wearing our sovereign like a glove. And every ward-knight who ever swore the old oath — every one of us who kept the borders while the temple quietly sold pieces of the covenant to farmers with blighted fields — we're the ones who let it happen. Our oaths held the door open. Mine did. Yours did."

"Stop."

"So if you want to arrest my conscience, Dain, get in line. It's already in custody."

"**Stop**." His hand shot out and gripped her shoulder, hard, and then — this was the thing she would remember afterward, the thing that would keep her from hating him even in the worst hours that came later — his grip gentled. Slid. Became, for one unguarded moment, the grip of a brother holding on. "Sera. If half of what you're saying is true, then everything I've done since the ford is—"

He couldn't finish it. She watched him try to finish it and fail, watched the sentence break apart in his mouth the way the border wards had broken, and she understood suddenly that she had one weapon left and no time to sharpen it.

"The seal," she said.

His head came up. "What?"

"They took it from me. The royal seal from the Regent's own table — the one that weeps black wax. Where is it? Evidence lock?"

"Why do you—"

"Because you need to see it. Not hear about it. See it. Touch it." She held his gaze and poured every ounce of truth she had through it, stripped of tactics, stripped of the knight's mask. "Dain. I have lied to superiors, smuggled charms, struck my oath-brothers, and I am telling you now, with whatever part of me you still believe exists: I would rather die in this cell than show you a lie about that seal. Bring it here. Five minutes. If I'm mad, you'll know in five minutes. If I'm right—"

"If you're right, I can't unknow it." His voice cracked on the last word. He stepped back, and his hand went to the door, and for a moment he simply stood there with his palm flat against the wood, head bowed, a man at a threshold far larger than a cell door.

"No," he said finally. "You'll see the magister tomorrow, per schedule. I can't—"

"Dain."

"—but I'll bring it tonight," he finished, barely audible. "After last bell. If anyone asks, I'm reviewing evidence." He opened the door, paused, and did not look back at her. "Eat what they bring you. I bribed the cook. Don't waste my bribe."

Then he was gone, and Sera sat back down against the wall, and shook, silently, for a long time.

* * *

He came after last bell, as promised. He came alone, which was either trust or doom, and he carried the seal wrapped in oilcloth like something dead.

He set it on the floor between them rather than the stool, which told her he already half believed her; you don't handle evidence like it might notice you. Then he unwrapped it.

Even in torchlight, even dulled by oilcloth and four days of dark, the seal was wrong. It was heavy gold, royal work, the Regent's sigil cut deep — and along its whole underside, where wax should sit clean and dry, the metal glistened. Black welled up from the engraving slowly, bead by bead, thick as tar, and where it touched the air it did not harden. It *moved*, slightly, the way a thing moves that is pretending very carefully to be inert.

"That's contamination," Dain said. His voice was steady. His hand was not. "Residue. Some alchemical—"

"Touch it."

"I am not going to touch—"

"With your bare hand. Like you'd touch any seal from any desk you've ever searched. Go on." She kept her voice low and almost kind. "It's royal property, Dain. It won't hurt you. It's the king's own seal. Unless it isn't."

He reached out. Stopped. Reached again — and Sera saw the exact moment the questioner's discipline lost to the Warden's instinct, six years of reading danger in a room overriding four days of refusing to read anything at all.

His fingertips brushed the weeping edge of the seal.

He did not cry out. What happened was quieter and worse: his whole body flinched as if doused in ice-water, and he snatched his hand back and stared at his own fingers, and Sera watched the color leave his face in a tide. Because anyone who had ever worked a ward, anyone who had ever stood a night watch on the border stones, knew that feeling. Cold where warmth should live. Absence where presence should be. The specific wrongness of standing next to a person and realizing nothing inside them is home.

"It looked at me," he whispered.

"Yes."

"It doesn't have eyes and it *looked* at me."

"Yes." She crouched painfully beside him, wrists raw, and spoke softly, the way you speak to a man standing on ice and hearing it begin to sing. "Now you know what I know. Now you know what you're carrying orders for. The questioners downstairs aren't questioning traitors, Dain. They're mapping the covenant. Every charm-holder, every marked wrist, every fragment of the old oath — the Regent wants them found and burned, because the fragments are the only thing left that can hurt it. And we handed them the lists. We built the machine. I swore the oath that let it in, and you've been tightening the screws."

Dain sat back against the wall. For a long time he said nothing at all. The torch guttered. The black wax on the seal gathered itself into another slow bead and slid, soundless, onto the stone.

"When I was twelve," he said at last, apropos of nothing, or everything, "my father beat me for lying about a broken plow. Not for breaking it. For lying. He said, *a lie costs twice — once the truth, once yourself*. I've thought about that my whole life. Every time I filed a report I shouldn't have softened. Every time I saluted an order I shouldn't have saluted." He looked at his hand, at the fingers that had touched the seal, as if expecting them to have changed. "Twice, Sera. It costs twice."

"I know."

"No. You don't. You broke early — you ran, you fought, you got captured, but you never *saluted*. I saluted for months. I questioned people, Sera. Charm-sellers. A grandmother in Ashford Row with a fever-charm stitched into her shawl. I wrote it all down in a neat hand and sent it upstairs and went home and slept." His voice did not break; it simply emptied, like a cup draining. "So don't tell me what I'm serving. I've known for longer than you have. I just told myself the crown must know best, because the alternative was my father's lesson, and I couldn't afford to pay it."

Sera reached over — cords and all — and gripped his forearm, Warden-fashion, grip to grip, the way they'd sealed watches at the border stones a hundred nights running.

"Then stop paying interest," she said. "Pay it once. Tonight."

* * *

The Questioning House had been built as a granary, back when Vhalen trusted its neighbors, and it still had a granary's bones: thick walls, few doors, one stair, one gate. Getting out was the problem everyone had already thought of. Getting *in* was what nobody guarded, because nobody imagined anyone would come.

But someone came.

Sera heard it first as a wrongness in the bells — the cracked bronze of the lower quarter striking midnight, and striking, and striking, eleven, twelve, thirteen, fourteen, a count that meant nothing until she understood it wasn't a count at all. It was a signal. Somewhere in Lowcandle, a charm-bearer was ringing a ward-alarm through a temple bell, and every piece of covenant-metal in the district was singing back, including — she felt it wake against her wrist like a second pulse — the ring on hers.

Then the lights in the corridor went out, all of them at once, and the shouting started.

Dain was already at the door with keys. "Charm-work," he said grimly, unlocking her irons. "Someone's burning fragments in the street. The whole guard detail is running toward the noise, because the Regent's men can't abide an unsanctioned light." He freed her wrists and shoved a bundle at her: her coat, her boots, a short sword. "Back courtyard. There's a sally gate the grain carts used. I can get us to it before they sort themselves out."

"Us," Sera repeated, working feeling back into her hands.

"Us," Dain said, and did not meet her eyes. "Unless you'd rather I stayed."

She wanted to say a great many things — that forgiveness wasn't hers to give, that the grandmother in Ashford Row was his to carry, that some debts cost twice and he hadn't finished paying. None of it fit in the time they had. So she said the truest thing available:

"We move together. Argue later."

They went into the corridor together, through darkness lit only by the ring on Sera's wrist pulsing faint blue-white, and the Questioning House was chaos around them — guards blundering toward windows, someone screaming that the lights in the east cells had ignited on their own, a questioner's voice cracking on the word *heresy*. At the stairhead they met two guards coming up with drawn steel, and Dain stepped past her with a speed she remembered from training yards long ago and put them both down without killing either, and for one bright moment it was six years ago and they were oath-brothers clearing a room, wordless, perfectly fitted.

Then they reached the courtyard, and the sally gate, and the plan ended.

Because the gate was barred from outside — she could see the beam through the gap — and behind them, in the doorway to the main house, lamplight bloomed, and voices, and boots, many boots, organized boots, the ones that had not run toward the distraction. A sergeant's voice, cold and close: *"Corvess! Step away from the prisoner!"*

Dain looked at the gate. At her. Sera saw the arithmetic finish behind his eyes, fast and final, and grabbed his arm. "No. No — the beam, we can lift it together—"

"Somebody has to hold the door," he said, gently, the way you say things you've decided a long time ago and are only now admitting. He pressed his keys into her hand and his short sword after them. "The beam drops on a counterweight — there, the rope, cut it and the bar falls free on the outside. Go through, cut it, don't look back."

"Dain—"

"*Don't look back*." He smiled, and it was the old grin at last, the winter-quarters grin, and it undid her more than any wound the inquisitors had given her. "Sera. My father said a lie costs twice. Well — so does the truth, sometimes. But it's the better debt." He turned to face the lamplight, rolled his shoulders like a man stepping into a river, and called out in his parade-ground voice, clear enough to reach every corner of the yard:

"Questioner Corvess, releasing the subject under authority of the Ninth Seat! Anyone who wants her comes through me!"

*Ninth Seat,* she thought wildly, shoving the gate, cutting the rope, hearing the great beam crash down behind her as she slipped through the narrowing gap. *He doesn't even know what that means. He heard me say it once.*

She looked back anyway. Of course she looked back.

Lamplight. Boots. Dain standing square in the gateway with his empty hands raised in a Warden's salute — the old salute, palm out, honest — and beyond him, above the rooftops of Lowcandle, a smear of orange glow and rising sparks where the charm-bearers burned their fragments in the streets, and somewhere in that glow, she knew, a soaked scribe with a satchel full of covenant was waiting.

Sera Ilvane ran toward the fire with her oath-brother's keys in her fist, and did not let herself cry until the Questioning House was out of sight — and even then only briefly, because dawn was coming, and with it the road east, and the First Hearth, and whatever Jonah Kavanagh's dreams had promised was waiting at the end of both.
