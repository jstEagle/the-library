## 15. The March of the Hollow

Jonah saw them first from the Hearth's high gallery, where the mountain opened its stone throat to the sky: a gray tide moving up the Cinderfell road, so slow it might have been mistaken for weather.

They came without banners. That was the worst of it, Jonah thought — armies were supposed to have banners, drums, the gaudy theater of war. This had none. Ten thousand figures walked in step through the ash-fog, and the only sound they made was the sound of their boots on the old lava-plain, a soft grinding hush like the mountain itself clearing its throat. No horns. No war-cries. The Hollow Regent did not need to frighten what it had already emptied.

"Count them," Sera said beside her. Her voice was flat in the way Jonah had learned meant fear held under military discipline, pressed flat as a flower in a ledger.

"I don't need to," Jonah said. "There aren't enough of us to matter."

Below, in the courtyard before the First Hearth's gates, the people of Lowcandle were doing the arithmetic anyway. Charm-bearers knelt in rough rows, tying fragments of the covenant into their hair, their collars, the wrappings around their wrists. Old Merren, who had bought a ward-charm from Jonah six years ago to keep her feverish son breathing through a winter that should have taken him, was teaching a boy of perhaps fourteen how to hold his fragment so the light would face outward. The widow Tass with her ink-marked hands. The debt-runners who had once paid Jonah in stolen candle-stubs. Two hundred souls, maybe fewer, standing between ten thousand hollow-eyed conscripts and a door older than the kingdom.

*And every one of them thinks I'm going to fix this,* Jonah thought, and their stomach turned over slowly, like a page being turned against their will.

Because there was a way. There had been a way since Vaskarath spoke it in the dark under the mountains, in that voice like a landslide remembering how to be gentle: *The flame must go to the Ninefold Throne. Someone must carry it. And carriers always burn.*

Someone must carry it. Jonah had volunteered before the dragon finished speaking — had raised their hand as if it were a market transaction, as if volunteering were something they knew how to do, the one skill twelve years of small dishonesties had taught them. What they had not done was say the rest of it aloud. Not to Sera. Not to Ondrey. Not to the two hundred kneeling in the ash below.

*Carriers always burn.*

* * *

They found Vaskarath in the deep chamber where the heart-flame lived, if a thing no bigger than a candle-flame could be said to live anywhere. The last dragon lay coiled around the hearthstone like a mountain that had decided to sit down, his scales gone the color of old ash, his ribs rising and falling with the slow patience of geology. The heart-flame guttered on its bed of fused glass, throwing shadows that moved wrong — or rather, that moved honestly, showing the shapes of everything the fire remembered: wings, courts, oaths spoken in voices ninefold.

"You're going to tell me again," Vaskarath said, without opening his eyes, "that you've changed your mind."

"No."

"Then you're going to lie to someone else about it." The dragon's eye opened at last, a slit of amber like a door left ajar on a burning house. "I can smell it on you, little scribe. Fear has a smell. Lies have another. You're wearing both like cheap perfume."

Jonah sat down on the cold hearthstone, knees folding more than sitting. "If I tell them, they won't let me carry it."

"If you tell them, they'll be right not to let you carry it."

"If I tell them, Sera will volunteer instead. Or Ondrey. Or Merren, or that boy with the badly tied fragment, or—" Jonah's voice cracked and they hated it. "Everyone down there is going to die anyway if the flame doesn't reach the Throne. But they don't all have to die *for* it. One death is enough. Mine is the cheapest one available. I'm a charm-seller and a debtor and an heir nobody wanted. My life has never been worth what it costs."

Vaskarath was quiet for a long time. When he moved, the whole chamber seemed to resettle around him, dust sifting from seams in the rock like slow snow.

"Do you know why carriers burn?" he said.

"Because the flame is too great for mortal flesh. Because—"

"Because the flame takes what it is given." The amber eye fixed on Jonah, and for a moment the guttering light steadied, as if listening to its keeper. "It does not want flesh. It has never wanted flesh. Flesh is simply what frightened people offer it. A carrier who comes to the flame believing their own life is worthless offers exactly that — worthlessness — and the fire, honest as it is, consumes what it is handed. Every carrier I have known climbed onto this stone already dead inside. They simply waited for the outside to agree with the inside." He exhaled, a long gray sigh. "You are planning to die, little scribe. That is not the same as being willing to give. The flame knows the difference. It has always known the difference."

"That's very beautiful," Jonah said, "and it changes nothing. If I speak my oath to it, maybe it takes less. Maybe it takes nothing. You said yourself the flame answers willing vows."

"I said it *might*. Might is not a plan. Might is a coin you spend when you have nothing else in your purse." Vaskarath lowered his great head until his breath stirred Jonah's hair, warm and smelling of cold stone and old smoke. "But hear me, Ninth Seat. I will ready the flame for you, because you asked, and because the alternative is watching everything I loved stay dead forever. I will not, however, help you lie. Lie to your knight. Lie to your magister. Lie to your whole ragged congregation of debtors, if lying is what love looks like when it's cornered. But do not lie to the fire. It will know. And it will take you at your word."

* * *

Sera found Jonah on the gate-wall an hour before dawn, watching the gray tide make its patient camp on the plain below. Torches now — the Regent's army had finally lit fires, hundreds of them, but the flames burned strange and low, giving almost no light, as if even fire forgot itself near the Hollow.

"They're not sleeping," Sera said. "I sent runners down the scree to look. They just stand. In ranks. Facing us." She paused. "Their reflections don't show in the river shallows. Dain told me about that, before — the Questioning House used it to test suspects. Hold water in front of their eyes. If there's nothing looking back..." She didn't finish. She didn't need to.

"How many did we get out of Lowcandle?"

"All who'd come. The rest stayed to keep the streets. Ondrey says the compact holds even scattered — that's rather the point of it, apparently. He lectured me for half an hour while packing books." Sera leaned on the wall beside Jonah, close enough that their shoulders touched. Below them, the charm-bearers' fragments gave off a faint glow, dozens of small lights like embers raked from one dying fire. "I've stood a doomed line twice now. Once at Greywater, once here. Do you know what I've learned?"

"That we lose?"

"That it matters who tells you you're going to die." She looked at Jonah sideways, and her eyes in the fragment-light were steady and terribly clear. "At the ford, nobody told me. I just knew. Here, everyone keeps looking at you. Waiting for you to say the thing that makes the dying mean something. So I'm going to ask you plainly, Jonah Kavanagh, and I want you to remember that I once swore an oath that let a monster into my king, and I have spent every day since learning what lies cost. Is there anything you haven't told us? About carrying the flame?"

The question hung between them like the ash-fog.

Jonah thought of Vaskarath's amber door. *Do not lie to the fire.* But Sera was not fire. Sera was a woman with a broken oath and a weeping seal and a dead friend behind a locked door, and if Jonah told her the truth she would take the flame herself, or find some third option that got them both killed, or stand here arguing while the Hollow marched, and there was no time, there had never been any time since the stars went out—

"No," Jonah said. "Nothing. We carry the flame to the Throne. We speak the vow. The Court relights. That's everything."

The lie went out of them like blood from a wound, and they felt lighter and filthier in the same instant.

Sera nodded slowly. Something flickered behind her eyes — doubt, maybe, or the memory of her own well-practiced lies — but she let it pass. "Then I'll hold the gate until you're through it. However long that is." She put her hand briefly on Jonah's shoulder, gauntlet and all, the weight of it almost tender. "Get some sleep, charm-seller. Tomorrow you become a legend, and legends should at least start rested."

She walked away along the wall. Jonah stayed, watching the torches that gave no light, and whispered to the dark, to no one, to the buried king in his far prison: *"Forgive me. I'm learning from the best."*

* * *

Dawn came gray and sourceless, the sun a pale stain behind the ash-clouds, and with it came the march.

No order was shouted. The army simply began, ten thousand boots finding their rhythm at once, the gray tide uncoiling toward the Hearth's gates across three miles of broken black ground. As they drew closer the defenders could see their faces — ordinary faces, farmer faces, faces that might have been pulled from any village the Regent had swallowed — except that nothing moved behind the eyes. They blinked when bodies require blinking. Their mouths were set in expressions their owners might once have worn. But the lookers-out were gone, and in the polished boss of a shield near the front rank, Jonah saw with a lurch, the reflection showed only empty air where a man should have been.

"They'll walk over our fragments," Ondrey said at Jonah's elbow. The old magister had dressed for war in his university robes, absurd and magnificent, ink-stains like battle honors on his sleeves. "Light remembers oaths. These poor wretches were taken without consent, without witness — there's nothing in them for the fire to catch. The fragments will break their formation, confuse them, maybe drop some. But they cannot turn them. You cannot swear an oath *for* someone. That lesson cost me thirty years to learn and it appears we'll relearn it by noon." He glanced down at Jonah, sharp-eyed beneath his wild white brows. "You've gone pale. Eat something. Even condemned men breakfast."

"I'm not hungry."

"You're frightened. Good. Fear is the body telling the truth when the mouth won't." Ondrey held out a heel of bread, and after a moment Jonah took it, mostly to end the conversation, and ate it without tasting anything.

The gates of the First Hearth were bronze and green with age, worked with nine interlocked rings worn nearly smooth by centuries of touching hands. Beyond them the antechamber waited, and beyond that, in the deep chamber, Vaskarath was waking the heart-flame — Jonah could feel it even here, a warmth in the coin in their pocket, in the scarred skin of their palms where twelve years of debt-marks had faded to silver lines. The fragments in the courtyard were brightening too, answering their elder the way candles answer a struck match.

Merren found Jonah at the gate. She was seventy if she was a day, and she had tied her fragment into her gray braid like a girl, and she was crying without any noise at all.

"You'll do it, then," she said. "Today."

"As soon as the line buys me the hour."

"My boy — the one the winter-charm saved. He'd be eleven now, if the fever hadn't come back for him the year after. I used to think that charm was a cheat. Bought luck. Then I thought it was stolen, since you sold it illegally, and I felt a fool for trusting a stranger in the dark." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, impatient, as though grief were an inconvenience. "Now I think maybe it was neither. Maybe it was the first piece of a promise, and I was holding it all these years without knowing whose promise it was." She gripped Jonah's wrist, hard, her fingers over the faded debt-marks. "Whatever happens today — whatever you're carrying that you haven't told anyone — you finish it. Do you hear me? Promises get kept. That's the whole law. Everything else is commentary."

*I haven't told anyone.* The words went through Jonah like a blade finding the gap in armor. Did she know? Had everyone known, all along, the way everyone had known the wards were failing?

"Merren," Jonah started, "if I—"

"Positions!" Sera's voice cut across the courtyard, clear as a bell over the rising grind of ten thousand boots. "Charm-bearers, to your marks! Light on my word and not before!"

Merren squeezed Jonah's wrist once, hard enough to leave marks over the marks, and went to her place in the line.

* * *

The last hour before battle passed the way Jonah supposed all such hours passed — too fast and too slow at once, full of small useless holy details. The boy with the badly tied fragment practicing his grip. Ondrey murmuring oath-law to himself like a rosary. Sera walking the line, saying each defender's name, because names were the oldest magic anyone had left. Somewhere below, in the deep chamber, Vaskarath's voice rose in a language older than kingdoms, and the heart-flame lifted from its bed of glass for the first time in a thousand years, growing, gathering, becoming a light no larger than a fist but bright enough to throw Jonah's shadow clean through the closed bronze gates.

Jonah stood before the dragon with the flame hovering between them, and Vaskarath looked at them for a long, long moment with his amber door-of-an-eye.

"Say it," the dragon rumbled. "Before you take it. Say what you're carrying it *for*."

"For them," Jonah said. Their voice came out steady, which surprised them. "For Merren and her dead boy. For the debtors and the marked and the ones the ledgers forgot. For a realm where justice is chosen, not inherited. For the man in the crown who only wants to be free. I carry it for all of them."

"And for yourself?"

Jonah hesitated. And because it was the fire, and the fire would know, and because some small stubborn honest thing in them refused to let the last words they might ever speak be a lie —

"For myself too. I want to live. I want to see the stars come back. I want—" their voice broke, mended, broke again, "—I want to keep the stall. I want to teach the child to write her own charms. Please. Let me carry it and live."

The heart-flame flickered. Steadied. And settled onto Jonah's cupped hands like a bird deciding to trust them — weightless, warm, and burning through skin and muscle and bone with a pain so vast and clean it was almost silence.

"There," Vaskarath said softly. "That was the truest vow I've heard in a thousand years. Now run, little scribe. Run and mean it."

Outside, the Hollow reached the gates, and the first fragments ignited, and the world filled with light.

* * *

What followed, Jonah would afterward remember in pieces, the way fire remembers — in flashes, in heat-shapes. Sera's voice roaring the line to hold. Merren's fragment blazing white in her gray braid as she stood shoulder to shoulder with strangers and refused to move. The front ranks of the Hollow faltering, stumbling, faces flickering — and in some few faces, horribly, briefly, *returning*, eyes widening with terror and confusion as the light touched something still human in them before the dark took them back. Ondrey striding the wall-top like a professor late for lecture, shouting clauses of covenant law at an army that couldn't hear him, and the fragments answering him anyway, flaring on his cadence like a choir.

And through it all, Jonah ran — through the antechamber, through passages that had not been walked since the Court fell, the heart-flame cradled against their chest like the smallest, most terrible child, pain climbing their arms in silver rivers, teeth gritted around a scream they would not release because the people behind them needed to believe their champion was unburned and unbowed.

Behind them the gates groaned. Behind them the line bent, and bent, and did not break.

Ahead, at the end of the long dark passage, the Ninefold Throne room waited — and with it, wearing a stolen morning like a garment, the thing that had eaten the sky.

Jonah Kavanagh ran toward it, burning, lying with every stride, and prayed to no one at all that the fire would accept the truth at the last, when there was nothing left to hide behind.
