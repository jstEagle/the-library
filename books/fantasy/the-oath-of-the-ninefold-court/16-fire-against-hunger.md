## 16. Fire Against Hunger

The Hollow came down from the northern pass at dusk, and they did not march so much as *pour* — a grey tide of men and women whose reflections had been drunk out of them, armor dulled to the color of old ash, eyes like windows into an unlit room. There were thousands. Sera Ilvane stood on the wall above the First Hearth's gates and counted until counting became a cruelty, and then she stopped.

Beside her, a baker's boy named Tam shifted from foot to foot, his mother's charm-knot clutched in one fist so tightly the cord had bitten grooves into his fingers. He was fourteen at most. He had never held anything sharper than a bread knife before this week.

"You don't have to be on this wall," Sera told him. "No one does. That's rather the whole point."

"I know." He didn't look at her. His jaw was set in a way that made her chest ache, because it was the same jaw-set she'd seen on green knights the morning before their first battle, all of them convinced that courage was a thing you could decide to have. "Kavanagh said the fragments answer best to people who *choose*. I'm choosing."

Below them, spread along the ramparts and crammed behind the barricades of the outer court, stood the militia of Lowcandle: debtors and smugglers, laundresses and poachers, a one-eyed fishmonger who had once bought a ward-charm from Jonah Kavanagh against river-hags and now wore it over his heart like a knight's favor. Three hundred souls, perhaps. Against an army whose numbers Sera had given up counting.

They were not soldiers. That was the thought that kept circling her like a carrion bird. They were not soldiers, and they were going to die here, and the only thing between them and dying *badly* was whatever she could put into their hands in the hour that remained.

She raised her voice, and it carried the way voices carry when the world has gone quiet enough to listen.

"When I took my oath as a ward-knight," she called, "they made me swear on a stone altar with nine rings cut into it, and they told me the oath would hold me. It did. It held me like a cage holds a bird — kindly, gently, but a cage all the same. And when the Court broke, my oath broke with it, and I learned what it is to serve a promise that's already dead." She drew her sword — not the palace-forged blade they'd stripped from her at her disgrace, but the plain soldier's steel she'd bought back with smuggler's coin. "These people beside you are not sworn by any altar. They swore to each other, in doorways and market stalls, with nothing but their word. And a word freely given burns hotter than any oath carved in stone. Tonight we show the thing in the crown what fire remembers."

Tam looked up at her. "That was a good speech," he whispered.

"It was adequate," Sera said. "Save your praise for after."

* * *

They came with no war-cries. That was the worst of it. The Hollow Regent's host advanced in silence, ten thousand boots striking the frozen ground in unison, a sound like a slow heartbeat made of stone. No banners. No horns. The Regent had no need to frighten them; fear was for people who still had something inside to frighten.

"Steady," Sera said quietly, walking the wall. "Let them come to the killing ground. Let them come all the way in."

The first rank crossed the boundary-stones of the Hearth's demesne, where the old covenant-law still lingered like warmth in a hearthstone hours after the fire dies. The second rank followed. The third.

Jonah Kavanagh stood at the center of the outer court, small and unarmored among the charm-bearers, and lifted the coin stamped with nine interlocked rings.

"Now," Jonah said — not shouted, just said, and every fragment in three hundred hands woke at once.

Sera had seen wards ignite before. She had spent twenty years around temple-magic, gate-spells, the cold blue architecture of imposed law. This was nothing like that. This was *light with memory in it*. A laundress's cheap love-charm flared gold and became the shape of a vow someone had whispered over it years ago. The fishmonger's river-ward burned the color of deep water and clean promises. Tam's knot-cord blazed against his knuckles, and the boy laughed — actually laughed, high and startled, as if something warm had taken his hand.

Three hundred small lights rose from three hundred ordinary hands and braided themselves together above the gates into a single wall — not of flame exactly, but of *remembering*, a barrier woven from every small kindness ever bound into the charms Kavanagh had sold in the dark for twelve years. *May your door hold. May your child sleep safe. May you be found when you are lost.*

The front rank of the Hollow hit that light and stopped as though they'd struck glass.

For a moment the whole battlefield hung suspended — the grey host pressing against the shining wall, the wall bending but holding, humming like a struck bell — and then the second rank surged forward and the first rank was driven *through*, and the battle began in earnest.

* * *

It was not a battle in any way Sera had been taught. There were no lines, no maneuvers, none of the elegant geometry of trained armies. The Hollow poured against the wall of remembered vows like floodwater against a sea-wall, and where the light touched them, some of them — some few, the newest taken, the ones whose reflections had been swallowed most recently — *shuddered*, and looked down at their own empty hands as if seeing them for the first time.

"The fragments!" Kavanagh's voice rang across the court. "Don't hoard them — give the light away! It feeds on giving!"

And they did. That was the miracle no tactician would have believed. The fishmonger pressed his burning ward-charm into a Hollow soldier's gauntlet and the man screamed — not in pain, Sera realized, but in *recognition* — and dropped his blade and sat down in the snow and wept at nothing, at everything, at the ghost of his own face returning to him like a sunrise. A laundress caught a deserter by the wrist and held him while her charm-light crawled up his arm and burned the grey away.

But there were ten thousand of them, and only three hundred lights, and for every soldier who woke, twenty more pressed on with those terrible windowless eyes, climbing over their waking comrades without malice or hesitation, because they had nothing left in them that knew how to hesitate.

Sera went down onto the killing ground because walls were for people with armies, and she had three hundred civilians and a promise to keep.

She fought at the breach where the light ran thinnest, where the press was worst, her plain steel doing the work that charms could not. Beside her fought the fishmonger with a gutting knife; behind her, Tam, who had discovered that courage and terror feel identical in the body and had decided to keep moving anyway. She killed a hollow-eyed sergeant and hated how easy it was, hated the Regent for making slaughter of people who deserved rescue more than death.

"Hold the line where—" she started, and then she saw it.

At the heart of the grey tide, a figure walked untouched. Tall, robed in white gone bone-colored, crowned with a circlet of black iron shaped like nine rings with the ninth ring broken open. The Regent's herald. Where it passed, the charm-light guttered like candles in a draft, and the woken soldiers went grey again, their hard-won faces smoothing back into vacancy.

It raised one hand toward the wall of light, and the wall *bent*.

"No," Sera breathed.

"Someone has to stop that thing," Tam said, "or the whole—"

"Stay here." She was already running.

* * *

Magister Ondrey reached the herald first.

Sera had met Ondrey exactly twice before this night: once across a university table, when he had refused the Ninth Seat's summons with a courtesy so polished it cut; and once on the road, when he had arrived uninvited with the Third Seat fragment and a warning, saying he had watched Lowcandle stand and changed his mind — not for the Court, he'd been careful to say, but for the people who chose each other without one. He was sixty years old, soft-bodied, nearsighted, and he carried a duelist's cane because, he'd explained mildly, he had won forty challenges with it before deciding scholarship paid better.

Now he stepped between the herald and the wall of light, planted the cane, and said, in the tone of a man correcting a student's citation:

"That gesture is improper. You are invoking the Fourth Ring's authority of dissolution — *my* authority, madam, or what remains of it. And I am here to inform you, formally and on the record, that the Fourth Seat does not consent."

The herald turned its head. Under the bone-white hood was a face Sera half-recognized — a temple canoness missing for years, perhaps, or just the kind of face the Regent preferred, smooth and generic as an unscribed page. When it spoke, its voice came from somewhere deeper than its throat, vast and patient, wearing sound the way it wore bodies.

"*The seats are ended. The oath is ash. Consent is a currency of the living.*"

"Then let us transact," Ondrey said, and pulled a scrap of parchment from his sleeve — Sera saw the nine-ring seal glowing faintly upon it, the Third Seat fragment, inked rather than embroidered — and pressed it flat against the head of his cane.

The cane ignited. Not with charm-gold, but with a fierce white clarity, the light of pure argument made manifest, of law loved rather than merely obeyed. Forty years of refused oaths and privately kept principles, all of it burning at once.

The duel, if it could be called that, lasted less time than it takes to tell. The herald moved like falling masonry; Ondrey moved like a footnote — precisely, inevitably, arriving exactly where the blow was not. He could not wound the thing; nothing so solid as a wound would do. But he could *occupy* it, and he understood, with a scholar's cold clarity, that occupation was the entire task. Every second the herald spent contending with the Fourth Seat's refusal was a second the wall of remembered vows stood unbent.

"Go!" Ondrey called over his shoulder, parrying a strike that cracked the frozen mud for six feet in either direction. "I have held this position against deans far worse than demons! *Go!*"

Sera went — but not where duty pointed. Because through the smoke of the duel she could see the other thing she had promised herself she would do tonight, the thing that had been waiting in her since Dain stayed behind in the Questioning House to hold the door.

The place where the Hollow line pressed hardest, where the charm-light ran thinnest and the dead lay thickest — it was the ford-side ground, the low ground by the stream, and she had chosen that post herself two days ago, standing on this same wall, telling Kavanagh: *Put me where it breaks first. I've been practicing for breaking points my whole life.*

Dain had died holding a door. She would hold ground.

* * *

The fighting blurred. Sera remembered it afterward in flashes, the way trauma keeps its own ledger: the fishmonger going down under three blades and rising again because someone's charm had caught him. Tam at her shoulder, white-faced, swinging a borrowed hatchet with his eyes shut and somehow connecting. The moment the wall of light above them tore at last — too many fragments spent, too much given away — and the grey tide began to pour through the gap, and the line should have broken, and did not break, because the Lowcandle folk locked arms. Debtors who had never trusted anyone linking elbow to elbow with strangers, singing — of all things — a laundry-song, a counting-song about stains and seasons, ragged and off-key and utterly unbreakable.

The Hollow faltered before them. Sera saw it happen and would swear to it until she died: the advance *stumbled*, not from wounds but from bewilderment. These soldiers had been emptied of reflection, of selfhood, of every tie that makes a person pause. And here was a wall made entirely of pausing — of people who had every reason to run and had chosen, aloud, together, to stay. The emptiness in them had no purchase on that. You cannot drink a well that refills itself from choice.

"Flame that remembers its oaths," Kavanagh had called it once, explaining why the fragments answered the poor best. Now Sera understood. The Regent's hunger fed on imposed law, on duty extracted, on oaths caging the caged. It had no teeth at all for what burned on this field tonight.

She killed and killed and killed, and wept while she did it, and did not stop.

* * *

Dawn found the field strewn with the grey and the golden alike, and the Herald of the Hollow Regent retreating up the pass with its white robes scorched and its composure cracked — dragged back, some said, by its master's will rather than its own defeat, called home like a tool returned to a belt. Ondrey stood amid the wreckage leaning on a shattered cane, bleeding from a scalp wound, looking mildly astonished to be alive.

"Well," he said to Sera as she limped over. "I believe I shall require a new thesis topic. Something on the martial applications of procedural objection, perhaps."

Behind them, the Lowcandle folk were counting their dead and their living with the same terrible tenderness, pressing charms back into each other's hands, binding wounds with strips of laundry-line. Tam sat against the wall, alive, his hatchet forgotten, staring at the sunrise with the expression of someone renegotiating every assumption he'd owned yesterday.

And then the eastern sky flickered.

Not clouds. Not light. The stars — those absent witnesses, dark for weeks — stuttered, once, like a candle in wind. All of them. At once.

Sera felt it go through the crowd like a shiver through water. The fishmonger looked up. Old Ondrey looked up. And Jonah Kavanagh came running along the wall with the nine-ringed coin held high in one fist, and their face was pale as the dawn, and they were shouting a name — Vaskarath's name — and shouting that the dragon-flame was guttering, guttering *now*, that the window was closing, that whoever meant to carry the fire to the throne had to leave within the hour.

Within the hour. Sera closed her eyes. She had heard, on the road from Cinderfell, what Vaskarath had named as the price of carrying. Carriers always burn.

She opened them again and turned to find Jonah already looking at her — looking at her with an expression she recognized too well, because she had worn it herself in mirrors for twenty years. The look of someone about to lie beautifully, for the kindest of reasons, to everyone they love.

"Don't," Sera said quietly, before Jonah could speak. "*Whatever you're about to volunteer for — don't you dare decide alone.*"
