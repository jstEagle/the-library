## 20. The Tenth Voice

The Hall of the Ninefold Court had not been swept in a hundred years.

Jonah noticed it the way they noticed everything now — with a scribe's eye, cataloguing small truths that larger ones tried to hide. The dust lay thick on the nine thrones, gray and undisturbed, except where the Regent's passage had carved a single dark furrow through it all: one line of ruin from the doors to the central seat, as if something had been dragged there rather than walked. The king's body still lay at the foot of that throne, wrapped now in the banner of a house that no longer existed, and around him stood the remnants of an army that should have been dead, and the survivors of a militia that should have been scattered, and the last dragon in the world, coiled in the great doorway because he would not fit anywhere smaller.

And every eye in the hall was on Jonah.

They could feel them — the weight of them, hundreds of gazes, the same weight they'd felt for twelve years across a market stall when someone wanted a charm and couldn't quite afford honesty about why. But this was different. This was hunger dressed as hope. Heir of the Ninth Seat. Flame-bearer. The one who unmade the crown. The names were already moving through the crowd like sparks through dry grass, and Jonah knew how quickly such fires caught, and how little they cared what they burned.

Sera stood beside them, armor scorched, one arm bound where the herald's blade had opened it. She hadn't spoken since the throne room. She didn't need to. Her silence said *they're going to ask*, and her silence also said *and I don't know if I want you to say yes or no, and I hate that*.

Magister Ondrey stood apart, near the eastern wall, arms folded, wearing his refusal like a coat he'd never taken off. Vaskarath's breath stirred the dust in slow tides. And in the center of it all, the Ninefold Throne waited — nine chairs in a ring, interlocked like the rings on the old coin, the ninth seat empty for a century, the first eight hollowed by death or desertion or, in one case, by a man who had chosen comfort over covenant and lived long enough to regret it in the specific way that comfortable men regret things: quietly, and too late.

It was the High Cantor who finally spoke — old Halvern, who had lied to Jonah for twelve years with a ledger in one hand and kindness in the other, who had burned with his temple and walked out of the ashes without so much as singed eyebrows because, Jonah suspected, guilt made poor kindling. His voice carried the way voices carry in halls built for oaths.

"The law is clear." He did not sound happy about it. That, at least, was honest. "When the seats are emptied and the flame is relit, the heir of the surviving seat assumes the regency until the Court is reforged whole. The Ninth Seat stands. The Ninth Seat's heir stands before us. The realm requires a sovereign."

"The realm required one yesterday," Jonah said. "Yesterday it got a hunger wearing a man's face. Today it's being offered me."

"Is that a refusal?"

"It's a question I'd like everyone here to sit with for a moment."

Jonah stepped forward, away from Sera, away from the shelter of allies, into the open space before the throne where the dust-furrow ran. Their boots left prints in a hundred years of settled silence. They had spent their whole life learning how to speak to people who wanted something from them — customers, cantors, debt-collectors — and they knew the first rule: do not begin with what you will give. Begin with what you see.

"Look at this chair," they said, and laid one hand on the armrest of the Ninth Seat. It was cold. It had been cold for a hundred years and it was cold now, and somehow that seemed important. "Look at all of them. Nine chairs in a ring, and not one of them facing the others — did you ever notice? Each throne faces outward. Each seat looks at the realm and not at its neighbors. We called it a court. It was nine people each alone with their own judgment, bound together by an oath none of them chose — born into it, or conquered into it, or tricked into it the way I was tricked, with debts and ledgers and a childhood spent copying wards I wasn't allowed to understand.

"And the oath held," Jonah went on, "because oaths imposed can hold for a long time. Cages hold. Chains hold. The Court held the borders and the laws and the dragons' fire and the stars themselves, and it held for a thousand years, right up until the moment it didn't. And when it broke, it broke all at once, everywhere, like glass heard through stone — because that's what imposed things do. They don't bend. They shatter."

From the doorway came a low rumble that might have been laughter or might have been agreement. Vaskarath's voice, when it came, filled the hall the way water fills a cellar — from underneath, rising.

"I sat witness to the First Oath," the dragon said. "I have told you this, little scribe. Nine knelt. None asked them whether they wished to. The realm needed binding, so they were bound. Even then — even at the very forging — I wondered what would happen on the day the bound grew tired."

"And we saw," Jonah said. "We all saw. So no." They turned to face the High Cantor directly, and let their voice drop from the register of address to the register of conversation, which paradoxically carried further in that hall. "No, I won't take the throne. Not because I'm unworthy — I've heard enough about worthiness this year to choke on the word. Not because I'm afraid. I burned carrying a dragon's heart through a battle; fear and I are old acquaintances now and it knows better than to make demands of me. I refuse because everything I suffered — the debt, the lies, the hidden inheritance, the collar dressed up as a birthright — everything the Regent fed on, everything the old law permitted — began with exactly this sentence: *the realm requires a sovereign.*"

Halvern flinched. Good, thought Jonah, and hated themselves a little for thinking it.

"You'd be a good king," Sera said quietly. It was the first thing she'd said in hours. "You know that's the problem, don't you? You'd be good at it. That's how it gets everyone."

"That's exactly how it gets everyone," Jonah agreed. "The crown doesn't look for the cruel. It looks for the competent. It looks for the person who sees the mess and thinks *I could fix that if only everyone would do what I say.* I've been that person my whole life — selling protection to people who couldn't question the price. If I sit in that chair, in ten years or thirty, some future scribe is going to stand where I'm standing and explain why the throne has to fall again. I intend to spare them the speech."

Silence, then. The particular silence of several hundred people realizing that the answer they'd braced for was not the answer they'd prepared arguments against.

It was Ondrey who broke it, and Jonah noted — filed away — that the old magister had moved closer without seeming to.

"Then what?" he asked, and there was no challenge in it, only the genuine puzzlement of a scholar confronting a blank page. "You cannot simply leave the realm unbound. The wards are relit but the law beneath them is ash. Without a covenant there is no justice; without justice there is only the strong doing as they please until someone stronger arrives. I refused the Fourth Seat because the Court failed me — but I never pretended the alternative was anything but wolves."

"No," Jonah said. "Not nothing. A tenth voice."

They drew the coin from their pocket — the old coin, stamped with nine interlocked rings, the one that had hummed against their charms on the night everything ended. They held it up where the light from the relit wards could find it.

"This is what bound the Court. Nine rings, closed. Closed rings don't admit anyone. Closed rings don't release anyone. Closed rings just tighten. What I propose—" and here Jonah turned slowly, addressing the whole hall, the soldiers and the charm-bearers and the Lowcandle militia with their patched clothes and their borrowed weapons, the envoys and the refugees and the dragon in the door— "is a ring that opens.

"A compact, not a court. Sworn freely, by any who choose to swear it — every seat and every settlement and every stranger who walks in off the road. Not inherited. Not assigned. Not owed by blood or bought with coin or extracted through twelve years of manufactured debt. Sworn the way I swore my oath in the Hearth: willingly, aloud, knowing what it cost and choosing it anyway.

"And here is the clause the old law never dared write." Jonah's hand tightened on the coin. "The compact renews annually. Every year, on the night the stars went out, everyone who holds it speaks it again — or doesn't. Justice by consent, renewed by consent, and dissolvable by the same. No cage. If the compact fails, it fails openly, in daylight, with everyone watching — not secretly, over decades, rotting into a hunger that wears a king's face. Better a bond honestly broken than a bond falsely kept. The old Court died of pretending. I would rather we live by admitting."

"You'd dissolve the realm every year," Halvern said. "On principle."

"I'd *offer* the realm back to itself every year," Jonah corrected. "There's a difference. The first is surrender. The second is trust. And yes — some settlements won't swear. Some will walk away. Let them. A belonging that can't be declined isn't belonging; it's occupancy. We learned what the Crown does with occupants."

A woman near the front — Jonah recognized her, one of theirs, a Lowcandle dyer who had stood at the gates with a fragment-charm clenched in her fist while the Hollow advanced — raised her voice. "And who enforces it? A compact anyone can quit protects no one. The strong will simply decline to be bound."

"Some will," Jonah said. "And the rest of us will have to decide, each year, whether they're worth resisting — openly, together, with our reasons spoken aloud instead of buried in a ledger. That's harder than a throne. That's the point. A throne makes one person responsible for everything, which means eventually one person is guilty of everything. A compact makes all of us responsible for each other, which means none of us gets to sleep through the consequences." They smiled, tired and crooked. "I sold charms to desperate people for twelve years and called it help. I am very familiar with easy answers. I'm done trusting them."

Ondrey laughed — a short, startled sound, as if it had escaped custody. "You realize you're describing government as an annual act of collective courage."

"I'm describing it as what it always was. The old Court just hid the courage inside a ceremony and called the residue 'law.' I say we keep the courage and skip the residue."

"And the seats?" That was Halvern again, grasping, Jonah thought, at the last solid thing. "Nine Seats. Are they dissolved?"

"Transformed. Nine voices become nine among many — stewards, not sovereigns. The Ninth Seat I claim for exactly one purpose: to cast the vote that dissolves itself if the compact ever forgets what it's for. After that—" Jonah set the coin down on the cold armrest of the Ninth Seat, gently, the way one closes a book whose story is finished— "the ninth ring opens, and stays open. An invitation, not an inheritance. Whoever wants in, swears. Whoever wants out, walks. Every year, again."

For a long moment nobody moved. Then, from the doorway, wings shifted like a landslide deciding to be gentle, and Vaskarath lowered his great head into the light.

"In the beginning," the dragon rumbled, "there were nine who knelt, and one who watched, and the watching one was not asked. I have carried that — that no one thought to wonder if *fire* might consent — for a thousand years. It is a small thing beside your human griefs, and it has weighed more." Smoke curled from his nostrils, and his eyes, ancient and ember-dim since the giving of his flame, fixed on Jonah with something that in a smaller creature would have been called hope. "You offer a bond that asks. Little scribe, I am old, and tired, and I have very little flame left — enough for one witnessing, perhaps, and nothing after. Spend me well."

Jonah's throat tightened. "Vaskarath—"

"Do not waste the moment with gratitude. Gratitude is interest paid on a debt, and we are finished with debts, you and I." The dragon's laugh was a sound like distant quarry-blasting, but warmer. "Bring the paper. Bring the ink. Let us see whether a thousand years of law can be unwritten by one honest sentence."

* * *

They wrote it in the Hall itself, on a table dragged from the ruined chancellery, and Jonah insisted on drafting it in full view of everyone — no scriptorium secrets, no cantors' Latin, no language the common person needed a priest to translate. Plain speech. Plain vows. When Ondrey objected that legal precision demanded formal construction, Jonah slid the draft across the table and said, "Then add a plain-speech copy beside the formal one, and if the two ever disagree, the plain one wins. Write *that* into the law." Ondrey had stared at them for a long moment and then, slowly, begun to smile like a man discovering a new branch of mathematics.

By lamplight they worked through the night. The compact ran to less than a page — Jonah refused to let it grow longer, on the grounds that no one would read a covenant longer than a market contract, and people actually read market contracts because their lives depended on it. Nine articles. Renewal each year on the Night of Falling Stars. Dissolution by the same consent that forged it. Stewards elected, recallable, and forbidden — Ondrey himself had insisted on this clause, with grim satisfaction — from holding any title that could not be taken away.

At dawn, they swore it.

Not nine. Hundreds. The soldiers first, then the Lowcandle militia, then the envoys, then the refugees, then — after a visible struggle that lasted most of an hour — Halvern, who wept as he signed, though whether from grief or relief no one asked and he did not say. Sera swore with her sword laid flat on the table, and when she spoke the words her voice did not shake at all, which Jonah found more moving than any trembling. Ondrey swore last of the living seats, and afterward stood looking at his own signature as if it belonged to a stranger he was willing to get to know.

Then the dragon.

Vaskarath came forward into the lamplight, and the crowd drew back, and he lowered his head above the compact until his breath stirred the ink. From his chest, where the guttering remnant of the heart-flame still lived — the candle-end that remained after Jonah had carried the greater fire to the Hearth — came a light like the last coal of a hearth at dawn.

"Witnessed," the dragon said, and breathed.

The flame that left him was small — a whisper of gold, no bigger than a sparrow — but it carried ten centuries in it, and it touched the parchment without burning it, and sank into the words like rain into drought-cracked earth. For one heartbeat every letter glowed. For one heartbeat the whole hall smelled of cedar smoke and mountain mornings. And then it was only ink on paper again, ordinary and enormous, and the last dragon in the world sighed — a sound like wind leaving a valley — and folded his legs beneath him, and lay down on the stones of his enemies' hall as if it were the warm shelf of a sunny cliff.

"Little scribe," he murmured, and his voice had gone quiet in a way that made several people step closer without meaning to. "I have been keeping a flame alive for a thousand years because no one asked me whether I was tired. You asked. That is enough. The eggs of my kind sleep beneath Cinderfell — cold, but not dead. Cold things wait. Perhaps someday someone will come asking, freely, and find a way to wake them." His eyes were closing, vast and slow. "Until then... mind the wards. They were always my favorite of your species' inventions. So much wanting, pressed into so little light."

He slept then — truly slept, not the thousand-year vigil but the ordinary deep sleep of a creature finally, finally done — and his breathing slowed to the pace of mountains, and the light in his chest dimmed to an ember, and then to a memory of an ember, and stayed there. Not gone. Jonah understood, with a certainty that bypassed thought entirely: not gone. Resting. Waiting to be asked again.

The hall emptied slowly, the way crowds do when they've witnessed something they need to process alone. Sera lingered, looking down at the compact, at the nine-hundred-odd signatures crowding the page like birds on a wire.

"They'll come to you anyway," she said. "People. With problems they'll want solved from above. It starts within the week."

"I know." Jonah gathered the lamp, the ink, the plain-speech copy. "That's the other half of the work, isn't it? Not just building something that can't become a throne. Teaching people to stop looking for one." They smiled at her, tired beyond measuring. "Fortunately, I hear there's a woman in Vhalen who's very good at ward-work, and a magister who's discovered teaching, and a stall in Lowcandle with room behind it for anyone who wants to learn to write their own protections."

"A stall."

"Every revolution needs a storefront, Sera."

She laughed — the first time in days, and it cracked in the middle like river ice in spring — and shook her head, and picked up her sword, and together they walked out of the Hall of the Ninefold Court into a morning that smelled of rain coming and woodsmoke and, faintly, unmistakably, of stars.

Behind them, on the cold armrest of the Ninth Seat, the old coin lay where Jonah had left it — nine closed rings catching the early light.

And beside it, no one had noticed until the lamplighter came at dusk, a tenth ring: drawn fresh in the dust of a hundred years, open-ended, waiting.
