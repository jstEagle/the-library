## 1. The Weight of Unspent Words

There are three rules to surviving the Vesperline Guild, and I wrote two of them down.

The first is that you never touch what's in the vaults without gloves, because some things remember hands. The second is that you never ask a client why they want what they want. Curiosity is expensive here; it's the only commodity the guild doesn't sell, because it can't be bottled, and everything that can't be bottled gets burned. The third rule I never wrote anywhere: be so useful no one looks at you twice.

I am very useful. I am Gideon Thackeray, ledger-keeper of the Vesperline, and I have spent eleven years making myself into furniture with excellent penmanship.

Tonight the counting house smelled of tallow and rain coming off the harbor, and I sat where I always sit—third desk from the brazier, close enough to keep my ink from freezing, far enough back that the lamplight made me more shadow than woman. Around me, the night's inventory waited in its crates like patients who hadn't been told their diagnosis yet.

Bottled storms, six of them, each jar sweating condensation though the room was cold enough to make my knuckles ache. A year of somebody's life, borrowed against a harvest that would never come—I know because I entered it myself, and I remember the name, though names are supposed to go in the ledger and stay out of my head. They don't. That's the trouble with being good at your work. Everything stays.

And in the smallest box, wrapped in wool that cost more than most people earn in a winter, a name that had never been spoken. Not unspoken—*never* spoken. It had existed for forty years inside a mother's mouth and died there when she did, and now it was merchandise, waiting on a buyer who wanted to give it a body again. I don't ask why. Rule two. But my pen hesitated over that entry longer than any pen should hesitate over ink and paper.

"You're doing the thing," said Marn, the under-clerk, shrugging into his coat by the door.

"What thing."

"The thing where you look at an entry like it insulted your mother."

"Go home, Marn."

"It's *your* home too, technically. Guild housing." He grinned at me. He is twenty-three and still believes the guild is a strange adventure he'll tell his grandchildren about. I envy him the way you envy someone standing on a bridge before you've seen what's underneath it. "Night, Thackeray. Don't haunt the ledgers all night."

The door closed behind him. The brazier ticked. And then it was just me and the books, which is how I prefer it, which is how I've arranged eleven years of my life to be.

Here is what I am, officially: the woman who keeps the Vesperline's accounts. Every impossible thing that passes through this building passes through my columns first—a storm in glass, a decade in a locket, a grief extracted and sold to a collector who wears other people's sorrow like jewelry. My figures are exact. My hand does not shake. In eleven years I have misentered nothing, and that precision is not virtue, whatever I tell myself. Precision is armor. You build it plate by plate, entry by entry, until one day nobody can find the seam where the person used to be.

Because here is what I am, unofficially, in the privacy of my own skull: an oathbreaker's ghost. A soldier of a god who stopped answering.

You won't have heard of the Quiet Tide. That's rather the point of them. They were a small faith, sea-flavored, older than the kingdoms that now pretend the coast was always theirs. We swore oaths in salt water and kept them or drowned trying, and our god—the Drowned Mother, we called her, though she was no one's mother and everyone's—listened the way the sea listens: totally, and without much comfort. Eleven years ago she went silent. Not dead, we thought at first. Sleeping. There were arguments about it. There are fewer arguments every year, because the faithful keep dying of ordinary things—fevers, knives, age—and the god does not raise so much as a ripple to see them off.

I was not there when it happened. That's its own wound. I'd sworn my oath at nineteen, kneeling in cold surf while an old priest poured seawater down the back of my neck and told me the words would sit in me like a hook sits in a fish. She was right about the hook. When the silence came, I was three kingdoms away, and by the time I got back there was no temple, no congregation, no coast worth the name—just water where a country used to be, and a handful of survivors scattered like spilled grain.

So I did what survivors do. I found work that required no soul, and I became indispensable at it.

I finished the storm jars, closed the column, and did what I do every night at this hour, every night for eleven years, the way other people bite their nails or mutter at the weather.

I prayed.

Not properly. Properly requires kneeling and salt and the old words, and I haven't managed the old words in years—not whole, anyway. Fragments come. The opening line, mostly, the way a song you learned as a child comes back to you in the bath. So I pray the way a man talks to a grave: quietly, without expectation, out of habit so worn into me it might as well be bone.

*Mother below. Still here. Still keeping.*

That's it. That's the whole liturgy now. Three words where there used to be pages. Some nights I add things—a report, almost. *The guild bought a grief today. The buyer has kind eyes and I hated him.* As if silence were a form of attention. As if somewhere under all that water something were reading my correspondence.

Eleven years of *still here, still keeping*, and the answer has been the same every time: nothing. Not even the cold brush of *no*. Just the brazier ticking and the harbor breathing and my own voice dying in a room full of other people's miracles.

And lately—and this is the part I don't say aloud, the part I barely let myself think in full sentences—lately I've started wondering what exactly I'm keeping. Faith without answer. Is that devotion, or is it stubbornness wearing a nicer coat? Because stubbornness and faith look identical from the inside. Both of them are just refusing, day after day, to admit the thing is over. The difference is supposed to be that God shows up eventually. Eleven years is a long time to stand in an empty doorway holding a coat you were told would be wanted.

I put the thought away the way I put everything away—neatly, in its place, where it couldn't bleed into the columns. I am excellent at putting things away. It's practically my trade.

* * *

Past midnight, I did my rounds.

It's not required of me. Nothing about the vaults is required of a ledger-keeper; the enforcers handle security and the factors handle stock. But I walk the shelves anyway, once a night, because the ledgers lie if you never look at what they describe. Numbers will tell you the guild holds seven vials of borrowed courage. Only standing in front of the shelf will remind you that each vial came out of a living chest, and that somewhere a person is walking around braver than they actually are, and that someday the debt comes due with interest.

The vaults of the Vesperline run under the counting house like roots under a tree. Stone older than the guild, older than the city, older than anything except maybe the sea itself. The deep shelves hold the serious inventory—years, names, the bottled weather—and past those, behind a door most clerks don't know exists, the true vaults. The ones that aren't for sale. Or weren't, historically. I try not to think about what Corvane's new contracts might contain.

Guildmaster Corvane keeps the true vault keys himself. I keep the record of what enters them, which means I know the weight, the dimensions, the date of acquisition, and nothing else—which is exactly how Corvane designed it, and exactly why I'm still alive. He trusts me completely within a circle drawn precisely around him. I've never decided whether that's love or bookkeeping. With Corvane it may be the same thing.

I was passing the reliquary shelf—guild acquisitions from defunct faiths, which is a sentence that should tell you everything about the Vesperline—when I felt it.

Felt isn't the right word. Heard isn't either. It was somewhere between: a hum, low and resonant, like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass, except it was coming from the shelf. From the artifacts. Dead gods' odds and ends—a cracked bell from a mountain cult, a prayer-wheel gone stiff with disuse, a fisher-charm shaped like a drowning woman—that hadn't made a sound since before I was born.

They were humming.

I stood in the dark with my lantern raised and my heart going like a smuggler's oars, and I listened, and under the hum—if I tilted my head, if I stopped breathing—I could almost catch the shape of words.

Old words. Salt-water words. Words an old priest poured down the back of my neck at nineteen, telling me they'd sit in me like a hook.

Every artifact in that case was silent again within ten heartbeats. Whatever it was, it passed—or stopped, or was answered. The vault went back to being stone and dust and inventory.

But I stood there a long while after, lantern shaking slightly in a hand that never shakes over a ledger, feeling the old words sitting in me the way hooks sit in fish. Feeling, for the first time in eleven years, like the line at the end of the hook had gone suddenly, violently taut.

Somewhere in this city tonight, something was praying in the Drowned Mother's tongue.
