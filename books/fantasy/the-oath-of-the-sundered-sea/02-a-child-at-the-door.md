## 2. A Child at the Door

The Vesperline Guild keeps its strangest inventory in the cellar, and its most dangerous in the ledgers, and I am the woman who writes the dangerous kind down.

I was three hours into the night's accounting when the knocking started. Not the front door—the guild's front door has a bell that costs more than a house and announces visitors with a chime like a struck coin. This was the delivery entrance, the one the smugglers use, the one that isn't supposed to be used by anyone after the second bell. Three knocks, a pause, then two more, soft as a beggar's knuckles.

I should have ignored it. That is the first rule of surviving the Vesperline: see nothing, note everything, care about neither. I have followed that rule for eleven years with the devotion other people reserve for their gods, and look how well it's served me.

I put down my pen. I told myself I was only going to look through the grate.

* * *

The girl couldn't have been more than ten or eleven, small enough that the storm shutters behind her made her look like something left on a doorstep rather than something that had walked there. She was soaked through—not rain-soaked, not river-soaked, but sea-soaked, and the smell of her came through the grate before I'd even finished opening it.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only prayer I have left. The sea smells of salt and rot and fish, everyone knows that. But this smelled of *cedar smoke and fig leaves and hot stone*, of a harbor town at evening, of bread baking somewhere near the water. It smelled of a place.

It smelled of a country that drowned four hundred years ago.

The Sundered Sea is a story we tell children to keep them from swimming too far out. A whole nation, gone under in a single night—cities, temples, the god who watched over them, all of it folded beneath the water like a letter no one wanted to read. Every mercenary company has its old songs about it. Mine had an oath. But nobody believes the stories anymore, least of all the people who tell them, and certainly not me. I had filed the Sundered Sea under *legend* eleven years ago, right around the time my god stopped answering, and legends are just debts that no one can collect.

The girl looked up at me through the grate. Her eyes were the color of deep water over sand, and she was shivering so hard her teeth clicked, though her mouth never opened around a sound.

"Mute," I said, mostly to myself. "Wonderful. The one child in Vesperline who can't talk her way into trouble."

She raised one hand—not begging, exactly. More like the way you'd reach toward a light. And then she spoke.

I need to be careful here, because what happened next rearranged something in my chest that I had spent a decade bricking over, and I don't love talking about it. But a ledger-keeper who lies in her own head is worse than useless, so: the girl spoke, and the language was old—older than the empire, older than the guild, older than any kingdom still standing—and I knew it.

Not from books. From kneeling.

It was the tongue I'd prayed in, once, when I still believed words went somewhere. The last prayer I ever spoke aloud, the one I finished at dawn on a battlefield with my hands full of someone else's blood, the one that got no answer and never has since—I had said it in that language, because my god had been old, and you speak to old things in their own grammar or not at all.

The syllables came out of a child's mouth, cracked and half-frozen, and every hair on my arms stood up like soldiers called to attention.

And then the artifacts started humming.

* * *

You should understand what the Vesperline keeps in its vaults. Bottled storms, yes—forty-one of them, catalogued by intensity and mood. Borrowed years, decanted into glass like good brandy. Names that were never spoken, which are the most valuable and the most trouble, because an unspoken name is a loaded weapon and half our clients are looking for ammunition. We keep the oldest pieces in the deep vault: relics whose provenance even Corvane doesn't fully document, because documentation invites questions and questions invite auditors.

When the girl spoke, the vault hummed.

I heard it through two floors of stone. A low resonance, like a wineglass finding its note, except it wasn't one note—it was dozens, every ancient thing in the building waking up at once and remembering what it was for. The reliquaries. The sunken bells we keep dry out of superstition. The little carved door, no bigger than a hymnal, that three separate appraisers have sworn is a fake and which has never once taken a scratch.

They were humming in harmony. In *her* language.

The girl stopped speaking. The hum died away reluctantly, like a congregation that didn't want the sermon to end. And she looked at me with those sea-colored eyes, and tilted her head, and I understood—with the sick certainty of a woman reading her own death warrant—that she hadn't been asking to come in.

She'd been asking if I knew the words too.

* * *

Here is where I should tell you I did the sensible thing. The sensible thing would have been to close the grate, bar the door, and mention to the night steward that a strange child was loitering at the delivery entrance, and let the guild do what the guild does with strays, which is appraise them.

Instead I opened the door.

I've been turning that moment over ever since, looking for the flaw in it, the selfish motive, the angle. Mercenaries don't get to be kind; kindness is a luxury item and I stopped being able to afford luxuries when I took an oath that turned out to be payable to a dead account. But I keep coming back to the sound of her voice saying words I'd only ever said to silence, and I think the truth is simpler and worse: for about four seconds, standing in that doorway, I wasn't Gideon Thackeray, ledger-keeper, invisible woman. I was the last person alive who could answer her.

So I answered. I stepped aside and let her in.

* * *

Getting her upstairs without being seen required every trick of unremarkability I possess, which is considerable. I wrapped her in the spare cloak I keep for hauling ledger-boxes—it smelled of ink and dust, which at least covered the impossible sea-smell—and I walked her through the back corridors with the confident boredom of a woman moving a delivery she's already logged. Two enforcers passed us on the stair. One nodded to me. Neither looked at the child, because nobody looks at anything Gideon Thackeray is carrying. Invisibility, it turns out, is transferable. You just have to hold the cargo like it's boring.

I put her in the records room. It's the one place in the guild where I am sovereign: floor to ceiling shelves of contracts, deeds, confessions, and the occasional soul held in escrow, all of it dustier than a tomb and guarded by the universal assumption that ledgers are dull. I sat her down behind the shelf of maritime salvage claims, gave her the heel of bread I'd been saving, and crouched in front of her.

"Who sent you?" I asked.

She looked at me and said nothing, which I expected. Then she reached out and touched my mouth—one cold finger against my lips, gentle as a librarian—and spoke again. Those old syllables, soft now, almost conversational. And I swear on whatever's left of my soul that I understood her, the way you understand a song you haven't heard since childhood. Not word by word. Meaning by meaning.

*You know the door,* she said. *You knew the words. Why did you stop?*

"I didn't stop," I said, before I could think better of answering a mute child in a dead language. "There was no one to—" I caught myself. "It doesn't matter. Who found you? How did you get here?"

She shrugged, the magnificent indifference of the young, and ate her bread.

I should explain why that question mattered more than it seemed. Children don't wash up in Vesperline by accident. The guild buys and sells impossibilities, and a child soaked in the water of a drowned country, speaking the tongue that makes our oldest relics sing—she isn't a stray. She's *inventory*. Someone brought her here, or someone is coming for her, and either way the transaction has already begun, and I am currently harboring the merchandise.

That thought arrived with a sensation I recognized from my mercenary days: the specific chill of realizing you're standing on ground that's about to become a battlefield.

"Stay here," I told her. "Don't touch anything. Don't hum."

She looked at me with an expression I can only describe as *deeply skeptical*, and I decided to take it as agreement.

* * *

I went back to my desk and did what I always do with a problem: I wrote it down. Not in the official ledger—never that—but in my private book, the one I keep in cipher, the one that would hang me twice over if anyone read it. *Child, female, approx. 10 years. Sea-water, anomalous. Language: pre-imperial, liturgical class. Vault resonance confirmed.* My pen slowed over the next line, because the next line was the question underneath all the others:

*Why does she smell like home?*

Because that was the thing, wasn't it. Cedar smoke and fig leaves and hot stone. I have never stood in a harbor town in the Sundered Sea; it was four centuries drowned before I was born. But my god was *of* that country, and when I prayed, all those years ago, I prayed in the language of a place I'd never seen, and apparently some part of me kept the smell of it the way you keep a key to a house that's burned down.

Eleven years of silence, and tonight the silence walked up to my door wearing a child's face and smelling like an answered prayer.

I hated it. I want that on the record. I hated how much I wanted to go back to the records room and sit behind the salvage shelves and listen to her talk in the tongue of my dead god, and I hated that wanting it felt like grief, because grief is a debt and I retired from debts when I hung up my sword.

Then Boots came down the stairs.

Boots is what we call Second Enforcer Aldric Vey, on account of his habit of announcing himself by sound alone—he wants you afraid before he arrives, says fear is cheaper than force. He leaned on my desk with the easy menace of a man who's never once been surprised, and he said, casual as weather:

"Odd night. Vault's been singing."

"Old buildings settle," I said.

"Not like this." He picked up my pen, turned it over, set it down. "Harbormaster's men pulled a body out of the shallows at low tide. Alive. Small. Girl-child. Slipped them at the customs gate and vanished toward the Guild quarter." He smiled at me with all the warmth of a drawn blade. "Corvane wants her found before morning. Says there's a buyer already asking."

My blood went very quiet and very cold, the way it does before a fight.

"A buyer," I said. "For a child."

"For whatever the child *is*," Boots said, and pushed off from my desk. "You hear anything, keeper, you log it. That's what you're for."

He went back up the stairs, whistling, and I sat in the dark with my private ledger open and my heart doing arithmetic it didn't like.

A buyer already asking. Before they'd even found her. Which meant someone knew what she was—which meant someone had *sent* her, or lost her, or sold her once already—and the guild intended to complete the sale by morning.

Behind me, faint through two floors of stone, the vault began to hum again. Soft. Patient. Like something waiting to be remembered.

I closed my ledger, and I went upstairs to stand between a child and everything I serve.
