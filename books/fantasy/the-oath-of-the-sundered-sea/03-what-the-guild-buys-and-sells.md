## 3. What the Guild Buys and Sells

The Vesperline Guild keeps three sets of books, and I keep all of them.

There is the public ledger, which is a work of fiction so polished it could hang in a gallery: bottled storms at honest prices, borrowed years with fair interest, nothing in the inventory column that would make a magistrate spit. There is the true ledger, locked behind the vault door, where we record what we actually move—names that were never spoken, griefs sold by the pound, the occasional saint's knucklebone—and which is only fiction in the sense that any honest confession is. And then there is mine. The private one. The third set.

Every guild has a third set. Most masters don't know they have one, because most book-keepers are honest, and honesty is just another word for being bad at your job. I am very good at my job. Eleven years of being very good at my job has taught me that the difference between the second ledger and the third is not what gets written down. It's who reads it.

The second ledger belongs to Corvane. The third belongs to me, and last night I used it to hide a child from my employer, which means as of dawn I am either a traitor or an auditor. With the Vesperline, those are frequently the same thing.

* * *

I went up to the garret before the morning shift arrived, because the child was still there, asleep in the space between the storm-jars where the light comes through green as underwater. She'd curled around one of the empty bottles like it was warm. Seawater had dried on her in white lines, salt mapped across her arms like the borders of some country cartographers refuse to draw.

She was talking in her sleep. Not words. Older than words—the sound came out of her the way heat comes off a stone at dusk, without effort, without permission. The storm-jars nearest her were humming in answer. Two of them had fogged over from the inside.

I did not touch her. Touching her felt like touching a lit fuse to see if it was real.

Instead I went downstairs, past the sleeping enforcers, into the counting room, and I did the only thing I know how to do when the world goes wrong.

I looked it up.

* * *

Here is what you must understand about ledgers: they do not lie, but they do not tell the truth either. They tell you *what happened*, in the order it happened, priced to the copper, and they leave the meaning entirely to whoever is brave enough—or stupid enough—to read between the columns.

The imperial contract was filed under Trade Agreements, Foreign, Standing, subcategory Tribute-in-Kind. Because of course it was. Empires do not buy children; empires acquire assets. The paper was three years old. It had been renewed twice, each renewal initialed by Corvane himself in that small, precise hand I could copy in my sleep—I have, once or twice, for legitimate reasons, and I have never let myself think too hard about what makes a reason legitimate.

The item specification read:

*One (1) living key, female, under twelve years. Condition: unspoiled. Delivery: intact and willing or intact and not, per contractor's discretion.*

*Intact and not.* I read that line four times. My hands were steady. That frightened me more than shaking would have. Eleven years of keeping books will teach your hands to be steady through anything, and somewhere along the way steadiness stopped being a virtue and became a symptom.

Below the specification, in the margin, someone had noted the search history. Coastal cities first. Then inland. Then, in the last quarter-year, entries in a hand I didn't recognize: *Rumor persists of spontaneous speech events among artifacts of pre-Sundering provenance. Recommend monitoring guild holdings. Recommend monitoring guild staff.*

Monitoring guild staff. Which staff? All of us? Or—

I turned back three years, to the original filing date, and found the acquisition clause, and there it was, in black ink, in Corvane's hand, the sentence that ended the woman I had been for eleven years:

*Vesperline accepts commission. Search to be conducted discreetly, leveraging internal expertise in obscure languages and pre-Sundering materials.*

Internal expertise in obscure languages.

Me.

He hadn't sent enforcers trawling the docks for a girl who spoke a dead country. He'd sent *me*. Every translation I'd ever done, every artifact I'd catalogued, every fragment of the Sundered Sea I'd authenticated for sale—he'd been using me as a divining rod for three years, and I had held so perfectly still, been so perfectly invisible, that I had never once noticed myself being *aimed*.

* * *

You want to know how it started, this arrangement between me and Corvane? I'll tell you, since apparently we're doing confessions now.

Eleven years ago I came to his office soaked to the bone, having walked out of a war nobody won, carrying nothing but a name I no longer answered to and an oath to a god who had stopped answering me. I wanted work. Any work. Work where nobody asked questions.

Corvane was younger then. He poured me wine I didn't drink and said:

"Thackeray. Gideon Thackeray. The Thackeray who stood on the sea wall at Carrek Vale and prayed loud enough that the whole retreat heard it." He smiled, and it wasn't unkind, which was somehow worse. "Your god died, girl. Or left. Or was never there. In this business the distinction doesn't matter, because all three pay the same."

"And what business is that?"

"The business of things people can't live without and can't get honestly." He turned a ledger toward me. "I need someone who can read what's written on them. Old things carry old words, and old words carry old prices. You'll sit in a room with a lamp and make lists, and no one will ever look at you twice. That's what you want, isn't it? To not be looked at?"

It was exactly what I wanted. That should have been my first warning—that he knew it before I said it.

"You'll be invisible here," he promised. "Invisible, and indispensable. The two best disguises a person can wear."

I signed. He watched me sign. And I have often wondered, these eleven years, whether he saw what I refused to: that a person who wants above all else to be unseen will not notice the hand reaching into her pocket. That invisibility is not safety. It is just a smaller cage, painted the color of the walls.

"Invisible and indispensable," he'd said. He never told me the third thing. That invisible people are also *usable*. That the perfect tool is the one that believes it's furniture.

* * *

So there I sat at dawn, in the counting room, with the contract open in front of me and my own name implicit in its margins, and I did what I always do. I made a list.

Item: one child, mute in every language except the one that matters.
Item: one empire, patient as erosion, paying in advance.
Item: one guildmaster, who calls me girl after eleven years and has never once called me friend, and whom I realize now I defended anyway, in my heart, the way you defend a wall you live inside.
Item: one book-keeper, complicit three years running, whose meticulous records helped narrow the search from *everywhere* to *here*.

Because that was the worst of it. Not the contract. The *search log*. Dates and locations, cross-referenced against acquisitions—against acquisitions *I had catalogued*. The humming jar from Lantern Wharf. The drowned bell fragment I'd authenticated two winters ago. Each entry in the log followed within weeks of something passing under my lamp. They had fed my findings into their hunt the way you feed grain into a mill, and the flour that came out the other end was a purchase order for a little girl, and my signature was on half the shipments that pointed the way.

Ledgers record sins as neatly as debts. Nobody tells you that when you take up the pen. Nobody warns you that precision has a body count, that every number you enter truthfully can be marched off to do murder, that *accuracy* is just a word for knowing exactly what you helped happen.

I had told myself for eleven years that keeping books was neutral. Water doesn't choose which fields it floods. A scale doesn't care what it weighs. But water and scales don't know better. I did. I had known since the night a child walked in smelling of a country my god died for, and I had known it and my first instinct—the instinct eleven years of invisibility had trained into me—was to check the contract terms first and feel guilty second.

Guilty second. Efficient even in treason.

* * *

The morning bells rang. Down in the vault, the artifacts had gone quiet again, but quiet the way a held breath is quiet.

I closed the imperial file, and then, because I am who I am, I opened my private ledger and entered the day's transaction properly, because if I was going to burn my life down I was going to do it in a legible hand:

*Item: one conscience, previously dormant. Condition: activated. Value: total.*

Then I blotted it, and locked the third ledger in its hiding place, and went upstairs to decide what kind of coward I was going to be for exactly one more day—because Ryn Okonjo owed the guild nine favors, and by tonight I intended to make sure one of them was spent moving a child the guild would kill to keep.

The problem, of course, was that Ryn Okonjo hated me, hated the guild, hated the city, and hated the sea most of all—which, for a smuggler, showed a commendable instinct for self-preservation.

The other problem was standing in the doorway of the garret, awake now, watching me with eyes the grey-green of deep water, and when she saw the contract papers still in my hand she reached out—not for the papers, but for me—and pressed her small salt-stiff fingers against my wrist, right over the pulse, and spoke one syllable of that drowned language.

Just one.

And every storm-jar in the Vesperline Guild sang.
