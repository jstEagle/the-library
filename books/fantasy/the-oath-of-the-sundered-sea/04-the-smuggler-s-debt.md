## 4. The Smuggler's Debt

There are nine favors I owe the Vesperline Guild, and I have spent four years making sure I never have to think about them.

This is harder than it sounds. The guild doesn't send letters about its debts. Letters can be burned. No—the debts live in the ledger, and the ledger lives in the head of a gray little woman named Gideon Thackeray, who has the emotional range of a filing cabinet and the memory of God on a grudge day. Every few months she sends me a slip of paper with no words on it at all. Just the guild's seal, pressed in wax the color of an old bruise. It means: *We remember. Do you?*

I keep every one of those slips in a tin under my bunk. I tell myself it's so I'll know when they're coming for me. That is a lie I find comforting, which is the best kind of lie.

So when I came down the gangplank of the *Salt-Luck* that gray morning—hold full of bottled weather, none of it legal, all of it accounted for—and saw her waiting at the foot of the pier, hands folded like a woman attending her own funeral, I knew two things. One: today was a slip-of-paper day. Two: I should have sailed for the southern islands last week, consequences be damned.

"Ryn Okonjo," she said, by way of hello.

"Gideon Thackeray," I said. "You look terrible. Has anyone ever told you that grief ages people?"

"I'm not grieving."

"You will be if you stand there much longer. You're blocking my dock."

She didn't move. Of course she didn't. Moving would require admitting the world had made an unexpected demand of her, and Gideon Thackeray treats unexpected demands the way a stone treats rain.

I want to say, before this goes any further, that I knew what she was. Not the specifics—nobody knows the specifics—but the shape of it. There are maybe thirty people in this city who took vows to gods that stopped answering, and we can smell each other. It's something in how you hold your hands. People whose prayers still work hold their hands open. People like us hold them closed, around nothing, like fists that forgot to unclench after the funeral.

Gideon held her hands closed. Always.

"The favor," she said.

"Not even a greeting? Not even a small bribe? A pastry, perhaps. I respond well to pastries."

"Favor one of nine," she said. "I need your ship tonight. Quietly. Out through the Lantern Gate, no manifest, no questions from the harbormaster—I've already handled the harbormaster—and a passenger delivered somewhere you choose, somewhere the guild doesn't own the water."

That stopped me, and I resented it. Nobody says *somewhere the guild doesn't own the water* unless they mean *the guild is hunting us*, and nobody means *us* unless they've already decided I'm in it.

"Passenger," I said. "What kind of passenger weighs enough to spend a favor on?"

"A child."

"A child." I looked past her shoulder at the fog coming off the harbor, buying myself a moment, because my face is a bad liar and I needed the time. "Gideon. People don't spend nine-favor currency on children. People sell children. That's usually why there's a market for them. What am I not being told?"

For a long moment she said nothing, and I watched her do the thing she does—that terrible arithmetic behind her eyes, weighing what a truth costs against what a silence costs, and hating both prices. I'd seen her do it across negotiation tables. I'd seen her do it to smugglers who thought they were clever. I had never once seen it aimed at me like this: not as a weapon, but as a confession trying to find a door.

"She speaks a dead language," Gideon said finally. "The language of the Sundered Sea. And the guild has sold her to the empire."

*Well,* I thought. *Damn.*

Everyone knows the Sundered Sea story. It's the story mothers use to explain why the tide comes up wrong some nights—a country that stood where the deep water stands now, drowned whole in a war nobody won, defended by a god who died doing it. Fishermen won't net those waters. Sailors won't sing in them. And here was the guild's own ledger-keeper telling me the empire had found a piece of that drowned country walking around with a heartbeat, and had bought it like a bolt of cloth.

"And you're stealing their purchase," I said.

"I'm correcting an entry," she said, and then, quieter, in a voice I suspect she hadn't used in years: "Yes. I'm stealing her."

"Why?" It wasn't hostility. I genuinely wanted to know. People like Gideon don't break pattern for sentiment; sentiment is a rounding error they've trained themselves out of. Something had to have cracked, and cracked things interest me, because I am one.

She looked at me for a long time. Then she said, "Because she prays in a language I know, and I have spent eleven years praying into a silence, and I could not watch her be sold into another one." She paused. "That is more truth than I intended to give you. Don't spend it."

"Too late. I'm spending it right now." I rubbed my jaw. "One question. If I say no—if I tell you to take your slip of paper and file it—you'll walk away? Or will there be enforcers behind that fish cart?"

"There are no enforcers," she said. "If you refuse, I will owe you an apology, and I will go find a stupider smuggler."

"That's the nicest thing anyone's said to me all year." I looked at my ship. The *Salt-Luck* is a ugly, loyal old tub, patched in six kinds of wood and one kind of stubbornness, and she has carried worse cargo than hope, though not often. "Where's the child now?"

"Somewhere safe."

"Define safe."

"Define safe," she repeated, "in a city where the guild owns the harbormasters, the lamplighters, and half the beggars, and the empire owns the rest." She almost smiled. Almost. It was like watching a door open a crack onto a room where someone had died. "Safe is a relative term, Ryn. I need absolute terms. That's why I need your boat."

* * *

The child's name—well. The child had no name I could pronounce, and Gideon called her nothing at all, just *she*, in a careful voice, like a man walking around a hole in the floor. I called her Sprat, because she was small and wet-eyed and looked at me like she was deciding whether I was edible, and because naming things is how smugglers pretend the world isn't going to take them away.

She came aboard after dark, wrapped in Gideon's own coat, which told me more than any confession. Ledger-keepers do not give up their coats. Their coats are armor, uniform, and hiding place all at once. She'd given hers to a mute girl off the street, and she hadn't even noticed herself doing it. I noticed. Noticing is my trade.

Sprat sat on my deck crates while we waited for the tide, and Gideon sat beside her, and the two of them did something I have never seen done on my ship or any ship: they were silent together without it being a fight. The girl's hands moved sometimes—small, quick shapes in the air, a language of fingers—and Gideon watched them the way starving men watch bread.

"What is she saying?" I asked.

"I don't know most of it. Some of it I know." Gideon's jaw worked. "It's the grammar of a prayer. Everything she signs comes out shaped like a prayer. Even asking for water. Especially asking for water."

"That's grim."

"That's faith," Gideon said. "It gets into the grammar. You never get it out again. Ask me how I know."

I didn't ask. Instead I checked the lines, which is what I do instead of having feelings, and that was when Sprat tugged my sleeve.

I looked down. She pointed at my chest—at the little tin charm I wear under my shirt, the one with the broken circle scratched on it, the mark of an oath that got no answer. Then she pointed at Gideon. Then back at me. Her expression was very grave, the way only children and judges can manage, and she made a small motion with both hands: closed fists, opening slowly, letting go of nothing.

Then she patted my arm twice, kindly, the way you pat a dog that's been left out in the rain.

"What did she—" I started.

"She said you and I are the same," Gideon said quietly. "And then she told us to put it down."

"She told us to—" I laughed, and it came out wrong, too sharp. "Kid's known me ten minutes."

"She's known oath-breakers her whole life," Gideon said. "Her whole country was one."

I turned away and pretended the mooring line needed my full attention, which it did not, and I felt her small eyes on my back the whole time, patient as tide. Broken circle. Eleven years, for Gideon; seven for me, though mine broke faster and uglier, and I don't pray anymore, I just flinch in the direction prayers used to go. And some soaked little ghost from a drowned nation had looked at the pair of us for ten minutes and diagnosed the whole disease, prescribed letting go, and patted me like a wet dog.

Children should come with warnings.

* * *

We cleared the Lantern Gate at slack tide, running dark, and the city fell away behind us in a smear of yellow lamps and debt. Gideon stood at the rail the whole way out, watching it go, and I gave her the courtesy of pretending not to see that she was watching it the way you watch a house burn that you set fire to yourself.

"Harbor ahead," I said, an hour later. "Little cove, island fishing town, guild presence zero. There's a woman there named Maren who owes me, who takes in strays and asks nothing, mostly because she can't hear the answers. Your girl will be salt-fed and boring within a month. Boring is the safest thing a person can be."

Gideon nodded. But she didn't answer, and I've run enough cargoes to know what a non-answer sounds like.

"What," I said.

"She shouldn't be hidden," Gideon said slowly. "She should be heard. Sable Vane used to sail the Sundered Sea. He's at Lantern Wharf, if he's alive, and he owes—" she stopped.

"Owes what?"

"Owes my god a debt," Gideon said, "and my god is in no position to collect. Which makes it mine."

I stared at her. "You're not taking her to safety. You're taking her to a liar."

"Sable Vane is the finest liar on this coast," Gideon agreed. "Which means he's the only man alive who might accidentally tell the truth. Liars always do, eventually—it's the one expense they can't avoid forever." She turned to me, and in the dark her eyes had something in them I hadn't seen before, and it took me a stupidly long time to name it, because I'd never seen it on her.

Hope. Badly maintained, badly repaired, leaking hope.

"Lantern Wharf," I said, "is three days back the way we came, directly through waters the empire patrols since they bought your girl. You understand that."

"Yes."

"And every league we sail toward that old fraud, we're sailing deeper into the guild's reach. They'll have your movements reconstructed by morning. You kept records, didn't you? You record everything."

Something crossed her face then—fast, gone, like a fish under ice. "Everything," she said.

"Wonderful. So we're not smuggling a child anymore. We're outrunning a bookkeeping system, and I'm crewed by the woman who built it." I spat over the rail, ceremonially, for luck. "Fine. Fine! Lantern Wharf. But hear me, Thackeray: this is favor one of nine, and if the empire's ships catch us, I am throwing you overboard and telling them the kid stowed away."

"Understood."

"And if Vane laughs at me even once—"

"He'll laugh constantly. It's how he breathes."

"—then I'm keeping the deposit." I turned the wheel, and the *Salt-Luck* groaned around toward Lantern Wharf like an old dog asked to fetch the same stick twice.

Behind us, Sprat was asleep in a coil of rope, and as I watched, her hands kept moving in her sleep—small, quick shapes, opening and closing, opening and closing. Praying, even unconscious. Even dreaming.

Gideon watched them too. Neither of us said anything.

Three days later, tied up at the rotting pilings of Lantern Wharf, with the fog coming in thick as wool, an ancient man in a coat worth more than my ship leaned over the rail above us, looked down at the child, at Gideon, at me—and began to laugh.

He did not stop laughing for some time.

His hands, I noticed, were shaking.
