## 5. The Old Liar of Lantern Wharf

*Gideon*

Lantern Wharf earned its name the way most places earn their names: by accident, and then by stubborn insistence on the lie. There were lanterns, yes—dozens of them, strung between the pilings like fruit gone rotten—but none of them were lit after dark, because light cost coin and coin was the one thing Lantern Wharf's residents had learned to keep closer than their lives. What you got instead was a thousand people who knew how to move in the dark without bumping into each other, which is its own kind of civilization, and in my experience a more honest one than most.

Sable Vane lived at the end of it, in a houseboat that had been sinking so gracefully for so long that it had become less a vessel than an argument with the water, conducted at a very slow pace, which the water was losing.

Ryn stopped at the top of the gangplank. "I hate this part."

"You've met him before?"

"Everyone who's ever bought anything impossible has met him before. He sold me my first ship." She spat over the rail, ceremonially. "It sank in harbor."

"Did he tell you it would?"

"He told me it *could*. Said it was important that I know that going in." Ryn's jaw did the thing it did when she was deciding whether to be angry or amused. "Worst part is he was right to sell it to me anyway. I learned more from that wreck than I'd have learned from ten good ships."

"That," said Sable Vane, from directly behind us, "is the only five-star review I have ever received, and I intend to have it engraved."

I will admit I made a small noise. Ryn denies making any noise at all, but she was holding her knife, so.

He was old the way driftwood is old—not bent, exactly, but shaped entirely by weather, every soft part long since gone. His eyes were the pale gray of sea glass, and his hands, when they emerged from his sleeves, were spotted and steady as a surgeon's. He looked at me for a long moment. Then he looked at the child asleep against my chest, wrapped in Ryn's spare coat, and something moved across his face like weather over open water.

"Well," he said. "Come in before the boat notices you're heavy."

The inside of the houseboat smelled of tea, tar, and forty years of secrets steeped into the wood. He cleared a bench with one sweep of his arm, scattering what looked like the collected correspondence of three dead admirals, and poured tea into cups that matched none of each other.

"So," he said. "Gideon Thackeray. Ledger-keeper of the Vesperline. Eleven years invisible. And now here you are at the end of my plank with a sleeping girl who smells—" he closed his eyes and breathed in through his nose, and his whole face changed, aged backward twenty years, then forward thirty, "—who smells like home."

"You've been there," I said. "The Sundered Sea."

"I've been *everywhere*, dear. That's not the question you came to ask." He set down his cup. "You came to ask if the child is what you think she is. So ask."

"The language she speaks. The guild's oldest artifacts hum when she talks. I recognized one word of it." I kept my voice level. Ledger-keeper's trick: put everything in columns, never let them see the totals. "It's the last prayer I ever spoke aloud."

Sable Vane laughed.

It was a good laugh—I want to be clear about that. Warm, rolling, generous, the laugh of a man who has practiced delight until it became genuine. It went on rather longer than the joke required, given that there hadn't really been a joke. Ryn shifted beside me. The child stirred and slept on.

"Oh, that's *perfect*," he said, wiping his eye. "Eleven years of silence, and the god answers you through a drowned child. That's the funniest thing the divine has done since the Flood, and the Flood was also very funny if you had the right seat."

"You knew my god."

"I owed her money." He said it lightly, but I watched his hands wrap around his teacup, and I watched the knuckles go white, and I thought: *there you are.* "A debt of passage, technically. She carried me out of the Sundered Sea when it was still a country with roads. I was nineteen and I had stolen something I should not have stolen, from someone I should not have stolen from, and she stood on the deck of a ship called the *Mercy of Small Hours* and asked me what I would give for the crossing."

"What did you say?"

"I said anything. Which is what everyone says, and which is why gods should stop accepting it." He sipped his tea. "She said, *then live long enough to help whoever comes asking next.* And I said, *that's not anything, that's just patience,* and she said, *yes, that's what anything means.*"

Ryn made a low sound in her throat. I understood it. It's the sound of hearing your own life described by a stranger.

"She's dead," I said. "Silent eleven years. Dead or silent; I've stopped pretending I know the difference."

"And yet." He gestured with his chin at the sleeping child. "And yet."

* * *

*Ryn*

Here's what nobody tells you about old men who laugh all the time: you learn to read their hands instead. Words are negotiable. Fingers don't lie unless they've had sixty years of practice, and even then they get tired.

Sable Vane's fingers were tired. The whole interview he'd been juggling his cup like a juggler who knows the trick too well, spinning it, tapping it, and every time the conversation touched the Sundered Sea—or the god, or the word *home*—his grip tightened like the cup might jump overboard.

Me, I was doing arithmetic. Nine favors owed to the Vesperline, minus the one Gideon had already spent getting me and this cargo out of the city, equals eight, and the price of every one of those eight was about to go up depending on what the old liar said next. I don't trust gods, guilds, or information-brokers, in ascending order of expense. But I'd seen the artifacts hum. I'd felt it in my own teeth, standing in that back room, like a tuning fork struck against the world. You can't buy that. Believe me—the Vesperline has tried.

"So confirm it," Gideon said. "Is she the last?"

Sable set the cup down. Carefully. Like setting down something breakable that wasn't the cup.

"There were four hundred speakers when the empire came," he said. "There were maybe sixty when the waters finished the work. I found records, over the years—scattered, always scattered, the empire was thorough but the sea was thoro-er. A woman in the Ashfang islands, died thirty years ago. Two brothers on a whaling crew, lost with their ship. And rumors. Always rumors. Rumors are how the dead keep talking." He looked at the child for a long time. "This is the first one that breathes."

"And the door."

"Ah. The door." He laughed again—that big warm laugh—and his hand found the edge of the table and held on. "You know what I love about the young? You say *the door* like it's a thing with hinges. The Sundered Sea didn't sink, dear heart. It was *closed*. There's a difference between drowning and being shut away, and the empire has spent two hundred years hoping everyone forgets there's a difference. A country doesn't need a key. A prison does."

Gideon's face did nothing at all. I was starting to understand that this was how she screamed.

"So she's a map," I said.

"She's a *mouth*." He said it almost gently, and almost sadly, and both of those almosts were real. "The last native speaker. Not a person who knows where the door is—a person whose *voice* the door remembers. You could torture the location out of a guide. You cannot torture a language out of a throat and expect it to still work. Which means—" he spread his spotted hands, "—that anyone who wants to use her has to keep her alive and willing. And the empire has never once in its history required the second condition. They'll settle for alive."

The child woke then. I don't know what woke her—the name of dying empires, probably, which would wake anybody—but she sat up in the coat, looked around the little cabin, and fixed those dark eyes on Sable Vane.

And she spoke.

One word. Soft. The same sound I'd felt in my teeth back at the guild, except now it wasn't humming through artifacts, it was hanging in the air of a sinking houseboat at the ass-end of Lantern Wharf, and the tea in our cups shivered, and the lanterns outside—unlit, unlit for years, I'd stake my ship on it—flickered once in their glass.

Sable Vane answered her.

In the same tongue. Fluent, worn smooth, a sailor's accent on a drowned country's words. Three syllables, four, five, and the child's face opened like a window, and she scrambled off the bench and crossed the floor and took his old spotted hand in both of hers.

I looked at Gideon. Gideon was looking at the old man's hands, and her eyes were wet, and she wasn't hiding it, which scared me more than anything else that night.

* * *

*Gideon*

He told her, in her own language, something that made her smile, and then he turned to me and translated, because apparently courtesy extends even to the end of the world:

"I said, *you're late, but you came, which is better than punctual.*"

"What else did she say? What did you say back?" My voice cracked in the middle. Eleven years of ledger-keeping, and one old man undoes the whole binding in an evening. "What word did she use? At the start?"

"*Veshari.*" He pronounced it the way you'd handle a bird with a broken wing. "It means—it has no clean translation. Closest is *the one who waits at the door.* It's what her people called the god. Or—" he paused, and his hands, resting flat on the table, trembled, just once, like a struck bell deciding whether to ring, "—or what the god was called *by the ones she carried out.* The ones who waited on this side. The ones who lived."

Outside, somewhere down the wharf, a dog started barking. Then another. Then a whistle—two notes, short-short, the Vesperline enforcers' call-sign, the sound I had made myself a hundred times to signal *found them.*

Ryn was on her feet before the echo died. Sable Vane was laughing again, big and bright and terrible, hauling open a hatch in his floorboards while the laughter shook him, and I finally understood that the laugh wasn't joy and it wasn't madness. It was a man laughing at a debt come due at last, sixty years late, with interest.

"They're early," he said cheerfully, throwing bundles down into the dark. "I do love an audience. Down the ladder, children—there's a tunnel under this boat older than the empire, and I owe a dead god a favor, and we are going to pay it *in full.*"
