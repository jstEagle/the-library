## 6. Hunted by the Book

I have always believed that a well-kept ledger is a form of prayer. Every entry balanced, every debt named, nothing hidden—except, of course, the things I hid in plain sight for eleven years, tucked between the bottled storms and the borrowed years like a knife folded into a psalter.

It turns out a ledger is also a confession. You just need someone patient enough to read it aloud.

We learned this at Lantern Wharf, at roughly the hour when honest people are asleep and I was doing what I do best: recording things.

* * *

Two days out from Sable Vane's parlor, we had taken rooms above a chandlery that smelled of tar and old rope, which is to say it smelled like every place Ryn Okonjo has ever slept and felt like home to her in a way that visibly unsettled her.

The child—I had started thinking of her as *the child*, because naming her felt like an act of ownership, and I had seen where ownership led in my ledgers—sat on the floor with a stub of charcoal and drew the sea. Not our sea. A sea with doors in it. She drew them over and over, patient as a monk illuminating scripture, and every time she finished one she would look up at me as though waiting for me to say something true.

I am bad at saying things true. It is why I keep ledgers.

"She's been at that since dawn," Ryn said, leaning in the doorway with a bowl of something gray that the chandlery's keeper called stew. "You going to tell me what Sable told you, or are you planning to carry it around inside you until it kills you? Because I've known you three days, Thackeray, and I can already tell you'd die of information before you'd die of a blade."

"Sable says she's the last speaker," I said. "Of the Sundered tongue."

"I got that much from all the laughing."

"And he says she isn't a key." I watched the child draw another door. "He says she's a map. That the language doesn't *describe* the way out—it *is* the way out. The door doesn't open, Ryn. It answers."

Ryn chewed her gray stew thoughtfully. "That's either very beautiful or very useless, depending entirely on whether anyone's asking questions."

"That's what frightens me. Everyone who wants her is holding the wrong question."

I said it lightly. Wryly. In the tone I use when I am terrified, which is a skill eleven years of guild work teaches you—the ability to sound bored while your soul climbs out through your teeth.

What I did not say was what else Sable had told me, after the laughing stopped and his hands began their small betrayals against the table. He had asked to see my ledger. I had shown him, because old liars are the only people alive I trust with paper. And he had gone quiet—not the theatrical quiet he sold to customers, but a real one—and touched the column where I recorded the guild's intake of seawater samples, dates, tides, coordinates.

"Thackeray," he said, "you wrote down everything. You always do. Which means Corvane doesn't need to find you." He tapped the page twice. "She just needs to read you."

* * *

They came at slack tide, which any competent enforcer knows is the worst time to escape by boat, and which means someone at the Vesperline had done their reading too.

I knew before they arrived. That is the ugly truth of it. I knew because of the gulls.

Lantern Wharf's gulls are professional beggars; they mob any crate, any hand, any hope of fish. That evening they were gone—all of them, wheeling far out over the black water, silent. Animals leave before violence the way sensible clerks resign before an audit.

"Ryn," I said. "Boat. Now."

She didn't ask questions. Three days of provisional trust, and already she moved when I said move, which frightened me more than the gulls did, because trust is just a debt with better manners.

The child came without being carried, which was its own small miracle—she had watched us both all day with those sea-colored eyes, and I think she understood the grammar of urgency in any language. She took my hand. Her palm was cold and smelled faintly of deep water, of a country under the country.

We made the end of the wharf. Ryn's boat—a smuggler's skiff named, with the sentimentality common to her profession, *Debt Repaid*—was tied at the pilings, low in the water, ready.

And then lanterns bloomed along the wharf behind us. Six of them. Then nine. The light came toward us with the unhurried confidence of people who have already read the ending.

"Gideon Thackeray," said a voice I knew.

Maren Solace. Senior enforcer. I had entered her wages into the ledgers for nine years. I knew the exact figure, the exact date of every raise, and once, memorably, the exact cost of the funeral flowers she bought for a colleague, because she'd expensed them and I'd approved them. She was not cruel. She was precise. We had always gotten along.

"You're holding my ledger," I called back, because if I could keep my voice dry then the rest of me might follow.

"Master Corvane has it," Maren said, stopping twenty feet away, lantern raised. The other enforcers fanned out with the ease of long practice. "It's remarkable reading, Gideon. You should be proud. The ink deliveries to Sable Vane's district. The ferry fares on days you claimed to be inventorying the cellar. The purchase of children's clothes—children's clothes, Gideon, from a woman whose entire life was built on never buying anything that fit two sizes of future."

Every entry. Every careful, honest, meticulous entry. My one virtue, laid out in columns, testifying against me.

"I taught half these clerks their hands," I said. "You're reading my handwriting like scripture, Maren."

"We read it like a map," she said simply. "Hand over the girl. Master Corvane will let the rest go. She said—" and here Maren hesitated, and I understood that Corvane had written her lines personally, and that Maren hated delivering them, "—she said to tell you that the door of the guildhouse is still open. That you were the best thing the ledgers ever kept."

There it was. Eleven years of invisibility, and in the end I was *kept*. An entry. An asset with sentimental value.

"The answer's no," I said.

"I know," Maren said, and sounded almost relieved, and drew her blade.

* * *

I will not make the fight more than it was. Enforcers are trained; we were three, and one of us was a child, and one of us was me, which statistically counts for less. What saved us was the wharf itself, slick with old fish-gut and fog, and Ryn Okonjo's particular genius, which is knowing exactly how much weight a rotten piling can bear before it betrays you. She'd chosen the berth herself. Two of the enforcers went into the water with a crash that swallowed their shouts.

But Maren was fast, and she wasn't looking at me. She was looking past me, at the child, because orders are orders and the empire pays for the key, not the clerk.

I saw the blade turn. I saw the arc of it. I was already moving and I already knew I was too slow—eleven years of desk work, Thackeray, your reflexes are filed somewhere with the archived invoices—

Ryn got there first.

She took the blade across the shoulder, high, the kind of wound that bleeds theatrically and kills only if you're stupid about it, and she swore at Maren in four languages, two of them possibly invented, and shoved the child toward me and the boat in the same motion.

"*Go,*" she snarled—at me, at everyone, at the general injustice of the world.

I cut the mooring line with the little ledger-knife I'd carried for eleven years, the one detail of my life that had never once betrayed me, and the *Debt Repaid* slid off the pilings into the dark.

Behind us, Maren stood at the wharf's edge, lantern high, not pursuing. Watching. And I swear on every balanced column I ever kept that she looked *sad*—sad the way Corvane must look when she signs away a life, sad the way I used to look at debts I couldn't collect.

"Gideon!" Maren called across the water, one last time. "She'll send ships! She has your whole book!"

I know, I wanted to say. I wrote it myself.

* * *

By dawn we were miles out, hulled in gray fog, and Ryn sat propped against the mast while I stitched her shoulder with sail-thread and hands that did not shake, because I refuse.

"You could've let her take me," Ryn said, watching me work. "Cheaper. Cleaner. One less impossible favor owed."

"Yes, but then I'd have to enter you in my ledger as a loss," I said. "I hate writing losses."

"You don't even like me."

"I'm warming."

She laughed, then hissed, then laughed again. The child sat between us with her charcoal and drew the boat we were in, and then drew the boats that would follow us—six of them, seven—and held the drawing up so I could see it clearly, because apparently the drowned tongue was not the only language she spoke fluently.

Ships. Corvane would send ships, and she would navigate by my own neat hand, my ferry fares and ink purchases, my entire invisible life turned inside out and read aloud.

So I did the hardest arithmetic of my career. I took out my ledger—my ledger, eleven years of entries, the record of everything I'd hidden and everything I'd survived—and I weighed it in my hands like a heart on a scale.

Then I fed it to the brazier page by page.

It burned beautifully. Of course it did. I buy good paper.

Ryn watched me with something that was almost respect. "Feels like losing a limb?"

"Worse. Feels like losing a witness." I watched the last page curl—the god's name, written there in the margin where no auditor would ever think to look, in the old tongue the child had spoken at our door. As it blackened I realized I didn't need the paper anymore. I had spent eleven years writing prayers down instead of saying them, filing my faith where it couldn't be audited, and the fire took that excuse with it.

The child reached out and touched my wrist, once, gently, the way you touch something that has finally stopped pretending.

Ahead of us the fog was thinning, and beneath it, where no chart agreed there should be anything at all, the water was beginning to rise in shapes.
