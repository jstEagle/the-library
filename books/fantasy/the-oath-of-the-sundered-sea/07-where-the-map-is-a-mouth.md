## 7. Where the Map Is a Mouth

The boat has a name, but nobody says it. The woman with the tired eyes calls it *the boat*, and the smuggler calls it worse things when the wind misbehaves, which is often. The child has decided this is because sailors are like gulls: they are loudest about the things they love.

She has been counting days by the way the light comes through her eyelids. Four, maybe five. She is not sure. Time on the water moves like something swimming — you feel it pass but you cannot hold its shape. On land, time had been a corridor of doors she was not allowed to open. Here it is just weather.

She does not speak. This is not the same as being unable to. She has words; she has a whole country of them, folded up small and carried somewhere behind her ribs where the salt got in and never left. But the words here are wrong-shaped. When she tried one once — the first night, in the doorway that smelled of lamp oil and fear — the lamps had hummed, and the tall man with the rings had looked at her the way people look at a door that opens where no door should be. So she put the word back. She has been putting words back ever since, one by one, like coins into a purse she is afraid to spend.

The woman — Gideon, though the child learned the name from the smuggler's mouth, not hers — watches her constantly. Not the way the guild people watched, which was weighing. The way you watch a fire you have decided to sit beside anyway. The child has decided this means Gideon is afraid of her and staying regardless, and she finds she likes this better than kindness. Kindness can be taken back. Fear that stays is almost a promise.

* * *

On the fifth day the sea changes color.

The child notices first, because of course she does; it is her sea, or the ghost of it, or the grave of it, whichever name the land-people use for water that used to be a country. It goes from the gray-green of ordinary ocean to a blue so deep it looks deliberate, like someone thought hard about this exact shade before the world was finished. Below the hull, shapes move. Not fish. Fish do not move in lines that look like writing.

The old liar sees it too. Sable — the child has catalogued him under *the one who laughs at everything, especially sad things* — stops laughing mid-sound, which on Sable is the equivalent of another man screaming. He grips the rail with both hands and his knuckles go the color of old paper.

"Well," he says, to no one, to the sea, to a god that may or may not be listening. "Well, well."

Ryn swears in three languages and adjusts the sail. Gideon comes to stand beside the child at the bow, close but not touching, the way she always stands, and says nothing, because Gideon's silences are load-bearing.

The child looks down into the deliberate blue and feels something behind her ribs shift. The folded country of her words unfolds a little. Not all the way. Just one corner, like a hand opening enough to show it holds nothing dangerous.

The sea rises in a shape.

It is not a wave. Waves do not have intention. This is more like a sentence — a long swell that lifts on the port side and holds there, curved, patient, and the foam along its crest breaks into patterns that repeat, and the patterns mean something, and the meaning is: *you are late.*

The child laughs. It startles everyone, including her. She has not laughed since the water came, since the towers went down singing, since the men in imperial armor taught her that running is a skill you practice until it is your whole self. But the sea just scolded her, the way her grandmother used to scold her for dawdling at the tide-pools, and some things survive drowning.

Gideon's head turns slowly. "What," she says. Not a question. A request. The difference matters to her; the child has noticed that Gideon only asks questions she is prepared to survive the answers to.

*You're late,* the sea says again, and this time the child lets one word out through her mouth instead of keeping it behind her ribs. It is not a word in any language on this boat. It comes out sounding like water pulling back from sand, like the pause between a wave and the next wave, and when it leaves her the whole surface of the deliberate blue shivers, and every rope on the ship hums one low note, and Ryn's compass needle spins twice and points at the child.

Everyone stares.

The child shrugs. She has learned shrugging from watching Ryn, who uses it to mean everything from *not my fault* to *we are probably going to die* to *nice weather.* It is a very efficient language. She approves of efficiency.

* * *

That night she cannot sleep, and she stops pretending to try.

The others sleep badly anyway — Gideon sits up against the mast with her ledger-less hands empty in her lap, because she burned the book, the child knows, she heard them talking about it, the book that knew everything Gideon did, burned page by page in a barrel on Lantern Wharf while Gideon watched with a face like someone amputating their own shadow. The child thinks about that a lot. She has never owned anything that knew her that well. She wonders if it hurt, and she suspects yes, and she suspects Gideon would say no, and she suspects both of those things are true at once, which is the most land-person thing the child has ever encountered.

The stars over the drowned coast are wrong. She knew they would be. Her people navigated by different ones — the ones that sank, the ones that only shine now underwater, if light still works down there, if anything works down there except memory. She lies in the bottom of the boat and looks up at the substitute stars, the borrowed ones, and she is homesick for a sky.

And then, because there is no one awake to be frightened by it, she begins to sing.

Quietly. Under the wind. The song is a lullaby, or it was, before it became an elegy, before it became the last copy of itself in the world. It is about a door. Most of her people's songs are about doors; they were a country of harbors and thresholds, and they believed every ending was just a door facing the wrong direction. The lullaby says: *sleep, the tide will carry what the current keeps, and what the current keeps is never lost, only waiting.*

The sea answers.

There is no other word for it. The child sings the second verse and the water around the hull begins to glow — faint, green-gold, like lamplight through bottle glass — and shapes rise beneath the glow, slow and enormous and gentle. She does not see them clearly. She does not need to. She knows them the way you know the melody of a song you haven't heard since childhood: rooftops. Streets. The arches of a harbor gate, standing upright on the seafloor two hundred fathoms down, crowned in coral, patient as furniture in a house where everyone has gone out.

The door is down there. The door was always down there. And it is not locked, and it is not broken, and it is not waiting for a key.

It is waiting for someone to knock correctly.

The child stops singing. The glow fades reluctantly, like a held breath finally let go. Her eyes are wet, which confuses her, because she thought she had used up all of that months ago, in a cellar, in the dark, learning silence the hard way.

Above her, very quietly, Gideon says: "I'm sorry."

The child didn't know she was awake. Land-people breathe so loudly; how did she miss it? She turns. Gideon's face in the dark is doing something complicated — the child reads faces the way other people read weather, and this one has storm systems in it.

"I had a plan," Gideon says. It sounds like a confession. "Get you to the coast. Get you to the door. Open it." She stops. Starts again, slower, the way people talk when they are taking apart something they built and finding it rotten in the middle. "I had a plan for the door. I didn't have a plan for you."

The child considers this. Then she reaches out — the first time she has touched anyone on purpose since the water — and takes Gideon's empty hand, the one that used to hold the book that knew her, and puts it flat against her own sternum, behind the ribs, where the folded country lives.

Gideon goes very still.

The child doesn't sing. She just breathes, deep and slow, and lets Gideon feel it: the words in there, all of them, a whole drowned nation of them, alive, waiting. Not a key. Keys are small and cold and made to fit locks other people build. This is a voice. Voices don't open doors. Voices make doors want to open.

"Oh," Gideon says. Very quietly. The way people say *oh* when a wall they've been pushing on for years turns out to be a door the whole time.

Then, far off across the deliberate blue, where the horizon should be empty, a lantern kindles. Then another. Then a string of them, white and orderly and horribly familiar — the running lights of ships, many ships, moving in formation, coming on with the patience of an empire that has waited eleven years and can wait eleven more.

Sable's voice drifts up from below, awake after all, laughing at nothing, his hands shaking on the rigging: "Company."
