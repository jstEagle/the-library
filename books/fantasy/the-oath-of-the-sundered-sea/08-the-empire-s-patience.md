## 8. The Empire's Patience

I have been Guildmaster of the Vesperline for nineteen years, and in that time I have learned that empires never knock. They arrive the way weather arrives: announced by lesser men, felt in the bones first.

The envoy's ship took three days to make harbor, which was a courtesy. Empires like you to have time to tidy up. I spent those days doing what I always do before an unpleasant meeting — reading ledgers. Not for numbers. Numbers are honest and therefore boring. I read them for handwriting.

Gideon Thackeray has kept my books for eleven years, and her hand is the most beautiful thing in this building. Every figure set down like a stone laid in a wall. Every entry dated, initialed, witnessed. If you wanted to know where she had been on any night of the last decade, you need only read her own account of it. That is the joke of Gideon, the one I have kept privately all these years: she built herself an invisibility out of precision, and precision is the opposite of invisible. Precision is a signature. She signed everything she ever did and thought no one would notice because the signature was so small.

I noticed. I am the guildmaster. Noticing is the whole of my trade.

* * *

Envoy Serath arrived at dusk with six guards who walked like furniture being moved by men pretending not to be furniture, and one clerk who carried a lacquered case the way clergy carry relics. I received them in the long room, under the guild's charter — the original, written on vellum made from something I have chosen never to ask about — and I had wine poured, because you offer an empire hospitality even when you know the hospitality is going to be itemized later as an expense against you.

"Guildmaster Corvane." Serath had a voice like dry paper folding. "You've prospered."

"We traffick in impossible things," I said. "Prosperity is the least of our impossibilities."

Something moved behind his face that might have been amusement, filed away for later use. "We are here regarding a contract. Clause forty-one." The clerk opened the lacquered case and produced a single page. I recognized the seal before I recognized the words. My seal. My purchase order, countersigned by an imperial hand.

*A living key, female, under twelve years.*

"The merchandise," Serath said, "has left your custody."

"It has been stolen," I said, "by a trusted officer. I have already begun recovery."

"I know." He let that sit. "Thackeray. Your ledger-keeper. We know her history, of course. We vetted her when you took her in. Or rather — we failed to vet her, because you asked us not to, and the Vesperline does not ask favors of the empire twice." He turned the page toward me. "She swore an oath once. To the god of the Sundered coast. Do you know what happens to oaths when their god goes silent?"

"They persist," I said. "That's rather the point of oaths."

"Yes," Serath said softly. "We're counting on it."

* * *

After they withdrew to their quarters in the guest wing — the empire always stays overnight; it likes to sleep inside your walls the way a knife sleeps inside a wound — I sat alone with the charter and did arithmetic.

Here is what the empire offered, in the language beneath the language of clause forty-one: deliver Thackeray and the child, and the Vesterline keeps its charters, its exemptions, its little sovereign darknesses. Refuse, and the guild burns. Not dramatically. Empires don't burn things dramatically anymore; they audit them. They will find irregularities in nine hundred years of ledgers, because there are always irregularities in nine hundred years of ledgers, and every bonded house we deal with will be visited, and every favor called in, until the Vesperline is a name on a list of names that used to mean something.

And here is what I did not say aloud, because I only ever say such things alone: they are right about her. Thackeray chose a person over an institution. It is the oldest treason there is, and I have executed people for less, or rather I have ruined them, which takes longer and hurts more.

But I knew her when she came to me. Eleven years ago, soaked and half-starved and lying beautifully about her references, carrying nothing but a silence where her faith used to be. I hired her because she could count. I kept her because she was loyal in the way broken things are loyal — not to me, I understood even then, but to the having of a place. A shelf to sit on. I told myself for eleven years that I loved her as a daughter, and I believed it, and believing it cost me nothing, which should have told me what kind of love it was.

A daughter you would hand to the empire for a clause in a contract is not a daughter. She is an asset with sentiment attached.

* * *

In the morning I went down to the vaults, because there was one more thing to check, and I already knew what I would find, and I checked anyway, because grief makes you do unnecessary things carefully.

Gideon's desk was immaculate. Of course it was. But the current ledger — the working book, the one with the running entries — was gone, and in its place, squared to the edge of the blotter exactly as she always left things, a single loose page in that beautiful stonemason's hand:

*Master Corvane — You taught me that every entry must balance. This one doesn't. A child for a contract. I can't make it balance. Forgive the mess. — G.T.*

No date. No initials witnessed. The first unprofessional document she ever produced, and she left it for me like a knife between ribs wrapped in apology.

I stood there a long time. Long enough that old Hesk, the vault-keeper, cleared his throat from the stairs, and I nearly took his head off for it.

Because here is the truth I will not put in any ledger: I could have warned her. Three nights before the child ever reached our door, a courier brought me word that something was moving along the coast roads, something that spoke in a dead tongue, and I read the report, and I understood what it was, and I said nothing to anyone, and I filed it. Because a key that walks into your own guildhall is worth ten times a key you buy at market, and because I have spent thirty years learning to see persons as line items, and I am very good at my work.

She is what I made her, and she betrayed me the way I taught her everything betrays: by finally being read correctly.

* * *

Serath requested a final audience at noon. No wine this time. The clerk stood by with fresh documents.

"The guild's answer," Serath said.

"You'll have Thackeray and the key," I said.

He waited. He was good at waiting. Waiting is how empires show patience, and patience is how they show contempt.

"With conditions," I said. "My conditions. One: she comes back alive. Whatever the empire intends for her — interrogation, execution, whatever liturgy you dress it in — she dies of old age, or the arrangement is void and every secret in my vaults goes to every enemy your throne has, simultaneously, in notarized copies. Two: the child goes into imperial custody alive and unmarked, and if a single hair of her head is harvested for whatever ritual your philosophers are imagining, the same applies. Three—" and here I paused, because condition three was not a condition, it was a confession, and I gave it anyway, quietly, looking him full in the face. "Three: when this is done, my name goes nowhere near it. The guild survives. Let it survive clean of me."

"That third condition costs you nothing," Serath observed.

"It costs me everything," I said. "You simply haven't read the ledger yet."

He studied me for a moment with those papery eyes, and I watched him decide — the way I have decided a thousand times over other people's lives — that I was a man doing an unpleasant duty well, and therefore safe, and therefore useful. Empires love a man who chooses the institution. They collect us. We are their favorite currency.

"We accept your terms," he said. "Ships sail tonight. Yours will lead. You know her routes."

Of course I know her routes. I know them because she logged them, because she writes everything down, because her virtue is her confession, because I trained her myself. Somewhere east of Lantern Wharf, past the deliberate blue, a small ship is sailing toward a country that doesn't exist, carrying the only two people alive who ever meant anything to me — the oath-broken woman I raised and the child I sold — and behind them, patient as tide, come the lanterns.

"Guildmaster," Serath said at the door, mild as milk, "you seem grieved."

"I'm not grieved," I told him. "I'm balanced. There's a difference, and my ledger-keeper could explain it to you, if you hadn't contracted to destroy her."

* * *

They sailed at moonrise. I stood on the guild's highest balcony and watched the formation spread white across the dark water, orderly as entries in a column, each ship a figure in someone else's arithmetic, and I found I could not look away from the empty horizon ahead of them.

Hesk came up beside me with a cloak against the sea air. Old fool. Loyal the way dogs are loyal, which is to say without reading anything.

"She was the best of them," he said. "Thackeray."

"Yes," I said.

"And you'll bring her back?"

I looked at the lanterns going east, and I thought about a page in beautiful handwriting saying *this one doesn't balance*, and I thought about thirty years of choosing the institution, every time, always, and how the institution had repaid me by making me exactly the sort of man an empire sends for.

"No, Hesk," I said, and heard my own voice go quiet as a closing book. "I'm going to bring her *home*. Pray to whatever's left listening that those turn out to be different things."

Far out, at the edge of seeing, the water off the drowned coast began — or seemed to begin, or perhaps I only needed it to — to rise in shapes.
