## 10. Ledgers Kept in Salt

The house we found was not for sale. This is the sort of thing you learn about coastal property when you travel with a smuggler: nothing worth having is ever for sale, and everything can be had anyway if you're willing to owe someone a favor.

"I'm not owing anyone anything ever again," I told Ryn.

"Then we'll owe *me*," she said, and signed the deed with a name that belonged to a man who'd died at Lantern Wharf two winters past, and that was how Gideon Thackeray, formerly of the Vesperline Guild, came to live in a fisherman's cottage on the cliffs above a drowned country, under an identity so flimsy it would have embarrassed a child. The tax assessor came once, looked at Ryn's smile, looked at Sable Vane dozing in the window with his hands folded like a bishop's, and decided the cottage belonged to whoever it obviously belonged to.

The first month, I kept waiting for the empire to come. The second month, I kept waiting for the guild to come. In the third month I caught myself standing at the window every evening like a woman watching for a lover, and I understood that what I was actually doing was waiting for my own life to end — for the other shoe, for the reckoning, for the neat column of consequences to come due.

It didn't come. That was the hardest arithmetic I ever did: nothing plus nothing, carried forward, forever.

* * *

The guild shattered the way old glass shatters — all at once, and then in slow falling pieces for months afterward. We heard it secondhand, from traders and tide-runners, each version more elaborate than the last. Corvane had been arrested by her own board of factors. No — Corvane had burned the contracts herself before the envoy could take them. No — there had been no guildmaster for years, only a chair and a voice, and the voice had finally gone quiet.

What I believe, because the ledgers taught me to believe only what has three independent sources: the door closed behind the child's people, and the Sundered Sea stopped being a claim and became a story again, and empires do not garrison stories. The envoy went home. The purchase order dissolved into the general fog of things the empire never quite said out loud. And the Vesperline Guild — which had trafficked in bottled storms and borrowed years and names that were never spoken — found that its most valuable inventory had walked through a door no one could follow her through, taking the market with her.

I heard they still sell bottled storms. I hope the storms bite.

Corvane I think about more than I want to. Not as a monster — she was never that, and it would be easier if she were. She chose the institution over the girl, the way she had chosen it over herself for forty years, and the institution ate her the way institutions do: politely, on schedule, with excellent bookkeeping. Some nights I draft letters to her in my head. *You were the closest thing to a mother the ledgers gave me,* I would write. *And you taught me that a number never lies, which is true, and also that a number never loves anyone, which is also true, and I mistook one virtue for both.* I have never sent them. There is no address for a woman who has become a warning.

* * *

The child — I will call her here what we called her at home: Merren, which is the closest our mouths could come to her real name, which was less a name than a small weather system — stayed with us through the winter and left with the spring.

Left is the wrong word. She went *through*, the way you go through a doorway into another room where your family is waiting. Twice a year, on the equinox tides, the water off the cliffs goes strange — flat as hammered pewter, then rising in shapes that are not waves — and twice a year she comes back to visit, trailing salt and songs, older each time in a way that doesn't track with the calendar. She is teaching me her language. I am a slow student. She finds this hilarious, and communicates her hilarity with great economy, since half the time I understand exactly none of what she says and must rely on tone, which is, she informs me by expression, the same problem everyone else in the world has always had with me.

She asked me once — in the old tongue, and then, seeing my face, in careful trade-speak — why I had finished the prayer. Why I had knelt. Why truth instead of oath.

I gave her the honest answer, which took me three days to find: "Because the oath was a promise made to something I couldn't hear anymore. The truth was a promise made to someone who could."

She considered this for a long time, the way children consider things, which is to say completely. Then she said a word in her own language and touched my forehead with two fingers, seawater-cold. Sable, watching from the window, laughed his laugh that means grief wearing its good coat.

"What did she say?" Ryn asked him later.

"It's untranslatable," Sable said. "Roughly: *the debt between us is paid, and now we begin something better than debts.*"

"And the word for that is—?"

"There isn't one," Sable said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

The new ledger sits on my desk in a house that smells of woodsmoke and low tide. It is a cheap thing, salt-swollen at the corners already, bought from a peddler who swore it had belonged to a customs officer who drowned, which I choose to find auspicious rather than ominous.

There are no columns in it. That took some doing. My hands wanted columns the way a retired soldier's hands want a sword — the ruling lines, the debit and the credit, the beautiful closed circuit of *what is owed*. For eleven years I believed a ledger was a moral instrument: that if you recorded everything truly, the truth would keep you safe. It nearly hanged me. My precision built the noose, entry by entry, date by date.

So this ledger has no columns. It has pages, and on the pages, names.

Merren, first page, as promised. Under her: *went home. comes back. teach her to whistle; she is terrible at it.*

Ryn Okonjo, who sleeps in the loft and pretends the nine favors are still outstanding even though we both know the count ran out somewhere around the night she took a blade meant for a child and complained about it for a month. Under her name I have written: *owes me nothing. I intend to keep it that way, which will require vigilance.*

Sable Vane, oldest liar of Lantern Wharf, who took one look at the cottage, declared it beneath him, and moved into the spare room within the hour. He tells the local children he sailed with the drowned kings. He did not sail with the drowned kings. He knew someone who delivered their wine. But he sits on the sea-wall in the evenings telling them true things wrapped in false ones, which may be the only honest way left to tell anything, and his hands have stopped shaking except when Merren visits, and then only a little, and I pretend not to see.

Under his name: *debt inherited from a dead god. Discharge status: he keeps paying it without noticing. Do not tell him.*

There are others. The factor's clerk in Greyharbor who lost his post for losing my trail and gained a fishing boat under circumstances he has wisely never investigated. The tide-runner who brings us news and takes nothing for it but supper. A list of the dead, too — Corvane among them eventually, when the letter finally came; and the enforcer whose blade found Ryn's shoulder instead of a child's heart, whom I forgive in ink if not yet entirely in fact; and at the bottom of that page, undated, the god itself, entered not as debtor or creditor but in the margin, sideways, the way you write something that belongs to no category you have rules for.

*Heard you at the end,* I wrote beside it. *Sorry about the eleven years. Sorry about the last prayer. Thank you for the silence after. It sounds different now.*

* * *

I still pray. Out of habit, I told myself at first, and then out of stubbornness, and now I've given up explaining it even to myself. Every evening I stand at the cliff edge while the light dies over the water, and I say the words my mother's mother's mothers said to a god who defended a country that is now a seafloor, and I get back —

Nothing. Exactly nothing. The same silence that answered me for eleven years in a candlelit counting-room while I wore my faith like a coat that had stopped fitting.

But it is a different nothing. That is the whole discovery of my life, and it fits on one line of a ledger with no columns: silence is not one thing. There is the silence of absence, which is a locked room. And there is the silence of company, which is two people sitting together who do not need to speak — which is Ryn and me on the sea-wall, passing a bottle back and forth, saying nothing for an hour and calling it conversation. Which is what the quiet over the Sundered Sea is now. Not a god who has gone. A god who has said goodbye, and stays anyway, the way the dead stay, the way love stays, in the register below speech.

Love against duty was never a war, I understand that now. You don't win it; nobody wins it; there is no treaty and no victory parade down Lantern Wharf. It is a choice, and then it is the same choice again tomorrow, and the day after, made in small denominations — stay at the table when the argument starts, burn the ledger, kneel to the child, say the true thing instead of the safe one. Compounding daily, like interest. Like debt. Everything I know, I learned in ledgers; I just had the sign backwards.

* * *

Last week, at the equinox tide, Merren surfaced long enough to press something into my hands and vanish again before I'd finished saying her name.

It was a page. Actual paper, watermarked with a crest I knew too well — Vesperline stock, salvaged from the wreckage of the guildhall, and written on it in her careful borrowed alphabet, in trade-speak, a single line:

*They found another one. Another speaker. In the empire's capital. She's younger than I was.*

I stood on the cliff until the stars were fully out, the page going soft with sea-damp in my hands. Behind me the cottage lamps were lit, Ryn arguing with Sable about whether eels have opinions, the kettle going, the ordinary machinery of a life running on.

Ahead of me the water lay flat and grey and listening, and somewhere south, in a city of marble and excellent bookkeeping, a child was learning to be quiet.

I went inside and opened the ledger to a fresh page.
