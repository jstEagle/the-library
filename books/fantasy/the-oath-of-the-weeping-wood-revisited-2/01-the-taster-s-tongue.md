## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The poison arrived on the third course, as poison so often did.

Orsen Kavanagh tasted it before they ever lifted the spoon—a faint bitterness beneath the honeyed quail, like a coin held too long in the mouth, like green wood burning somewhere out of sight. It sat under the glaze the way a lie sits under a smile: not hidden, merely layered. They let the spoon rest against their lower lip for the prescribed three seconds, long enough for the watching steward to see diligence performed properly, then took the full measure into their mouth and swallowed.

Around them, the Great Hall of Vellmarin went on glittering. Two hundred candles. Four hundred guests. A duke from the coast laughing at something the Lord Chancellor had not actually said, his rings catching light like fish scales. Orsen stood at the narrow side table with its white linen and its row of silver implements laid out with surgical precision, and they stood there because no one looked at them. That was the whole art of it. A food-taster must be visible enough to be trusted and forgettable enough to be dismissed, and Orsen had spent nineteen years perfecting the second part.

The bitterness bloomed at the back of their throat, spread along the jaw, settled into the roots of their teeth.

They did not reach for the antidote vial sewn into the lining of their left cuff—not yet. Timing mattered. Take it too soon and the court physicians would wonder how a mere servant recognized aconite's gentler cousin so quickly; take it too late and Orsen would spend the evening gray-lipped and sweating while some duchess complained about the wine. So they counted instead. They were good at counting. Counting was, if truth be told, the only thing they had ever been truly great at.

*Four.* The taste turned metallic.

*Nine.* A fine tremor started in their fingers; they folded their hands to hide it.

*Fifteen.* Their heartbeat developed a hitch, like a wagon wheel striking a rut.

At twenty-two, Orsen slipped behind the screen of potted orange trees at the hall's edge, thumbed open the cuff, and drank. The vial held a tincture of their own devising—goat's rue and ash-bark and one ingredient they had never told anyone about, an ingredient that came, in fact, from a place most people believed was only a story told to frighten children near the borders. The hitch in their heart smoothed out. The tremor stilled. The world regained its edges.

Nineteen years of this. Nineteen years of small doses, measured deaths, a body slowly taught to refuse what should kill it. The palace physicians called Orsen's survival luck. The kitchen staff called it a charmed tongue. Only three people alive knew the truth of what Orsen's constitution really was, and by morning there would be two fewer than that, if Orsen's arithmetic held.

They returned to their station in time to signal the third course safe. The duke from the coast ate his quail. Somewhere in the city below, someone who wanted him dead would learn tonight that patience is expensive.

* * *

The cellar beneath the Tanners' Row tenement smelled of tallow, cold stone, and—faintly, always—of sap.

Orsen descended the stairs by feel, having long since stopped needing light for the fourteen steps, and lit only the single candle permitted by custom. Its flame found the shrine.

To call it a shrine was generous, perhaps. It was a low vaulted room no larger than a rich man's tomb, its walls furred with pale mold that was not mold, its floor dusted with a fine grit that glittered when the candle moved. At the room's center stood a table of black wood that drank rather than reflected the light, and on the table, the ledgers.

Eleven volumes. Leather gone soft as old skin, spines cracked like riverbed clay. Each one taller than a prayer book and heavier than guilt, and each one filled, cover to cover, in Orsen's own careful hand—or in the hands of those keepers who had come before, whose entries Orsen had copied forward whenever a page threatened to fail. The Weeping Wood kept accounts of everything it traded, and someone had to write them down. For sixty years, that someone had been a member of Orsen's line. For nine years, it had been Orsen.

They set the candle in its iron cradle and opened the current ledger to tonight's page.

*Received:* one borrowed year, drawn against the life of Marla Quill, seamstress, aged thirty-four, repayment due upon her sixtieth birthday or her death, whichever arrives first. *Paid:* to Aldous Ren, chandler, that he might see his daughter wed before his lungs finished failing. Interest: one memory, minor, of the smell of rain.

*Received:* one name, surrendered willingly by a debtor who wished to escape his creditors and could not do so while answering to anything at all. *Held in trust:* pending the Wood's judgment. Do not lend this name out again, whatever Master Ferren says. The last man who wore it drowned in his own bathtub and no one remembered grieving him.

*Received:* grief, bottled, four measures' worth, from the widow at Cordwainer Street. She wept for an hour in the back room and left lighter than smoke. *Sold:* to a lord who liked to feel things safely, at a distance, in small sips.

Orsen wrote until their hand ached, in the ledger's required ink—oak gall cut with a single drop of sap, so that the pages themselves were half-alive and would resist any hand but their keeper's. This was the work no one praised and no one saw: the quiet bookkeeping of impossible things. Borrowed years and stolen names and bottled sorrow, all of it entered fair and square, because the Wood was ancient and patient and *kept count*. Debts here were not metaphors. A debt unpaid in the Weeping Wood's ledger did not go to collections. It went to the door of the debtor at midnight, wearing the debtor's own face.

Invisibility, Orsen had learned, ran in both directions. By day they were nothing—a thin figure in serviceable gray whom dukes looked through and stewards forgot by supper. By night they were the only thing standing between the city and the arithmetic of the uncanny. No one thanked them for either. That was the arrangement. That was the safety of it.

*You are safe as long as you are no one,* Sable Vey had told them, years ago, in a voice like dry leaves. *A person can be found, Orsen. A function cannot.*

Orsen had believed her. Believed her still, mostly, in the way one believes in load-bearing walls: without looking too closely.

They blew out the candle and sat a moment in the dark, listening to the cellar breathe. The mold on the walls gave off its faint green scent, and beneath it, as always, the sap-sweetness, and beneath that—Orsen pressed their palm flat against the black table—the smallest pulse, like a heartbeat heard through a floor. The Wood was far away, beyond the covenant-line where the old maps said human law ended. But it was never entirely away. It leaked. It remembered. On nights when Orsen balanced the books just so, they could almost feel it attending.

"Nothing new," they murmured, the way one speaks to a horse or a god. "Everything square."

The pulse slowed. Settled. Or almost—because as Orsen withdrew their hand, the table pulsed once more, hard, deliberate, like a knock.

Then another. Then a sound from above, from the top of the stairs: footsteps, uneven, dragging, and a fist against the door at the head of the steps—weak, frantic, striking the wood in a rhythm that was almost words. Almost a name.

Almost, though Orsen's blood had gone cold and their counting-mind had already begun to run the figures, like a summons.

*One who counts. One who counts. Open the door, counter of debts—I have something for you to count.*

No one knew about this place. No one living.

Orsen picked up the candle with a steady hand that cost them more than the poison ever had, and climbed toward the knocking.
