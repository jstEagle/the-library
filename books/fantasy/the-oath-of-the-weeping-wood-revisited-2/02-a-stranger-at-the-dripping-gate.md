## 2. A Stranger at the Dripping Gate

The rain had been falling since dusk, and by the time Orsen finished their duties at the palace it had turned the streets to rivers of lamplight and refuse. They walked with their collar up and their ledger wrapped in oilcloth beneath their coat, close against the ribs, where a second heartbeat might have been. The city did not look at them. It never did. A food-taster was furniture that walked, a spoon with legs, and Orsen had spent thirty years perfecting the art of being overlooked—shoulders slightly stooped, eyes down, footsteps soft as spilled flour.

But tonight the city looked back.

It began small. In the black water pooled in the mouth of Cooper's Alley, Orsen saw not the warped reflection of a guttering streetlamp but trees—tall ones, grey-barked, standing in ranks like mourners. They blinked, and the water held only rain and lamp-oil again. In the window of the chandler's shop, dark now, the glass showed the same: a forest where a shelf of candles should be, weeping something amber and slow down the panes. Orsen told themselves it was exhaustion. Thirty years of swallowing small deaths left a residue; even an immunity was a kind of wound, and wounds dreamed.

The Dripping Gate opened off a courtyard behind the tanneries, where the smell of lime and hides covered all other smells, including the guild's. To any watcher it was a drainage arch, choked with weed, dripping even in drought because the cisterns above leaked on purpose. Orsen pressed their palm flat to the wet stone and spoke the countersign—not words, but numbers, the first entry of the first ledger, which no one wrote down because everyone who mattered carried it in their blood:

*One year, borrowed. One name, lent. One grief, sold.*

The stone drank the rain off their hand and swung inward without sound.

Inside, the cellar shrine was warm with tallow-light. Rows of ledgers climbed the walls in pigeonholes, each spine lettered in the guild's private cipher: *Years, Borrowed — 3rd Recension*. *Names, Stolen — Under Dispute*. *Grief, Bottled — Returns Pending*. At the center stood the reading desk, its wood worn into a shallow valley by two centuries of elbows, and above it hung the only ornament the guild permitted: a dried sprig of Weeping Wood, its sap long hardened into beads like fossilized tears.

Orsen lit the desk lamp, unwrapped their own ledger—the personal one, the one they kept apart from the guild's official volumes—and began to write. Tonight's entries were small. A widow in Salt Row had pawned six months of her life to pay her husband's debts; the interest was compounding cruelly, and Orsen noted it for review. A minor lord had purchased a stolen name and was discovering that names, like dogs, remembered their former owners. Routine. Tidy. The world balanced on the page, and for an hour the page was the only place where anything balanced.

Then came the knocking.

Not at the gate—at the *wall*, three chambers past the shrine, where no door was. A fist striking stone in a rhythm that was almost the countersign but wrong, slurred, like a man counting while drowning. Orsen froze, quill lifted, ink gathering at the nib in a black bead.

The knocking came again, weaker.

They went toward it the way one goes toward any strange sound in the guild's under-halls: slowly, lamp raised, ready to bolt. Through the third archway, in a passage that smelled of wet chalk and old iron, they found him.

A courier. Guild livery, or what remained of it—a riding coat soaked through, one shoulder dark and stiff with blood that had stopped trying to be blood some hours ago. He was maybe forty, hollow-cheeked, mud to the thigh, and he was holding himself upright against the wall by the simple refusal to fall. His eyes found Orsen's lamp and fixed on it as if it were shore.

"You're real," he said. "Good. I was afraid I'd walked into a memory."

"Who sent you?" Orsen kept the lamp between them, out of habit more than sense. "This entrance is sealed after midnight."

"I know." He tried to smile. Blood threaded from the corner of his mouth. "I've been dying since the ford at Marrow Bridge. Two arrows. I pulled them out myself because there wasn't time to be careful." He slid down the wall an inch, caught himself. "I need—I need the one who counts."

Orsen's mouth went dry. That phrase belonged to the oldest stratum of guild law, older than Sable Vey, older than the ledgers themselves. *The one who counts.* Not the matriarch. Not the enforcers. The keeper of debts. It had never once, in Orsen's hearing, been used to mean a person.

"There are many who count here," they said carefully.

"No." The courier's voice dropped, went urgent, went *young* somehow, though his face was old. "There's one. There's always been one. The covenant named them before it broke. Listen to me—" He pushed off the wall and took a step, and his leg folded, and Orsen caught him, and the weight of him drove them both to the floor, and his blood was soaking through Orsen's coat before they could think about whether to allow it.

He had a satchel strapped across his chest, and his hands went to it with the last coordination left in his body. Fingers like frozen roots worked the buckle loose. From inside he drew a leather tube, wax-sealed, and then—when Orsen made no move fast enough—a map already half-unrolled, stained along one edge in a broad brown wash of his own drying blood.

"Take it," he said. "Take it *now*."

Orsen took it.

The map was drawn on skin. Not parchment—Orsen knew parchment the way a priest knows prayer books—and not vellum either. Skin, thin and fine-grained, cured soft, and marked in ink that shifted color when the lamplight moved across it: green, then grey, then a bruised violet. It showed the Weeping Wood entire, every path the guild used rendered in careful line, and at its heart, circled twice in a hand heavier than the cartographer's, a mark Orsen had seen only once before—in the forbidden first folio of the founding ledger, the page Sable Vey kept locked and rarely let anyone remember existed.

The Broken Stone.

Around the circle ran an inscription in Old Covenant script, the letters half-erased as if something had licked them away:

*WHERE THE OATH WAS SWORN AND SWORN AWAY. THE WOOD REMEMBERS WHAT WAS PAID. LET THE ONE WHO COUNTS COME AND SET THE SUM RIGHT.*

Below the Wood, sketched small and precise, lay the human realm—every city, every road—and stitched between the two realms a dotted line like a seam. Someone had cut the seam. Someone had drawn it back together in ink that didn't match.

"Who gave you this?" Orsen asked, pressing their palm hard against the courier's wound, feeling the terrible warmth leaving him. "Who is still alive to have given you this?"

"The Wood," he said, and laughed, and the laugh cost him what little voice he had left. "You think the maps come from us? We copy them. We've always just been copying them. It sends them out when it wants—" His breath hitched, whistled. "It wants counting done."

"Stay awake. Look at me. Stay awake and tell me who shot you."

"Crown men." His eyes were losing their focus, sliding toward the lamp as if it were a person he'd been introduced to. "And others. Others wearing no colors, moving like the Wood moves. Both sides want this map burned, taster. Both sides want the one who counts to stay uncounted." His hand found Orsen's wrist and gripped it with shocking strength, the strength of a man spending his final coin. "I carried it eleven days. Eleven days pretending to be a wool merchant. I buried the real map twice and dug it up twice because I couldn't decide which fear was worse."

"Whose name do I record? For your death. Every death in guild service gets an entry."

Something eased in his face at that. As if the promise of a line in a ledger was, to a courier, the same as a priest's absolution.

"Hale," he said. "Merrick Hale. And write that I delivered it. Write *delivered*."

"I'll write it."

"And—" His grip tightened. His eyes cleared for one last moment, terribly clear, the way dying men's eyes are clear, seeing straight through the present into whatever stands behind it. "You're them. Aren't you. You're the one who counts. I saw it at the gate—you counted the knocks. Everyone else would have run."

Orsen said nothing. Saying nothing was the oldest skill they owned.

"Merrick Hale," the courier whispered, "delivered." And then the strength went out of his wrist like water out of a cracked jug, and his head tipped back against the stone, and he was looking at the ceiling of the world instead of the room.

Orsen sat with him a while, the way one sits with any body in the guild's halls, and did the work. Checked for breath twice, three times, out of superstition rather than hope. Opened his satchel: a change of clothes, a false passport in the name of a wool merchant, a stub of candle, and a second document wrapped in waxed linen—which Orsen did not open, not yet, because some things should only be read once, at a desk, with the door barred.

Then they stood, and fetched the shroud-cloth from the niche where the guild kept it, and laid him out properly, and closed his eyes. Merrick Hale, delivered. Orsen's hands were steady. Their hands were always steady; that was the whole point of them.

It was only when they returned to the reading desk and spread the skin-map flat in the lamplight that the shaking started, low in the belly, below the reach of discipline.

Because the map had changed.

Where the route to the Broken Stone had been traced in shifting ink, a new line was surfacing now, rising through the skin like a bruise coming up—starting not at any gate Orsen knew, but at the Dripping Gate itself. Starting here. Starting with them. And at the end of the line, beside the double-circled Stone, small letters were pushing up through the grain of the skin, letter by letter, in a handwriting Orsen recognized because it was their own:

*A debt is called. Come alone. Come counted.*

Orsen looked at the dead man in the passage. Looked at the sprig of Weeping Wood hanging above the desk, its fossil tears catching the light. Somewhere above, through sixty feet of earth and stone, the rain went on falling, and somewhere beyond the city the Wood stood waiting, patient as a creditor, and it had finally, after thirty years of invisibility, learned Orsen Kavanagh's name.

They reached for a fresh page in their own ledger, and wrote, in a hand that only trembled a little:

*Merrick Hale, courier. Delivered.*

Then, beneath it, because the ledger demanded honesty even when its keeper had none:

*Received: one map, one death, one summons. Payment pending.*

* * *

Sable Vey was going to want to know about the courier. That was the thought Orsen fell asleep clutching, in the narrow cot behind the shrine, the map locked in the strongbox with the key tied to their wrist. Sable Vey was going to ask who he was, who sent him, why he came to *this* gate and not the southern post, and Orsen rehearsed answers in the dark like a man rehearsing alibis—because every answer they could construct was a lie, and every lie led back to the same question they could not stop asking:

Why had the Wood addressed its summons to *the one who counts*?

And why, when Orsen read those words in their own handwriting, had some buried part of them—not the mind, something older than the mind—whispered back: *finally*?
