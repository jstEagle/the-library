## 3. What the Ledgers Omit

Sleep did not come. Orsen had not truly expected it to.

They lay in their narrow room above the palace sculleries, listening to the building settle around them—the groan of old beams, the drip of a pipe that no one had ever fixed because fixing it would mean admitting the pipe existed, and the palace preferred its imperfections unacknowledged. Orsen understood that preference intimately. They had built an entire life on it.

The courier's map lay beneath their pillow, and even through linen and straw they could feel it, or imagined they could: a faint warmth, like a stone kept too long in a hand. Blood had dried into its creases in colors the eye kept trying to correct—rust-brown becoming red-brown becoming something darker whenever Orsen wasn't looking directly at it.

*The one who counts.*

Near dawn, when the bells of the low quarter tolled the hour that belonged to no honest work, Orsen rose, dressed, and did what they always did with unbearable things. They carried them downstairs to be filed.

* * *

The guild's archives occupied three cellars beneath the Dripping Gate, though *cellars* was a courtesy of language. The first was a proper archive: shelved ledgers, wax-sealed boxes, dust arranged by decade. The second held the older holdings, where the ledgers were bound in leathers Orsen preferred not to name and written in inks that shifted when the light moved. The third cellar was locked, and Orsen had been keeper for eleven years without once being given the key. Sable Vey kept it herself, on a cord around her throat, where it hung against her sternum like a second heart.

Tonight Orsen went to the second cellar.

The founding record of the Weeping Wood lived in a lectern of black oak at the room's center—a book so old its title had worn away, so that everyone simply called it *the First Ledger*, as one might call a mountain *the mountain*. It recorded everything: every borrowed year, every stolen name, every vial of grief drawn off and sold since before the city had walls. And near its beginning, transcribed in a hand so careful it looked printed, lay the covenant itself—or what remained of it.

Orsen had read these pages a hundred times. They knew the cadences by heart. *Let the Wood remember what men forget, and men forget what the Wood remembers, and between them let the border hold.* They knew the clause about tolls paid in memory. They knew the signatures: nine founders, nine names, each followed by a date.

What they had never done—not once, in eleven years of faithful keeping—was count.

That was the cruelty of it. That was the joke the dead courier must have appreciated, if the dead could appreciate anything. Orsen's whole life was counting. They counted grains of arsenic in the king's broth; they counted drops of belladonna tincture under the tongue until the tongue stopped tingling; they counted the seconds between swallowing and knowing whether they would live to swallow again. Counting was the wall they stood behind. But behind that wall, they had trusted. They had read the covenant the way one reads scripture, drinking the words without weighing them.

Now they counted.

Nine founders. Nine signatures. But the ledger's own index—kept, significantly, in a different hand, added later—listed the founding transaction across *twelve* pages.

Orsen turned back to the covenant and counted the leaves between the binding cords. Ten.

Two pages gone. Not torn—torn leaves leave stubs, ragged ghosts along the gutter—but razored, cut flush against the spine with a blade so fine the surviving parchment showed only a hairline scar, a pale seam like an old wound. Someone had wanted the absence itself to be invisible. Someone had wanted the book to lie smoothly, the way a courtier lies, without ever technically saying anything untrue.

Orsen sat down on the cold floor with the book open across their knees and felt something shift in their chest, some load-bearing certainty giving way.

Eleven years. Eleven years of updating these ledgers, of entering debts in a fair hand, of believing that the guild was the one honest institution in a dishonest city—that here, at least, everything was written down, and what was written down was true. Lies lived upstairs, in throne rooms and merchant halls. Down here there was only arithmetic. Debts entered, debts paid, the balance sacred.

And the founding document of that sacred balance had been mutilated by someone who knew exactly how books worked.

Orsen lifted the page to the light of their shuttered lantern, angling it, hunting for ghost-impressions—ink pressed through from whatever had been written on the removed leaves. There was nothing. Of course there was nothing. Whoever had cut them out had known to blot the facing pages first, or had replaced them entirely. This was not vandalism. This was *curation*.

But paper remembers being a book. Orsen ran a fingertip along the pale seam and thought of the map hidden under their pillow, bloodstained and warm, showing a route through the Wood to the Broken Stone. The courier had died naming Orsen *the one who counts*—not *the one who keeps*, not *the one who believes*. Counts. As if the whole summons were an instruction: stop reading. Start counting.

The dates, then. Orsen turned to the signatures again and made themselves look at what had always been there, plain as a bruise.

Eight of the nine dates fell within a single season, thirty-seven years past—the spring and summer of the covenant's making. The ninth signature bore a later date. Much later. Fourteen years after the others.

Fourteen years. The gap between the covenant's signing and its breaking, if the stories were true—if anything in this room were still true. One founder had signed the covenant fourteen years late, which meant one founder had signed it *again*, which meant somewhere between the ninth signature and its date, the original oath had failed, or fractured, or been broken—and someone had re-signed, re-sworn, re-entered the pact in a hand that trembled slightly, the only unsteady line in the entire founding record.

Orsen knew that handwriting. Everyone in the guild knew it, because it was everywhere—in the margins of a thousand ledgers, in the seals on the debt-boxes, in the letters that summoned keepers to audience. A slanted, elegant, ruthless script, each letter a small blade laid neatly beside its neighbors.

Sable Vey.

Sable Vey had signed the covenant *fourteen years after its founding*. Sable Vey had put her name to the oath's remaking—or its replacement—at precisely the hour the histories said the realms' bargain broke. And then, sometime in the decades since, she had taken a razor to the First Ledger and removed the two pages that explained why.

Orsen closed the book very gently, the way one closes the eyes of the dead.

* * *

Upstairs, the city was waking into gray rain, and Dame Corvane was awake with it.

She had come to the palace three days ago, a tall woman in travel-gray with the crown's inverted-tower badge at her shoulder, and she had begun, quietly and terribly, to ask questions. She did not interrogate; she *conversed*. She spoke to the under-cook about the sourcing of mushrooms. She spoke to the gate sergeant about which servants left the grounds at night. She had a way of listening that made people confess things they hadn't decided to say, the way a plucked string makes other strings hum.

On the morning after Orsen's sleepless night, Corvane stood in the pantry corridor with a slate in her hand and asked the master of kitchens a question she had already asked four people, in four phrasings:

"The food-taster. Kavanagh. They're diligent?"

"Diligent," the master agreed. "Best I've had."

"They taste alone, I understand. In private."

"Standard practice. Can't have the tasting observed—reactions get... performed." He smiled. "You know how it is below stairs. Everyone acts for an audience."

"Mm," said Corvane, who did not smile, and wrote nothing on her slate, and remembered everything. "And when they're not tasting? Where does a food-taster go, in the hours between services?"

"Cellars, mostly. Their own business." He paused, hearing himself. "Why do you ask?"

"No reason worth your time," Corvane said pleasantly, and moved on down the corridor, her boots making no sound at all on the wet stone.

Later that same day she stood for a long while at the mouth of the stair that led down toward the old wine vaults—stairs Orsen had descended perhaps two hundred times—and looked at the damp dark below with the expression of a woman reading a ledger herself, and finding a column that did not sum.

A passing scullery boy asked if she was lost.

"No," said Dame Corvane. "I'm auditing."

* * *

Orsen spent the day in a fog of performed normalcy, tasting the morning's soups (clean), the noon roasts (clean, save an excess of cloves meant to disguise mutton past its prime—Orsen noted it and said nothing, because invisibility was safety, invisibility had always been safety), and all the while the razored seam in the First Ledger burned behind their eyes like an afterimage.

At evening service they nearly missed it. A new dish, presented by a steward Orsen didn't recognize—some minor cousin of some minor house, sweating slightly, watching Orsen's lips as they touched the spoon. Almond. Bitterness under the almond, faint as a whisper under a whisper. Cyanide of peach kernels, old-fashioned, detectable only by a tongue trained to distrust kindness.

Orsen swallowed anyway. That was the job. Then they met the young steward's eyes, and held them, and let their face do nothing at all—which frightened him far more than any reaction would have—and watched him excuse himself and flee the hall.

So. Someone else wanted Orsen dead now, or wanted to know if they *could* be killed. Word traveled. The courier was dead, the map had changed hands, and somewhere in the city a chain of consequences was tightening link by link around a food-taster who had believed, for eleven years, that no one important even knew their name.

Afterward, in the scullery passage, the kitchen girl Tansy caught Orsen's sleeve. She was sixteen, quick, fond of Orsen in the uncomplicated way of the young.

"There was a lady asking after you," she whispered. "Gray lady, tower badge. Asked where you go at night. I said the chapel, 'cause you're always—" she gestured vaguely, meaning *pious*, which was the best cover story Tansy had ever invented, and possibly the worst, given what Orsen actually worshipped. "Was that wrong?"

"No," Orsen said, and heard their own voice come out steady, and marveled at it from a great distance. "No, that was perfect. Thank you."

But walking back through the dripping dark toward the Dripping Gate that night, ledger-key cold in their palm, Orsen understood that the walls were closing from both sides at once. Behind them, an inquisitor was learning to count cellars. Ahead of them waited an archive built on a beautiful, meticulous lie—and the woman whose handwriting trembled across the only signature that mattered.

Sable Vey had summoned them two days hence. *Come to me when you've finished grieving the courier,* the letter had said, in that elegant, bladelike script. *We have much to discuss about the Wood.*

Orsen had assumed it was about the map. About the summons. About duty.

Standing before the First Ledger with the lantern low, they drew out the razored seam one last time with a fingernail and felt the truth settle over them like ash: Sable Vey did not want to discuss the map.

She wanted to know whether Orsen had found the missing pages.

Because Sable Vey had cut them out herself—and she had kept Orsen close, all these years, for reasons that had nothing to do with ledgers.

The lantern guttered. Somewhere above, through three floors of stone and earth, Orsen heard—or dreamed they heard—the long, slow sound of the Weeping Wood weeping.

It sounded, tonight, like a countdown.
