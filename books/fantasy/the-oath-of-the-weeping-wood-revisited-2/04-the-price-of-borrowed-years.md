## 4. The Price of Borrowed Years

The summons came before dawn, as the crown's ugliest business always did.

Orsen heard it through the servants' stair—a knock at the steward's door, three measured raps, and a voice like a key turning in a lock. *"By order of Dame Corvane, Royal Inquisitor. All kitchen staff to the lower hall."* The words fell down the stairwell one by one, and Orsen counted them the way they counted everything: four knocks' worth of footsteps overhead, six breaths, one heartbeat that would not slow.

They dressed in the dark. They had practiced this—dressing without light, without sound, without existing—and their hands were steady even as their mind catalogued exits. The coal chute behind the scullery, too narrow for an armored shoulder. The wine cellar's second door, bricked over but bricked badly. The drain beneath the fishmonger's stall in the courtyard, wide enough for a thin body and a desperate one.

The ledgers went first, wrapped in oilcloth against their ribs, bound tight with the cord they'd braided from their mother's apron-string. After the ledgers, the map. After the map, nothing else mattered enough to carry.

In the lower hall, forty servants stood in rows like spoons in a drawer, and Dame Corvane walked among them.

She was not what rumor promised. No towering menace, no black-and-scarlet regalia—just a woman in travel-grey with a face like a closed ledger herself, every entry balanced, nothing wasted. She carried no instruments of torture. She carried a small notebook and a pencil worn to a stub, and she used them the way a surgeon uses a blade: precisely, and without apology.

"Name," she said to the first boy.

"Fenwick, m'lady."

"What did you carry from the cellar on the third of Frostmonth?"

"I—I don't recall, m'lady."

"You do. Everyone recalls what they carry; it's how they know when they've been robbed." She wrote something. "Try again."

Orsen watched her work and felt the old cold settle into their marrow. She wasn't hunting randomly. She had dates. She had *cellars*. Someone had talked, or someone had watched, and either way the invisible life Orsen had built was shedding its invisibility layer by layer, like paint off a wet wall.

When she reached Orsen, her pencil paused.

"Name."

"Orsen Kavanagh. Food-taster."

"Yes." She looked up, and her eyes were the grey of ash after rain. "You taste for the king."

"When asked."

"And in your unasked hours?" A silence, delicate as spun sugar. "The steward says you visit cellars. Several cellars. Cellars with no kitchens attached."

Orsen let their shoulders curve, let their gaze drop to the middle distance—the posture of a tired servant, a boring servant, a servant who had never once been interesting. "I count stores, m'lady. Wine, salt, tallow. Numbers don't lie about themselves unless someone helps them."

Something flickered across her face—not belief, not dismissal. *Interest.* Which was worse than either.

"Hm," said Dame Corvane, and wrote.

They were dismissed at sunrise, all forty of them, back to their stations as though the night had been nothing but a bad dream. But as Orsen climbed the stair, they felt her eyes between their shoulder blades, patient as a debt accruing interest.

By noon they were gone. Not fled—*gone*, the way smoke is gone from a snuffed candle, leaving only the shape of where it had been. A note left folded in the flour bin read only: *Sick. Fevered. Do not visit.* The steward would grumble. The king's table would suffer a stranger's tongue for a day or two. And Dame Corvane would find an empty room and understand exactly what it meant.

Orsen understood something else: the time for hiding in plain sight was over. Now came the other kind of hiding, the kind with teeth.

* * *

Dame Corvane stood in the food-taster's cell and knew, with the certainty of thirty years' interrogations, that the room itself was lying to her.

It lied well. A cot, a stool, a shelf of cheap devotional pamphlets, a cup with a hairline crack. Everything humble, everything forgettable. But humility this thorough was a costume, and costumes had seams. She ran her thumb along the underside of the shelf and found nothing. She checked the straw tick and found nothing. It was the *nothing* that told her. Rooms lived in honestly accumulated debris—a button, a bone needle, a stain with a story. This room had been curated.

"He counts," the dying courier's sister had wept, two nights ago, in a house that smelled of tallow and terror. "He kept saying he needed to reach the one who counts. *The one who counts.* I thought he meant a moneylender."

Corvane did not think so.

She descended to the lower hall again that afternoon and took the scullery girl first, because scullery girls saw everything and were thanked for nothing, which made them either loyal or mercenary, and both could be worked with.

"The taster," she said. "Their habits."

The girl wrung her hands. "Quiet, m'lady. Good at their work. They once drank broth for the king that put two men in the fever-ward, and never turned a hair."

"Never turned a hair." Corvane wrote that down. Immunity was not a gift; it was a practice, and practices left records. "And their hours? Their friendships?"

"None as I know. Except—" The girl hesitated, and Corvane waited with the stillness of a heron over water. "Except they'd go out some nights. Down toward Tanner's Row. Nobody goes to Tanner's Row for pleasure."

No. Nobody did. Corvane closed her notebook. Somewhere under this palace, under this city, there was a cellar with no kitchen attached, and somewhere in it, a man who counted things that had never been entered into any royal book. She had served the crown since before this taster was weaned, and she knew the oldest truth of her trade: *every kingdom keeps two ledgers, and the second one always frightens the first.*

"Tanner's Row," she said to her lieutenant. "Tonight. Quietly. If anyone runs, do not chase. Follow."

* * *

Tanner's Row at midnight smelled of lime and rot and old blood, and Orsen moved through it like water finding its level.

Down, always down. The city had an underside the way a body has organs—necessary, hidden, working in the dark. Orsen knew its streets the way they knew ledger-columns: the gutter-run where beggars sold dreams by the pinch; the drowned chapel where the fence called Mother Hessel held court among broken pews; the stairways that went down and kept going down, past any depth the surveyors had ever charted.

At the drowned chapel's threshold they stopped, because the door was polished black oak and in its polish, wrong things moved.

Not reflections. *Revisions.*

The oak showed the alley behind them—but the alley it showed had trees. Birch-pale trunks where there should have been brick, a canopy where there should have been sky, and hanging from every branch a slow bead of sap that caught light that wasn't there. Orsen blinked, and the alley was an alley again. They pressed a hand flat against the door until the wood stopped trembling. Wood. It remembered being wood. Perhaps that was all it was.

Perhaps.

Inside, Mother Hessel sat amid her inventory like a spider of respectable years: hundreds upon hundreds of small glass bottles racked floor to ceiling, each stoppered, each labeled in her crabbed hand, each holding a grief. Bottled sorrow, sold by the dram. Weep into a vial and be free of it; buy another's and feel something, anything, in place of the numbness wealth brings. The whole chapel glittered faintly, like frost given commerce.

"Little counter," Hessel rasped. Her eyes were cataract-pale but missed nothing. "You've come to sell, or to buy?"

"To trade a favor." Orsen set a coin on the pew before her—not coin, actually, but a lead seal stamped with the Weeping Wood's mark, the kind that bought one true favor from any guild hand in the city. "I need the first waypoint. The old road. The courier's road."

Hessel picked up the seal and turned it, and her lips thinned. "Dangerous currency these days. There are people asking after you, counter. Grey woman. Sharp pencil. She was here yesterday, asking if anyone had traded in maps."

"And what did you tell her?"

"That nobody trades maps here. Grief, yes. Years, sometimes. Names, when the price is obscene." She smiled with too few teeth. "Maps are memory, little counter, and memory is the one thing I never touch. Too haunted stock."

"The waypoint."

Hessel weighed the seal in her palm a long moment, then jerked her chin toward the chapel's drowned crypt, where black water lapped at sunken steps. "The old road begins where the city forgot its own dead. Under the Leechgate, there's a well the surveyors sealed two generations back—Marrow Well, the old folk called it, because the water tasted of iron. The covenant-road starts at its bottom. Your map will know the rest." She leaned close, and her breath smelled of vinegar and violets. "But hear me, counter. That favor you're spending—it buys the answer, not the passage. What waits down there doesn't sell. It *collects*. Go before the third bell, or don't go at all."

"Why the third bell?"

"Because that's when the trees start walking." She said it the way she said everything, flat as a bill of sale. Then she pocketed the seal and waved him away. "Spent and done. Don't come back, little counter. You're bad luck now. Bad luck leaks."

Orsen climbed out of the drowned chapel into an alley that had changed while they were underground. The bricks glistened. Moss grew in the mortar-lines where moss had no business growing, green-black and damp, and in a rain barrel's standing water the reflection showed not the sliver of sky above the alley but a canopy—branches interlacing, dripping slow amber tears.

Orsen did not look again. Looking, they understood with animal certainty, was a form of answering.

They ran instead, through streets that grew stranger with each turning—ivy in the gutters, sap-smell where there should have been sewage, a window-box sprouting birch seedlings overnight—and behind them, faint but persistent, came the sound of boots. Not running. *Walking.* Patient. Following without chasing, exactly as someone very disciplined would order.

Marrow Well. Before the third bell. Orsen clutched the oilcloth bundle tighter against their ribs and ran deeper into a city that was forgetting to be a city, and thought, with a clarity that felt almost like peace:

*Everything I hid inside is coming outside to find me.*
