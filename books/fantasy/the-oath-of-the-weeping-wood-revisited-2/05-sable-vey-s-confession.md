## 5. Sable Vey's Confession

The house at the end of Candlewick Row had no door that Orsen could find, and yet everyone in the guild knew the way in. You went to the wall where the ivy grew thickest, you pressed your palm against the brick until the mortar remembered it was flesh, and you walked through. It had been explained to Orsen once, when they were nine and still new to the cellar shrine, that the guild's oldest houses did not keep doors because a door could be barred, and what Sable Vey kept could never be permitted to be barred.

Tonight the ivy was wet. Tonight everything in the city seemed wet, weeping, as if the Wood's grief had seeped beneath the cobblestones and risen into the gutters of the human realm like water through a cracked pot. Orsen stood before the wall with the bloodstained map folded against their chest, beneath three layers of clothing, and felt the way they always felt here: small, summoned, safe.

*Safe.* The word tasted different now. It tasted like something borrowed.

They pressed their palm to the brick.

The mortar gave with a sigh, warm as a mouth, and Orsen stepped through into a hall lit by candles that burned without shortening—wax bought from the Wood itself, years of tallow poured out of someone's stolen future—and found Sable Vey already waiting, seated in her high chair of black oak, hands folded on her knee like a woman who had been expecting a visitor for thirty years and had finally stopped pretending otherwise.

"Orsen," she said. Her voice was low and even, the voice she used for settling debts. "You have been in my archives."

Not *you have brought me a map*. Not *you are in danger*. The archives first. Of course the archives first.

Orsen kept their feet. In this hall, before this woman, they had learned to stand very still while poison worked through them; stillness was an old skill, and it held.

"The covenant ledger has pages missing," Orsen said. "Razored out. Cut so cleanly it was done by someone who loved the book."

"I did love that book." Sable rose. She was not tall, but the room arranged itself around her anyway, shadows leaning in like courtiers. "I had it bound myself, the year the guild was chartered. Come. Sit. You look like a man who has swallowed something bitter and is waiting to learn whether it kills him."

"I know how that feels," Orsen said. "I do it every day."

Something moved across her face—there and gone, too quick to name. She gestured to the table, and Orsen sat, because there was nowhere else to go, because the walls behind them had already closed back into brick, because some part of them, the part raised in these cellars, still wanted to obey.

Sable poured two cups of wine from a decanter of green glass. She drank from neither.

"You found the razored pages' ghosts," she said. "Ink impressions. Marginalia. Enough to know that the founding record lies by omission." She set the decanter down with a click. "So ask me, Orsen. Ask me what you came to ask. I am too old, tonight, to make you dig for it."

Orsen's hands were steady. They were always steady; that was the curse of the taster's trade, hands that would not shake even when the body wanted to die. But inside, something was shaking loose, something that had held since the courier died in their arms whispering *the one who counts.*

"The covenant between the realm and the Weeping Wood," Orsen said. "The oath sworn at the Broken Stone. The one every child learns broke by accident—by grief, by war, by the Wood's own sorrow turning inward." They drew breath. "It didn't break. It was broken. On purpose. By us."

The candles guttered, all together, though there was no draft.

"Yes," said Sable Vey.

Just that. No defense. No softening. The word fell into the room like a coin into a well, and Orsen heard it strike bottom, and the depth of that well frightened them more than any denial could have.

* * *

She told it plainly, which was the cruelest thing. Sable Vey did not lie; lying was for people who feared consequences, and Sable had long ago purchased immunity to those, the same way Orsen had purchased immunity to arsenic—one small dose at a time, over years, until the poison became food.

"In the beginning," she said, "the realms shared one memory. That is what the covenant was—not a border, not a treaty. A memory, held in common. Every soul born under either sky carried a piece of it. The Wood remembered what men forgot, and men dreamed what the Wood had never lived. There was no grief in the world that could not be set down somewhere and picked up again later by another hand. Do you understand what I am describing? A world where no one had to carry anything alone."

"And the founders sold it." Orsen's voice came out flat. "They sold the shared memory."

"They *divided* it." For the first time, something heated flickered in Sable's eyes. "There is a difference, whatever the tribunal's sentimental histories say. The Wood was dying of remembering, Orsen. Do you know what it is to be a forest that holds every grief ever given to it? To hold it forever, because that was the oath? The founders saw a way to save both realms from drowning in each other's sorrows. Yes—they took payment. Yes—the guild was built on what remained. Borrowed years, bottled grief, names traded across the divide. Someone had to keep the ledgers of what was lost. We made ourselves the keepers of the wound."

"You made yourselves the owners of it."

The words left Orsen before they could weigh them, and once spoken they could not be unspoken, and Orsen found they did not wish to unspeak them. Sable regarded them for a long moment across the candlelight, and then she inclined her head, slowly, like a duelist acknowledging a touch scored.

"Perhaps," she said. "I was young. I believed the arithmetic. Seven founders, seven signatures, and each of them certain the others would betray the plan if they did not all proceed together. That is how such things are done, Orsen. No one breaks a world alone. They break it in company, each holding the next one's hand, so that guilt can be passed around the circle until it wears thin as coin." She turned her cup without drinking. "I razored out those pages thirty years ago. I told myself it was to protect the guild. The truth is I could not bear to read my own name in the founding hand."

Orsen thought of the courier dying at the Dripping Gate, blood on the map, the route to the Broken Stone drawn in a hand that shook. Thought of Dame Corvane asking questions in the palace kitchens, her shadow falling across cellar doors. Thought of the Wood appearing in reflections—in the polished lid of a tasting spoon, in a window at dusk, trees standing where trees should not be—and how lately it seemed to be looking at them.

"Why tell me now?" Orsen asked. "Why not let me keep counting? I counted for you for twenty years. I never asked what the numbers meant."

"Because you asked tonight." Sable leaned forward. "And because the map you carry has already answered for you. You think I did not know about the courier? I know everything that crosses my thresholds. I let him die at your feet, Orsen, because I wanted the map to find you, and I wanted you to choose to bring it to me rather than run with it. Loyalty must be chosen or it is worthless. I taught you that."

The floor seemed to tilt. Orsen gripped the table's edge. "You *let* him—"

"He was dead when he reached the gate. The Wood had already taken its toll for carrying what he carried. All that remained was the choosing." She said it gently, and gentleness from Sable Vey was worse than cruelty; cruelty at least admitted what it was. "Now. The last truth, since we are spending honesty so freely tonight. Ask me why a food-taster was raised in the guild's cellars. Ask me why you, of all the orphaned children who came through those doors, were given the ledgers. Ask me why I fed you poison in doses small enough to survive, year after year, building up your blood like a bank builds gold."

Orsen knew. Some deep animal part of them had known since the courier's lips shaped *the one who counts*, and knowing it did not lessen the blow; it only meant the blade had time to arrive slowly, so they could watch it come.

"Tasters' blood," Orsen said. Their voice sounded far away, someone else's voice. "What is it that you think my blood does?"

"It remembers resistance." Sable's eyes were old, suddenly—old as the hall, old as the razored pages. "Every poison survived leaves a signature in the veins. Twenty years of small deaths, endured. An oath requires a willing payment to reseal—that is the law beneath all laws, older than the covenant itself. And there is no payment more willing than the blood of one who has spent a lifetime choosing, each morning, to swallow what might kill them. Your blood is not immune, Orsen. It is *accustomed* to dying and declining. It is the finest sealing wax in either realm."

The candles bent toward Orsen, all together, like a congregation.

"You didn't raise me," Orsen said. "You *cured* me. Like a hide. Like a vellum waiting to be written on."

"I raised you AND cured you, and I will not insult you by claiming the two were separate." Sable stood, and came around the table, and for a moment her hand hovered near Orsen's shoulder—hovered, and withdrew. Even now, perhaps especially now, she would not take what was not freely given. That restraint was the most damning evidence of all. "I will not order you to the Broken Stone. Understand that. If I commanded it, the payment would fail—the oath would taste coercion on your tongue and reject it. You must walk there yourself. You must open your own vein yourself." She paused. "But I will tell you what waits if you do not. The Wood is coming out of its borders. It is looking through glass and silver and standing water, hunting the last living witness to what was done. When it finds you—and it will find you—it will not ask nicely. And Dame Corvane has already traced you further than you know. The crown wants the covenant reforged on its terms: the Wood bound as property, fenced and taxed and bled on a schedule. The guild wants it reforged on ours. And you, Orsen—" she smiled, and it was the saddest smile Orsen had ever seen on her face, "—you are the only ink anyone agrees upon."

Orsen rose. Their legs held, the way trained legs hold. They looked at the woman who had pulled them from an orphan cart at six years old, who had taught them letters and ledgers and the precise bitterness of hemlock at one drop in ten thousand, who had been mother and employer and jailer in a single seamless garment they had worn for twenty years without feeling the seams.

"I need to think," Orsen said.

"Of course." Sable returned to her chair and sat, folding her hands, a queen dismissing nothing and awaiting everything. "Take the servant's passage. It opens onto Candlewick Row. And Orsen—" her voice followed them, soft as ash settling, "—whatever you decide, decide quickly. The Wood does not wait for deliberation. It remembers faster than you can reason."

* * *

The servant's passage was narrow and smelled of tallow and old stone, and Orsen walked it slowly, one hand trailing the wall, the other pressed flat over the map against their chest. Behind them, the house sealed itself back into silence. Ahead, gray evening light leaked under a door that was only a door, wood and iron and hinges, and Orsen stood before it a long moment, unwilling to step through into the city, into the hunt, into the choosing.

Their forearm itched where the blood ran close beneath the skin, and for the first time in twenty years Orsen thought of their veins as something written on, something legible, something other people had been reading all along.

*Sealing wax.*

They pushed open the door. The street received them, ordinary and dim, lamplighters working their way down Candlewick Row with long brass poles, and Orsen turned toward the cellar shrine out of pure habit—

—and stopped.

In the puddle at the kerb, rain-watered and rippling, the reflection showed no lamplighter, no row of shuttered houses. It showed trees. Black trunks, dripping branches, a path leading away between them toward something pale and broken in the far distance, and along that path, small and unmistakable, a figure walking. Wearing Orsen's coat. Carrying Orsen's gait. Already deep among the trees.

Orsen looked up at the empty street, then back down. The figure in the water had stopped. Its head lifted. Though it had no face the lamplight could find, Orsen felt it look at them—felt it the way one feels poison on the tongue before the swallowing, that first bright bell of recognition.

The Wood had sent ahead an invitation. Or a warning.

Orsen turned south, away from the shrine, away from the guild, away from everything that had raised and cured and claimed them, and began to walk toward the river, where the map said the first true crossing lay—and where, somewhere behind them, unhurried as falling sap, footsteps in the puddle-reflections kept pace.
