## 6. Into the Weeping Wood

The Wood did not begin so much as it accumulated. Orsen walked out of the farmland in the grey hour before dawn, past hedgerows, past a shrine to some forgotten harvest saint with its offering bowl full of rain, and then the hedges were gone and the trees simply *were*, closing overhead like a hand folding shut.

They stopped at the first tree and put their palm against its bark, because the map told them to.

*Touch what remembers,* the courier's ink had said, though the ink had been his blood at the time, and Orsen had hoped it was delirium. It was not. The bark was warm—wrongly warm, like skin—and beneath their fingers something moved, thick and slow, rising through channels in the wood the way grief rises through a body that has decided to stop fighting it.

Sap welled up around their hand, dark as strong tea, smelling of iron and honey and something older underneath, something like a cellar where no one has lit a candle in forty years.

Then the memory came.

Not theirs—or not yet theirs. A woman's hands kneading bread in a kitchen with blue tiles; a child laughing at the flour on her nose; the ordinary golden light of an afternoon that had felt, to the woman living it, endless. Orsen stood inside it for three heartbeats, four, watching through eyes that were not their own while the woman hummed a song about a river—

The sap cooled. The vision let go. Orsen staggered back from the tree, wiping their hand on their coat, and found they were crying without any memory of having started.

*It bleeds what you touch it with,* they thought. *No. It bleeds what touches it.* Every tree in this forest was a ledger entry made flesh, every ring of the wood a line of debt recorded in the only ink that never faded: lived time. The guild kept ledgers of borrowed years and bottled grief, but the guild had only ever been copying. This was the original scriptorium.

Orsen looked down at the map in their hands.

The parchment had changed. Where yesterday it had shown a single route threading north to the Broken Stone, there were now two paths—one inked firm and black, one faint, drawn in something like watercolor, as if the map itself were unsure. At the fork, new words had surfaced in the margin, in handwriting that was almost the courier's and almost not:

*Which debt do you carry?*

"I don't carry any," Orsen said aloud, and the Wood received the lie the way a ledger receives a false figure: silently, and forever.

* * *

They walked until the light went strange. Under the canopy the sun came down in coins rather than beams, scattered and green-gold, and the air was thick enough to chew. The trees here grew close, their trunks scarred with old weeping—long black streaks down the bark like tear-tracks on a face that had given up wiping them away. Orsen learned quickly not to brush against them. Each accidental touch cost them a memory that wasn't theirs, someone's wedding, someone's drowning, someone's last look at a door, and each one left a residue behind, a grit in the mind that would not wash out.

By midday they were carrying perhaps twenty strangers' deaths and half again as many joys, and they understood, with a taster's precision, how the poison worked. Small doses. That was all survival had ever been, in the palace cellars or anywhere else. You took a little of everything and built up an immunity, and you called the numbness peace.

The map forked ahead of them at a stream that ran backward—Orsen watched a leaf fall into the water and travel upstream, unhurried, as if returning home. The black path followed the stream. The pale path climbed toward a ridge where the trees grew white and leafless, bone-bright even in shadow.

Orsen knelt and touched the map again, meaning only to steady it.

This time the sap-memory came from the map itself, and it was theirs.

* * *

A cellar. Not the guild's shrine—their own, under a house in a city district whose name they had not thought of in years, because thinking of it had become, somewhere along the way, impossible, the way swallowing certain poisons had once been possible and then wasn't. Their mother was measuring flour by lamplight. Their father was mending a shoe. There was a younger sister—there had always been a younger sister, small and loud, with a gap in her teeth she was vain about—and she was singing the same river-song the bread-woman had hummed, because everyone sang it, it was nothing, it was a children's rhyme:

*Count the years and count them true,*
*count what's owed and count what's due—*

And then the knock at the door. Three knocks, evenly spaced, patient as arithmetic. And Sable Vey's voice, warm as a ledger-line, saying words Orsen had heard ten thousand times since without ever hearing them: *collateral, security, a fair rate, the family will want for nothing.*

Their mother signing. Their father not looking at the paper. The sister asking if they were going somewhere nice.

And Orsen—young Orsen, seven years old, tasting sugar for the first time in their life because the guild's man offered it—Orsen remembering, decades later, in a wet green forest, that the sugar had tasted of iron. That they had known, even then, in the body's dumb ledger where no erasures hold, that something was being purchased with that sweetness. That they had eaten it anyway.

The vision released them and Orsen was on their knees in the mud of the backward stream, and the grief was not a feeling anymore. It was physical. It sat in the chest like a swallowed stone, it ached in the teeth, it ran from the nose and would not be wiped away, and Orsen understood suddenly why the trees wept—not from sorrow, but from mechanics. This was what happened when memory pooled too long in one place. It had to go somewhere. It ran downward. It stained.

*Collateral.* They had never been raised by the guild. They had been *held* by it. A taster's tongue, a counter's mind—a child with the two gifts the covenant required, bought with sugar and kept in a cellar, and Sable Vey checking on her investment twice a year for thirty years, calling it kindness.

"That's why you chose me," Orsen said to the stream, to the trees, to the map. "Not loyalty. Interest."

The map answered. The pale path thickened, darkening toward black, and the black path faded to a ghost of itself, and at the fork the marginal writing shifted:

*One road is owed. One road is chosen. The Stone takes only the second.*

* * *

They took the chosen road, because there had never really been a choice, and because the Wood seemed to approve—it leaned back from the path, giving them room, which was somehow worse than crowding.

Toward evening Corvane's presence announced itself the way weather does before it arrives: a pressure in the ears, a hush among the birds. Orsen found the sign at a stream crossing—boot prints in the soft ground, a cavalry man's stride, too long for a woman of her height because she refused to shorten it for anyone. Beside the prints, pinned to a branch with a knife, a strip of parchment in a clerk's immaculate hand:

*I know what you are now, taster. I also know what you were. The Stone will ask you who you serve. I am asking first.*

No threat in it. That was the frightening thing. Corvane did not waste threats; she spent questions.

Orsen took the note, and the knife, and walked on. Behind them, distantly, the Wood began to bleed along the trail Corvane would follow, and Orsen wondered what memories she would wade through to reach them—what the trees would show a crown inquisitor, and whether anything in her past could survive the touching.

The map was rewriting itself faster now, lines crawling like insects across the parchment, and the last waypoint before the Broken Stone had resolved into a single word where a drawing should have been:

*IDRIS.*

Orsen stopped walking.

The exile's name. The founder the guild burned out of its own histories, whose pages were razored from the record, whose existence Sable Vey denied with the flat certainty of a woman denying a scar. The Wood was not guiding Orsen to the Stone. Not yet. It was guiding them through the debts in order, oldest first, the way any honest ledger must be read.

The trees around them went still—not quiet, *still*, attentive, a held breath of forest. Sap beaded along every trunk within sight, hundreds of dark eyes welling open at once, and the whole Wood leaned in to watch what Orsen would do with the name.

"Well," Orsen said, to the trees, to the map, to the seven-year-old still standing in a cellar somewhere inside them with iron-tasting sugar on their tongue. "Let's go meet the author."

They walked on, and behind them, softly, the Wood began to weep.
