## 8. Corvane at the Broken Stone

The Wood ended the way a held breath ends—not gradually, but all at once, in a clearing where the trees refused to grow.

Orsen stepped out of the treeline and stopped. The sap had been running down their arms for hours, warm as tears, carrying other people's memories in its slow red-gold descent, and now it dried on their skin in the open air like a second, older self laid over them. Ahead, rising from the center of the clearing, stood a stone the size of a chapel: black, faceted, split clean through from crown to base as though something enormous had once struck it and walked away unsatisfied. The Broken Stone. Even split, even scarred with decades of moss, it radiated a pressure Orsen felt in the teeth—a silence so complete it was less an absence of sound than a presence of listening.

The map in their pocket had gone still. All through the Wood it had rewritten itself, ink crawling like insects, testing them; now the parchment lay flat and quiet, and every line of it pointed here, to the fissure at the stone's heart, where the old covenant had been sworn and then unsworn.

"You came the long way," said a voice behind them.

Orsen turned too fast; the world tilted. Dame Corvane stood at the edge of the trees, and she had not come out of the Wood—she had come *around* it, or through some drier path, because her boots were clean and her gray cloak carried only the dust of the city. Her sword was sheathed. Her hands were empty. Somehow both details were worse than the alternative.

"You shouldn't be able to find this place," Orsen said.

"Neither should you." She walked toward the Stone, unhurried, her gaze moving over the fissure with something that was not quite reverence and not quite hunger. "But grief leaves a scent, taster. The Wood has been bleeding yours since the day you touched that map." She stopped an arm's length from the black facets. "Do you know what I felt when I first saw it? Twenty-two years ago. I was nineteen."

Orsen said nothing. In the guild they taught you that silence drew more truth than questions ever did. But Corvane was not a suspect at a table; she talked anyway, as though the words had been waiting decades for a witness.

"My sister Wren ran messages for a trading house out of Harrowmere. The year the covenant broke, she took a commission into the Wood and never came out. No body. No blood. The searchers came back saying the forest had *closed*, like a door." Corvane's voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "The crown called it an accident of geography. I have spent my career learning what really happened. That the realms shared a memory once, one memory, flowing both ways like water—and that men sold the flow and dammed the river and let a girl drown in the dry bed of it."

"She's not drowned," Orsen said quietly. "She's *kept*. The Wood keeps everything."

"I know." Corvane turned, and in the flat light of the clearing her eyes were the color of old ice over deep water. "I've seen her. Twice, at the treeline, when the sap runs high. She looks nineteen years old, taster. She has looked nineteen for twenty-two years, and she does not know my name anymore, because no one on either side of the covenant remembers her whole." Something moved beneath the level voice then, brief and terrible, like a fish under ice. "So yes. I want the oath reforged. Desperately. More than you do, perhaps."

Hope is a dangerous thing to feel standing in front of a broken altar. Orsen felt it anyway, and hated themselves for it. "Then we can—"

"On the crown's terms." The hope shut like a door. "The covenant restored, with the realm of men holding title. The Wood bound as crown property—its gifts licensed, its debts collected by the treasury, its memory flowing only as far as the border stones. My sister returns. Your guild burns its ledgers publicly. And every soul who profited from the breaking answers to the throne." She smiled, and it did not reach anywhere near her eyes. "It is a generous offer. It is also a cage, and we both know it. But it is a cage with Wren inside it alive."

"The Wood isn't property."

"No. It's a person. Which is why binding it is a crime—but it is a crime I will commit gladly, if the alternative is leaving my sister a ghost in a tree forever." Corvane drew her sword then—not to threaten, simply to plant the point in the earth before her, hands resting on the pommel, a woman prepared to wait out eternity. "Choose your cage, taster. Everyone does."

* * *

Dame Maren Corvane had interrogated forty-one people in her career, and she knew the exact weight of a lie told by silence.

The taster stood across the clearing hugging the map to their chest like a child with a stolen loaf, and everything about them screamed *I have not decided yet*—which meant they could still be decided *for*. Good. Corvane preferred negotiable people. It was the ones with nothing left to want who killed you.

She had tracked Kavanagh through three days of city and two days of Wood, and she would not pretend the pursuit had been duty. Duty was the shape she gave it, the way she'd given her sister's death the shape of an accident, because shapes kept a person functional. The truth underneath was simpler and more shameful: she wanted the river back. She wanted to stand at the treeline again and hear Wren say her name, and she would put the whole rotting covenant in a crown's iron box to get it.

A sound made her head turn. Not from the Wood—from the *city side*, back along the dry track, where the human world still technically began.

Lanterns. Many lanterns, moving in formation, and ahead of them a tall figure walking without hurry, as though the road had been laid down for her convenience. Sable Vey wore mourning-gray despite the fact that no one in her guild had died—or rather, Corvane thought, precisely *because* everyone in her guild was dead and didn't know it yet. Six enforcers fanned out behind her in the practiced geometry of people who hurt others for coin and had stopped noticing the taste.

"Taster," Sable Vey called, and her voice carried across the clearing without effort, the way certain voices carry in churches. "Step away from the inquisitor."

"They're not mine," Corvane said pleasantly, "but I'm keeping them."

Sable Vey's eyes found Corvane's and held them, and Corvane had looked into the eyes of regicides and parricides and had never once felt catalogued the way she felt now. "Dame Corvane. The crown sent a woman with a dead sister. How very economical of them." She continued forward, skirts whispering over the dead grass. "You should understand what you're standing beside, inquisitor. This stone is the last piece of evidence against my guild. When it's dealt with, there is no covenant, no crime, no case. Only trade as usual."

"And how exactly does one 'deal with' a stone?"

"One doesn't." For the first time, something flickered in the matriarch's composure—fast, quickly buried. "One deals with the person trying to use it. Taster." Her attention swung to Orsen, softening into something almost maternal, which was the most frightening transformation Corvane had ever witnessed. "Child. You've read the ledgers. You know what reforging costs. A willing payment—the oath demands flesh for its mortar. These two—" a gesture that took in both Corvane and the Stone itself, "—will spend you like coin and call it destiny. Come home. We'll seal the archives ourselves, you and I, and no one need be spent at all."

Every offer is a cage. Corvane watched the thought land on the taster's face like weather. The girl—no, not a girl, whatever Kavanagh was, servant and scribe and poison-drinker—looked between the two of them with the expression of someone doing arithmetic with their own life as the units.

"There's a third option," Orsen said at last, very quietly.

"Name it," said Corvane.

"Refuse all of you."

Sable Vey laughed—a real laugh, brief and almost warm. "And then what? Stand between us until winter?"

"Then I do what I was raised to do," Orsen said. "I count."

They opened the ledger then—the ledger Corvane had been chasing across half the kingdom—and Corvane's hand tightened on her sword, because she understood in one cold flash what a book could be in the hands of a keeper. Not paper. *Witness.* The old oaths had needed witnesses, and the founding witness lay broken ten feet away, and this small gray person was proposing to replace it with ink.

"Stop them," Sable Vey said, and it was not a request.

Corvane drew steel. So did six enforcers, and the lanterns came up the track like a slow burning tide, and above them all the Broken Stone stood silent in its fissure—and then, faintly, from somewhere inside the split, came a sound none of them had heard in twenty-two years.

Weeping.

Not the Wood's weeping. Something newer, closer, wetter: footsteps splashing through sap, running toward the clearing from deep in the trees, and a voice calling a name that made Dame Corvane's sword arm go numb.

"Idris?" Orsen breathed. "Idris is *alive*?"

The exile was coming—and he was coming fast, and he was not coming alone.
