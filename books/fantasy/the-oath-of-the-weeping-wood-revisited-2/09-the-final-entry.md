## 9. The Final Entry

The Broken Stone did not look broken. That was the first cruelty of it.

Orsen had expected a shattered monolith, some dramatic ruin to match the ruin of the covenant, but the Stone stood whole in its clearing—tall as three men, black-veined, slick with sap that fell in slow silver threads from its crown like rain that could not decide to fall. It wept, and had wept, for forty years. The ground around it was soft with accumulated grief, and Orsen's boots sank into it as though the earth itself wanted to hold them still.

Behind them, through the trees, came the sound of the guild arriving: boots on root, steel whispering from sheath, Sable Vey's voice cutting cold and clear as a razor through paper. Ahead, downslope, torchlight bobbed between the trunks—Corvane's soldiers, weary, mud-caked, but marching still. Between the two forces, alone at the Stone, stood a food-taster with a ledger under their arm and no weapon worth naming.

Orsen knelt and opened the ledger on their knee.

Their hands were steady. That surprised them. Forty years of swallowing poison in small doses had taught their body a terrible calm; apparently it extended even to this.

*Read aloud,* Idris had said. *Every entry. The oath does not want a sacrifice—it wants a witness. It wants everything said where the Stone can hear it.* He had been rotting gently in his hollow of roots for four decades, kept alive by the covenant's residue the way a fly is kept alive by the spider's carelessness, and when he spoke of witnessing his voice had held something Orsen had not expected to find in an enemy: hope. *Truth spoken aloud is the only coin the old magic never counterfeited. Say it all. Even the parts about me.*

The first clash came before Orsen read the first word.

It began not with a shout but with a silence—the Wood itself holding its breath—and then Corvane's soldiers broke the treeline and the guild's enforcers met them at the clearing's edge, and the night filled with the ugly music of bodies and iron. Orsen flinched. A man went down two dozen paces away with a sound like a sack of wet grain, and the sap on the grass around him turned briefly pink, as if the ground were tasting him.

*Don't watch,* Orsen told themselves. *Watch the page.*

They lifted their voice above the fighting.

"Entry one," they called, and the strange acoustics of the clearing took the words and carried them outward, into the bleeding dark. "The year of the Grey Frost. The guild records a purchase: one hundred years of harvest-memory, bought from the village of Ashenmere, price paid in grain that never rotted. The seller signed with a thumbprint because she could no longer remember how her name was spelled."

Somewhere in the melee, someone stopped fighting to listen.

"Entry two. Borrowed years, seven of them, taken from a child of six to extend the life of a magistrate. The ledger notes the child 'did not miss them.' The ledger is wrong."

A sword rang off a shield. Torches guttered. The Stone's sap fell faster, silver threads thickening, and Orsen felt the words land somewhere—not in ears but in the roots, in the deep listening dark beneath the clearing. The map in their coat had gone warm against their ribs. Rewriting itself. Testing them. *Keep going*, the warmth seemed to say.

"Entry nine." Orsen's throat tightened. They had saved this one without planning to, the way a tongue finds a sore tooth. "Sable Vey, acting for the founders' circle, sells the shared memory of both realms—the memory of the covenant itself—to purchase the guild's independence. The buyers are unnamed. The payment is power. The cost is recorded here as—" Orsen's voice cracked, and they steadied it with a taster's discipline, the same discipline that had let them smile at kings while their blood learned to survive murder. "The cost is recorded as *acceptable losses*."

The fighting faltered. Not ended—faltered—the way a storm falters when lightning shows men what they are standing in.

And through the gap in the noise, Sable Vey's voice, silk over a blade: "Close the book, Orsen."

Orsen looked up. Sable stood at the clearing's far side, enforcers around her, and forty years had not bent her spine nor dulled her eyes, which were the grey of coins worn smooth by too many hands. She did not look like a woman who had ordered a realm's memory sold. She looked like a mother disappointed in a child who had wandered into traffic.

"You were raised on these pages," Sable said. "Fed by them. Sheltered by them. And now you would read our debts aloud to the dirt?"

"Not to the dirt," Orsen said. Their voice carried farther than they intended; the Stone was amplifying them, drinking each word and giving it back larger. "To the witness. That's what you never understood, Sable. The covenant didn't break because someone attacked it. It broke because no one *said* what you'd done. You buried the crime so deep even the Wood forgot how to grieve properly. It's been weeping ever since—half-grief, half-memory, unable to finish either." They turned a page. Sap spotted the paper, and where it landed, the ink bloomed like a wound opening. "I'm finishing it."

"Kill them," Sable said quietly, and her enforcers moved—

—and Dame Corvane stepped into their path.

She was mud to the knees, one arm hanging wrong, her inquisitor's white collar grey with ash, and she planted herself between Orsen and the blades with the particular stubbornness of a woman who has already lost something worse than death. Her soldiers rallied to her. The enforcers hesitated, caught between two commands, and in that hesitation Orsen saw every face clearly: none of them wanted this. They were men who guarded ledgers, not made graves.

"The crown's terms," Corvane said, not turning her head from the enforcers, speaking to Orsen sideways, fast. "Bind the Wood as crown property. Write it into the new oath. It's the only way the throne ratifies anything—"

"No," Orsen said.

"Kavanagh, be reasonable—"

"You want the realms bound again with the Wood in chains, and Sable wants them bound with the guild holding the key." Orsen rose, the ledger open across both forearms like an offering. "Every offer tonight is a cage. So I'm making a different kind."

* * *

They read until dawn threatened and then past it.

They read the borrowed years and the bottled griefs; the names stolen and resold, the marriages purchased, the plague-year entries where the guild had traded in desperation and priced it accordingly. They read Idris's crimes—he had asked for that, had wept to ask it—and the quiet, patient erasures Sable had made in the founding record, page after razored page. They read aloud the entry Orsen hated most: *Collateral received, family of four, village of Thistledown, sold north as surety against the founder's debts. Do not record names. Names make the ledger sentimental.* Orsen's own family. Orsen's own name, omitted.

When they reached it, the clearing had gone entirely still. Soldiers and enforcers alike stood in a ring around the Stone, weapons lowered or forgotten, and sap rained down on them all, and no one wiped it away. Sable Vey stood apart, and her face had finally changed—not to guilt, Orsen thought, but to something older and more frightened: the face of a woman hearing her own ledger read back to her and discovering it had been keeping accounts she never authorized.

"The last entry," Orsen said.

The Stone's weeping slowed. Every thread of sap hung suspended, waiting.

"The covenant requires a willing payment," Orsen read, from the map that was no longer a map but a page in their own hand, written in ink they did not remember spilling. "Not coerced. Not substituted. Willing. The debt of the broken oath is memory withheld, and so the payment must be something held dear and surrendered whole." They looked up. Corvane watched them with her sister's grief plain on her face. Sable watched them with calculation failing into something almost like awe.

"What are you doing?" Sable whispered.

"Paying," Orsen said.

They took out the small knife every taster carried, the one for peeling fruit that might be poisoned, and drew it once across the meat of their thumb, and pressed their blood to the final line. And they gave the Stone the only thing they had that was truly theirs: the immunity. Forty years of small poisons, the tolerance built dose by dose, the armor that had let an invisible servant survive a court of knives—they felt it leave them the way heat leaves a stone at evening. Not pain. Just absence. A door closing quietly inside their own body.

They swayed. The world tilted, ordinary and sudden. For the first time since childhood, Orsen Kavanagh was exactly as vulnerable as any other person standing in a dangerous place at the end of a long night, and the terror of it was so clean and so simple that they nearly laughed.

"I, Orsen Kavanagh," they said, and their voice was only a human voice now, thin and mortal and carrying anyway, "keeper of the Weeping Wood's ledgers, do set down the last account as paid. Truth witnessed. Debt entered. Oath—"

The Stone finished it.

The voice came up out of the roots and the rock and the forty years of falling sap, vast and genderless and tired, a voice like a forest remembering how to speak: *"—oath reforged."*

The sap reversed.

Every silver thread that had fallen for four decades lifted, streaming upward into the black-veined crown, and the Stone drank back its own grief, and the veins of it flushed from black to a green so deep it was nearly gold. Warmth rolled out through the clearing like the first morning of a kinder year. Where it touched the wounded, wounds closed. Where it touched Sable Vey, she staggered as memory poured back into her—the full weight of everything she had sold, returned with interest—and she went to her knees in the soft earth, weeping, for the first time in living memory, alongside everyone else the covenant touched.

Corvane caught Orsen as they folded.

"You fool," the inquisitor said, hoarse, holding them up. "You absolute—" She couldn't finish it. Her grip said the rest.

Orsen looked past her shoulder, at the Stone standing whole and silent now, its weeping done, and felt the ledger go light in their arms. Lighter than paper should be. Lighter than a life's accounting should be.

In the treeline, half-shadowed, Idris was dissolving into root and moss with a smile like a man setting down a load he'd carried forty years—and beside the Stone, where no one had seen her arrive, a figure in guild grey bent to pick up the fallen map, and Orsen's heart stopped, because the figure turned, and under the hood was a face Orsen knew better than any ledger line.

Their own face, smiling.

"*Now,*" it said, "*the epilogue.*"
