## 10. Where the Wood Stops Weeping

The silence came first.

Orsen had expected a sound—the crack of stone, a thunderclap of released power, some great exhalation to mark the mending of what had been broken for forty years. Instead there was only quiet: the sudden absence of weeping, like a door closing on a room where someone had cried for decades. The sap stopped running down the Broken Stone's scarred face mid-drop, one bead hanging at the lip of the crack, refusing to fall.

Then the bead fell. And the Wood breathed.

Orsen felt it move through them like water through dry ground—memory, unbound and pouring home. It came not as images but as *weight*, the accumulated grief and joy of two realms finding their old channels again. They sank to their knees on the moss, hands flat against the earth, and let it fill them. Their mother's voice, singing in a kitchen they had almost forgotten. The smell of bread and tallow candles. A brother's laugh, high and cracked with youth—a brother whose name had been sold before Orsen could learn it, and which now arrived like a gift left too long at a door: *Tomas*. His name was Tomas.

They wept then, openly, without shame, in front of Dame Corvane and the scattered enforcers who still stood among the trees with drawn steel and no idea what to do with it. They wept for Tomas, for the parents collateralized against a debt that was never theirs, for every borrowed year that had passed through their ledgers into strangers' veins. The tears were ordinary salt. That was the miracle of them.

When Orsen looked up, Corvane was watching them from ten paces off, her inquisitor's coat torn at the shoulder, her face unreadable in the way of people who have spent a lifetime making themselves unreadable. But her hand was nowhere near her sword.

"It's holding," she said. Not a question. She had laid her palm against the Broken Stone while Orsen read the ledger aloud, and whatever she had felt through it, she was still feeling it now.

"Yes."

Corvane nodded slowly. Then she did something no inquisitor of the crown should ever have done: she took out her report—the formal account of the Broken Stone, of Sable Vey's arrival, of the food-taster who had unmade a guild with a ledger—and fed it page by page to a small blue flame she conjured between her fingers.

"The crown will want answers," Orsen said.

"The crown wants stability," Corvane answered. "It will get stability. The covenant holds; the borders are quiet; the Wood has stopped bleeding. What I witnessed at this stone is a matter I am still investigating." She met Orsen's eyes, and beneath the iron of her composure something moved—grief, old and banked, finally allowed its proper name. "My sister would have liked you, Kavanagh. She trusted ledgers too. She believed that if everything was written down honestly, no one could lie about what happened." A pause. "She was wrong, but it was a beautiful wrongness. Keep writing anyway."

* * *

The tribunal convened three days later, in the guildhall above the Dripping Gate, in the long chamber where impossible transactions were once sealed with wax and blood. Orsen attended as witness—not as keeper, not as taster, just as witness—and sat through eleven hours of testimony with the patience of a person who had spent years swallowing poison in small doses and could therefore swallow anything.

Sable Vey stood before the assembled elders in grey, stripped of her rings. She did not deny anything. That was somehow worse than denial. When the eldest reader asked if she acknowledged the founding crime—that the covenant's shared memory had been sold, deliberately, by the guild's own founders, to purchase their power over both realms—she said only, "I acknowledge it," in the level voice of a merchant confirming a price.

She looked at Orsen once across the chamber. There was no hatred in it. There was something more terrible: recognition. As if she had always known the ledger would come due, and had merely hoped to die before the audit.

The sentence was exile from office, forfeiture of her accounts, and surrender to the Wood's own judgment—which, everyone understood, meant walking into the trees that remembered everything and letting them decide what to do with a woman who had helped teach them to forget. Sable Vey accepted it standing straight, and when she passed Orsen on her way out, she paused.

"You counted honestly," she said. "In forty years, no one else did. Whatever else I built on lies, I chose you well."

"I wasn't chosen," Orsen said. "I was collateral. You told me so yourself, in the Wood."

A flicker crossed her face—pain, or its close cousin, pride wounded past pretending. "Yes," she said quietly. "So I did." And she walked out into the green dark, and the trees closed behind her, and the guildhall exhaled.

* * *

The cellar shrine took an afternoon to empty.

Orsen worked alone, by candlelight, carrying each item up into the ordinary air of the alley behind the chandler's shop: the brass scales that had weighed borrowed years; the bottles of grief, returned to the fence Marta for distribution back to their original owners, free of charge, per the covenant's new arithmetic; the founding ledgers themselves, wrapped in oilcloth for delivery to the royal archive, where Corvane had promised they would be catalogued honestly—or as honestly as archives ever manage.

Last of all, Orsen's own ledger. The one begun in a stolen notebook at fifteen, when a frightened apprentice taster started recording every debt the guild pretended didn't exist. Every entry was paid now, or discharged, or transferred to parties who would honor it. Only the final line remained, and it remained because Orsen could not bring themselves to ink it yet: *Paid in full — the taster's tongue.* The immunity itself, surrendered at the stone. Already they could feel the difference. The candle smoke, which they had breathed carelessly for twenty years, stung now. The crust of bread they ate tasted of dust and something faintly bitter, and they could not know if it was harmless.

Ordinariness, they were learning, was its own kind of vulnerability. It fit strangely, like shoes bought for someone else. But it fit.

They dipped the pen. Wrote the last entry. Closed the book.

For a long moment they stood in the empty cellar, listening to the drip of the hidden spring that had given the gate its name. The walls still smelled of wax and myrrh and decades of secret commerce. Someone would find this place eventually, or they wouldn't. Either way, nothing here needed keeping anymore.

Orsen set the ledger atop the altar stone, open to the final page, and left the candle burning beside it—a small light for whoever came next.

* * *

The city received them like a stranger, which was precisely right.

Orsen walked the evening streets with no hood, no ledger, no purpose except walking. Vendors shouted prices. Children chased a hoop along the cobbles. A woman argued cheerfully with a fishmonger about the freshness of his eels, and somewhere a fiddler butchered a dance tune badly enough that people danced anyway. All of it ordinary. All of it, Orsen realized, was what they had spent a lifetime protecting without knowing how to look at it directly.

At the corner of Tanner's Row, a baker's boy thrust a roll at them—"end of day, nearly stale, take it, take it"—and Orsen took it and bit into it and tasted, unmistakably, nothing poisonous at all. Just bread. Just yeast and salt and the small miracle of wheat. Their eyes stung, and they let them.

Memory was returning everywhere. They heard it in fragments all down the street: an old man suddenly weeping over a locket, calling a name no one in his family recognized; two sisters in a doorway staring at each other as though seeing each other for the first time in thirty years, which perhaps they were; a whole tavern falling silent and then erupting as someone's grandmother remembered, out loud and all at once, the summer before the forgetting. The realm was grieving and rejoicing in the same breath, and it would be months, years, before anyone learned how to hold both at once. But they would learn. The covenant held. The channels were open.

Near the river, where the city thinned into warehouses and the air smelled of tar, Orsen found a bench overlooking the black water, and found Dame Corvane already sitting on it, out of uniform, looking remarkably like just a tired woman watching the current.

"They gave me a commendation," she said without turning. "For resolving the matter of the Weeping Wood. I'm considering retiring to the coast and becoming insufferable about my roses."

"And the report?"

"Still under investigation." She smiled faintly. "Some matters resist conclusion."

They sat together in silence for a while, two people who had tried to kill each other at a broken stone and had instead, between them, mended the world's oldest promise. It seemed enough. It seemed, for the first time in Orsen's remembered life, like more than enough.

Eventually Corvane rose, wished them well in the tone of someone who meant it, and walked away into the lamplight.

Orsen stayed. The river moved. Across the water, far beyond the rooftops, the Weeping Wood stood dark against the last of the dusk—and if one looked closely, one might have seen it shimmering, not with sorrow but with something quieter: attention, perhaps, or gratitude, or simply the deep green patience of a thing that remembers everything and forgives most.

Orsen finished the baker's roll, brushed the crumbs from their hands, and thought about tomorrow—an entire day with no entries due, no poisons pending, no debts outstanding. A blank page. The first one they had ever owned.

They stood, and turned toward the lit streets, and began—visible, mortal, unburdened—to walk into it.
