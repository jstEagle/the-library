## 1. The Smuggler's Psalter

The mule knew the path better than Ulyana did, which was a mercy, because by the third hour of the night climb their eyes had stopped being eyes and become two smears of cold.

Thornfell did not have roads so much as grudges—switchbacks the mountain held against travelers, narrow ledges where the rock remembered landslides and resented being trusted again. Snow came sideways in the high passes, hissing like sand, and the wind found every seam of a body's clothing with the patience of a pickpocket. Ulyana pulled the wool scarf higher over their nose and kept one gloved fist knotted in the mule's lead rope, letting the animal's stubborn certainty drag them both upward through the dark.

Below and behind, the lanterns of Grayharbor were swallowed already. Ahead, nothing. That was the trade: you carried what couldn't be carried honestly, and in exchange you learned to love the dark for hiding you.

*Unbranded saltwine under the grain sacks. Tallow candles stamped with a temple seal that was never paid for. And this* — Ulyana patted the flat oilcloth bundle strapped to the mule's other flank, feeling the corners of books through the wrapping — *whatever Brek Onda's buyer wanted badly enough to pay in silver instead of promises.*

They had stopped asking what they carried years ago. Asking questions in Thornfell was how you ended up a story told quietly in taverns, usually ending in *and no one ever found the body.*

The snow thickened. The pass narrowed until the walls rose on either side like the shoulders of something crouched and waiting, and Ulyana ducked their head beneath an overhang furred with icicles, each one hanging a hand's length down, dripping in the windless pocket of air there — *drip, drip, drip* — a sound so much like counting that it made their skin crawl.

Habit was a strange animal. It lived in the hands and the throat long after the mind had starved it out.

Ulyana's lips moved before they noticed. Words, half-swallowed by the scarf, shaped by muscle memory older than their smuggling name, older than *Ulyana Zhou*, even — because that wasn't the name their mistress had given them, but names could be shed like coats, and this one had fit through seven winters of running.

*"Veyl who weeps, Veyl who keeps,"* the old cadence went, *"keep me walking, keep me sleeping—"*

They caught themselves at *sleeping.* Stopped. Laughed once, a small bitter sound that the overhang threw back at them doubled.

Dead god. Everyone said so. The priests in Grayharbor said so with the brisk embarrassment of people discussing a bankrupt uncle; the old women in the mountain villages said so with more grief, leaving bowls of milk on doorstones out of habit, the way you still set a place for someone ten years buried. A century silent, the scholars wrote. A century since any prayer to the wood-gods of Thornfell had been answered with anything but weather. The Weeping Wood itself — the black heart of the range, where no one logged and no one hunted and even smugglers gave three days' distance — had been dying tree by tree for longer than anyone living could remember. Standing dead silver trunks like candle stubs burned down to nothing.

Ulyana had been taught those prayers kneeling on a cold flagstone floor by a woman who smelled of ash and juniper, whose lessons always cost something. They had run from her. They had left her cottage burning behind them in the night — not lit by their hand, never by their hand, but they hadn't gone back to carry water either, and that distinction had grown thinner every year until now it was gossamer.

*Keep me walking,* their mouth insisted, finishing the line without permission, because habit did not care about theology.

And the mountain answered.

Not with thunder. Not with anything so honest. It was smaller than that, and therefore worse: the dripping from the icicles above them stopped. All together. Mid-fall. As if the whole overhang had drawn breath.

Ulyana stood in the sudden enormous silence with their hand on the mule's rope and felt, distinctly, the sensation of being *seen.*

Seven years of invisibility — of slouched shoulders and averted eyes and a voice pitched to be forgettable — and all of it peeled away in one instant, like a blanket snatched off a sleeper. Something looked at them out of the dark of the pass, and it did not look the way men looked, measuring and wanting. It looked the way winter looks when it decides whether you'll survive the night.

The mule screamed. Not brayed — screamed, a human sound, and wrenched free of the rope. Ulyana lunged, missed, went down hard on one knee in the snow, and watched their cargo and their livelihood bolt back down the switchback into darkness, oilcloth bundles leaping against its flank like wounded wings.

"Marek's teeth—" They got up. Took two steps after it.

Then the sky opened.

There was no other way to say it. Above the notch of the pass, between two peaks black against the faint gray of cloud-glow, a light fell — not streaked like a meteor but *descending*, slow, deliberate, a white coal sinking through the air with the unhurried grace of something that had all night and knew it. It came down past the tree line. Down into the fold of the mountain where, Ulyana knew with a cartographer's sick certainty, the first dead trees of the Weeping Wood began.

It touched ground without sound. The silence afterward was total — no wind, no drip, no mule, only Ulyana's own heartbeat banging away like a fist on a locked door.

And then a voice. Not in the ears. Deeper in, somewhere behind the sternum, spoken into them the way you'd whisper into cupped hands.

***"You prayed."***

Ulyana did not run. Later, they would wonder at that — seven years of running, and when the moment finally demanded it, their legs simply declined. They stood in the snow with their heart trying to exit through their ribs and heard their own voice come out thin and cracked and absurdly polite:

"That's — no. Habit. It doesn't count."

A pause. The dark seemed to consider this.

***"Prayers don't need to count,"*** the voice said, and it sounded tired, the way old doors sound tired, ***"they only need to be true. Yours was the first true one in a hundred years. Do you understand what that means?"***

"No," Ulyana whispered. "I understand that I'm talking to a dead god in a pass full of stolen candles, and I'd very much like to wake up now."

Something that was almost laughter moved through the silence — or maybe just wind returning, finding its work again. The icicles resumed dripping. *Drip, drip, drip.* The world's ordinary machinery grinding back into motion around a hole where a miracle had stood.

***"I am in the wood,"*** the voice said, softer now, as if spending strength it could ill afford. ***"Where the trees die. Come and see whether I am dead. Bring nothing. Tell no one. You will want to tell no one anyway, little oath-keeper, once you understand what is waking beside me."***

*Oath-keeper.*

The word went through Ulyana like a hook through soft flesh. No one had called them that. No one *could* have — it existed in exactly one place, in a grimoire that should have burned with the cottage, in a life they had amputated and cauterized and left bleeding on a flagstone floor seven years ago.

"I'm not anything," they said aloud, to the empty pass, to the falling snow, to whatever listened. "I'm nobody. I carry other people's sins over a mountain and forget them by morning. That's the whole shape of me."

The silence was kind, which was somehow the cruelest thing about it.

***"Yes,"*** said the voice of Veyl who wept, Veyl who kept, Veyl whom everyone in Thornfell agreed had been dead for a hundred years. ***"That is why I chose your prayer."***

Then it was gone, entirely, and the wind returned all at once with a shove that nearly took Ulyana off their feet, and the pass was only a pass again — cold, indifferent, full of nothing but weather and the tracks of a runaway mule stitching down the mountainside.

Ulyana stood a long time.

Snow gathered on their shoulders and in their hair and they let it, because moving meant deciding, and deciding meant admitting what had happened, and admitting it would make it real in the way that things became real when you spoke them. For seven years they had built a self out of smallness: quiet steps, quiet hands, a face no one remembered. Invisible as frost forming on glass. And in the space of a hundred heartbeats, something ancient had looked straight through all of it, had known the name under the name, had called them by a title they'd buried deeper than any grave.

Being seen, it turned out, felt like being flayed and warmed at once.

Their breath steamed. Somewhere below, the mule was no doubt eating someone's winter hay and ending Ulyana's career with every mouthful. Somewhere ahead, down in the dying wood, a light sat on the snow where a god had fallen out of a century of silence like a man too weak to stand.

*Come and see whether I am dead.*

Every instinct they owned — every scar-earned rule of survival — said walk away. Go down to Grayharbor, find Brek, eat something hot, forget. Prayers were answered for saints and fools, and Ulyana had spent seven years making sure they were neither.

But habit was a strange animal. It lived in the hands and the throat.

And their feet, without consulting them at all, had already turned toward the wood.

* * *

Three days later, at the edge of the Weeping Wood, where the first silver-dead trees stood like the bones of a congregation, Ulyana stopped at the border of shadows and saw, far in among the trunks, a figure tending a small fire — and the figure raised its head, though no footfall had made a sound, and looked directly at them across the frozen ground.

"You came slowly," said the god, in a voice that was, horribly, almost human. "Sit. We have very little time, and I remember everything, and that is going to be a problem for both of us."
