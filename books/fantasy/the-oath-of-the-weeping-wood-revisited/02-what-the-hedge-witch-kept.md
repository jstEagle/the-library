## 2. What the Hedge-Witch Kept

The cottage had burned the winter Ulyana left, and they had always told themselves those two facts were strangers to each other.

Now, standing at the tree line with the frost of early spring still silvering the bracken, they could no longer hold that story in their hands without it crumbling. The ruin sat in its clearing the way a wound sits in flesh—black beams like splintered bone, the chimney still upright and stubborn, trailing no smoke at all. Smoke would have meant someone lived there. No smoke meant what it had meant for seven years: nobody.

*Go back,* the voice under the wood had said. Not in words, exactly. In the way that a falling star is not exactly light. *What she kept, you must keep.*

Ulyana's boots had carried them down out of Thornfell's high passes on smuggler legs, quick and quiet, before their heart could file an objection. Now their heart objected anyway. It objected in Mistress Iveta's voice, which was the worst possible way for anything to object.

*You run when you're afraid,* the old woman used to say, kneading bread or grinding yarrow, not even looking up. *That's honest. But don't lie to yourself about which one you're doing.*

They were lying to themselves right now, probably. About whether this was courage or just another kind of running—running backward, downhill, into the past.

They crossed the clearing anyway.

---

The door had never been a door since the fire; it was a charred suggestion of one, leaning into the frame like a drunk against a wall. Ulyana pushed it and it gave with a soft sigh of rot, and the smell hit them—the smell of old burning soaked deep into everything, tar and ash and something underneath it that was only ever going to be *Iveta*: dried wormwood, tallow, the particular bitterness of her tea.

Seven years, and the smell was still here. Grief has good storage habits.

Inside, the light fell gray through the broken roof in long dusty columns, and Ulyana stood in it and let themselves be small again. That was the trick of returning to a place you'd abandoned: your body remembered its old size. Their hands looked wrong against the air, too big, scarred from rope work and mountain cold, hands that had hauled contraband and never once ground yarrow since.

"I'm sorry," they said aloud, because the silence demanded some offering and they had nothing else.

The silence did not forgive them. It didn't condemn them either. It just held the words, the way the cottage held the smell, patiently, without opinion.

Ulyana moved through the rooms like a trespasser at a funeral. The hearthstone was cracked down the middle—a clean split, as if struck—and they remembered, absurdly, how Iveta had scolded them for setting a kettle directly on the iron rather than the trivet. The bed frame was a black lattice. In the corner where the herb shelves had been, there was only soot-shaped absence, rows of ghosts where jars had stood: round shadows on the scorched stone, thyme, comfrey, boneset, each one labeled by its absence.

And there, half-buried under a fallen rafter near the cold hearth—

A shape. A box shape. Iron-banded, fire-scarred, but intact, because Iveta had warded everything she loved twice and her love had been mostly iron and stubbornness.

Ulyana knelt. The lid came up hard, shrieking on rusted hinges, loud enough in the silence to feel blasphemous.

Inside lay the grimoire.

It was smaller than they remembered. Everything from childhood is larger; everything from guilt is heavier. It was bound in gray leather gone green at the corners, and the clasp was a hand of hammered tin, small and slightly crooked, and Ulyana knew that hand—knew it because they had watched Iveta make it at this very hearth, muttering, saying it wasn't a lock, it was a *promise*, and child-Ulyana had asked, *a promise to whom?* and gotten no answer, only a look over spectacles that said *you'll find out, and I pray you find out late.*

Their fingers shook. They made themselves steady. They opened it.

---

The first pages were ordinary craft. Salves. Weather-reading. A recipe for fever tea annotated in Iveta's crabbed hand: *more willow, less honey—the body must be persuaded, not bribed.* Ulyana read these and wept a little, quietly, without drama, the way water finds cracks. These pages smelled of the living woman. They turned them slowly.

Then, a third of the way in, the handwriting changed.

Not Iveta's. Older. The ink brown-black, the letters tall and narrow, pressed hard enough into the vellum to leave grooves on the reverse. And beneath that older hand, in the margins, in faded pencil, someone had translated—someone whose pencil strokes Ulyana knew as well as their own heartbeat, because they'd been taught their letters by that hand.

*Iveta copied this,* Ulyana realized. *She found it, or was given it, and she copied it, and she never told me.*

The fragment ran perhaps forty lines. It was not a spell. Spells have instructions. This had the cadence of a contract—or a confession. The old tongue was stiff in Ulyana's mouth as they sounded it out, but the pencil translation walked beside it like a lantern:

*In the year the wood began to weep, the oath was sworn at its heart, by blood freely spilled and memory freely given, that the gods should not forget the world, nor the world the gods.*

*Keepers of the covenant shall be of the line of Zhou, whose blood remembers what mouths forget.*

*Let there be, in every generation, one who keeps the wood's account. Let them walk the weeping trees and name what dies, and carry the names to the roots.*

*If the keepers fail—if the line runs dry, or turns away—then shall the binding thin, and the gods fall silent, and the wood begin to die, tree by tree by tree.*

Zhou.

Ulyana read the name four times. The word refused to become familiar. It sat on the page like a stranger wearing their mother's coat.

They had been born Ulyana Zhou in a river town they'd left at nine years old, sold in all but name to a hedge-witch who needed hands and got a heart instead. They had thought *Zhou* was nothing—a father's name, a father they barely recalled, a man who mended nets and died of damp lungs. Fishermen didn't have lineages. Fishermen had boats, and then they didn't.

But the page said otherwise. The page said *blood remembers what mouths forget,* and Ulyana thought of the prayer they'd spoken into the pass three nights ago, out of pure habit, to a god everyone said was dead—and the star that had fallen, and the voice that had risen out of the weeping wood like sap rising, warm and impossible:

*You kept my name. All this time, you kept it.*

They hadn't understood then. They thought it was gratitude. It wasn't gratitude. It was *recognition.* The god hadn't heard a stranger praying. He'd heard a keeper, keeping.

"No," Ulyana said to the empty room. "No, she should have told me."

The accusation rang false even as it left their mouth, and they knew why, and the knowing was worse. Because there, folded into the grimoire's spine where the binding had cracked open, was a packet of letters. Tied with waxed thread. Each envelope bore no name at all, only a single word in Iveta's hand:

*Keeper.*

---

They read them by the gray columned light, kneeling on the floor of the woman they'd abandoned, and the letters were dated across seven years—one for every year Ulyana had been gone.

*To the Keeper, who will find this when I am past explaining myself—*

The first was short. Almost angry.

*You'll want to hate me for not telling you. Good. Hate is a kind of attention, and you'll need attention for what follows. Your bloodline swore the oath, child—I never learned which shape you'd grow into and it never mattered, and I mean that kindly: it never mattered, until now. I found this fragment twenty years ago in the root-cellar of a drowned chapel. I spent ten years hoping I'd misread it. Then I met you, and stopped hoping, and started dreading.*

*You were nine. What was I supposed to do? Hand a child a dying god and say, mind him?*

*So I taught you everything except the one thing. I taught you the herbs and the wards and the old tongue, and I told myself I was waiting until you were ready, and the truth is I was waiting until *I* was brave. I am not brave. You noticed. You ran.*

The second letter was longer, softer, dated two winters after the fire.

*The fire was not an accident. I'll write that plainly once and never again: someone came asking questions. Crown men, or dressed like crown men. They asked if I kept old oaths. I burned my own cottage myself, to give them ashes instead of answers, and I went to ground in the low country, and if you are reading this, I am dead, because I wrote these letters in case the ground proved shallower than my luck.*

*Do not look for me. Look for the wood. The trees are dying, aren't they? I felt it even here, the way you feel a tooth go bad in a stranger's mouth. Every tree that falls is a sentence of the oath going dark. Someone has to keep the count.*
