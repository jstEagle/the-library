## 3. The Company of Small Treasons

Brek Onda had smuggled saints' knuckles, tax-dodgers' gold, and once—though she'd sworn never to speak of it—a live basilisk egg packed in wool and prayer. She knew what contraband looked like when it walked in the door on its own two feet.

Ulyana Zhou stood in the doorway of the Wayhouse at Cold Saddle with snow melting off their shoulders and a look on their face Brek recognized from her own mirror: someone who had run so long that standing still felt like drowning.

"Captain," Ulyana said. "I need to get over the Thornfell passes before the melt."

"The melt's six weeks out."

"Then I need you to lie about the weather."

Behind them, the door swung shut on wind that smelled wrong for midwinter—not pine and stone but something green, something growing, as if spring had followed Ulyana up the mountain like a dog that wouldn't be called home.

Brek poured two cups of plum brandy without being asked. "Sit down, Zhou. You look like a starved crow."

"I've been told I'm worse than that."

"You're worse than that." Brek slid the cup across. "Drink. Then tell me why there are crown riders in every market town between here and the coast asking after a hedge-witch's runaway by description instead of name."

Ulyana drank. Their hand shook only a little, which was better than Brek expected. "Because they don't have my name."

"They will." Brek leaned back against the bar, boots crossed at the ankle, and studied them. Ten years since Ulyana Zhou had slipped away from her crew in the night at Graywater Landing, owing her eleven marks and one apology. They'd come back older, thinner, and carrying something behind their ribs that made the lamplight bend toward them like it wanted to listen. "You know what I do to people who bring heat down on my route."

"Kill them?"

"No. That's quick, and I'm not quick." She let that sit. "I leave them somewhere the cold can take its time. So understand me when I say: whatever's following you, I need to know if it has legs."

Ulyana set down the cup. For a long moment they said nothing, and the fire popped, and somewhere upstairs one of Brek's crew snored like a landslide. Then Ulyana reached into their coat and laid something on the bar between them: a scrap of vellum, scorched at the edges, covered in ink that seemed to move when you weren't looking directly at it.

"It's a fragment," they said. "Of an oath. A covenant, maybe. My mistress kept it hidden inside her grimoire, under a false binding, and she burned rather than give it up—or the inquisitor burned her; we don't know which came first. But Halvard wants what I'm carrying, and he'll burn anyone who helps me carry it."

The ink moved again. Brek had seen forged relics before, had sold enough of them. This wasn't forgery. Forgery didn't make your back teeth ache.

"And why," she said slowly, "would you bring this *here*? To me. Who left you on a dock in Graywater with no boat and my knife in your boot."

"Because you were the only person who ever lied well enough to keep me alive," Ulyana said. "And because every other road ends with Halvard."

Brek picked up the fragment. The ink crawled under her thumb like something sleeping. Outside, the wind changed again—that impossible green smell—and far off in the dark, she could have sworn she heard water falling where there was no waterfall.

*Gods below,* she thought, and then corrected herself: *or above, or waking up, or whatever they do.*

She put the fragment down. "We'll talk in the morning. Full crew. You sleep in the cellar where the salt pork is, because nothing stupid lives near the salt pork."

"Brek—"

"*Morning,* Zhou. And if you run again, I'm keeping your deposit." She held out her palm. Ulyana stared at it, then laughed—one short, cracked sound, like ice giving way—and pressed the eleven marks into it, plus interest.

* * *

They argued until the candles drowned in their own wax.

Tamsin, who ran the river leg and had a voice like gravel in honey, said it plainest: "There's a price on their head, Brek. A big one. Enough to buy us all new boats and retire somewhere warm. We could solve our whole problem tonight."

"We could also hang ourselves with the reward rope," said Doss, who was old and had been old for as long as anyone remembered. "Inquisitors don't pay informers who deal with heretics. They question informers. Have you met Halvard's people? They ask questions with pliers."

"So we don't inform. We just—" Tamsin made a vague gesture toward the cellar stairs. "Lose track of them somewhere past the second pass. Bad luck. Snowslide."

"That's murder with extra steps."

"That's *business*, Doss."

Brek let them go around twice more, the way you let a rope twist before you anchor it. Around the table sat nine faces she trusted with her life, which is to say nine faces who would each sell her out differently if the price was right, and she loved every one of them with the specific fierce love of a woman who had chosen her family the way you choose a route: carefully, knowing the hazards.

"There's a tenth thing nobody's saying," she said finally. The room went quiet. "That thing they brought isn't paper. It answered me. When I touched it, I heard—" She stopped. How did you say it? *I heard snowmelt in winter. I heard a century of silence ending.* "The gods aren't dead, Tamsin. Whatever the temples taught us about the Quiet Century, whatever the crown says—the wood at the heart of Thornfell is weeping again, and prayers are landing somewhere. And Halvard is burning shrines from here to the sea because he knows it too."

Doss spoke softly. "If the covenant's real—if it's unraveling like the priests whispered before they got defrocked or dead—then it doesn't matter whether we shelter Zhou or not. It matters whose side we're standing on when the sky finds out."

"My grandmother used to leave milk out," Tamsin said, unexpectedly. "For the house-gods. Said her mother did, and hers. We all stopped when the Quiet came, because what's the use feeding something that won't answer?" She turned her cup in her hands. "But I never quite stopped feeling stupid about the milk."

Silence. Then, from the corner chair where he'd been sitting all evening unnoticed, in the way that some men learn to sit unnoticed, the lodger said:

"May I?"

Brek had taken him in three weeks ago—Sef, he'd given as a name, a gray defrocked priest who paid in honest coin and asked only for a room facing east. He rose now, and even stooped he was tall, and when he pulled back his hood the firelight caught a face carved by forty years of weather and five years of shame.

"Sef," Brek warned.

"You have something on this table that belongs in a reliquary," he said, "and you're passing it around like a gambling chit. May I see it?"

She shouldn't have. Every instinct she owned said don't. But the room wanted it, and Brek Onda had learned long ago that a crew is a tide—you can fight it or sail it—and so she slid the fragment across the boards to the disgraced man of god.

Sef went white when he touched it.

Not pale. *White*, the white of paper, of bone, and his breath came in once and stopped, and Tamsin half-rose, and he raised a hand—*wait*—and knelt there holding the scorched vellum like a man holding a child he'd buried.

"This is the Oath of the Weeping Wood," he said. His voice had gone strange, liturgical, the voice he must have had before the church stripped it out of him. "This is the *first clause*. 'By blood remembered and freely bound.' I read copies in seminary. Copies with the living ink scraped out and replaced with lead, because the originals were ordered destroyed in the third year of the Quiet." He looked up at Brek, and his eyes were wet. "Where did they get this?"

"From their dead mistress's grimoire," Brek said.

"Then their mistress died protecting the most dangerous document in the mortal realm." Sef folded the fragment with unbearable gentleness and held it out—not to Brek, but to the stairwell, where Ulyana Zhou had come up silent as smoke to stand listening in the doorway. "You should know what you're carrying, oath-keeper."

"Don't call me that."

"It's what the blood says."

"It's what the blood says," Ulyana repeated bitterly, "and the blood is exactly the problem."

"No." Sef rose fully upright now, and something had kindled in him that Brek suspected hadn't burned since the day they took his orders away. "The problem is that a hundred years ago the gods went silent and everyone decided that meant they were gone, and we built a comfortable world on top of that lie, and now the lie is dying tree by tree in a wood full of graves. And the problem is that an inquisitor is coming up these mountains with fire in his ledger. And the problem—" his voice caught, gentled, "—is that you have been alone with this for too long, and no one carries a covenant alone. Not even gods do. That's the whole point of it."

The fire crackled. Nine smugglers looked at each other across a table that suddenly felt like an altar.

"Well," said Doss at last, into his cup. "I'm too old to die rich anyway."

Tamsin laughed, rough and startled. Someone else—little Wren, who hadn't spoken in the whole debate—said quietly, "My mother prayed every night for ten years after the Quiet took my brother. If somebody up there finally heard her, I'd walk into Halvard's camp barefoot before I'd sell that person out." And that was that; that was how it always went with crews, one voice tipping the tide.

Brek looked at Ulyana in the doorway, snow still in their hair, wearing the expression of someone who had braced for a knife and been handed a seat at the table instead.

"Eleven marks and interest," she said. "You're paid up through the passes. We leave tomorrow, before dawn, the high route—Halvard's riders watch the low ones. You travel as my cousin's kid from the coast, mute if anyone asks, and if the crown asks *me*, I never saw your face." She jabbed a finger at them. "And Zhou. You don't vanish in the night this time. You want to run, you come tell me first, and I'll decide if I let you."

Something moved through Ulyana's face—grief and relief braided tight together, guilt with nowhere to hide. "Why?" they asked. "After Graywater. After everything. Why help me?"

Brek shrugged, uncomfortable, and reached for the brandy. "Because somebody answered your prayer, kid. And I've been praying since I was nine years old, same as everybody, into a silence so thick we forgot we were doing it." She poured. "If the silence broke for you, I want to be standing next to you when it breaks for the rest of us."

* * *

They left at the hour the stars go gray, twelve souls and two mules on the high trail above Cold Saddle, breath smoking, boots crunching frost. Behind them the wayhouse sank back into the dark. Ahead the Thornfells stood up black against a sky that was doing something it hadn't done in a hundred years.

Sef saw it first. He stopped in the trace, head tipped back, and the whole column halted behind him without a word, because you don't talk over a priest when he looks like that.

Over the highest peak, where the weeping wood slept under snow, the clouds had opened into a ragged wound of clear air—and through it fell a single star, slow and deliberate, trailing green fire, descending not like something burning but like something *arriving*.

It sank behind the ridge. The mountain drank it.

For three heartbeats, nothing. Then, faint across the miles of frozen forest, every mule and every human heard the same thing: the creak and groan of ancient timber flexing, branches lifting, sap moving in trees that should have been asleep—as if a wood the size of a small country had drawn one long, deep breath.

"Wake up, wake up," Doss whispered, though it wasn't a warning. It sounded like a greeting.

Brek pulled her collar up and started walking. "Passes close in four hours. Move."

But Ulyana stood a moment longer, staring at the ridge where the light had gone down, and Brek, watching them, thought: *they know who that is.* And thought, less comfortably: *and by the time we reach the heart of that wood, so am I going to know. And God help us all—or whoever's left to be helped.*

Far off, beneath the snow, beneath the roots, something old turned over in its sleep and began, very quietly, to remember a name.

It was not its own.
