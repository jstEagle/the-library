## 4. A God Who Remembers Being Human

The first thing Veyl remembered was the taste of rain.

Not the rain of the last century—that thin, indifferent drip that had fallen through his branches while he slept—but real rain, warm and sudden, the kind he had once caught in cupped hands as a boy in the lowland villages when the sky broke open after drought. He had been human then. He had had a name that was not *Veyl*, a name his mother called across barley fields at dusk, a name that the oath had taken from him like a tooth pulled clean, leaving a socket of ache where it used to sit.

He woke to the weeping wood, and the wood was dying.

He felt each death the way a man feels a hair pulled from his scalp: small, precise, maddening. Somewhere north of his sleeping heart, an alder with three centuries of memory in its rings went gray and hollow. The covenant frayed another thread. He could feel the whole weave of it now that he was awake enough to notice—could feel the looseness in it, the way a sleeper feels a blanket sliding off in winter. A century of silence, and beneath the silence, the slow unraveling of the promise that held the mortal world and the old dark divinity together like mortar between stones.

*They stopped remembering us,* he thought, and even thinking hurt, because thought came slowly, creaking, like a door unopened for a hundred years.

His body, such as it was, lay folded into the roots of the eldest willow at the heart of the wood—the Oath-Tree, the first witness, the place where blood had been spilled on bark and words had been spoken that neither gods nor mortals could unsay. Sap moved through him instead of blood. Moss grew along what had once been shoulders. His eyes, if he opened them, were knots in the wood, and through them he saw the world in slants of green-gray light.

He opened them.

Dusk. Snow beginning, fine as sifted flour. And there—at the edge of his awareness, stumbling up the frozen streambed with a stolen lantern guttering against the wind—was a mortal.

Veyl knew mortals the way he knew weather. Most of them passed through the wood the way wind passes through a house: rattling things, taking nothing, leaving nothing. But this one dragged prayer behind them like a torn hem. He could smell it. Old habit, half-abandoned, muttered under the breath the way smugglers mutter countersigns—and addressed to *him*. To a god everyone said was dead.

*Ulyana Zhou.*

He did not know how he knew the name. Perhaps the wood told him; perhaps the covenant fragment their bloodline carried sang its little song into the roots, and the roots whispered upward. The Zhou line. Oath-keepers by inheritance, by accident of birth. Runners, too—he could taste that in them, the salt-sweat-and-fear smell of someone who had spent years being invisible on purpose.

The mortal stopped ten paces from the Oath-Tree, and Veyl watched them look at the dead and dying trees with an expression he recognized from his own human face, long ago: grief worn like an ill-fitting coat.

"You prayed," Veyl said.

His voice came out wrong—bark-dry, split with frost-cracks, a voice like a door in an abandoned house. The lantern swung up wildly. Ulyana's face, young and sharp and exhausted, went white as the snowfall.

"Who said that?" they demanded, and their voice shook only a little, which Veyl noted with something like approval. "Show yourself."

"I am shown," he said. "You are simply not looking correctly."

* * *

It took them most of the night to understand one another.

Veyl could not leave the tree—not yet, not while the covenant starved. What strength he'd gathered from that single answered prayer was barely enough to speak, to move sap and shadow into shapes a mortal mind could parse. So he showed himself as he could: a figure of dark grain and pale lichen standing among the roots, more suggestion than flesh, eyes like knotholes full of slow amber light.

Ulyana sat on a fallen log with the lantern between their feet and their knife loose in their belt, and asked questions the way a smuggler asks them—which is to say sideways, circling, checking every answer against the exits.

"The weeping wood is dying because the covenant is dying," Veyl told them. "The covenant is dying because no one remembers it. Prayer without memory is just noise. We have been fed noise for a hundred years. Noise keeps a body from starving, but not from weakening." He gestured with a branch-limb at the gray alders around them. "Your prayers reached me because your blood is in the old ink. You are not a petitioner, Ulyana Zhou. You are a clause."

"A clause," Ulyana repeated flatly. "Lovely. I fled my mistress to avoid being anyone's clause."

"I know. I heard you flee. I heard everything, all century. That is the cruelty of being a god—you are awake for every silence."

Something moved behind their eyes at the word *mistress*, some old wound flexing. They changed the subject the way water changes course around stone. "If you're so weak, why wake at all? Why answer? Why me?"

"Because the covenant must be reforged before the last tree falls, or both worlds come apart at the seam. And because you prayed." He paused, and the honesty cost him something, this ancient thing. "And because I was lonely. Gods are not supposed to say that either."

Ulyana stared at him for a long moment. Snow gathered on their shoulders and in their dark hair, and they did not brush it away. "What were you," they asked quietly, "before?"

And there it was. The question he had carried alone through a hundred years of sleep.

"I was a man," Veyl said. "In the lowlands. A river-warden's son. I remember barley. I remember my mother's voice calling a name I can no longer hold—the oath took it as binding ink, the first payment. I remember drowning, actually." He smiled, and it was a strange expression on a face of bark. "The river took me at nineteen, and the old gods were passing, and they offered a drowned boy a bargain: divinity in exchange for name and grave. I said yes. Everyone says yes. Drowned boys are not famous for their negotiating position."

"That's monstrous."

"It was mercy. It felt like mercy." He let the snow fall between them. "I tended rivers for two hundred years. Then the oath was sworn here, and I was bound into it, and the tending became larger and lonelier. And then mortals forgot us, politely, generation by generation, and we went silent, and I slept inside a tree dreaming of rain."

Ulyana looked down at their hands. When they spoke again, their voice was rougher. "I have to ask. If I help reforge this—if I'm a *clause*—what does it cost? There's always a cost. My mistress taught me that much before I ran."

"Wise woman," Veyl said dryly. "Memory. The new ink will be memory, given freely. That is all I know. The old terms died with the old oath."

"Memory," Ulyana echoed, and something in their face closed like a shutter, and Veyl—who had been human, who remembered what it was to hoard a grief—did not press.

Instead he said: "Before any of that, I would know what you are worth. Forgive me. It is an old habit. Gods test."

"Do it, then." They stood, chin up, snow-melting defiance. "Test me."

* * *

So he tested them, and it shamed him afterward, how easily they failed.

He reached into the wood's deep memory and pulled forth illusions the way a fisherman pulls nets: a road out of the mountains, safe and snowless, leading south to the free ports where no inquisitor would ever find them. He made it shimmer at the tree-line, warm lamplight, the sound of gulls. "There," he said. "Walk away. Reforge nothing. Someone else's bloodline may still carry ink. You owe these mountains nothing—you ran from them."

Ulyana looked at the false road for a long time. Their breath came shallow. Veyl watched them want it—watched the wanting move through their whole body like wind through wheat—and he thought, with a tired old bitterness: *there it is, the same as always, mortals choose the door out.*

Then Ulyana laughed, short and ugly, and the illusion rippled.

"No," they said. "That's not a road. That's a wish wearing a road's clothes. My mistress showed me that trick my second year—I ran from her anyway, but I learned the difference." They turned to face him fully, and their eyes were wet and furious. "Ask me something real. Ask me whether I'm sorry. Because I am—Gods, I am—I left her cottage and it burned and I don't even know if she got out, and I've been praying to *you* for years out of guilt because I couldn't pray to her. Is that worthy enough for you? Because it's all I have."

The false road dissolved into snowfall.

Veyl stood in the quiet of his dying wood and felt something shift in his chest that had not been sap. They were wanting—they had failed the test, chosen honestly over nobly, confessed cowardice and grief and guilt with no varnish on it—and somehow that honesty rang truer than any hero's answer would have. He had been tested once, a drowned boy offered mercy, and he had said yes without weighing it, and the yes had become a century of loneliness. This one weighed. This one flinched. This one told the truth about flinching.

"You are wanting," he said aloud. "Wanting in worthiness, and full of want. Both true."

"Thanks ever so," Ulyana muttered, scrubbing their face with a sleeve.

"But you are honest about it. Do you know how rare that is, in a hundred years of listening? Mortals lie to gods the way they lie to mirrors. You didn't." He lowered himself, root and shadow folding down, until his knothole eyes were level with theirs. "I find I trust you more for failing."

Ulyana blinked at him. "That's backwards."

"So is a god who remembers being human." A pause. The snow thickened, hushing the wood. "Go back down the mountain, Ulyana Zhou. There are people who will need to decide whether to shelter you—a captain, a defrocked priest. Tell Sef Marrow that the fragment he carries is genuine, and that the ink of the old oath was never obedience. It was remembrance. That word will mean something to him."

"Sef—" They went still. "How do you know Sef?"

"I listened for a century. You all talk in your sleep." The amber light in his knothole eyes dimmed, gentled. "Come back when you have decided. Not when you are brave. When you are honest. Those are different days, and only one of them can hold a covenant."

Ulyana shouldered their pack and turned toward the darkening trail. At the tree-line they stopped, not looking back.

"What was your name?" they asked. "Before. Your mother's name for you."

The ache opened in him, the empty socket where the name had been. He reached for it and found, as always, smooth absence.

"I don't remember," Veyl said. "That is what the oath cost. Whatever the new one costs—it should cost less than that."

They nodded slowly, and went down into the snow.

Veyl folded himself back into the roots of the Oath-Tree and listened to their footsteps fade, and felt—for the first time in a hundred years—something that was almost hope, and entirely fear. Because far to the south, down where the roads flattened and the crown's riders moved, he could feel another prayer being answered: cold, orderly, burning.

Someone else was listening to answered prayers too.
