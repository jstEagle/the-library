## 5. The Inquisitor's Ledger

The ledger was bound in calfskin the color of old milk, and Halvard had filled it in a hand so precise that a stranger might have mistaken him for a scrivener rather than a servant of the Crown's Inquisition. He wrote standing at the inn's single window, the quill moving in small, patient strokes, while below him the village of Marrow's End went about its evening as if nothing had changed.

Something had changed. Three prayers answered in nine days. A fevered child risen whole from her bed. Rain on a field that had been cursed dry for a generation. And now this: a woodcutter's wife who swore she had heard a voice answer from the weeping wood itself, a voice like water over stones, and had wept for an hour afterward without knowing why.

Halvard wrote it all down. Date. Name. The exact words the woman remembered hearing — *the root remembers* — because the exact words mattered. The gods were creatures of pattern, even now, even waking half-blind out of their long silence. Patterns could be traced. Traced things could be followed to their source.

And sources could be cut.

He blotted the page, closed the ledger, and let his fingers rest a moment on its cover. People assumed a man in his trade must hate what he hunted. They imagined fire in the blood, zealotry, the hot joy of the pyre. Halvard felt none of it. What he felt was closer to accounting. Somewhere in the arithmetic of the world, a debt had gone unpaid — a debt owed to him personally, by every god still drawing breath beyond the veil — and he intended to collect it with interest, line by line, entry by entry, until the ledger was full or the heavens were empty again.

His daughter's name was Ilse. She had died eleven years ago, in the space between one prayer and the next.

He did not write that in the ledger. Some entries he kept only in himself.

* * *

They burned the shrine at dawn.

It was a small thing, as shrines went — a hollow oak at the village's edge, its trunk carved with a hundred generations of cupped hands and open mouths, wax pooled in the bark's crevices like amber sap. The villagers had tended it quietly through the century of silence, the way you keep watering a plant whose owner has died, out of habit or hope. Lately, Halvard knew, they had come more often. The wax was fresh. Someone had left bread.

He gave them the choice first, because he always gave the choice. Gathered them in the square, forty souls with frost on their shoulders, and told them in a level voice: renounce the old covenant, swear the Crown's oath, and the shrine would stand. It would cost them nothing but words. Words, he said, were cheap. He knew better than most how little they weighed against what was taken from you.

An old man stepped forward — the miller, hands white with flour even at that hour — and said, "We prayed to them when your Crown wouldn't feed us, inquisitor. They answered slow, but they answered. You'll not have us curse them."

Not defiance, exactly. Grief, Halvard thought. The same grief, worn differently. That was the trouble with the old gods' people: they were never fanatics. They were simply people who had once been heard, and could not bear to go back to the silence.

So there was fire. There always was, in the end. The oak caught reluctantly, green wood hissing and spitting, and as it burned Halvard stood close enough to feel the heat on his face and watched the carved faces blacken one by one — the cupped hands, the open mouths, a child's crude rendering of a figure with leaves for hair. The smoke smelled of resin and something sweeter underneath, something almost like incense, and for one unwelcome moment he thought of the votive candles in the chapel where Ilse had been christened, where he had carried her on his shoulders past the painted saints while she laughed and pulled his hair.

He had prayed in that chapel for six days while she burned with fever. Six days of the proper prayers, the licensed ones, addressed to a Crown-sanctioned god who took no notice whatever. On the seventh day a neighbor — a hedge-faith woman, since hanged — had whispered to him about the older names, the forbidden ones, and in his desperation he had knelt in his own kitchen and broken every law he was sworn to uphold and begged the old powers to take anything, *anything*, in exchange for his daughter's life.

The fever broke on the eighth morning. Ilse opened her eyes, smiled at him, and lived four more hours.

That was the shape of it. That was the joke the universe had made of him: the gods had heard, after all. They had accepted the bargain. They had simply taken the wrong life — or taken the payment and given nothing back, which amounted to the same thing. Either way, he had sold something precious into the dark for nothing, and the dark had kept the change.

The villagers watched their shrine burn in silence. One woman keened softly, a low thread of sound. Halvard did not look at them. Looking made it harder, and harder things made mistakes, and mistakes in his work cost lives — usually other people's, lately, though he had stopped trusting that boundary to hold.

"The gods are returning," he told them, when the oak was mostly embers. "You should understand what that means. A god's attention is not mercy. It is weather. It falls on the just and unjust alike and it does not care which roof it tears off." He paused, and heard how much of himself was in the sentence, and finished anyway, quieter: "I have seen what it does to the ones who pray hardest."

Then he rode out, because the trail was freshening, and grief is a poor excuse for falling behind.

* * *

The sister lived in a crofter's hut two valleys east, above a stream still edged with ice, and she was expecting him.

Halvard understood this before he dismounted. Smoke rose from her chimney in a straight, unafraid line; the door stood unlatched; and when he ducked beneath the lintel she was already seated at her table with two cups poured, tea going cold, as if she'd read his arrival off the wind. A plain woman past fifty, gray-streaked hair pinned back, hands folded with the composure of someone who had decided years ago exactly how far she could be pushed and how she would meet it.

"Sef," Halvard said, laying his ledger on the table between them. "Your brother. Defrocked eight years ago for heretical sympathy. Last seen in Thornfell, in company I am very interested in."

"Maren Ashvale," she said. "Since you've come all this way, you may as well use my name."

"Maren, then." He sat. He did not touch the tea. "Where is he?"

"Doing the gods' work, I expect. Or the smugglers'. With Sef it was always hard to tell the difference."

"You believe the gods are real, then. Returned."

She looked at him for a long moment, and what was in her face was pity, and that was worse than hatred would have been. "I believe my brother heard something in the wood last month that made him weep like a boy," she said. "I believe he'd spent thirty years listening to a silence and pretending it was holy. And I believe you didn't come here to ask me questions you don't already know the answers to."

"No," Halvard agreed. "I came to see what protecting him costs you."

He turned the ledger around and opened it to the newest pages, and began, in his mild clerk's voice, to read. Not accusations — entries. The fevered child of Marrow's End. The rain on the cursed field. The woodcutter's wife and her voice like water over stones. Each answered prayer named, dated, located, each one a knot in a thread that ran, when you laid the pages side by side, unmistakably toward Thornfell. Toward the weeping wood. Toward whoever — whatever — was pulling the old powers up out of their graves of silence.

Maren listened with her chin lifted, refusing to flinch, but he watched her pulse jump at her throat when he read the last entry. Good. There was someone new in the story. Someone whose name she knew.

"A price on your head now, Maren Ashvale," Halvard said, closing the book. "Harboring knowledge of covenant-breakers. I'll not pretend it's a fair law. Only that it's the one written down." He stood, tucked the ledger under his arm, and paused at the door with the gray light behind him. "Tell your brother this, if your letters reach him. Tell him the gods are waking, and everything that wakes is hungry, and the first thing they will eat is the people who fed them when they starved. Tell him I have watched it happen."

"And tell him—" she began, and stopped, because her voice had cracked on the word *brother*, and Halvard waited, and she did not finish.

Outside, he swung into the saddle and sat a moment in the cold, looking back at the croft with its thin honest smoke. He thought: she loves him. He thought: so did I love something once, and look what the heavens made of it.

Then he opened the ledger across his saddlebow, dipped his pen, and added the day's entries. Shrine burned, Marrow's End. M. Ashvale questioned, released, marked. And beneath them, in his smallest, most careful hand:

*A fourth prayer confirmed. Source nears. The wood itself is answering now.*

He underlined it twice.

By week's end, if the pattern held, he would know which road the covenant-breakers were taking into the mountains — and there were men in the passes who sold information cheaper than bread.

Halvard snapped the ledger shut, and the sound was very like a door closing.
