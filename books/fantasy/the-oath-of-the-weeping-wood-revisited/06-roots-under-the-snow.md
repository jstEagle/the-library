## 6. Roots Under the Snow

The snow came down in silence for three days, and on the fourth day the trees began to weep.

Ulyana had heard of the weeping wood all their life, the way you hear of a thing you hope never to meet: the wood at Thornfell's heart where the smugglers refused to cut even kindling, where even the crown's foresters turned back at the treeline and marked their maps with the old word—*cursed*, or *sworn*, depending on which mapmaker had drunk more. They had imagined something theatrical. Black pines, bones in the drifts, the usual furniture of a story told to frighten children.

It was not that. It was worse, because it was beautiful.

The trees were birch and rowan and one species Ulyana had no name for, tall and silver-skinned, and from the joints of every branch hung beads of clear sap that caught the grey light and held it, so the whole wood seemed strung with lanterns that had gone out but not yet been taken down. The sap fell in slow drops, one every few breaths, striking the snow beneath with a sound too soft to be a sound—more like the memory of one. *Tik. Tik. Tik.* A thousand trees keeping a thousand broken clocks.

"It weeps because it remembers," Sef said quietly. He walked at Ulyana's shoulder, his defrocked priest's collar long gone but the pale stripe of skin where it had sat still visible above his scarf, like a scar from a wound that had been an honor. "That's what the fragment says. The wood was the first witness. It was made to remember the oath, and now the oath is failing, and it weeps because it cannot hold what it was made to hold."

"Can a tree grieve?" Brek asked from ahead, not turning. The captain's breath plumed and froze in her beard. She had not wanted to come into the wood—Brek Onda, who feared nothing with a price on it, had stood at the treeline for a full minute with her hand on her axe before walking in—but she would not say so, and no one would dare to say it for her.

"Can a smuggler love her crew?" Sef answered mildly.

"That's different."

"That's the same thing," said Ulyana, and was surprised by their own voice, and by the look Brek gave them over her shoulder—half anger, half the other thing she would not name.

They walked on. The snow here did not crunch. It gave beneath their boots with a sound like a held breath released, and Ulyana understood after a while why: the ground was not frozen all the way down. The wood's roots went deep, and something in them was still warm, still living, still *working*—a heart laboring under a weight it could no longer lift.

They passed the first dead tree an hour in.

It stood apart from its fellows, silver bark gone grey as ash, and where its neighbors wept it did not weep at all. Its branches were bare in the wrong season for bareness, and when Ulyana touched it—without deciding to, the way you touch a body—the bark came away under their fingers like old skin, and beneath it the wood was dry all the way to the heart. No ring of living green. Nothing.

"One of a hundred," Sef said. "There were more last winter. The fragment says the wood was planted as a ledger—every tree a page, every page a name. When the covenant frays, the ledger burns from the outside in."

"Whose names?" Ulyana asked.

Sef did not answer, and Ulyana did not ask again, because some part of them had already begun to dread the answer, the way you dread the shape of a thing before you turn to see it.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow ring of the living trees, and the wood watched them do it.

Ulyana felt it the way you feel a gaze on the back of your neck—except it came from everywhere, patient and enormous, and it was not hostile. It was *attentive*. The sap-beads above their fire caught its light and threw it back down in slow, trembling constellations, and when the wind moved through the branches the sound was not wind. It was syllables. Almost. Nearly. A language one vowel away from being speakable.

Veyl came to them at the edge of the firelight, as he always did, wearing the shape he wore now: a man of no particular age, grey at the temples, with hands that looked like they had done honest work for a very long time. Only his eyes were wrong—too old, too still, the eyes of something that had watched a century pass from inside a tree.

"You felt it," he said. Not a question.

"The wood watching. Yes."

"It has watched your family for nine generations." Veyl crouched by the fire, warming hands that did not need warmth, a habit he had kept from being human and refused to explain. "It remembers them all. It is trying to decide what it remembers about *you*."

Ulyana pulled their cloak tighter. "And what does it remember about my family? You've been circling it for days. Sef's fragment names my bloodline oath-keepers. The grimoire named them. Everyone knows but me."

Veyl was quiet a long moment. The fire cracked. Somewhere in the dark, a tree wept its slow bead of sap, and the sound fell into the silence like a stone into a well.

"Tomorrow," he said. "At the heart. You should learn this standing where it was done."

"That's a kindness, I suppose."

"No," Veyl said. "It isn't. But it's the only kind I have left." He looked at them across the fire, and for a moment the stillness in his eyes cracked, and Ulyana saw something move underneath it—grief, or guilt, or the memory of guilt, which after a hundred years amounts to the same. "I would spare you if I could. That is the one thing I will tell you tonight, and it is true, and it is all I have."

He rose and walked into the dark between the trees, and the wood closed behind him like water.

* * *

The heart of the weeping wood was a clearing, and the clearing was a grave.

They came to it at midmorning, Brek leading with her axe loose in her grip, Sef murmuring the fragment's verses under his breath like a man walking a ledge, and Ulyana in the middle, because no one had said so but everyone had arranged it so. The trees ringed the clearing in a perfect circle, and every one of them was old—older than old, their silver trunks wide as watchtowers, their crowns lost in the low cloud. And at the center stood a single tree, dead, vast, and carved.

The carving covered it from root to the first great branches. Spirals, knotwork, lines of script in an alphabet Ulyana had never been taught and yet could almost read, the way you can almost read a language your mother spoke to you before you could speak. The sap-beads hung from the dead branches too, but here they did not fall. They hung frozen, mid-fall, hundreds of them, as if the tree had been weeping when something stopped its heart.

Sef sank to his knees at the treeline. "This is the Oath-tree," he whispered. "The first one. The one they swore on."

"Who swore what?" Ulyana asked, and their voice came out small in all that silence.

Veyl stood beside the dead tree with his hand flat against the carved bark, and he did not look at Ulyana when he spoke. He looked at the carvings. At the names.

"The covenant was failing even then," he said. "Nine generations ago. The old oath—obedience, sacrifice, the binding of the mortal realm to the gods'—it was fraying, because it was built on compulsion, and compulsion rots. Your ancestor was the covenant's keeper then. She came here with her whole family, and the gods came down, and they tried to re-swear it. And it would not take." He paused. "It would not take because the old ink was blood, and blood was no longer enough. The covenant had to be written in something truer. A memory. A memory freely surrendered—the one thing a person can give that cannot be taken back."

Ulyana's mouth had gone dry. "Whose memory?"

"The rite requires an anchor," Veyl said. "A single memory, held in the wood, that all the rest of the covenant hangs from. Your ancestor chose one. She chose deliberately. She weighed it, and she chose." His hand moved on the bark, tracing a line of script, and the script glowed faintly under his fingers, and then went dark. "She chose her daughter's memory of her. The child was six. The rite took the girl's memory of her mother entire—every day, every song, every time she had been held—and it bound the covenant with it. The mother walked out of this wood knowing her daughter would never know her face again. And the daughter grew up, and became a keeper, and raised her own children never knowing why her heart ached when she passed a certain kind of tree."

The clearing was very quiet. The frozen sap-beads hung above them like a held breath nine generations long.

Ulyana heard their own voice from very far away. "She did it on purpose."

"She did it to save the world," Sef said softly, from his knees. "Or what she believed was the world."

"She did it on *purpose*." The word came out of Ulyana like a blade drawn wrong, tearing on the way. "She looked at her child—her six-year-old child—and she calculated. She weighed a little girl's whole knowing of her mother against—against *everything else*, and the little girl lost. And then—" their voice cracked, and they hated it, hated the tears that came hot and fast, hated that the wood was watching, that it had watched for nine generations, that it had *remembered*— "and then she made it our inheritance. She made it the family work. Every keeper after, sworn the same way, bound the same way, and none of us ever knew. We thought we were running from a hedge-witch's chores. We were running from *this*. From being the kind of person who could do that."

Brek shifted at the treeline. The captain said nothing. There was nothing to say, and Brek Onda was too honest to pretend otherwise.

"You're asking," Ulyana said to Veyl, "whether I'm the same. Whether I'd do it. Whether it's in the blood."

"No," Veyl said. "I'm not asking anything. I told you—I tested you once already, and you were wanting, and you were honest about it, and honest was enough." He came away from the tree at last, and the frozen beads of sap caught his face as he passed beneath them, lighting it and unlighting it, so that he seemed for a moment to be many men, all of them grieving. "But the wood is asking. It has been asking since you crossed the treeline. It wants to know if the daughter of that choice will make the same choice, or a different one, or no choice at all."

"And if I turn around?" Ulyana wiped their face with the heel of their hand, rough, angry. "If I walk out of this wood and go back to the passes and never pray again? What happens?"

"Then the ledger burns," Sef said quietly. "Page by page. Tree by tree. And when the last page burns, the covenant fails, and the gods go silent again—or worse, and I have read what is worse. The veil thins. Things come through that were bound out for good reason. I burned my collar over less than this."

The wind moved through the ring of ancient trees, and the sound it made was that almost-language again, and this time Ulyana heard a syllable in it, clear as a bell in snow. It sounded, horribly, like a child's name. It sounded, horribly, like it might have been *theirs*, nine generations back, sung by a mother whose face the child could no longer call up no matter how she wept.

Ulyana looked at the dead tree. At the carvings. At the frozen tears of a witness that had held one small girl's memory of love for nine generations and was dying of the weight.

They thought of the hedge-witch's burned cottage. Of the grimoire. Of the letters in a hand they almost remembered, and could not, and ached over anyway—ached, they understood now, because the ache was the inheritance itself, passed down like a ring that no longer fit anyone.

They could walk away. That was the horror of it, and the mercy. The old covenant had been built on compulsion, and compulsion was dead; nothing in the wood could make them stay. Their ancestor had chosen for a child. No one had ever chosen for Ulyana Zhou. The choice, for the first time in nine generations, was actually theirs.

And that, they realized, standing in the grave of a six-year-old's memory, was the most frightening thing of all. Because a compelled choice could be regretted. A free one could only be owned.

"Keep walking," Ulyana said. Their voice was wrecked and did not care. "I'm not her. I'm not making her choice. But I'm not running from it either. Show me the rest of the rite. Show me what it costs. *All* of it, Veyl. Before I decide anything, I want the whole ledger open."

Veyl looked at them for a long moment, and in his too-old eyes something moved that might, in a man, have been hope.

"The whole ledger," he said quietly, "is more than you know. And some of it is written in me."

It was then that Brek, who had been scanning the treeline with the restless habit of a woman who had been ambushed before, went very still.

"Captain?" Sef said.

"We're being followed," Brek said. "Been followed since the second ring of trees. Someone's patient." She turned her head slowly, reading the snow, the shadows, the weeping dark between the trunks. "And they're not trying to hide it anymore."
