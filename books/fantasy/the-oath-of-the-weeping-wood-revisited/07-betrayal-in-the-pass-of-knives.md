## 7. Betrayal in the Pass of Knives

Brek Onda had run the Pass of Knives eleven times in her life, and every time she counted the teeth of the mountain before she entered it. Old habit. Her mother's habit, and her mother's mother before that, back to some grandmother who'd first learned that the Thornfells did not forgive the careless.

The pass earned its name honestly: a mile of granite spires leaning over a track barely wide enough for two mules abreast, with drops on either side deep enough to swallow a scream whole. In summer the wind came through it like a whetstone drawn along bone. In early winter, when they crossed now, the snow lay in the shadows like something spilled, and the light came down thin and gray and honest, showing everything, hiding nothing.

Which was why Brek hated what she was feeling this morning, three hours into the crossing, with the company strung out single file behind her and the mules' breath steaming like small ghosts.

She felt watched.

Not the ordinary watchfulness of the mountains—she knew that one the way she knew her own hands. This was different. This was the prickle at the nape, the sense of eyes with intent behind them, and she had survived twenty years of smuggling by never once ignoring that prickle.

She stopped at a bend where the track widened around a fallen pillar of stone and let the line catch up. Counted heads out of habit. Nine souls besides herself: Dalla and her brother Fen on the lead mules, old Marta with the salt, the twins Ivo and Ivka sharing one mule because Ivka's knee had gone bad, Sef walking with his prayer book wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like a second heart, and Ulyana Zhou near the rear, hood up, quiet as always, watching the cliffs the way a deer watches treeline.

And Tam.

Tam brought up the rear, as he had for two days now, ever since the night camp below the shrine ruins where he'd volunteered twice for last watch. Tam who'd been with her six years. Tam who'd carried Ivka through the flooded ford at Graywater without being asked. Tam whose laugh could be heard from one end of a market to another.

Brek looked at him now, at the set of his shoulders under his patched coat, and the prickle at her neck said *him*, and she hated it, and she made herself look away, because six years of loyalty deserved more than a feeling.

But feelings were how smugglers stayed alive, and she'd buried too many of the ones who'd trusted their hearts over their necks.

"Onda." Dalla drew level with her, voice pitched low under the wind. "The snow's wrong."

Brek frowned. "Wrong how?"

"Look at the ledges." Dalla nodded upward, at the shelves of stone above the track where snow should have lain in smooth white sheets. Instead there were dark streaks, slides, places where the white had come down recently and been trampled. "Something's been moving up there. Something heavy. And—" she hesitated, and Dalla was not a woman who hesitated, "—I found a boot print an hour back. Fresh. Half-buried, but fresh. Crown issue, by the hobnails."

The word *crown* went through Brek like cold water through cloth.

"Could be hunters," she said, not believing it.

"Could be." Dalla spat. "Could be we've been sold."

Brek didn't answer. She turned and looked down the line again, at each face in turn, doing the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the shrine ruins. Who knew the route? All of them. Who knew *this* route, the Knives, the specific day, the specific hour? Only the ones who'd been at the fire when she'd laid it out—and that was everyone, because she'd trusted them all, because that was the whole point of the company of small treasons, that you trusted, that trust was the only wealth worth carrying in a country that taxed you in blood.

Her eyes came to rest on Tam, and Tam met her gaze, and smiled, and waved.

*Him,* said the prickle.

*Prove it,* said her heart.

She couldn't prove it. Not here, not now, not with a crown inquisitor somewhere behind or ahead of them and no time to hold a trial in the middle of a pass with walls on both sides. So she did what her grandmother would have done: she changed nothing outwardly, and inwardly she began to grieve.

"All right," she called, loud enough to carry. "We push through before the light goes. No stops past the pillar. If anyone's tired, say so now, because after this bend there's no ground soft enough to sleep on till the far side."

Nobody said so. The line moved. The wind picked up, moaning between the spires, and Brek told herself the sound was only wind, only stone, only winter.

It was not only wind.

* * *

They were halfway through the narrows when the first crossbow bolt took Fen off his mule.

No warning shout. No challenge from the rocks. Just the flat crack of a bowstring rolling down the canyon walls a half-second after the impact, and Fen pitching sideways into the snow with a black-fletched shaft standing out of his throat, and then the world coming apart.

"AMBUSH!" Dalla's scream tore the air. "DOWN! EVERYONE DOWN!"

Brek hit the track behind the fallen pillar as the rocks above bloomed with soldiers—crown livery under gray cloaks, a dozen of them at least, more on the far ledge, crossbows leveled down into the kill zone they'd built for exactly this track on exactly this day. Bolts came down like iron rain. One splintered off the stone a hand's width from Brek's cheek. Another took old Marta through the shoulder, spinning her into the drifts, and her salt sacks burst around her and the white snow went white-salted and red.

"They knew," Brek breathed. They knew the route. They knew the hour. They knew the narrows, the one place the company couldn't scatter. Someone had drawn Halvard a map with their names on it.

Someone had drawn him a map with their *names* on it.

She raised her head just enough to see down the line, and what she saw would live behind her eyes until she died. Ivo dragging Ivka behind the mule's carcass, both of them screaming. Sef standing upright in the open—not frozen, not stupid, but *standing*, arms spread, shouting words Brek half-recognized from his defrocked liturgies, words about shelter and witness, putting his body between the crossbows and the wounded like a man who had decided years ago how he wanted to die and was only waiting for the invitation.

"Sef! Get *down!*"

"He won't shoot a priest!" Sef shouted back, absurd, magnificent, wrong. "He'll think twice—"

The second volley came down and thought nothing at all. It missed Sef by a finger's width and killed the mule beside him, and the animal's fall pinned Marta's legs, and her cries went up thin and terrible into the wind.

And Tam—

Brek found Tam with her eyes across the chaos, and Tam was not fighting. Tam was not shooting. Tam was standing apart, by the southern wall, hands empty at his sides, face turned up toward the soldiers above with an expression Brek had seen once before, years ago, on a man watching his own house burn: helpless, guilty, already grieving.

"TAM!" Her voice cracked across the pass. "TAM, GET COVER!"

He looked at her. Just looked. And in that look was the whole confession, six years of it, the volunteering for watches, the questions about routes asked too casually, the way he'd lingered at the shrine ruins talking to a charcoal-burner who'd never existed before or since.

"I'm sorry, Captain," he said, and the wind took most of it, but she read the shape of it. "They have my sister. Halvard has my sister. He said— he said nobody had to die if—"

A soldier on the ledge, hearing voices, loosed at the sound. The bolt caught Tam high in the chest and sat him down in the snow like a puppet with cut strings.

Brek came up out of cover without deciding to. Grief does that; it pulls the trigger of you. She had three strides toward him and a knife in her fist and no plan whatsoever when the third volley came, and this one was aimed at her, and she saw it come—the crossbowman on the near ledge, reloading fast, leveling down at her exposed back—and she knew, with the strange clarity of the final moment, that she was going to die in the Pass of Knives counting the teeth of the mountain, and that her last thought would be anger at herself for trusting.

Then the air *changed*.

There was no other word for it. The wind stopped—not died, *stopped*, mid-moan, as if the pass itself had held its breath. The falling snow hung suspended. And between Brek and the crossbowman the air split open with a sound like a word spoken in a language older than stone, and the bolt meant for her heart struck something that was not there and shattered into sparks and slivers, and the crossbowman screamed and dropped his weapon, clawing at his eyes, babbling about a light, about a woman made of winter standing on the air.

Brek turned.

Ulyana Zhou stood in the middle of the track with their hood thrown back and their arms raised, and they were *glowing*. Not metaphorically. Light came off them like heat off a forge, pale gold shot through with green, the same green as the dying leaves of the weeping wood, and their eyes had gone the color of deep water over deep stone, and when they spoke, their voice came out doubled, theirs and something vaster underneath, speaking in unison:

"*Enough.*"

The word rolled down the pass like an avalanche given grammar. Snow slid from the ledges. Two soldiers lost their footing and fell shrieking into the deep drifts. The crossbows wavered, and men who had fired on unarmed smugglers without a flicker of conscience now stumbled backward from a single figure standing unarmed in the snow, because men will forgive themselves killing a person long before they can stomach killing a *presence*.

Ulyana turned their head, slowly, and looked up at the ledges, at the soldiers, and Brek saw their face and understood that Ulyana was as terrified as any of them—that this power had been torn out of them against their own vow, spent to save a life they'd sworn to keep hidden, and that the cost was written all over them.

"You came for smugglers," Ulyana said, voice shaking, human again, ordinary again. "You found something else. Tell your inquisitor. Tell him the wood is waking, and it knows its own."

For a long moment, nothing. Wind returned in a rush. Snow resumed falling. And then, from the far ledge, a horn sounded—a single low note, the recall—and the soldiers withdrew, backing away along the heights, leaving their dead and their bolts and their unfinished work behind them.

Silence came down over the pass like a shroud.

* * *

Afterward there was only the work. There is always only the work.

Dalla knelt by Fen and closed his eyes and did not speak, because there was nothing to say that the snow wouldn't hear better. Ivo held Ivka while she wept for a brother who wasn't dead and a mule that was, which is the arithmetic of grief among smugglers—you count what you still have, and you hate yourself for counting. Marta lived; the bolt had gone through meat, not lung, and Sef bound her shoulder with hands that shook and prayers that didn't, whispering thanks to gods who were listening now whether anyone liked it or not.

Brek stood over Tam for a long time before she let anyone touch him.

He was still breathing. Shallow, rattling, the wet whistle of a pierced lung. She knelt in the bloody snow beside him and took his hand, and his eyes found hers, and they were clear, terribly clear.

"Captain," he whispered. "Didn't want— I told myself they'd only take the goods. I told myself—"

"I know," Brek said.

"My sister. He has my sister. He showed me her letters. He knew things about her that—" A cough took him, and blood came with it, bright on his lips. "You'd have done it. For someone you loved. You'd have done it."

And that was the worst of it, Brek thought. That it was true. That she could not stand over this man in judgment without standing in judgment of her own heart, which would have sold the whole pass, the whole range, the whole rotten covenant if it bought back one of her own.

"Hold on," she said instead. "Sef will—"

"Don't waste him on me." Tam's mouth bent toward something like his old laugh, and failed halfway. "Captain. The wood. What the witch said. Is it true? Are they really—"

"Waking," Brek said. "Seems so."

"Good." His eyes drifted toward the peaks, toward something beyond them. "Somebody ought to be keeping accounts. Somebody ought to finally be *watching*." And then the rattle in his chest went long, and quiet, and stopped, and Brek Onda knelt in the Pass of Knives holding the hand of the man who had betrayed her, and wept for him anyway, because that was also the work.

* * *

They burned the dead at dusk, on a shelf of stone above the pass, because the ground was too hard for graves and the company had never liked leaving their people for wolves. Fen and Tam together, wrapped in canvas, the fire fed with pine and the broken mule tack, smoke going up straight and black into the windless evening sky.

Sef spoke the old words, the ones that had cost him his office, and this time, for the first time in his life, Brek saw the words *land*—saw the flames lean toward the speaker, saw the smoke curl into shapes that might have been listening.

Ulyana stood apart, at the edge of the firelight, and would not come closer.

Brek went to them. Stood beside them in the cold, looking at the fire, not looking at them, because some things are kinder seen sideways.

"You swore it off," Brek said quietly. "Whatever that was. You swore it off, and you spent it anyway, on us. On me."

"On you," Ulyana agreed. Their voice was hoarse, scraped hollow. "I'm sorry. I know what it costs us all, me being—"

"Visible." Brek huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Aye. That's the word for it."

They stood in silence. Below them, the pass stretched away into blue dusk, littered with bolts and bloodstains and the prints of men who would report everything they'd seen to a man with a ledger and a grudge. By tomorrow, maybe sooner, Halvard would know exactly what walked with the smugglers of Thornfell. Would know the hedge-witch's runaway apprentice had come into power the mistress had died guarding. Would know where they were bound, because there was only one place left to go.

"The heart of the wood," Brek said. It wasn't a question.

"It's the only door left," Ulyana said. "And he'll follow us there. He'll follow us anywhere now."

"Aye." Brek watched the fire collapse in on itself, sending up a fountain of sparks that rose and rose and winked out one by one, like prayers going unanswered—or answered, she couldn't tell the difference anymore. "Then we'd best get there first. And Ulyana—" she turned at last, and looked at them straight, the way she'd looked at Tam, the way she looked at no one she didn't mean it with, "—whatever you're carrying, whatever the god gave you and whatever it's going to take out of you—I need to know it before we walk into that valley. All of it. Because after today, I don't bury secrets alongside my people anymore. Secrets are what put them there."

Ulyana held her gaze, and in the firelight their eyes were ordinary again, tired, young, afraid—and beneath the fear, steady as bedrock.

"Then sit down, Captain," they said. "Because what my bloodline swore, a hundred years ago, on a child's memory—it's worse than you think. And it's why the trees are dying. And it's why Halvard's ledger isn't the thing I'm most afraid of anymore."

Beyond the shelf of stone, far to the north, the peaks of the weeping wood stood black against the last light, and the wind coming off them smelled, faintly and impossibly, of green things growing.
