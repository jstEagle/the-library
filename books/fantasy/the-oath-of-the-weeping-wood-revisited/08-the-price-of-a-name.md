## 8. The Price of a Name

Ulyana could not sleep in the heart of the weeping wood.

They had made camp in the hollow beneath the Mother Tree—the oldest birch, the one whose bark ran silver and wet even now, in the dry cold of late winter—and the others slept around the small fire like spoons in a drawer: Brek with her boots on, Sef murmuring half a psalm into his dreams, the twins curled back to back. The wood itself did not sleep. It listened. Ulyana felt it listening, the way they had once felt their mistress's eyes through the cottage wall.

*You knew,* they said silently, to the dark between the trees. *You knew what you would ask.*

*I hoped I would not have to.* Veyl's voice came not from any direction but from everywhere at once, from the sap in the roots and the frost on the branches. He had grown stronger since the pass—since the blood, since the two graves Brek's crew had dug with frozen hands—but strength had not softened him. If anything, it had made his sorrow heavier, because now he had the power to carry it visibly. *Come to the spring. Bring nothing.*

The spring was a pool no wider than a cartwheel, fed by snowmelt that wept down a shelf of black stone, and it never froze, though everything around it wore a rime of white. Ulyana went alone, as they were always alone with him, and found him standing at its edge in the shape he favored: a man of no particular age, gray-eyed, with hands that looked like working hands, like hands that had once hauled nets or split wood or held a child.

They had asked him once, weeks ago, what those hands had done before the oath took him. He had smiled and said, *I'll tell you when it matters.*

It was beginning to matter.

"You've worked it out," Veyl said. Not a question. The stars moved behind his eyes when he blinked, slow as seasons.

"The fragment my mistress kept." Ulyana's breath fogged. "The covenant isn't inked in blood. Blood is only the seal. The binding is memory—a true memory, freely surrendered. That's why the old oath was monstrous. My ancestor didn't give her own memory. She took her daughter's. A child's whole remembering of her mother, poured out like wine to seal the covenant, and the child lived the rest of her life not knowing what she'd lost." Their voice cracked on the last words. "And now the covenant is failing because the memory that holds it is finally wearing thin. A century of erosion. And the only way to reforge it—"

"—is to pour out a new memory," Veyn finished gently. "A new ink. Freely given this time."

"Then it should be mine." Ulyana stepped closer, chin up, the way they did when they were terrified and refusing to show it. "My bloodline broke the balance. My inheritance. I'll give something. Take my memories of Brek, of Sef, of the road—take my childhood, take my name, I don't care—"

"No."

"No?" The word came out as a laugh, ugly and short. "You get to say no?"

"I am the god here," Veyl said, and for a moment the wood flexed around them, every branch bending a degree toward the spring, every star leaning in. Then he exhaled—or did whatever a god did instead—and he was just a tired man at a pool again. "Ulyana Zhou. Listen to me. You have spent seven years making yourself small so that nothing could be taken from you. Do you know what I see when I look at you? I see someone who has already given up too much to forgetting. I will not be one more thing that takes."

"Then don't take. Let me give."

"It isn't yours to give." He turned to face the water. "There are rules older than either of us. The ink must be a true memory of love. Not duty. Not guilt—guilt is cheap, and the covenant would drink it and fail again inside a decade. It must be love freely named. And there is exactly one such memory in this wood strong enough, old enough, deep enough to hold a realm together." His jaw tightened. "Mine."

Ulyana understood then, all at once, the way ice understands the thaw—one moment solid, the next gone.

"No," they said.

"You haven't heard—"

"I don't need to hear it. Your human life. Your memory of being—" They stopped. Started again, quieter, which was worse. "That's why you told me about your hands. That's why you kept saying *I'll tell you when it matters.* Because if you tell me, and then the rite takes it, you'll lose it anyway, and I'll remember a story that means nothing to the man standing in front of me."

"Yes."

"And it doesn't stop there." Ulyana's voice rose; somewhere overhead, a dying tree groaned as though in sympathy. "Because I'm part of it, aren't I? The memory you'd pour out—it's not just nets and firewood and some village I've never seen. You loved someone. You *were* someone who loved. And everything that grew from that love—everything downstream of it—that goes too." They were shaking now. "Including me. Including every night at this pool. Including the falling star. Including my name."

Veyl said nothing, which was answer enough.

"You don't get to do this." Ulyana grabbed his sleeve—the audacity of it, grabbing a god—and the fabric was warm, impossibly warm, like sunlit stone. "Do you hear me? Everyone takes from me. My mistress took my years. Halvard wants to take my freedom. The crown would take my life. And you—you're the one person in this whole cursed world who *gave* me something first. You answered. When everyone said you were dead, when I prayed out of stupid habit into an empty sky, you *answered.* And now you want to make yourself into one more theft?" Their voice broke entirely. "I won't let you. I'll refuse the rite. I'll let the wood die. I'll let the gods go silent for another hundred years—"

"You won't." He said it without cruelty, the way one says *the river will reach the sea.* "Because you've already walked farther for strangers than most people walk for family. Because you dug graves for two smugglers in frozen ground with your bare hands and then blamed yourself for not digging four." He reached out—not touching, just hovering, a warmth near their cheek. "And because you know, as I know, what happens to the mortal realm when the covenant fails. The wards fail. The old hungers wake. Halvard's nightmares, the ones he thinks are ambition—they'd come true, and worse than his worst ledger. You won't let that happen. It's the best and most infuriating thing about you."

"Then find another way."

"There is no other way. There is only whose memory, and whether it's given with open hands." He finally touched them, one fingertip against their brow, and the warmth of it went all the way down their spine. "Let me choose this, Ulyana. It is the only choice left to me that is truly mine. For a century I have been bound—bound by oath, bound by silence, bound by the shape of what mortals needed a god to be. This—this is the one act in a hundred years that no one compels. Let it be mine."

Ulyana stood at the edge of the unfreezing water for a long time. The wood waited. Even the wind held its breath.

"When," they said at last, hoarse. "When do we begin."

"At moonrise," Veyl said softly. "But first—let me tell you about my hands."

* * *

Veyl had been a fisherman's son on a coast that no longer appeared on maps, drowned under the sea-rise three centuries past, and his name then had been Aldous Vey, and he had been unremarkable in every way except one: he remembered things. Faces, songs, the exact weight of his mother's bread in his hands, the color of his daughter's first fever-flush. In a village where everyone forgot quickly because grief demanded it, he kept everything, and the old god of that coast—an aging, failing deity who needed a vessel—had chosen him for precisely that.

He told it plainly, sitting on the black stone while Ulyana sat in the shallows' margin, and the telling took until the moon was halfway up the sky. The daughter's name had been Wren. She had liked to catch frogs and let them go. She had died at six years old, before the god ever came for him, and that was the secret under the secret: Veyl had not been chosen despite his grief but because of it. A heart already broken wide open makes the deepest well.

"So you understand," he finished, "when I say that pouring out this memory is not losing everything. Wren has been gone from me a long time—I gave her up in pieces over centuries just to keep functioning. What remains of my mortal life is mostly this: the keeping. The habit of love. And you, Ulyana. Four months of you. The prayer in the pass. Brek's terrible cooking. The way you talk to horses like they're colleagues." He smiled, and it was a human smile, crooked and tired. "That's the ink. All of it, mixed together. A true memory of love, freely given."

"And afterward you won't know me."

"Afterward I will know that a mortal comes to visit every week and tends the spring, and I will think: *how kind.* And I will wonder why my chest hurts when they leave." He said it steadily, and only the water betrayed him—the spring rippling with no wind, ring after ring, like a thing trying not to weep.

"That's not fair," Ulyana whispered. "To either of us."

"No," he agreed. "Covenants rarely are. But the old one was unfair downward—onto the powerless, onto a child. Ours can be unfair upward. Onto me. That's the whole point of the new oath, isn't it? Remembrance freely given, not obedience compelled. So I am giving. Freely." He rose, and the moonlight gathered on him like a garment. "Unless you forbid it. You have that right, oath-keeper. Say the word and I will find another century of silence to hide in, and we will both watch the wood die knowing we could have saved it."

Ulyana looked up at him for a long, long moment.

Then they knelt at the water's edge, and drew the covenant fragment from their coat—their mistress's careful script, the bloodline sigil—and laid it on the black stone, and began, in a voice that shook and did not stop:

"Ink of remembering, witness of root and rain..."

Veyl closed his eyes.

Far to the south, down in the passes, torchlight was moving—many torches, in disciplined ranks, climbing toward the heart of the wood behind a man carrying a ledger. But here, in the hollow, there was only the moon, and the spring, and the sound of a god giving away his name.

"...freely given," Ulyana said, "and never compelled. Begin."
