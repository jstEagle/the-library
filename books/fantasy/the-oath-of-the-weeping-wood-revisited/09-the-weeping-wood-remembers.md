## 9. The Weeping Wood Remembers

**Halvard**

The wood wept before he reached it.

Halvard heard it from the ridge above the pass—a sound like a thousand small griefs, water finding its way out of bark, and beneath it a hum that was not wind, not birdsong, but something older than either. His men shifted behind him, twenty of the crown's finest, torches hissing in the wet air, and he knew what they were hearing too. He saw it in their faces: the animal recognition of a thing that should not be.

He had spent eleven years telling himself the gods were dead because dead things could not be blamed. Dead things could not have watched. Dead things could not have let a fever take a girl of six while her father prayed until his knees bled on the temple floor.

But the prayers were being answered now. He had followed them like a blood trail—village to village, shrine to shrine, each answered petition a bright stitch in a pattern he could finally read. And the pattern ended here, at the heart of Thornfell, where a heretic hedge-witch and a god who remembered being human meant to unmake the covenant that held the world's edges straight.

*If the gods return,* he had written in his ledger, in his careful clerk's hand, *they return as conquerors. Everything they gave was a loan. Everything we built was collateral.*

He believed it still. He clung to it the way a drowning man clings to a spar, even as the spar drags him under.

"Torches high," he said. "We take them alive if we can."

He did not say what to do if they could not. He had stopped saying such things aloud months ago. The orders wrote themselves now.

* * *

**Ulyana**

The rite had no altar. That was the first thing Ulyana understood, kneeling in the black loam between the dying trees, Veyl's cold hand in theirs. The old covenant had been carved into stone, into law, into flesh—their ancestor's blood dried into an oath of obedience so absolute it had starved the gods of everything except worship, and the mortals of everything except duty. A covenant like a cage holds only until something inside it grows strong enough to hate the bars.

This one would be spoken. Freely. Or not at all.

The weeping trees stood around them in a wide ring, most of them grey and bare, sap gone to resin tears that glittered on their bark like a congregation of frozen mourners. A few still lived—one, near the ring's center, green to its crown, and it was beneath this tree that Veyl knelt with them, his borrowed human shape flickering at the edges like a candle in draft.

"You can still stop," Ulyana said. Their voice came out rough. They had been arguing with him for three days, since the moment he told them what the binding ink must be: not blood, not obedience, but memory—and his choice of which memory. "There's another way. There has to be."

"There isn't." Veyl smiled, and it was the smile of a man remembering how smiles worked, fitting the pieces together from old habit. "I was human once. I had a name before the oath took it. I had a mother who sang off-key and a brother I hated and loved in equal measure and a winter I nearly froze to death outside a shrine because I'd given my coat away." He turned his hand over in theirs, palm up, ordinary. "That is the memory. All of it. When the rite binds, I will no longer know whose hand I am holding or why I chose to hold it. I will know only that I chose. That is enough. That is more than the old covenant ever asked of anyone."

"And I'll be carrying it for you. Your whole life. Your mother's voice. Your brother's face."

"Yes." His eyes were pale as birchwood and very steady. "You'll remember me better than I remember myself. That is what remembrance means, Ulyana Zhou. It isn't nostalgia. It's labor."

Ulyana bowed their head over their joined hands. Somewhere beyond the ring of trees, Sef stood watch at the eastern path, Brek at the southern, the crew strung thin along the wood's edge with knives and nerve and nothing else. They had come anyway. Every one of them had been told what waited here, what price might be paid, and every one had come anyway.

*Freely given,* Ulyana thought. *All of it.*

They began to speak the first words.

* * *

**Sef**

Sef heard the soldiers before he saw them, which was wrong, because Halvard's men moved like professionals, and professionals did not crack branches.

Unless they wanted you to know.

He looked back once toward the ring of trees, where Ulyana's voice rose and fell in cadences older than any liturgy he'd ever served, and then he stepped out onto the path with his empty hands visible and his sister's wooden prayer-bead in his pocket, the one thing he'd kept when they stripped his vestments.

"Halvard," he called. "You're late. The gods woke up without you."

The inquisitor came through the undergrowth with his torch held high and his blade sheathed, which Sef noted, and noted what it cost him to keep it sheathed. Up close Halvard looked less like the ledger-keeper of rumor and more like a man who had not slept in a year and had made peace with never sleeping again.

"Sef Carrow." Halvard's voice was quiet, almost gentle. "Defrocked for heresy. Shelterer of heretics. You could walk away. You've walked away from everything else."

"Not from this." Sef spread his arms, blocking the path, knowing exactly how little his body weighed against twenty armed men and knowing also that some weights are not measured in pounds. "You think they're returning to conquer. I served the church thirty years, Halvard—I read the same ledgers you did, the sealed ones, the ones that say the covenant was never about obedience at all until our ancestors made it so. They're not coming back as kings. They're coming back as *neighbors.*"

"The gods let my daughter die."

"They let all of us die. It's the price of being free instead of kept." Sef's throat tightened. "I don't pretend that's comfort. But there's a difference between a god who won't save us and a master who owns us, and your daughter—" he gentled, because this needed gentleness or nothing, "—your daughter deserved better than to be the reason you burn villages."

For one heartbeat, one long suspended heartbeat, Halvard's torch wavered.

Then the man behind him shouted—*movement on the flank, the smugglers*—and the world broke open.

It happened fast, the way terrible things do. Torches scattering into the dark. Brek's crew rising out of the bracken like ghosts with blades. Halvard drawing at last, the steel singing free, and Sef moving—not thinking, just moving, the way he had once moved toward altars—into the space between the inquisitor and the treering where a witch's voice was weaving the world new.

The blade went through him low, under the ribs, angled up, the way soldiers are taught.

Halvard caught him as he fell. That was the strange part. That was the part Sef would carry with him into whatever came next: the inquisitor's arms closing around him, lowering him down into the loam and the leaf-mold, the great careful hands pressing the wound as though pressure could argue with what was already true.

"Why," Halvard said. His composure was gone; underneath it was just a tired father holding a dying priest. "Why would you—"

"Because someone had to show you," Sef said. Blood filled his mouth; he swallowed it and kept talking, because he had talked his whole life and he would finish this sentence if it killed him, and it was killing him, and that was fine. "That's what freely given looks like. No commandment made me stand there. No oath. I chose. Remember that. Not my death—my *choice.*" His hand found Halvard's wrist, gripped it, weak as a child's. "Remembrance, Inquisitor. That's all the gods ever wanted. That's all anyone wants. To be remembered by someone who chose to remember."

The trees around them were weeping harder now, resin tears running silver down the bark, and Sef thought the wood sounded almost like singing, and then he thought it sounded like his sister's voice, and then he thought no more thoughts at all.

* * *

**Brek**

Brek Onda had buried crew before. She had dug graves in frozen ground and said words over them in four languages and drunk herself stupid after, twice. She knew loss like she knew the passes: intimately, by scar tissue.

She had never seen a man die *well.*

From the treeline, knife in hand, pinned behind a deadfall with two of hers and three of Halvard's bleeding around her, Brek watched Sef Carrow take a blade meant for a witch and a god, and watched him spend his last breath not on curses or prayers but on teaching his killer the difference between obedience and love. And she watched Inquisitor Halvard—Halvard, the butcher of the shrine-villages, the man whose name mothers used to frighten children—kneel in the mud holding the body like something breakable and holy.

"Brek." Tam's voice, ragged, beside her. "Do we press? We could take him now. He's done."

Brek looked at her knife. Looked at the man kneeling in the ruin of his certainty.

"No," she said, and lowered the blade. "We witness. That's the job tonight. Anyone who strikes now answers to me."

Her crew stared at her—hard people, pass-people, people who survived by striking first—and one by one, they put their weapons away.

Later she could not have said why. Later she understood: because some things you do not win. Some things you only get to be present for.

* * *

**Ulyana**

Ulyana felt Sef die the way you feel a door close in a house you love: a change in the air, a hush where a voice had been. The words of the rite faltered in their mouth. Veyl's grip tightened.

"He chose it," Veyl said softly. "Finish what he bought."

Ulyana finished it.

They spoke the old covenant's terms and named them broken—not shattered, *outgrown*, the way a child outgrows a coat. They spoke of their ancestor who had sworn obedience out of terror, who had bound the gods with fear because fear was the only ink she trusted, and they forgave her, out loud, in front of witnesses, because forgiveness was also a kind of remembrance. They spoke of the hedge-witch in her burned cottage, of letters never sent, of apprenticeships fled and returned to. They spoke of smugglers and defrocked priests and a god who remembered being human.

And then they spoke the new terms, and their voice carried through the weeping wood the way light carries through water:

*"Let the covenant be remade not on obedience compelled but on remembrance freely given. Let no god command and no mortal kneel unless kneeling is chosen. Let the binding ink be memory—held, honored, carried for one another. What is remembered freely cannot be lost. What is loved openly cannot be caged. This we swear, and may it be unsworn the day it stops being true, for a covenant that cannot be refused cannot be real."*

Veyl exhaled—a long, long breath, like a man setting down a burden carried across a century—and began to forget.

Ulyana felt it happen. Felt the memory pass into them like water into dry ground: a mother singing off-key, a brother's laugh, snow, a coat given away, a boy freezing outside a shrine because kindness mattered more than warmth. All of it, pouring across the bridge of their joined hands, settling into Ulyana's bones where it would live for the rest of their life.

When Veyl opened his eyes, they were kind and clear and completely empty of him.

"Hello," he said, in the tone of a man greeting a stranger he somehow already trusted. "I don't know your name. But I think I'm supposed to stay here. With you. Is that right?"

Ulyana laughed, and it broke in the middle, and they let it.

"That's right," they said. "Stay."

* * *

**Halvard**

Halvard did not remember walking to the center of the ring.

He remembered the weight of Sef going slack in his arms. He remembered the trees—the dead ones—shivering as the words passed over them, buds breaking grey bark like knuckles cracking open, green flooding upward through a hundred years of dying wood. He remembered falling to his knees, though whether from wound or awe he could not have said, and he remembered the witch turning to look at him across the greening ground, and the god—no, the man, he was only a man now, Halvard could see that, he could see it plain as morning—the man standing beside them with a stranger's gentle face.

"Inquisitor." The witch's voice was hoarse, wrecked, alive. "You came to kill us. You can still try. But first—look."

Halvard looked.

The weeping wood, weeping no longer. Leaves where there had been bare grief. His daughter had loved trees; she had pressed leaves in her mother's books until the pages smelled of autumn. He thought of his ledger, all those neat entries cataloguing answered prayers as evidence of conquest, and understood suddenly, with a pain like the blade going in again, that he had been reading the book upside down.

The gods had not returned to take.

They had returned to be remembered. And the first thing they had shown him—the first gift of the new covenant, given to the man who came to destroy it—was a priest who died choosing, and a forest learning to breathe.

His torch had gone out somewhere. He did not relight it. He sat down in the loam among the roots, in the dripping green dark, and wept with the trees, and no one struck him, and no one commanded him to stop.

Above him, the last dead tree of the weeping wood put forth a single leaf, and held on.

Far below, in the cottage at Thornfell's edge, a chimney that had been cold for years would soon learn smoke again—but that story belonged to the morning, and to the woman who would carry two lives' worth of memory up the mountain path, one week at a time, to visit a man who would never ask her name, only smile, and make room beside him on the root, as if he had always known she was coming.
