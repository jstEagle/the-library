## 10. Visible

Spring came late to Thornfell that year, and when it came, it came like a confession.

Ulyana stood at the edge of the weeping wood on the first morning the snow gave up its hold, watching a birch that had been gray as bone since midwinter push out leaves so green they seemed lit from within. Around it, tree by tree, the wood was remembering itself. Sap ran where sap had stopped. The blackened hollows in the old oaks were knitting over with pale new bark, slow as scar tissue, stubborn as anything the mountain had ever grown.

They had not done this alone. That was the thing Ulyana kept having to learn: nothing here was done alone. The covenant was remade on remembrance freely given, and remembrance turned out to be a crop — you planted it with words, watered it with visits, and then you waited through seasons you could not command while the ground decided what to do with what you'd offered.

The wood had decided well.

Ulyana walked in along the path they had worn with their own boots, once a week since the rite, every week without fail, even the week Brek Onda married her quartermaster under a sky full of smugglers' kites and Ulyana had drunk far too much plum brandy to be walking anywhere. Even the week Sef's sister had come down from the high valleys to stand where her brother died, saying nothing, leaving a candle stub on the root of the oak that had caught him like a mother's arms when he fell. The wood held all of it now. Every grief, every small treason of tenderness. It had learned that from them.

The path bent past the stream, past the stone where the old oath's last words were still carved and would always be carved, because Ulyana had argued long into the night that erasure was not remembrance — and Veyl, nameless then and nameless still, had tilted his head and said, *Then let it stay, as a scar stays. Scars are how bodies remember.*

He was at the stream now. He was always at the stream, or the birch stand, or the eastern slope where the first of the dying trees had finally, fully, come back. He worked from before dawn until the light went thin, barefoot half the year, his hands in the soil or against the bark, doing work he could not have named the purpose of if anyone asked — though no one asked, because no one came here but Ulyana.

He did not know their name.

That was the price, and the price had been paid, and paying it had not made it smaller. Veyl had given up not his divinity but his humanity — the single true memory of the man he had been before the oath took his name, the life that had made him capable of love in the first place. And folded inside that memory, inseparable from it, was Ulyana. Their voice. Their hands. The night they prayed out of habit to a god everyone said was dead, and the star fell, and something ancient looked up out of the dark of the world and saw them for the first time in a hundred years.

He remembered everything else. He remembered being a god; he remembered the covenant; he remembered, in the abstract way one remembers a story heard in childhood, that there had been someone. A mortal who spoke the new oath. Someone whose prayers had woken him. He remembered that this someone visited every week.

He never knew why.

"Good morning," Ulyana said, from the path.

Veyl looked up. His face was still the face she had first seen in the wood's heart — a human face, weathered and plain, the kind of face that belonged to a farmer or a ferryman or a man who mended nets — except for the eyes, which had never quite learned to be only human. When he smiled, and he smiled often now, the whole clearing seemed to lean toward him like plants toward a window.

"You again," he said warmly. "Every week. You must live near."

"I do," Ulyana said. "I live at the cottage."

"The burned one, on the south slope? It has a roof again." He frowned faintly, pleased and puzzled, a man trying to recall where he'd set down a tool. "I remember smoke there. I don't remember why."

"There was a hedge-witch who lived there. She taught me everything I know and most of what I regret." Ulyana crouched at the stream's edge, cupped cold water, drank. "I've taken the cottage back. I'm going to be the hedge-witch now."

"A good trade," Veyl said seriously, "water for smoke," and Ulyana laughed, and the laugh startled a wren out of the birch, and the wren's alarm-call scattered into the green morning like seed.

* * *

The cottage smelled of rosemary and woodsmoke and, underneath both, of ash that would never fully leave — Ulyana had stopped trying to scrub it out of the beams. Some things should be remembered in the body, Mistress Ivenna used to say, pressing Ulyana's fingers into rich loam. Not just in the head. The head forgets. The hands don't.

Ivenna's grimoire sat open on the table now, weighted at both corners with river stones. Ulyana had copied it out twice — once faithfully, once amended — because the second copy was the one that mattered. In its margins, in their own blunt hand, they had written the new covenant entire, the words spoken aloud in the wood's heart with Halvard's soldiers at the treeline and Sef's blood still dark on the snow:

*We remember freely. We bind no one. Let every soul who speaks these words give one true memory to the keeping of the wood, and let the wood keep it kindly, and let nothing be compelled, now or ever, in the name of god or crown or kin.*

Below it, the names. Brek Onda, who had given the memory of her brother's face, because she wanted to stop seeing it in ambushes and start seeing it laughing. Sef's sister, who had given a memory of Sef at eight years old, stealing honey, unrepentant. Halvard himself — Halvard, who had walked out of the weeping wood unarmed, ledger left open on a stump to the wind, later resigning an office that had eaten him alive from the inside. His memory given had been his daughter's voice singing. He said afterward, in a letter Ulyana kept folded in the grimoire, that he had expected giving it away to feel like losing her. It felt, instead, like setting her down somewhere safe.

And at the top of the list, the first entry, written in ink Ulyana had mixed from oak-gall and their own blood because the old ways deserved at least the courtesy of consistency:

*Veyl. Who gave everything, and remembers nothing of what it cost, and tends the wood anyway. Remember him for two.*

That was the apprenticeship, really. Not the herbs — though Ulyana learned the herbs, and taught them, and the village children came up the south slope on rest days to learn yarrow from nettle and to be fed honey-cakes by a witch who was rumored, correctly, to have talked with a god. No. The apprenticeship was this: carrying what others had laid down. Holding a name for someone who could not. Being the hands that remember, since hands don't forget.

Some mornings it was almost bearable.

* * *

On the day the last dead tree budded — a great blackened pine on the eastern slope, dead longer than any living soul could say, its covenant-fraying older than Ulyana's whole lineage of oath-keepers — Ulyana brought two cups of nettle tea up the path and found Veyl sitting against the pine's trunk with his eyes closed, bark dust in his hair, listening.

"To what?" Ulyana asked, sitting beside him, handing over a cup.

"Roots," he said. "Underground. There is—" He searched, the way he always searched, a man patting his pockets for keys that had been sold. "There is a sound they make when they find each other. Under the snow, under the soil. I think I used to know its name."

"Would you like me to tell you?"

"No." He said it gently, but firmly, and Ulyana nodded, because they had this agreement too: he did not want to be handed his own history in fragments, the way one feeds scraps to something tamed. Whatever he rebuilt, he would rebuild forward. "Tell me instead about the cottage. You had a student this week. You had that look."

"Mira. The cooper's girl. She floated a feather for four counts without touching it, then threw up in the herb bed."

"Four counts is very good."

"It's excellent. I didn't manage three at her age." Ulyana wrapped both hands around the warm cup. "She asked me today if the gods are real. The other children have been asking too. Word gets down the mountain. And I told her yes — some of them. One of them. And she asked what he was like, and I said—" Ulyana paused. The pine above them shivered in a wind that hadn't reached the ground yet. "I said he was like a man who works very hard at something he loves and doesn't remember why, and does it anyway. And she said, *that's just my da.*"

Veyl laughed — a real laugh, unguarded, human as bread — and knocked his shoulder against theirs. "Wise child. Your da sounds better than a god, honestly. Gods get ideas."

They sat a while. The tea steamed. Somewhere below, the stream ran loud with meltwater, carrying winter downhill toward the lowlands, toward villages where shrines were being rebuilt not by decree but by volunteers, badly, lovingly, out of whatever stone people had at hand. The covenant held. Not the old one — not obedience, not compulsion, not the terrible arithmetic of the original oath that had spent a child's memory like coin. This one breathed. It required tending, which meant it required people, which meant, Ulyana thought, watching Veyl's dirt-cured hands around the cup, that it would always be kept by someone showing up. Weekly. Without fail. With tea.

"I should go," Ulyana said at last, as the light began to lean. "Mira's coming at dawn. First lesson on roots — the actual roots. Yarrow hates being transplanted, so we'll see how she handles failure."

"Will you come back next week?" Veyl asked. He asked every week, and it never stopped catching somewhere under Ulyana's ribs — the simple, guileless need of it, the god who had forgotten them asking the question as if the answer weren't the spine of his whole rebuilt world.

"Every week," Ulyana said. "You couldn't keep me away."

He smiled and turned back to the roots, and Ulyana walked down through the greening wood, past the stone with the old scarred oath, out onto the south slope where the cottage waited with its roof of new shakes and its ghost of smoke in the beams.

Inside, the grimoire lay open to the list of names. Ulyana dipped a pen, hesitated, and beneath the entries — beneath Brek, beneath Sef's sister, beneath Halvard's daughter's singing voice — wrote a new line, the beginning of a page that would grow for as long as Ulyana lived and, they intended, beyond:

*Mira, cooper's daughter. Gave nothing yet. Too young. But she asked what a god is like, and when I told her, she said she'd like to give him something someday, so he wouldn't be lonely.*

*So. There will be more of us.*

Outside, far upslope, faint on the wind, someone was humming — wordless, tuneless, contented — a song with no name, sung by a man with no name, to a wood that remembered everything, for both of them.
