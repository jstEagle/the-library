## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The candle had been burning for six hours, and Felix Fairweather had counted every one of its drips.

Not on purpose. Counting was simply what happened to a mind that had spent eleven years keeping the ledgers of the Gilded Thorn. Numbers arranged themselves in Felix's head the way other people's thoughts arranged themselves—uninvited, insistent, occasionally useful. The candle had wept forty-one rivulets of wax down its own length. The ink in Felix's well was three fingers from empty. The audit was nine entries from finished, and entry four hundred and twelve was going to be a problem.

Felix turned to it with the resignation of a person who has already read a letter they dread and must now read it again slowly.

*Item 412: One (1) jar of last words, collected at the deathbed of Magistrate Hollen of Ashford. Contents verified. Provenance disputed. Buyer: anonymous. Payment received: partial.*

The jar sat on Felix's desk where the courier had left it three days ago, wrapped in oilcloth like something ashamed of itself. It was a plain preserve jar, the kind a grandmother might fill with plums. Inside, instead of plums, there was a soft grey murk that shifted when you weren't looking directly at it—the way a cat settles only when it believes no one is watching. Last words were like that. Shy goods.

Felix had verified the contents twice, which was twice more than anyone should have to verify a dead man's final utterances. The first time, the murk had whispered something in a voice like wind through a keyhole. *The second drawer*, it had said, or possibly *the second daughter*. Deathbed acoustics were notoriously unreliable. The second time, Felix had worn wool over their ears and heard nothing at all, which was either proof of authenticity or proof of nothing, depending on which manual one consulted. Felix had written *contents consistent with expectation* in the ledger and hated themselves a little for it.

*Provenance disputed.* There was the real trouble. The jar had come through three intermediaries, each of whom swore the last words belonged to Magistrate Hollen, and each of whom told a different story about how he'd died. One said fever. One said a fall from his own balcony. One, a woman with a tattooed scalp and the honest eyes of a career liar, had said "he died of knowing too much," and then had charged Felix double for the color commentary.

Felix dipped the pen. Wrote: *Recommend holding item until provenance resolved or buyer produces seal of verification. Note: buyer has produced neither. Note also: buyer's coin is genuine, which is more than can be said for most things in this building.*

Then, after a moment, crossed out the second note. A keeper of records recorded facts. Feelings went in the margins, if anywhere, and even there they were supposed to be small.

Felix's margins were not small. Anyone who ever stole these ledgers—and people did try; the Gilded Thorn traded in impossible things, and impossible things attracted thieves the way honey attracted bears with ambition—would find a second history hidden in the margins. Complaints. Jokes. Once, memorably, a full recipe for plum cordial, recorded because the courier who'd delivered a debt paid in teeth had lingered long enough to describe her mother's version, and Felix had been lonely enough to write it down as though it mattered.

It hadn't mattered. But Felix had kept the page.

* * *

The Gilded Thorn occupied a former countinghouse in the Saltcloth Quarter, three stories of soot-dark stone that had once held the city's grain accounts and now held considerably stranger inventory. On the ground floor, the trading room, where clients came to buy what could not be bought elsewhere: bottled grief by the dram, borrowed years under strict contract, names not yet spoken (a niche market, but lucrative—a name spoken before its owner drew breath made a powerful gift and a terrible curse, sometimes both at once). The second floor held the vaults. The third floor held Felix, a desk, two thousand ledgers, and a window that looked out onto a chimney, because even the architecture seemed to agree that record-keepers should see as little sky as possible.

Felix liked it here. Felix would have said, out loud, in a firm voice, that Felix loved it here.

There were things to love. There was the particular satisfaction of an audit that balanced, which was a satisfaction like cool water on a burn. There was Odile, who worked the vault floor and brought up tea at odd hours without being asked, and whose tea tasted like bark steeped in regret but whose company tasted like almost-friendship, which was a flavor Felix had learned to savor the way one savors the smell of bread through a bakery window. There was Guildmaster Corvane, who called Felix *my good Keeper* in a voice like oiled walnut and had never once, in eleven years, asked Felix anything personal—which Felix had decided to interpret as respect rather than indifference, because the alternative interpretation didn't bear auditing.

And there was the work itself, which was the truest thing about Felix, the way a spine is the truest thing about a book. Felix Fairweather was the keeper of the Thorn's records, and the records were the guild's actual treasure. Bottled grief could be re-bottled. Borrowed years could be stolen back. But a ledger, properly kept, was a promise with a memory. Every impossible transaction in the city passed eventually through Felix's columns, and as long as those columns balanced, the impossible stayed merely strange instead of becoming catastrophic. It was Felix who had caught the year-vendor selling the same borrowed decade to four different buyers. It was Felix who had traced the shadow-debt back before it metastasized. It was Felix who knew, to the drop, how much grief the guild had in stock, and whose grief it had originally been, and whether it had been ethically harvested, and—if one read far enough into the margins—whether it smelled faintly of rain, which some griefs did, and which Felix privately found beautiful.

Competent. Detached. Reliable. These were the words used about Felix Fairweather, always warmly, never intimately. *Send it to Fairweather. Fairweather will know. Good old Fairweather.*

Good old Fairweather ate dinner alone in the third-floor office more nights than not, and had recently begun, in a development they refused to examine closely, saying "good night" aloud to the empty room when blowing out the candle. Just to hear a voice say it. Their own voice would do. It was beginning to be the only one available.

But tonight the candle still burned, and entry four hundred and thirteen waited, and entry four hundred and thirteen was the shadow-debt.

* * *

Debts paid in shadows were not illegal, precisely. They were *unregistered*, which in the Saltcloth Quarter amounted to the same thing with better posture. The theory was simple enough: a debtor too poor to pay in coin could pay in shade—one hour of their own shadow, detached, signed over, and stored. Shadows kept well. They didn't spoil like memories or sour like grudges. And they were worth something to buyers Felix preferred not to think about, because thinking about them meant writing about them, and writing about them meant the ledger developed opinions, and a ledger with opinions was a ledger that stopped balancing.

This particular debt had been incurred by a cobbler named Bram Tuller, who owed the guild fourteen crowns and had instead surrendered thirty hours of shadow across five payments. Felix pulled the folio and checked the arithmetic, because checking the arithmetic was the closest thing Felix had to prayer.

The arithmetic was wrong.

Not dramatically. That was the trouble. Fourteen crowns at the standard exchange came to twenty-eight hours of shadow, and Tuller had paid thirty. Two hours overpaid. A careless auditor would have let it stand—an overpayment in the guild's favor, after all, and who complains about a surplus? A lazy auditor would have flagged it and moved on. Felix did neither. Felix sat very still for a while, in the manner of a person who has noticed that one picture frame in the house is hanging crooked and now cannot stop seeing it.

Two hours of shadow, unaccounted for, sitting somewhere in the guild's stores like a guest no one remembered inviting.

Felix pulled the storage manifests. Cross-referenced. Checked again. The vault records showed twenty-eight hours received against Tuller's account. The payment folios showed thirty tendered. Somewhere between the trading room and the vault, two hours of a poor man's shadow had evaporated—or, less pleasantly, had been diverted. And diversions required a signature. Felix found the receiving slip at the bottom of the folio, and the signature on it was a neat, confident hand that Felix knew extremely well, because Felix saw it every week on requisitions and wage orders and the occasional note reading *my good Keeper, attend me at your convenience.*

Corvane had personally countersigned a receipt for two hours of shadow that the vault had never recorded receiving.

Felix set down the pen.

Now. A reasonable person, at this point, would consider possibilities. Ink fades; perhaps this was a copyist's error. Clerks are human; perhaps the vault manifest was mislaid. Two hours of shadow is a trivial sum; perhaps it wasn't worth the paper it would take to query it.

But Felix Fairweather was not, by trade or temperament, a reasonable person. Felix was an auditor. And an auditor knows that no error is trivial, because errors are never singular—they are the visible tips of mistakes that keep the rest of themselves submerged. If Corvane's signature appeared on one receipt that didn't match the vault, then either the Guildmaster of the Gilded Thorn was sloppy, which Felix did not believe for one heartbeat, or the Guildmaster of the Gilded Thorn had a second set of books.

And a guild that trades in impossible things, with a second set of books, was a thought with teeth.

Felix copied the discrepancy into the margin—small, precise, dated—and closed the folio gently, as one closes a door on a sleeping animal one hopes will remain asleep. Then Felix did the thing that eleven years of discipline demanded and eleven years of loneliness made easy: told no one.

Not yet. Not until the numbers were certain. Numbers first. Numbers were safe. Numbers didn't look at Felix with pity or curiosity or that particular soft concern that Odile sometimes wore like a shawl she kept offering and Felix kept declining.

Outside, the chimney stood black against the evening. Somewhere below, the trading room was closing up, bolts sliding home, the day's impossible goods locked away from the city's hungry hands. Felix blew out the candle—said *good night* to the room, quietly, hating the habit and performing it anyway—and gathered the nightly ritual things from their drawer: the small iron bell, the strip of grey cloth, the folded card bearing the words of the oath.

Because there was one account Felix kept that appeared in no ledger, audited no column, and balanced nothing. Eleven years ago, Felix Fairweather, mercenary, had sworn service to Vess, the silent god—the god of witnesses, of records, of things seen and spoken truly. Every mercenary under Vess drew power from the oath: steadiness of aim, iron nerve, the uncanny knack for being believed. In return, nightly, they rang the bell and spoke the words, and the god answered—or had answered, once—with a presence like being remembered.

Tonight would make one hundred nights since the answer had come.

Felix stood at the window with the little bell in hand, looking out at the chimney, at the dark beyond it, at whatever silence lived past all of it.

"Still here," Felix told the darkness, in the tone of a bookkeeper reporting to a client who had stopped paying. "Ledgers balance. Mostly. I'm—" and here the voice did something unprofessional, something small, "*I'm still here.*"

Felix rang the bell. Spoke the oath. Waited.

The silence, for the hundredth time, said nothing back.
