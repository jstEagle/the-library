## 2. A God Who Stopped Answering

The ritual took eleven minutes, if Felix did it properly, and nine minutes and change if they were honest about how tired they were. Tonight they were honest.

The Gilded Thorn's shrine occupied a closet on the third floor that had once, according to guild legend, housed the accounting office of a tax collector who had been eaten by his own ledger. Whether this was true or merely aspirational, the room retained certain qualities: a smell of old paper and faint menace, walls that seemed to listen, and a single window through which the moon could be coaxed to look in, if one stood in exactly the right spot and did not look back too eagerly. Gods were like cats in that respect. Everyone said so. Felix had never owned a cat.

They knelt on the worn stone — the depression in it shaped, over generations of knees, into something almost comfortable, which struck Felix as theologically suspicious — and drew the circle of salt with a steady hand. Salt from the eastern flats. Vess had opinions about salt. Most gods had opinions about everything; that was generally what made them gods rather than large, disappointed weather systems.

Then the words. The Oath of the Weeping Wood was not written down anywhere — writing an oath invited lawyers, and lawyers invited gods' lawyers, and no one wanted that — so it lived in the mouth, passed from sworn knight to sworn knight like a fever. Felix spoke it now, low and even, the way you'd speak to a horse you suspected was thinking about bolting.

*"Vess who listens, I bring you my day."*

The ritual required an accounting. This was, Felix privately suspected, the real reason Vess had attracted so many mercenaries over the centuries: the god of witnesses demanded bookkeeping. You laid out your day before him like coins on a counter, each one turned so he could see both faces.

"Three entries today," Felix said aloud, their breath fogging in the cold closet air. "First: settled the dispute between Mistress Halloway and the ferryman over the drowned ring. The ring was recovered. The marriage, I judge, is beyond recovery, but that wasn't in my remit. Second: catalogued the jar of last words, all forty-one of them accounted for, none escaped, though the one belonging to the fisherman tried the lid again. Third—" they hesitated, because honesty was the entire point of the exercise, and lying to Vess felt like forging a signature in front of the signatory — "third, I ate dinner alone again, and I told Odile I'd been busy when really I just didn't want to make conversation. That's the whole entry. It's small. But you're supposed to get the small ones too."

Silence answered.

Not dramatic silence. Not the silence of thunder holding its breath. Just the ordinary, room-temperature silence of a closet at midnight, which was exactly the problem. A hundred nights of it now. Felix counted the way they counted everything — precisely, obsessively, in a ledger with columns — and tonight's tally came up round.

One hundred nights since Vess had last spoken. One hundred nights since the salt had warmed, since the faint pressure of being *heard* had settled over the shoulders like a hand. One hundred nights of speaking their day into a room where nobody lived.

Felix finished the ritual anyway. That was the thing about oaths: they were not weather. They did not stop being binding because the other party had stopped showing up. *"Vess who listens,"* they said, closing the circle, sweeping the salt into its pouch, *"I keep my accounts current whether or not you read them."*

And then, because the closet was empty and the hour was late and there are things a person can only say to a god who isn't answering, Felix added, quietly:

"I'm frightened."

The words hung in the dark like smoke with nowhere to go.

* * *

The trouble — and Felix had been circling this trouble for weeks the way a tongue circles a loose tooth — was that oaths were not decorative. When a mercenary swore to Vess, the god gave something back. Not fire from the fingertips, nothing so vulgar. Vess gave *witness*: the ability to see a lie as it left a person's mouth, to hear the true name under the false one, to stand in a room and know, absolutely, what had happened there. To a keeper of records, it was worth more than any sword. To the Gilded Thorn, whose entire business rested on knowing which impossible things were genuine, Felix's gift made them indispensable.

And it was fading.

Two nights ago Felix had examined a debt paid in shadows — a genuinely beautiful piece of work, the shadow of a dead man's youth, folded like cloth — and had felt nothing. No hum. No certainty. They'd had to judge it by eye and instinct like some common appraiser, and they'd gotten it right, probably, but *probably* was a word that got record-keepers killed in this trade.

Tonight, kneeling in the closet, Felix had tested it deliberately. They'd spoken a lie into the empty circle — *the guildmaster pays me fairly* — and waited for the old flinch of divine attention, the sensation of the lie being seen, weighed, found wanting.

Nothing. Not even a draft.

Felix sat back on their heels in the dark and did the arithmetic every mercenary dreads. If the power went entirely, the oath remained — binding them still, forbidding false witness, demanding their days — while giving nothing back. An oath with obligations and no benefits. In the guild trades, that arrangement had a name. It was called *being swindled*.

* * *

Odile was in the kitchen, which she always was after midnight, on the theory — loudly held — that anything cooked before midnight belonged to yesterday and therefore to whoever had cooked it, while anything cooked after belonged to tomorrow and thus to her.

She was frying eggs in the guild's enormous black pan, barefoot, her grey-streaked braid coming loose, and she looked up when Felix came in with the particular expression of someone who has already decided to feed them.

"You have your 'I've been auditing' face," she said. "Sit down. Eggs fix auditing faces. It's practically medicine."

"It's nearly one."

"And yet here are eggs." She cracked two more into the pan with the ease of long practice. "Sit, Fairweather. You're blocking the light from nothing."

Felix sat. The kitchen of the Gilded Thorn was the only warm room in the building, mostly because Odile had declared war on the chimney sweep years ago and won, and the stove ran hot and stubborn, like its owner. Someone had pinned a drawing to the icebox — a child's sketch of the guildhouse with everyone standing outside it, done by the cook's daughter — and beneath it someone else had written *no exceptions*. Felix looked at it longer than they meant to.

"Eggs," announced Odile, sliding a plate across.

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me, audit me. What's wrong?"

"Nothing's—"

"Felix." She said it the way she said *the pan is hot*, a simple statement of physical law. "You came into my kitchen at one in the morning wearing the face you wear when a number doesn't add up, except it's not a number, is it. So. Out with it."

Felix turned the fork over twice. There was a version of this conversation where they said *nothing*, finished the eggs, and went upstairs to lie awake doing the same arithmetic until dawn. They had been having that version for weeks. It was, they were discovering, exhausting.

"The hundredth night," Felix said.

Odile's spatula stopped moving. Then resumed, slower.

"Ah," she said.

"You knew?"

"Everyone knows. You count out loud when you think no one's listening. You count in your sleep, sometimes, too." She sat down across from him with her own plate. "Hundred nights of what? Say it plain."

"Vess has gone quiet." The words came easier than expected, once started, like water finding a crack. "Not quiet like a god being mysterious. Quiet like a room where the person left. I do the ritual every night. Full rite, proper salt, proper words. And there's nothing. And Odile—" Felix heard their own voice do the thing they hated, the careful, level thing, competence as camouflage — "and my gifts are going with it. The seeing-lies. The reading of rooms. It's like... a lamp running out of oil. Slowly. From the edges inward."

Odile chewed, considering. Outside, rain had begun against the kitchen window, soft and businesslike.

"How long?" she asked.

"Since the fading started? Months. Since I noticed? Six weeks. Since I admitted it?" Felix looked at the plate. "About four minutes."

"What happens if it goes all the way?"

"That depends on what the oath actually is underneath. If it's a contract — service given, power returned — then when the power stops, the contract stops, and I'm free. Which sounds nice." Felix laughed, without much humor in it. "It isn't. Because if it's not a contract — if it's a vow, one-way, the way the old knights swore — then the obligations stay forever and I'm just... a man who talks to an empty closet every night until he dies. Keeping accounts for a god who will never read them."

Odile set down her fork.

"And which is it?"

"That," said Felix, "is the question, isn't it. And there's no one left to ask. The old knights are dust. The orders that trained us burned a century ago. There's me, and a salt pouch, and a hundred nights of nothing." They rubbed their eyes. "I'm mid-contract, Odile. That's the thing that keeps me up. You don't abandon a job halfway. You especially don't abandon a job halfway when the client is a *god*, because whatever the god wanted done, it isn't done, and I don't even know what it *was*. I'm standing in the middle of a sentence someone started saying a hundred nights ago and never finished, and I can't leave the room until I find out how it ends."

The rain ticked against the glass. Somewhere above, the guildhouse creaked through its nightly argument with the wind.

Odile reached across and put her hand flat over Felix's, rough palm, warm from the stove.

"Listen to me," she said. "Gods go quiet. My grandmother used to say the river god of her village slept through whole winters, and the fishermen just kept leaving the offerings, because that's what faith is — talking to somebody who might be asleep. Maybe Vess is asleep. Maybe he's sulking. Maybe—" she waved her fork vaguely upward — "maybe gods have their own audits and yours got filed under *later*. Either way, you show up tomorrow night, and the next night, and the one after. That's the whole job. That's always been the whole job."

"And if the powers never come back?"

"Then the Thorn still needs someone who can tell a true ledger from a forged one with nothing but eyes and stubbornness. You managed it before the oath, presumably. You were a person first, Felix. The god came second." Her voice gentled. "The family comes third, if you'd ever let it."

Felix looked at their hands. At the plate. At the child's drawing on the icebox, *no exceptions*, in a wobbling childish hand.

"I don't know how to be a person first," they admitted. "I've been the Keeper of Records for eleven years. Before that I was Vess's. Before that I was..." A pause, a small frown, like reaching for a stair that wasn't where the foot remembered. "Before that I don't clearly recall. Isn't that strange? You'd think a man who keeps ledgers would keep better track of himself."

Odile's expression did something complicated and fast — a flicker, quickly smoothed, like a page turned before it could be read. Later, much later, Felix would understand exactly what that flicker meant. Tonight it passed unremarked, the way most important things do.

"Everyone's got blank pages," she said lightly, and stood, and collected the plates. "Go to bed, Fairweather. Do your ritual tomorrow. Talk to your sleeping god. And if the closet gives you any more trouble—" she pointed the spatula at them with terrible warmth — "you come to my kitchen. That's an order from the family."

Felix smiled, said goodnight, climbed the stairs.

At the landing they paused. Below, faintly, they could hear Odile washing the plates, humming — an old song, older than the guild, a marching cadence with the words worn off it, the kind of tune soldiers sing when they've forgotten why they're singing. It snagged somewhere in Felix's chest like a hook in cloth. They almost went back down to ask where she'd learned it.

They didn't. They were tired, and the arithmetic was waiting, and some questions feel safe to postpone indefinitely.

Tomorrow, Felix decided, they would finish cataloguing the seized archive from the Halloran estate — three crates of confiscated texts, dull work, safe work, work for a man whose gifts were fading and whose hands needed to stay busy.

In the third crate, wrapped in oilcloth and breathing faintly, as if asleep, lay the Weeping Wood.

But that was tomorrow.
