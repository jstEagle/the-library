## 3. Tonight, Written a Century Ago

The seized archive smelled of mildew and old money, which was to say it smelled like most of the Thorn's acquisitions. Felix Fairweather had been cataloguing it for six days, and in that time they had developed three firm opinions: first, that the late Baron Ulvane had been a collector in the way a magpie is an architect; second, that at least forty percent of his "priceless antiquities" were convincing fakes; and third, that whoever had crated these items had done so during a minor earthquake, blindfolded, as punishment.

"Item four hundred eleven," Felix murmured, dipping their pen. "*One jar, sealed wax, contents: last words of a dying sailor, preserved per standard grief-distillation.* Condition: leaking."

The jar sat on the workbench before them, sweating faintly through its seal. Every so often a word escaped—a syllable of salt and sorrow—and drifted up to the rafters where it hung like fog. Felix had learned, over the years, to catalogue such things without listening. That was the trick of the ledger-keeper's trade: you wrote down what a thing was without ever asking what it meant. *Jar of words* went into the column. The fact that the words were somebody's mother saying *come home*—that stayed outside the ledger, where Felix preferred to keep all the things that might make their hands shake.

Their hands did not shake. That was rather the point of Felix Fairweather. In nine years with the Gilded Thorn they had appraised a debt paid in shadows (tricky; shadows breed), a vial of Tuesday that never happened (worthless, technically, but Odile had wept holding it), and once, memorably, a king's apology, still warm. They were known throughout the guild as the person who could write anything down without flinching. Corvane called them "the steadiest quill in the city," which Felix suspected was both praise and a warning about what would happen if the quill ever wavered.

Felix was fine with this. Truly. A person needed only one skill to be valuable, and steadiness was cheaper than swordsmanship and paid better than honesty.

They finished the jar's entry, set it on the *leaking, handle carefully* shelf, and turned to the crates stacked against the archive's far wall—the ones that had arrived yesterday from the baron's estate, wrapped in oilcloth and smelling faintly of forest.

This crate was different. For one thing, it was heavier than its size suggested, in the way that ledgers are heavier than their paper should allow, or that guilt is heavier than its absence of substance. For another, the oilcloth was damp on one side, though it hadn't rained in a fortnight.

"Item four hundred twelve," Felix said, and cut the twine.

Inside, packed in straw gone brown with age, lay a book.

Or—it looked like a book. It had the right shape, roughly folio-sized, and something that functioned as a cover. But when Felix lifted it free of the straw, the weight was wrong: dense, organic, faintly warm, like lifting a sleeping cat that disapproved of being lifted. The "cover" had grain to it. Not leather-grain. *Wood*-grain, whorled and pale as birch, and along the spine ran what appeared very much to be bark.

"Well," said Felix, to the empty archive, because talking to oneself was one of the few luxuries of working alone past midnight. "You're going to be a problem."

They set it on the workbench beneath the lamp and looked closer. The pages—if pages they were—had the soft translucence of fresh vellum but a texture that made Felix think of the inside of a leaf, of veins, of green things pressed flat. And here was the strangest part: the edges of the book were not cut. They were *grown*, tapering naturally, the way branches taper, as though somewhere in some impossible orchard a tree had decided, after long consideration, to become literature.

Felix knew the trade names for impossible books. *Grimoires* were merely difficult. *Reliquaries* were dangerous. But there was a third category, spoken of only in the oldest appraisal manuals, in footnotes written by authors who seemed embarrassed to mention it: texts not made but *cultivated*. Books grown. The manuals agreed such things were vanishingly rare, fabulously valuable, and absolutely not to be touched without guild sanction.

Felix looked at the book. The book, insofar as a book can look back, looked at Felix.

"Just an inventory entry," Felix told it firmly. "*One volume, botanical origin, provenance unknown.* Then you go in the restricted case, and Master Corvane decides what you are, and I go home and sleep the dreamless sleep of a person whose ledgers balance."

It was a good plan. Felix had made many good plans in nine years and executed nearly all of them, which was more than could be said for anyone else in the Gilded Thorn.

Then they opened the cover.

* * *

The first page stopped Felix's breath for a moment—not from beauty, though it was beautiful, but from recognition. The text was handwritten, ink-brown, flowing in a script a century out of fashion. And the language was Vessic.

Most people, hearing that, would have thought: *so what, old religious text, the god of oaths and silence, half the contracts in the kingdom invoke him.* But Felix had sworn to Vess thirteen years ago, kneeling in a rain-soaked gully with a mercenary company dying around them, and had spent every night since performing the small ritual—candle, coin, the four words of the oath renewed. They knew Vessic the way you know your own heartbeat: intimately, constantly, and without ever quite being able to explain it to anyone else.

And this text was *personal*. Not scripture. Not liturgy. It read like a journal—or like testimony. Page after page in different hands, dozens of hands, each entry beginning the same way:

*I saw.*

Felix turned pages slowly. *I saw the god refuse the war. I saw the generals meet beneath the moon. I saw the sealing, and I swore to remember, and the remembering is heavy.* Each entry signed with a mark instead of a name—a symbol, a rune, something like a sigil. Twelve distinct marks, repeating across the decades, the same twelve witnesses writing again and again across a century, the handwriting aging and changing but the words continuing, patient as roots.

*A century,* Felix thought. *These entries span a century.* Which was impossible, unless—

Unless the book was still growing. Unless somewhere, someone was still writing.

Felix's professional detachment, that well-worn coat they wore against the world, began to feel thin at the shoulders.

They kept turning. The entries grew stranger, more urgent, the later hands pressing hard enough to score the leaf-flesh of the pages. *The prison weakens. We count the turns. When the moon makes her thirteenth turn—*

A date followed. A full date: day, month, year.

Felix read it once. Read it again. Set the book down very carefully, walked to the window, opened it, breathed the cold night air for the length of ten heartbeats, closed the window, and returned.

Because the date was tonight.

Not near tonight. Not in the vicinity. It was today's date, exactly, to the day, circled twice in that century-old hand—and beside it, drawn small and precise, an image that Felix's eyes refused at first to parse and then parsed all too well.

A figure in a hood. Holding a ledger.

The hooded figure stood at the edge of a grove of trees, and the trees were weeping—drawn with fine strokes, tears falling from the branches like fruit—and the hooded figure's face was left blank. Unfinished. As though the artist had intended to fill it in later.

As though the artist had been waiting to see who would arrive.

"No," Felix said aloud. Their voice came out steady, which was somehow worse than if it had shaken. Steadiness, at this particular moment, felt less like competence and more like dissociation wearing a clerk's apron. "No, that's—coincidence. The Thorn deals in prophecy-texts. Half of them are frauds. You find any date you want in a fraud if you squint."

But Felix was the best appraiser of frauds in the guild, and they were looking at this book with all their skill, and their skill was telling them, in the calm voice they used for valuations, that this was no fraud. Fraudulent prophecy aged badly; the ink sat on the surface of lies. This ink had *soaked in*. This page had grown around its own words the way a tree grows around a nail driven into its trunk young. Whatever hand had circled tonight's date had done so generations ago, and the book itself had accepted the writing, healed around it, made it flesh.

Tonight had been written a hundred years before tonight.

And the figure in the picture held a ledger. Felix glanced down at their own satchel, where the Gilded Thorn's account-book sat against their hip, worn smooth by nine years of daily carrying. Hooded figure, ledger, grove of weeping trees. There were perhaps four people in the whole city who carried a ledger everywhere, and only one of them was standing in a basement at midnight cataloguing a book grown from wood.

The vertigo arrived quietly, the way floodwater arrives in a cellar—you don't see it coming, you just notice one day that everything you're standing on has shifted. Felix had built their entire self on a simple architecture: *I am nobody in particular. I keep records. Things happen to other people; I write them down.* Nine years of deliberate, comfortable, unremarkable invisibility. And now some tree a century dead—or worse, a century *alive*—had reached across the years, tapped Felix on the shoulder, and said *you*.

Felix sat down on the workbench stool, which was sensible, because their knees had opinions of their own.

"All right," they said to the book. "Let's be professionals about this."

They were not professionals about this. They turned the pages fast now, hunting, the lamp guttering as their sleeve stirred the air. More entries. More *I saw*s. One page bore a pressed flower that was somehow still green. Another showed a map of the city with one street inked over so many times it had torn. And then, near the end—near the *end*, where the newest growth was, where the pages were palest and thinnest, like the skin of a sapling—Felix found an entry in a hand they almost recognized.

Almost. That was the horror of it. The handwriting looped and slanted in a way that snagged on something deep in Felix's memory, a hook catching in cloth, and Felix stared at it for a long time trying to place where they'd seen that hand before—in a contract? a letter? their *own* old correspondence?—and could not, and the not-knowing rang in them like a struck bell.

The entry was short. It read:

*I saw the keeper come. Not yet. But soon. The Wood remembers what the keeper forgot, and on the night the moon turns her thirteenth turn, forgetting will no longer protect them. May Vess forgive us for what we took to keep them safe.*

Below it, one of the twelve marks—but this mark had been crossed out, deliberately, by a different hand, and beside the crossing-out someone had written a single word in Vessic:

*Soon.*

The lamp flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft.

Felix became aware, gradually, of three facts arranged around them like the points of a triangle. First: a book grown from living wood had recorded tonight's date a century in advance, alongside a portrait of Felix themselves. Second: the book spoke of a "keeper" who had forgotten something—something taken *to keep them safe*—and Felix's own past before the Thorn was a pleasant blur they had never much examined, because examining it had always felt oddly like pressing on a bruise. Third: the moon was full tonight, high and white above the archive window, and Felix did not know—had never thought to know—how many turns it had made this season.

Somewhere upstairs, the great house of the Gilded Thorn creaked and settled. Somewhere beyond it, the city slept around its secrets. And somewhere in the dark of the archive, in a crate of straw that had crossed half a kingdom to arrive precisely here, precisely now, a book made of tree-flesh waited with the patience of forests for Felix to do something foolish.

Felix looked at the entry again. *May Vess forgive us for what we took.*

Thirteen years of nightly ritual. Thirteen years of candle, coin, and silence. Felix had always assumed the silence was simply what gods were—that Vess heard and did not answer, the way mountains hear. But what if the silence wasn't distance? What if it was *removal*? What if the god's voice had been taken the way the keeper's memory had been taken, by the same hands, for the same purpose?

The thought was too large to hold. It slipped through Felix's fingers like the escaped words of the jar upstairs, drifting toward the rafters where all unspoken things gathered.

What Felix did next was, by any professional standard, foolish. They did not close the book. They did not lock it in the restricted case. Instead they fetched their satchel, wrapped the Weeping Wood—it had named itself in their mind already, though no page bore that title—in the oilcloth it came in, and tucked it beneath their arm, where it settled with a warmth that was definitely, certainly, absolutely just residual heat from the lamp.

Tomorrow, Corvane. Tomorrow, questions. Tonight, Felix Fairweather—who had spent nine years making sure nothing remarkable ever attached itself to their name—climbed the stairs into the moonlight carrying the single most impossible object in the kingdom, humming, very quietly, the four words of an oath to a god who had not answered in a hundred nights.

Behind them, on the workbench, the inventory ledger lay open to item four hundred twelve.

Someone—no one; the archive was empty—would notice later that under Felix's neat entry, in handwriting that matched no one's, a single additional line had appeared in ink still wet:

*Received: the witness. Condition: forgotten.*
