## 4. The Guild Takes Notice

Felix Fairweather had been summoned to the Guildmaster's office exactly four times in seven years of service, and each summons had arrived in a different flavor of dread.

The first had been to praise their arithmetic — a memory Felix kept close, the way some people kept a lover's letter. *Your columns balance, Fairweather. In this house, that is a form of love.* The second had concerned a client who'd tried to pay for borrowed years with counterfeit ones, and Corvane had wanted Felix to know, quietly, that the man now lived three fewer decades than he'd planned. The third was about Odile, and the less said of it, the better.

The fourth came at dawn, delivered by a boy who wouldn't meet Felix's eyes, on paper so thin it was nearly a rumor: *The Guildmaster will see you now.*

"Now," before the kettle had even finished muttering. That was new. That was wrong.

Felix dressed with the particular care of someone who suspected they were walking into an audit of their own. They checked that the scrap of birch-bark — torn from the Weeping Wood's edge without quite meaning to, the way one's hand moves before one's mind agrees — was still sewn into their coat lining. It was. It felt warmer than bark had any right to feel, and heavier, as though it were slowly remembering being part of something alive.

The Gilded Thorn at dawn was Felix's favorite version of it: the long counting-house empty except for the smell of yesterday's candle smoke and tomorrow's ink; the great ledgers shelved like patient soldiers; morning light falling through windows stained with the guild's sigil, a rose made of coins, so that the floors bloomed gold. A family, Corvane called them once a year at midwinter feast, and the rest of the time he called them assets, which Felix had always understood to be the same thing said honestly.

Corvane's office sat above the counting-house, behind a door of black oak carved with thorns that seemed — if you looked too long, which Felix tried not to — to have grown rather than been carved.

"Sit," said the Guildmaster, without looking up.

He was not a large man, but rooms arranged themselves around him anyway. He had the kind of face that would have been handsome in a painting of a tax collector: precise, gray at the temples, eyes like two well-kept accounts. He wore his rings the way other men wore scars — one for every decade of the Thorn, or so rumor held, and there were many.

Felix sat. Said nothing. In seven years they had learned that silence, deployed correctly, was the loudest thing in any room.

Corvane let them have ten full seconds of it. Then he set down his pen, aligned it parallel to the desk's edge with a small, terrible neatness, and looked up.

"You found the Wood."

Not a question. Felix's stomach performed a maneuver usually reserved for falling off horses.

"I catalogued a seizure from the Vellum House," Felix said carefully. "Among the items was—"

"The Weeping Wood, yes. Book grown from living tree-flesh, sap for ink, pages that bleed when cut. Do you know how I know what it looks like, Keeper?"

*Keeper.* He only used the title when he wanted Felix to remember exactly what they were to him. A keeper keeps things. Keeps them safe. Keeps them where they're put.

"No, Guildmaster."

"Because I burned the last one." Corvane folded his hands. "Forty-one years ago. It took three days to die and it screamed the whole time, and I have not slept entirely well since, though I've learned to live with it, the way one lives with a bad knee. So you will understand my interest when a seized archive surfaces containing another."

Felix's mouth was dry. "It screamed?"

"They all do." He said it the way he said *the quarterly taxes are due.* "Here is what happens next, Fairweather, and I want you to attend closely, because I am going to say it only once, and because I am fond of you, which should make this easier and doesn't. The Wood does not go back to the vault. It does not go into your ledger. You will tell me precisely where it is, and then you will forget its catalogue number, forget its shelf, and forget that tonight's date was written in it a hundred years before you were born."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere below, the counting-house clock cleared its throat.

"You knew," Felix said. Not *you knew about the Wood.* Just: *you knew.* Because the vertigo wasn't really about the tree-book anymore. It was about the shape of the sentence Corvane had just built — *forget* — aimed at the one person in the Thorn whose entire soul was built out of remembering.

"I knew before your parents knew each other existed," Corvane said mildly. "The Thorn has known about the Weeping Wood for longer than the Thorn has had this name. It is the oldest account we hold, Felix, and it is the one entry in our books that must never be balanced. Some debts are safer unpaid. Some questions are safer unasked. This is both."

"And the date?" Felix heard their own voice go flat and careful, the voice they used with clients who lied. "Tonight's date. Circled. With a hooded figure holding a ledger beside it. A century old, Guildmaster. Either the trees can see the future or somebody carved a very long con, and I have audited enough cons to know which explanation costs more."

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes — not anger, worse, recognition. The look of a man watching an account begin, against all instruction, to balance itself.

"That," he said softly, "is why you are going to stop reading it."

*

Felix walked out of the office with their spine straight and their thoughts in open riot.

They made it as far as the stairwell before Odile materialized beside them, which was her gift; she moved through the Thorn like smoke finding drafts. Small, quick, dark-haired, with ink permanently staining the sides of her right hand and a smile she used the way other people used knives — often, and with precision.

"So," she said. "You touched it."

"How did you—"

"You have the look. Everyone gets the look." She steered him by the elbow toward the records room, talking low and fast. "The look of a person who has just found out the floor of their life is a lid. Come on. Not here. Walls in this building have better hearing than I do, and I hear everything."

The records room smelled of dust and vellum and the lavender Odile sprinkled against silverfish. She shut the door, wedged a chair under the handle — theatrical, but it made her feel better, and Felix loved her enough to let it.

"Tell me what you saw," she said.

Felix hesitated. Then told her: the archive, the crate from the Vellum House seizure, the book that was not a book. Bark pages. Sap ink. And the date — last night's date, inscribed a hundred years past, circled twice, beside a small carved figure in a hood, holding a rectangular thing that could have been a ledger, could have been anything, but wasn't anything.

Odile listened without blinking. When Felix finished she sat down on a stool of disallowed inventories and pressed both hands flat over her face for a moment, and Felix saw — truly saw, for the first time in years — that she was frightened. Not annoyed-frightened, not caught-frightened. Frightened the way people are frightened of deep water.

"Others have read it," she said through her fingers. "Haven't they."

"Two that I know of." She dropped her hands. "Maren Voss, senior appraiser, before your time. Brilliant woman. Terrible singing voice. She read the Wood for one night — one, Felix, she swore it to me herself — and the next morning she asked the Guildmaster for leave to visit her mother in Harrowfen. She had no mother in Harrowfen. She had no mother anywhere. She walked out the gate and nobody has seen her since, and the Guildmaster marked her ledger *account closed, settled in full,* and when I asked what that meant he smiled at me with all his teeth and changed the subject to shipping tariffs."

"And the second?"

"A courier named Pell." Odile's jaw tightened. "Pell read it and didn't vanish. Pell got better, everyone said. Cheerful again, sleeping well. And then one morning Pell couldn't remember the oath-words. Stood at the shrine with his hands out like a man asking for alms, mouthing nothing. A mercenary of the Thorn who cannot swear to Vess is a sword with no edge and no handle, so they pensioned him off to a farmhouse upcountry." She paused. "He sends letters sometimes. They're just lists. Names of things in his kitchen. Spoon. Cup. Door. Like a man proving to himself the world still has edges."

The lavender did nothing against the cold that had settled into the room.

"Odile." Felix kept their voice level. "Why does the Guildmaster keep a thing that eats people?"

"Because keeping it is the only way to control who it eats," she said simply. "That's the whole guild philosophy, isn't it? Don't destroy dangerous things — own them. Put them in ledgers. Give them catalogue numbers." She stood, crossed to him, and took Felix's hands in hers; hers were warm and ink-stained and shaking slightly, which frightened Felix more than anything Corvane had said upstairs. "Listen to me. I have known you seven years and you are the most stubborn honest person I have ever met, and that combination is exactly the kind the Wood likes. It showed you tonight's date. It *circled* it. Books don't do that, Felix. Trees don't do that. Something in that grove of paper and blood is waiting for you specifically, and whatever it wants, it is not good, because nothing that waits a hundred years wants anything good."

"Or," Felix said, and hated themselves for saying it, "something in that wood has been waiting a hundred years for anyone at all, and I'm merely the first fool to open the cover."

"Felix—"

"Vess has been silent for a hundred nights, Odie." It came out quieter than Felix intended, almost embarrassed. "A hundred nights of nothing. And now a tree-book writes my today down before I lived it, and my Guildmaster tells me to forget, and Maren Voss walks to a mother who never existed. I'm not stubborn. I'm *scared.* And scared people don't stop reading. Scared people turn the page, because the alternative is sitting alone in the dark wondering what was on it."

Odile held his gaze for a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had gone soft in a way Felix had heard maybe three times ever, always at funerals.

"If you go back to it," she said, "and something happens to you — the vanishing kind of something — I need you to know that I'll come find you. Whatever it costs. Wherever you walk to. Harrowfen or farther." Her grip tightened. "But I'm asking you, as the closest thing either of us has to family in this gilded den of thieves: don't make me."

Felix looked at their oldest friend and lied to her with their whole face.

"I won't," they said.

*

That night the Thorn slept badly, or Felix did, which amounted to the same architecture of creaking beams and restless wind.

They lay in their narrow room above the counting-house and turned the day over like a suspicious coin. Corvane's forty-one years. Maren Voss's fictional mother. Pell and his lists of spoons. *Account closed, settled in full.* The phrase kept snagging, kept catching light the way a buried wire catches sun.

Because here was the thing Felix knew about closed accounts, having closed hundreds: you didn't settle a debt by making the debtor disappear. You settled it by paying. And here was the other thing Felix knew, having audited this guild's stranger books for seven years: the Thorn did not destroy valuable inventory. The Thorn *warehoused* valuable inventory. Bottled grief, row after row. Borrowed years, racked and labeled. If Corvane had truly burned a screaming tree-book four decades ago, then somewhere between then and now he had learned something that made him change his mind about burning this one.

Which meant the Wood wasn't being destroyed.

Which meant it was being *kept.*

Felix lay in the dark and thought: *a family.* Corvane's word, once a year, over roast goose and watered wine. And Felix had taken it, hungry as they were, the way a stray takes a saucer — gratefully, without asking what was in the milk. Seven years of balancing impossible accounts for these people. Seven years of swearing to a silent god alongside them, side by side in the shrine-dark, and not one of them — not Odile until tonight, not anyone — had thought to mention that the guild owned a prophecy with Felix's own tonight circled in it like a target.

Betrayal, it turned out, didn't arrive shouting. It arrived in the voice of a mild man aligning his pen with his desk, telling you what to forget.

Felix sat up.

Below, through the floorboards, the counting-house clock struck two. And beneath the clock, fainter, from the direction of the sealed archive vault — a sound Felix first mistook for wind in the chimneys, until they remembered that the archive had no chimney.

A sound like trees. Creaking. Growing.

And underneath the creaking, so soft it might have been imagination, might have been the ghost of a hundred nights of unanswered prayer:

*Felix.*

They were moving before they had decided to move — coat, boots, the birch-bark warm in its hiding seam, the dark staircase taking their weight without complaint, because Felix had walked it ten thousand times and knew exactly which steps betrayed a person. At the ground-floor window, while pulling on gloves, they glanced out of pure habit across the moonlit courtyard —

and stopped.

A carriage stood at the Thorn's gate. Unmarked. Black. Its lamps burned a white that was not lamplight, and beside it, checking a document against the gatehouse by the glow of a hand-lantern, stood a woman in the dove-gray coat of the Crown Inquisition, her warrant-case open under her arm like a second heart.

Even at this distance, even at two in the morning, Felix could see that she was not going to be lied to easily.

And Felix — keeper of ledgers, servant of a silent god, newest entry in a tree's hundred-year plan — discovered that they were already rehearsing the lie.
