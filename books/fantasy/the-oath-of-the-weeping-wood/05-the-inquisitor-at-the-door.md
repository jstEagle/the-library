## 5. The Inquisitor at the Door

The knock came at the worst possible hour—half past the ninth bell, when honest people had gone to bed and dishonest ones were only just warming up.

Felix was in the counting room with a pot of cold tea and the Thorn's quarterly accounts, which was to say: alone, comfortable, and quietly miserable. The ledger open before them concerned a debt owed by a miller in Lowmarsh who had paid his interest for eleven years in the shape of shadows. Not metaphorically. The man's shadow came down off the wall every new moon, folded itself small as a moth, and sat in the strongroom until called upon. It was one of the tamer entries. Felix had grown fond of it, in the way one grows fond of a spider that lives in a corner of the ceiling—you never speak to it, but you'd notice if it left.

The knock came again. Three strikes, evenly spaced, with the patience of someone who knew the door would be answered eventually because refusing to answer a royal knock was itself an answer.

"Keeper," Odile hissed from the doorway of the back stair, still half-dressed, her grey braid loose over one shoulder. "There's a carriage in the yard. Crown black. Two outriders and—" she squinted through the shutter gap "—a woman with a warrant box chained to her wrist."

Felix set down their pen with the deliberate calm they had practiced for years and did not actually feel.

"Inquisitors don't call at half past nine," they said.

"No," Odile agreed. "They call at dawn, when you're asleep, or at midnight, when you're guilty. Half past nine means she wants us *comfortable*. Comfortable people answer questions badly." She paused at the top of the stairs. "Felix. The archive."

"I know."

"The Wood is in there."

"I *know*, Odile."

She looked at them for a long moment, and something passed across her face that Felix would spend weeks afterward trying to name—not fear exactly, but the look of a person watching someone step onto ice they cannot see the far edge of.

"I'll wake Corvane," she said, and went.

* * *

Inquisitor Serah Vale was not what Felix expected, which was, Felix suspected, entirely the point.

He had braced for the Inquisitorial type he'd met before on contract work: tall men with voices like closing doors, who enjoyed silence the way other people enjoyed wine. Vale was none of that. She was small, sharp-faced, perhaps forty, with ink stains on two fingers and the weary posture of someone who had spent the day riding and would spend the night reading. She stood in the Gilded Thorn's entrance hall—the one Corvane had furnished with deliberately shabby grandeur, all worn velvet and honest-looking dust—and she looked around it the way an accountant looks at another accountant's books. Not hostile. Professional.

"Felix Fairweather," she said. It wasn't a question. "Keeper of Records for the Gilded Thorn."

"You have me," said Felix, and immediately wished they hadn't, because it sounded like surrender and also like an innkeeper's greeting.

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. "I'm told you have everything here. Bottled grief. Borrowed years. A shadow or two." She lifted her wrist slightly; the warrant box swung on its chain. "By order of the Crown, under the Statute of Unlicensed Futures, I am empowered to inspect and seize any text purporting to foretell events not yet occurred. Prophecy-texts, almanacs of divination, dream-ledgers, oracle fragments, and"—she consulted a paper without appearing to need to—"books of living wood. That last category was added recently. Within the month, in fact."

Felix felt the floor tilt very slightly, the way it does on a ship at anchor.

"A book of living wood," they repeated.

"The statute is admirably specific this time. Someone high up received a tip." Her eyes came up and settled on Felix with the flat patience of a cat watching a mouse decide between two exits. "May I see your catalogue?"

Here is the thing about Felix Fairweather, and about the oath they had sworn to Vess thirteen years ago on a battlefield they could not remember: the oath bound them to truth. Not honesty—that was a mortal virtue, negotiable, situational—but *truth*. When asked a question by one who held authority over them, they spoke what was true, or they kept silent, and the god's quiet weight sat behind the words like a hand on the shoulder. Thirteen years of it. Thirteen years of answering magistrates and guild arbitrators and once, memorably, a lovesick duke, with nothing but the plain unvarnished facts. Mercenaries lie for a living; Felix Fairweather had built a reputation as the mercenary who couldn't. It had made them trusted, which is its own kind of armor.

It had also, they realized now, standing in the entrance hall while Odile fetched the catalogue with suspicious promptness, made them spectacularly unpracticed.

"Your catalogue," Vale prompted gently.

"It's being fetched," said Felix. True. "Our holdings are extensive." Also true. "You'll find no prophecy among them." This was the moment. Felix felt it arrive the way one feels a step miss a stair.

Because it was true—technically, beautifully true. The Weeping Wood was not a prophecy-*text* in any sense the statute meant. It was older than the statute, older than the Crown that wrote it, older possibly than the language the statute was written in. It did not *purport* to foretell anything. It simply recorded, in tree-flesh and sap-script, things that had already been written a century ago—including tonight, including this hour, including, Felix strongly suspected, this exact conversation. To seize the Wood under a warrant for prophecy-texts was like arresting a mountain for jaywalking.

Vale waited. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked under Odile's hurrying feet.

"We keep accounts," Felix said. "Debts, payments, interest. Things owed and things paid. Nothing about the future. The future isn't collateral—it doesn't hold value until it happens."

"Hm," said Vale, in the tone of an examiner who has heard a technically correct answer from a student she suspects of knowing more.

And then it happened. It happened so smoothly that Felix almost missed it: the words went out into the hall air and nothing stopped them. No weight. No hand on the shoulder. No pressure behind the breastbone where the oath lived, that faint constant hum of Vess's attention that Felix had complained about privately for a decade and would have given anything, in that moment, to feel again.

Nothing. The lie—or near enough to a lie to count, near enough that old Brother Cassian would have boxed their ears—slipped out clean as a coin from a cut purse.

Felix's heart did something complicated. Part of it soared: *it worked, the silence freed me, I can protect the Wood, I can protect the guild.* And part of it dropped like a stone down a well, because the absence of the god's weight was not freedom. It was a door standing open in a house where every other door had always been shut, and the draft coming through it smelled like abandonment.

Odile returned with the master catalogue, and the inspection began.

* * *

It took four hours.

Vale was thorough in the manner of people who have been made to suffer for being insufficiently thorough early in their careers. She worked through the public ledgers page by page, cross-referencing against her warrant's schedule, asking questions in a voice that never rose above conversational. Felix sat across from her and answered, and discovered, with each passing hour, a strange and terrible fluency.

"The entry marked 'Grief, jarred, vintage unspecified'—provenance?"

"Bereaved widow, eastern provinces. She sold it to stop weeping at her son's grave. We resold it to a playwright. Everyone got what they paid for." True.

"This debt of 'one remembered afternoon, repayable in kind.'"

"A confectioner's. He borrowed the memory of a festival to survive a famine year. Repayment schedules are in the annex." True.

"And your guildmaster? Where might I find Master Corvane at this hour?"

"Asleep, Inquisitor. He keeps late hours and later mornings. I can wake him if the warrant requires his presence personally." True, true, and—Felix checked carefully—true.

Each answer landed clean. Each one sailed out into the room unopposed, and the exhilaration of it was worse than guilt, honestly. It felt like discovering a talent. Felix, who had spent thirteen years being the straightest arrow in a crooked quiver, found they had a gift for the bend. They could hear the truth-shaped space inside a sentence and pour their meaning right up to the edge of it without spilling. It was craftsmanship. It was, they thought with a giddy lurch, *accounting*—every lie a balanced entry, every omission a column carried forward.

Somewhere beneath the exhilaration, quieter, the guilt sat doing its own arithmetic. *Thirteen years,* it said. *One evening.* But guilt, Felix reflected, was a creditor too, and it kept excellent books.

At some past-midnight hour, Vale closed the last ledger and pressed both palms flat on the table.

"Well," she said. "Your records are immaculate. Genuinely. In fifteen years I haven't seen accounts this clean outside the Royal Mint, and the Mint cheats." She stood, rolled her shoulders with a rider's stiffness, and looked at Felix for a long moment. "You know what interests me, Keeper Fairweather?"

"The Mint cheating?"

A breath escaped her that might, in a less disciplined woman, have been a laugh. "The timing. The statute amendment specifying living wood came down from the capital eleven days ago. Eleven days ago, Keeper, a courier arrived at this guildhouse carrying crates seized from a smuggler's cache in the Salt Marshes. Crates logged into *your* archive. I know because I read the toll records, and I always read the toll records." She picked up her warrant box. "So I'll ask you plainly, once, and you may answer or not as your conscience and your contracts allow: is there anything in that archive I ought to have seen tonight?"

And there it was. Not a trap—a door, held open, courteously. An offer to let him answer honestly, if he chose.

Felix thought of the Wood in the archive room, wrapped now in oilcloth and tucked inside the great folio of *Outstanding Debts, Secured Against Persons Unknown*—Odile's suggestion, delivered in a fierce whisper an hour ago, and brilliant, because no Inquisitor alive would voluntarily open a six-hundred-page ledger of debts secured against persons unknown, on the sound principle that such ledgers bite. They thought of the hooded figure in the sap-script, holding a ledger just like theirs. They thought of the hundred nights of silence, and how the silence had suddenly, tonight, become useful.

"There is nothing in my archive," Felix said slowly, "that answers to the description in your warrant."

Every word true. Every word a stone laid neatly on the path leading away from the truth.

Vale studied them. Whatever she saw, she filed it—Felix could see her filing it, behind those tired, clever eyes—in a drawer marked *later*.

"Then I'll bid the Thorn goodnight," she said. At the door she paused, one hand on the frame, and added, without turning around: "For what it's worth, Keeper, I don't think you're a liar. I've watched liars all week. Liars fidget." She tapped the doorframe twice, a strange little habit, like knocking on wood. "You didn't fidget once. Goodnight."

The carriage rolled out of the yard. The outriders' hoofbeats faded toward the city gate. Odile emerged from the shadows of the stairwell looking like a woman who had aged a season in an evening.

"She bought it?"

"She bought the ledger," Felix said. "Whether she bought me—" They rubbed their face with both hands. "She counted my fidgets, Odile. Who *counts fidgets*?"

"An Inquisitor who'll be back," Odile said grimly. "With a second warrant, and this time she'll bring readers who enjoy six-hundred-page ledgers of persons unknown. You've bought days, Felix. Maybe a week."

*A week.* Felix nodded and found their legs were carrying them, unbidden, down the corridor toward the archive, where the great folio lay closed on the table with the Wood hidden in its belly like a heart in a chest.

They should go to bed. They should report to Corvane, who would want—no, who would *demand*—a full account, and whom Felix would now have to deceive a second time, and a third, and every day after, because Corvane forbade the reading and yet the reading had already begun, because the date circled in the sap-script was *tonight*, and tomorrow it would be tomorrow's date, and the Wood was not going to wait politely for the Crown's paperwork.

They should go to bed. Instead Felix stood before the folio with their hand flat on its cover, feeling their own pulse tick against the oilcloth, and admitted the truth to the empty room, where at least it cost nothing:

The lying hadn't frightened them. That was the frightening part. It had felt like coming home to a house they didn't remember owning.

And beneath their palm, through oilcloth and vellum and six centuries of tree-flesh, something in the Weeping Wood gave the faintest shudder—like a sleeper turning toward a familiar voice—as though it had heard them say it.
