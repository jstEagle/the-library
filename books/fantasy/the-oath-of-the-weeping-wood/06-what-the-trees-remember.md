## 6. What the Trees Remember

Sleep, it turned out, was a liar's country, and Felix Fairweather had never learned its language.

They lay in their narrow bed above the counting-house and listened to the guild settle around them like an old dog turning in circles. The pipes knocked. Somewhere below, the vault of impossible things hummed its low, patient hum—the sound, Odile had once said, of a building digesting. Felix had laughed at that. Tonight they did not laugh.

Tonight's date was circled in a book grown from living wood, and Guildmaster Corvane had said *no* in that soft voice he used for saying things that were not requests.

*You will not read further.*

Felix had been many kinds of fool in their life—a mercenary's life offered a generous curriculum—but they had never yet been the kind of fool who obeyed.

* * *

The archive occupied the Thorn's lowest floor, below even the vault, in a space the guild's founders had carved out of the city's oldest foundations. The stairs down were narrow and smelled of cold stone and older paper. Felix went barefoot, boots in hand, because boots announced themselves on these steps the way a town crier announces an execution, and because there was something about descending toward a secret that felt righter with skin against stone.

They carried one candle stub, unlit. They didn't need light. That was the thing about ledgers—about records of any kind—that nobody who wasn't a keeper ever quite understood: you did not read them with your eyes first. You read them with your hands. Paper had weight, texture, temperature. A page that had been turned often felt worn at the corner like a dog-eared ear. A book that had been hidden carried a certain tension in its binding, the way a person carries tension in their shoulders.

The Weeping Wood sat where Corvane had ordered it shelved: third row, second alcove, wedged between a tax codex so dull it might have been a weapon and a merchant's diary from a harbor town that no longer existed. Deliberately dull company. The kind of shelf where a book could be forgotten on purpose.

Felix lifted it down and felt the difference immediately.

Every other object in the archive was dead in the way records are dead—preserved, embalmed, useful. This was not. It was warm. Not feverish-warm; blood-warm. The warmth of something asleep rather than something ended. Its cover was bark, fine-grained and pale as birch but smoother, almost like skin that had never seen weather. Along its spine ran what Felix had taken, in the lamplight of the cataloguing room, for grain or scarring. Up close, in the dark, with fingertips instead of eyes, they understood.

Pores. The spine had pores.

*It grew,* Felix thought, and their stomach performed a slow, sick revolution. *It is still growing.*

Corvane had forbidden them to read further. Very well. Felix was a keeper of accounts, and there was more than one way to keep an account. You did not always need to open the book. Sometimes you needed only to hold it, the way you held a coin to know if it was clipped, the way you held a man's wrist to know if his story about being healthy was true.

They sat down on the cold floor with the Wood in their lap, took a breath, and laid both palms flat against the covers.

The tree-flesh pulsed once beneath their hands—startled, Felix thought later, like a cat touched by a stranger—and then the world came apart along its seams.

* * *

Rain. Rain falling sideways, the way rain falls when the wind has decided the battle's outcome before the soldiers have. Mud the color of old bruises sucking at boots. And banners—dozens of them, snapping wet and ragged, bearing sigils Felix knew from history books that predated history books: the Ninefold Crown, the Choir of Iron, the sunburst of a god whose name had been scraped off every monument and replaced with a chisel-smooth blank.

Felix stood in the rain and was not standing in the archive at all.

A horn sounded, long and low, the note bending at the end as though the horn itself had doubts. Across the churned field, something enormous moved—not an army, not exactly. An *absence*. A shape made of the place where light should be, and men were running from it, and men were running into it, and neither group came back the same.

"Left flank!" someone screamed, a voice raw as a crow. "Vess's own, to the left flank! *Move!*"

And Felix—Felix's body, which had not asked permission, which apparently remembered things Felix's mind did not—turned left.

They saw themselves from behind.

That was the detail that would stay. Not the battlefield's horror, though there was horror enough to fill a hundred ledgers of impossible things. It was this: a figure ahead of them in the mud and rain, hooded, moving with a particular gait that Felix knew intimately because it was the gait they saw reflected in shop windows every day—the slight favoring of the left knee, the habit of leading with the shoulder, the way of walking that said *I am here to do a job and then I am going to go home.* Their own walk. Their own hooded shoulders. Their own ledger strapped across the back, because even in memory, even a century ago, apparently Felix Fairweather had been carrying the books.

The figure turned, just briefly, shouting something to a soldier whose arm ended above the elbow, and for half a heartbeat the rain-lit face beneath the hood was visible.

It was Felix's face.

Not similar. Not a family resemblance, not a trick of shadow and wishful thinking. It was their face, exact, down to the small scar through the left eyebrow that Felix had earned at nineteen falling off a warehouse roof in Vellisport while fleeing a man they'd cheated at cards. Unaged. Unchanged. A century old and wearing yesterday's expression of grim concentration.

The memory shattered like dropped glass.

* * *

Felix came back to themselves on the archive floor with their cheek against cold stone and their heart going like a debt collector's fist on a door. The candle stub had rolled away somewhere. Their hands were still on the Wood's covers—they had fallen backward without letting go, dragged down by the book like a drowning man clutching a spar—and the tree-flesh under their palms beat now with a slow, unmistakable rhythm.

Like a heartbeat. Like something that had been holding its breath for a hundred years and had, just now, exhaled.

"No," Felix said aloud, to the dark, to the book, to themselves. "No, that isn't—I'm thirty-one years old. I was born in—" 

And here their mind did the thing minds do when they meet a fact too large to swallow: it reached for the supporting details and found the shelf empty.

They had been born in Vellisport. They were sure of that. They could see the harbor, the gulls, the smell of tar and frying fish. They could see their mother's kitchen—could they? There was a kitchen, certainly, warm and yellow-lit, and someone in it, someone whose back was turned, stirring a pot. But when Felix reached for the face, the memory slid like wet rope through their fingers. When they reached for the mother's name—

Nothing.

When they reached for their nineteenth year, the year of the warehouse roof and the card cheat and the scar—there was a scar, yes, proof written into their own flesh—but the year around it was fog. Smooth, seamless fog, the fog of a story told well enough to pass inspection but never actually lived.

Keepers noticed things. It was the whole trade. And now, lying on a freezing floor with a heartbeat under their hands, Felix noticed the largest thing they had ever noticed: their own past had the texture of an entry audited after the fact. Accurate in its totals. Hollow in its margins.

"The oath," they whispered.

Their mercenary powers came from Vess. Every mercenary sworn to the silent god drew strength from the covenant—luck that bent at crucial moments, wounds that closed a day early, the uncanny steadiness of the hand. In exchange: service, and silence kept about the god's affairs. One hundred nights ago, Vess had stopped answering. One hundred nights ago, the luck had begun to thin.

But what if that wasn't the beginning? What if the beginning was a hundred *years* ago, on a rainy field, when a hooded keeper with Felix's face had stood in the mud beside a god's army—and something had happened, something so terrible that someone had decided the only mercy was to make Felix forget it entirely?

*Someone cut my past out of me,* Felix thought, and the thought arrived not with horror but with a strange, professional calm, the calm of a clerk finding a falsified page. *Someone edited my ledger. And they were good. They were very, very good.*

Which raised the obvious question, the question that made the calm crack down the middle:

What had been worth cutting?

* * *

Felix did not remember climbing the stairs. They found themselves in the counting-house with their boots on the wrong feet and dawn graying the windows, and Odile sitting at the big table with a pot of coffee and the expression of a woman who had been waiting up on purpose.

"You look," she said, "like something the tide refused."

"I went downstairs."

Odile set down her cup with exaggerated care. "Downstairs-downstairs."

"Third row. Second alcove." Felix lowered themselves into a chair. Their hands, they noted distantly, were shaking. "I held it. I didn't even open it, Odile. I put my hands on the cover and it—it showed me a battlefield. A century ago. The war. The real one, the one they scraped off the monuments."

"Felix." Odile's voice had gone flat and careful, the voice she used with clients who were about to say something expensive. "People who touch that book don't come back chatty. They come back wrong, and then they come back gone. Corvane told you—"

"Corvane lied to me." It came out quieter than Felix intended, and somehow that was worse. "He's known. He's *known*, Odile, and he shelved it between a tax codex and a dead man's diary like it was contraband snuff. And I need to tell you what I saw, because you're the only person in this building whose face I can still read."

Odile poured a second cup of coffee and pushed it across the table. Her hand was steady. Her eyes were not. "Tell me."

"I saw myself." Felix wrapped both hands around the cup because the cup was real, the cup was *now*, the cup had a verifiable provenance. "In the mud, in the rain, a hundred years ago. My face. My knee. My ledger on my back. Unaged, Odile. Not a descendant. Not a lookalike. Me."

Silence. The pipes knocked overhead. Somewhere above them, the guild was waking—the apprentices' door, the kettle in the kitchen, Tam whistling three notes of a song and forgetting the rest, the small ordinary machinery of the family Felix had built their life inside. It all sounded suddenly fragile, like music heard through a wall in a house that might be on fire.

"That's not possible," Odile said at last.

"No," Felix agreed. "And yet I've got a scar through my eyebrow and no memory of a mother's name, and until tonight I called that a normal childhood." They looked up. "You've been in this guild eleven years. You're the closest thing I have to a witness of my own life. So I'm asking you, keeper to keeper, and I want you to understand how much it costs me to ask: what do you remember about where I came from?"

Odile stared into her coffee for a long time.

And Felix—who had spent the night learning to read the tensions in hidden things—watched her shoulders carry a weight they had never carried before, watched her arrive at some internal crossroads and stand at it, unmoving, for the space of four full breaths.

"There's something," she said finally, "that I've owed you for six years. And I think you're about to ask the question that makes it due."

The coffee had gone cold in Felix's hands. Outside, the dawn was coming up over the Gilded Thorn like an auditor over a suspicious account, patient and pitiless, and in the cellar below them a book made of living wood beat its slow heart against the shelves, waiting.

"What," Felix said, "did you sell?"
