## 7. Odile's Bargain

Odile Vasse had robbed a cardinal, swindled a dragon-cult out of its founding endowment, and once talked a border garrison into surrendering a fortress they did not technically have to surrender. She had done all of this without losing a minute of sleep.

So it was insulting, really, that the thing keeping her awake now was a confession she hadn't even made yet.

She stood outside Felix's door in the Gilded Thorn's residence hall for the third night running, holding two cups of spiced wine as a bribe against her own cowardice. The hallway smelled of tallow and old paper, because everything in the Thorn smelled of tallow and old paper; Corvane said it was cheaper than incense and twice as flammable in an emergency, which Odile had never decided was a joke.

Through the door came the small, familiar sounds of Felix at work: the scratch of a nib, the soft thump of a ledger closing, the occasional sigh of a person who found arithmetic more comforting than conversation. Odile had known Felix Fairweather for six years. She knew how they took their tea (bitterly), how they slept (badly, upright, like a horse), and how they laughed (rarely, suddenly, and always looking surprised about it afterward).

What Odile did not know—what she had spent six years carefully not knowing—was whether Felix would ever be able to look at her again after tonight.

*You could still walk away,* said the sensible part of her, the part that had kept her alive through three guild wars and one unfortunate marriage. *The Eye already knows their face. Telling them changes nothing.*

But that was the trouble with selling pieces of yourself to buy back other pieces: you always ended up owing someone, and the interest compounded nightly, and lately the interest had a face—a tired, ink-stained face that trusted her, gods help them both.

She knocked.

"Enter," said Felix, in the tone of someone who had been expecting either bad news or a delivery and was prepared for both. "If you're here about the salt invoice, I've already told Corvane we are not paying forty crowns for—"

"It's me."

A pause. The scratch of the nib stopped. "I know it's you, Odile. You're the only person in this building who knocks like she's apologizing to the door."

Odile came in before her courage could reconsider. Felix sat at their desk amid the usual topography of their work—stacks of ledgers, a cold lamp, the little brass scale they used to weigh things that had no weight—and looked up at her with those gray eyes that missed nothing and commented on less.

"I brought wine," Odile said, holding up the cups like evidence at her own trial.

"That's suspicious."

"I know."

"You only bring me wine when you want something or when you've done something."

"...I know."

Felix set down the pen. And Odile watched, with the particular horror of a con artist watching her mark get clever, as Felix's gaze went from her face, to the wine, to the way she was standing—which was, she realized too late, exactly like a person guarding a wound.

"Sit down, Odile."

"I'd rather stand."

"I know. Sit anyway."

---

She sat on the edge of the cot because the chair by the desk felt too much like a witness box. For a while neither of them drank. Somewhere below them, the Thorn's night staff were arguing cheerfully about whether a bottled grief had gone off—if grief could go off, the way milk went off—and the ordinary noise of it made what Odile had to say feel even more extraordinary, like trying to confess a murder during a festival.

"When my sister was taken," Odile began, and stopped, because starting at the beginning meant admitting there was a beginning, and she had spent years pretending this story had none. "You remember. Maren. Six years ago."

"The slavers off the Amber Coast." Felix's voice was careful now. Flat. The voice they used for reading accounts aloud. "The ransom was eleven thousand crowns. You raised it in nine days. Everyone said it was a miracle."

"It was not a miracle." Odile turned her cup in her hands. The wine had gone still as a dark mirror. "Miracles don't have receipts. I got the money from three sources. Two of them I've told you about—the Corsair's pension I forged, and the dowry I invented for myself in Vell."

"And the third?"

And there it was. The door she had been standing outside of for six years.

"The Order of the Unblinking Eye," Odile said, "paid very well for memories. Specific ones. Ones belonging to specific people."

The silence that followed was so complete Odile could hear the lamp wick burning.

"They bought mine, mostly. Cheap ones. My mother's face. The name of the village I burned down when I was nineteen—don't ask, it's not interesting. But mine weren't worth enough. Eleven thousand crowns is—" she laughed, and hated the sound of it, "—eleven thousand crowns, Felix. That's not a memory. That's a *person*. A whole history. They wanted something rare."

"Odile." Felix's voice had gone very quiet. Not angry yet. Worse than angry. Careful. "What did you sell them?"

"Yours."

---

Later, Odile would try to explain to herself why she hadn't stopped, why she laid the whole ugly ledger of it open on the cot between them, itemized and totaled, because she was a bookkeeper's daughter if nothing else and shame, properly organized, hurt less.

"They came to me eight months after you joined the Thorn," she said. "An Eye factor, very polite, very soft-spoken. He didn't threaten anything. He just put a number on the table and told me what my sister's fingers were worth per day. Slavers count, Felix. It's the only poetry they know." Her hands were shaking now; she let them. "He wanted your face. Your habits. Where you'd been before the guild. What you called yourself before 'Fairweather.'"

"And you gave it to them."

"I gave them—" Odile closed her eyes. "I sold them a memory of you from the hiring feast. Your first week. You got drunk on Corvane's terrible brandy and told me things you've never told anyone sober. Where you grew up. What happened to your family. What your name was before." She opened her eyes. "They took it out through the eye, the way they do. It doesn't leave a mark on the seller. Only on the... the goods."

For a long moment Felix did not move at all. Odile had seen statues with more life in them. When Felix finally spoke, each word arrived separately, like coins counted out.

"So that's how they know my face. That's how Vale described me to the harbor watch before she ever saw me. That's how the warrant had my old name in it." A breath. "Six years, Odile. Six years I've been walking around thinking I was a ghost no one remembered, and I've been a portrait hanging in their gallery the whole time."

"Yes."

"Why?" And it wasn't cruel, which was the worst part. It was genuine. Felix Fairweather, keeper of impossible accounts, honestly asking *why*, as though the answer might balance somehow. "You loved Maren. I understand loving someone enough to burn the world. I'd have helped you raise the money if you'd asked. You know I would have helped you."

And that—that was the knife she'd been carrying point-first against her own ribs for six years, and now Felix handed it to her politely, hilt-first, like a lady.

"I know," Odile whispered. "That's the whole crime of it, isn't it? I didn't need to betray you. I betrayed you because it was *faster*. Because asking for help meant admitting I couldn't fix it alone, and I'd built my whole life on being the one who fixes things alone, and pride is just fear wearing nice boots." She set down the untouched wine because her hands couldn't be trusted with it. "The Eye's factor told me you'd never know. He promised. And I believed him, because I wanted to believe him, because believing him meant I got to keep you *and* my sister, and Odile Vasse has never in her life met a bargain she didn't think she could beat."

"Did you keep the memory?"

"What?"

"After they took it. Did they give you anything? Some people say the Eye pays in kind—a memory for a memory."

"No." Odile shook her head slowly. "They paid in coin. Cold, clean, spendable coin. That's the horror of the Eye, Felix—they're not monsters. Monsters at least make it dramatic. They're clerks. They shook my hand and gave me a receipt." Her mouth twisted. "I still have it somewhere. In a lockbox. Because apparently I'm the kind of woman who keeps receipts for her own soul."

---

Felix stood then, and walked to the window, and looked out at the rain coming down over the city rooftops, and Odile sat very still and waited to find out what kind of silence she had purchased with her honesty.

When Felix spoke again, they didn't turn around.

"Do you know what I thought, all these years?" Their voice was steady in the way ice is steady. "I thought I was unremarkable. I thought the reason I had no past worth mentioning was that there simply wasn't one. A blank page. And blanks are comfortable, Odile. Blanks don't get hunted." Now they did turn, and their eyes were wet, which Odile had never once seen, not through wounds, not through funerals, not through the affair with the tax assessor. "And this whole time, someone out there owned the missing pages. Someone who smiled at me across the breakfast table every morning and knew exactly where they'd gone."

"Felix—"

"No. Let me finish, because I'm going to say some true things and I'd like to do it before I lose my nerve." Felix crossed the small room and knelt—not dramatically, awkwardly, catching their shin on the cot frame and swearing under their breath, because even fury at Odile Vasse apparently had to share quarters with being human. They knelt in front of her, eye level, and took her shaking hands in their ink-stained ones.

"Maren is alive," Felix said. "Because of what you sold. Whatever else is true, that is true, and I am not going to pretend it isn't."

"She'd hate it. She'd be sick if she knew—"

"Probably. She strikes me as a woman of strong opinions. But she's alive to have them." Felix's grip tightened. "Here are the other true things. I am furious with you. I may be furious with you for years. There will be mornings I look at you across the ledgers and remember that my own history was currency, and it will taste like poison. That's not forgiveness. That's just weather."

Odile nodded, blinking hard. "I'll take the weather."

"But." Felix exhaled, long and unsteady. "But you came here. Third night in a row, by the sound of your pacing—you pace like a cat with a conscience, it's dreadful. You could have run. The Eye is hunting me and you could have quietly packed and been in Vell by Thursday. Instead you brought bribing wine and confessed to the one person whose forgiveness you can't con." A ghost of a smile crossed Felix's face, brief and startled, the way all their smiles were. "That's either love or incompetence, and I've audited your work for six years, Odile. You are many things, but incompetent has never been one of them."

Something in Odile's chest cracked open—painfully, like ice on a river in spring, the kind of breaking that means the water underneath is finally moving.

"I'm sorry," she said, and it came out small and unsophisticated, without any of her famous charm, which was how she knew it was real. "Not for the bargain—I can't undo it—but for letting you believe you were nobody. Every day I watched you be somebody and said nothing."

"I forgive you," Felix said. "Not fully. Not cleanly. But I'm putting it on the books—an open account, to be settled over time, with interest." They squeezed her hands once, hard, and stood, wincing at their shin. "Which brings us to the practical matter."

"Which practical matter?"

"The one you've been circling since you walked in." Felix went to the desk and lifted the false bottom of their document drawer, where the Weeping Wood lay wrapped in oilcloth—tree-flesh pages, grown rather than written, warm as a sleeping bird. "The Eye bought my memories six years ago. Fine. But Odile—the Wood shows a hooded figure with a ledger, dated *tonight*. The Eye's Grandmaster has guarded Vess's prison for a century. Which means somewhere in that order, there's a record of what they bought from you. My name. My face. Possibly..." Felix's jaw tightened. "Possibly *why* a mercenary with no remarkable past was worth eleven thousand crowns."

Odile stared at them. Then at the wrapped Wood. Then at Felix, whose eyes had gone bright with a purpose she'd never seen in them before, and whose hands—she noticed now—were trembling almost as badly as hers.

"You want to steal your memory back from the Unblinking Eye."

"I want to *audit* it," Felix said, with a smile that had far too many teeth for someone so mild. "There's a difference. Stealing is a crime."

"And auditing?"

"Also a crime," Felix admitted, pulling on their coat. "But it's *ours*."

Outside, the rain thickened, and somewhere across the dark city, in a spire where nothing was ever permitted to blink, a clerk filed away a receipt that was about to come due.
