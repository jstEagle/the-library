## 8. The Thirteenth Turn

The Observatory of Saint Halvard's sat at the top of a hill that had once been a mountain, before the masons of three successive centuries had quarried it down to something more cooperative. Felix climbed its spiral stair with the Weeping Wood wrapped in oilcloth beneath their coat, pressed against their ribs like a stolen loaf of bread, which was, in fairness, not far from what it was.

They had sent word to Magister Ilex Threefold two nights ago—a single line, in the old cipher the Thorn used for debts too delicate for ink: *I need to know about the moon.*

Ilex had answered with a single line of his own: *Come at dusk. Bring nothing you love.*

Felix had considered this advice carefully, then brought the Wood anyway, because it seemed increasingly clear that Felix did not love anything so much as they feared it, and fear traveled lighter.

* * *

Magister Ilex Threefold was seventy if he was a day, built like a question mark that had given up on ever being straightened, and he greeted Felix at the top of the stair with a cup of mulled wine and the expression of a man who had been waiting all evening to say *I told you so* to someone who hadn't yet earned it.

"You look terrible," he said warmly.

"Thank you, Magister. I've been working hard."

"No, no, I mean specifically. You have the look of a person who has found something in an archive and hasn't slept since." He handed over the wine. "I did forty years of cataloguing before the stars got me. I know the face. It's the face of a person who keeps checking whether the thing is still where they left it."

Felix drank. The wine was heavy with cloves and, faintly, with brandy that had certainly never paid its taxes. "Is it that obvious?"

"To me? Yes. To anyone else? Also yes, but less kindly." Ilex led them through the dome room, where brass instruments hung from the rafters like sleeping bats and the great orrery turned at the center with its patient clockwork planets. "Now. You asked about the moon. Before I answer, I want you to understand that I have spent my whole life measuring things, and the thing I have learned is that most people do not actually want measurements. They want permission to keep believing whatever they believed before."

"I want the measurement," Felix said.

"Mm." Ilex stopped at the orrery and tapped the small silver sphere that circled the brass earth. "Then here it is. The moon makes twelve turns a year. Twelve full turnings, twelve dark turnings. This has been true for as long as anyone has written anything down—eleven hundred years of records in this observatory alone, and older records besides, scratched into cave walls by people who thought the moon was an eye. Twelve turns. Always twelve." He paused. "Except twice."

Felix set down the cup. "Twice when?"

"Once, nine hundred and forty years ago. The records from that year are fragmentary—the observatory burned, though whether the fire caused what followed or merely coincided with it, no one can say. What we have are accounts of a night when the moon rose full, set, and rose again before dawn. A thirteenth turning, crammed into a single year like a guest arriving after the doors were barred." Ilex's voice had gone quiet, the way men go quiet in churches and libraries, the two places where quiet is load-bearing. "And once, one hundred years ago."

The oilcloth bundle against Felix's ribs felt suddenly very warm.

"One hundred years ago," Felix repeated.

"Yes. And here is where my colleagues and I part ways with the comfortable explanations. Because a thirteenth turn should be impossible. The moon does not hurry. It is the most punctual object in creation; it has never once been late, and until those two occasions, it has never once been early. So we asked: what could make it hurry?" Ilex reached out and, with one crooked finger, nudged the silver sphere off its track. The orrery's gears ground, complained, and then—this was the part that made the hair rise on Felix's arms—the mechanism *adjusted*, redistributing its motion, pulling the other planets fractionally faster to accommodate the intruder. "Something pulled on it. Something with enough weight to bend the arithmetic of the sky. And when the extra turn was done, the weight was gone, and everything went back to twelve."

"What kind of weight bends the sky?"

"The kind," Ilex said, "that we used to call a god."

* * *

They stood there a while, watching the little planets grind through their corrected orbits. Somewhere below them, the city of Coriander was lighting its lamps, street by street, like a fever chart of itself.

"Nine hundred and forty years ago," Felix said slowly. "What happened then? In the histories?"

"Nothing," Ilex said. "That's the interesting part. Or rather—nothing that anyone wrote down. Which for an event that bent the heavens suggests either that nobody survived to write it, or that somebody very powerful made sure nobody wrote it." He gave Felix a sidelong look. "You'll notice both of my thirteenth turns fall near events your guild would know more about than mine. One hundred years ago. That was the end of the Godswar, wasn't it? The armistice. The Silence."

"The Concordance of Ash," Felix said automatically, the way one recites a catechism. "The gods withdrew from mortal affairs. The age of miracles ended."

"Mm. And did the gods withdraw, in your experience?" Ilex asked mildly. "Or were they withdrawn?"

Felix thought of a hundred nights of silence. Of kneeling in the guild chapel with their oath-words going up like smoke into nothing. Of Vess, who had spoken to them every night of their mercenary life—briefly, dryly, the way a good clerk speaks—and then, one hundred days ago, had simply stopped, mid-sentence almost, as though a door had closed between one word and the next.

"I don't know anymore," Felix said, and was surprised at how much it cost to admit.

"Good," said Ilex. "'I don't know' is the only honest prayer. Now." He shuffled to a desk buried under star-charts and produced, from beneath a paperweight shaped like a frog, a single sheet covered in his cramped, spidery hand. "This is why you're here. I finished the calculations yesterday. The moon is early again, Felix. Not visibly—not yet—but measurably. It is running ahead of its ephemeris by eleven minutes, and gaining. At the current rate of acceleration, it will complete a thirteenth turn in—" he turned the sheet around— "one month. Twenty-nine days, give or take the mood of the tides."

Felix read the figures twice. They were a ledger-keeper; numbers spoke to them more plainly than words ever had. The numbers were clean. The numbers were certain. That was the horror of them. A prophecy-book grown from tree-flesh could be doubted, could be dismissed as the ravings of some mad arborist. But an ephemeris was just arithmetic, and arithmetic did not care what anyone believed.

"One month," Felix said.

"One month. And I will tell you what I told the Royal Society, who laughed me out of the hall, and what I told the Inquisition, who did not laugh, which frightened me considerably more." Ilex folded his hands atop the desk. "A thirteenth turn happens when something of impossible weight presses against the world from somewhere else. Nine hundred and forty years ago, something pressed, and something happened, and history went blank. One hundred years ago, something pressed, and the gods fell silent forever, and we called it peace. Now it is pressing again. Whatever prison, or door, or wound caused the first two—it is weakening. And this time, Felix, there are people alive who remember what was locked away. Your people. My people. The Crown's people."

"The Eye," Felix said.

"The Eye," Ilex agreed. "Who visited me four days ago asking very similar questions. I told them the moon was a clockwork deception maintained by the College to justify our funding." His eyes twinkled without warmth. "Inquisitors believe any lie that flatters their suspicion of scholars. It's their one weakness."

"Why tell me the truth, then?"

Ilex looked at Felix for a long moment. Outside, the first stars were coming out over the quarry-scarred hill, indifferent and exact.

"Because forty years ago, before the stars, I kept accounts for a shipping concern in Vell's Landing. And I remember the reputation of the Gilded Thorn's ledger-keeper—the one who caught the smuggling ring inside their own guild, the one whose books were so honest that thieves trusted them." He smiled, thin as a page-marker. "Honest books are rare, Felix Fairweather. When one comes looking for me, I open the door."

* * *

Felix walked back down through the city with the calculations folded inside their coat beside the Wood, two impossible documents riding together like conspirators.

The streets of Coriander at night were familiar enough that Felix usually moved through them half-asleep, mind already on tomorrow's audits. Tonight every lantern seemed to cast the same shadow: the hooded figure from the Wood's carved page, holding out a book. Tonight every clock tower they passed seemed to be counting down from twenty-nine.

They cut through Chandler's Row, where the guild's candle-shops were closing, and nearly walked into Odile.

She was leaning against a shuttered stall eating roasted chestnuts out of a paper cone, which was how Odile did everything—leaning, eating, waiting—as though the world were a queue she had long ago made peace with. But her eyes, when they found Felix's face, sharpened immediately.

"You've seen the astronomer," she said.

"How could you possibly—"

"You walk differently when you're carrying bad news. Shorter steps. Like the ground might invoice you." She held out the cone. "Chestnut?"

Felix took one, mostly to have something to do with their hands. "He confirmed it. The moon is early. It will complete a thirteenth turn in twenty-nine days. It's happened twice before—once a century ago, during the Concordance, and once nine hundred years ago, and no one knows what happened that time because the record was erased."

Odile chewed her chestnut slowly. Around them, the lamplighters were working down the row, each flame a small surrender to the dark.

"A god's prison," she said quietly. "That's what the Wood showed you. That's what tonight's date means."

"That's what I think it means. Ilex thinks it too, though he'd sooner swallow his astrolabe than say the word 'god' in front of witnesses." Felix stared at the chestnut in their hand without seeing it. "Do you understand what this is, Odile? I came to the Thorn because I wanted a contract. A good contract, a long one—oath-service to Vess, steady wages, a bunk, a family of sorts. I thought my problems were the size of my problems. Late payments. Rival guilds. Whether Corvane's cook was putting something illegal in the stew." A breath. "And now there's a countdown in the sky. Twenty-nine days, and something that bent the heavens twice before comes pressing through again, and every power in this country—the Crown, the Inquisition, the Unblinking Eye, and whatever Corvane is actually doing behind his locked office door—is going to come down on whatever's underneath the Weeping Wood. And I'm the only person alive who's read the book that grows there."

"Not the only person," Odile said. "The only person *left*. There's a difference, and the difference is graves." She looked at Felix with an expression Felix couldn't entirely read—something under the usual wry calm, older and sadder. "You can't carry this alone, Felix."

"I'm not carrying it alone. I'm carrying it with you."

"You know what I mean." She bumped their shoulder with hers, gently. "Twenty-nine days. What do we do first?"

Felix looked up. Above the rooftops of Chandler's Row, the moon hung fat and silver and punctual, and for the first time in their life Felix Fairweather looked at it and saw not a lamp in the sky but a cell door, straining on its hinges.

"We go back to the archive," they said. "There's marginalia in the Wood I haven't deciphered—notes in the margins, older than the carvings. If someone grew that book a century ago, someone knew why. Someone wrote down what the prison is, and who built it, and maybe—" the hope felt fragile as spun sugar— "maybe how it ends. And Odile—if the Eye visited Ilex four days ago, they're ahead of us. They may already know about the turn. Which means whatever's written in those margins, we need it before they do."

They started walking, side by side, toward the river and the Thorn's darkened warehouse district, two small figures under a hurrying moon.

Neither of them noticed the man in the grey coat who detached himself from a doorway and followed, keeping always exactly one lamp-post behind—or that he carried, folded in his pocket, a warrant bearing the seal of the Order of the Unblinking Eye, and a sketch of Felix Fairweather's face drawn from a memory that had been sold.
