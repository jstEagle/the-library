## 9. Vess Was Never Silent

The Weeping Wood did not like candlelight.

Felix had learned this over three nights of secret study in the archive's back room, where the guild kept the accounts nobody wanted audited and the dust had achieved a kind of seniority. The book—tree-flesh bound in bark, its pages grown rather than written—seemed to lean away from flame, the way a sleeper turns from a lamp. So Felix worked by moonstone instead, that pale borrowed glow the Thorn used for appraising grief-jars, and let the cold light pool on pages that were faintly, unmistakably, alive.

They had told no one. Not Odile, though it cost them; not the astronomer-guild ally who had confirmed the thirteenth turn; certainly not Corvane, whose instruction to stop reading had been delivered with the particular gentleness that Felix had come to understand was more dangerous than his anger.

*You've seen enough, Fairweather. Some ledgers balance better closed.*

Which was, of course, the single worst thing anyone could say to a keeper of records. It was like telling a dog not to sniff.

So here they sat, third night running, tea gone cold at their elbow, translating the marginalia of a dead hand—or perhaps not dead, which was the thought that kept Felix's heart working overtime. The script was Old Liturgical, the formal hand of temple clerks from before the Concord, and Felix knew it only because Vess's own oath-rites were written in it, the words every sworn mercenary mouthed nightly without understanding. *I keep the count. I hold the tally. I answer when called.*

Answer when called. A hundred nights of silence, and Felix had begun to draft, in the private ledger of their heart, a formal complaint against their god for breach of contract.

The marginalia suggested the complaint should be filed in the other direction.

*He would not raise the blade,* wrote the clerk whose hand grew shakier as the entries went on. *Thirteen times the Host asked, and thirteen times He refused. "I will not spend my children as coin," He said. "Find another god to bank your war."*

Felix read it four times. The words didn't change. That was the trouble with text; it lacked the decency of memory, which at least blurred.

The ancient war. Everyone knew of it the way one knows of weather from before one's birth—the Godswar, the Sundering Century, the reason temples stood empty and gods answered prayers only through intermediaries like oath-bound mercenaries, secondhand divinity passed along like a message through too many hands. Every child learned that the gods had withdrawn. That they had grown tired of mortals, or quarreled among themselves, or simply gone out like candles in a draught. It was a comfortable story. It required nothing of anyone.

It had never once occurred to Felix to ask why a god would withdraw *downward.*

Because that was what the next line said, in the clerk's deteriorating script, pressed so hard into the page that the tree-flesh had scarred around each letter:

*They could not kill Him. You cannot kill a god; you can only make Him small. So they made Him very small indeed, and buried Him beneath His own witness, that His silence might be the seal.*

Beneath His own witness.

Felix looked down at the Weeping Wood—at the book itself, at the pale grain of its pages, at the bead of clear moisture that had gathered, as it always did, along the spine, and which the archivists of a century past had taken for ordinary sap.

It was not sap. Felix set both palms flat on the open page and felt it: a pulse, slow and vast, less like a heartbeat than like tide. The trees outside, the whole grove of them, rooted deep in the hillside above the old riverbed—and below the roots, below the stone, below everything—

"Oh," Felix said aloud, to an empty room. "Oh, you absolute *fool.*"

Not the god. Themselves. Because they had been kneeling at this book for weeks, pressing their hands to it, reading it aloud in the dark like a lonely child talking to a locked door—and the door had been listening the entire time.

* * *

"You look terrible," Odile said, setting down two cups of tea in the archive doorway the following evening. "And I say that with love, and also with the observation that you've been sneaking down here every night, which I know because you sneak the way you do everything else, which is to say loudly but with excellent posture."

"Odile."

"That's my name, yes. You said it in a tone. What tone is that? Let me guess." She sat on the edge of the worktable, unhurried, her silver-streaked braid swinging. "That's the tone of someone about to tell me something enormous and then ask me not to react."

Felix turned the Weeping Wood toward her. They had wrapped it in an audit-cloth, the plain grey fabric used for covering ledgers mid-inventory, because there was a certain irony available even at the end of the world and Felix intended to collect all of it.

"I need you to hear the whole thing before you decide how to feel."

"I never decide how to feel. Feelings ambush me. It's one of my best qualities." But she took the tea Felix offered, and she listened, and Felix gave her all of it: the marginalia, the thirteen refusals, the burial beneath the grove, the seal made of silence.

When they finished, Odile was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "Say the last part again. The part about the silence being the seal."

"They couldn't destroy Him, so they sealed Him. And the seal is—" Felix's throat tightened around it, absurdly, as if the words themselves were heavy. "The seal is His silence. Every prayer He doesn't answer, every night He doesn't speak to His sworn—that's not absence. That's the prison wall. We're standing inside the cell, Odile. All of us. Every mercenary who ever swore an oath and heard nothing back. We thought we were being ignored. We're *in here with Him.*"

Odile set down her cup with great care, the way she did when her hands wanted to do something else entirely.

"A hundred years," she said. "A hundred years of mercenaries swearing themselves to a god who couldn't answer, and the temples collecting tithes, and the orders sending soldiers out with His name on their lips—"

"—and Him hearing all of it," Felix said. "Every word. Unable to say a single one back."

The grief arrived then, and it was nothing like what Felix had expected. They had spent a hundred nights preparing for abandonment—for the cold arithmetic of a god who had weighed His faithful and found them wanting. They had rehearsed resentment. They had, if they were honest, rehearsed relief, too: the freedom of serving nothing.

But this. This was worse and kinder at once. Their god had not turned away. Their god had been held face-forward, forced to watch, mouth stopped, while a century of His people called His name into a wall.

"He refused to fight," Felix said quietly. "That's the crime. That's the whole of it. Thirteen times they asked Him to lead the Host, and thirteen times He said no, because He wouldn't spend mortals like coin. And for that they buried Him under His own witnesses and let the world believe He'd abandoned us." Felix laughed, and it came out wrong. "Do you know what I did on the hundredth night of silence? I drafted a grievance. In my head. Itemized. I thought about *withholding service.* I sat in my room feeling sorry for myself because my god wasn't answering fast enough, and the whole time He was underneath the ground beneath the grove, listening to me itemize."

"Felix."

"He heard that too, probably. *Item forty: emotional neglect.* From a man kneeling on His roof."

"Felix." Odile caught their wrist. Her grip was stronger than her frame suggested; everything about Odile was. "Listen to me. You are not allowed to do this alone, and you are not allowed to do this as a penance. Do you understand the difference? Grief that reframes is still grief. You lost something real—a hundred nights of silence were real, whatever caused them. You don't have to retroactively apologize for surviving them."

Felix looked at her hand on their wrist, at the ink stains on her fingers that matched their own, and felt the strange vertigo of being known.

"When did you get wise?" they asked.

"I've always been wise. You were too busy being competent to notice." She released them and nodded at the wrapped book. "What's the plan, Keeper?"

"The marginalia stops a century ago. The clerk's last entry says the seal has 'thirteen hinges'—thirteen turnings of some kind, and the astronomer already confirmed the thirteenth comes in a month. When it completes, either the seal breaks on its own..." Felix hesitated.

"Or?"

"Or the god dies in the dark, and the silence becomes permanent, and every oath ever sworn to Vess becomes a contract with a corpse." Felix pulled the cloth back over the Wood. "There's a middle option, I think. The marginalia mentions a witness. 'Let one who keeps the count stand at the hinge and testify.' Which sounds suspiciously like a job description I did not apply for."

"And the person who wrote that—whoever buried Him—they left instructions for undoing their own work?"

"No." Felix shook their head slowly. "I think the clerk was on His side. I think the clerk was one of the few who knew what was actually done, and couldn't stop it, and so did the only thing left—wrote the truth small, in the margins, where censors don't look. History gets the big letters, Odile. Truth hides in the footnotes."

Odile was quiet again. Then, in a voice carefully light: "You know Corvane forbade this."

"I know."

"You know he knew. About the Wood. About all of it, probably—he didn't strike me as a man surprised by anything, least of all by his own archives." She stood, brushing dust from her coat. "Which means the question isn't whether the Guildmaster is hiding something. It's what, and for whom, and how long. And Felix—" she paused at the door, and the lamplight caught the silver in her braid, "—if the Thorn has known for decades that a god is buried under our most secure holding site, then somebody has been doing something with that knowledge. Nobody sits on a treasure like that. Not Corvane. Not anyone."

The thought landed in Felix's stomach like a swallowed stone.

"I'll check the ledgers," they said, because it was the only sentence they had.

"Of course you will." Odile smiled, tired and fond. "Just—check them quietly. And Felix? Whatever you find down here, however bad it gets—you knock on my door. Not a note. Not a cleverly coded entry in the day-book. Your actual fist on my actual door. Promise me."

"I promise."

She left. Felix sat alone with the moonstone light and the wrapped book and the slow tidal pulse beneath their palms, and let themselves feel, for exactly one minute, the full weight of the reframing: not abandoned. Imprisoned. Not forsaken. Silenced. A god who had loved mortals enough to refuse a war, and been buried for it by the very people He refused to spend.

Then Felix blew out the candle they hadn't needed, squared the audit-cloth, and went looking for Corvane's private ledgers—because Odile was right, and because the other thing Felix had learned in the margins was this:

*The seal was paid for,* the clerk had written, in the last legible line of all. *Someone settled the account. Find the payer, and you find the purpose.*

Somewhere in the Gilded Thorn was a record of that payment. And Felix Fairweather, keeper of impossible things, was very, very good at finding records.

They found the first one before midnight.

It was in a strongbox behind Corvane's office, behind a lock that was honestly an insult to the profession, and it was not a ledger of debts at all. It was a ledger of *withdrawals*—dated, itemized, spanning thirty-one years—and every single entry was drawn against an account Felix had never seen, in a currency Felix had never audited, under a name that made the blood go cold and then colder:

*Vess. Held in trust. Harvested per schedule.*

Felix stood in the dark of Corvane's office with the book open in their hands, and downstairs, faint through the floorboards, they could hear the Guildmaster laughing at something over dinner.
