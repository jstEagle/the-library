## 10. Corvane's True Ledger

The laughter downstairs went on for a while.

Felix stood very still and listened to it the way one listens to rain—distant, ordinary, belonging to a world that had not yet learned what Felix was holding. Corvane laughed the way he did everything: generously, at full volume, with his whole chest. He laughed at apprentices' jokes and merchants' flattery and the same three stories he'd told for twenty years, and Felix had spent a decade quietly cataloguing that laugh among the guild's reliable assets, right alongside the roof that didn't leak and Odile's uncanny ability to win arguments she hadn't started.

*Harvested per schedule.*

The phrase sat in Felix's mind like a stone in a boot. You could walk with it. You could even forget it briefly, if something else demanded attention. But every few steps it announced itself again, and each time it hurt in a slightly new place.

They looked down at the ledger in their hands and made themselves do the thing they were best at in all the world, better than fighting, better than lying, better than surviving: they read.

* * *

The book was bound in grey calfskin gone soft as cloth at the corners, the kind of binding that came from being handled often and lovingly, which was somehow worse than if it had been chained shut. The hand inside changed over the years—three different clerks by the ink, four by the letterforms—but the format never varied, because the format was Felix's own. Entry, date, quantity withdrawn, quality noted, disposition of goods. It was the Thorn's standard audit template. It was *Felix's* template; they had inherited it from their predecessor, who had inherited it from hers. Somewhere in the last century some clerk had designed this exact column layout, and Corvane had simply picked it up and used it to inventory a god.

The early entries were modest. Almost shy.

*Year One, Thirdmonth: 4 drams, essence-grade, weeping. Sold to the College of Chirurgeons, Lys. Payment rendered: 900 crowns plus standing favor.*

Weeping. Felix knew that word from the trade ledgers. Essence-grade weeping was the finest emotional stock the Thorn handled—grief distilled to its purest fraction, sold in vials no bigger than a thumb to nobles who wanted to feel something honestly for once, or to widows who couldn't cry anymore and feared themselves monsters for it. Felix had audited hundreds of such jars. They had always assumed the source was the city's graveyards, its deathbeds, its fashionable funerals where grief ran thick enough to skim.

There was no graveyard entry here. There was only the account. *Vess. Held in trust.*

Felix turned pages. The quantities grew. Year Four: eleven drams. Year Nine: a full pint, "deep draw," with a note in Corvane's younger handwriting—*the seal thins on the solstice; take advantage*—that made Felix's hands go cold enough to fumble the page.

And the buyers. That was the part that made Felix sit down on the floor of Corvane's office like an apprentice who'd drunk the cooking sherry, sitting with their back against the Guildmaster's own desk, reading by candlelight they'd stolen from his own sconce.

The College of Chirurgeons bought essence for healing. Fine. Medicine was medicine; Felix could swallow that, barely, the way one swallows a fishbone sideways.

But there were other names. The Lord Marshal's office, buying *courage-grade* essence by the quart—liquid bravery for troops who'd otherwise break, which meant the Crown's armies had been running on a god for thirty years without knowing or caring whose god. The Opera bought sorrow in bulk. A cardinal's name appeared twice. And once, in Year Sixteen, in an entry so bland it had to be deliberate: *1 dram, witness-grade, to the Order of the Unblinking Eye. No payment recorded. Marked: standing arrangement.*

Standing arrangement. The Eye hadn't hunted the Thorn at all. The Eye had been a *customer*. All those years of raids and warrants and Inquisitors sniffing at the doors—and underneath it, the Order had been buying from the cellar like anyone else, paying not in crowns but in silence. Felix thought of Serah Vale's warrant, her careful legal face, her questions about prophecy-texts, and wondered—not for the first time and not for the last—if Vale herself knew what her own Grandmaster's signature was doing in a smuggler's accounts.

*Witness-grade.* Felix had never seen that grade before. It wasn't in any catalogue they kept. They wrote it down anyway, in their head, in the great cold ledger of things that would need answering later.

* * *

Somewhere past midnight, the floorboards downstairs creaked their particular creak, the one that meant Odile, and Odile alone, climbing toward the archive corridor where she was absolutely not supposed to be.

Felix closed the grey ledger, tucked it inside their coat against their ribs—it fit there with horrible neatness, as though the book had been measured for the space—and doused the candle, and waited in the dark.

Odile found them by sound, the way she found everything. "You've been up here two hours," she whispered. "I counted. I count when I'm worried, it's a flaw." A pause. "Felix. Why are you sitting on the floor in the dark?"

"Corvane's lock was insulting," Felix said. Their voice came out level. They were almost proud of it. "I wanted to see how much worse the books behind it were."

"And?"

Felix hesitated. Here was the strange arithmetic of the night: an hour ago they had been furious with Odile too, furious with everyone who had ever smiled at them across the guild tables, and now here was Odile in the dark finding them by sound because she'd been counting hours, because counting hours when worried was a flaw she owned out loud. The rage didn't dissolve. But it made room.

"They're selling him," Felix said.

The silence that followed had texture. Odile lowered herself to the floor beside them, skirts whispering, until their shoulders touched—a thing she did, a thing she'd always done, and which Felix had entered years ago under assets, under *belonging*, without ever writing the word.

"Selling who?" she asked, though Felix watched the answer arrive in her face as she said it, watched her get there ahead of her own voice. "Oh. Oh, Felix. Vess."

"Thirty-one years. Scheduled draws. Essence-grade, courage-grade, sorrow—" Felix stopped. Started again, quieter. "He never stopped answering because he stopped caring. He answered the way a man bleeding out answers. Slowly. In pints."

Odile took the news the way she took everything, with a stillness that most people mistook for calm and which Felix knew was the opposite—the stillness of someone holding a door shut against weather. Then, softly, with the gentle humor she used like a splint on broken things: "Well. I did once tell you the guild traded in impossible things. You wrote it down yourself. First page of your first ledger. Very tidy hand."

"I was proud of that hand," Felix said.

"I know. You showed me."

They sat together in the dark, and after a while Odile said, "What do you want to do?" and Felix understood that this was the real question of the night, larger than the ledger, larger than the rage. Because there were laws for theft, and contracts for breach, and guild codes older than either, and none of them covered this. There was no court where a god could file a claim. There was no warrant that named Corvane's crime, because the law did not recognize that a prisoner existed to be robbed.

"The law won't touch him," Felix said slowly, testing the shape of it. "The Crown buys from him. The Eye protects him. The guild codes—" they laughed, a small bitter sound they didn't recognize as their own— "the guild codes say the Guildmaster's dispositions are final. I've enforced that clause myself. Twice."

"So justice has to come from somewhere that isn't a law."

Felix turned that over. It felt like handling a blade by the wrong end and finding, unexpectedly, that it fit the hand better. Every structure Felix had sworn to—the oath to Vess, the contract to the Thorn, the quiet professional faith in records and audits and the idea that truth, properly filed, would out—every one of them had been built around the robbery, load-bearing walls of the thief's house. You could not burn a house down from inside its own walls. You needed to be outside them first.

Which meant leaving. Which meant everything they had called home for ten years was about to become the thing they fled from, and the thought should have terrified them, and did, but underneath the terror was something harder and cleaner, something that tasted like the first cold morning after a long fever.

"Yes," Felix said. "Outside every law I served."

* * *

They read the rest together, by matchlight, taking turns striking matches because neither wanted to relight the candle and be seen. And it was Odile—because of course it was Odile, who read people the way Felix read ink—who found the worst page.

It was near the back. The entries there were recent, in Corvane's current hand, and the quantities had grown obscene—whole flagons drawn at once, the account visibly failing, the way a well fails, the way anything fails when you ask it forever. But it wasn't the quantities. It was a marginal note, dated nine years ago, in the week Felix had signed their contract:

*New keeper engaged. Fairweather. Sworn to the account itself—he'll guard it without knowing what he guards. Best kind of clerk: devout. His oaths will bind him tighter than any chain, and he'll thank us for the work.*

Felix read it three times. The matches burned down between readings and Odile struck fresh ones without comment, giving them light the way she gave everything: steadily, without making a ceremony of it.

"He hired me because of my faith," Felix said at last. Each word arrived separately, like coins counted out. "Not despite it. My oath was the *qualification*. Ten years of nightly ritual, ten years of keeping his secrets safe from myself—" Their hands had begun, very slightly, to shake, and they watched them shake with a distant professional interest, as though auditing someone else's body. "He built me into the vault door, Odile. He made my devotion the lock."

"You're also the key," Odile said. "You noticed. He didn't plan for that. Men like Corvane never plan for the clerk who actually reads."

Cold rage is a strange fire. Hot rage shouts and smashes and feels wonderful for an hour. Cold rage does none of those things. Cold rage sits down with paper and makes lists. Felix felt it settle into them now, not like a flame but like winter coming on—gradual, total, arriving in the bones first—and they found that beneath it, steadying it, was a single clear thought that surprised them by how little it resembled hatred:

*He drew against an account that was never his. Thirty-one years of withdrawals, unrecorded income, unsanctioned disposition. By the guild's own codes—which I have enforced, twice—that makes Corvane the greatest debtor in the history of this house.*

Every debt comes due. Felix had written that maxim into a hundred ledgers, had believed it the way other people believed in weather. Well then. Let the audit begin.

* * *

Near dawn, they put everything back. The grey ledger returned to the strongbox—no. Felix stopped, hands on the box lid, and reconsidered. Evidence left behind was evidence Corvane could destroy the moment he smelled smoke. Instead Felix copied. Three hours of copying, cramped and perfect, every entry, every buyer, every damning marginal note, into a blank ledger they took from the archive shelves—one of the plain black ones used for debts, deliberately, because if the copy was ever found it should look like exactly what it was: an accounting. Numbers do not lie and cannot be accused of treason.

Then, and only then, the original went back behind the insulting lock, and Felix stood in the greying light of Corvane's office and felt the strangest sensation: that the room had shrunk around them overnight, that the desk and the good wine and the generous laugh had all been revealed as stage dressing on a set built over a pit.

Downstairs, the guild was waking. Someone was frying eggs. An apprentice was singing badly. The Gilded Thorn, being itself, being home, being the scene of the crime.

"Felix." Odile, at the door, low. "What happens now? Truly."

"Now?" Felix settled the copied ledger inside their coat, against their ribs, in the space measured for it. "Now a debt comes due. And I have to decide who collects it—the Crown, the Eye, the company of everyone Corvane ever cheated..." They paused at the window. Below in the street, unhurried, unmistakable, two figures in the slate-grey of the Inquisition stood talking to the gate watch, and one of them held papers, and even at this distance Felix knew the posture of a person holding a warrant.

"—or nobody," Felix finished quietly. "Because I think, Odile, that whatever we choose, we're going to have to choose it running."

Below, the grey figure lifted the papers to the morning light, and began, patiently, to knock.
