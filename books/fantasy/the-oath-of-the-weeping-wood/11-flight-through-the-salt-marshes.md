## 11. Flight Through the Salt Marshes

The trouble with keeping ledgers, Felix had learned over eleven years at the Gilded Thorn, was that you always knew exactly how much trouble you were in.

They knew it now to the copper. Three days since Odile's confession, two days since Felix had stopped sleeping properly, and one hour — by the chime of the harbor bells — since a boy from the fish market had pressed a scrap of paper into their hand behind the tannery and run without waiting for payment. The note was written in Odile's quick, slanted hand, the one she used when she didn't want anyone reading over her shoulder:

*The Eye has your face. Vale has your name. The outposts are burning. Go tonight, not tomorrow. Tonight.*

Felix read it three times, folded it into the smallest possible square, and did what any sensible keeper of accounts would do with an item of catastrophic value.

They filed it.

Not in the guild archive — never there again — but inside the false spine of their personal ledger, beneath the floorboard under their bed, wrapped in oilcloth beside the Weeping Wood itself. The book sat where it had sat for three nights, breathing its faint green smell into the dark of the room, patient as only something grown rather than made could be patient. When Felix lifted it, they could have sworn the weight had changed. Heavier, maybe. Or maybe that was just them.

"Right," Felix told the empty room. "Inventory."

One satchel. Two shirts, because even fleeing for your life did not excuse smelling like a tannery. The jar of last words — they hesitated over it, then took it. Some debts you carried whether or not they were yours. A knife, a whetstone, forty crowns in mixed coin, and the small tin of salted plums that Odile kept sneaking onto their shelf no matter how many times Felix insisted they hated them.

They took the plums too. They would deny this later, under oath if necessary.

Downstairs, the Thorn's main house was quiet in the way that meant wrong. The night clerk's stool stood empty. The lamp over the exchange desk had been left burning, which against guild rule seven and simple sense both. And on the great door of the counting room, someone had chalked a single mark: a circle, open at the bottom, with a line struck through it.

The Unblinking Eye's sign. The old one, the true one — not the pretty version stamped on their warrants, but the crude sigil scratched on doors across three kingdoms for a hundred years, meaning *we see, we remember, we come back.*

"They were here," said a voice from the shadows of the stairwell, and Felix's knife was out before the voice finished its second word. "Put that away, it's me."

Odile stepped into the lamplight, and Felix's stomach performed a complicated maneuver involving several knots. Her left eye was swelling shut. There was blood at her temple, dried brown, and she was carrying her sister's good shawl bundled around something that clinked.

"Odile—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't do the face. We haven't got time for the face. Four of them came through the fish gate an hour ago. I gave them the clerk instead of you, which he'll forgive me for eventually because I paid him to, and then I gave them my face, which he'll never forgive me for because I didn't pay him enough to watch it happen." She touched her temple, winced, and grinned with the half of her mouth that still worked. "I've had worse. Remember the thing with the goose?"

"I try very hard not to."

"The Eye doesn't fight fair, is the point. They had some kind of — I don't know, a lantern that showed them things. Showed them *you*, walking out the back toward the marsh road, except you weren't, because I'd already moved your boat." She thrust the bundle at him. It clinked again. "Your coin. Your papers. And the deed to the boathouse, which is technically mine, so consider us square for the memory I sold, and don't argue, arguing costs time."

Felix held the bundle and found, to their considerable irritation, that their eyes were doing something inconvenient.

"Stop that," Odile said gently. "You'll smudge the ledger."

"You're coming with me."

"No."

"Odile—"

"Somebody has to stay and be seen being *not you*," she said. "Somebody has to walk down Salt Row wearing your coat, buy oysters at your stall, be tall and tragic at all your usual tragic heights, until Vale's people are certain they've got the right trail. That somebody can't be you, because you're the one holding the tree-book that every spook and priest in this city would burn a district for." She said it lightly, the way she said most important things, but her good eye was steady on his. "It's arithmetic, Felix. You taught me arithmetic. You're the one who says everything must balance."

"Not like this."

"Like lots of things," she agreed. "Now go. Marsh road, third cut past the drowned chapel, ask for Maren. Tell her the Thorn owes her and that the debt transfers." She pushed him toward the back door, and then — because she was Odile, and because the world was ending and she had never once let that interfere with her sense of occasion — she grabbed him by the collar and kissed his cheek hard enough to leave a mark. "For the record," she said, "in case nobody keeps one after tonight. You were the best friend I ever sold a piece of. Don't make me regret buying you back."

Then she pulled his coat off the peg, shrugged it over her shoulders, and walked out the front way into the firelight, tall and tragic, while Felix went out the back with a book full of god and a throat full of broken glass.

* * *

The salt marshes began where the city's courage ended.

That was the local saying, anyway, and standing knee-deep in black water at midnight with the glow of burning outposts staining the sky behind them, Felix decided it undersold the situation considerably. The marsh stretched eastward in flat silver sheets, cut by channels that shifted with the tide and by paths that existed mostly as rumor. Everything smelled of brine and rot and the particular mineral cold of mud that had been mud since before the gods started making mistakes.

Felix had been walking for two hours. Behind them, the sky over the river district glowed orange — Vale's soldiers were thorough, or Corvane's orders were, and Felix suspected the latter. Three of the Thorn's storehouses had gone up. The counting house on Anchor Lane. The little office above the chandler's where Felix had first signed their contract, eleven years ago, with ink that Corvane had sworn was ordinary and Felix now understood had probably been anything but.

Every outpost. Every record. Every trace of the Thorn's trade in impossible things, burned clean — except the one impossible thing that mattered, which was currently strapped to Felix's back and growing heavier with every mile, as though the Wood disapproved of running.

"It's a figure of speech," Felix muttered to it. "We're not actually fleeing. We're conducting a strategic audit."

The Wood said nothing. Gods imprisoned in tree-flesh, in Felix's experience, were poor conversationalists.

The drowned chapel appeared exactly when the note said it would — a roofless hulk of grey stone rising from the shallows, its bell tower leaning like a drunkard listening for his name. The tide had taken the nave decades ago; only the tower and one arched wall stood above the water, furred with salt-white weed. Felix waded the last channel, teeth chattering, and found a rowboat tied to the arch, and in the rowboat a woman of perhaps sixty with forearms like mooring cable and a face that suggested she had personally buried everyone who'd ever disappointed her.

"Maren?" Felix managed.

"Depends who's asking." Her eyes went to the bundle on Felix's back, and narrowed. "You'd be the Fairweather one. The book-keeper." She said *book-keeper* the way other people said *grave-robber*. "Odile sent word ahead. Said you'd be wet, rude, and carrying something that shouldn't exist."

"She knows me well."

"Aye, she does." Maren untied the boat and nodded at the stern seat. "In. Mind the eels. Third cut past the old oyster beds, then you're out of the marsh and into the downs, and after that you're on your own, because I don't go past the boundary stone and neither does anyone sensible." She shipped the oars and began pulling with long, unhurried strokes, the boat sliding between channels that seemed to rearrange themselves politely in her wake. "So. The Thorn's burning. Half the city's asking who lit it. And you're sitting in my boat with a tree growing out of your bag. Want to tell me what you actually did, book-keeper?"

Felix considered lying. It was, they reflected, getting easier. That should have frightened them more than it did.

"I found out what my employer really sells," they said instead. "And I found out I'm in the inventory."

Maren snorted. "Aren't we all." She rowed on in silence for a while, water chuckling against the hull, and then, without looking around: "The god in the trees. That's what it is, isn't it? My grandmother used to say the Wood was a church before it was a forest. Said people went there to swear oaths that couldn't be broken, and the trees drank the swearing." Her shoulders rolled with the oars. "Everyone thought that was just old women talking."

"Old women," said Felix, "are criminally under-cited in official histories."

That earned a real laugh, short and rusty, and something in Felix's chest loosened by a single degree. It was a strange thing to notice at such a moment — that they were alone in a rowboat in a dying marsh with everything they owned in one satchel, and yet some small cold knot of loneliness that had lived in them for eleven years had come loose. Perhaps it was Odile's kiss on the cheek. Perhaps it was Maren's laugh. Perhaps belonging, Felix thought, was less a place than a habit you caught from other people, like yawning.

They were nearly through the third cut when the sky behind them turned wrong.

Not more orange — Felix had almost gotten used to orange — but white. A hard, sourceless glare from somewhere south of the river district, the color of lightning that had decided to stay. The air pressure changed; Felix's ears popped. And along the whole southern horizon, where the city's rooftops made their jagged silhouette, Felix saw the light bend — actually bend, curving like water around a stone, folding inward on a single point.

Toward the fish gate. Toward Salt Row. Toward a woman in a borrowed coat walking tall and tragic past the oyster stalls.

"No," Felix said. "No, no — Odile—"

"Sit down!" Maren barked, but Felix was already up, already turning, gripping the gunwale as the boat rocked. Far off, thin across the miles of night, came a sound like a bell struck underwater, and the white light collapsed inward with a soundless flash, and every bird in the marsh rose at once, thousands of them, a black storm of wings screaming east.

Maren swore with impressive variety and shipped the oars, letting the boat drift. "Witch-light," she said grimly. "Eye-work. That was a taking, book-keeper. Somebody just got scooped."

Felix stared south until their eyes burned. The light was gone. The birds settled slowly, reluctantly, back into the reeds. The marsh resumed its silence, vast and indifferent, and somewhere in the middle of it Felix became aware that they were shaking — not from the cold, or not only.

They had done this arithmetic before. On contracts, on audits, on the long columns of the Thorn's books where gains balanced losses and someone's grief bought someone else's miracle. One life weighed against a secret that could end a war, or start one. Odile herself had made the same calculation, hours ago, in a lamplit stairwell, and had not asked Felix's permission because she'd known perfectly well what answer she'd get.

Knowing the sum was correct did not stop the remainder from hurting.

"Row," Felix said quietly. Their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect, and they noticed that too, and filed it away in the new ledger they seemed to be keeping lately — the one where they recorded, entry by entry, the person they might have to become. "Please. Before I do something stupid."

Maren looked at them a long moment. Then she bent to the oars. "Stupid's free tonight," she said. "Everything else costs."

* * *

They parted at the boundary stone in the grey hour before dawn, when the marsh gave way to wind-scoured downs and the sky went the color of dishwater. Maren pointed Felix along a shepherd's track toward the hills and refused payment twice before accepting it on the third offer, because, she said, "a debt unpaid breeds interest, and I've no use for either." Then she turned her boat back toward the water and the burning city, and Felix climbed.

The Wood rode their back like a passenger. Somewhere in the last mile it had stopped feeling heavy and started feeling *present* — a warmth between the shoulder blades, a faint pulse like a heartbeat heard through a wall. Twice Felix caught themself adjusting their stride to match it, the way you fall into step with a companion.

At the top of the ridge they stopped and looked back.

The city lay in the valley below, smoking. Most of the fires had guttered; only the river district still glowed, and even that was dimming, the flames eating down into embers. From this distance it looked almost peaceful — a wound viewed from far enough away stops bleeding and starts looking like scenery. Somewhere down there was Odile, in a cell or a sack or worse, having spent her freedom like small change to buy Felix an hour's head start. Somewhere down there was Corvane, whose hidden accounts Felix carried now in their head alongside everything else, every entry, every sale of the god's stolen power, dated and priced and ready for an auditor who did not yet exist. Somewhere down there was Inquisitor Serah Vale, following orders written by the same crown that bankrolled the trafficking, and Felix found themself hoping — absurdly, angrily — that she was having doubts, because someone in this whole rotten ledger ought to.

And somewhere down there, beneath a forest at the city's eastern edge, a god who had never once abandoned them lay silent in the roots of living trees, waiting a hundred years for a witness who had forgotten their own name on purpose.

Felix sat down on the boundary stone, opened the tin of salted plums — they were, infuriatingly, delicious — and ate four of them while the sun came up red over the downs.

Then they took out the Weeping Wood, laid it on their knees, and spoke aloud for the first time since the marsh, because there was no one left to hear and no longer any point pretending otherwise.

"I'm coming back for her," they told it. "Understand that first. Whatever you are, whatever I was, whatever this costs — she comes out, or none of the rest of it happens. That's the price. That's the *first* price." They turned a page with a thumb gone suddenly careful. "Second price is Corvane's books, opened in public, every line. Third is the Grandmaster answering for a century of theft. And fourth—" their voice caught, and they let it, because a witness who lied about the small things wasn't worth trusting with the large ones "—fourth is me finding out what I was. All of it. Even the parts I ran from."

The Wood warmed under their hands. Just once, slow as a sigh — but unmistakably, undeniably, for the first time in a hundred years, something answered.

Felix closed the book, shouldered the satchel, and turned east toward the hills, toward the downs, toward the long road to the grove — and froze.

On the far side of the track, half-buried in the gorse at the exact center of the path they had to take, stood a milestone no map had ever mentioned. Old, lichen-crusted, older than the kingdom's roads. And carved fresh into its face, the stone dust still pale in the cuts, were words in a hand Felix knew better than their own:

*THIRTEEN WITNESSES. TWELVE FORGOTTEN. ONE MONTH REMAINS. COME HOME, KNIGHT.*
