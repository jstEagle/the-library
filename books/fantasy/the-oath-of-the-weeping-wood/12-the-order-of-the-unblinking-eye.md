## 12. The Order of the Unblinking Eye

They did not bind Felix's hands. That was the first insult.

They bound the Weeping Wood instead—wrapped it in oilcloth and iron chain like a corpse being prepared for burial—and carried it ahead of Felix through the marsh in a litter borne by four silent men. Felix walked behind it with wrists free and a hood over their head that smelled of lamp oil and old incense, listening to the squelch of boots and the creak of the litter, thinking: *they know exactly what matters here. It isn't me.*

The second insult was the tea.

When the hood came off, Felix was seated in a chair too comfortable for a prisoner, in a round white room at the top of a spire Felix recognized from every map they'd ever falsified—the Eye's tower at Grayharbor, its windows arranged like the iris of something enormous and patient. A woman in gray robes set down a tray: a pot of tea, two cups, a small plate of honeyed figs.

"Chamomile and blackthorn," she said. "For the marsh chill. You're not a prisoner, Keeper Fairweather. You're a guest who hasn't been told so yet."

"I've been a guest before," Felix said. "In Vell. The host turned out to be a debt collector."

"And how did that end?"

"He forgave the debt." Felix lifted the pot, sniffed it, poured two cups with the ease of long habit. "After I audited his books and found he'd been cheating himself for years. Sentiment makes terrible arithmetic."

The woman smiled—a real one, brief and tired—and left without another word. Felix drank the tea because refusing it would look like fear, and fear was the one thing Felix had sworn, privately, never to show anyone again after the marshes. Odile's face when the soldiers took her. The sound she'd made—not a scream, worse, a laugh, as if to tell Felix *go on, you fool, don't you dare stop for me.*

Felix had stopped anyway. For three heartbeats. And those three heartbeats had cost them everything: the soldiers had seen their face in the torchlight, and the Wood had been found in the reeds where Felix dropped it, and now here they sat, drinking chamomile in the eye of the Order of the Unblinking Eye.

*Odile is alive,* Felix told themselves. *Captured, but alive. The Eye doesn't kill what it can question.* This was either intelligence or hope wearing intelligence's coat. Felix had never learned to tell them apart.

* * *

The Grandmaster came at dusk.

Felix had imagined him a hundred ways on the journey—inquisitor-crisp, sorcerer-remote, some vast presence filling the room like weather. What arrived instead was an old man in a plain brown robe, walking with a cane of black wood, his face seamed and sun-spotted like a farmer's, his eyes—

His eyes were the problem. They were pale as frost on a windowpane, and they did not blink. Not once. Not when he sat, not when he accepted tea, not when he looked at Felix for a long, unhurried moment with the expression of a man reading a letter he'd written himself, long ago, in different ink.

"You have her chin," he said. "The knight-commander. My sister-in-arms. I never knew she had children until the Order showed me your face, and even then I didn't believe it. Time doesn't work that way." He sipped his tea. "And then I read your ledger-work. The Thorn's accounts. Do you know what I noticed?"

"That they're immaculate," Felix said.

"That they're *old.* Your hand changes somewhere around the year 1189. Before that, you write with a quill-grip nobody has used since the war. After, you write like a modern clerk. As though someone taught you a whole new way to hold a pen." He set down the cup. "Or as though someone erased a century and left the calluses."

Felix kept their face still. It was a skill. They had lied to an Inquisitor with this face; they could sit through an old man's riddles.

"I'm a mercenary," they said. "I keep books. I'm good at both because I've done both a long time. That's all the mystery there is."

"Mm." The Grandmaster reached into his robe and produced something that made Felix's stomach drop through the floor of the world: a small wooden token, worn smooth, carved with the sigil of a tree shedding leaves that fell upward. "Do you know this?"

"No."

"Liar," he said, without heat, the way one notes the weather. "It was yours. Knight-Commander Sera Fairweather carried eleven of them—one for each witness of the Sealing. She gave hers away the night before the purge. To a soldier she trusted." His unblinking eyes rested on Felix. "To me, as it happens. I've kept it ninety-nine years, waiting for someone to come claim it. And then last month, a memory surfaces in our archives—a memory we purchased from a frightened woman in Vell eight years ago—and in it, a guild clerk hums a marching song that only the Ninth Legion ever sang. *Your* legion."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, gulls argued over the harbor.

"You think I'm a knight from a hundred years ago," Felix said carefully.

"I think," said the Grandmaster, "that you are whatever is left when a person is emptied out to keep them safe, and the emptying was imperfect. I am something similar. Shall I show you?"

He drew back his sleeve.

Felix had seen old skin before. Guildmasters, dying patrons, a swamp-witch in Kettering who claimed two hundred years and looked every day of it. But the Grandmaster's arm was wrong in a way Felix's professional eye catalogued before their courage caught up: the skin was old, yes, but the veins beneath it were dark and slow, moving like roots under soil, and where the wrist met the palm there was a seam—a fine, deliberate line, as if the man had been *repaired*, or maintained, the way one maintains a roof. Patched. Year after year after year.

"The Order found me dying in 1191," he said calmly. "A fever, after the Sealing. I was fifty-three. The god's prison required a warden whose oath would outlast ordinary flesh, so my brothers and sisters in the new order made certain it would. Every seven years, they renew me. Every seven years, I forget a little more of what I was before, which is, I suspect, a mercy neither of us was offered." He rolled the sleeve down. "I have guarded the Weeping Wood for ninety-nine years, Felix Fairweather. I watched your commander die defending it. I watched the Crown burn every copy of every text that named what sleeps beneath it. And I have waited, patiently, for the thirteenth turn—for the month when the prison weakens and something inside it begins, very softly, to knock."

"Why are you telling me this?" Felix's voice came out lower than intended.

"Because you stole the book. Because you ran from the Thorn with it under your arm while their outposts burned—which tells me Corvane no longer controls you, which tells me you finally read his true ledgers." Something moved in the frost-pale eyes; not warmth, exactly. Recognition. "You found the accounts where he sells pieces of a sleeping god, didn't you. Bottled grief. Borrowed years. You thought you were keeping records of impossible things, child. You were keeping the inventory of a prison break conducted one vial at a time."

* * *

They talked until the moon rose.

That was the unsettling part, later—how easily it went. Felix had prepared scorn, deflection, the smooth merchant's patter that had gotten them past Serah Vale. None of it was needed. The Grandmaster asked questions like a man doing sums, and answered Felix's questions in return, and somewhere in the third hour Felix realized they were sitting across from the enemy of everything they'd come to believe, nodding along.

He explained the war the way no surviving text did: not as gods versus mortals, but as a quarrel in a family that had run out of words. Nine gods, twelve mortal witnesses sworn to each, and a thirteenth—Vess—who refused to choose a side when the others began burning kingdoms over the shape of prayer.

"Vess was the god of testimony," the Grandmaster said. "Of things said truly and written down. When the war came, Vess declared they would bear witness to all sides and fight for none. The other gods called it cowardice. Mortals called it treason. So the twelve witnesses and their gods sealed Vess beneath the grove they loved best, and the general—" he touched his own chest, briefly, "—the general laid the final stone, because someone had to be willing to be hated forever in exchange for the war ending. And it worked. Ninety-nine years of peace. Children born who never saw a city fall from the sky. Whatever else I am, I bought that."

"But the price," Felix said.

"The price." He laughed, short and dry. "Yes. The price was that we sealed a god of truth inside a lie—told the world Vess had simply gone silent, abandoned their faithful, faded like morning mist. Every oath-bearer who prayed to Vess afterward prayed into a locked room. Including, I imagine, you. A hundred nights of silence, wasn't it? I know the number because the prison weakens on a schedule, and the silence deepens as it does. You weren't abandoned, Keeper. You were knocking on a door with the knocker removed."

Felix looked at their hands. They were shaking, slightly, and they let them, because the old man had earned honesty if anyone had.

"I hated them," Felix said. "For a hundred nights I told myself the god had left, and I built a life on top of that hatred like building on a sinkhole. And you're telling me the silence was a wound we inflicted."

"A wound *I* inflicted." He said it plainly, no flinch. "I have had ninety-nine years to make peace with it, and I will tell you what peace I found: none. Only purpose. Purpose is what you use when peace isn't available." He leaned forward, and for the first time his voice lost its ledger-flat calm. "But hear the rest, because this is why you are a guest and not a prisoner. If the seal breaks wrongly—if it is broken by force, or broken by thieves harvesting it piece by piece the way Corvane has done—Vess wakes in pain, betrayed twice, with a century of evidence that mortals are jailers and liars. That is how wars reignite. Not with armies. With a wounded god and a justified grievance."

"And if it breaks rightly?"

The Grandmaster was quiet a long moment. Outside, the moon climbed, one night fatter than the last, counting down.

"Then it requires a witness," he said. "One of the thirteen, still living, still sworn, willing to testify—to give up the oath itself, freely, as the final entry in the record. That was the design. Vess believed, to the last, that truth spoken willingly could unlock anything, even a cage built by gods." His unblinking eyes fixed on Felix, and there was no triumph in them at all, only a terrible, patient sorrow. "Eleven witnesses died in the purge. The twelfth was your commander, and she died buying time for someone to survive who might one day stand in the grove and speak. I have spent a century hoping I was wrong about what survived. And now you sit in my tower humming the Ninth Legion's song, and I find, to my considerable discomfort, that I do not want you to be the answer. Because if you are the answer, then everything I guarded, everyone I burned, every year they stitched into my failing body—it was all in service of undoing my own great work. And I am too old to carry that and too stubborn to die."

* * *

They gave Felix a cell that was, insultingly, nicer than any room they'd ever rented: a bed with clean linen, a window slit facing the harbor, a shelf of books that included, Felix noted with a jolt, a first-edition collection of Vessian liturgy banned by the Crown for sixty years. Someone had clearly decided the guest should be comfortable. Someone had also locked the door, warded it with six layers of silencing script, and posted a guard whose breathing Felix could hear—steady, awake, unblinking presumably.

Felix lay on the bed and stared at the ceiling and tried to feel hatred.

It wouldn't come. That was the horror of it. They lay in the enemy's tower, having lost the Wood, having lost Odile, having lost the race entirely—and all they could feel was a dreadful, treacherous kinship. Here was a man who had also been hollowed out by duty. Who had also served a law that lied to him. Who had spent a century keeping faith with a decision made by a younger self, because the alternative was admitting the decision had been a wound dressed up as wisdom.

*I could have been him,* Felix thought. *Give me ninety-nine years of silence and a badge, and I'd have patched my sleeves and called it purpose too.*

And beneath that, quieter, more dangerous: *he's right about the seal. He's right that it needs a willing witness. Which means he knows how to free Vess—and he's spent a century making sure no one ever does.*

Felix sat up.

The guard outside shifted. The wards on the door murmured like bees.

Ninety-nine years of maintenance. Every seven years, renewed. Which meant schedules. Rosters. Rituals. A prison of flesh was still a prison, and Felix Fairweather had spent their whole life learning one lesson above all others:

Every prison, however holy, keeps books.

And somewhere in this tower, the Order of the Unblinking Eye kept theirs.

Felix pressed their ear to the wall, listening for footsteps, counting doors, and began—as they had begun every contract, every con, every impossible audit of their strange and unlived life—with the only prayer Vess had ever needed:

*Show me the ledger, and I'll find you the way out.*
