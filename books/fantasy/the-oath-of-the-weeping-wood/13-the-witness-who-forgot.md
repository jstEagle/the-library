## 13. The Witness Who Forgot

The Order of the Unblinking Eye kept excellent books.

This was, Felix reflected as they crouched in a linen closet that smelled of lye and incense, both admirable and deeply inconvenient. Excellent books meant exact inventories. Exact inventories meant that when one jar of shadow went missing from Vault Nine, someone would notice within the hour.

Felix had noticed it first, of course. That was the whole point.

Three days in the Spire as a "guest" — a word the Grandmaster's stewards used the way a butcher used *fresh* — had taught Felix three things. First, the Order's acolytes walked in perfect silence, which meant you listened for breathing instead of footsteps, and everyone in this building breathed like they were being graded on it. Second, the Grandmaster took his evening meal alone at the same hour every night, and his guards took theirs together at the same table, and neither habit was ever varied, because men who planned to live forever stopped believing anything could surprise them.

Third — and this was the one that mattered — the Eye did not destroy what it confiscated. It *filed* it.

Somewhere below the chapel, behind a door that required no key but only a spoken name, sat the vault where the Order stored everything it had ever taken from anyone: heresies, heirlooms, memories, names. Including, if Odile's tearful confession in a salt marsh a lifetime ago (nine days; it felt like nine years) could be believed, a small clay jar labeled in a careful hand:

*One memory, purchased from O. Vasq, concerning F. Fairweather.*

Felix had come to steal it back. Not for revenge — though there was a ledger page somewhere in their heart with Corvane's name at the top and a great deal of red ink beneath it — but because a person could not audit their own life without the original documents. And Felix's original documents, they were beginning to suspect, were older than any document had a right to be.

The closet door opened six inches. A young acolyte stood in the corridor, eyes down, counting something on her fingers. Felix slipped out behind her, soft as an unpaid debt, and let her close the door on empty air.

*Show me the ledger,* they thought, heading down. *I'll find you the way out.*

* * *

The memory-vault's door was pale stone, unmarked except for a single carved eye with the lid carved half-closed. No lock. No handle. Just a small brass funnel set at mouth height, like something between a speaking-tube and a collection plate.

Felix had watched two acolytes pass through it that morning. The first had murmured a name and been admitted. The second had murmured the same name and been politely, firmly refused — the door hadn't moved, but the air around it had gone stiff, disapproving, like a butler pretending not to hear a drunk uncle.

So the door knew voices. Which meant the Order trusted its own records more than its own locks, which meant — Felix allowed themselves a thin smile — the Order, for all its incense and eternity, thought like accountants.

Accountants could be beaten. Accountants *loved* to be beaten by other accountants. It was practically flirtation.

Felix had spent the afternoon in the Spire's scriptorium, ostensibly copying penitential verses, actually memorizing. Acolytes introduced themselves constantly here — humility was doctrine — and names drifted past like moths: Brother Anselm, Sister Ivetta, Brother Caspian-of-the-Ninth-Lamp. Felix needed only one, and they chose carefully: a round, apologetic young man named Brother Tobbe whose duties kept him in the lower archives, whose voice was soft and nasal, and who — crucially — had spent the entire afternoon complaining about his sinuses.

Felix pressed their lips to the funnel and said, in a fair imitation of congestion and genuine faith:

"Brother Tobbe, seeking access to the record of debts."

The stone considered. Somewhere inside it, Felix imagined, a very old clerk checked a very long list.

The door swung open.

"I'm going to burn in a special hell," Felix whispered, and stepped through.

* * *

Inside was nothing like a vault.

It was a library, but wrong — shelves of clay jars, each the size of a fist, arranged not alphabetically or by date but by *weight*, apparently, rows of them humming faintly with the particular hush that memories made when they were kept from their owners too long. Felix knew that hush. They'd catalogued enough bottled grief at the Thorn to know how the stolen past sounded when it got lonely.

They found the jar on the fourth shelf, third row, exactly where a well-run operation would put it. The label read, in that careful clerical hand:

*One memory. Seller: Odile Vasq. Subject: F. Fairweather. Contents: identity, provenance unknown. Purchased under warrant of the Unblinking Eye, price: one sister's life.*

Odile had told them the shape of it in the marshes, weeping into her sleeves while soldiers burned the Thorn's outposts behind them: that seven years ago, broke and desperate, she'd sold a single memory of Felix to the Order's agents to pay her sister's ransom. She'd never known what the memory contained. She'd only known that afterward, whenever she tried to think about where she'd first met Felix, her mind slid off it like rain off glass.

Felix held the jar up to the lamplight. Inside, the shadow didn't swirl like grief-shadow did. It sat still, patient, folded — like a letter waiting to be opened.

Their hands shook. They noticed this with detached professional interest, the way they'd notice a discrepancy in a column of figures.

"Well," they said aloud, to the shelves, to the humming dark, to nobody. "Let's see what I paid for my own life."

They pulled the stopper.

* * *

The memory came in like weather.

First: mud. Cold, deep, sucking mud, the kind that takes boots and then feet and sometimes the men inside them. Rain like nails. Banners so soaked they hung dead on their poles. And over it all a sound Felix had never heard before and recognized instantly anyway — the sound of gods arguing with each other across a battlefield, which is not thunder, though thunder tries.

*I am standing on a hill above the field. My armor is black lacquer over silver, Vess's colors, and the rain beads off it like it knows better than to linger. Beside me stand twelve others in the same livery, the Twelve Witnesses, and our faces are bare because knights of Vess do not hide from what they must testify to. I am holding a book. It is not paper. It breathes.*

*"Thirteenth turn comes," says the knight beside me — a broad woman with a scar splitting her lip, Serah-something, no, not Serah, a name like a struck bell — "and the others will not wait."*

*"Then we go to the grove," I hear myself say, and my voice is mine and it is not mine, younger, harder, certain in a way I have never been certain of anything, "and we tell the truth out loud, whatever it costs. That is the whole of our oath. We were not sworn to win. We were sworn to witness."*

*And then fire, white and total, falling from a clear sky, and someone screaming the word PURGE, and my hands tearing the breathing book into— no. Not tearing. Hiding. Slivers of tree-flesh pressed into my own skin, into the meat of my forearms, and the pain is—*

Felix came back to themselves on the floor of the vault, cheek against cold stone, gasping like a landed fish.

The jar in their hand was empty. The shadow had poured into them the moment the stopper came free, and now it was *in* them, unfolding, and Felix understood with terrible clarity that it had not finished yet. This was not a sip. This was a cellar door opening onto a flooded basement.

The rest of the memory rose.

* * *

*A garden behind a safehouse in a city that no longer has its name, because names were among the first casualties. I am kneeling in the dirt with my gauntlets off, and Vess stands before me — and this is the part that stops Felix's heart a hundred years later, kneeling on a stranger's floor — because Vess is not vast. Everyone expects gods to be vast. Vess is a quiet figure in grey robes with kind eyes and ink-stained fingers, a god who looks like a scribe, because Vess was the god of testimony, of record, of the sacred act of writing down what happened so it could not be unwritten. The god of ledgers.*

*"If they find you," Vess says, "they will kill you last, after they make you watch. So I am going to do the cruelest thing a god can do to a knight, and you have the right to refuse me."*

*"Say it," I say.*

*"I will take your name and your century. You will wake in a new life with new hands and no history, carrying the truth in your blood where no purge can read it. And when the moon turns thirteen times—" the god's voice frays, just slightly, the only crack in all that calm, "—you will remember, and you will speak, and I will finally get to hear my own story told back to me by someone I trust. Because I am so tired, little knight. Even prisons keep books, and mine are all written in other people's handwriting."*

*"What do I lose?" I ask, because I am thorough even then.*

*"Everything," Vess says gently. "That is the price. But you will not feel the loss. You will only feel a room in you that seems oddly empty, and you will spend your whole new life trying to fill it."*

*I laugh. Gods, I laugh — I hear myself laugh, warm and sad. "A room that's always empty. That sounds familiar already."*

*"Yes," says my god, and touches my forehead, and the world folds shut like a book—*

* * *

Felix woke properly this time, still on the vault floor, with tears on their face they had no memory-fragment to explain and also, somehow, complete explanations for everything.

They lay there a while. The shelves hummed around them. Somewhere overhead, the Order's eternal clock chimed the hour with smug precision.

"Felix Fairweather isn't my name," they said experimentally.

No. Of course it wasn't. It was a coat they'd woken up wearing, a name stitched on the collar by whoever had pulled them out of the purge's aftermath and pointed them toward the Gilded Thorn. A good coat. Warm. But underneath—

Underneath was a knight of Vess. The thirteenth. The last. Sworn not to fight but to witness, hidden in plain time, walking around for a hundred years with the truth tucked into their blood like a sliver of tree-flesh, waiting for the moon.

"It was me," Felix said to the ceiling, and started laughing, and the laughter came out wet and cracked and slightly unhinged. "The hooded figure with the ledger. In the Wood. Tonight, circled a century ago. I circled it. I *drew myself an appointment.*"

Because that was the thing — the detail that turned horror into something almost like tenderness. Their past self had known exactly what they'd become. Had known they'd end up a bookkeeper, because of course they would; you couldn't take a knight of the god of ledgers and expect them to be anything but a keeper of accounts. Had known they'd find the Wood, find the date, find the way. Their forgotten self had left them a trail of breadcrumbs a century long, and every crumb was an entry in a ledger, because Vess knew precisely who they'd made.

*You will spend your whole new life trying to fill the room.*

Well. The room had tenants now.

Felix got up. Their legs worked, mostly. In the dark glass of a display case they caught their reflection — same face, same crooked nose, same ink-stained fingers, and utterly, irrevocably different, the way a ledger is different after the final audit line is drawn.

"Right," they told the reflection. "New column."

* * *

Getting out was easier than getting in, which offended Felix professionally. They returned the jar — emptied, but a good accountant returns containers — to its shelf, sealed the vault with Brother Tobbe's congested voice one last time, and was halfway to the laundry chute they'd scouted on day one when the alarm began.

Not bells. The Eye didn't use bells. The alarm was silence itself — a sudden, total absence of sound that pressed on the ears like deep water, and Felix knew, with the sick drop of a stomach hearing bad news, exactly what it meant.

The Grandmaster had checked his records. Or rather — someone had checked them *for* him, and found a jar of shadow missing from Vault Nine, and a purchase record seven years old flagged, and a guest who should have been copying penitential verses and was instead nowhere at all.

*Odile's memory*, Felix thought, sprinting for the chute. *They'll trace it back to her. They'll trace it to the marsh camp, to the company, to—*

They hit the chute, folded themselves into darkness and soap-smell, and fell.

At the bottom, in the tangle of wet linens, Felix did the fastest moral arithmetic of their life. Every instinct, every rule of survival, said *run* — run tonight, run now, carry the secret to the grove alone and let no one else pay for it. That was what a mercenary did. That was what a hundred years of careful forgetting had trained into their bones.

But knights of Vess weren't sworn to survive.

They were sworn to witness. And you couldn't witness a thing alone. That was the entire point of testimony — it required someone to tell, and someone to believe, and a family, however ragged, however cheated, however furious, willing to stand in the square and listen.

"The Chapel of Owed Debts," Felix said into the dark, naming the place where the discarded gathered, and felt the words settle into place like a figure balancing at last against its column. "Everyone Corvane ever shorted. Everyone the Eye ever filed away. Everyone who's owed."

They climbed out of the linens and ran for the marsh road, under a sky where the moon — swollen, strange, nearly sideways in its orbit — hung like a seal about to break.

Behind them, the Spire's silent alarm spread outward through the city like ink through water.

Ahead of them, a company waited to be forged.

And inside Felix Fairweather — inside the keeper, the con-man, the coat-wearer — a knight with a century-old oath flexed newly remembered muscles and said, in a voice like a struck bell:

*We go to the grove, and we tell the truth out loud, whatever it costs.*

Felix ran faster.
