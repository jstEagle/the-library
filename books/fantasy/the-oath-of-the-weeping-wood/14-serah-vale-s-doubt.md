## 14. Serah Vale's Doubt

Serah Vale had been awake for thirty-one hours when she finally cornered Felix Fairweather in the lantern-lit mouth of Cooper's Row, and she would have liked to report that her first thought was of the law.

It was not. Her first thought was that they looked terrible—hollow-eyed, salt-crusted, their ledger satchel clutched to their chest like a child clutching a stolen sweet—and her second thought was that she looked worse, because she had spent the last two days burning guild outposts and reading requisition forms by candlelight, and somewhere around hour twenty-nine she had caught her reflection in a rain barrel and thought, *who is that tired woman, and why is she holding a warrant for a god?*

"Fairweather," she said, stepping out from behind the cooper's cart. "Don't run."

Felix did not run. That was the first strange thing. In her experience, mercenaries ran with impressive speed and even more impressive cowardice. But Felix Fairweather simply stopped in the middle of the lane, rain beginning to spit down around them, and looked at her with an expression she could only describe as *resigned politeness*, as though she were a bill collector arriving at a funeral.

"Inquisitor Vale," Felix said. "You've been following me since the marshes."

"I have."

"You stepped in a very deep puddle outside Millhaven. I heard it."

Serah felt heat climb her neck. "That puddle was an ambush position."

"It was up to your hip."

"The water table in this district is notoriously high." She drew herself up, which was difficult, given that the water had also gotten into one boot, and produced the warrant with a flourish she had practiced in three separate mirrors. "By order of His Majesty's Crown, I am authorized to detain any party in possession of proscribed prophecy-texts, to seize said texts, and to—"

"To what?" Felix asked quietly.

The flourish died. Serah read the rest of the clause silently, because she had read it forty times in the last two days, and every time she read it, it seemed to grow another tooth. *"…and to render such parties unto the Office of Inquiry for disposition as the Crown sees fit."*

*Disposition.* A lovely word. It meant whatever the man signing the order wanted it to mean, and the man signing the order was Lord Chancellor Hesse, whose private accounts Serah had been obliged to audit last spring, and whose private accounts included—she now knew, having spent two sleepless nights cross-referencing burned ledgers against customs records—a quarterly payment from the Gilded Thorn marked only as *"harvest, ongoing."*

"Detention and seizure," she said, which was at least true, if not the whole of it.

Felix shifted the satchel. Rain dripped off their hood. Somewhere down the lane, a dog barked twice and gave up, apparently deciding the night wasn't worth the effort.

"You know what's in this bag," Felix said.

"I know what I'm told is in this bag."

"And what are you told?"

"That you carry a text grown from living wood, containing prophecy, and that prophecy in unlicensed hands destabilizes the realm." She recited it flatly, the way one recites anything one has stopped believing.

Felix tilted their head. "And who licensed the hands that do hold it?"

*There it is,* Serah thought. *The question.* She had been circling it for two days like a moth around a lamp she suspected might burn her.

"That's not relevant to the execution of my warrant."

"No," Felix agreed mildly. "It never is."

* * *

Here is what Serah Vale knew about the law, and loved about it, once:

She had grown up in the Tannery Quarter, where the law arrived late, drunk, and on horseback, and left again before anyone finished explaining the problem. When she was nine, a cloth merchant cheated her mother out of a winter's wages, and her mother—who could neither read nor afford a solicitor—sat at the kitchen table and wept into her hands, and Serah had decided, with the terrible clarity available only to children, that the law was simply a thing other people couldn't reach, and that she would become someone who reached it.

She had studied. She had starved. She had passed the examinations third in her cohort, behind two young men whose fathers owned the examination hall, and she had been assigned to the Office of Inquiry, where she learned within a year that reaching the law and the law reaching justice were two entirely different journeys, often traveling in opposite directions.

But she had believed, stubbornly, stupidly, in the shape of it. The warrant system. The idea that power should be *written down*. That a king's will, inked and sealed, bound even the men carrying swords. Her warrant had always felt like a small lantern she carried into dark rooms.

Tonight, in the rain, holding it out toward a soaked mercenary, she noticed that the lantern had gone out some time ago and she'd been carrying the smoke.

Because here was the trouble, and she had known it since Millhaven: the warrant cited the Proscription Act of the Old King's reign, amended under the Regency, sealed under the current Crown. Three layers of law, each written by men who took Thorn money. The same Crown that funded the trafficking of bottled grief. The same Crown whose Lord Chancellor received quarterly payments marked *"harvest."*

Harvest of *what?* She hadn't let herself finish the question until the marshes, standing in that infamous puddle, watching smoke rise from the Thorn's burning outpost, and letting herself finish it had felt like swallowing something with edges.

"You're stalling," she said aloud, mostly to herself.

"A little," Felix admitted. "You're stalling too."

"I am executing a lawful warrant."

"You're standing in the rain arguing with a person you could have arrested in Millhaven, had you wanted to arrest them." Felix's voice wasn't mocking. That was somehow worse. It was gentle, the way one speaks to someone standing at the edge of a decision. "Why didn't you?"

Because at Millhaven there had been soldiers. Because here there was no one but a dog that had already quit. Because warrants were easy to execute in front of witnesses and hard to execute alone in the dark, where the executed part of you—the part that read ledgers at three in the morning—could be heard.

"Because you'd have run," she said.

"Would I have?"

"No," she admitted. "You'd have stood there looking at me like that. Like I'm the one being detained."

"Are you?"

* * *

They ended up, absurdly, in the doorway of a shuttered chandler's shop, out of the worst of the rain, because Serah's boot was squelching and Felix observed—with that infuriating mildness—that hypothermic inquisitors executed warrants very poorly.

"I'm going to ask you a question," Felix said, sitting down on an overturned crate with the satchel in their lap. "You don't have to answer. But I think you've already asked it yourself, so consider this professional courtesy."

"I'm not in the habit of taking professional courtesy from fugitives."

"No. But you're cold, and tired, and you've read the ledgers, haven't you? The ones from the outposts."

Serah said nothing.

"The entries marked *harvest*," Felix pressed softly. "Quarterly. Thirty years of them. You found the older ones in the Millhaven archive—I saw you take them. Did you finish the arithmetic?"

She had. She hated that she had. "Payments totaling—" She stopped. Recalculated. Said it anyway, because saying it made it real and she was tired of things being unreal. "Enough to fund the Crown's entire western fleet. From one guild. Selling—" She faltered on the word. "What is it they're selling?"

Felix looked at her for a long moment, rain running off their hood, and Serah realized with a strange lurch that beneath all that weariness there was something almost like kindness, and that kindness, aimed at her, was more dangerous than any blade.

"Bottled grief," Felix said. "Borrowed years. And pieces of something older. Something that was put away a long time ago, by men who then built an entire legal apparatus to make sure nobody ever asked what the pieces came from." They patted the satchel. "Your warrant exists to protect that apparatus. Not the realm. Not the people. The *paperwork*."

"The paperwork is the realm," Serah said automatically. It was the first lesson of the Office. It sounded, in the chandler's doorway, in the rain, like something a child would say about a blanket fort.

"Is it?" Felix asked. "When your mother was cheated—"

"How do you—" She was on her feet before she knew it. "How could you possibly—"

"Odile talks," Felix said simply. "About everyone she cares about. You'd be amazed how much of the world is in her head." They didn't rise, didn't brace, just watched her with those tired eyes. "I'm sorry. I shouldn't have used it. But I want you to understand that I'm not speaking to you as an Inquisitor. I'm speaking to you as the daughter of a woman the law arrived too late for. You became the law so that wouldn't happen again. So answer honestly: has it happened again?"

The rain filled the silence. Down the lane, the dog barked once, reconsidered, went quiet.

"Yes," Serah said.

It came out smaller than she intended. Then, because it was out, the rest followed like water finding a crack:

"I've served eleven years. I've executed sixty-two warrants. I used to keep count because I believed each one was a small victory for the idea that power gets written down. And then I started reading what I was writing down *for*. The Proscription Act cites the safety of the realm. The realm's largest prophetic archive sits in the Thorn's vaults, catalogued and *licensed*, and has for decades. My warrant doesn't say 'seize dangerous texts.' It says 'seize texts the Crown hasn't sold yet.'" She laughed, and it came out cracked. "I checked. There's a licensing schedule. Appendix C. Prophecy-texts are listed as *inventory pending transfer*. Pending transfer to whom, Fairweather?"

"Hesse," Felix said. "Mostly. Some to the Order of the Unblinking Eye, as payment for services rendered."

"Which services?"

"Guarding a prison. But that's—" Felix paused, and something moved across their face, grief or fear or both wearing each other's coats. "That's a longer conversation than either of us can survive tonight."

* * *

Serah sat back down on the crate. This was, she understood dimly, the moment. Warrants did not permit hesitation. Hesitation was how inquisitors ended up as footnotes in other people's trials. She had seen it happen—to Aldous Rennick, who hesitated over a smuggling warrant and spent four years auditing grain tariffs in a coastal town so far north the sun forgot it.

But she sat on the crate anyway, and the strangest thing happened: nothing bad. The rain fell. Felix waited. The world declined to punish her for thinking.

"My warrant says *disposition as the Crown sees fit*," she said.

"I know."

"Do you know what that means? In practice?"

"I've read enough ledgers to guess."

"Rennick hesitated over a warrant with that clause in it. He was lucky. There was a woman in Corvane's books—Maren Ostwick, a Thorn clerk who tried to resign. *Disposition as the Crown sees fit.* She's listed in the customs records as exported cargo." Serah heard her own voice go flat and careful, the way it did when she was handling evidence she couldn't bear to hold gently. "A person, Fairweather. Filed as cargo. Under laws your bag of tree-flesh is threatening."

Felix flinched. Actually flinched, shoulders drawing in, and Serah understood suddenly that this person—who had lied to her face in her own office, who had fled through salt marshes while soldiers burned buildings—was afraid of her, and more than afraid: ashamed. Of what? Of the book? Of whatever was coming?

"You're not what I expected," she said.

"What did you expect?"

"Someone harder. Someone who'd offer me money, or a knife, or a speech about freedom." She shook her head. "You look like a clerk who's found an error in a ledger so large it goes all the way down."

"That's exactly what I am," Felix said, and smiled, and the smile was exhausted and real. "Inquisitor Vale. Here's my proposal, and I'll make it only once, and then you must do what your conscience demands, because I'm done asking people to betray themselves. Walk away. Off the record. Report that you lost me at the marshes. Report anything—say I turned into a flock of pigeons; your superiors will believe it, they believe anything the Thorn tells them. Or take the book and me in, and deliver us to Hesse's disposition, and live the rest of your life doing arithmetic in the dark like I did, knowing what the numbers bought."

"And if I refuse both? If I take you in myself, properly? To a magistrate, not to Hesse?"

"Then I'd go," Felix said quietly. "And Odile would still be in the Eye's spire. And the book would sit in a vault next to Maren Ostwick's shipping manifest, and in a month the moon will finish its thirteenth turn, and everything we're all pretending isn't happening will happen without a single honest witness present." They spread their hands. "I'm not asking you to break the law, Vale. The law broke itself years ago; you've read its accounts. I'm asking whether you serve the paper or the promise underneath it."

*The promise underneath it.*

Her mother's kitchen table. The weeping. Nine-year-old Serah, furious and certain, deciding that somewhere above the tanners and the tax collectors there was a rule that even powerful men had to obey, and that she would find it and hold it up like a lamp.

She had found it. It was Appendix C.

* * *

What happened next took perhaps ninety seconds, and Serah would spend years turning it over, and would eventually write it down—not in any official record, never in any official record—but in a small private notebook she kept thereafter, labeled *Discrepancies*, in which she recorded every instance where the law and the promise diverged, and what she chose, and why.

She stood. She drew the warrant from her coat.

Felix stood too, slowly, the way one stands when accepting a sentence.

Serah held the warrant out. Then, at arm's length between them, in the rain, she tore it in half. Then in half again. The vellum was good stock; it resisted; she tore it anyway, and the sound it made was deeply unsatisfying, less like thunder and more like wet bread, and she thought, absurdly, *eleven years, and it tears like lunch.*

"Off the record," she said.

Felix stared at the falling pieces. "Vale—"

"Officially, I pursued a suspect into Cooper's Row and lost them in the rain. Unofficially, I have never been here, you have never shown me anything, and if anyone asks, I tore that warrant because it was damp and I was frustrated." She wiped rain off her face with a sleeve that was past helping. "I want something in return."

"Anything within reason."

"Maren Ostwick. Find out where she went. If she's alive, tell her someone audited the entry." Serah's voice held steady, barely. "If she's dead, remember it. Names get lost in ledgers, Fairweather. I spent eleven years making sure numbers added up. I'd like to spend whatever comes next making sure *names* add up."

Felix looked at her for a long moment, and then did something Serah truly did not expect: they opened the satchel, withdrew a slim volume bound in gray leather, and held it out.

"What is this?"

"A copy of the Thorn's export manifests. Corvane's true ledger, partial. Every name we could recover, including Ostwick's, including dates and destinations." Felix's expression was grim. "It's the worst thing I own. I'm giving it to you because you're the only person I've met in months who reads ledgers the way they should be read—as accusations."

Serah took it. It was heavier than it looked, the way guilty things always are.

"For the record," Felix added, as they pulled their hood up and stepped back into the rain, "you're the first officer of the Crown I've ever met who tore their own warrant instead of tearing someone else's life with it. If any of this ends well—if *anything* ends well—it'll be because of people like you."

"Don't flatter me, Fairweather. I'm still going to arrest you someday. Properly. With a warrant I wrote myself."

"I look forward to it." A pause. The rain softened. "Ask your questions carefully when you do. I suspect you'll like the answers better than the ones you've been given."

Then they were gone, down the lane, footsteps fading toward the river, and Serah Vale stood alone in the doorway of a shuttered chandler's shop with half a warrant soaking in a gutter and a book of names against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

* * *

She walked back to her lodgings the long way, past the Office of Inquiry, its windows lit against the night, clerks inside filing tomorrow's injustices in triplicate. She did not go in. She stood across the street for a long time, watching the lamplight, feeling the torn warrant's absence in her coat like a pulled tooth.

Then she went home, lit a single candle, and opened the gray ledger to the first page.

*Maren Ostwick. Exported, 14th of Thaw-month. Destination: unrecovered.*

Below it, another name. Below that, another. Pages of them. Decades of them. She took out her pen, opened her private notebook to a fresh page, and wrote at the top, in her precise examiner's hand: *Discrepancies, continued.*

Then, because she was who she was—because nine-year-olds in tannery quarters do not become inquisitors to tear up easily—she dipped her pen again and wrote the second line:

*Item one: the moon completes its thirteenth turn in one month. Find out what happens then.*

Outside, far to the east, over the black line of the Weeping Wood, the clouds parted for a moment, and the moon looked down—pale, patient, and very slightly wrong, like a coin someone had shaved—and Serah, glancing up from her page, felt the hair rise on her arms, and wrote one final note before she slept:

*Item two: trust the clerk. The tired ones have read the most.*
