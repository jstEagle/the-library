## 15. The Chapel of Owed Debts

The chapel sat at the crooked end of Candlemaker's Row, wedged between a tannery that smelled of regret and a pie shop that smelled considerably better. It had been a chapel once, in the way that most things in Lowmark had been something once—devoted to a god whose name had worn off the lintel sometime before the current king's grandfather learned to walk. Now it belonged to the Gilded Thorn on paper, which meant it belonged to no one at all.

Felix Fairweather stood at its door in the gray hour before dawn, holding a key that didn't fit, and tried to remember when exactly their life had become the kind of life that involved recruiting.

*Three days ago,* they thought. *Three days ago I was an accountant.*

Well. An accountant with a sword, a prophecy-book grown from the body of a chained god, a stolen memory rattling around their skull like dice in a cup, and a face now worth more dead than alive in three separate jurisdictions. But fundamentally an accountant. There was comfort in that. Accountants knew how to balance books, and Felix intended to balance this one if it took the rest of their unnatural, century-old life.

The lock surrendered to a hairpin and a prayer. Inside, the chapel smelled of dust and old tallow and, faintly, of pies smuggled in through a side window by persons unknown. Rows of pews faced an altar where someone had long ago painted over the god's faded sigil with a cheerful mural of fruit. Felix set the Weeping Wood down on the altar with the care owed to a sleeping animal, then went about lighting candles, because if one was going to commit treason against one's own guildmaster, one ought to do it in flattering light.

They had sent eleven letters. Not letters, precisely—more like debts called in. That was the beauty of having kept the Thorn's ledgers for six years: Felix knew exactly who Corvane had cheated, and by how much, and down to which copper coin. Every entry in those hidden accounts had been a wound. Tonight, each wound would get a chance to become a soldier.

Or, more realistically given the caliber of people available, a very motivated argument.

The first arrived early, as promised, and announced herself by kicking the door open hard enough to rattle the fruit mural.

"You," said Brannick Salt, former quartermaster of the Thorn's Rivergate outpost, "are supposed to be dead."

"Reports of my death are premature and, frankly, flattering." Felix gestured to a pew. "Sit. There's tea. It's terrible. It was all I could afford after my employer put a price on my head."

Brannick was a mountain of a woman gone soft at the edges, with forearms tattooed in inventory tallies—she'd inked her stock counts directly onto her skin for years because she claimed paper lied. She lowered herself into the pew with the groan of a ship changing course, accepted the tea, drank it, and grimaced with what Felix chose to interpret as affection.

"Corvane says you stole from him."

"Corvane says a great many things. Corvane also says he pays his people fairly." Felix slid a folded page across the pew. "Page forty-one of his private ledger. Look at the entry for the Rivergate tithe. The one where he docked your crew half a season's share for 'shrinkage' that never existed, and routed it to an account marked only with a symbol."

Brannick read. Her jaw did something complicated. "That's my mark."

"It is."

"He told me the shrinkage came out of *my* pay because I'd signed off on the manifest." Her voice had gone quiet in the dangerous way. "I worked double shifts for eight months to cover it. My daughter thought I'd been gambling."

"I know. I entered the correction myself. He made me do it in red ink so it wouldn't show in the annual audit." Felix watched the anger settle into her like ballast. "I'm not here to apologize on his behalf. I'm here because you're owed, and I intend to see you paid."

"Paid how?"

"Ask me again in an hour, when everyone's here, and I'll only have to say it once."

* * *

They came in ones and twos as the sky lightened over Candlemaker's Row, each one greeted at the door by Felix with a name, a grievance, and a cup of terrible tea.

Marrow Quill, the guild's smallest forger and its most nervous, who had spent four years copying documents under threat and discovered via Felix's letter that Corvane had been holding her sister's debt papers as collateral against her loyalty—and that the debt had been paid twice over by work she'd already done.

Old Tam Vessing—no relation to anyone, he insisted loudly—who had driven carts for the Thorn for thirty years until his knee gave out, and whose pension had been quietly struck from the books the same week Felix had been ordered to strike it. Felix had refused, once, in writing. It was the only act of rebellion in six years of ledgers, and it had cost them a week's pay and earned them a lecture on discretion. Tam arrived leaning on his cart whip like a halberd and sat in the front row like a man attending his own vindication.

The Twins—Hesh and Hosh, no surnames, professional finders of things that did not wish to be found—who arrived through the side window instead of the door on principle, and were deeply offended to discover the pies they'd smelled had been a figment of Hosh's optimism.

And last, slipping in just as Felix closed the shutters, came Wren Aldous, junior archivist, nineteen years old, still wearing her nightshirt under a coat three sizes too big, clutching a satchel of documents to her chest like a shield.

"They burned the Sable Street outpost," she said, breathless. "Last night. Inquisitors. I got out with the C-tier files but the B-tier—" She stopped, saw the room full of faces, and went pink. "Oh. Is this a meeting? Am I early?"

"You're right on time," Felix said, and meant it in a way that surprised them both.

Eleven souls. Not an army. Barely a decent dinner party. But every one of them had been bled by the same ledger, and Felix had learned long ago that nothing in this world moved faster than a person shown the exact line where they'd been robbed.

* * *

Felix stood at the altar beside the Weeping Wood, and told them almost everything.

Not everything—the thirteenth turn, the true nature of the seal, the shape of what Felix had been a hundred years ago. That confession could wait for a braver hour, or perhaps never; some weights you carry so others don't have to. But they told them enough: that Corvane had been selling pieces of a imprisoned god's power for decades. That the Crown's warrant was real but rotten, written to protect the very trafficking it claimed to hunt. That the Order of the Unblinking Eye held Odile Marchetti in the Spire, and that she had been taken covering Felix's escape.

"And Odile," Marrow said softly, "sold a memory of you. Years ago. To feed her sister."

"She did." Felix kept their voice level. "I've since recovered it. I know what she gave up and why. Whatever anger I'm entitled to, she paid for it in advance, and then she paid again in that cell. So we're going to get her back."

Hesh raised a hand. "Question. Are we criminals now? Because I feel like we're criminals now, and I want to be clear on the terms."

"We're creditors," Felix said. "There's a difference. Criminals take what isn't theirs. We're collecting what is."

"That's exactly what a criminal would say," Hosh observed.

"It's also exactly what a creditor would say," Felix agreed, "which is why the distinction requires excellent bookkeeping. Fortunately, you have me."

A ripple of laughter—not much of one, tired and thin, but real. Old Tam snorted loud enough to startle the candles.

Then Brannick stood, arms crossed, tallies flexing on her forearms. "Say it plain, Keeper. What do you want from us?"

The title landed strangely. *Keeper.* They hadn't asked to be called anything yet. But the room had already decided, the way rooms do, and Felix found—to their considerable alarm—that they didn't hate it.

"I want the Thorn back," Felix said. "Not Corvane's Thorn. The one it was always supposed to be. A guild that trades in impossible things honestly—which sounds like a contradiction, but so does most of the world. Corvane turned our ledgers into a weapon pointed at everyone beneath him. I want to turn them back into what they should be: a record. Of what we owe, and what we're owed. Open to audit. Any member, any time."

"You want to reform the guild," said Wren, "in the middle of a manhunt."

"I want to survive the manhunt *as* the reformed guild. One thing at a time. First item on the agenda—" Felix touched the Weeping Wood, and felt the faint warmth of it answer through their palm, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. "We need allies with sharper edges than ours. And there is a debt in this city large enough to buy any edge we need."

* * *

It was Marrow, of course, who spotted it. Forgers read documents the way hawks read fields—everything, all at once, hunting motion.

She had spread Felix's transcriptions across two pews and gone through them twice while the others argued logistics. Now she held up a page with one finger. "This. The salt-debt."

Felix crossed to her. "What of it?"

"The debt owed in shadows. From the old accounts—you told us about it, the contract where a client paid the Thorn in shadow instead of coin." Her finger traced the notation. "You said the debt was never collected. Look at the counterparty."

Felix looked. And felt the floor of the world tilt gently, the way it had been tilting for a month now, ever-learning new angles.

The counterparty's seal was an eye, open, unblinking, rendered in ink that still shimmered faintly after decades—as though the ink itself were afraid to look away first.

"The Order," Felix said slowly. "The Order of the Unblinking Eye owes the Gilded Thorn a debt. A debt payable in shadows."

"Payable in shadows," Marrow confirmed. "Which means—" She swallowed. "Which means somewhere in that Spire of theirs, there's a piece of paper that says they have to give one back."

The chapel went quiet. Even Hesh and Hosh stopped arguing about pies.

"A debt in shadows," Old Tam said at last, squinting. "What's that mean, practical-like?"

"It means," Felix said, and the plan assembled itself in their mind with the clean click of figures summing correctly for the first time in weeks, "that the Order has a vault where they keep things that used to belong to other people. Memories. Names. Shadows. And it means that when we walk through their front door to collect Odile, we won't be thieves breaking in." They smiled, and felt the smile fit their face better than any expression had in years. "We'll be bailiffs, executing a lawful collection on behalf of the Gilded Thorn. With documentation."

Wren raised her hand as if in class. "Won't they just... deny the debt exists?"

"They can try." Felix lifted the transcription, and beneath it, the original page Marrow had copied it from—an original pulled from Corvane's own hidden ledger, sealed with the guild's oldest stamp. "But this is a Thorn instrument, countersigned by a guildmaster, and the Order's whole religion is that contracts are sacred. Their Grandmaster built his order on keeping oaths. He cannot refuse a properly executed claim without breaking the one law he's spent a century pretending to uphold."

"His conviction," Brannick said slowly, "is our crowbar."

"His conviction," Felix agreed, "is our crowbar."

Hesh leaned over to Hosh. "I like her," he whispered, audibly.

"She's terrifying," Hosh whispered back, also audibly. "I like her too."

* * *

They worked through the morning, and by noon the chapel of owed debts had become something else entirely—a war room, though nobody used the word. Maps on pews. Wren cataloguing files by candle stub. Tam telling anyone who'd listen about the Spire's cart deliveries in the old days ("Nobody searches a cheese cart, boy, nobody *ever* searches a cheese cart"). Marrow forging travel writs with the serene concentration of a woman finally doing honest work dishonestly.

And Felix stood among it, ostensibly supervising, actually just—watching.

Six years behind a desk. Six years of entries and corrections and red ink, of knowing everyone's debts and no one's names beyond what the payroll required. They had told themselves it was professionalism. It had been fear, dressed neatly. A ledger couldn't leave you. A ledger couldn't sell a piece of you to pay for its sister. A ledger couldn't look at you across a prison cell and say *go, I'll hold the door*—

"Keeper." Brannick appeared at their elbow with fresh tea, equally terrible. "You've got the look."

"What look?"

"The look of a person doing sums they don't like the total of." She handed over the cup. "Whatever you're counting, stop. Count what's in the room instead. Eleven people who showed up. Nobody paid them. Nobody threatened them. They came because a stranger wrote them a letter that said *you were wronged*, and they believed it." She shrugged her mountainous shoulders. "In my experience, that's rarer than magic."

Felix looked out at the chapel—at Tam demonstrating cheese-cart concealment with a hymnal and a loaf, at the Twins volunteering for reconnaissance in exchange for future pie considerations, at Wren falling asleep upright over her files and jerking awake with tremendous dignity.

Belonging, they had always assumed, was something recorded. A name on a roster. A mark in a margin, proof that you existed in someone else's accounting. They had spent a century—two lives, two names—looking for the page where they were written down.

And here was the joke of it, bitter and warm at once: the page had never existed. No ledger had ever held them. They had simply walked into a room of discarded people, and been counted anyway.

"Well?" Brannick prompted.

"Tea's terrible," Felix said. "Pour me another."

* * *

Dusk came early that evening, or seemed to. Felix pinned the map of the Spire district to the fruit mural and tapped the eastern wall.

"The Order expects thieves. Thieves come at night, over walls, quietly. So that's exactly what we won't do." They looked around the circle of faces—scared, stubborn, and present, every single one. "Tomorrow, midday, we walk up to the Spire's gate in broad daylight, in guild colors, with a writ of collection, and we demand our property. All of it. Including Odile Marchetti."

"And when they laugh at us?" Wren asked.

"Then," said Felix, "we show them the seal, and we watch a hundred years of certainty hit a wall it can't argue with." They paused, and added—because the Weeping Wood had warmed against their hip all day, pulsing faster since sundown, urgent as a second heart—"And if I'm wrong about any of it, we improvise. I'm told the Twins are good at that."

"We're *excellent* at that," said Hesh.

"We've never once been caught," said Hosh.

"Because we run immediately," said Hesh.

"Speed is a skill," said Hosh.

Outside, over the crooked rooftops of Candlemaker's Row, the moon rose—and Felix's breath caught, because even to the naked eye, even to eleven pairs of ordinary eyes looking up through the chapel windows, it hung wrong. Too full. Too slow. Turning, very slightly, against its ancient habit, like a wheel deciding whether to roll forward or come loose.

One month remained on the thirteenth turn. Somewhere beneath a forest, something vast was running out of time. And tomorrow, God willing and the ledgers balanced, the Gilded Thorn would come to collect.
