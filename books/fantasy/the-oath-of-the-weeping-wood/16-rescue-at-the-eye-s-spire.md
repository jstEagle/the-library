## 16. Rescue at the Eye's Spire

The Spire of the Unblinking Eye rose out of the Lantern District like a needle someone had driven through the city's eye in a fit of temper. It was pale stone, windowless on its lower floors, and at its crown burned a single lamp that never went out—the famous Lidless Light, which the Order claimed could see through stone, lies, and the souls of men.

"It can't actually do that last one," said Wren Halloway, our astronomer, squinting up at it from beneath a borrowed fishwife's shawl. "I've read their own treatises. The light is mostly for show. Very good for show, mind you. Terrible for actual seeing."

"Comforting," muttered Brack, a former Thorn enforcer with forearms like ship's cable and a face that suggested he'd been hit by most of them. He was dressed as a wine merchant, which fooled no one, least of all himself. He kept tugging at his collar as if the disguise were strangling him.

We crouched in the shadow of a chandlery across the square, six of us pressed between barrels of tallow, watching guards circle the spire's base in slow, patient orbits. I had counted eleven rotations before Brack asked me to stop counting out loud.

"The plan," I whispered, "one more time."

Wren sighed the sigh of a woman surrounded by amateurs, which was unfair, since she had designed the plan herself and simply enjoyed reciting it. "The Eye rotates its watch at the turn of every bell. During the changeover there are forty seconds when the ground-floor door is watched by no one but old Tammas, who owes the Thorn a debt from 1847 and has been avoiding collection ever since." She smiled sweetly at Brack. "Until tonight."

"I'm not going to hurt him," Brack rumbled.

"You're going to stand next to him," she corrected, "which is worse."

* * *

I want to say something noble here about how we went in for Odile and only Odile. That would be tidy, and tidiness is a kind of lie, and I had lately developed strong opinions about lies.

We went in for Odile first. But we also went in because the Eye's archives held the ledger of everything Corvane had sold them—every vial of stolen godlight, every name, including, very possibly, mine—and because Serah Vale's soldiers would reach this district within two days and burn whatever they found, warrant or no warrant. If we didn't take the proof tonight, it would be ash by week's end.

So: Odile, the ledger, and out. In that order, if the gods loved us. They didn't, historically. But I was told the odds improved when you stopped waiting for them and started moving.

Tammas, the door-watch, was seventy if he was a day, and when Brack loomed up beside him in the doorway, the old man looked at him for a long moment and then said, with tremendous weariness, "It's about the debt, isn't it."

"It's about the debt," Brack agreed.

"I always meant to pay it." Tammas stepped aside and held the door. "Mind the third stair. It screams."

* * *

Inside, the spire smelled of cold incense and hot iron—a strange combination, like a church that also sharpened knives. We moved through corridors lit by the Lidless Light bleeding down through shafts in the ceiling, so that everything was washed in a pale gold that made even the shadows look official.

Wren led us to the archive with the confidence of someone who had memorized the building from stolen floor-plans and would be insufferable about it later. She was right about everything except the lock, which turned out to be a prayer-lock: a mechanism that opened only for a spoken verse of the Order's liturgy, and shrieked otherwise.

"A prayer-lock," she hissed, kneeling before it, her composure cracking for the first time. "Who locks an archive with *prayer*?"

"People who believe," I said, and knelt beside her.

Here is a thing I had learned in the past weeks, though I had no memory of learning it the first time, a century ago: oaths have weight. Not metaphorically. A vow spoken truly leaves a residue, the way footsteps leave prints in snow, and some locks—old ones, honest ones—can feel the shape of a promise in a voice.

I was still sworn to Vess. Broken in my heart, perhaps, but unbroken on my tongue; I had never renounced it aloud. And Vess was the god these people had imprisoned. Their liturgy was built on His silence. Every hymn of the Unblinking Eye was, at its root, a song about my god.

I spoke the opening verse of their vigil-hymn—I had heard it sung outside our safehouses often enough—and put behind it every ounce of the oath that lived in my chest.

The lock opened like a flower deciding to trust the sun.

"That," whispered Wren, staring at me, "is either the bravest or stupidest thing I've ever seen."

"Both," I said. "It runs in my family." I did not know yet how literally true that was. Some part of me suspected.

* * *

Odile was not in a cell. She was in a *reading room*, which was somehow worse.

They had her seated at a table under a lamp, a cup of untouched tea at her elbow, and an elderly Brother of the Eye reading aloud to her from a slim volume. When we burst in—Brack first, then me, then Wren and the others—the Brother didn't even flinch. He simply closed his book and folded his hands.

"She's been telling us the most interesting stories," he said pleasantly. "About a jar of last words. About debts paid in shadows. About a certain ledger-keeper who once—" he glanced at Odile with genuine warmth, "—well. She protects you even now, you know. Even tired. Even frightened. Remarkable loyalty, in a woman who sold your memory to us."

"Shut up," said Odile. Her voice was hoarse but level. Her wrists were chained to the table—not cruelly, the Eye preferred comfort to cruelty, which was its own kind of obscenity—but her eyes found mine and they were fierce and ashamed and alive. "Felix. The ledger. Third shelf, grey spine. Don't read it here. Don't read it *anywhere* near him."

"Near me?" The Brother chuckled. "Child, I'm ninety-one years old. What could I possibly—"

The Grandmaster came through the wall.

Not through the door. Through the wall—stone parting around him like water around a keel, silently, without dust, which is the detail my mind snagged on afterward, again and again: *no dust.* He was tall and spare and his eyes were the pale grey of the sky before snow, and he moved like a man who had been alive for a very long time and had gotten bored of hurrying.

He looked at me. Only me. The others might as well have been furniture.

"So," he said. "The thirteenth witness comes to visit the prison."

Behind me, Wren made a small sound. Brack shifted his weight, calculating, as enforcers do, the distance to the nearest exit and the nearest throat.

"You know what I am," I said.

"I knew your predecessor. I knew your *first* self, girl." He said it without malice, which was the terrible part. There was no gloating in him, no hunger. There was only duty, worn smooth as a riverstone by two hundred years of holding it. "I sealed your god with my own hands because if I hadn't, He would have burned the world to save one city. You think I'm your villain. I am the world's scar tissue. And scars do not apologize for existing."

"The world didn't ask to be saved that way," I said.

"No," he agreed, and something ancient and exhausted flickered across his face. "It didn't."

For one absurd moment we stood there, jailer and thief, almost understanding each other, while my friends held their breath and Odile pulled quietly, patiently, at a chain-link that was, I noticed, already nearly sawed through—because of course she had been working on it the entire time, tea untouched, stories flowing, keeping every eye in the room on the conversation.

Then the Grandmaster raised one hand, and the lamp above the table guttered, and he said, gently: "You cannot take what is kept here. I am sorry. I have liked knowing you exist."

* * *

What happened next, I will set down plainly, because plainness is all I have left to offer anyone.

Brack threw a chair through the window. This sounds like a joke. It was tactics—Wren's tactics—the window faced the canal, and the canal was our exit, and a man built like Brack does not need doors when he has momentum. The Brother dove for an alarm-cord. Wren tripped him with a fishwife's basket, which she had kept, shawl and all, on the grounds that a good prop earns its keep twice.

And Odile stood up, chain broken, and put herself between me and the Grandmaster.

"Run," she said.

"I'm not leaving you twice."

"You're not leaving me. I'm leaving *with* you, you sentimental idiot, but *run first*—"

The Grandmaster reached into his coat and drew out a vial.

I knew it instantly, the way you know a face in a crowd that you've dreamed about. Pale light, thick as honey, moving inside the glass with a slowness that suggested it was aware of being watched. A fragment of Vess. Stolen, decanted, bottled by Corvane's people and sold upward until it reached the one man mad enough—or devoted enough—to drink it.

"You wouldn't," Odile breathed. "Not here. Not over the district—"

"The district is acceptable losses," the Grandmaster said, and drank.

* * *

There are no adequate words for watching a mortal body try to hold a piece of a god. I have since tried to write them, in other pages, and failed. What I can tell you is that the Grandmaster screamed—not in pain, exactly, but in the manner of a dam screaming, structural, geological—and that the light came out of him the way dawn comes over mountains, from everywhere at once.

We ran. Gods forgive me, we ran, and it was the right choice, and I will carry it anyway.

The corridor behind us filled with white. Stone did not burn; stone *unhappened*. Floors became suggestions. Somewhere below, the great bell began to toll—not rung, but struck by the shockwave itself, a single deep note that I felt in my teeth and my bones and, I am quite sure, in the place where my oath lived, because something in me answered it. Something old. Something that recognized its own blood in the blast.

"*Vess,*" I gasped, running, and didn't know if I was praying or naming the weapon.

We went out the window. All of us. Brack caught Wren mid-fall with a grunt that would have been funny any other day; Odile grabbed my hand as the canal took us, cold and black and mercifully solid, and we swam and waded and staggered to the far bank, and turned, and looked back at what we had done.

Half the Lantern District was gone.

Not burning. Gone. A perfect hemisphere of absence where the spire's lower floors had been, and beyond it a ragged bite taken out of the chandleries, the tenements, the little tea-shops where dockworkers warmed their hands at dusk. Dust hung in the air like a held breath. Out of that dust came sounds—crying, calling, the ordinary terrible music of a neighborhood discovering what it had lost.

The Grandmaster stood on a shard of his own spire, floating, or nearly floating, haloed in light that was eating him even as he wielded it. He looked down at the ruin. Even from the canal I saw his expression, and it was not triumph.

It was the face of a man who had guarded a prison for two centuries and had just, in one desperate moment, proven exactly why the prison was necessary.

* * *

We regrouped in a cooper's yard three streets away, soaked, coughing dust, counting each other like misers counting coin. All present. Odile's hands were shaking; Brack wrapped her in a tarp without a word. Wren sat down abruptly on a barrel and put her head between her knees.

"We got her out," Brack said finally. "And the ledger. Say it. Someone say it, so the night isn't all loss."

"We got her out," I said. "We got the ledger."

"And half a district paid for it." Odile's voice cracked on the word *district*. She was looking back toward the smoke. "People who never touched a ledger. People who never sold anything but fish and candles."

Nobody answered, because there was nothing to say that wasn't a lie.

I opened the grey-spined ledger there in the cooper's yard, by the light of a distant fire, because I could not wait, because the dead deserved to know what had killed them. Page after page of Corvane's sales: grief in quarter-measures, years skimmed off orphans' lifespans, and godlight—godlight, godlight—decanted from a sealed source beneath the Weeping Wood, batch after batch after batch, decades of it. And in the margins, in Corvane's neat clerk-hand, a note beside the earliest entry:

*Source weakens faster than projected. Thirteenth turn approaches. Harvest accordingly.*

Harvest accordingly.

The moon above the smoke was three-quarters full and climbing toward its impossible thirteenth turn. One month. Perhaps less, now—because I understood, sitting in the dark among barrels, soaked to the bone with my friends breathing around me and a city groaning in the distance, that the Grandmaster's stolen burst had not weakened the seal.

It had *cracked* it. Every vial ever sold, every theft, every year of Corvane's harvesting—we had all been digging at the same wall from opposite sides, and tonight the wall had felt it.

If we freed Vess now, with the prison already fractured and the god's power loose in bottles and bitter old men, we would not free a witness. We would free a wildfire.

And if we waited—if we let the thirteenth turn pass with the seal intact—Vess would die in the dark, and everything He was would go out of the world forever, and the ledgers would balance, and the Gilded Thorn would go on trading in impossible things, and somewhere in an unmarked column I would remain a line item: *one knight, forgotten, per contract.*

I closed the book.

"Felix?" Odile said. "You've got your thinking face. I hate your thinking face."

"There's no clean way out of this," I told her. "None. Whatever we choose, we choose it bloody."

Around me, in the firelight and the smoke-smell, my ragged, cheated, borrowed family waited to hear the rest—and I realized I was going to have to tell them everything, and ask them to choose anyway.

That conversation, though. That came at dawn.

First we had a city to dig out.
