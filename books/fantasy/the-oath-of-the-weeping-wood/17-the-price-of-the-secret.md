## 17. The Price of the Secret

The problem with hiding in a ruined chapel full of people who owed you their grievances was that eventually they wanted to talk to you.

Felix had discovered this within an hour of arriving at the Chapel of Owed Debts, a leaning stone relic half-swallowed by ivy on the edge of the Salt Marshes, where the company had gathered like orphaned kittens finding the same warm hearth. There were eleven of them now. Eleven. Felix kept counting, as if the number might behave better the fourth time.

There was Marta Quickfingers, who had once sold a bottled grief back to its own weeping owner and been cast out for the audacity of refunding it. There was Brother Halloway, a defrocked priest of the Unblinking Eye who had left the Order over what he called "a difference of opinion regarding the definition of *heresy*"—namely, that his was asking questions. There were the twins, Pip and Poppy Thornbury, who had run the Thorn's night ledger and knew precisely which accounts Corvane had cooked, because they had done the cooking. There was old Sef, who had guarded the archive for forty years and never once been told what he was guarding.

And there was Odile, freed from the Eye's spire with a bruise blooming along her jaw like a dark flower, sitting by the fire with her arms crossed and her eyes fixed on Felix with the particular patience of someone waiting for you to say the thing you are very obviously not saying.

"You're doing it again," she said.

"Doing what?"

"The face. The one where you've read something terrible and you've decided that carrying it alone is somehow noble instead of just exhausting."

"I don't have a face for that."

"You have three faces for that. I've catalogued them. I catalogue things; you may recall it's why I'm in this mess." She nudged a stool toward him with her boot. "Sit. Talk."

Felix sat. He did not talk. But he held the Weeping Wood on his lap—a slab of living tree-flesh bound in bark, its pages grown rather than written—and let his fingers rest on the newest growth, the pale ring near the center where the marginalia lived.

He had spent two days deciphering it. Two days of cross-referencing the Wood's root-script against the astronomer-guild's charts against the fragment of Vess's power that still hummed faintly under his skin like a struck bell refusing to finish ringing. And he had arrived, late last night, at the answer. Both answers. The two answers that could not coexist.

*The seal is weakening,* the trees said. *The thirteenth turn completes in twenty-two days. When it does, the prison opens.*

*The prison opens from the outside only while the moon turns. After it completes, the god inside must open it—or die with it.*

But there was a third line, older, carved so deep into the heartwood that Felix had needed candlelight held at an angle to catch it, and it was this line that had kept him awake until dawn:

*A seal broken early breaks differently. What wakes before its time wakes hungry. What wakes hungry remembers the war.*

That was the shape of it. That was the terrible arithmetic of it.

Free Vess now—while the thirteenth turn was still turning—and the god would come out of a century of imprisonment the way a drowning man comes out of water: gasping, grasping, remembering every hand that had held him under. Vess would wake with Corvane's thefts in his awareness and the Grandmaster's face burned into a century of memory, and gods did not, as a rule, respond to injustice with strongly worded ledgers. Vess would wake to war. The ancient war, the one that had salted half the old maps, the one whose battlefields Felix had walked through in borrowed memories—the mud, the screaming, the sky full of wings that had no business existing.

Or wait. Wait the twenty-two days. Let the turn complete. And then the prison would close around a dying god forever, because a seal that finished turning sealed *permanently*, and everything Felix had learned about Vess—the quiet kindness of the nightly ritual, the way the god's silence had felt less like absence than like someone pressing a hand over their own mouth—suggested that Vess would choose to die rather than break out wrong. Would choose it politely. Would choose it the way Vess did everything: silently, so as not to trouble anyone.

Twenty-two days to commit deicide or watch it happen. Twenty-two days, and no clean choice anywhere in the arithmetic.

"Felix." Odile's voice, gentler now. "Whatever it is—you're doing the thing where your jaw does the clench. The accounting clench. You get it when the numbers don't balance."

"They don't," Felix said quietly. "The numbers. They don't balance. For the first time in my career, there is no entry that makes them balance."

Odile went still. Across the chapel, Pip dropped a spoon. Somewhere in the rafters, one of Sef's pigeons cooed with what Felix chose to interpret as sympathy.

"That bad?" Odile asked.

"Worse. It's not even a question of courage. Courage would be easy. This is—" Felix searched for the word and hated all the candidates. "This is a choice between ending the world quickly or letting something good die slowly. And either way, I'm the one holding the pen."

* * *

The trouble with secrets, Felix reflected later that evening while the company argued cheerfully about dinner logistics—as though the world were not balanced on the head of a pin above them—was that they grew heavier in direct proportion to how much you loved the people you were keeping them from.

Marta found him at the well behind the chapel, drawing water in the last grey light. She leaned against the stones and watched him work without offering to help, which was, in Marta's language, a profound courtesy.

"Halloway says the moon's off-schedule," she said. "He checks it every night like it owes him money. Says it'll do something called the thirteenth turn. Says that's bad news wearing a hat."

"Halloway talks too much."

"Halloway's frightened. Frightened men talk. So do guilty ones." She tilted her head at him. "You look like both, Keeper."

Felix hauled the bucket up and set it down harder than necessary. Water slopped over his boots. "If I told you something," he said, "something that would make everything we're doing more dangerous—not less, *more*—would you want to know?"

Marta considered this with the seriousness she usually reserved for lock mechanisms. "Depends. Would knowing help me help you?"

"It might. Or it might make you run. Or it might make you do something brave and stupid and dead."

"Then tell me on the day knowing helps. Until then, keep it. That's what a keeper's for, isn't it? Keeping?" She pushed off the wall and paused beside him. "But Felix—if the thing you're keeping starts keeping *you*, that's when you hand it off. Even half of it. Even badly. Nobody carries a whole secret alone and comes out the other side a person. I've seen it happen. Well. I've seen the part where they stop coming out the other side."

She left him with the bucket and the darkening sky and the moon rising fat and pale over the marshes, already visibly wrong, already a hair ahead of where the charts said it should be.

Felix looked up at it for a long time.

*What wakes before its time wakes hungry.*

*The god dies with the moon.*

Somewhere between those two sentences, standing at the well with cold feet and colder certainty, Felix Fairweather understood that this was the true price of the secret. Not the risk. Not even the choice. The price was that the secret would eat him alive before either outcome arrived, because a man who carried a forked prophecy alone became two men, and neither of them slept.

* * *

At midnight, Felix took the Wood to the chapel's broken altar and tried, one more time, to find the loophole. It was habit. It was profession. Somewhere in every impossible account there was always a misfiled entry, a double-charged debt, a shadow paid twice for one withdrawal. The Gilded Thorn had taught him that. Corvane had taught him that, in the cruelest possible classroom.

He read the deep-carved lines until the candles guttered. He read the marginalia until his eyes ached. He pressed his palm flat against the heartwood and felt the faint pulse beneath it, slow as a sleeping heartbeat, slower now—he was almost sure it was slower now—like something conserving its final strength.

"You'd wait, wouldn't you," Felix murmured to it. "If it were you choosing. You'd let the moon close over you like water and you wouldn't make a sound, and a hundred years from now some guild clerk would list you under *assets, unrecovered*."

The pulse did not answer. Of course it didn't. Silence was the whole of their relationship, a hundred nights of it, and Felix had spent most of those nights believing the silence was abandonment. Now he knew it was imprisonment, and somehow the silence was worse, because now he knew what was being said inside it.

"You didn't fight," Felix said. "In the war. That's why they sealed you. You refused, and refusal was treason, and treason got you buried alive in your own grove." His thumb traced the image of the hooded figure with the ledger—himself, a century ago, unaged, sworn. "And now the only person who can free you has to decide whether freeing you kills everyone else. Which means your mercy, the mercy that got you imprisoned, is going to be the thing that—" His voice caught. He was surprised by how much it caught.

Behind him, a floorboard creaked.

Brother Halloway stood in the doorway of the nave, wrapped in a blanket, looking less like a defrocked inquisitor and more like a grandfather who had gotten up for water and found the house on fire. In his hands, incongruously, was a plate of bread and hard cheese.

"I wasn't eavesdropping," Halloway said, which was what eavesdroppers always said, and then, with the honesty that had gotten him expelled from the Order: "I was absolutely eavesdropping. Old habit. I brought bread."

Felix stared at him.

Halloway set the plate on a pew and sat down on the step below the altar, arranging his blanket with tremendous dignity. "I spent thirty years in the Eye," he said. "Do you know what the Grandmaster told us, the first week of every novice class? He told us that the hardest duty of the faithful is not vigilance. Vigilance is easy; fear does it for you. The hardest duty is deciding *when to speak*. Every heretic I ever hunted began as someone who knew a truth too heavy to carry and too dangerous to share, and chose wrong." He nodded at the Wood. "That's a heavy one."

"You don't know what it is."

"No. But I know the thirteenth turn is coming, and I know our dear Guildmaster sold pieces of a god for decades, and I know you've been walking around for two days looking like a man doing sums that end in funerals." Halloway tore the bread in half and held one piece out. "So here is my professional assessment, offered freely, worth exactly what you paid for it: whatever the choice is, you cannot make it alone. Not because you lack wisdom. Because a choice made alone belongs to one person, and this one will need to belong to all of us, whichever way it falls. If we wake a hungry god, we should all be guilty together. If we let him die, we should all have to live with it together. That's not strategy, Keeper. That's just what a family is *for*."

The word landed in Felix's chest like a key turning in a lock he hadn't known was locked.

Family. The guild had been that, once, or he had believed it was, right up until Corvane's hidden ledger proved the family had been fattening him for slaughter. But this—this ragged congregation of the cheated, the exiled, the refunded—this was something else. Something chosen. And chosen things, Felix was beginning to understand, came with obligations that sworn oaths never demanded: honesty being chief among them.

"I can't tell you yet," Felix said. "Two days. Let me be sure. Let me check the astronomer's figures one more time, and the seal-timings, and—" He stopped himself. Laughed, briefly and without much humor. "Listen to me. Auditing my own apocalypse. Looking for the rounding error that saves everyone."

"Accountants make excellent conspirators," Halloway said comfortably. "We're used to the truth being ugly and the books needing to balance anyway." He stood, gathered his blanket, and paused at the door. "Two days, then. But Felix—when you tell them? Tell them everything. All of it. Including the part about who you were." His old inquisitor's eyes, for one unguarded moment, were kind. "People forgive the truth far more readily than they forgive being managed. I know. Managing people was my entire career, and look where it got me: eating bread with mercenaries in a haunted chapel, happier than I've been in thirty years."

He shuffled out. Felix sat alone with the Wood, the bread, and the moonlight pooling through the broken roof like spilled silver.

* * *

On the second morning, Felix woke before dawn with the answer to a question he hadn't consciously asked, which was how the conversation would go. Not whether to tell them—that battle was lost, had perhaps been lost the moment Halloway said *family*—but how. And the answer, arriving with the clarity that only sleepless dread can provide, was: all of it. Everything. The Wood, the seal, the forked choice, the stolen memories, the hooded figure in the century-old carving, the name that wasn't really Felix Fairweather's at all.

Because Odile had sold a memory of him once, to ransom her sister, and had confessed it weeping, and he had forgiven her—had meant it, truly—and what kind of forgiveness was his if it came with a reserve clause?

Because Vale had let him go off the record, betting her conscience on his honesty, and debts in shadows were not the only ones that came due.

Because the company had marched through blockade and fire for him, eleven strangers stitched into something like kin, and kin deserved the truth even when—especially when—the truth was that there was no good option waiting at the end of the road.

Felix dressed in the dark. He took the Weeping Wood down from the altar and carried it into the main hall, where the embers of last night's fire still breathed, and set it on the long trestle table with a care that felt like setting down something explosive. Which, in every sense that mattered, it was.

One by one, the company stirred. Sef first, who had slept in chairs since 1743 and considered beds a decadence. Then the twins, whispering. Then Marta, then Halloway, then the rest, drawn by some instinct common to people who have lived by their wits—that quality in the air, that pressure drop before weather.

Odile came last. She looked at the Wood on the table, then at Felix's face, and something in her expression shifted from worry to readiness, the way it had before every job they'd ever run together.

"All right, Keeper," she said quietly. "Whatever it is—we're listening."

Felix put both hands flat on the table, on either side of the book that was also a god's body, and began:

"My name isn't Felix Fairweather."

Outside, over the salt marshes, the moon hung low and swollen and wrong, one turn from finishing what a century had begun—and inside, in the ember-light of a borrowed chapel, eleven people leaned in to hear the whole of a secret no one should have carried alone.

It was, Felix thought wildly, rather like opening the ledgers for public audit.

He only prayed the accounts would survive inspection.
