## 18. Confession Before the Company

The Chapel of Owed Debts had never been a chapel. It had been a counting-house once, then a granary, then a place where a man named Hobb stored smuggled cheese until the Crown seized it and forgot about it entirely. The cheese smell had never quite left. Felix had come to think of it as the building's honest incense.

Tonight it was full of people who should not have trusted each other, sitting on crates that smelled faintly of gouda, waiting for Felix Fairweather to speak.

There were eleven of them, which felt significant in a way Felix couldn't yet explain. Maren the salt-witch, whose contract with the Thorn had been rewritten twice without her knowledge until she owed Corvane more than she'd ever earned. The twins, Brack and Bess, debt-collectors turned fugitives because they'd refused to collect a debt from a dying child. Old Tam, who had sold thirty years of his own memory to pay his wife's medical debts and now could not remember her face—only that he'd loved her, the way you remember warmth after the fire goes out. And others, nine more, each carrying a ledger-entry they'd never agreed to.

And Odile, freed three days ago from the Eye's spire, sitting closest to the door with her arms crossed and a bruise going yellow-green along her jaw, looking at Felix like a woman deciding exactly how much of a liar she was prepared to forgive.

Felix set the Weeping Wood on the crate before them all.

It looked smaller than it had in the archive, smaller than it had pressed against their chest through three days of flight through the salt marshes. A book grown from tree-flesh, bark for covers, pages that were leaves pressed thin as vellum, veins running through them like script. Even now, closed, it wept—a slow bead of sap gathering at its spine and falling onto the crate, where it hissed faintly, as though the wood resented being indoors.

"You all came here," Felix said, "because the Thorn cheated you. Because I told you the ledgers could prove it, and together we could make Corvane answer for it." They paused. "That was true. But it wasn't all of it. And if I ask you to walk into what comes next without hearing everything, then I'm just another guildmaster keeping a hidden account."

Maren shifted. "Keeper," she said—the name had stuck within a day, and Felix had not corrected anyone—"if this is the speech where you tell us it's dangerous and we should go home, most of us haven't got homes."

"It's the speech where I tell you what we're actually walking toward. Then you decide." Felix took a breath. "All of you. Together. That's the whole point of tonight."

* * *

They started, as they had learned to start, with the small lies.

"The Thorn trades in impossible things. You know that. Bottled grief. Borrowed years. Names not yet spoken." Felix touched the Wood's cover, and the bark was warm under their fingers, warmer than wood had any right to be in an unheated granary. "But there is one impossible thing the Thorn has been trading in longer than any of us have been alive. Pieces of a god."

They told it plainly, as plainly as they could. How Vess, the silent god—the god of oaths and witnesses, the god every sworn mercenary in the realm carried somewhere behind the breastbone—had refused to fight in the ancient war between gods and mortals. Had laid down their divinity rather than take a side. How the other powers had not rewarded mercy; how a mortal general, the man who would found the Order of the Unblinking Eye, had sealed Vess beneath a grove of trees and let the world forget. How the grove had become a book. How the Gilded Thorn, generation after generation of guildmasters, had drilled thin channels into the prison and sold what seeped out—a whisper of strength here, a shard of stillness there—to buyers who never asked why the goods worked so well.

How Felix's own contract, the one that had bound them since they were nineteen years old and very good with a blade and very bad at belonging anywhere, had been drafted specifically to keep them ignorant.

"Corvane didn't hire me despite my oath to Vess," Felix said. "He hired me *because* of it. A sworn knight makes the perfect blindfold. My nightly ritual, the one I've performed every night for years—speaking my day's witness aloud to a god who never answered—it wasn't devotion. It was drainage. Every word I gave in faith, the guild siphoned and bottled."

Old Tam made a sound low in his throat. "That's what my memories were," he said quietly. "Bottled. Sold."

"Yes." Felix did not soften it. Tam deserved better than softening. "I'm sorry. I audited those accounts. I held your memory in my hands and logged it as inventory, and I didn't know what it was, but I should have wondered."

Tam nodded slowly, the way a man nods when a wound has finally been named. "You're telling us now," he said. "That counts. In my experience it's the only thing that counts."

* * *

Felix opened the Weeping Wood.

The pages turned themselves partway, as they always did now, eager or resentful—Felix had stopped trying to guess which—and settled on the page with tonight's date circled in sap-dark ink by some hand a century dead. Beside it, the image: a hooded figure holding a ledger.

"That's me," Felix said. "Or someone wearing my face. The Wood drew it a hundred years ago, and I found it eleven days ago, and since then I've learned why." They looked up, and found that looking at Odile was hardest, so they made themselves look at her first. "I'm not twenty-six. Or I am, but I was also there. On the field, the day Vess fell. I was Vess's last sworn knight. My name then was—" and here Felix's voice caught, because the name was right there, recovered from Odile's sold memory, whole and heavy and *theirs*, and saying it felt like setting down a weight they'd carried so long they'd forgotten it was a weight—

"No," Felix said instead. "I'll say it once, at the end, if you want it. But hear the rest first. Because the name isn't mine anymore, and I need you to understand that I'm not asking you to follow a knight of the old war. I'm asking you to decide whether you'll follow *this*—"

They laid out the arithmetic. The thirteenth turn of the moon, confirmed by Astronomer Quill and her borrowed instruments: one month hence. The prison weakening. The choice that was no choice. Free Vess too early, while the seal was still strong enough to shatter violently, and wake a god into a world full of stolen divine fragments and old grudges—the war reignited, mortal cities as the board. Wait, and the seal failed on its own timetable anyway—but failing *how*? Dying, perhaps. Starving. A god suffocating under a grove while the moon counted down.

"There might be a third way," Felix said. "The seal requires a willing renunciation. A sworn knight breaking oath—not in despair, not coerced, but as testimony. Naming every crime committed in the god's silence. That's me. That's what I am apparently *for*. But I don't trust myself alone with that kind of power over the outcome. I spent ten years as a keeper of records because I believed accounts should be public. So this one will be too."

They stepped back from the crate.

"Ask me anything. Anything at all. Then we vote. Everyone. Not majority rules—I won't move unless it's unanimous, because if even one of you believes this is wrong, then I need to know why before I do it, not after."

* * *

The silence afterward had texture. The cheese-smell seemed louder than the silence.

It was Brack who broke it, in the way of twins who split tasks between them: "You said the Thorn sold pieces of a god. Did—" he glanced at Bess, and she finished it, as she always finished things:

"Did we collect any? Deliveries, shipments, anything with our names on the manifest?"

Felix hated this answer and gave it straight. "Three. Over four years. Small parcels, sealed, addressed to a buyer in the capital. You wouldn't have known."

Brack sat back. Bess stared at the floor. For a moment Felix thought they'd lost them—and then Bess looked up, jaw set in the exact shape of her brother's. "Then we're owed," she said. "Corvane used our hands. That's a debt. We collect."

Old Tam raised a hand like a boy in lessons. "The thirteenth turn. One month. What happens to the rest of us if the god dies? Us being people who've—" he gestured vaguely at his own missing decades— "*traded* with the divine, so to speak."

"I don't know," Felix admitted. "Quill thinks a god's death wouldn't be quiet. Oaths might unravel. Every contract sworn under witness, everywhere. Which is, incidentally, nearly every contract in the realm."

"So the banks fail, the marriages dissolve, the armies disband—" Maren counted on her fingers, "—and the Crown collapses into forty warring duchies inside a season."

"Optimistically," Felix said, and someone laughed, short and startled, and the room breathed a little.

It was Odile who stood.

She moved slowly, the way she always did when she wanted people to watch, and she crossed to the crate and put one finger on the Weeping Wood's cover, and the wood went still under her touch—all its weeping pausing, the way a room goes quiet when the wrong person walks in.

"Three days in the Eye's spire," Odile said. Her voice was level, which everyone present understood to be its own kind of fury. "Three days of questions. And the worst part—the genuinely worst part, worse than the irons—is that I already knew half the answers. Because seven years ago I sold a memory of you to the Unblinking Eye to ransom my sister, and I have spent seven years knowing I did it, and telling myself it was one memory, one small coin, everyone pays their debts somehow." She turned to Felix. "And then I find out the memory I sold was worth a *god's freedom*. That the reason they hunted you, the reason Vale had your face on a warrant before you ever opened that book, the reason Odile of the Gilded Thorn is the reason all of this happened—"

"Odile—"

"Let me finish." Her voice cracked and she let it. "I voted with my feet, Keeper. I stayed in that spire and lied to the Grandmaster's face for three days about where you'd gone, and I did it *gladly*, because if I hadn't sold you seven years ago none of us would be sitting on cheese crates waiting for the sky to fall. So yes." She slammed her palm flat on the Wood, and the book flared warm gold at the contact, and nobody in the room doubted anymore that it was alive. "I vote we try. Not because it's wise. Because I owe you a debt no ledger can hold, and I am *sick to death* of debts."

The silence after that was different. Warmer. Felix found they were gripping the edge of the crate hard enough to hurt.

"I forgave you in the marshes," Felix said quietly. "I'd forgive you again. But thank you for saying it here, where it counts. Witnesses matter. That's—" a breath of a laugh— "that's rather the theology of the whole thing."

* * *

The questions went long into the night. Good ones, sharp ones. Maren wanted to know what happened to her if the ritual succeeded—if a salt-witch's bargains survived a god waking. Nobody knew. Tam asked whether Vess would remember him, whether the god kept accounts of the faithful, and Felix answered honestly: "Vess kept accounts of everything. That was the problem. That was always the problem." Bess asked the practical question—"What do you actually need from us?"—and Felix gave the practical answer: Vale's blockade broken or bypassed, the grove reached, Corvane's hired legions delayed, and thirteen witnesses standing in the clearing when the moon turned, because the prophecy-page showed twelve figures around the hooded one and Felix intended to read that number as an invitation rather than a limit.

Near midnight, Brack yawned enormously and said, "Right. Vote's done, then. Unanimous, unless anyone's shy?" He looked around the granary. Eleven faces, tired and frightened and resolved. Nobody spoke. "Unanimous," he declared, and Bess thumped the floor twice in approval, and Old Tam said, "Well. I've forgotten most of my life; may as well spend what's left on something."

Felix stood among them and felt something loosen in their chest that had been tight since the archive, since the date circled in century-old sap, since the first night of silence from a god they'd loved.

One month. Thirteen witnesses. A grove that wept.

"We march at dawn," Felix said. "To the Weeping Wood. To free a god—or to bury one properly, with witnesses, which is more than the world ever gave them."

Outside, high over the smuggled-cheese chapel, the moon hung fat and pale, one turn short of an impossible thirteenth. And as the company filed out into the cold, the Weeping Wood, left open on its crate, turned one leaf-page on its own—and wrote, in sap that gleamed like dawn, a single word in a language no one had spoken aloud in a hundred years:

*Ready.*
