## 19. The March on the Weeping Wood

Dawn found us at the edge of the forest, and the forest did not want us there.

I could tell because the mist came down off the ridgeline like a landlord who'd heard about the party. It rolled through the pines in long grey fingers, and somewhere beyond it — past the picket lines, past the watchfires Vale had lit at every road and deer-trail and probably a few places that weren't trails at all — the Weeping Wood stood waiting. I couldn't see it. But I could feel it, the way you feel a tooth that's been aching so long you've stopped noticing until something touches it.

The others felt it too. Nobody said anything. They just stood in the wet bracken at the tree line with their breath steaming, looking like what we were: eleven people with a grievance, a grudge, and no business whatsoever attempting this.

"Well," said Odile, breaking the silence the way she broke everything — cheerfully and slightly too hard. "It's very green. I'll give it that."

"It's blockaded," said Marta the locksmith, who had once been the Thorn's finest forger of debts and now carried a crossbow like she was still deciding whether she liked it.

"It's *green*," Odile insisted. "After the salt marshes I will personally kiss every tree in that forest if it asks nicely."

"The trees won't ask," I said. "They'll just cry on you."

"Even better. Free bath."

I let them have the joke. Jokes before a battle are like ballast — useless cargo you carry anyway, because throwing it overboard is how ships capsize. I checked my satchel instead, for perhaps the ninth time that morning: the Weeping Wood's severed branch, still faintly warm, wrapped in oilcloth against my ribs; Corvane's true ledger; and the map of the grove that the astronomer Ilse had drawn from her charts of the thirteenth turn, all careful ink and terrible implication. The moon hung above the eastern ridge, one night shy of whole, wearing its impossible extra sliver like a coin someone had slipped into a purse without asking.

One month, Ilse had said, weeks ago. She'd been generous. The moon had been hurrying.

"Vale's lines run along the old charcoal road," said Bren, our ex-Inquisitor deserter, tracing the ridge with a scarred finger. "Two pickets deep, maybe three near the pass. She's not stupid. She knows where we're going."

"She also let me go once," I said.

"She let you go *once*," Bren agreed. "Off the record. Records have a way of getting written after the fact when a general's breathing down your collar. And Corvane has bought himself legions, Felix. Whatever Serah Vale is when she's alone at three in the morning, in daylight she's the Crown's warrant with legs."

I looked at the mist, and the mist looked back, and neither of us blinked first.

"Then we go where the warrant isn't," I said. "Ilse?"

Our astronomer pushed her spectacles up her nose and unrolled her chart with the reverence other people reserved for scripture. "The grove sits in a fold of the land where the streams braid — the book grows at the center, fed by water from three directions. There is no road. That is rather the point of prisons." Her finger moved. "But water goes under things. And there's an old flood-cut here, a channel the river abandoned centuries ago. It runs beneath the picket line through a culvert wide enough for—"

"A person?" asked Odile.

"For most of a person," said Ilse primly. "The rest of the person will need to be motivated."

* * *

We went into the dark under the world at midmorning, which felt appropriately dishonest.

The culvert was exactly as bad as advertised. Cold water to the thigh, a ceiling of roots pressing down like a congregation leaning in to hear gossip, and ahead of us only the sound of our own splashing and, beneath it, something else — a low, rhythmic pulse in the stone, like a heart beating under the floor of the world.

"Do you feel that?" whispered Tam the tally-clerk, who had joined us in the chapel with nothing but his abacus and his hatred of Corvane, both of which he wielded admirably.

"I feel it," I said. My teeth were chattering, but not entirely from cold. "That's him."

Vess. The silent god. My god, though I hadn't known it for most of a century, and the thought still caught in my chest like a fishbone. Somewhere above us, past two picket lines and however many soldiers money could buy, the god I had sworn to — sworn twice, once as Felix Fairweather and once as someone whose name I'd traded away to survive — lay buried under his own weeping body, dreaming in the dark while merchants sold pieces of him by the ounce.

The pulse quickened as we went deeper. Or perhaps I imagined it. Perhaps the heart of a god beats faster when his last knight comes home, even a knight who arrives soaked to the waist and smelling strongly of culvert.

Odile bumped against my back in the dark. "Felix. When we get there. What are you going to say to him?"

I had wondered the same thing at four in the morning, and at midnight, and during the entire wretched walk through the salt marshes. *You left me* had been the answer, once. Then: *you didn't leave me, they buried you, forgive me.* Now I suspected the truth was smaller and worse. What do you say to someone you failed, who failed you, whom everyone failed, for a hundred years?

"I'm going to say I kept the books," I said finally. "Every entry. Every debt. Somebody had to remember."

Ahead of us, the culvert bent, and grey light leaked down through a crack in the root-ceiling, and I heard — distantly, muffled, but unmistakably — the sound of many boots moving in step.

Corvane's legions were already in the forest.

* * *

We surfaced in a hollow of alders half a mile short of the grove, wrung out and filthy, and discovered that the war had arrived without us.

Smoke stood up from the trees to the north — not the thin cooking smoke of pickets but the greasy black of burning supply wagons. Drums beat somewhere out of sight, arrhythmic, wrong, mercenary-company drums that keep their own time because they're paid to. And over it all, thin and clear as a knife laid on silk, came a sound I knew in my bones: the Weeping Wood itself, keening. A hundred years I'd thought it a wind through the leaves. Now I knew better. The trees were crying, and today they had reason.

"They're fighting each other," Bren breathed, worming up beside me to the lip of the hollow. Below, through the trunks, I saw it: Vale's blue-and-silver pickets clashing with Corvane's hired banners — the Gilded Thorn's colors worn openly now, gilded thorns on black, because Corvane had evidently decided that hiding was no longer profitable. Soldiers screamed and shoved in the mud between the pines. An officer of Vale's line was shouting something about warrants and treason. A Thorn sergeant was shouting back something about payment in advance.

"Vale tried to hold them at the road," Bren said. "She hasn't got the numbers. She never did — the Crown sent her a fraction of what the warrant required, because half the ministers funding her warrant are the same men selling Corvane his legions." His mouth twisted. "Everything's the same ledger, Felix. It always was."

"Then we don't fight the ledger," I said. "We go around it."

We moved through the trees in a loose string, hugging the ground, and the forest helped us — or the god did, I couldn't say which, but branches that should have cracked beneath our feet stayed silent, and mist pooled obligingly in the hollows to swallow our passage, and once, when a Thorn scout turned toward the alder brake where Odile crouched with her knife drawn, a root rose just enough to catch his boot and spill him, cursing, into the ferns. He blamed the ground. He was wrong.

I touched the bark of a pine as we passed and whispered, thank you, and the bark was wet.

The grove opened before us in the late afternoon light like a secret finally told.

I had seen the branch. I had read the fragments. Nothing prepared me for the whole.

There was no temple. There was no ruin. There was simply a ring of trees older than kingdoms, vast and silver-barked, their faces — and they had faces, sorrowful, serene, human in the way weather is human — turned inward toward the center, where a shape grew out of the living rock that was part trunk and part spine and entirely a book. The Weeping Wood. Its pages were leaves the size of doors, pale and veined, trembling without wind, and every page bore writing in a hand I recognized because it was carved into my own memory whether I remembered carving it or not.

And beneath it all, under everything, slow and immense and unmistakable now, the heartbeat. The ground pulsed with it. My own pulse answered, embarrassed at being late.

"Oh," said Odile softly, beside me. All the jokes had gone out of her. "Oh, Felix. He's *beautiful*."

"He's a prisoner," I said.

"Both things can be true. You taught me that."

We started across the grove, and that was when the horns sounded behind us, and Corvane came to harvest his god.

* * *

He arrived the way everything of his arrived: expensively.

They came through the western trees in column — not skirmishers, not scouts, but a proper legion, two hundred at least, in polished half-plate with the gilded thorn blazoned fresh, flanking a palanquin of lacquered wood carried by eight bearers. Guildmaster Corvane stepped down from it into the sacred mud of the grove and paused, delicately, to wipe his boot on a root.

I had spent years keeping his ledgers. I had admired, in a distant professional way, the tidiness of his mind. Looking at him now, standing small and immaculate before a bound god, I understood that I had confused tidiness with order. There was nothing orderly about a man who would carve up eternity to balance a quarterly account.

"Fairweather," he called, pleasantly, across the grove. His voice carried the way money carries. "You've cost me three outposts, two contracts, and a considerable reputation. Do you know what I admire about that? The *itemization*. You were always my best auditor."

"You sold pieces of a god," I said. My voice didn't carry as well as his, but it carried. "Bottled grief. Borrowed years. I audited those ledgers, Corvane. I know what the entries meant now. Every vial. Every draught. Every year you sold some poor soul back their own decade at interest."

"Someone was going to harvest him," Corvane said, with the mild patience of a man explaining arithmetic to a child. "The seal was failing regardless — the moon tells you that better than I. Would you rather his power leak out piecemeal into rivers and madmen? I offered *stewardship*. Provenance. Documentation." He gestured, and the legion began to fan out along the grove's edge, unhurried, professional. "I am the last honest man in this transaction, Fairweather. Even your Inquisitor is compromised. Even your little chapel of the cheated."

Behind him, at the treeline, blue-and-silver banners appeared — Vale's remnant, maybe sixty soldiers, bloodied and late, forming up with the weary competence of people who have decided which losing fight is theirs. I searched the line and found her: Serah Vale, helm under her arm, looking at the grove, at the legion, at me, with the expression of a woman reading a contract she'd been handed too late to renegotiate.

My company gathered at my back. Eleven souls and an abacus. Two hundred against us, sixty against everyone, one imprisoned god underfoot, and the moon rising fat and wrong over the eastern ridge with one night left in its impossible arithmetic.

"Felix," Odile murmured. "Tell me you have a plan."

"I have a ledger entry," I said.

"That's not—"

"It's the only kind of plan I've ever had." I stepped forward, alone, into the open grove, my boots printing the mud above the heartbeat of a god, and raised my voice so that both armies could hear it. "Guildmaster. Before your legions take another step — I call a public audit."

Corvane's smile flickered. "On what authority?"

"On the oldest authority there is." I put my hand flat against the nearest silver trunk, and the tree shuddered beneath my palm like a held breath, and the pages of the great book at the grove's heart began, slowly, to turn themselves. "A witness. Thirteen turns, thirteen witnesses — the seal was built on testimony, Corvane, and tomorrow the moon finishes the count. So hear mine."

Above the grove, the first stars came out. Beneath my feet, the heartbeat stumbled — and then, impossibly, hopefully, quickened.

And at the edge of the trees, Serah Vale set her helmet down in the mud, drew her sword, and pointed it — not at me.
