## 20. The Breaking of the Oath

The grove at the heart of the Weeping Wood was not what Felix had imagined in a hundred fevered nights of wondering. They had pictured something grand — a cathedral of trunks, perhaps, or a black pool ringed in standing stones. What they found instead was almost embarrassingly small. A clearing no wider than a chapel. Moss soft as a confession. And at its center, one tree.

Not the largest tree Felix had ever seen, though it was old enough that its roots had swallowed three boulders and made them part of itself. But it *wept*. That was the thing. Sap ran down its bark in slow silver threads, catching the moonlight — the wrongness of the thirteenth-turn moon, swollen and pale and hanging far too low — until it looked exactly like what it was: a body crying through its skin.

The book grew from it. Of course it did. The Wood was not a book about Vess. The Wood *was* Vess, or the visible tenth of him, the way a ledger's cover is the visible tenth of an account.

"Right," said Odile, cracking her knuckles behind Felix. "Which one of us knocks?"

"No one knocks," Felix said. "That's rather the point."

They came out of the treeline in ones and twos, because a company forged from grievance does not march well. There was Marta of the Salt Road, who had been paid for eleven years of smuggling in coin that turned to leaves at dawn. There were the Fenwick twins, who between them owned half a memory of their mother's face and had bought the other half back at ruinous interest. There was old Quill, the astronomer, wheezing and delighted, carrying his brass instruments like a man bringing wine to a wedding. And there was Serah Vale, out of uniform, her warrant folded into nothing in her pocket, standing slightly apart from all of them with the expression of a woman who had broken every rule she'd ever loved and was still deciding how she felt about it.

Behind them all, down the hill, through the burning smoke of Corvane's hired legions meeting Vale's blockades — chaos. Here, only the tree, weeping silver into the moss.

Felix walked to it alone.

Up close, the bark was warm. That undid them more than anything else. All this time — the silence, the hundred unanswered nights, the slow dread that they had been abandoned by their own god mid-contract — and the bark was warm, like a hand held too long near a fire, like a body trying to stay alive in a room where nobody visited.

"I'm here," Felix said quietly. "You enormous, ridiculous, silent—"

The tree shuddered. A single thread of sap fell and struck the ground beside Felix's boot, and where it struck, a small white flower opened, blooming out of season, out of order, out of hope.

And Felix understood.

It arrived the way understanding always arrived for them — not as thunder but as arithmetic. The seal beneath the grove, the prison Corvane had been bleeding for decades, the whole rotten architecture of it: it was not built of stone or iron. It was built of *oath*. Vess was a god of witness, of record, of the sacred keeping of accounts. You could not bind such a being with chains; you bound him with his own nature. The Grandmaster's order had sworn the sealing. Twelve witnesses had sworn the testimony that made it lawful — twelve, Felix thought, remembering the marginalia, remembering the thirteenth page torn out a century ago and burned — and so the lock was made of sworn words.

And a lock made of words could only be opened by words. Not forced. Not picked. *Renounced*. Willingly, publicly, by a knight still under oath — a knight whose renunciation would stand as testimony against everything done in the god's silence.

A sworn knight. Singular.

Felix looked down at their own hands. Ink-stained. Ledger-keeper's hands. And underneath the ink, older than the ink, older than the name Fairweather, the ghost-grip of a sword hilt they hadn't held in a hundred years.

"Well," Felix said to the tree, conversationally, because their voice would have broken otherwise. "You might have mentioned."

The sap fell faster.

* * *

They gathered the company close, because this was not a thing to be done quietly. Quiet was how crimes survived. Quiet was Corvane's whole trade.

"I need you all to hear this," Felix said, turning to face them, back to the tree. "All of it. Because what I'm about to do only works if it's witnessed properly. If I break my oath alone, in the dark, it's just despair, and despair doesn't open locks. Testimony opens locks. And testimony needs witnesses."

Odile crossed her arms. "Felix. Whatever you're about to say—"

"You sold my memory once," Felix said gently, "and I forgave you, and you've been insufferable about it ever since, which is how I know you're sorry. So you'll forgive me this." They drew breath. "My name is not Felix Fairweather. Or rather — it is now, but it wasn't first. A century ago I was sworn to Vess as his last knight. I stood on the field at Carrowmere when the twelve sealed him. I was made forgetful so I'd survive the purge, and I forgot so thoroughly that I built myself a new life out of ledgers and loneliness and called it enough. It wasn't. It never was. I've been keeping accounts my entire existence because somewhere under everything, I remembered that someone's books needed balancing."

Silence. Even the smoke on the wind seemed to hold its breath.

Marta spoke first, in her flat salt-marsh way. "So you're a hundred and some years old and you've been doing our audits."

"The audits are *immaculate*, Marta."

"They are," she conceded. "Carry on."

Quill was crying openly, which for an astronomer was practically applause.

"So here is what happens now," Felix said. "I break my oath. Deliberately. Publicly. Not because I've lost faith — I haven't, I've finally *found* it — but because the law that holds him prisoner requires my silence, and I am finished being silent. When I speak the renunciation, I'll name every crime committed while he wept unheard. Every jar of grief bottled and sold. Every year borrowed and never returned. Every name traded before it was spoken. His power, harvested piece by piece for decades. I'll name Corvane. I'll name the Grandmaster. I'll name the crown that funded it and the laws that permitted it. My breaking will be the account, and you—" they gestured at all of them, ragged and smoking and utterly improbable— "you will be the witnesses. Thirteen, counting the flower."

"Why thirteen?" asked one of the Fenwick twins.

"Because twelve made the lock," Odile said softly. "Thirteen breaks it. That was always the arithmetic, wasn't it? The torn page."

Felix nodded. "One more thing. When I finish — when the last word is said — the oath takes my true name with it. The old one. The knight's name. Maybe more than that. I don't fully know what I'll lose." They smiled, and found to their surprise that the smile was steady. "But I've been thinking about names lately, and I've concluded they're overrated collateral. I've seen what people pay for them. I'd rather be owed nothing and belong somewhere than own everything and stand alone."

"Felix—" Odile started.

"That's the name I keep," Felix said. "Fairweather. You gave it back to me every morning for six years, calling it across the guildhall. Whatever else burns tonight, that one stays. Now. Stand witness."

They took out the ledger.

Of course they did. It was the only scripture they had ever truly trusted — the Gilded Thorn's great book of impossible things, smuggled out of the burning outposts strapped to their back. They opened it to a fresh page, dipped a pen in the silver sap pooling at the tree's root, and wrote the date.

Tonight's date. The one circled in the Wood a century ago, beside a hooded figure holding a book.

"Vess," Felix said aloud, and their voice carried strangely in the grove, the way voices carry in chapels and courtrooms and other places where truth is supposed to live. "I swore to you: *I will keep the records. I will speak only what is written. I will hold my tongue until you answer.*"

The moonlight thickened. The sap ran brighter.

"You never stopped answering," Felix said. "We stopped listening. And in your silence, they did this—"

And they began to name it.

* * *

They named the jars of last words, four hundred of them, sold to collectors who wore strangers' dying breaths as jewelry. They named the debt paid in shadows — the village of Hollowmere, whose shadows were collected at harvest each year until the people there cast none and the sun passed through them like they were already gone. They named borrowed years, seven thousand two hundred of them, taken from the poor and lent to the rich at interest. They named names not yet spoken — children's names, traded before birth, so that some were born already owing.

They named Corvane's hidden ledgers, entry by entry, because Felix had read every line and had the terrible gift of perfect recall for numbers. They named the Grandmaster, general Aldric Vane, who had sealed a god to end a war and then spent a century refusing to admit the war was over. They named the crown, and the Inquisitors' warrant, and the laws written by men who owned the ink.

Each name struck the grove like a stone dropped into deep water. The tree shook harder. The silver threads became streams. Somewhere beneath the moss, beneath the roots, beneath a hundred years of enforced quiet, something vast turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to listen.

Felix's voice cracked. They let it crack. A testimony delivered in a courtroom voice would have been a performance; this was an audit, and audits are allowed to hurt.

"And I name myself," Felix said, and the company stirred. "I name the knight who stood witness at the sealing and let it happen. Who was made to forget rather than made to object. Whose silence — my silence, a hundred years of it — was purchased with mercy and cost a god his voice. I was the thirteenth page, torn out and burned, and I let myself be paper."

The sap was falling like rain now, silver rain, and the flowers were opening everywhere the drops struck, hundreds of them, the whole clearing going white and luminous.

"So hear the renunciation." Felix closed the ledger. Held it over their heart. "I, knight of Vess, last-sworn of the Order of the Open Hand, do willingly, knowingly, and before these witnesses, break my oath. Not in despair. In testimony. Let my broken word be the key. Let my forfeited name be the price. Let everything done in your silence be named, and counted, and *owed*—"

The word *owed* rang out across the grove like a bell.

"—and let the account be settled. I release you. I release us all. By every record I ever kept: *you are free.*"

Felix tore the page from the ledger — the page bearing tonight's date, the prophecy fulfilled, the circle closed — and pressed it against the weeping bark.

The tree went still.

Then, all at once, it *exhaled*. A sound like ten thousand pages turning. The silver threads reversed, flowing upward, inward, home. The roots glowed. The moss blazed white. And the warmth Felix had felt in the bark spread outward through the whole grove like sunlight through cold water, touching every person standing witness, and every one of them later told the same story: that for one moment, they felt *accounted for*. Known completely. Forgiven absolutely. As if some vast and gentle clerk had reviewed the whole messy ledger of their lives, sighed, and stamped it *acceptable*.

Felix felt the old name go. It left quietly, without pain — a word slipping off a shelf into deep water. They reached for it once, reflexively, and found only warm space where it had been, and beneath the space, absurdly, stubbornly: *Fairweather*. Odile's voice across the guildhall. Breakfast. Belonging.

The tree split.

Not violently — the way a seed splits, the way a book opens when it has finally been waited for long enough. And what stepped out of it—

What stepped out of it was waking.

* * *

Down the hill, the battle stuttered and stopped, soldiers on both sides dropping their weapons to stare at a sky suddenly full of silver light rising out of the forest like a second dawn. In the royal city, every bell rang once, unpulled. In the Eye's spire, an old, old man fell to his knees in a room full of stolen light and whispered, "*No. Not yet. I kept it safe—*" And in the guildhall ruins, a locked strongbox containing forty years of Corvane's most secret accounts simply opened itself, its pages fanning out into the wind like birds.

In the grove, Felix Fairweather — the Keeper, they supposed they were now, though the title hadn't quite settled yet — leaned against Odile's shoulder and watched a god take his first free breath in a hundred years.

"He's looking at us," Odile whispered.

"He's reading us," Felix corrected. "There's a difference."

And indeed the god's gaze moved across them slowly — Marta, the twins, Quill, Vale, all of them — and where it rested, each felt the strange comfort of being accurately recorded. Then the gaze fixed on the road below the hill, where torches were converging: Corvane's legions regrouping, and among them a sedan chair borne by eight men, and behind the sedan chair's curtains, a cold and calculating presence beginning, very late, to understand what it had woken.

Vess straightened to his full height — too tall, much too tall, bark-skinned and silver-veined and wearing a face like a judge's and a mourner's at once — and spoke for the first time in a century. His voice was the sound a quill makes on good paper.

"*Bring me the accountant,*" he said, "*who kept my books. And bring me the thief,*" — the torches below flared higher— "*who sold them.*"

Odile squeezed Felix's arm. "Well," she said. "He knows about the ledgers. This should be interesting."

Felix watched the torches climb the hill, and the god wait, patient as a closing entry, and felt — for the first time in a hundred years, in two lives — entirely ready for the audit to begin.
