## 21. Thirteenth Turn

The grove held its breath.

Felix felt it in the soles of their boots first—the Weeping Wood had stopped weeping. For the whole of their flight through Vale's blockade, through fire and arrow-song and Corvane's hired legions, the trees had bled sap like slow silver tears, and now, as the moon climbed toward the last finger's width of its thirteenth turn, every wound on every trunk sealed itself at once.

The silence that followed was not empty. It was *attentive*.

"They know," Odile whispered beside them. Her wrists still bore the marks of the Eye's manacles, and she stood like a woman who had decided that if death wanted her, it could bloody well queue behind everything else. "The trees. They know it's almost time."

Felix didn't answer. Their hand rested on the Wood—not the book grown from tree-flesh they'd carried across half a kingdom, but the grove itself, the original, the root of all copies. Bark pulsed faintly under their palm, warm as a sleeping animal. Warm as a heartbeat.

*One month ago,* Felix thought, *I was counting jars of last words and complaining about mislabeled debts.* The memory felt like something belonging to a much younger person. Which, in a way that still made their stomach lurch, it did.

Around the clearing, their company took up positions that were less tactics and more hope arranged in a circle. Marta the forger had wedged herself between two roots with her crossbow and an expression of profound skepticism aimed at the sky. Old Hewn, who owed the Thorn eleven years of his life and had come to collect them back, gripped his woodcutter's axe with hands that shook only slightly. The twins, Pip and Perrin, stood shoulder to shoulder, having solemnly agreed beforehand that if the god woke up angry, they would run in opposite directions to confuse it.

And beyond the treeline, audible even here, Corvane's legions were coming. Drums like a slow heart attack.

"Felix." Serah Vale stepped out of the shadows without ceremony—she had let them pass her lines an hour ago, off the record, off every record, and had then done the far more dangerous thing: followed them. Her warrant-case was slung over her shoulder like a coffin she'd grown tired of carrying. "Whatever happens when that moon finishes turning—I need you to understand that I am standing here as a witness, not a soldier."

"That's the best thing anyone's said to me all week," Felix said.

"It's the only thing," Vale said. But there was something almost gentle under it.

The last sliver of shadow slid from the moon's face.

*

Corvane arrived at the moment the light changed.

Guildmaster Sable Corvane came into the grove the way he entered every room: as though it had been built in anticipation of him. He wore his merchant's velvet beneath a general's half-plate, and forty hired soldiers fanned out behind him with the bored competence of men paid by the hour rather than by conviction. In his gloved hands he carried the harvest-lattice—a thing of gold wire and captive sigils that Felix had seen only in the hidden ledgers, the instrument by which the Thorn had been siphoning pieces of a god for sixty years.

He stopped at the edge of the clearing and looked at the sealed trees, and for one unguarded instant his face did something Felix had never seen it do.

It went hungry.

"There you are," Corvane said softly—to the grove, not to Felix. "Sixty years I have fed you patience, old one. Time to be collected."

"You're late," Felix said. "You missed the part where I named your crimes. All of them. Out loud. With witnesses."

Corvane turned. His gaze found Felix and did the arithmetic it always did—what is this asset worth, what is this liability going to cost me—and Felix watched him arrive at a number and discard it, because tonight none of his numbers worked.

"Keeper of ledgers," Corvane said. "Do you imagine the god will thank you? Do you imagine it wakes *grateful*? I have read the accounts, child. Gods wake hungry. And I—" he lifted the lattice, and the sigils inside it began to sing, a thin awful chord, "—have spent sixty years learning what feeds it."

The moon completed its turn.

*

Later—much later, in the rebuilt guildhall, over bad wine and good company—they would argue about what waking looked like. Marta insisted it was a sound. Hewn swore it was a smell, resin and rain and old blood. Pip maintained to his dying day that it was the feeling of being *remembered*, which everyone privately agreed was the closest anyone got.

For Felix, it was simple. It was the hundred-and-first night of the oath-ritual, the one they had performed alone in a cold room while the world called them mad—and this time, the silence answered.

Vess woke.

Not as a figure. Not as fire or thunder, though both happened, briefly, apologetically. Vess woke as *presence*—a pressure of attention so vast and so gentle that every person in the grove fell to their knees without deciding to, except Corvane, who stayed upright because falling down would mean looking up, and looking up meant being seen.

The god saw him anyway.

Felix felt the judgment before it was spoken—not words, not yet, but a weighing, the way a ledger-keeper weighs a page before writing the sum. The attention moved through the grove like lamplight through water. It touched Hewn's axe and knew every tree he'd felled honestly. It touched Vale's warrant-case and found the blank paper she'd hidden at the bottom, the one she hadn't known why she carried. It touched Odile, and paused, and Odile gasped as though doused in warm rain, and Felix understood that somewhere in the god's vast accounting, a debt of shame was being marked *paid in full*.

Then it settled on Felix, and the world narrowed to a single line of ancient text written in light against the inside of their skull:

*MY KNIGHT. MY LAST WITNESS. YOU KEPT COUNTING.*

"I kept counting," Felix said aloud, voice cracking on the second word. "Every night. Even when you didn't answer. *Especially* when you didn't answer."

*I DID NOT ABANDON YOU.*

"I know." Felix's throat burned. "I know that now."

Corvane moved.

To his credit—he would have wanted credit—it was quick. The harvest-lattice sang its chord and struck downward, golden wire unspooling toward the roots of the eldest tree, toward the place where the book grew from living flesh, where a god's power pooled just beneath the bark like marrow in bone. Sixty years of stolen sigils flared at once. Half the clearing lit gold.

The god regarded the lattice with what Felix could only describe, later, as *professional interest*. Then, gently, the way one picks up a child's toy to examine it, Vess took hold of the working and unwound it.

Every sigil Corvane had ever bought came home.

Felix felt it happen in their own bones, because some of those sigils lived in Felix too—in the mercenary strength the oath granted, the quiet gifts that had been fading for a hundred nights as the prison weakened. Power that had been sold, siphoned, hoarded in vaults beneath three cities, tore loose and flooded back to its source, and Vess stood—there was no other word for it—stood up inside the grove, tall as the trees, made of sap-light and the memory of every prayer ever whispered into silence.

Corvane fell to his knees after all.

"Wait," the Guildmaster said, and for the first time in Felix's memory, Sable Corvane sounded like exactly what he was: an old man holding an empty wire frame. "Wait. I can account for it. Every piece, every sale—I have ledgers, I have *records*—"

The god leaned close.

Felix, alone among them, heard what was said next, perhaps because they were the only one fluent in the grammar of accounts.

*YOU KEPT LEDGERS TOO,* Vess said, and the voice was terrible and sad. *SO TELL ME, KEEPER: WHAT IS THE SUM OF SIXTY YEARS OF A STARVED GOD'S PATIENCE? WHAT DO YOU OWE THE THING YOU FED ON?*

"I—" Corvane's mouth worked. "I don't—"

*NO,* the god agreed. *YOU NEVER LEARNED THAT COLUMN.*

Felix expected the ending then. Everyone did. A hundred years of stories said this was how it went: the god rises, the god burns, the war begins again, mortals learn why their grandparents prayed facing away from forests. Pip had already grabbed Perrin's sleeve. Hewn had raised his axe in a gesture that was either defiance or welcome.

Instead, Vess straightened, and looked out past the grove—at the legions, at Vale's soldiers frozen at the treeline, at the distant glow of the city—and chose.

*I COULD TAKE IT BACK,* the god said, to everyone and no one. *EVERY YEAR THEY SOLD. EVERY NAME. EVERY GRIEF IN EVERY JAR. I HAVE BEEN ROBBED FOR SIXTY YEARS BY THIS MAN AND FOR THREE HUNDRED BY HIS ORDER, AND EVERY MORTAL SOUL PRESENT HAS PROFITED OR SUFFERED FROM THE THEFT, AND THE OLD WAR BEGAN OVER EXACTLY THIS ARITHMETIC.*

The silence stretched. Somewhere a nightbird sang, absurdly.

*THE WAR ENDED LAST TIME WITH NO WINNERS AND ONE PRISONER,* Vess said. *I DECLINE TO REPEAT THE EXPERIMENT.*

"What," said the Grandmaster of the Unblinking Eye, who had come silently into the grove during the waking, leaning on a staff older than kingdoms, his bandaged eyes turned up toward the light, "will you do instead?"

The two ancient enemies regarded each other—the god and the general, the prisoner and the jailer, both worn smooth by the same three centuries.

*EXILE,* said Vess.

The Grandmaster's breath caught. "Exile."

*I WILL GO WHERE THERE ARE NO LEDGERS TO KEEP AND NONE TO BREAK. NO WORSHIPPERS TO FAIL. I WILL WALK OUT OF YOUR WORLD THE WAY THE MOON WALKS OUT OF ITS TURN—COMPLETE, AND GONE, AND AT PEACE. AND YOU—* the light bent toward the Grandmaster, almost tenderly, *—old soldier, you may finally put down your post. There is nothing left to guard. There never was, except me, and I am leaving.*

"And him?" Vale's voice, steady, official, her whole career balanced on the question. She pointed at Corvane. "Justice has to be mortal, my lord. Or it isn't justice. It's just weather."

The god considered this with visible approval—Felix felt it, a warmth like sun through leaves.

*TRUE. VERY TRUE. THEN LET THE MORTALS JUDGE THE MORTAL. I MERELY RENDER HIM... AUDITABLE.*

The harvest-lattice crumbled in Corvane's hands to ordinary gold wire. And every hidden ledger in every vault beneath every Thorn outpost—every false column, every laundered year, every name bought and sold—rose up out of the dark all at once and flew, pages streaming like startled birds, to land open-faced in the grove's clearing at Vale's feet.

The Inquisitor stared down at sixty years of crimes in immaculate bookkeeping, and laughed once, short and disbelieving.

"Well," she said. "That saves me the paperwork."

*

The cost came due at dawn.

Felix had known it would—that was the shape of the oath, the shape of the seal, the shape of every fair trade the Thorn had ever pretended to make: something given, something given up. As the light returned and the presence began to withdraw from the grove, gathering itself for the long walk out of the world, it paused beside Felix one final time.

*KNIGHT. WITNESS. KEEPER.*

"Just Felix," they managed. "Or—not even that. Whatever comes next."

*WHAT COMES NEXT IS MINE TO ASK AND YOURS TO REFUSE. THE SEAL IS BROKEN. THE DEBT IS PAID. BUT A NAME WAS SPENT TO OPEN THE WAY, AND NAMES ARE NOT FREE. I CAN RETURN YOURS—ALL OF IT, THE CENTURY OF IT, THE BATTLEFIELD AND THE PURGE AND THE FACE YOU WORE BEFORE THE FACE YOU WEAR NOW. IT WILL COST YOU THE LIFE YOU'VE BUILT ON TOP OF IT. OR YOU CAN LET IT GO, AND WALK FORWARD LIGHT, INTO THE FAMILY THAT CHOSE YOU WITHOUT IT.*

Felix looked around the grove.

Odile, watching them with red-rimmed eyes and no apology left to make. Marta, pretending not to listen and failing completely. Hewn, who had put his axe down sometime in the night and hadn't picked it back up. The twins, asleep against each other's shoulders, because even miracles couldn't outrun a fourteen-year-old's bedtime. Vale, cataloguing evidence with tears running openly down a face that would deny them forever.

They had spent a hundred nights afraid of being abandoned. It had never once occurred to them, until this exact moment, that they had already been found.

"Keep it," Felix said. "Keep the name. I've got better things to carry."

Something that was very nearly a smile moved through the sap-light.

*THEN GOODBYE, KEEPER OF THE THORN. WHEN MORTALS SPEAK OF ME AFTER THIS NIGHT, LET THEM SAY THE SILENT GOD LEFT LOUDLY—AND LEFT THEM THEIR LEDGERS, TO KEEP HONESTLY WITHOUT ME.*

The moon set. The presence withdrew like a tide going out, and where the eldest tree had grown, there remained only a smooth pale stump shaped like an open book, and on its pages, in characters that would fade within the hour, a single line:

*PAID IN FULL.*

Felix Fairweather—who was not, strictly speaking, Felix Fairweather anymore, though the name still fit like an old coat someone else had broken in for them—sat down hard in the wet grass and started, to their own considerable surprise, to laugh.

Odile sat down beside them and put her head on their shoulder.

"So," she said. "Back to work tomorrow?"

"The ledgers are all over the ground and an Inquisitor is auditing them by sunrise," Felix said. "We're going to be doing paperwork for *years*."

"Good," Odile said, and meant it.

Across the clearing, Vale looked up from the scattered pages, found Felix's eyes, and held something out. It took Felix a moment to recognize it: a royal warrant, heavy cream paper, the Crown's seal still soft enough to press.

It was blank.

"Fill it in yourself," Vale said gruffly. "Whatever you want to be. Apparently that's how we're doing things now."

Felix took it. And in the grey light before dawn, in a grove that had stopped weeping, surrounded by the family they'd stumbled into one impossible debt at a time, they began to write.
