## 22. The Name Left Behind

The grove was quiet in the way a room is quiet after a storm has passed through it—everything rearranged, everything damp, everything still standing.

Felix knelt at the heart of the Weeping Wood, before the great pale trunk that had once been called Vess and was now, again, called Vess, and felt the last of their oath humming in their chest like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing.

Around them, the company waited. Odile stood closest, arms crossed, jaw set in the expression she wore when she intended to argue with fate itself and had already picked her opening. Behind her: Marta the salt-witch, still smelling faintly of marsh; Bramble, the forger who had never forged anything half so true as the documents that got them through Vale's blockade; old Hesk with his ruined hands; and eleven others, the discarded and cheated, the ledger's orphans, all of whom had marched into a forest full of soldiers because Felix Fairweather asked them to.

Corvane lay bound in shadow-debt on the moss, his own hidden accounts read aloud to him line by line until he had stopped arguing. The Grandmaster of the Unblinking Eye sat against a root, ancient and unbound, watching with his one dry eye an expression that might have been relief. Vess's judgment had fallen on both men not as fire but as *witness*—every crime named, every victim named, every name spoken into the air where no god could pretend not to hear it. It had been worse than vengeance. Corvane had wept. Somehow that had been the most frightening thing Felix had ever seen.

"You don't have to do this," Odile said, for the fourth time.

"I know." That was rather the point.

The seal required a sworn knight's willing renunciation—not an oath broken in despair, which only fed prisons, but an oath *ended* as testimony ends, with the whole truth said out loud. Felix had done the naming yesterday, hours upon hours of it, every crime committed in Vess's silence catalogued in the flat, precise voice they used for audits. The trees had drunk every word. What remained now was simpler and much harder: the name.

Because names were currency here. Everyone knew that. The Gilded Thorn traded in them—names not yet spoken, names sold cheap by people who didn't know what they were worth. And Vess's prison had been paid for, a century ago, with a knight's name held in trust, locked away so the purge would pass over him. Felix Fairweather was the alias. Had always been the alias. Somewhere beneath thirty years of ledgers and mercenary contracts and Odile's terrible cooking was a person whose true name had been collateral in a war between gods.

To free Vess fully—to let the god walk out from under the Wood instead of merely waking within it—the name had to be surrendered. Not erased. *Given.* There was a difference, and the difference was everything. Erasure was what Corvane did to people. Surrender was what you did when you chose where you belonged.

"Vess," Felix said quietly, "if you can hear me—and I know now that you always could, you enormous patient tree—I want to say something first."

The leaves above stirred. Not wind. Attention.

"When I thought you'd abandoned me, I kept the oaths anyway. Not because I believed you were watching. Because I wanted to be someone worth watching." They laughed, a little wetly. "That's the whole secret of me, I think. A hundred years of trying to be worth remembering by someone who couldn't remember."

The bark beneath their palm warmed.

"So take it back. Take the name. You've carried it long enough—it was yours before it was mine. And I'll—" Their voice caught. They looked up at Odile, who nodded once, fierce. "I'll be whatever's left. Which is apparently a very good accountant."

*It will hurt,* the god said, in that voice like rain finding its way down through soil. *Not the losing. The choosing.*

"Everything worth doing hurts in the choosing," Felix said. "You taught me that. Or someone did. Someone with very strong opinions about proper ledger margins."

The leaves shivered—Vess laughing, Felix realized, the first laughter the world had heard from that throat in a century.

Then Felix closed their eyes and reached inward, past the mercenary's calm, past the keeper's careful order, past thirty years of small kindnesses and one enormous lie of omission, down to the sealed door at the bottom of themselves. They had seen it in the recovered memory: a door of white wood, carved with thirteen moons. On the other side, a young knight kneeling in a grove, swearing something in a language that predated the Crown, predated the Thorn, predated almost everything except the trees.

*I am the thirteenth witness,* Felix thought. *I testify.*

They opened the door.

---

The name left like a tide going out.

It didn't hurt the way losing a limb hurts. It hurt the way setting down something enormously heavy hurts—afterward, when your arms don't know what to do with their lightness. Felix felt a hundred years drain away, not the memories exactly, but the *weight* of them, the old griefs and older loyalties settling into the soil of the grove like silt. What remained was thinner and cleaner: the audit-mind, the stubbornness, the specific love they felt for Odile's terrible cooking despite all evidence.

And a blankness where the name had been. Not painful. Just open. Like a page waiting for its heading.

They opened their eyes. The grove had changed. Light came through the canopy in shafts, and the great trunk had split along a seam no axe could ever have made, and out of it stepped—unfolded—a figure that made everyone present forget, briefly, how to breathe.

Vess in flesh was nothing like Vess in absence. The god was small. That was the shock of it. Small and gray-eyed and ordinary-shaped, wearing a form like a well-worn coat, with bark still clinging at the wrists like cuffs they hadn't bothered to remove. The silent god looked around at the ragged, bloodied, exhausted company of debtors and deserters and said, in a voice like a ledger closing:

"You are all witnesses. Remember it accurately."

"We'll do our best," said Bramble. "Some of us were concussed."

Vess turned to Felix, and the gray eyes held something Felix had spent a lifetime hungering for without knowing its name: recognition. Complete, unhurried, unconditional.

"The name is kept," the god said. "It is safe. It will wait for you as long as you wish—even forever, if forever is what you choose."

"And if I never want it back?"

"Then you will be what you already are," Vess said. "Which is enough. It was always enough. That was the lesson you were meant to learn, and I apologize that my imprisonment made the curriculum somewhat longer than planned."

Odile made a sound that was either a sob or a laugh. With Odile it was usually both.

* * *

They came down out of the forest over three days, because armies move slowly and so do gods learning to walk again. Vess insisted on walking. "I have been furniture for a century," the god said. "I intend to experience gravel personally."

The journey gave the world time to hear what had happened, because witness travels faster than cavalry. By the time they reached the city, half of it had already heard the story told seven different ways, and the eighth way—the true one, the boring one, the one about accounting—was somehow the version that stuck. *A guildkeeper testified. A god woke and declined to fight. The war didn't come.* People found that last part harder to believe than the rest.

Serah Vale met them at the forest road's end with her soldiers drawn up in formation, which for one horrible moment looked like an arrest. Then she ordered the formation apart to let them pass, walked her horse alongside Felix's cart, and rode in silence for a while.

"My warrant," she said finally, "is void. The laws it cited were written by Corvane's money. I've submitted my findings to the Crown, which means I've likely ended my career, which means I'm currently unemployed, which is very strange."

"You could work for us," Felix said. "We're rebuilding."

"Inquisitors don't work for guilds."

"This guild doesn't want inquisitors. It wants an auditor who can't be bought. Corvane proved we need one. I proved I can't be trusted to do it alone."

Vale looked at them for a long moment. "You're offering me a job because you lied to me once and felt bad about it."

"I'm offering you a job because you *let me lie to you once* and then chose conscience over law in front of your own soldiers. That's rarer than bottled grief, and I should know, I've inventoried it."

Something that was almost a smile crossed Vale's face. "I'll want a salary."

"We'll put it in writing. Properly. With margins."

* * *

The rebuilt Gilded Thorn opened its doors in spring, in a new hall with tall windows and a counter where anyone could walk in and ask to see the books. This was Odile's idea, and Felix had fought it for two weeks before admitting it was brilliant. Public audit. Every account open. Bottled grief listed beside its source and its consent. Borrowed years logged with repayment dates in ink that couldn't be forged—Bramble had designed the ink himself and was insufferable about it.

The strangest accounts stayed strange. Impossible things were still traded; that was the guild's nature and its pride. But now every impossible thing came with a story attached, and the stories were read aloud on the first of each month, in the hall, to anyone who wished to listen. Attendance grew steadily. People came, Felix thought, less for the wonders than for the permission—to hear that other people's griefs had weight, and prices, and endings.

Felix worked the front counter most days. They answered to Keeper now. When people asked their name—they always asked, eventually—the answer came easily, without the hollow ache they'd braced for:

"I gave mine away. It's in safekeeping. But you can call me Keeper."

Some pitied them. Most, oddly, envied them. There was something about a person with no past to defend that made strangers tell them things—real things, quickly, the way you'd tell a priest or a good bartender. Felix collected these the way they'd once collected debts: carefully, completely, and with genuine interest.

Odile managed the back office. She had never once apologized for selling the memory, and Felix had never once asked her to. "You did what love cost," Felix had told her, in the grove, while the god watched. "I've audited worse transactions. Far worse." She had cried anyway, and then made dinner, badly, and that had been the whole of the reckoning between them. Some accounts balance best when nobody totals them aloud.

On quiet evenings Felix sometimes climbed to the hall's roof and looked east, toward where the Weeping Wood stood under royal protection now—or rather under *witness* protection, since Vess had pointed out that kings protect things they intend to sell, whereas witnesses merely promise to remember. The moon rose over the trees. Twelve turns and resting. It would keep its count honestly from now on; Vess had spoken to it personally, and gods, it turned out, could be very persuasive with their siblings.

Sometimes, on those evenings, Felix felt the shape of the missing name the way you feel a tooth that isn't there anymore. Not pain. Just a place where something used to fit. They had learned to press their tongue against the absence and let it be empty, and even, gradually, to like the emptiness—because emptiness, they had learned, was just another word for room.

* * *

On the first anniversary of the freeing, the hall held its monthly reading, and afterward, when the crowd had gone, Serah Vale stayed behind, sitting stiffly on a bench with a document case on her knees.

"You look official," Felix said, locking the strongbox.

"I am officially retired, which means anything I do next is unofficial, which is why I'm doing it this way." She opened the case and drew out a single sheet of heavy paper, sealed with the Crown's crest—which she broke herself, deliberately, in front of Felix, letting the wax fall in pieces to the floor.

A warrant. Royal issue. The kind that authorized search, seizure, arrest. Except every line describing its target had been left blank.

"It's real," Vale said. "Signed, sealed, lawful. It authorizes the bearer to investigate any crime, enter any premises, demand any record—in perpetuity." She held it out. "I pulled strings I shouldn't have. I'm probably going to regret it. But a guild that trades in impossible things ought to have at least one impossible document, and frankly, after Corvane, the Crown owes you people more than this."

Felix took the warrant and read the blanks. Name of bearer: *blank*. Term of authority: *blank*. Limits: *blank*. An instrument of power with no one attached to it, waiting to be filled in.

"Why give it to me? I'm nobody. Literally. There's no legal person to hold it."

"That's rather the point." Vale stood, straightened her coat, and allowed herself the smile she'd been rationing for a year. "A warrant needs a holder. So does a home. Choose yourself, Keeper. Fill in the blank. That's the whole trick of belonging—nobody grants it to you. You just write your name in the space."

Felix looked at the paper for a long time. Then they went to the counter, took up the good pen, the one Odile wasn't allowed to touch, and wrote in the blank line—not the lost name sleeping in the grove, and not *Keeper*, either, though that was printed neatly below as a courtesy.

What they wrote was: *the family of the Gilded Thorn, jointly and severally, all of us, in perpetuity.*

Vale read it. Her eyebrows went up. "That's not how warrants work."

"No," Felix agreed, blotting the ink. "But we're a guild that trades in impossible things. File it accordingly."

Somewhere east of the city, in a grove of listening trees, it began—very softly, very distinctly—to rain, though the sky was clear, and the sound it made on the leaves was unmistakably, improbably, laughter.
