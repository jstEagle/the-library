## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The fog came down off the shoulders of the hills every morning like an old dog settling in for a nap, and every morning Emrys Delacroix let it.

She had never known anything else. Ashvale did not have weather so much as a temperament — damp, secretive, inclined to sulking — and the fog was its chief virtue. Other places, she'd been told by her mother, had skies. They had sun you could point at and horizons you could see and neighbors three valleys over who might notice what you were. Ashvale had none of that. Ashvale had a wool blanket pulled up to its chin, and Emrys had learned to be grateful for the dark.

"Hyacinth," she said that morning, in the voice she used for everything — plain, flat, valley-bored, "you are standing on my foot."

Hyacinth, a goat with the coloring of scorched bread and the personality of a debt collector, declined to move.

Emrys sighed, shifted her weight, and tried again. "I will turn you into a rug."

Nothing. Hyacinth chewed.

So Emrys glanced around the barnyard out of habit rather than need — there was no one, there was never anyone, the fog swallowed the lane whole — and then she opened the other door inside herself, the one she kept shut with both hands most hours of the day, and spoke.

*Move, your grace. My toes are not fodder.*

It didn't sound like sound. That was the trouble with it. It went straight from her chest into the goat's skull without troubling the air between them, the way warmth goes through a wall from a hearth. And Hyacinth — imperious, ungrateful, beloved Hyacinth — stepped aside at once and butted Emrys's hip in apology, which is to say in acknowledgment that an apology would not be forthcoming.

"There," Emrys muttered aloud, rubbing her bruised foot. "Was that so hard?"

From the paddock came the low, rolling commentary of the rest of the herd: eleven goats who considered themselves less livestock than a small parliament, with Emrys as their harried clerk. Old Bramble, the wether, held the seat of elder statesman and abused it constantly. The twins, Sorrel and Sorrel-Also (Emrys had named them as a kid and refused to revisit the decision), were backbench rabble-rousers. And Marigold —

Marigold was watching her. Goats watched everything, but Marigold watched *her*, with yellow eyes that had always seemed to know more than a creature who ate thistles should. She'd been her mother's favorite too. There were days when Emrys suspected the herd remembered more about Mama than Emrys did, tucked away in whatever place goats kept their knowing.

*I remember her hands,* Marigold had once told her, years ago, in that voice-that-wasn't-a-voice. *They smelled of rosemary and lamp oil.*

Emrys had cried into Marigold's coarse neck for an hour after that, and neither of them had ever mentioned it again. That was another thing the gift taught you: some doors open only once, and you learn to leave them closed.

She milked, she fed, she mucked, and she talked — aloud, harmlessly, the way any lonely woman talks to her animals — while underneath it ran the quiet current of the other speech, the true one, the one that would see her ash if a soul in this valley heard it.

Not heard it. That was the maddening part. No one could *hear* it. If a king's man stood in this barnyard and she spoke beast-speech until her lungs gave out, he'd hear nothing but wind. But the edict hadn't come yet, and her mother's warnings were older than edicts, older than kings: *They don't need proof, love. Suspicion burns just as hot.*

So: concealment. Concealment in the morning, concealment at noon, concealment laid over her life like a second skin, worn so long she sometimes forgot it wasn't skin at all. Until something reminded her.

Market day usually managed it.

* * *

The fog lifted to knee-height by midmorning, the way it did on market days, as though even the valley understood that commerce required one to occasionally see whom one was cheating. The village of Hollowmere straggled along its single street like a row of crooked teeth, and twice a month those teeth bit down on carts and stalls and the smell of frying onions, and everyone within four miles came to trade gossip and grudge in equal measure.

Emrys walked in with two wheels of cheese and a sack of late apples and her face arranged into its market expression: pleasant, forgettable, a touch slow around the eyes. It had taken her years to perfect the slowness. Clever women got asked clever questions.

"Emrys Delacroix!" bellowed Tam Wicker, the smith, whose voice could have curdled milk at forty paces. "Still keeping goats, then? Still talking to 'em?"

"Someone has to," she called back, with the exact right note of weary humor. Laughter rippled. Good. Laughing people don't look closely.

It was a joke the whole valley knew: mad Emrys and her muttering. Her mother had built it deliberately, brick by brick over years — a reputation as armor, a little madness worn openly so no one would ever look for what was hidden beneath. *Let them think you're odd,* Mama used to say, braiding Emrys's hair before market. *Odd is safe. Odd gets left alone.* Then Mama would smile, and the smile would flicker, just once, like a lamp in a draft, and go out.

Emrys set up her stall between the weaver's cart and Old Nan Pell's astonishing collection of roots, tubers, and things Nan insisted were medicinal and everyone else suspected were decorative. Business was business. Cheese changed hands. Apples changed hands. A neighbor's boy paid for his mother's order with coins counted twice because he was seven and proud of arithmetic.

And then, in the gaps between transactions, the talk started. Talk always started; it was the valley's real currency.

"Fog's thinner up past the saddle this season," someone said. "My brother-in-law swears he saw the ridge line clear as noonday."

"Fog's been thin since the old queen died," said Nan Pell darkly, "and don't let anyone tell you different."

"The old queen's been dead twenty years, Nan."

"And the fog's been thinning twenty years, boy. I count better than you."

Emrys weighed out cheese for a customer and kept her eyes down and her ears open, the way she'd been taught before she could read.

Because here was the arithmetic nobody else in Hollowmere seemed to be doing: the fog was not merely weather. Every gifted child raised in Ashvale was taught it early, in whispers, the way other children were taught prayers. The valley's fog was a kindness — or a wound — left over from the fall of Sablemark, the drowned kingdom whose name had been scraped off maps and out of songs a lifetime before Emrys was born. Magic had broken something there, broke it so thoroughly that the land itself had scarred over, and in the scar-tissue of Ashvale magic simply... couldn't live. It slid off the fog like rain off oiled cloth. Which meant the fog hid the gifted. Which meant the gifted had crept here for generations, generation after generation, until half the valley's bloodlines carried something small and secret: a hedge-healer's knack for fever, a farrier's uncanny way with horses, a girl who spoke to goats.

Small magics. Dampened magics. Magics smothered under wool and fog and silence, alive the way embers are alive under ash — which is to say, alive exactly as long as nobody stirs them.

"My cousin's girl," said the weaver's wife, lowering her voice in the way of people who want very much to be overheard, "down in the river towns — she says the new king's criers have gone through every market from here to the coast. New edict. About the Gifted."

The word dropped into the market square like a stone into a well, and Emrys felt the splash of it go through everyone, all at once, in the sudden careful stillness of thirty faces pretending not to listen.

"What sort of edict?" asked Tam Wicker, too loudly.

"Testing," the weaver's wife said, savoring it. "Every soul with the Gift is to be registered. Taken to the capital. For testing." She pronounced the word as though it might bite.

"Well," said Tam, after a moment, "good riddance, if you ask me. Nothing good ever came of—"

"You'd say that," snapped Nan Pell, "with your grandfather being a horse-whisperer and your aunt lighting candles with a snap of her fingers, may she rest."

"That was *indigestion,* Nan—"

Laughter, nervous and too quick, papering over the crack. But the crack was there now. Emrys saw it in how people looked at each other afterward — a half-beat too long, a question behind the eyes where friendliness used to sit. The weaver's wife looking at the farrier's son, whose horses were famously calm. The miller glancing at his own daughter, who talked in her sleep in no language anyone knew.

Faces turning calculating. Not cruel, not yet. Just... counting. Counting neighbors the way you count coins, wondering which ones ring false.

Emrys folded her hands on her counter and made herself breathe.

Inside her chest, behind the door she kept locked, something stirred — the ember under the ash, turning over in its sleep. She pressed down on it. *No. Quiet. We are cheese-sellers and goat-farmers and nothing else, and we are dull, dull, dull.*

A customer bought her last wheel of cheese. Another bought apples. The market went on, because markets do, and the word *testing* was passed along the street like a cold caught from a sneeze, and by the time the light began to fail, everyone in Hollowmere had it and no one quite knew what to do with it except repeat it.

Emrys packed her empty baskets and started home with the fog closing back around her ankles like a faithful hound coming to heel.

* * *

The walk home took an hour along the drover's path, and she spent it doing what she always did on the walk home: letting the mask loosen joint by joint. Shoulders first. Jaw next. By the last mile she was only herself, tired and twenty-six and alone, and the valley was only itself, gray and green and smelling of wet peat, and the two of them understood each other perfectly.

At the gate, the parliament was waiting with grievances.

*The tall one fed us late,* reported Sorrel.

*The tall one is YOU, Sorrel,* said Bramble wearily. *You fed yourself. That was the problem.*

*There is a strange smell on the wind,* offered Marigold, quietly, from her place on the rise.

Emrys paused, hand on the gate latch. "Strange how?"

But Marigold only turned her yellow eyes toward the western pass, where the fog lay thick and unmoving as ever, and said nothing more, in the manner of goats, who dispense prophecy at their own convenience and consider follow-up questions vulgar.

Emrys stood a moment in the gathering dusk, listening. Wind in the gorse. Water somewhere under the hill. The ordinary sounds of a valley that had forgotten how to be anything else.

Probably nothing, she told herself. Foxes. Weather. Nan Pell's roots finally getting to somebody.

She let the gate swing shut behind her, and the latch clicked home like a small, final sentence.

Inside the cottage, she lit the lamp, and its glow made a warm island in the dark, and she sat with her supper and her mother's old shawl around her shoulders, and she thought — not for the first time — about how a life could be built entirely of doors kept shut. Door against the gift. Door against the neighbors' questions. Door against the wide world beyond the passes, where kings issued edicts and queens died and kingdoms drowned. Twenty-six years of shutting things, done so well she could barely remember what the rooms behind those doors looked like.

She was so tired of it. That was the terrible truth she only admitted to herself at night, in lamplight, with no witnesses but eleven goats dozing in the barn and one old ewe-shaped ghost of memory. She was bone-tired of being small on purpose. Tired of the slowness around her eyes. Tired of loving a valley that loved her back only on the condition that she remain nothing at all.

Outside, far off toward the western pass, something called.

It was faint — a horn-note, maybe, or wind through rock, the fog bending sound the way it bent everything — but it came again, longer, and Emrys sat very still with her spoon halfway to her mouth.

Then, high above the cottage, riding the night air she could not see, a crow answered her.

Not aloud. Not in crow-speech either. In *her* speech — the voice-that-wasn't-a-voice sliding into her head uninvited, dry as old paper, amused as a judge:

*They are coming, little heir of voices. The ones who test. And they are asking, in every market from here to the coast, whether anyone has seen a girl who talks to beasts.*

Emrys did not move. Did not breathe. The lamp flame stood perfectly straight, as if it too were listening.

*Well?* said the crow. *What will you tell them?*
