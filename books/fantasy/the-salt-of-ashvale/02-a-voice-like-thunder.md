## 2. A Voice Like Thunder

The criers came on a Tuesday, which Emrys would later decide was typical of kings. Kings, in her limited experience of them—limited to proclamations nailed to the market post and the occasional tax collector—had never once done anything useful on a day when people were busy. They always arrived at midweek, when the bread was half-baked and the goats needed milking, as though inconvenience were a royal prerogative.

She was elbow-deep in a bucket of feed when the first horn sounded from the direction of the village. It was a long, brassy note that rolled up the valley and set every dog in Ashvale barking in a ragged chorus, and beneath it came the foghorn-deep voice of a crier trained to be heard over armies.

"By order of His Majesty, King Aldric, Second of That Name, Protector of the Realm and Shield Against the Old Darkness—"

Emrys kept her arms moving. Grain into troughs. Grain into troughs.

"—let it be known throughout the vale that a Gift-Hunt is declared. All persons harboring unnatural gifts, of whatever kind or degree, are commanded to present themselves for testing at the capital within forty days."

The bucket went very still in her hands.

"All such persons will be received with honor," the horn-voice continued, in the tone of a man reciting words he did not believe, "tested fairly by the King's Own Assessors, and given places fitting their talents in His Majesty's service."

*Places fitting their talents.* Emrys had heard what happened to "talented" folk in the capital. Everyone had heard, though nobody said it above a whisper, and the whispers had gotten quieter these last two years, the way a fire gets quieter just before someone throws a blanket over it entirely. The last hedge-witch down in Millbrook who'd answered a summons like this one had sent back letters for a while—cheerful letters, suspiciously cheerful letters, all about how lovely the capital was and how well she was being fed—and then the letters had stopped, and when her sister had gone to ask after her, the King's clerks had produced a document saying the woman had taken a position in the royal household and could not receive visitors. A position. As though magic were a job one clocked into and out of, with wages and a pension and perhaps a small garden.

The horn sounded again from the far end of the valley, and then a third time, so that the proclamation seemed to come from everywhere at once, which Emrys supposed was the point. You couldn't run from a sound.

She looked down at the goats.

They were looking back at her. Eleven pairs of eyes, patient and yellow, waiting to hear what she'd say. Marigold, the eldest doe, gave a low, questioning bleat—the goat equivalent of *well?*

"It's nothing," Emrys said aloud, because saying it aloud made it more true, or she hoped it did. "Proclamations are nothing. Paper and noise."

The goats did not look convinced. Goats rarely do.

*It's nothing,* she thought at them, harder, folding the thought into the wordless way she spoke to beasts, the way that wasn't speech at all but something older, something that lived under language like water under ice. *Nothing has changed.*

And eleven goats relaxed, because they trusted her, which was the most terrifying thing in the whole valley that morning.

* * *

Market day in Ashvale happened once a week, rain or fog or proclamation, because Ashvale's people held to the belief that commerce was one of the few things sturdier than fear. The square filled with carts and awnings and the smell of frying onions, and by the time Emrys arrived—with a basket of cheese she absolutely should have priced higher—the crier had planted himself in the center of it all like a flagpole, scarlet-liveried, flanked by two soldiers whose armor was too clean to have ever seen weather.

That was its own message. Armor that clean meant the wearer had come from somewhere the sun shone. Somewhere beyond the fog line. Somewhere real.

Emrys took her usual place between Widow Hartley's wool stall and the Perrys' apple cart, and tried to be a woman selling cheese, and only a woman selling cheese, and nothing else in any world.

"Cheese, mistress?" called a voice, and there was Tam Perry, sixteen and all elbows, holding out an apple he had absolutely not been authorized to give away. He grinned at her with the particular grin of boys who have decided a grown woman is safe to tease. "For the cheese. Trade you fair. This one's got a worm in it but he's a friendly worm."

"You'll ruin me, offering quality like that." She took the apple anyway, because refusing Tam was like refusing weather. "What's the news?"

Tam's grin flickered. He glanced toward the crier, and something moved behind his eyes that hadn't been there last market day—a little shutter drawing closed. "You heard it same as us. Gift-Hunt." He lowered his voice. "Da says it's nothing. Says the king's testing is for court wizards and such, folk with lightning in their fingers. Says no one round here's got anything worth hunting."

"No one," Emrys agreed. "Of course not."

"Course not." Tam nodded, satisfied, and turned to bark apples at the next customer, and Emrys stood there with the friendly-worm apple in her hand and felt the ground shift a half-inch under her feet.

Because Tam Perry believed what his father believed. Tam Perry had never once wondered whether his neighbors might have secrets. But today he had glanced toward the crier before speaking. Today he had lowered his voice. And around her, in ones and twos, Emrys watched the same small motion pass through the crowd like wind through barley: people looking at each other. Not looking *for* each other—looking *at* each other. Measuring. Widow Hartley's gaze lingering a beat too long on old Fenwick, whose rheumatism had vanished last spring as if scrubbed away. The miller's wife smiling at the tanner's daughter, who had always been odd, who talked to herself walking home along the river—or had it been to the river?

Nobody said anything. Nobody accused anybody. It was much earlier in the day than that, much earlier in the season. But Emrys had spent her whole life reading faces—not human faces, mostly; she'd never dared practice much on those—but the principle held across species. She knew what a herd looked like when it began to sort itself: when the gentle jostling of animals who trusted each other became the careful spacing of animals deciding who stood where.

A Gift-Hunt didn't need to catch anyone to work. That was the cleverness of it. It simply needed everyone to start wondering about everyone else, and then the valley hunted itself.

She sold her cheese. She smiled at people. She made small talk about rain and root vegetables and the price of lamp oil, and through all of it she kept her gift folded up inside her like a moth shut in a drawer, wings pressed flat, utterly still.

She was good at stillness. She had practiced since she was six years old, since the afternoon her mother had caught her talking to a wounded fox behind the woodshed and had gone white as flour, gripped her shoulders, and said, *Never. Never again, Emrys. Not where walls can hear.* Her mother had died when Emrys was nineteen, taking to the grave both her love and the reason for her terror, and Emrys had kept the rule the way you keep a debt to the dead.

But stillness, she was learning that morning, is a thing that can be measured. A person who is too calm while everyone else is frightened is itself a kind of signal.

So she let herself spill a jar of honey at the Hartley stall, and swore colorfully, and laughed too loudly when Tam helped her mop it up, and generally performed clumsiness until her cheeks ached. If the valley was going to watch her, let it watch a fool. Fools were invisible.

It almost worked.

* * *

The trouble started on the walk home, and the trouble had feathers.

The fog had thickened toward evening, the way it did, rolling down off the fells and pooling in the lanes until the world shrank to twenty paces in any direction. Emrys liked the fog. The fog was Ashvale's oldest resident and its best defender, the gray blanket the valley pulled over its own head. Under fog, she could almost pretend the country beyond—armies, kings, Gift-Hunts—was a story told to children.

She was halfway up the goat path, basket empty, boots muddy, when the crow landed on the wall beside her.

It was a big one, ragged-feathered, with the insolent sideways stare crows reserve for the universe in general. It hopped once closer. Tilted its head.

Emrys stopped. Crows were common enough on the fells, but this one had flown *to* her, deliberately, out of all the fog in the world, and it was looking at her with an attention no ordinary bird wasted on a cheese-seller.

Keep walking, she told herself. Don't think anything. Think about cheese. Think about mud. Think about absolutely nothing—

*You,* said the crow.

Not aloud. Aloud it only made the dry croaking rasp crows make. But underneath the rasp, in the place where meaning lived—in the place where Emrys had spent thirty years hearing things no one else heard—it said *you*, clear as a bell dropped down a well.

Her heart slammed against her ribs. She kept walking. One boot. The other boot. The crow lifted off the wall and glided ahead, landing on the next stone, waiting, patient as a toll collector.

*You,* it said again. *The quiet one. The loud-quiet one.*

There was no word for what she was doing as she walked—she was thinking at it without meaning to, the way your hand goes to a wound. *I don't know what you mean. I'm just a woman going home.*

The crow made a sound that was unmistakably, impossibly, laughter.

*All the two-legs are shouting today,* it said. *Shouting about gifts, about testing, about the king's iron city. Shout-shout-shout. But you—you fold yourself up small. I can see the folds.*

*Then you can see wrong,* Emrys said, and put every ounce of dullness she owned into it, the flat gray taste of fog and turnips and minding one's own business. *I'm a cheesemaker. My greatest gift is curd.*

Another rasping laugh. The crow hopped along the wall beside her, escorting her now, and Emrys understood with cold clarity that she was having a conversation in broad evening light on a public path, that if anyone from the village crested the hill right now they would see her staring at a bird, and birds stared back sometimes, didn't they, that was normal, people talked to crows all the time—

No. They didn't. People didn't. Not anymore. Perhaps not ever, in Ashvale, not in living memory.

*I've a message,* the crow said, casual as gossip.

*Take it elsewhere.*

*Can't. It's yours.* The bird ruffled its feathers, and for just a moment its black eye seemed less like a bird's eye and more like something older wearing one. *The wind brought me up the valley tonight, and the wind says: the fog is thinning, quiet-one. The king's men have instruments now. Iron needles that swing toward what's hidden. Your gray blanket won't cover you much longer.*

Emrys's mouth had gone dry. *Instruments.*

*Needles and glass and men who count,* the crow agreed cheerfully. *Awful chatty, needles. Anyway. Message delivered. Good luck with the curd.* And it spread its wings, and before she could summon a single question—*whose message, sent by whom, why me*—it beat upward into the fog and was gone, leaving only a single black feather drifting down onto the wall like a signature.

Emrys stood in the lane a long time. Somewhere below, the village lights were coming on one by one, warm and gold and ordinary, and she found herself counting them the way you'd count the people at a funeral.

*The fog is thinning.*

She had built her entire life inside that sentence's opposite. Every careful silence, every performed smile, every year of talking to goats in a voice that existed nowhere else in the world—all of it rested on the fog, on Ashvale's forgetfulness, on the blessed gray anonymity of a valley the maps barely bothered with. Take that away, and what remained? A woman with a secret the size of a pyre.

She picked up the feather. Stupid thing to do. Evidence, a sensible woman would call it. She put it in her pocket anyway.

* * *

That night the goats wouldn't settle.

They weren't frightened, exactly—they were agitated in the specific way of animals who know more than they're telling, stamping and rearranging themselves in the barn, and when Emrys finally sat down on the milking stool among them and opened herself, carefully, the way you open a door you're not sure what's behind, the gossip came pouring in.

Strangers on the north road. Two days ago. Men in traveling cloaks, polite enough, asking for beds at the inn. Carrying long boxes wrapped in oilcloth, and the boxes hummed—no, not hummed, that wasn't the word the goats used, the goats didn't have a word, but the sense of it was *hummed*, a thin singing like a wet finger on a rim of glass. Marigold had smelled it from the high pasture and hadn't liked it. None of the beasts on the north side of the valley had liked it. The deer had gone quiet. Even the worms in the north fields, said Hesper the youngest doe, in the vague way goats discuss worms, had turned restless, as though the ground itself were being listened to.

*Instruments,* Emrys said to them, and the word fit the humming like a key.

Eleven goats pressed close around her, warm and solid and smelling of hay and honest dirt, and she leaned her forehead against Marigold's flank and let them hold her up, because her legs had quietly stopped volunteering.

Forty days. The proclamation had given the gifted forty days to surrender themselves, which meant the hunters expected to need no more than forty days to find them. The strangers were already here. The needles were already singing. Somewhere out in the dark, a box wrapped in oilcloth was pointing at her valley, swinging slowly, patiently, toward whatever was hidden in it.

She thought about running. She actually got as far as imagining it—throwing a bundle together, slipping out past the fog-line before dawn—and then she imagined the roads east, the checkpoints, the questions, a woman alone with no trade but cheese and no story but lies, and every lie a thread the needles could pull.

She thought about staying. About stillness. About becoming so perfectly, boringly invisible that even iron would slide past her. And then she remembered the crow's eye, ancient and amused, and understood that the age of invisibility was ending whether she hid well or hid badly. There was no prize for hiding best. There was only a longer wait in the same trap.

Marigold nudged her, gently, the way the old doe did when Emrys stayed up worrying. *Sleep,* said the goat, in the wordless way. *The grass comes again tomorrow. Most things do.*

But as Emrys banked the fire and lay down in her narrow bed, listening to the barn's familiar rustlings through the wall, one last thing drifted up through the fog of her exhaustion—one loose thread her mind refused to drop.

The falcon.

Three days ago, a royal falcon had crashed into her barn, half-dead, bearing a sealed message addressed to Queen Sable—who had been dead for twenty years. She had healed the bird, fed it, asked it—who *sent* you—and the answer had chilled her then and chilled her worse now:

*The one who remembers the Door.*

She had told herself it was fever-talk, a dying bird's delirium. But the falcon had healed strong and true, and it was roosting yet in her loft, and it had not left, and tonight—through the floorboards, faint but unmistakable—she could hear it shifting its feet on the beam, awake, alert, listening to the same darkness she was.

Outside, far off on the north road, something sang. Thin as a wet finger on glass.

The fog was thinning. And Emrys Delacroix lay in the dark, wide awake, holding a black feather, waiting to find out what the fog had been hiding from everyone else all along.
