## 3. The Falcon's Errand

The storm came up the valley the way trouble always did in Ashvale—slowly, then all at once, and mostly aimed at Emrys.

She had spent the afternoon doing what she did every autumn afternoon: arguing with a goat named Duchess about the concept of shelter.

"You have a barn," Emrys said, hands on her hips, rain already needling through her shawl. "It is *right there*. I built it. With my hands. For you."

Duchess chewed thoughtfully and stood in the downpour, radiating the serene defiance of her kind.

*"Barn smells of Bramble,"* Duchess observed, which was true, because Bramble had claimed the barn as his personal kingdom sometime around midsummer and defended it against all comers with the ferocity of a much larger animal.

"Then you'll both learn to share like civilized creatures."

*"We are goats,"* said Duchess. *"Civilization is for people who can't headbutt."*

Emrys sighed and went to fetch Bramble herself, which meant wading into the dark of the barn and being butted affectionately in the thigh by eighty pounds of opinionated wether. She was halfway through the delicate diplomacy of relocating him to the far stall—a negotiation conducted partly in goat-speech, partly in dried apples, and partly in sheer stubbornness—when the sky tore open above the valley and something fell out of it.

Not rain. Not hail. Something with weight and wings.

It hit the barn roof like a dropped anvil, slid down the thatch, and landed in the muck of the yard with a sound Emrys felt in her teeth.

She froze. Bramble froze. Even the rain seemed to hesitate.

"Well," Emrys said to no one sensible. "That's new."

* * *

She found it huddled against the gatepost: a falcon, and not just any falcon. Even soaked and broken, even with one wing bent at an angle wings were never meant to bend, it had the look of something bred in marble halls. Its feathers were the grey of storm-light over slate, banded darker at the tips, and on its left leg sat a band of silver worked into the shape of a crown.

A royal bird. A *king's* bird.

Emrys stood very still in the rain and did the arithmetic every soul in Ashvale did automatically, the way other folk counted coins. Royal bird. King Aldric's mark. The Gift-Hunt criers had come through only days ago, their voices flat as slate roofs, promising testing and mercy in the same breath, which in Emrys's experience meant neither.

The falcon should go back where it came from. The falcon should be someone else's problem. The falcon should be reported, or ignored, or—

Its eye rolled toward her. Gold, ringed with black, and full of the particular fury of a creature that had flown until its heart nearly quit and refused to be embarrassed about it.

"Oh, don't look at me like that," Emrys muttered, kneeling in the mud. "You're the one who fell out of the sky."

The falcon opened its beak. No sound came out. But Emrys felt it anyway—not words, not yet, just a pressure against the part of her mind she kept locked and barred and buried under twenty years of careful silence. A flicker. A spark trying to catch.

*Hungry,* the spark said. *Cold. Falling.*

Her gift stirred before she could stop it, the way a dog stirs when a stranger knocks. She clamped down on it hard—but not entirely. She told herself she was only listening, only reading the poor creature's hurt, the way any beast-wise woman might without ever saying a word aloud. She told herself a great many things while her hands moved of their own accord, gathering the bird against her chest, feeling the frantic hammer of its heart slow by degrees as her warmth seeped into its soaked feathers.

"You are going to be the death of me," she informed it. It blinked at her with terrible dignity, which she chose to take as agreement.

* * *

In the kitchen, with the door bolted and the shutters drawn and Duchess peering accusingly through the window as if Emrys were harboring the bird in *her* barn specifically to spite her, Emrys set about the work of keeping a royal falcon alive.

This involved, in order: a towel warmed by the hearth; a broth of marrow and gristle; splinting the bent wing with a lollipop stick and strips of linen; and a long argument about dignity, which the falcon lost, because dignity does not survive being wrapped in a towel like a hatchling.

*"I have carried messages across three mountain ranges,"* it said at last, when the broth had restored enough of its voice to complain properly. *"I have outridden storms that swallowed whole flocks. I have been struck by hawks, by hail, and once by a wedding arrow, which was nobody's finest moment."* It glared at the splint. *"And now I am swaddled."*

"Yes," Emrys said. "You're very impressive. Drink your broth."

To her mild astonishment, it drank its broth. There was something oddly companionable about the whole business—the fire ticking, the rain working the thatch, a wounded predator glaring at her from inside a towel—and Emrys caught herself almost smiling for the first time in days.

Almost. Because the other thing sat on the table between them, and she couldn't stop looking at it.

The message tube. Silver-capped, sealed with wax the deep red of old blood, pressed with a sigil she didn't recognize: a circle broken by a straight line, like a door standing ajar. And scratched into the cap itself, in a hand that had been shaking—or hurrying—was the address:

**TO HER MAJESTY QUEEN SABLE OF SABLEMARK, AT THE DOOR.**

Emrys read it four times. Each time, the words rearranged themselves into worse shapes.

Queen Sable was dead. Everyone knew Queen Sable was dead; she'd been dead for twenty years, dead along with her whole country, dead so thoroughly that Sablemark wasn't even a word anymore except in old songs sung quietly, and half those songs had been burned too. The kings after the fall had erased it—maps redrawn, roads renamed, records burned in such tidy bonfires that entire coastlines had simply stopped having existed. Children in Ashvale learned there had never been anything east of the marshes but fog and drowned ground.

And yet here was a message, fresh-sealed, addressed to her at a place called the Door, carried by a king's own falcon across mountains and weather to a valley that forgot things on purpose.

The falcon watched her read. She felt its attention like a hand on the back of her neck.

"That seal," she said carefully. "That's the king's bird. That's the king's errand. Why would the king write to a queen who's been dust for twenty years?"

The falcon said nothing. Falcons, in her experience, could be closemouthed as priests when they chose.

"I healed you," Emrys pointed out. "Marrow broth. Warm towel. Splint made from my best honey sticks." She let a little of the beast-voice slip into her tone—not enough to be dangerous, just enough to be *understood*, the way she spoke to frightened ewes. "You owe me an answer, wing-brother."

The falcon roused its feathers, considering. When it finally spoke, its voice in her mind was dry as old paper.

*"I do not carry the sender's name. I carry the seal and the address. A falcon is a road, not a map."*

"But you know something."

Another silence, longer. Outside, the rain softened to a whisper. Duchess gave up her vigil at the window and stalked off toward the barn, offended beyond further comment.

*"I know,"* the falcon said at last, slowly, as if each word cost it something, *"that this message has been sent before. Many times. Over many years. To the same dead address. And every year the roads refuse it, and every year it is given again to a new bird, and every year the birds fly until they fall."* It looked down at its splinted wing. *"Do you understand? I am not the first. I am the eleventh. The others did not come back."*

Emrys sat down slowly on her own stool, which was fortunate, because her knees had opinions of their own.

"Eleven birds," she repeated. "Over how long?"

*"Since the fall of Sablemark. Since the Door was closed."*

"The Door," Emrys said. The word came out of her mouth strange and heavy, like a stone she'd picked up not knowing it was hot. "What door?"

The falcon fixed her with its gold-ringed eye, and Emrys had the sudden, skin-prickling certainty that it was looking not at her face but at something underneath it—at the locked room behind her ribs where the beast-speech lived, the voice she had never once used at full strength, the inheritance her mother had pressed into her hands with her dying breath and a warning: *never in the valley, never where men can hear.*

*"You speak,"* the falcon said softly. It was not a question. It was not even surprised. *"Old speech. Deep speech. The speech the Door answers to."*

Every hair on Emrys's arms stood up. "I speak to animals," she said, which was true, and insufficient, and they both knew it.

The falcon tilted its head. Rain ticked on the roof. The fire popped.

*"Ask me who sent the message,"* it said. *"You've been wanting to ask since you pulled me out of the mud. Ask properly, speaker, and I'll answer what I'm permitted to answer."*

Emrys's mouth was dry. Every prudent instinct she owned—the instincts that had kept her alive through twenty years of market days and crier's proclamations and neighbors' sidelong glances—told her to say nothing, to feed the bird and bind its wing and put it out at dawn and forget the whole impossible evening.

But her mother had also told her, once, in the last winter, in a voice gone thin as candle smoke: *Curiosity is just courage that hasn't found its feet yet. Mind it doesn't walk you off a cliff.*

Emrys leaned forward. She let the smallest thread of the deep voice enter her words—not loud, not carrying past these four walls, but *true*, resonant, the way a bell is true.

"Who sent the message?"

The falcon drew itself up inside its ridiculous towel. Its gold eye caught the firelight and held it.

*"The one who remembers the Door,"* it said.

The words dropped into the kitchen like a stone into a well, and Emrys waited for the splash, the echo, some end to them—and none came. They just kept falling.

"Who—" Her voice cracked. She tried again. "Who *is* that? Who remembers it? Nobody remembers it. It's not a thing that can be remembered. There's nothing *to* remember."

*"Someone remembers,"* the falcon said. *"Someone has remembered for twenty years, well enough to seal wax and spend birds. Eleven of us, speaker. Think what a message must weigh, that eleven falcons have died carrying it east into the fog."* It shifted its splinted wing, wincing. *"Think what it must weigh, that the twelfth fell out of the sky over *this* valley—of all the valleys in the world—rather than turn back."*

"I don't know anything about doors," Emrys said, too quickly, too loudly.

The falcon looked at her. It did not say *you lie*, because it was courteous, and because it didn't have to.

Instead it said: *"My wing will mend in nine days. Ten, if you keep feeding me that broth, which I notice you intend to."* A pause, and something almost gentle entered its dry voice. *"Birds remember kindness longer than they remember orders, whatever my trainers believed. Whatever you decide in nine days—whether you send me on, or hide me, or burn that letter rather than touch it—I will not betray you. But I will tell you one more thing, freely, because you warmed me when I was falling."*

Emrys wrapped her arms around herself. "Say it, then."

*"The message was not sealed in the capital."* The falcon's gaze drifted toward the eastern wall, toward the fog and the marshes and everything beyond them. *"I took flight from the drowned coast. From the edge of the maps. Whatever waits at the end of my road has been waiting there a long time—and lately, speaker, it has begun to wait *impatiently*."*

The fire settled. Somewhere out in the yard, water dripped from the eaves in a slow, patient rhythm, like a knock at a door that hadn't been knocked on in twenty years.

Emrys looked down at the message tube, at the broken-circle seal, at the shaking handwriting addressed to a dead woman at a place that did not exist.

Nine days, the falcon had said.

She had a bad feeling that was exactly how long she had to decide who she was going to be when the twelfth message finally reached its addressee—or failed to.

Outside, far off in the direction of the village inn, a dog began to bark. Then another. Then, faintly beneath them, a sound Emrys had heard only once before in her life, on the day the criers came: hoofbeats, more than one, coming up the valley road in the rain.

The falcon's head snapped toward the window.

*"Ah,"* it said quietly. *"And there is the rest of my news."*
