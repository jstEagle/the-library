## 4. What the Goats Know

The trouble with goats, Emrys had long ago decided, was that they had no respect for the sanctity of a lie.

People lied beautifully. Her neighbors in Ashvale could look a crier full in the face and swear they'd never so much as dreamed of magic. They could smile at the tax collector, nod at the priest of Aldric's new order, and bury their grandmother's charms under the turnip bed without a tremor. People had practice. People had been practicing for twenty years.

Goats, on the other hand, said whatever they liked, whenever they liked, to whomever they liked — provided the whomever was Emrys, which was the only reason she survived it.

She sat on her milking stool in the grey light before dawn, forehead pressed to Marigold's flank, and listened.

*Strangers,* Marigold was saying, in the way goats say things — not words exactly, but a rich stew of image and smell and opinion that Emrys's gift translated into something very like speech. *Strangers on the high path. Two legs, wrong boots, wrong smell. The tall one smells of iron and ink. The small one smells of* — here Marigold paused, chewing, savoring the horror of it — *of lightning.*

"Lightning," Emrys repeated softly. "You mean like after a storm?"

*No.* Marigold's opinion of this question was low. *Like the inside of a storm. Like the air over the fell when the sky is deciding what to do.*

Emrys kept her hands moving, steady on the milk pail, though her heart had begun to knock against her ribs like a trapped bird. *How many?*

*Two that we counted. Maybe three behind them, down where the fog thins by the standing stones. They have a dog that does not smell like a dog.*

That last detail settled into her stomach and went cold there. A dog that did not smell like a dog. She thought of the criers' bells two weeks past, the proclamation nailed to the kirk door with its wax seal fat as a beetle: *By order of His Majesty Aldric, Second of That Name, all persons harboring the Gift shall present themselves for testing.* And beneath the official script, in smaller letters someone in the village had added and no one had dared scrape off: *Testing lasts one day. Burials follow.*

"You're sure," she murmured to Marigold, though asking a goat if she was sure was itself a kind of madness. "You're sure they came up from the south road?"

*The south road has teeth now,* said Marigold, which was goat for yes.

Emrys finished the milking in silence. Around her the barn breathed its familiar warmth — hay dust turning gold in the first thin light, Bramble dozing in the corner stall, old Hesper chewing the same cud she had been philosophically chewing since before Emrys was born. It was a good barn. It was a safe barn. It had been a safe barn her whole life, the way Ashvale had been a safe valley her whole life: because the fog came down off the fells every evening like a mother drawing blankets over a child, and nothing entered, and nothing left, and the world forgot them so thoroughly that even kings forgot.

Except that this morning, apparently, the world had remembered.

* * *

She told herself, walking up through the terraced fields with the pail swinging at her knee, that it might be nothing. Traders sometimes lost their way. Pilgrims sometimes wandered off the shrine roads looking for shortcuts. Two strangers smelling of iron and storm-stuff did not an army make.

But Ashvale hadn't seen an outsider in a generation. Not one. The valley sat at the end of a road that wasn't on any map worth having, ringed by fells that shrugged off travelers with weather and rockslide and, more reliably than either, with the sheer discouraging fact that there was nothing here worth climbing for. The last outsider Emrys could remember hearing of was a tinker when she was six, and he'd stayed two days, sold her mother a copper kettle, and left muttering about a valley that made him feel like he was being politely forgotten.

Her mother would have known what to do with strangers who smelled of lightning. Her mother had known what to do with everything, right up until the fever took her, and even then she'd known how to die with her back straight and her hands folded, telling Emrys, *Keep your voice small, love. Keep your voice small and they'll never hear you.*

Emrys had kept her voice small for twenty years. She spoke to the goats only in the dark of the barn, lips barely moving, the words shaped more than sounded. She had learned to think her beast-speech instead of saying it, folding the gift down inside herself like a letter into a very tight envelope. Some mornings she woke and couldn't remember what her own voice sounded like at full volume. That was the price, and she'd paid it gladly, and paid it daily, and never once resented it except on nights when the wind came down the chimney and sounded almost like a wolf, and she ached so badly to answer it that she had to bite her own wrist.

The fog was burning off the upper fields as she climbed, pulling back in rags. From the sheep-walk below came the bleat of her neighbor Tam Wetherby's flock, and threaded through it, faint but clear, the gossip of his ewes — ewes were worse gossips than goats, if such a thing were possible, though their information was mostly about grass.

This morning it wasn't about grass.

*Men on the ridge,* the ewes were telling each other, agitated, wool-deep in alarm. *Men with a box that hums. The tall man opened the box and the needle inside stood up like a startled adder and he laughed. He laughed at our hill. Who laughs at a hill?*

Emrys stopped walking.

A box that hums. A needle that rose when they passed over land — or over beasts, or over anything touched by the Gift. She had heard of such things only in her mother's oldest stories, stories from before the Edict, from the days when the crown's hunters carried instruments instead of pyres because instruments were quieter. Her mother had called them *truth-needles* and spat, which was the only time Emrys had ever seen her spit.

They weren't lost. Lost men didn't carry truth-needles laughing up sheep-walks.

She turned and looked back down the valley, really looked, for the first time in years. Ashvale lay below her in the morning haze — forty roofs, two mills, one kirk with Aldric's sunburst nailed over the older carving, smoke rising straight and domestic and harmless from thirty chimneys. Fog still pooled in the low fields like milk. The fells stood around it all, green and patient, keeping their old secret.

It had always seemed eternal. It had always seemed like the one place in the world where the shape of her life — the small voice, the folded gift, the careful days measured out like medicine — was simply how life was, everywhere, for everyone. Now she saw it differently: a bowl, and the fog a lid, and the lid lifting.

Marigold trotted up beside her, having abandoned the barn out of pure nosiness. *They are coming down toward the wells,* the goat reported. *The small one keeps tasting the air. He tastes like a heron tastes, all patience and fish.*

"The well road comes past the kirk," Emrys said. "Past everyone."

*Yes,* said Marigold, with the serene cruelty of her kind. *Everyone.*

* * *

Market day saved her from having to decide anything, in the way that market days decide everything whether you want them to or not.

By midmorning the square was thick with the usual commerce — Tam Wetherby's cheese, the Fenwick sisters' eggs, a pig whose opinions of the entire proceedings were loud enough to be heard by ordinary ears. Emrys sold her soft goat-milk cheese from her usual corner, wrapped in dock leaves, and kept her eyes down and her voice pitched at market-murmur, and told herself she was calm.

Then she saw them.

They came into the square the way a stone enters a pond. Two men — no, three; the third hung back by the well, pretending to drink, watching everything. The tall one wore a traveler's coat too fine for a traveler, city-cut, and carried himself like a man accustomed to rooms where people stood when he entered. The small one — heron-patient, sharp-faced — carried a case slung at his hip that he held the way a priest holds relics.

The whole square rearranged itself around them subtly, the way water rearranges around a dropped stone. Conversations dipped. The Fenwick sisters' smiles went fixed. Old Marta, selling onions, suddenly found urgent business with her own apron.

"Good morning," the tall man said, to the square at large, with the warmth of a man reading it off a card. "Travelers. We've come up the south road — remarkable country. Remarkable fog." He smiled. It was a good smile. It was the sort of smile that had been practiced in front of mirrors by people who understood what mirrors were for. "We're surveyors. Crown surveyors. Mapping the remoter valleys at His Majesty's pleasure."

Nobody said anything. In Ashvale, silence was a civic art.

"Cheese," the tall man said pleasantly, arriving at Emrys's corner before she could arrange to be elsewhere. He picked up a wheel, weighed it in his hand, set down a coin that was worth three times the cheese. "Keep the change. You don't get much company up here, I'd imagine."

"No," Emrys said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Roads are bad."

"Mm. Bad roads keep a place honest." He peeled back the dock leaf, sniffed the cheese, took a bite, chewed with genuine appreciation. Up close he smelled of ink and iron, just as Marigold had said, and underneath that of something else — cold stone, deep underground, the smell of a cellar that had never once been opened for airing. "Tell me," he said, conversational, terrible. "Folk in these parts keep animals, I see. Goats, sheep. Any of them... special?"

"Special," Emrys repeated.

"Unusual. Clever. Dogs that find things they shouldn't. Beasts that seem to understand more than beasts should." He said it lightly, lightly, a knife worn as a hat pin. "The crown pays for reports. Curiosities. It's all quite harmless."

"They're goats," Emrys said. "They eat my shirtsleeves. That's the extent of it."

He laughed — a real laugh, warm as fresh bread, and somehow that was worse. "Well. If any shirt-eater develops a talent for prophecy, you know where to write." He tipped an imaginary hat and moved on, and Emrys watched him go, and watched the small man by the well open his case a finger's width, and watch the needle inside stand up like a startled adder as a pigeon flew overhead.

The pigeon. Just a pigeon.

But the needle had risen anyway, hungry, undiscriminating, drinking in the faint animal magic that lived in everything alive — and Emrys understood, with a lurch like the ground tilting, that the fog had never hidden her at all. It had hidden her from eyes. From memory. From maps. But a needle didn't need to see. A needle tasted the air the way a heron tastes, all patience, and somewhere in this valley was a thing that could tell the difference between a pigeon's spark of wildness and a woman who had spent twenty years learning to speak wolf.

She sold the rest of her cheese without tasting a bite of the morning.

* * *

That evening she did not go to the kirk, where a deacon read out new regulations about the reporting of unusual livestock. Instead she sat in the barn among her goats with the door barred and a single candle lit, and let them talk, because tonight she needed to hear everything they knew, and the goats knew everything; the goats always knew everything.

Hesper, ancient and unbothered, chewed and reported. The strangers had gone up to the standing stones and spent the afternoon there, the small one walking slow circles with his humming box, the tall one standing very still facing south, as if listening for something beyond the fells. They had spoken little, but Hesper had grazed close — Hesper grazed close to everything; it was her one vice — and had caught fragments.

*They said,* Hesper announced, with tremendous gravity, *that the readings are weak but not empty. Weak but not empty. And the tall one said—"*

She paused. Goats loved a pause.

*"Said, 'Twenty years the map showed nothing, and now the needle stirs. Something woke. Find it before it finds its feet.'"*

The candle flame leaned in the draft, though there was no draft.

Bramble pressed her warm head against Emrys's knee, sensing — as beasts always sensed — that the human was frightened. Emrys buried her fingers in the coarse warmth of Bramble's neck and stared at the candle and did the arithmetic she had been avoiding all day.

Weak but not empty. The fog muffled her, then. Twenty years of silence had kept her a whisper instead of a shout. But whispers could be followed. And the strangers were not leaving. The small one had bought bread from the Fenwicks and asked, casually, casually, which farms kept animals apart from the flocks — separate animals, solitary ones. Ones kept close to the house.

She thought of Brannoc, the falcon — the wounded royal falcon sleeping now in her loft, leg splinted, message sealed against its breast, sent to a queen twenty years dead by someone who remembered a Door. She thought of the way the bird's eyes had fixed on her when she'd asked, in the smallest voice she owned, *who sent you*, and the way the answer had come back like a stone dropping down a well:

*The one who remembers the Door.*

The falcon was a spark the size of a bonfire. Every day it slept in her loft, the needle in the small man's case must be twitching like a hooked fish. Sooner or later — soon, soon — the strangers would walk up her lane with their polite smiles and their humming box, and they would find a widowless cottage, a herd of ordinary goats, and one bird wearing a royal seal, and none of her smallness would matter anymore.

*I have to move him,* she thought. *Tonight. Over the fell, away from—*

And every goat in the barn turned and looked at her at once.

Not at the door. Not at the loft. At *her* — forty amber eyes catching the candlelight, ears forward, utterly silent, in the particular attentive way they had only ever shown her, the way that meant they were listening to the shape of her thinking, the way that meant they already knew what she was, had always known, and had kept her secret the way they kept everything: loudly, to no one who mattered, and faithfully.

*We knew,* Marigold said simply, into the silence. *We always knew. You smell like home.*

Emrys put her face in her hands and laughed, or cried, or both, quietly, among the goats, while outside the fog rolled down off the fells to cover the valley one more time — thinner now, she could feel it, thinner every year, a blanket worn through at the elbows.

Tomorrow, she decided. Tomorrow she would take the falcon over the north fell. Tonight—

Hooves on the lane. One horse, unhurried, stopping at her gate. A knock at the cottage door, polite as a knife laid flat on a table, and a stranger's voice calling through the wood:

"Mistress Delacroix? Forgive the hour. My name is Torv. I believe you have something in your loft that belongs to my family — and time, mistress, is shorter than either of us would like."
