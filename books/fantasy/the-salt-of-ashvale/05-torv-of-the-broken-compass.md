## 5. Torv of the Broken Compass

The inn at Ashvale was called the Drowned Bell, which Torv had always considered an honest sort of name. Everything in this valley sounded drowned. The fog pressed against the windows like a patient creditor, and even the fire in the hearth seemed to burn quietly, apologetically, as though it didn't want to disturb anyone.

Torv sat at his usual table—the one nearest the wall, farthest from the door, with a clear view of both—and drank his fourth ale of the evening with the discipline of a man who had once measured coastlines to the width of a hair and now measured only the distance between himself and the bottom of a cup.

He was forty-one years old. He looked fifty-five on bad days and sixty-two on honest ones. His beard had gone the color of wet ash, his hands were stained permanently with old ink that no scrubbing would lift, and around his neck, on a cord he never removed, hung a brass compass whose needle pointed nowhere at all—had pointed nowhere for twenty-three years, spinning in slow, drunken circles whenever anyone consulted it.

*The Kavanagh compass,* people used to call it, back when that name meant something. Back when the Kavanaghs drew maps for queens.

Now it pointed at nothing, and so, more or less, did Torv.

"Another?" asked Marta the innkeeper, materializing beside him with the jug. She was a broad, kind-faced woman who had decided years ago that Torv was a harmless drunk rather than a dangerous one, and had built her entire business model around the distinction.

"One," he said. "A small one."

"You said that three ales ago."

"And I've kept faith with it each time. That's practically virtue."

Marta snorted and filled the cup anyway. "You'll be wanting food with that. You're getting thin."

"I'm getting *economical.* There's a difference. Thin men buy fewer coats."

"Thin men fall down stairs." She set a bowl of stew in front of him without being asked and without mentioning payment, which was her other habit, and the one that made him feel like a thief every single night. "Goat stew. From the Delacroix girl's stock. Best meat in the valley, if you ask me."

"It's the only meat in the valley," Torv said, but he ate it, because she was right about the second part too.

He had been in Ashvale four months now. Long enough to learn that the fog never lifted before midmorning; long enough to know that the miller cheated on flour weights and the smith watered his beer and everyone knew both facts and no one said them aloud, because in a valley where survival was narrow, courtesy was load-bearing. Long enough to notice the strange quality of the place itself—the way sounds died quickly here, the way birds flew over the fog-line and did not land, the way the goats of a certain shepherdess watched people with what he could only describe as *opinions.*

And long enough to know that he was hiding from something that had eventually, inevitably, begun to look like purpose.

His family had been cartographers to the crown of Sablemark, back when Sablemark existed. That sentence required unpacking, and Torv had spent twenty-three years refusing to unpack it. The short version, the tavern version: the kingdom of Sablemark had fallen in a single night—a catastrophe of magic that swallowed its capital, poisoned its coasts, and frightened the surviving powers so thoroughly that they erased the country from every map, every record, every memory they could reach. The Kavanagh family, keepers of Sablemark's surveys and sea-charts, had been given a choice: burn their archive or be burned with it.

His grandfather had chosen a third option. He had hidden the true maps inside a false one—a vellum sheet that showed nothing, blank as a winter sky—and smuggled it out under the noses of the king's men, along with the broken compass, and passed both down through the generations with instructions Torv's father had delivered with wine-flavored solemnity every solstice:

*Someday, someone will come asking after the Door. When they do, boy, you'd better not be drunk.*

Torv looked into his ale. "No promises, Da," he murmured.

The trouble was that nobody ever came asking. Twenty-three years of nobody. He had waited through his twenties with his ears pricked like a dog at a door, and through his thirties with his ears drooping, and now, in his forties, he had come to the fog-bound valley where his grandfather had first hidden, telling himself it was sentimentality when it was really just surrender with better scenery.

He carried the blank map everywhere. It lived rolled in oilcloth inside his coat, against his ribs, where he could feel it like a second heartbeat. Sometimes, late at night, he unrolled it by candlelight and stared at all that beautiful nothing and hated it with the particular tenderness one reserves for family.

Tonight, however, something was different. Tonight the Drowned Bell had a guest.

She had arrived at dusk, soaked to the knees and furious about it, and Torv had noticed her the way a man notices weather—not because she was remarkable, though she was, but because she changed everything. A girl of perhaps nineteen or twenty, dark-haired, sharp-eyed, with the sun-browned forearms of someone who worked animals for a living and the wary stillness of someone who had learned young how to be invisible in plain sight.

Marta had fed her stew. The girl had paid in small coin, counted carefully, and taken the corner room upstairs. And she had carried, wrapped in her shawl, a falcon.

That was the thing. That was the thing that had made Torv's heart perform a maneuver it hadn't attempted since his youth.

He knew raptors. Any cartographer of the old school knew raptors—they were the crown's couriers, faster than any rider, trained to fly sealed messages across kingdoms. And this bird was unmistakably royal stock: a gyrfalcon, white-and-slate, the kind bred only in the mews of kings. Its left wing had been bound with a splint of lashed reed, competent work, the work of someone who knew animals but not necessarily birds. And on its leg, above the jesses, sat a ring of dull silver stamped with a seal.

Torv had seen that seal perhaps two hundred times in his childhood. It was pressed into the wax of half the documents his father had ever handled. A tower beneath a wave. The mark of Queen Sable's own household.

Queen Sable, who had died twenty years ago, when Torv was eighteen and the world still had edges.

He told himself he was imagining it. He told himself this while watching the girl's table all evening, and while noting that she ate like someone rationing joy, and while noting further—this was the detail that finished him—that the falcon's head turned toward her every time she moved, tracking her with the absolute, uncomplicated devotion of a creature that has decided whom it belongs to.

Royal falcons did not imprint on goat girls. Royal falcons did not crash into barns in forgotten valleys by accident. Royal falcons *carried things.*

Torv drank, and thought, and felt the old compass knocking gently against his chest as though it, too, had noticed.

*You'd better not be drunk, boy.*

He was moderately drunk. It would have to do.

* * *

She came down near midnight, when the common room had emptied to Torv, two sleeping drovers, and a cat with a proprietary attitude toward the hearth. She moved quietly, heading for the door—out for air, or out for the bird, or simply out, the way hunted things sometimes need to be out.

Torv stood up too fast. The room performed a small, courteous rotation, and he gripped the back of his chair until it agreed to stop.

"Master Delacroix," he said.

The girl stopped. Turned. Her eyes did a thing he recognized from his own mirror: the swift inventory of exits, weights, weapons, witnesses. "I don't think we've met."

"No. But Marta talks, and I listen. It's my only talent besides drawing." He gestured at the empty chair opposite his. "Sit? Please. Five minutes. I promise to be either brief or fascinating, and I'm honestly not sure which."

"I should—"

"The falcon's wing is splinted well but bound tight," Torv said, and watched her go absolutely still. "Whoever did it knows beasts. Birds are different. The joint will stiffen if the wrap isn't loosened tonight. I spent six years charting the northern eyries. I've seen a hundred birds grounded by well-meant bandages."

It wasn't entirely true. He'd spent four years at the eyries and had personally bandaged exactly one hawk, badly. But it landed. He saw it land—the flicker in her face, the calculation behind her eyes shifting from *threat* to *problem*, which was the only doorway he was going to get.

"He's sleeping," she said finally. "Waking him to fuss at the wrap would upset him more than the wrap does."

"A practical answer." Torv sat down slowly, and after a moment—curiosity beating caution, always, in the end—she sat too, perched on the chair's edge like a bird herself, ready to launch. Up close she smelled of woodsmoke and goat and some sharper note underneath, clean as cold stone. Her eyes were gray-green, the color of the valley itself, and they did not trust him even slightly.

"Who are you?" she asked.

"Torv Kavanagh."

"Should that mean something?"

"It used to." He smiled, and was aware of how the smile must look—weathered, wry, a little sad around the edges. "My family made maps. Good ones. Maps of places that don't exist anymore, which is a specialty, if not a lucrative one. These days I mostly drink and annoy Marta."

Her gaze flicked to his cup, then back. "And you know falcons."

"I know *that* falcon's lineage." He lowered his voice. This was the point of no return, and he stepped over it the way he'd once stepped off a cliff-face ledge onto a handhold barely wider than his boot: with total commitment and immediate regret. "White gyrfalcon, slate-barred, hatched in the royal mews of Aldric's court—I'd wager my last coin on it. And wearing a courier's ring stamped with the seal of Queen Sable's household." He paused. "Which is interesting, because Queen Sable has been dead for twenty years, and Aldric's court doesn't exactly send gifts to goat farms."

The silence stretched. Outside, the fog leaned against the shutters. Somewhere upstairs, faintly, the falcon shifted and clicked its beak—a sound like a small lock turning.

Emrys Delacroix—though he didn't yet have the surname confirmed, only Marta's gossip—looked at him for a long, long moment. And then she did something unexpected. She laughed. Quietly, without much humor, but genuinely.

"You're drunk," she said.

"Moderately. It's my best state. Sober, I'm insufferable."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because—" and here the truth came up out of him like water finding a crack, twenty-three years of it arriving all at once, and he heard his own voice go rough, "—because I have spent my whole life waiting for that bird. Or one like her. Because my family buried a secret that no one ever came to dig up, and I have grown old and soft and foolish carrying it, and tonight a queen's falcon walks—or crashes—into the same valley where my grandfather hid, on the leg of a girl who binds wings like a beast-speaker and watches doors like a fugitive, and I am *tired*, mistress, I am so tired of being the only one who remembers."

He stopped. Breathed. Realized he was leaning forward with his hands flat on the table like a man begging, and straightened up, mortified.

The girl hadn't moved. But something in her face had changed—not softened, exactly. More like a window opening in a house he'd assumed was shuttered.

"What secret?" she said.

Torv's hand went, without his permission, to his chest—to the cord, to the compass, to the roll of oilcloth beneath his coat. "Before I say another word," he managed, "I want to ask you something, and I want you to understand that I am not your enemy. Whatever you are. Whoever sent your bird. I am not your enemy. I may be the opposite of your enemy. I may be—" he laughed at himself, helpless, "—gods help me, I may be the answer to a question you haven't asked yet."

"That's a lot of promise from a drunk man."

"My grandfather used to say the Kavanaghs were at their best slightly compromised. Courage is easier when the fear is blurred."

Despite herself—one corner of her mouth betrayed her—she almost smiled. Then it was gone, folded away, and she said, quietly: "Ask your question."

Torv took a breath. The whole inn seemed to lean in: the cat, the embers, the fog at the glass.

"The message your falcon carries," he said. "Is it addressed to a dead woman?"

He watched her decide. He actually watched it happen—the fractional settling of her shoulders, the way her eyes went from measuring him to *weighing* him, which is different, which is the difference between a merchant and a judge. Twenty years of watching strangers' faces had taught him every expression except this one. He had no idea which way she'd fall.

"She crashed into my barn three nights ago," Emrys said at last. "Half-dead. Wing torn, starved, flying through weather no bird should cross. There was a message on her leg. Sealed." Her voice dropped to nearly nothing. "It's addressed to Queen Sable of Sablemark. Who has been dead for twenty years. Who ruled a country that isn't supposed to exist anymore."

The words went through Torv like cold iron through warm wax.

Sablemark. Said aloud. In a public room. By a stranger.

He glanced instinctively at the empty corners, the sleeping drovers, the cat—as though the word itself might summon soldiers—and when he looked back, the girl was watching him watch the room, and understanding moved between them like a current.

"You know the name," she said. "Really know it."

"I grew up on it. My father's ghost-stories were survey charts." Torv pulled the oilcloth bundle from inside his coat and laid it on the table between them, and his hands were shaking now, properly shaking, the drink gone suddenly irrelevant. "Twenty-three years I've carried this. My family's charge. Our shame and our inheritance, depending on the decade." He began unwrapping it, slowly, like a man disarming something. "There's a story that goes with it. It's long, and parts of it are terrible, and I'll tell you all of it tomorrow when I can speak without the room swaying. But tonight you should see the outside of it, at least."

The oilcloth fell open. Inside lay a scroll of vellum, ancient and supple, and Torv unrolled just enough of it to show her the top edge.

Blank. Perfect, unbroken, creamy blankness, all the way down.

Emrys frowned. "It's... empty."

"So it appears." Torv touched the vellum's edge with something close to reverence. "To me. To anyone. For twenty-three years it has shown me nothing at all, and I have hated it with my whole heart. But my grandfather swore—and my father swore, and I have no reason left to believe them except that I have never had any other choice—that this map shows its truth to exactly one kind of person." He raised his eyes to hers. "Someone who speaks to beasts."

The fire ticked. The cat yawned. Far above them, through the floorboards, came a single soft sound—the gyrfalcon, shifting in her sleep, settling her good wing.

Emrys had gone very pale. Her hand had risen halfway to her collarbone and stopped there, hovering, as if guarding something beneath her skin.

"You're mad," she whispered. But it wasn't denial. He'd heard denial. Denial was loud. This was quiet, this was the sound of a person standing at the edge of their whole life rearranging itself.

"Almost certainly," Torv agreed gently. "But answer me one thing first, Emrys Delacroix. One question, and then we can both go to bed and pretend none of this happened, if that's what you choose." He leaned forward. "When the falcon came to you—half-dead, out of the storm, out of all the valleys and all the barns in all the world—did she come to you by accident?"

Emrys stared at him. And Torv watched the last wall come down—not crumbling, but lowering, deliberately, like a drawbridge, like a decision—and when she spoke, her voice was so low he had to lean across the table to catch it.

"She looked at me," Emrys said, "and she said, *you're the one who hears.*"

Outside, the fog pressed close against the Drowned Bell's windows, thick and white and listening, and somewhere beyond it—miles away, days away, closing like a hand—something else was pressing too. But inside, at a scarred table by a dying fire, a disgraced mapmaker and a goatherd sat very still, two secrets finally face to face, each recognizing in the other the shape of their own chains.

"Well," said Torv, and reached for his ale with a hand that trembled like his old compass needle. "I believe we're going to need a map."
