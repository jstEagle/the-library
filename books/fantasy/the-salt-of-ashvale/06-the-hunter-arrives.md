## 6. The Hunter Arrives

The fog of Ashvale did not behave like ordinary fog.

Wren had hunted in the fens of Maldros and the pine shadows of the Karst, and fog there was a thing of weather—dumb, drifting, obedient to wind. Ashvale's fog was a wall. It stood at the valley's mouth like a sentry that had forgotten it was allowed to leave, and his hounds balked at it the way they never balked at anything else.

"Come on," he murmured to them, low, the voice he saved for work. "It's only weather."

Sable, the eldest, looked back at him with an expression he chose not to interpret as contempt.

He walked them through anyway. And on the far side, the world changed its smell.

That was the first strange thing: inside the valley, the hounds went quiet—not the quiet of nothing found, but the quiet of too much. Every scent was muffled, layered over with damp stone and wet wool and old smoke, as though the whole valley were a room with a heavy curtain drawn across it. Wren crouched, pressed two fingers into the moss beside the road, and thought about what that meant.

Magic left residue. That was the entire premise of his trade. A hedge-healer's cottage smelled of it for years; a battlefield where sorcery had been done smelled of it for centuries. But Ashvale smelled of almost *nothing*, which meant either the valley was clean—

—or the valley was very good at hiding.

He straightened, brushed the moss from his fingers, and let himself feel the small cold pleasure of a puzzle worth solving. It was the only pleasure he permitted himself these days, and he rationed it carefully.

The village proper sat in a bowl of green, a scatter of slate roofs around a well, goats on every slope like grey punctuation marks. Smoke rose thin and domestic from chimneys. Somewhere a woman was singing off-key to a child. It looked like peace.

Wren had learned, long ago, that places that looked like peace were either peaceful or practicing.

* * *

The inn was called The Drowned Bell, which struck him as an odd name until he asked the innkeeper's boy about it and got a shrug so comprehensive it seemed to include the boy's opinions on all subjects everywhere.

"One room," Wren said. "Stabling for the hounds. Hot water."

The boy's eyes flicked to the dogs—at their broad skulls, the iron-grey collars stamped with the crown's mark—and something moved behind his face. Fear, yes. But under it, calculation. Wren noted it the way he noted everything: without comment, in the ledger behind his eyes.

Word traveled faster than horses in villages like this. By the time he'd eaten, half the parish would know that the Gift-Hunt had come to Ashvale. Some would lock their doors against him. Others would be already composing what they'd say when they knocked on his door tonight.

Both kinds told him things. He needed both.

He ate his stew slowly and listened to the room. A cartographer drank alone in the corner—a big man gone soft at the edges, with ink stains to the elbow and the particular slumped dignity of someone who used to be somebody. He watched Wren with more attention than fear, which was interesting. Most men watched hunters with fear or flattery. Attention was rarer, and always meant something.

Two farmers argued amiably about a fence line. A girl wiped tables and hummed. An old man by the fire told a story about a goat that had climbed onto the chapel roof, and the room laughed in the comfortable way of people who had heard the story before and loved it anyway.

No one mentioned magic. No one glanced at the door when it opened. Nobody did any of the hundred small guilty things people did when they were hiding something.

Which was, itself, the most suspicious thing Wren had seen in six weeks of hunting.

* * *

He worked the village for three days before he requested interviews, because method mattered. He walked the fields. He bought apples and complimented the orchards. He let the hounds be seen and petted by children—the beasts were trained gentle as lambs unless given a word, and the word stayed in his pocket. He asked about weather, roads, taxes, and the price of wool, and wrote none of it down, because the writing-down was theater and he despised theater.

What he learned, in the manner of water finding cracks:

Ashvale kept no records older than thirty years. Ask why, and you got shrugs, or the fog, or *we've always been as we are.* The parish priest had died in spring and no replacement had been sent, and no one seemed troubled. The valley's boundary stones were worn smooth in a way that suggested they'd been moved, deliberately, within living memory.

And the goats. Everyone had goats. Whole slopes of them. He asked one shepherd why goats and not sheep, and received a look of such profound pity for his ignorance that he nearly laughed for the first time in a year.

*Goats,* the shepherd said, as if explaining rain to a fish. *Goats keep.*

Wren wrote nothing down, but in his ledger behind his eyes, under *Ashvale*, he added: *a valley that forgets on purpose.*

There was a memory attached to that phrase. He put it away quickly, the way a man puts his hand away from a stove he has already burned it on once.

* * *

On the fourth morning, he began the interviews. Officially, this time, with the crown's seal shown and the list taken out: names of every soul in the parish, each to be asked the same eleven questions in the same order, so that lies could be weighed against each other like coins from different mints.

The goat-girl came third.

He knew her name already—Emrys Delacroix, twenty-two years of age, orphaned, keeper of her late mother's herd, known to be solitary, known to be *strange* in the way villagers called anyone strange who preferred animals to gossip. He knew it because the innkeeper's boy had told him for free on the second night, eager, helpful, already learning the shape of betrayal the way children learn skipping rhymes.

She arrived at the inn's back room smelling of hay and rain, with a smear of something unidentifiable on her jaw and the level, unhurried gaze of someone who had decided in advance not to be afraid of him.

"Hunter Wren," she said, reading the seal upside down before he could turn the paper. "You're a long way from the capital."

"The edict travels. I travel faster." He gestured to the chair. "Sit, please. This is routine. Eleven questions, the same for everyone."

"I'm the third?"

"You're the third."

"And how am I doing?"

Something in his chest shifted—an unfamiliar twitch that might, in another life, have been amusement. He kept his face in order. "You haven't answered anything yet."

"Then there's hope for me still."

She sat. She folded her hands. And Wren began.

The questions were dull by design. Name, birth, parents' names and manner of death, trades practiced, illnesses survived, journeys taken in the last five years. She answered cleanly, without the tiny hesitations liars manufactured, without the over-quickness of the rehearsed. When he asked whether she had ever seen "manifestations of the gifted arts"—the edict's own absurd phrasing—she said, "Once a cow bloated terribly and old Marta sang a charm over her, if that counts. The cow lived. Marta mostly sings to her vegetables."

"It doesn't count."

"That's a mercy. Half the county sings to their vegetables."

*Careful*, he thought, and could not tell which of them he was warning.

Question seven was the hinge. It always was. *Have you, at any time, felt a compulsion to communicate with beasts in a manner beyond common husbandry?*

He watched her while he asked it. Not her eyes—liars managed eyes—but her hands, her breath, the small muscles along her jaw.

Her hands stayed folded. Her breath stayed even. And she said, with a faint, rueful tilt of her head: "I talk to my goats all day, Hunter Wren. Mostly complaints. They're terrible listeners. If that's beyond common husbandry, then half of Ashvale ought to be arrested, and you'd need a bigger prison."

It was perfect. That was the trouble. Every answer she gave was perfectly weighted, perfectly humble, perfectly laced with just enough self-deprecation to seem honest. Honesty, real honesty, was clumsy. Real people tripped. This woman moved through his questions like a dancer through a known floor.

Unless, said a colder voice in him, she is simply very good at telling the truth because she has been doing it her whole life.

He set down the paper. He looked at her directly, letting the silence do what questions couldn't.

"Your mother," he said. "How did she die?"

"Fever. Eleven years ago."

"What was her name?"

"Sera Delacroix."

And here it was—the slip, the hairline crack, arriving so fast and so softly he nearly missed it. Because the parish records, such as they were, listed Sera Delacroix as having died *thirteen* years ago. He had read it yesterday evening by candlelight, one of the few documents in the valley not eaten by damp or indifference.

Two years. A small discrepancy. Widows misremembered grief all the time; dates blurred; a daughter might count from the last good year rather than the last one.

Or a daughter might lie about everything that touched the dead, because the dead were the load-bearing walls of a false story, and she knew exactly which walls would fall if she got them wrong.

"Thank you," Wren said quietly. "That will be all."

She rose, paused at the door, and turned back with an expression he hadn't expected—something almost kind.

"They say you turned in your own sister once," she said. "Under the old king, before it was even law. Is that true?"

The room went very still, and the stillness was entirely his.

"That's not one of the eleven questions," he said.

"No," she agreed. "But you'll notice I answer extra ones honestly." And she left, closing the door gently behind her, as though she were the one conducting the interview and had gotten everything she came for.

* * *

He did not sleep that night.

He sat by the inn's dying fire with a cup of wine he didn't drink, and the ledger behind his eyes opened to a page he kept locked, and there she was as always: Liss, seventeen, laughing in the doorway, saying *they'll only test me, Wren, it's only testing now, you said so yourself* —and him, nineteen and righteous and stupid with faith, walking her to the magistrates because the law was the law and magic was dangerous and surely, *surely*, they would test her and find her harmless and send her home.

They had not tested her. Testing was a word for later, for softer kings. Under the old king they had simply burned her, and Wren had stood in the crowd because running would have looked like guilt, and he had watched, and he had written in his report that the matter was concluded satisfactorily.

*Satisfactorily.* He had used that word. He could still see it in his own handwriting.

Now there was a new king with a new edict and the same old fire, and Wren rode ahead of it with hounds bred to smell out what he had sold his sister for, and everyone in the free cities called him the crown's best tracker, and the crown's best tracker sat alone in a fog-bound valley knowing—knowing—that the goat-girl with the steady hands and the honest-seeming eyes was lying about something, and could not decide whether finding out what would be justice or just murder with better paperwork.

Sable padded over and laid her great head on his knee. The hounds were bred to scent magic, but they had other senses too, untrained ones, and sometimes Wren suspected they could smell grief, because they always came to him at night and never in the morning.

"I know," he told her. "I know."

Outside, the fog pressed against the windows like something listening.

He thought about her parting question. *Is that true?* She had asked it knowing the answer—everyone knew the story; it followed him like a scar. She had asked it not to wound him but to show him she wasn't afraid, and underneath that, to tell him something else, something she'd disguised even from herself:

*I see you. I know what you are. Do you?*

Wren finished the wine at last, all of it, and made his decision the way he made all decisions: coldly, in the dark, alone.

Tomorrow he would walk the valley's edges. Tomorrow he would ask about the boundary stones, and the missing priest, and the fog that behaved like a wall. Tomorrow he would find what Ashvale was hiding, because that was what he was for, and a man who abandoned his purpose had nothing left but the watching.

But tonight, one more entry in the ledger behind his eyes, in the place where the truth went that he told no one:

*Emrys Delacroix. Third interview. No manifestations observed.*

*Yet.*

Far across the dark valley, in a barn that smelled of hay and rain, a falcon with a broken seal on its leg slept with its head under its wing—and Wren, who missed nothing, had not yet seen it at all.
