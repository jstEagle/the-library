## 7. Ashes in the Well

The hedge-healer's name was Maren Odo, and she had once cured Emrys's worst goat of a cough with boiled willow bark and a prayer to no particular god. That was the whole of her magic, as far as anyone in Ashvale could tell: herbs, patience, and a voice that made even dying animals feel they were being listened to. She was seventy-one years old and walked with a stick she'd carved herself into the shape of a heron, because, she said, herons never hurry and neither should anyone.

On the morning of her execution, the fog lifted for the first time in nine days, as if the valley itself wanted a clear view of what it was about to do.

* * *

Emrys heard the drums before she understood them. Ashvale had no drummers. Ashvale had goats, arguments about fence lines, and the annual dispute over whose turn it was to dredge the millpond. Drums meant the king, and the king meant the Gift-Hunt had come all the way up the switchback road to their door.

She was in the market square before she fully decided to go, pressed among her neighbors in the grey light, close enough to smell the wet wool of Tam Bricker's coat and the onion on old Sef's breath. The criers stood on the well platform — the same well from which every child in the village had drunk, the same well where Maren Odo had once fished out young Pell Hask when he fell in, using nothing but a rope and a great deal of scolding.

There were six soldiers and one man who was not a soldier. He stood slightly apart, in a coat the color of river mud, watching the crowd the way Emrys watched a storm come over the western ridge: not with fear exactly, but with arithmetic.

"Hedge-witchery," the captain read from a scroll, "is magic. Magic is treason. The traitor Maren Odo is sentenced by decree of His Majesty Aldric, King of Vell and its territories, to public execution, that all who harbor the gifted may witness the crown's mercy in its swiftness."

*Mercy,* thought Emrys, and hated the word.

They brought Maren out between two soldiers. She had been allowed to wash, someone had combed her hair, and she carried her heron stick as though she were simply going out to check on a sick ewe. When her eyes found the crowd, they moved slowly across the faces — and stopped, just briefly, on Emrys.

Maren smiled at her. A small smile. The kind you give a frightened animal so it doesn't bolt.

Emrys's throat closed. *Say something,* said a voice inside her that sounded like her mother, or like what she imagined other people's mothers sounded like. *You can talk to anything with a heartbeat. Say something.*

But there was nothing to say it *to*. No beast would carry her words anywhere useful in time. And if she opened her mouth here, in this square, in front of the man doing his quiet arithmetic — then Maren would be joined by a second pyre, and possibly a third, because Emrys Delacroix had spent twenty-three years learning exactly how much noise her silence required, and she could not unlearn it in a morning.

The executioner was efficient. The crowd made the sound crowds make — a single collective intake of breath, like the valley inhaling.

And then, worse than the drumbeat, worse than the fall of the blade: applause. Scattered at first. Tam Bricker clapped. Then Sef. Then more, a ripple of it, hands coming together in the cold air, because the thing was done now and it was over and *they* were safe, weren't they, safe people applauded their safety, didn't they—

Emrys did not clap. She kept her hands very still inside her sleeves and stared at the well, at the dark water in it, and thought: *I drank from you my whole life. You fed me and I said nothing.*

Beside her, an old woman — Widow Corren, who had taught Emrys to knit badly — dabbed her eyes and whispered, "Poor creature," and then, catching herself, added loudly, "but rules are rules, and better her than the whole valley."

*Better her than us.* Emrys felt those four words settle into her chest like a swallowed stone. She looked down at her boots. There was goose grease on the left one from morning chores. The world continued to contain goose grease and knitting lessons and onions on breath, and Maren Odo was dead on the platform above the well, and the man in the mud-colored coat turned his head — slowly, deliberately — until his gaze crossed the crowd like a lantern beam.

It passed over Emrys. Paused. Moved on.

Or perhaps it hadn't paused at all. Perhaps she only imagined it, the way she imagined everything dangerous.

* * *

That night the fog came back, thick and apologetic, as though the valley wished to hide its own face.

Emrys did not sleep. She sat by her cold hearth with Brannoc — the falcon, who had healed remarkably and now regarded her hearth as his personal property — and listened to the wind worry the barn shutters. The goats had been restless since morning. Goats always knew. They had told her at dawn, in their flat, chewing way: *the tall thin one walked the fence line twice. He touched our gate. He smelled the air by your door.*

"He's going to come back," she said aloud to Brannoc. "He counted the houses today while everyone was watching the platform. I know he did. That's the kind of man he is."

Brannoc clicked his beak. In falcon-speech it was less a word and more a direction: *east.*

"I know east," Emrys said. "East is the pass. East is out." She had been assembling the idea all evening, the way she assembled anything: quietly, stubbornly, one piece at a time. A sack of oats. The good knife. Her mother's ring, which she wore anyway but would grip tighter now. Leave before first light, take the shepherd's track over Cold Fell, and be a rumor by the time the man in the mud-colored coat knocked on any doors.

It was a good plan. It was, in fact, the only plan.

Which was, of course, when someone knocked on her door.

* * *

Her heart performed a maneuver she felt in her teeth. Brannoc went still on his perch, feathers tight, every inch of him a drawn bow.

"Emrys Delacroix." The voice was low, male, and pitched carefully beneath the wind — the voice of a man who did not wish to be heard by neighbors. "I'm not alone, and I'm not the one you're afraid of. May I come in? I'd rather not say the rest through a door."

She considered the poker. She considered the window. She considered that anyone hunting her would have brought soldiers, not manners.

She opened the door on Torv — the cartographer from the inn, the man with the ruined compass and the eyes of someone perpetually reading a map nobody else could see — and behind him, bundled against the fog, he had brought two loaves of bread and an expression of profound apology.

"You shouldn't be here," she said.

"No," he agreed, stepping past her with the confidence of a man who had already decided she wouldn't shout. "Also, I couldn't sleep, the inn smells of tallow, and I've worked out something you need to hear before you do something brave and stupid at dawn." He set the bread on her table as if he'd lived there for years. "You're leaving east. Over Cold Fell. Don't look surprised — you've got the face of a woman rehearsing an escape, and you asked the miller's boy three days ago how long the pass takes to walk. He tells me things. People tell me things; it's the one skill my family left me besides drinking."

"How—"

"Sit down," Torv said gently. "Please. Because running east doesn't take you away from the army, Emrys. Running east takes you *into* it."

* * *

He spread his hands on her table like a man laying out cards he was ashamed of.

"Aldric isn't hunting strays anymore," he said. "The Gift-Hunt in the valleys is housekeeping. The main host crossed the Sorrel last week — forty thousand men, supply trains, siege engines, the works. They're moving on the free cities, and their line of march runs straight under Cold Fell. Your shepherd's track comes down off the mountain directly into their baggage train. Congratulations. You'll have escaped one hunter by delivering yourself to forty thousand."

Emrys sat down slowly. "You're lying."

"I've spent the last ten years being paid to lie beautifully on paper — borders that flatter dukes, coastlines that please admirals. I promised myself I'd stop." He met her eyes. "The host is real. I saw the dust from the ridge two days ago. It looked like the sky had decided to come down and crawl."

Outside, the wind shifted, and for a moment the fog thinned at the window, and Emrys thought of Maren Odo's heron stick lying somewhere in a soldier's pile of confiscated sticks. Thought of the applause. Of Widow Corren's tears drying into *rules are rules.* Of the stone in her chest.

"If I run west," she said, "there's sea. If I run south—"

"South is the Salt Marshes, and beyond the marshes—" Torv stopped. Something moved behind his eyes, quick and guarded, like a fish under ice. "Beyond the marshes is nothing. That's what the maps say."

"And what do *you* say?"

For a long moment he didn't answer. Brannoc ruffled once on his perch, and Emrys noticed Torv watching the bird with an intensity that had nothing casual in it. Watching the falcon the way a man watches a locked box he recognizes.

"I say," Torv said at last, very quietly, "that my family did something terrible a hundred years ago, and were paid well for it, and have been ashamed of it ever since, and that I came to this damp, fog-ridden, admirable little valley because the shame finally outweighed the money." He rubbed his jaw. "I say that tomorrow, Hunter Wren is going to knock on doors starting at the north end of the village, and I say — don't ask me how I know, cartographers know maps and I know men — that he has already narrowed himself down to maybe a dozen houses. Yours is on the list. Mine may be too."

The fireless hearth seemed colder. "Then we're both dead."

"We're both *here*," Torv corrected. "Dead is a scheduling matter." He leaned forward, and now the apology was gone from his face and something harder had taken its place — hope, actually, which was worse. "Emrys. Before you decide anything tonight, I need to ask you a question, and I need you to understand why I'm asking it, because the answer changes which direction we run."

"What question?"

"The message the falcon carried," he said. "When you asked it who sent it — you told me it answered. I haven't stopped thinking about that answer since. *The one who remembers the Door.*" His voice dropped further. "My family helped bury that Door, Emrys. Buried it, erased it, burned the maps. And I am the last of my line, sitting in your kitchen with bread getting stale, asking you: does the word *Door* mean something to you? Because if it does — if there's even a chance — then east is suicide, west is drowning, south is nothing..."

He spread his hands.

"...and south is also everything."

Emrys looked at him. At the bread. At the falcon, who clicked his beak once, in the tone of a bird saying *well?*

Somewhere down the valley, faint under the wind, a dog began to bark — and then another, and another, a chain of them running along the north end of the village toward the church lane, toward the first doors.

Torv went pale. "He's started early."

Emrys stood, and the stone in her chest did not soften, but it changed — guilt hardening, cooling, taking an edge. She had held her tongue in the square. She would not hold her tongue forever. But tonight, she understood at last, the choice was no longer *run or stay.*

It was *which nothing*, and how fast.

"Get your map," she said. "The blank one. I've seen how you look at it."
