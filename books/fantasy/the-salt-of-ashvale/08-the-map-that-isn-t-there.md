## 8. The Map That Isn't There

Torv had been drunk in the Drowned Gull's back room for three days when the girl knocked on his door, and he had been sober for exactly one hour when she asked the question that undid him.

"You said you knew what the seal meant," Emrys said. She stood in his doorway with her arms crossed and her chin up, which he would later learn was her way of being frightened. "The falcon. You recognized it. I want to know how."

He should have lied. He was, by his own honest accounting, a liar of long standing — he had lied to guild examiners, to innkeepers, to two wives (sequentially, thank the salt), and most faithfully of all to himself, every morning for eleven years, when he looked in a mirror and said *Torv Kestrel-Maker, cartographer*, as though those words still meant anything.

But she stood there smelling faintly of goats and woodsmoke, with a royal falcon perched on her shoulder like a duke on his throne, and Torv found that all his lies had packed up and left without leaving a forwarding address.

"Sit down," he said. "This is going to require the good bottle."

"I thought you finished the good bottle."

"I did." He pulled it out from under the bed anyway. "That's why it requires *courage*."

* * *

The room at the Drowned Gull was small enough that two people in it constituted a crowd. Torv sat on the bed; Emrys took the one chair, dragging it so its back faced the door — he noticed that, noted it as the habit of someone who had spent a lifetime listening for footsteps — and Brannoc the falcon settled onto the curtain rod like a gargoyle with opinions.

"The seal," Torv began, turning the empty bottle in his hands because a man needed something to do with his hands when the truth came out of hiding. "You saw it on the falcon's message case. A circle broken by nine strokes."

"Nine lines through a ring. Like a wheel someone gave up drawing."

"That's not a wheel. It's not even a symbol, properly speaking. It's a *map*." He held up his hand, fingers spread. "Nine strokes. Nine rivers of old Sablemark, radiating from a central ring — the capital, Sable-on-Sea. Every document of the crown bore that mark for four hundred years, and then one morning it didn't, and no one alive remembered why, because remembering why was made illegal before your grandmother's grandmother learned to walk."

Emrys went very still. In the valley, he had learned, people went still the way other people flinched.

"Aldric's edicts," she said carefully. "Against magic."

"Before Aldric. Long before. Aldric just dusted off the old law and put his own face on it." Torv laughed, and it came out wrong, the way his laughs had done for eleven years. "You want to know the joke, girl? The joke is that Ashvale isn't hiding from the king. Ashvale is hiding from *history*. This whole valley — the fog that eats compasses, the roads that won't stay where you leave them — this is the last corner of Sablemark that forgot on purpose."

Brannoc shifted on the rod. The falcon had been watching Torv with that flat golden stare raptors reserved for things they were deciding whether to eat, but now the bird cocked its head, and something in the quality of its attention made the hair rise on Torv's neck.

"And you know this how?" Emrys asked.

"Because my family helped make everyone forget." He set down the bottle. It was time. Eleven years of drinking toward this moment, and here it was, arriving not with trumpets but with goat-smell and cheap tallow light. "My family were mapmakers. Not the kind that draw coastlines for merchants. The kind that *unmake* maps. When the old kingdom fell — when whatever happened at Sable-on-Sea happened — the survivors who wanted the past buried hired the Kestrels to bury it. We redrew the borders. We burned the atlases. And we made the Door of Salt into a rumor, and the rumor into nothing at all."

"Door of Salt." She said it slowly, tasting it.

"The only way out of this country that doesn't go through Aldric's armies. Or rather — the only way out of the country that used to be here, before the country was erased and this valley was all that was left of it, tucked under its fog like a coin under a mattress." Torv rubbed his face. His beard needed washing. Everything about him needed washing; that had been the point of the drinking. "And yes. Before you ask. There's a reason a disgraced cartographer crawled into the one valley in the world that can't be mapped. I was hiding too. From a different thing. Same fog, though."

Emrys leaned forward. "What happened at Sable-on-Sea?"

"No one knows. That was the entire point of my family's work. The ones who knew were killed, and the ones who wrote it down were burned, and the places it was written about were renamed until language itself lost the thread." He smiled thinly. "Except my family kept one thing. One inheritance. Passed down with instructions that sounded like madness: *Keep it blank, keep it hidden, and give it to whoever asks the right question.*"

"What question?"

"*Where does the sea remember?*" Torv said. And then, because he had wondered for eleven years and could wonder no longer: "Why do you ask? What did the falcon tell you?"

Emrys looked at Brannoc. The falcon looked back. Something passed between them — some silent negotiation, some weighing — and Torv felt like a man watching two diplomats conclude a treaty over his head in a language he'd never learn.

"It said," Emrys said at last, "when I asked who sent it — it said, *the one who remembers the Door.*"

The words went through Torv like cold water through cloth.

Eleven years he had carried the blank map, wrapped in oilskin at the bottom of a bag he never unpacked, and for eleven years he had told himself he was keeping it out of guilt, out of duty, out of the stubbornness of a family tradition that had ruined four generations of Kestrels. But the truth — the truth he'd drowned nightly in the Drowned Gull's cheapest vinegar — was simpler and more shameful: he had been waiting. Waiting for someone to walk into his shabby room and say the right question. Waiting to be needed by history again, after history had chewed him up and spat him into a valley full of goats.

"Well," he said, and his voice cracked like a boy's. "Then I suppose we'd better open the bloody thing."

* * *

He knelt to dig the oilskin packet from the bottom of his traveling bag, and while he dug he talked, because talking was easier than trembling.

"There are rules. My grandfather's notes say there are rules, and my grandfather was a drunkard but he was a *precise* drunkard, which is the best kind." He laid the packet on the bed between them. It was small, the size of a prayer book, heavy with wax seals that had been broken and resealed so many times the wax was more scar than seal. "Rule one: the map shows ink only to certain people. Not everyone. My father could stare at it till his eyes bled and see nothing but vellum. My mother saw it whole. The gift runs sideways in families, like red hair."

"What does it show?"

"Everything that was erased. Every road, every river, every town of old Sablemark — drawn in ink that appears only for—" He stopped. Started again. "For people who speak to beasts."

Emrys's breath caught audibly.

"I guessed," Torv said quietly, not looking at her. "I've guessed since the market day, if you want the truth of it. You talk to your goats. Not the way farmers talk to animals — I've watched farmers, they talk *at* them, they talk like the animal is furniture that occasionally produces milk. You talk *with* them. And they answer. And nobody notices, because nobody in this valley looks at anything anymore except their own fear." He finally turned to face her. Her face had gone white, and her hand had drifted toward the door, and he raised both palms quickly. "I'm not going to shout it from the rooftops, girl. I'm the man who spent three days teaching a bottle that the world had forgotten him. Secrets are the only trade I have left."

"Why?" she whispered. "Why would a map show itself to — to *my kind*?"

"Because of who drew it." Torv unwrapped the last layer of oilcloth. Beneath lay a roll of vellum tied with a cord gone grey with age. "The last royal cartographer of Sablemark wasn't a man. My family's oldest letter says the final survey of the kingdom was done by a woman who rode the marsh-elephants and read the coastline by asking the gulls. When the kingdom fell, she made one last map — not of what Sablemark was, but of what it *kept*. The roads that survived the erasure. The drowned causeways. And the Door." He swallowed. "She made it so that only the beast-speakers could find their way home. In case anyone ever needed to come home."

The room was very quiet. On the curtain rod, Brannoc had gone motionless in the way of birds who understood that a hinge of history was creaking open.

Torv untied the cord with fingers that refused to be steady.

"Rule two," he said. "This is the rule that matters, so listen. The ink doesn't appear gently. It's not like lighting a candle. The old letters call it *the unveiling*, and it burns. Magic goes into the vellum and comes back out again, and any instrument built to taste magic within miles will taste it. The Gift-Hunters carry such instruments. The king's new sensors — iron needles that swing toward the gifted like compasses toward north." He looked up at her, and hated what he had to say next, and said it anyway, because a Kestrel's whole cursed inheritance was saying true things at the worst possible moments. "If you unveil this map in Ashvale, Emrys Delacroix, every needle for fifty miles will point at you like a hundred fingers."

She was quiet for a long moment. Then, in a voice much smaller than the chin-up voice from the doorway: "How far is the Door?"

"Beyond the drowned coast. Four days' hard walking, if the fog lets us take the straight road — which it won't, the fog never lets anyone take the straight road. Six, maybe seven, the winding way." He hesitated. "There's a third rule. Rule three is the one my grandfather underlined three times, which for my grandfather was practically a religious experience."

"Tell me."

"The map shows the way *out*." Torv ran his thumb along the cord. "Once. The unveiling happens once, and then the vellum goes blank forever. Whatever road it draws, we get one reading of it. One chance to memorize. After that, my family's work is finished — truly finished — and there's no second map, because the woman who drew this one has been dead for two hundred years."

Emrys laughed. It startled him — a short, helpless sound, edged with something close to hysteria.

"So let me be sure I understand the bargain," she said. "I use my forbidden gift — the one that gets people burned — to reveal a map that leads out of the country. The moment I do it, every hunter and needle and informer for fifty miles knows exactly what I am and roughly where I stand. And I get one look at the route, and then the only guide out of this valley becomes my memory and your sense of direction." Her eyes flicked to him. "Which, judging by the state of this room—"

"My sense of direction is *excellent*," Torv said with dignity. "It's my life choices that wander."

That got another laugh out of her, thinner this time, but real. She looked down at the blank vellum lying on the blanket between them — plain as a shroud, patient as a grave — and Torv watched her face do the arithmetic he had already done a hundred times over eleven years: the cost on one side, the door on the other, and no column anywhere for staying safe.

"They're coming anyway," she said at last. "The strangers with the measuring instruments. The goats told me — well. The goats told me." She shook her head as if at her own life. "Three days ago I thought the fog would hold forever. Now I know it won't. So the question isn't whether I'm exposed, Torv. It's whether I'm exposed running *away* or running *toward* something."

"That's remarkably clear-eyed for someone facing a lifetime of exile."

"I raise goats. Clarity is a survival trait." She reached out and touched the vellum, one fingertip, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you hoped wouldn't wake angry. "Show me how it works."

"Not yet." Torv put his hand over hers — gently, and immediately aware of how long it had been since he'd touched another person on purpose. "Not tonight. Tonight we sleep, and eat something that isn't despair, and at first light we go to the eastern edge of the valley, away from the village, away from the hunters' instruments, as far as this fog-bound bowl of a country allows us to get. If the needles are going to sing, let them sing where there's a head start behind us."

Emrys nodded slowly. Then she paused, and her brow creased.

"Torv. Your family erased a kingdom. Helped bury the Door, burned the histories, unmade the maps." Her eyes lifted to his, and they were not accusing — that was somehow worse; they were simply curious, the way the falcon's were. "Why keep the map? Why not burn it too, and be done?"

He had waited eleven years to be asked that question, and had composed a dozen answers, all of them eloquent, none of them true. What came out instead was the truth, raw and unplanned:

"Because my great-great-grandmother, the one who hid it, wrote a note inside the oilskin. One line. I've read it so many times I could carve it on my own bones." He picked up the packet and turned it over, showing her the inner flap, where faded brown ink spelled out a single sentence in a careful antique hand:

*A country deserves a door, in case it ever wants to come home.*

"She couldn't undo what the family did," Torv said quietly. "None of us could. But she could leave one key in one lock, and trust that someday somebody would come along who fit it." He looked at Emrys — at the beast-speaker, the heir of voices, sitting in his squalid room smelling of woodsmoke with a dead queen's falcon on the curtain rod. "I don't believe in destiny. Cartographers can't afford destiny; destiny plays hell with scale and distance. But I believe in keys. And I believe you're the shape this lock has been waiting for."

Outside, somewhere across the dark fields, a dog began to bark — not the idle barking of dogs complaining at the moon, but the sharp, rhythmic alarm of a dog that had smelled strangers on the night wind.

Emrys and Torv looked at each other. On the curtain rod, Brannoc rose to his full height, feathers tight, eyes fixed on the window.

"First light," Emrys said. "Eastern edge. Bring the map."

"And you bring the courage," Torv said. "I'll bring what's left of the bottle. Between us we might manage a miracle."

The dog, far off in the dark, was still barking.
