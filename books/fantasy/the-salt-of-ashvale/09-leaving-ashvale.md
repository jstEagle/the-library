## 9. Leaving Ashvale

The goats knew before she did.

Emrys woke in the grey hour to a silence that was not silence at all—the particular hush of the herd standing awake in the dark, all their attention pointed east like needles finding north. She lay in her narrow bed and listened to them through the wall, and what she heard, in the wordless way she heard such things, was: *now. Now. The girl must go now.*

"You could have mentioned this last week," she murmured.

From the barn came a single indignant bleat, which in goat meant something between *we told you Tuesday* and *stop blaming us for your own stubbornness.* Goats were not known for their comfort. It was one of the many things Emrys loved about them.

She dressed in the dark, which took no time, because there was so little to dress in. That was the arithmetic of her whole life, she thought as she pulled on the good boots—her mother's boots, resoled twice—and began gathering what little she owned into the sacking bag: the copper kettle too small for anything but sentiment, her mother's bone comb, the knife, half a wheel of cheese wrapped in waxed cloth. A life built entirely around concealment fit in a sack with room left over for apples. She stood a moment with her hand flat against the table where she'd eaten every meal since she was seven, and let herself feel the shape of it: small, safe, and ending.

Then she went out to say goodbye to the only family who'd never once asked her to be less than she was.

* * *

The barn smelled of hay and warm animal and, faintly, of falcon. Brannoc sat on the high beam where he had appointed himself sentry, feathers sleek again now after his week of mending, watching her with the yellow-eyed patience of royalty in exile. He still would not tell her plainly who had sent him—*the one who remembers the Door*, he'd said, and then gone silent as a locked drawer—but he had stopped pretending to sleep when she spoke, and that was nearly the same thing as trust.

"Watch the goats," she told him. "No. Wait." She laughed, and it cracked a little coming out. "You can't watch the goats. I'm taking the goats' opinion with me and leaving the goats themselves."

She went down the line, pressing her forehead to each broad skull in turn. Clover, who had been her first friend in the world and remained her most honest critic. Bramble, who ate hats. Old Marigold, whose milk had fed Emrys through two winters when coin ran short, and who leaned into her now with the full weight of a creature who understood exactly what a goodbye weighed.

*Where do you go?* Marigold asked—not in words, but in the deep slow current beneath words.

"East," Emrys said aloud, though it cost her. "Or east-ish. Torv says east is death, so we're going east the way water goes downhill, which is to say sideways."

Marigold chewed on this, both literally and otherwise. *Take the grey blanket,* she said finally. *It smells of you. When you are afraid, hold it.*

"I'm going to cry," Emrys informed her. "I want you to know that in advance."

*I know,* said Marigold. *Goats always know. It is our burden.*

Clover headbutted her knee hard enough to bruise, which was goat for *I love you, now stop being maudlin and go save yourself.* Emrys wiped her face on her sleeve, shouldered the sack, and stepped out into the last fog she might ever walk through as a woman of Ashvale.

Torv was waiting at the well, and he was not alone.

* * *

"You brought the donkey," Emrys said.

"He brought himself," Torv said. "There's a difference. Legally speaking."

The donkey in question—a long-eared, deeply skeptical creature named Ptolemy, whom Torv had apparently won at cards from the innkeeper sometime during the night—regarded Emrys with the expression of an accountant reviewing a suspicious expense. She liked him immediately.

"It's dawn soon," Torv said, and up close she could see he hadn't slept either; there was a tremor in his hands he kept trying to disguise by fussing with map cases. "We need to be past the fog-line by midmorning. Wren will have worked out by now that the hedge-healer's death flushed nothing, which means he'll widen his net. And Emrys—" he hesitated, and she watched him decide to say it honestly, because whatever else Torv of the Broken Compass was, he had stopped being able to lie to her somewhere around chapter eight of their acquaintance, "—once we cross, everything changes. The fog isn't just weather. It's been eating your gift your whole life, keeping you small enough to survive. Past the line, you'll feel all of it at once. Every beast within miles. Every thread. I've seen gifted folk cross that line, back when there were lines to cross. Some of them wept. One of them tried to walk into the sea."

"What happened to her?"

"The sea declined," Torv said. "She's a lighthouse keeper now, last I heard. Very content. Screams at gulls."

"That's the most comforting thing anyone's said to me in a month."

"It's the only comforting thing I know. I'm saving the rest in case of emergencies."

Brannoc dropped from the barn roof onto Ptolemy's rump, causing the donkey to express his views at length, and they set off—Torv leading, Emrys beside him, the fog thick and silver-grey and smelling of salt that had never seen the sea. Ashvale slid away behind them in pieces: the well, the crooked lane, the roofs going pale in the growing light. Emrys did not look back. Looking back, she suspected, was a door of its own, and she wasn't sure she could afford to open it.

They walked through the valley's heart, past the terraced fields, past the shrine where her mother's name was carved so small among hundreds of names that Emrys had needed years to find it. There, she did stop. Just for a breath. *Maren Delacroix,* worn shallow by weather. She touched it once, lightly, the way you touch something you're afraid of waking.

"Come on," Torv said softly. Not impatiently. Just kindly, the way you speak to someone walking out of a burning house.

They climbed. The path toward the fog-line was an old drover's track, and as they rose, the fog began to thin in ragged patches, showing sky above—actual sky, enormous and blue-edged and indifferent. Emrys felt her heart start hammering somewhere around the third switchback, and couldn't have said whether it was fear or joy, and supposed, with a distant part of her mind, that the answer was yes.

"Torv," she said. "When it comes. When I feel it. What do I do?"

He stopped and turned to face her fully, and behind his scruff and his ruined reputation and his father's broken compass hanging at his throat, she saw the thing that had made her trust him despite every sensible instinct: he was afraid for her, and he wasn't hiding it.

"You breathe," he said. "That's all. You breathe, and you let it come, and you remember that it's yours. It was never the enemy, Emrys. The edict is the enemy. The fog was just a wall somebody built inside you without asking." He paused. "Also, possibly, hold my arm. People have fallen down."

* * *

The fog-line was not a wall or a border stone. It was nothing. It was a place where the mist simply ended, a clean edge like the hem of a garment, hanging in the air across the mountainside as if the world had been mended badly and this was the seam.

Beyond it: morning. Green hills running down to a drowned coast she could not yet see but could suddenly, impossibly, *smell*. Light everywhere. A sky so wide it seemed rude.

Emrys stood at the seam with her toes almost touching it, and Brannoc lifted off her shoulder and hovered ahead of her, wings catching the sun, waiting.

"Right," she said, to no one. "Breathe."

She stepped through.

It hit her like the sea.

Not gradually—there was no gentle slope, no warning shallows. Magic came back into the world all at once, a flood, a tide, a great bell struck inside her chest, and Emrys gasped and staggered and heard Torv grunt as he caught her weight against his arm. The world *opened*. She could hear—no, not hear, she could *be heard by*, she could reach—everything. Grass underfoot humming its low green note. Worms turning soil in patient choirs. A hawk three valleys over riding a thermal and thinking, in its cold bright way, about voles. Somewhere far south, something enormous breathing slowly in salt marsh water, something that felt old as the drowned kingdom itself and turned, in its sleep, toward her voice like a flower toward sun.

And they all felt *her*. She felt the ripple go out from her body like a stone dropped in a pond, her presence announced to every living thing for miles—*here, here, a voice, a speaker, the heir of voices*—and it was terrifying, it was being naked in a cathedral, it was—

It was wonderful.

Emrys laughed. She couldn't help it; it tore out of her wild and ringing, and the hills answered—a fox barking from the gorse, cattle lifting their heads in a far field, birds rising in a black glad cloud. Her gift poured through her like light through clean glass, and she stood in the morning with tears streaming down her face, laughing, holding Torv's arm with a grip that would leave bruises, feeling more herself than she had ever been permitted to be.

"Oh," she managed. "Oh, it's—nobody told me. All those years in the fog, and nobody told me I was—"

"Small?" Torv said quietly. "I think they told you constantly. That was rather the point."

She turned to look back at the seam of fog, at the valley hidden inside it, at the whole small gray life folded up neat as a letter in a drawer. Grief and exhilaration rose in her together, braided tight, inseparable, and she understood that this was how it would be from now on: joy with a wound in it, freedom with a bill attached.

"They'll burn the shrine next," she said. "When they figure out where I lived. Wren will stand in my kitchen and read my mother's name off the wall and write it down in his ledger like a fact."

"Wren," Torv said, "is currently asleep in the inn, dreaming of paperwork."

Behind them, unmentioned, the fog hung in its seam. Emrys looked at it a moment longer—her whole childhood, sealed in grey—and then turned away and did not look back again.

Ptolemy, sensing drama had concluded, began to walk.

* * *

Hunter Wren was not asleep in the inn.

He stood instead in Emrys Delacroix's kitchen, very early, very quiet, while his hounds moved through the small house with their noses down and their hackles smooth. Cold hearth. Swept floor. The kettle gone from its hook—he noticed absences the way other men noticed presences—and a rectangle of cleaner wood on the shelf where something small had sat until quite recently. A comb, perhaps. Something held often enough to wear the shelf beneath it.

The goats were gone from the paddock. All of them. That was the detail that made him smile, because a thief leaves stock behind; only a woman saying goodbye takes thirty-one goats to new pasture in the night, or sells them weeping to a neighbor, or does whatever arrangement of grief and practicality she'd managed before dawn. The goats were the signature. Kind people always signed their work.

His senior hound, Sorrel, put her paws on the cold hearthstone and breathed in deeply, then looked back at him with the particular apologetic tilt of the head that meant *old magic, faded, weeks of it soaked into this stone like tea into cloth—but nothing hot, nothing recent, nothing here anymore.*

"She's crossed the line," Wren said.

Sorrel wagged once. Hounds did not care about treason. Hounds cared about the hunt, and the hunt had just become interesting.

Wren stood a while longer in the kitchen, in the grey light coming through the small window, and let himself admit what he'd known since the hedge-healer burned—that he had hoped, in some chamber of himself he did not inspect, that the girl with the steady eyes and the careful answers would get away. He had asked her questions in her own doorway and watched her lie to him with her chin up, and he had walked away knowing it was a lie, and written in his ledger that it was not. That was already one betrayal, small and deniable, filed neatly among true ones.

Now here he stood in her empty kitchen, smiling.

He didn't examine the smile. Examining it would ruin it, the way examining any mercy ruins the mercy. Instead he took out his ledger and wrote, in his precise hand: *Delacroix, E. Fled prior to dawn. Direction: east. Accompaniment unknown, possibly the disgraced cartographer. Pursuit recommended.* And then, after a pause, he added a second line, smaller: *She took the goats somewhere safe first.*

He closed the book. Outside, the fog of Ashvale lay over the valley like a bandage over an eye, and beyond the ridge, somewhere east, a woman was feeling magic flood back into her for the first time in her life, and Wren found—to his lasting irritation—that he was glad.

"Saddle up," he told the hounds. "Three days' start is generous. Two is prudent."

Sorrel barked once, joyously, and the hunt began.

* * *

By midday they were well down into the green country, and Emrys had learned three things.

First: that she could quiet a startled horse with a thought, which made Ptolemy deeply jealous and required diplomacy.

Second: that Brannoc, freed of the fog, flew differently—higher, harder, with a joy that matched her own, and that when he cried out overhead it was less a falcon's cry now and more a word she was on the edge of understanding.

Third: that Torv sang while he walked. Badly. Constantly. Songs about rivers, mostly, with invented verses when he forgot the real ones, and when she pointed this out he said, "A cartographer who admits his maps are blank has earned the right to improvise," and she laughed until her ribs hurt, and the hills laughed with her in their green wordless way, and for one whole hour the grief stayed quiet.

They camped that night in the lee of a ruined milestone, one of the old Sablemark stones, its carved letters worn to ghosts. Torv built a fire while Emrys laid out the cheese and the apples, and Brannoc came down to sit by her knee like a small fierce king accepting hospitality, and for a while nobody said anything at all, because the silence was a good one, the kind you earn.

"Torv," she said eventually. "The map. Tomorrow?"

He looked at the fire. "Tomorrow," he said. "And Emrys—when you wake it, when the ink comes, every gifted thing within fifty miles will feel it. Including, I'm sorry to say, things that serve the crown."

"I know."

"And you're still—"

"I spent twenty-four years being safe," Emrys said, and found that it was true, and that truth sat in her chest like a coal, warm and terrible. "Safe got the hedge-healer burned in the square while her neighbors applauded. Safe is a room getting smaller. I'm done with rooms."

Torv nodded slowly, and threw another branch on the fire, and sparks went up toward the enormous uncaged stars.

"East, then," he said. "Sideways, the way water goes downhill."

Far behind them, at the fog-line, unseen in the dark, the mist hung in its seam like a held breath. And far behind *that*, moving fast through the sleeping valley with hounds streaming ahead of him like black water, Hunter Wren rode east too—following a woman he could not bring himself to catch, toward a Door neither of them yet understood, carrying in his ledger a single sentence he did not remember writing:

*Some fires are lit to see by, not to burn.*

The road was long. The morning would come. Emrys slept with the grey blanket against her cheek, smelling faintly of goats, and dreamed—not of her mother's face, though she did not yet know why that mattered, but of a coastline, drowned and waiting, calling her name in salt.
