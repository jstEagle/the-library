## 10. The Drowned Road

The fog of Ashvale let them go the way a mother lets go of a child's hand at a market stall—reluctantly, in small increments, with a tug at the sleeve that Emrys felt for three days after.

She had thought, all her life, that the fog was a wall. It was not. It was a held breath. And when they crossed the last of it on that first grey morning, the world inhaled around her, and she nearly fell off the causeway from the sheer *noise* of it.

"Emrys?" Torv caught her elbow. He was thinner than she'd realized, all elbows and cartographer's squint, his broken compass hanging at his neck like a penance. "What is it? What do you feel?"

*Everything.*

That was the truth, but it wasn't a word she could hand over whole, so she gave him pieces. "The grass," she said. "It's—talking. Not talking. Humming. The grass is humming, Torv, and there's something under the water that knows we're here, and the wind has an *opinion* about us." She laughed, and heard how unhinged the laugh was, and kept laughing anyway. "In Ashvale I had one voice in my head that wasn't mine. The goats. Now there are ten thousand voices and every one of them is louder than I am."

Torv watched her with the careful expression of a man who has already decided to follow someone off a cliff and is now measuring the drop. "Can you make them quiet?"

"No."

"Can you make yourself quiet?"

She considered this. The voices weren't hostile. They were curious—a whole drowned country's worth of curiosity, pressing against her skin like moths against a lantern. "Maybe," she said. "Ask me again in a week."

They walked on.

The road itself was the first wonder, and it did not stop being a wonder no matter how many hours they spent on it. Sablemark had been a kingdom of causeways—Emrys knew this only because Torv wouldn't stop saying it—but knowing was different from walking. The old engineers had built their roads out of white stone veined with something dark and glittering, and they had built them *low*, so that the sea crossed them twice a day in long silver sheets, and the stone didn't mind. Water slid over the road and off it as if the road were shrugging. In places the sea had won anyway: whole spans lay fathoms down, visible through water so clear it seemed ashamed of its own depth, and the road simply picked itself up on the far side as though nothing had happened.

"It's been two hundred years," Torv said, for perhaps the ninth time, crouching to run his thumb along a seam in the stone where mortar should have been and wasn't. "Two hundred years underwater, half of it, and the joints haven't failed. Do you know what that means? That means the mortar was never mortar. That means—" He stopped himself, visibly rationing his excitement, the way he'd apparently learned to do after a lifetime of people glazing over. "Sorry. You don't care about masonry."

"I care that you care," Emrys said, which was true, and which made him go slightly pink and walk faster.

* * *

They camped the second night on a wide span where the causeway widened into what must once have been a plaza, its edges marked by stubs of pillars that rose from the shallows like the stumps of pulled teeth. Torv built a fire of driftwood that burned green and salt-scented, and Emrys discovered that her gift, unchained by Ashvale's fog, had opinions about dinner.

"You're holding the fish wrong," said a voice from the tide pool beside her.

Emrys dropped the fish.

It was a crab. An enormous crab, mottled grey-green, regarding her from beneath a shelf of rock with eyes on stalks that tilted with unmistakable judgment.

"You're holding the fish wrong," the crab repeated. "You've stunned it but you haven't bled it. It'll taste of mud. Everything you land-dwellers eat tastes of mud. This is why your kind is so grim."

Emrys looked across the fire at Torv, who was chewing thoughtfully and had clearly heard nothing. She looked back at the crab. "You can hear me?"

"Hear *you?*" The stalk-eyes swiveled with something like outrage. "I can hear the whole shore. I could hear the queen's kitchen-gardens complaining before the waters came, and they complained *constantly*. I heard the last king argue with his horse, and the horse was winning." A claw gestured at her, delicately. "But you—you speak the old way. The deep way. The way things were spoken before men decided words belonged to them." The crab paused, and when it spoke again its voice had gone strange and formal, like a recitation worn smooth by centuries of repetition. "*Heir of voices.* We felt you coming, heir. The eels sang about you on the night tide. The oysters closed their shells in respect, which for oysters is a great honor."

"I'm not—I think there's been a mistake," Emrys said faintly. "I talk to goats. Badly. One goat, Bess, mostly ignores me."

"The goats are a hard audience," the crab conceded. "Everyone knows this."

Behind her, Torv had stopped chewing.

He hadn't heard the crab—he'd heard *her*, Emrys realized, speaking aloud into empty air with the particular cadence of conversation. He set down his tin plate very carefully, the way a man sets down a thing he might need both hands free for.

"There's a crab," Emrys said helpfully.

"A crab."

"A large crab. It says I'm bleeding the fish wrong."

Torv looked at the fish. At her. At the tide pool. Then, being Torv, he took off his spectacles, polished them on his shirt, put them back on, and asked, "Is it right?"

"It says everything I eat tastes of mud."

"That tracks," Torv said gravely. "My mother's cooking. Yes."

And that was that—that was how easily it happened, Emrys would remember later. No argument, no crisis of faith. Just a man who had spent his life mapping erased things deciding that a talking crab was simply another territory to be surveyed, and accepting it into the map.

The crab, whose name was Ossel and who considered names a form of vanity practiced mainly by birds, gave them detailed instructions on preparing shellfish and then, as the fire burned low, grew quieter and stranger.

"We have waited a long time for the deep speech," Ossel said. "Since the waters came. Since the Breaking. Your kind sealed the Door and forgot us, and the forgetting went into the stones themselves. But the beasts remembered. Beasts always remember; it's our whole profession." The eyes tilted toward Emrys, and for a moment the sardonic tone dropped away entirely. "You carry it wrong, heir. You hold it like a secret. It isn't a secret. It never was. Secrets die with their keepers. But a *voice*—" the claws opened, slow, like a man showing empty hands—"a voice passes mouth to mouth, forever."

Emrys didn't sleep much that night. The stars over the drowned coast were enormous and unfamiliar, arranged in constellations no Ashvale shepherd had ever named, and the sea murmured against the causeway in a language she was beginning, horribly and wonderfully, to understand.

* * *

On the fourth day, the wolves came.

Emrys heard them before she saw them—or rather, she heard the grass hear them, a ripple of attention running through the marsh-meadows on either side of the causeway, every blade turning its whisper toward the dunes to the north. She stopped walking. Brannoc, riding her shoulder in the manner of a falcon who had decided that shoulders were a reasonable mode of transport for royalty, went rigid and made a sound like a kettle deciding whether to scream.

"Wolves," she said.

"How many?"

"All of them."

There were nine. They came over the dunes in a loose line, big grey-brown coastal wolves with salt-stiffened fur and pale eyes, and they did not circle or threaten or do any of the things wolves did in stories. They sat down on the causeway ahead, all nine at once, in a row, like a delegation. Like a committee that had reviewed her file.

Torv's hand found his knife and Emrys found his wrist. "No," she said. "Wait."

She stepped forward alone. The fog of Ashvale would have swallowed her gift whole; here it stood up inside her like a second spine. When she spoke, she spoke in the deep way, and the words came easier than they ever had among the goats, as though the air of old Sablemark had been waiting two hundred years to carry them.

"Why have you come?"

The lead wolf—an old female, grey at the muzzle, one ear notched by some ancient disagreement—rose and dipped her head. Not submission. Something older than submission. Recognition.

"*Heir of voices,*" she said, and her pack said it after her, nine voices in ragged chorus, and the words rolled out over the marshes and were taken up by things Emrys couldn't see—herons somewhere, something vast and patient in the deeper channels, the grass itself—until the name came back to her from every direction like an echo that had gotten there first.

"We smelled you on the wind three days ago," the lead wolf said. "Wind-bringers carried word. The eels sang. The crabs gossiped, because crabs gossip." A ripple of what Emrys could only describe as dry amusement passed through the pack. "We came to look at you."

"And?" Emrys asked. Her heart was going like a drum. "What did you see?"

The wolf studied her for a long time. Pale eyes, old as the drowned road, missing nothing—the falcon on her shoulder, the cartographer behind her, the tremor she couldn't quite keep out of her hands.

"A girl," the wolf said at last. "A girl who talks to goats and bleeds fish wrong and walks like she's sorry to take up space." The notched ear flicked. "And underneath the girl, something older. The voice goes back further than you do, heir. Further than your mother, and hers, and hers. It goes back to the ones who built this road and spoke to every stone they laid. The kings of Sablemark didn't rule the beasts. They *asked* them. There is a difference, and the beasts remember the difference, and we have been waiting two hundred years for someone who might ask us again instead of telling us."

"I don't want to lead anything," Emrys said. It came out too fast and too honest, the way truths do when they've been sitting in the chest too long. "I want to go home. I want my kitchen and my goats and my fog. I'm running from a hunter, not toward a throne."

"Yes," the wolf agreed. "So were we, once. So was everyone, the first time." She turned, and the pack turned with her, and for a moment the whole line of them stood silhouetted against the flat silver light of the drowned coast. "We will walk with you to the edge of the marshes. After that, the road belongs to other voices. But hear this, heir, and carry it: the Door remembers who opens it. Whatever waits beyond the salt will ask you what you're willing to pay. Decide *before* you arrive. Decisions made at doors are made badly."

Then the wolves fell in around them—not herding, escorting—and the strangest day of Emrys Delacroix's life got stranger, because she spent six hours walking between nine wolves while Torv, walking beside her, quietly filled an entire page of his notebook with sketches and, at the bottom, in his small precise hand, wrote: *Day 4. Wolves polite. Emrys is either the most important person in the world or the most doomed. Possibly both. Fish still tasted of mud.*

* * *

They parted from the pack at dusk on the fifth day, where the causeway climbed onto a headland and the marshes gave way to rougher country. The old female touched her nose once, briefly, to Emrys's palm—an honor, Ossel had explained via a passing gull, normally reserved for the dead and the newly crowned—and then they were gone into the dunes like smoke deciding to be smoke again.

Emrys stood a long time at the headland's edge, looking back the way they'd come. The drowned road ran behind them to the horizon, white stone and silver water, a kingdom's spine still straight after two hundred years of neglect. Somewhere along it, crabs were gossiping about her. Eels had sung about her. Wolves had walked with her and called her *heir* as if it were simply her name, as if it had always been her name, waiting for her to grow into it like a coat left in a will.

"Torv," she said. "When your family buried the Door. Did they bury instructions?"

He came to stand beside her, following her gaze down the impossible road. "Instructions?"

"For who gets to open it. Whether anyone can, or whether—" she turned her hand over, looked at the palm the wolf had touched— "whether it was always meant to be someone like me. Because the beasts talk like I'm expected. Like I'm late."

Torv was quiet for a moment. When he answered, he answered slowly, feeling his way along the words the way he felt his way along unmapped coastlines.

"My grandfather used to say the Door doesn't open *for* people. It opens *from* both sides." He shrugged, uncomfortable with his own inheritance of knowledge. "I always took that for drunk poetry. But the beasts keep saying *waiting*. Waiting implies someone promised to come back." He looked at her sideways. "Emrys—if your gift is older than you, maybe so is the promise. Maybe somebody in your line made a bargain two hundred years ago, and the beasts have been standing at the door ever since, watching every generation pass, wondering which one would finally keep it."

The evening wind came up the headland, smelling of salt and old stone, and far below them in the darkening channels something enormous moved without surfacing—something that had been waiting even longer than the wolves, probably, and with considerably more patience.

"Well," said Emrys Delacroix, heir of voices, keeper of nothing, girl who just wanted to go home to her goats. "I hope they're not in a hurry."

Behind them, far to the east, on the ridge of the drowned road, a single figure stood motionless against the last light—too far away to be sure of, too still to be anything friendly.

Brannoc saw it first. The falcon rose off her shoulder with a cry she had never heard him make before, sharp and urgent, and wheeled eastward into the dark.

Toward Rivergate. Toward the free cities. Toward whatever was coming up the old road behind them, faster than two walkers and a wounded bird could ever outrun it.

Emrys watched him shrink to a speck, and her stomach, which had spent five days learning wonder, remembered how to be afraid.
