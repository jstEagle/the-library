## 11. Refugees at Rivergate

The first thing Torv noticed about Rivergate was the smell. Not the river smell he'd expected—fish, tar, wet stone—but the smell of too many people in too little space: woodsmoke and unwashed wool, boiled cabbage and fear. Fear had a scent of its own, he'd learned. It was something like old pennies.

The second thing was the noise.

"Keep your hood up," he murmured to Emrys, though she needed no telling. She'd pulled it low the moment the causeway road crested the last hill and the city revealed itself—a sprawl of slate roofs and crooked towers spilling down to the water, walled in old white stone that had gone the color of teeth. Above the western gate, the banners of the Free Cities snapped in the wind: three fish, a wheel, an open hand. *Come freely,* the open hand promised. *Leave freely,* it added, in smaller letters nobody said aloud.

Beyond the walls, on the mudflats and the ditch-roads and every scrap of ground between the river and the tree line, there were tents. Hundreds. Thousands. Cookfires like scattered embers. Children chasing each other between cartwheels. An old man playing a fiddle badly enough that everyone let him continue out of respect.

"I've never seen so many people," Emrys breathed.

"You've never seen a war before, either." Torv reined the mule aside as a family streamed past them, carrying everything they owned on their backs—a loom frame, a cage of hens, a grandfather who'd clearly lost the argument about walking versus riding. "This is what Aldric's Gift-Hunt looks like from the outside. Every gifted soul in the eastern provinces, plus every soul related to one, plus every soul who looked sideways at a soldier once. They all come here, because here is the last place the king's writ doesn't run."

"And if his writ starts running?"

Torv didn't answer. He didn't have to. She could do arithmetic as well as anyone.

They joined the queue at the west gate, which moved with the ponderous patience of people who had nowhere better to be. The gate guards weren't checking for weapons; they were checking faces against a board of charcoal sketches—Wren's work, Torv realized with a lurch, or Wren's office's work—and waving through anyone who didn't match. He kept his shoulders slumped and his eyes on the mud, doing his best impression of a man whose life had been ruined by circumstance rather than by choice. It wasn't much of an impression. His life had been ruined by both.

Emrys, for her part, had discovered the particular art of looking forgettable, which Torv found remarkable given that she was the least forgettable person he'd ever met. There was a gravity to her, a stillness, that made you want to keep looking. But under the hood, with her chin tucked, she became simply a tired traveler, and tired travelers were the one commodity Rivergate had in surplus.

Inside the walls, the crowd thickened. The main thoroughfare—the Salt Road, running from gate to harbor—was a river of its own, choked with carts and barrows and a man selling roasted chestnuts at a volume suggesting the chestnuts had wronged him personally.

"We need lodging," Torv said. "And information. And food, in roughly that order."

"We need coin," Emrys said, "and we have very little."

"One problem at a time."

They found a room above a chandler's shop on Cordwainer Lane, four floors up, reached by stairs that groaned like a ship in a storm. The room cost nearly everything they had left, and came with a bed, a stool, a window overlooking a lightless airshaft, and a landlady named Mistress Pell who looked at Torv's travel-worn clothes and charged him double anyway, because she could tell he'd pay.

"The harbor district's where the news is," she offered, counting coins into her apron. "If you're looking for work. Or passage. Everyone at the harbor's either looking for work or passage, or selling something to those who are."

"News," Torv said. "About the war."

Mistress Pell snorted. "War. That's a grand word for a man burning villages two provinces over. Council's been arguing for a month whether to fight him or kiss him. Kiss him, most likely. They're calling it 'negotiation.'" She spat, with great precision, into a corner bucket. "My nephew says the army'll be at the Marrow ford by month's end. After that—" she shrugged, a gesture that contained multitudes of doom, "—after that, it's just a question of how polite the burning is."

* * *

The harbor at dusk was beautiful the way storms are beautiful: magnificent, and entirely indifferent to your survival. Masts crowded the water like a forest of bare trees. Gulls wheeled and screamed their opinions. On the quay, refugees slept in rows under sailcloth, and harbor officials with ledgers decided, family by family, who would get passage east and who would stay to greet the king's army with whatever politeness remained.

Torv and Emrys walked the quay, listening. This was Torv's old trade—not mapmaking exactly, but *reading*: reading terrain, reading towns, reading the way a crowd leaned. And everywhere the crowd leaned the same direction. Inward. Away from the sea. Toward the council hall, where the Free Cities' delegates had gathered, and where hope went to be negotiated into smaller and smaller pieces.

"They're going to surrender," Emrys said quietly. "Aren't they."

"It's what cities do. Cities are built to survive. Fighting isn't surviving; it's betting everything you have on one throw." Torv watched a dockhand coiling rope with the mechanical rhythm of a man trying not to think. "Most bets like that lose. So they'll offer tribute, hostages, promises. Aldric will take all three and burn the place anyway, because his quarrel was never really with the free cities. It's with magic. And this harbor is full of things he'd call magic."

"Like me."

"Like you." He glanced at her. Her face was turned toward the water, and the fading light caught the line of her jaw, and he thought—as he had thought with increasing frequency and decreasing usefulness—that if the world were a fairer place, he'd have liked to draw maps of her instead of coastlines. "Which is why we keep moving. Tomorrow, maybe. There's a fishing fleet going north to the marshes—"

He never finished the sentence, because that was when the oxen broke.

It started as a sound—a low belling of panic from the cattle pens near the granary, up-slope from the harbor. Then a crash of splitting wood, a scream that wasn't quite human, and the crowd on the quay turned as one body, the way crowds do, toward trouble.

"The pens," someone shouted. "The draft teams—they've spooked!"

Torv saw it unfold in pieces, the way disasters always come in pieces. A team of great dun oxen—forty of them, maybe fifty, penned in a stockade awaiting sale to the council's supply wagons—had gone mad. Later he'd learn why: a boy had thrown a firecracker, some festival leftover, and the crack of it had landed in the middle of a herd already wound tight from a week's march down-country. Whatever the cause, the effect was simple. The stockade fence splintered like kindling, and fifty animals weighing half a ton each came boiling down the hill toward the quay, toward the sleeping refugees, toward the narrow lane between a granary wall and the harbor steps where there was nowhere—nowhere—for anyone to go.

The crowd screamed and stampeded too, which made everything worse. People fell. Sailcloth shelters collapsed. Somewhere a child was crying in a register that cut through all the other noise.

Torv grabbed for Emrys and found empty air.

She was already moving. Not away—*toward*, up the lane, straight into the path of the lead team, a massive black-patched bull with its head down and its hooves striking sparks off the cobbles. She threw back her hood, planted herself in front of six thousand pounds of terrified muscle, and did the one thing she had spent her whole life not doing.

She spoke.

Torv couldn't hear the words. Even standing ten feet away, in the open, he caught only a shape of sound—a low, rolling cadence, less like speech than like weather, like wind finding words in a canyon. But the oxen heard it. The bull's charge faltered. Its head came up, ears swiveling forward, and the panic in its eyes changed into something else—recognition, perhaps, or the animal equivalent of a drowning man feeling bottom under his feet.

The herd slowed. Slowed again. Fifty great beasts, packed flank to flank in a lane barely wide enough for six, shuddered to a halt close enough that their breath ruffled Emrys's cloak. And then, impossibly, they began to settle—huffing, stamping, but no longer running, turning their great heads toward her voice the way sunflowers turn toward sun.

Torv stood frozen, watching the woman he—well. Watching her work. He'd seen her speak to goats. He'd seen Brannoc land on her wrist like she was furniture the falcon approved of. But this was different. This was her gift at full voice, in public, in a city full of frightened people and worse things than frightened people.

Around the quay, the crowd had stopped screaming and started staring. Relief was spreading through them like warmth—but so was something else. Torv saw it move from face to face, quick as fire along a fuse. Wonder in some. Confusion in more. And in a few—

In a few, a bright, calculating interest that he recognized from Ashvale market day, from the criers' announcements, from every edict nailed to every post since Aldric took the throne.

*Gift.*

The word traveled through the crowd without being spoken aloud. He saw lips shape it. He saw a woman pull her child closer. He saw a man in a dyer's stained apron elbow his way to the front of the gawking ring and stand there, watching Emrys with his head tilted, memorizing.

Torv began to push through the crowd toward her, cursing under his breath with a fluency he usually saved for bad terrain.

She lowered her arms. The bull—its handler arriving at last, weeping with relief—took the rope and led the team away, and the dun herd followed, calm as churchgoers. And Emrys stood alone in the emptied lane, breathing hard, hood down, in front of several hundred witnesses.

She turned and found Torv's eyes. He read her expression perfectly: *I know. I know what I've done.* And beneath it, unrepentant: *But the child.*

Because there had been a child. Torv had seen it too, in the final moment—a little girl knocked flat against the granary wall, directly in the herd's path, scooped up by her mother a heartbeat after the oxen stopped. Emrys hadn't calculated it. He knew her well enough now to know she hadn't calculated anything. She'd heard a child scream, and the part of her that had spent twenty-five years swallowing her own voice had simply refused to swallow it one more time.

That was the thing about compassion, Torv thought grimly, reaching her side and steering her firmly into the shadow of the granary wall. It didn't weigh consequences. That was its whole virtue and its whole crime.

"Don't say it," she whispered.

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were going to say it with your face."

"My face," Torv said, "is a professional instrument, and right now it is telling you that we leave Rivergate tonight."

"The fishing fleet—"

"Goes north tomorrow morning, yes, and we will be on it, and between now and then you will not speak to so much as a pigeon." He glanced back at the quay. Most of the crowd was dispersing, the miracle already curdling into anecdote—*did you see, the beasts just stopped, some say the gods did it*—but a few figures lingered. The man in the dyer's apron lingered longest. He was talking now to a harbor official, gesturing toward the granary wall, toward the spot where Emrys had stood.

Toward the spot where they were still standing.

"Walk," Torv said. "Casually. Like people with boring lives."

They walked. Behind them, Torv did not look back, but he listened the way he'd taught himself to listen in thirty years of mapping country that didn't want to be mapped, and what he heard was footsteps falling into rhythm behind theirs. Two sets. Unhurried. Patient.

At the mouth of Cordwainer Lane, he paused at a stall selling fried dough, bought two with their last copper, and used the reflection in the vendor's brass pan to check the street.

The man in the dyer's apron stood at the corner, watching them go. Beside him, a boy of perhaps fifteen was tying his shoelace with tremendous focus, and the boy wore, half-hidden under his collar, the grey cord of the Gift-Hunt's civilian informants—the ones who worked for bread and protection, the ones Wren had told him about in another life, in another interview, across another table.

The informer caught Torv looking. He smiled. It was not an unfriendly smile, which somehow made it infinitely worse.

Emrys bit into her fried dough. "Is it good?" she asked, mouth full, oblivious, exhausted, alive.

"No," Torv said honestly. "It's terrible."

But he ate it anyway, all of it, while the footsteps behind them faded and the lamps of Rivergate came alight one by one along the harbor, and somewhere across the city a message was already being written—a description, a nameless name, *a woman who speaks to beasts*—and set on its way south, toward a hunter who could smell magic like blood.

Three days, Torv thought. If they were lucky, three days before the crown came to Rivergate.

He looked at Emrys, licking grease off her fingers in the lamplight, and began, silently, to plan how to spend every hour of them.
