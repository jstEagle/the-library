## 12. Wren's Ledger

Hunter Wren had a ledger in his head, and he kept it the way other men kept prayer.

*One hedge-healer burned at Ashvale, for a gift that could not have bruised a fly. Two strangers fled the valley the same night. One cartographer, disgraced. One girl of goat-keeping age who would not meet his eye and whose hands smelled, faintly and unforgivably, of crow.*

The ledger was how he slept at night. Every entry a debt. Every debt, sooner or later, collected on behalf of the crown.

He rode into Rivergate three days behind them, with hounds that whined at the city gates like children being dragged to church. The city did not want him in it. Cities never did. But cities were also full of people, and people were full of fear, and fear was the one scent his hounds could follow better than magic itself.

"Gift-Hunter," said the gate sergeant, reading his warrant with the enthusiasm of a man reading his own death warrant. "We've got four thousand refugees camped along the river and bread riots every second morning. You've come to hunt *magic*."

"I've come to do my work," Wren said. "Yours appears to be letting me through."

The sergeant stepped aside with the particular courtesy of a man deciding where he'd be standing when the mob came.

* * *

Rivergate smelled of wet wool, woodsmoke, river mud, and terror. The last scent was thickest near the livestock pens, where refugees from the eastern valleys had crowded their animals together in a space meant for half as many. Oxen, ponies, goats, a bad-tempered mule or six—all of them pressed flank to flank, all of them breathing panic into each other like a fever passing hand to hand.

Wren had seen what happened when a pen like that broke. At Marrowford, two years past, a firecracker in the wrong stall had turned forty oxen into a single animal with ten thousand hooves, and the animal had gone through the market square like a flood through a dam. He still kept the list of names in a separate ledger, the one he never showed anyone. Eleven entries. A child named Pell who had sold him apples.

So when the crack came—a cart axle snapping, iron-bound wood striking cobbles—and the eastern pens surged, Wren knew exactly what he was looking at.

And then he saw her.

She came out of the crowd like someone stepping out of a house she'd lived in all her life but never invited guests to. No running. No shouting. She walked straight toward two thousand pounds of stampeding oxen with her chin up and her hands loose at her sides, and she *spoke*—or seemed to speak; the sound didn't carry, or perhaps it carried somewhere else entirely, some register below hearing—

The oxen stopped.

Not slowed. Not scattered. Stopped, all of them, in the space of three heartbeats, turning their great stupid heads toward her as if she'd called them by name. The front rank stood so close to her that their breath moved her hair. She said something else, low, almost tender, and the whole mass of horn and hide settled like water finding its level.

Around the square, people were cheering. They thought it was luck, or courage, or some saint's intervention. They surged forward to touch her sleeve, to thank her, and she let them, smiling the smile of a woman being congratulated for a trick she hadn't performed.

Wren stood very still at the edge of it all, with his hand nowhere near his sword, and felt the first crack go through the ledger in his head.

* * *

His sister's name was Liss. Everyone who needed to know it knew it already, because Wren had been the one to say it aloud, eleven years ago, in a room with a brazier in it and men in grey robes asking careful questions.

Liss could make plants grow. That was all. Beans out of season. Roses in winter. She'd done it to make their mother smile after their father died, and it had worked, and their mother had smiled, right up until the day the grey-robed men took Liss away to the capital "for testing," from which no one returned with either their gifts or their futures intact.

Wren had told himself a story about it, the way hunters must. The story was: *the law is the law, and I am the law's hand, and a hand does not choose what it holds.* He had repeated this story so many times it had worn smooth as a river stone, smooth enough that most days he couldn't feel the edges anymore.

But he could feel them now, standing in a cheering square in Rivergate, watching a girl calm beasts with a voice that should have seen her chained by now.

Because here was the thing the ledger could not hold: the girl had just saved lives. Perhaps dozens. Perhaps more, if that stampede had reached the bread lines. And she had done it *because* of the thing the crown had taught him to hunt, not despite it. The gift was the rescue. There was no separating them. It was like trying to arrest the rain for wetting the road.

A hound pressed against his leg and whined. Even the dogs knew which direction the truth lay in.

"You're staring, hunter." A fishwife beside him, cheerful, oblivious, offering him a smoked eel off her tray. "That girl's a blessing, eh? Sent by the Mother of Rivers herself, my nan would say. Eel? You look like a man who forgets to eat."

"I eat," Wren said, and took the eel, because refusing kindness felt suddenly like a profession he was tired of practicing.

* * *

He found Torv of the Broken Compass that evening without difficulty, which annoyed him. Cartographers, in Wren's experience, were men who made their living knowing where things were, and Torv ought to have known better than to be findable at the Drowned Anchor inn, drinking small beer in the corner seat with his back to the wall and a rolled tube of vellum on the table between his elbows like a man guarding a child.

"Hunter Wren." Torv didn't look up. "I'd say you're a long way from Ashvale, but we both know you're exactly one step behind us, which is closer than any distance I know how to draw."

"Where is she?"

"In bed, I hope, since she hasn't slept properly in a week. Or on the roof talking to pigeons. With her it's genuinely difficult to say." Torv finally raised his eyes. They were bloodshot and level. "What happens now, Wren? In your ledger, I mean. What's the entry?"

Wren sat down across from him uninvited. The innkeeper brought beer nobody had ordered and departed quickly, which told Wren everything about how news traveled in Rivergate.

"The entry is: two fugitives located. One under edict of arrest." He turned the mug slowly on the table. "The other is a disgraced mapmaker whose worst crime is honest work done for the wrong family name."

"And yet here you sit, having a drink with him."

"And yet."

Torv studied him for a long moment. "You watched her today. In the square. With the oxen."

"I watch everyone. It's the trade."

"You watched her," Torv repeated, "and you haven't reported it. I've been in this city two days and I know how fast word moves to the garrison. If you'd sent your report this morning, there'd be grey robes in this inn by now." He leaned forward. "So the question isn't where she is, hunter. The question is why you're still sitting there."

It was a good question. Wren had been avoiding it all afternoon with the discipline of a man walking past an open cellar door.

"My sister," Wren heard himself say, and stopped, appalled. Twenty years of silence on the subject, and it fell out of him over bad beer because a stranger asked a direct question. "Her name was Liss. She grew roses in winter."

Torv went very still. "Ah," he said softly. "One of yours, then."

"One of mine." Wren laughed, and it came out wrong, too short. "She grew roses in winter and I told the grey robes where to find her, because the law is the law and a hand doesn't choose what it holds. That's the story I tell. I tell it very well. I've had practice."

"And today?"

Today. Today he had watched a girl walk into a stampede with nothing but a voice the crown had outlawed, and he had felt—not fear, he knew fear, fear was a tool—he had felt something older and worse. Recognition. The specific grief of seeing a thing done rightly that had only ever been done wrongly in his presence. Liss growing beans out of season to make their mother smile. Emrys Delacroix stopping two thousand pounds of terrified flesh with a word, and a fishwife calling her blessed.

"Today," Wren said, "I watched the law be wrong in public, in daylight, in front of four thousand witnesses, and the law clapped."

* * *

He wrote the report that night in his room above the stables, by candlelight, with the hounds asleep across the threshold like furry accusations.

The report was a work of art. He had written many; falsification, done properly, was simply cartography of another kind—you drew the road that suited you, and you drew it well enough that no one looked twice.

*Pursued subjects through hill country south of Ashvale. Trail compromised by heavy refugee traffic on the eastern roads; scent-work unreliable amid livestock concentrations.* True, technically. His hounds had complained bitterly about the sheep.

*Sightings reported of a woman matching the subject's description at the Rivergate markets; investigation ongoing. No confirmation of gifted activity. Recommend continued surveillance before committing resources to arrest; subject believed to be traveling toward the coast, likely alone.*

Alone. That was the load-bearing lie, the one that bought time. A lone traveler was a matter for routine pursuit. A pair, plus a royal falcon, plus whatever the girl had done in the square—that was a matter for the grey robes, and the grey robes traveled fast and asked no questions afterward.

Three days. He counted it out like a miser: the report would take two days to reach the regional commander at Harrow Ford, one day for the reply to crawl back. Three days in which Emrys Delacroix could be anywhere, and the ledger in his head would show no new debts.

He signed it. Hunter Wren, in service to King Aldric, in the fourth year of his reign. Then he sat looking at his own signature for a long time, because it occurred to him that he could not explain what he had just done even to himself. He tried the usual stories on for size—the law is the law; a hand does not choose—but they wouldn't fit anymore. The stone had cracked clean through.

In the end he settled on the only account that held weight: *Somewhere in this kingdom there are roses growing out of season tonight, and for once I am not the reason they get torn up.*

It wasn't loyalty. He knew that much. Whatever it was, it sat on the far side of loyalty, in country he had no map for—which was, he supposed, why he needed a cartographer.

* * *

Two mornings later, the reply came back from Harrow Ford faster than any reply had a right to travel, and Wren knew before he broke the seal that his arithmetic had failed him.

The handwriting was not the regional commander's. It was crisp, black, and terrible—the hand of a man who signed death warrants before breakfast. Aldric's own chancery.

*To Hunter Wren. Your report is noted. Be advised that independent sources place both subjects within Rivergate as of three days past, in company of a royal messenger bird. The crown is not deceived by stale trails.*

*You will take them yourself. Alive. The girl especially. His Majesty has questions regarding the Door of Salt, and has been informed she knows how to answer them.*

*Fail in this, and your sister's file will be reopened. We understand she left effects. Effects can be examined.*

Wren read it twice. Then he folded it, precisely, along its original creases, and put it inside his coat over his heart, where he kept the other things that hurt.

Outside, the bells of Rivergate were ringing for the free cities' council—the great debate, the whole city talking of surrender terms and treaties while Aldric's army ate the eastern roads mile by mile. Three days, he had bought them. Perhaps less, now. Somewhere across the city a girl who spoke to beasts was about to stand in front of men with maps and armies and ask them to do the unthinkable, and the only person in Rivergate who understood exactly how unthinkable the crown found it was the man they'd sent to drag her back.

Wren picked up his hounds' leads. The dogs looked up at him with their honest yellow eyes, waiting, as always, for him to point them at something.

"Not yet," he told them quietly. "Not this time."

He went to find a cartographer.
