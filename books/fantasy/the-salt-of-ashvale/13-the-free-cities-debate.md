## 13. The Free Cities Debate

The Council Hall of Rivergate had been built by people who expected to be listened to.

Torv felt it the moment he walked in under the great arched doors—everything about the room was designed for agreement. The ceiling rose in pale cedar ribs like the inside of a ship's hull. Light fell through colored glass onto a floor of polished river-stone, and nine chairs sat on a dais at the far end, each one carved higher and grander than the last, so that whoever sat in the center—the Speaker of Speakers, currently a broad, comfortable woman named Maren Ostwick—looked less like a magistrate and more like a monument to herself.

It was, Torv thought, exactly the kind of room where nothing useful had ever been decided.

He stood at the back with Emrys and Brannoc, or rather Brannoc perched above them on a rafter beam with the smug stillness of a bird who has claimed the high ground and knows no one can do anything about it. Around them pressed perhaps four hundred people: merchants in good wool, guildmasters with ink-stained cuffs, a delegation of fisherfolk who smelled of tar and had clearly come straight off their boats, refugees from the eastern villages standing along the walls because no one had thought to give them seats.

Three days since Wren's false report had bought them time. Two days since Emrys had stood before this same council and told them what she could do. One day since the council had adjourned "to deliberate"—which, in Torv's long experience of committees, meant *to find a way to say no politely*.

"They're going to surrender," he murmured.

"I know," Emrys said.

"You could at least pretend I might be wrong. It's bad for my self-esteem when you agree with me that quickly."

She almost smiled. It didn't reach her eyes. She'd barely slept; he'd seen her at the inn window before dawn, watching the road east, where the dust of Aldric's army would eventually rise if it hadn't already. Her hands kept moving—turning over a goat-bell she carried in her pocket like a talisman, clicking it open and shut. Click. Click. Click.

On the dais, Speaker Ostwick rose. She had the voice of a woman who'd spent thirty years talking over fish markets, and she used every ounce of it now.

"Citizens of Rivergate, guests, and friends." A pause, weighted, rehearsed. "The council has deliberated on the matter of King Aldric's advance, and on the… unusual proposal put forward two days past."

*Unusual.* Torv winced. In his experience, *unusual* was what politicians said when they meant *mad*, and *bold* was what they said when they meant *someone else should try it first*.

"The council recognizes the courage of the young woman who addressed us," Ostwick continued, nodding graciously toward Emrys as though she were a prize marrow at a harvest fair. "And we honor her spirit. But courage is not strategy. We are cities of trade, not war. Our walls were built to keep out weather and tax collectors, not armies. And so the council has resolved—" here she lifted a scroll with the ceremony of a priest raising relics, "—to send envoys to His Majesty King Aldric, offering our submission, our tribute, and our assurances of loyalty, in exchange for the preservation of our persons and property."

A murmur ran through the hall—not outrage, Torv noted. Relief. He watched it move through the crowd like oil through water: shoulders dropping, glances exchanged, a merchant near the front actually exhaling and mopping his brow. They wanted it to be over. Four hundred people, and most of them wanted to kneel and be allowed to keep their shops.

"Submission," Emrys repeated, very quietly.

"Don't," Torv said.

"I'm not going to—"

"You're doing the thing with your jaw. The thing you do before you do the thing."

She ignored him, which was her right and, lately, her habit. But it was Torv who felt the true weight of the moment settle on him, cold as river-mud, because he knew things the room did not. He knew what submission to Aldric meant, because he'd read the edicts. Gift-testing was only the beginning. The king didn't want tribute from the free cities; he wanted *certainty*. Every child examined. Every strange talent catalogued or cut out. The free cities wouldn't be conquered—they'd be *corrected*, the way a cartographer corrects an error on a map. Inked out.

He'd spent his whole life studying erased places. He recognized the process from the inside.

Ostwick was still speaking. "The envoys depart at first light. Any citizen wishing to lodge concerns may submit them in writing to the clerk's office, where they will be read in due course—"

"In due course," Emrys said, louder.

The murmur died. Heads turned. On the dais, Ostwick's gracious smile held, but her eyes sharpened, finding Emrys in the crowd the way a gull finds a dropped fish.

"Ah. The young lady has something to add."

"She does," Torv muttered, "unfortunately."

Emrys stepped forward into the aisle, and Torv followed because someone had to, and because—though he'd have denied it under torture—he liked being near the center of things when they went wrong. It was the cartographer in him. Disasters were just geography happening quickly.

"You speak of envoys," Emrys said. Her voice wasn't loud, but it carried; there was something in it that made the room lean in, some animal quality that even untrained ears responded to. "Envoys carrying tribute. Tell me, Speaker—what does Aldric do with tribute?"

"He accepts it, presumably, and—"

"And then he tests your children." The words dropped into the hall like stones into a well. "I've seen the edict. *Every person found possessing unnatural communion with beasts shall be conveyed to the capital for examination.* That's not a threat against witches, Speaker. That's a census. He will count your children the way you count coin. And the ones who fail the counting—"

"We understand the edict," said a guildmaster from the second row, a thin man with a voice like a filing cabinet. "We also understand armies. You propose we fight one? With what?"

"Yes," Emrys said. "That is my proposal. With what."

Silence. Somewhere in the rafters, Brannoc shifted his feet with a sound like a knife being drawn slowly across stone, and several people looked up nervously.

"With what," the guildmaster repeated flatly, "do you imagine we fight?"

Torv saw it coming the way you see weather coming off the sea—a darkening on the horizon of her face—and he stepped half in front of her, purely on instinct, the way you'd step between a friend and a cliff edge.

"Emrys," he said quietly. "Not here."

"Where, then?" she asked, not looking at him. "There's nowhere else. This is the room where they're deciding to hand over every gifted child in the valley cities, and everyone in it knows it, and everyone in it is going to let it happen because the chairs are comfortable and the surrender scroll is already written." She turned back to the dais, and her voice rang off the cedar ribs now, full and unhidden, and Torv felt the old fear climb his spine like frost. "You want to know with what? With the beasts."

The word moved through the hall like wind through wheat. People looked at each other. Someone laughed—one short, disbelieving bark—and then stopped when no one joined him.

"There is a kingdom under the marshes south of here," Emrys went on. "Old Sablemark. Your grandparents' grandparents burned its maps and drowned its roads, and you think that means it's gone. It isn't. Its beasts are still there. Wolves with memories longer than your archives. Marsh-elephants older than this city. Falcon clans that remember every treaty men ever broke. They have waited a hundred years behind a door that only a speaker can open. And I am a speaker."

"A *speaker*," the guildmaster said, tasting the word like spoiled milk.

"Someone who talks to animals, yes. Say it plainly. Half this city already does, in the market stalls, when they think no one's listening." She spread her hands. "I'm not asking you to believe me out of charity. Open the Door with me. Let me go to them. If Sablemark's beasts stand with the free cities, Aldric's army meets something it has never met—something the crown has spent a century pretending doesn't exist."

"And if they don't stand with us?" Ostwick asked. Her voice was gentle now, which was worse. Gentle was what you used on children and lunatics. "If you open this door of yours and the wolves decide they prefer men dead? What then, child? You've brought a second army into a war, and no guarantee whose side it fights on."

"I'd give them my word—"

"*Your* word." A new voice, from the fisherfolk delegation—a broad woman with salt-cracked hands and a face like a figurehead. "No offense meant, girl, but whose word is yours worth? Who vouches for you? Who are your people?"

"My mother spoke too," Emrys said simply. "She's dead."

"Convenient," the fisherwoman said, not cruelly, just the way practical people are cruel without meaning to be.

Torv stepped forward. He hadn't planned to. His mouth simply opened, and twenty years of disgrace came pouring out ahead of his good sense.

"I vouch for her."

Every head turned. He was aware, distantly, of Brannoc descending from the rafter in a slow spiral of grey feathers to land on the rail beside him, which lent the moment a theatrical quality Torv had neither requested nor appreciated.

"My name is Torv, son of Halvard, of the Cartographers' Guild of Sablemark. Which makes me, depending on your histories, either extinct or a liar." He let that sit. "My family drew the maps of the Door of Salt. My grandfather helped erase them, on the old king's orders, and it broke something in us that never healed. I have spent my life drinking away that inheritance, and I'll thank you not to look so surprised—I know how I look. But hear me on this one thing, because it's the only thing I know better than drink: Sablemark is real. The Door is real. The beasts beyond it are real, and they are *waiting*, and they have been waiting for a speaker since before any of you were born. I have a map that proves all of it. Blank to your eyes, plain as print to hers."

"Then show us the map," Ostwick said.

"No," Torv said.

The single syllable landed harder than anything Emrys had shouted. Even she looked at him.

"The map works through her gift," he said. "To use it here—in this hall, in this city—is to light a beacon that Aldric's hunters can smell for fifty miles. There is a man named Wren who has tracked us across three hundred miles of fog and causeway, a man who can scent magic like blood in water. The only reason we're standing here free is that he chose, for reasons I'll never understand, to lie in his report and buy us three days. Those three days are nearly gone." He turned to face the whole hall, and heard his own voice shake and let it shake, because honest trembling was worth more than brave calm. "So no, I won't show you the map. I won't spend the proof to convince people who've already written their surrender scroll. I'll tell you instead what surrender buys. I've read the edicts. It buys your shops—for now. Your children—examined. Your grandchildren—born into a country where anyone who hears what beasts say is taken away in a wagon. You are not buying peace, citizens of Rivergate. You are buying time for Aldric to finish the work he started in Sablemark. And the last time this crown finished such work, my family drew the map of what was left. It was mostly water."

Silence again—but a different silence. Torv had spent enough years in taverns to know the difference between silence that rejects you and silence that is thinking. This one was thinking. Small mercies.

It was the fisherwoman who broke it, arms crossed. "Even if the girl's beasts came—even if—they'd fight for us? Men used beasts in war once. My grandmother's songs are all about it. Why would they forgive that?"

"They wouldn't," Emrys said. "They shouldn't. They'll ask me why they should trust any human ever again, and I don't have an answer that isn't shameful. All I have is the truth: Aldric will burn the marshes after he burns your cities. Their door closes forever either way. I'm asking them to choose, the way you're choosing now. That's all leadership is, in the end. Asking people to choose with their eyes open." She paused, and her voice gentled in a way that undid Torv more than the shouting had. "You asked who vouches for me. No one living can. But the falcon on this rail carried a message sealed for Queen Sable, twenty years dead, from someone who remembers the Door. Something in this world still believes a speaker is coming. I'd like to prove it right before Aldric proves it wrong."

For a heartbeat—just one—Torv thought it had worked. He saw it in faces along the walls: the refugee families, the boat-people, a young apprentice clutching his master's sleeve with shining eyes. The ones with nothing left to lose were leaning toward her.

Then Speaker Ostwick called the vote.

"All in favor of sending envoys to His Majesty King Aldric, offering terms of peaceful submission?"

Hands rose down the length of the dais, one after another, smooth as tide. Eight of nine. The ninth—a young councilor from the tanners' quarter, a man with burn scars on his forearms—hesitated, glanced at Emrys, glanced at the scroll, and raised his hand too.

"Carried," Ostwick said. "Envoys depart at first light. This session concludes."

The hall exhaled. Chairs scraped. The merchants began immediately discussing freight rates, because the world insists on continuing, and within moments the great cedar room was full of small talk and the smell of snuffed candles, and Emrys stood alone in the aisle with her fists closed at her sides, watching the surrender scroll being rolled up and tied with ribbon. Ribbon. Torv wanted to burn the ribbon.

"They voted," she said. It wasn't a question.

"They voted," Torv agreed.

"To hand over their children. With ribbon on it."

"Yes." He touched her elbow, gently. "Come away from here. People are looking."

"People are always looking." But she let him turn her toward the doors, and they walked out together into the grey afternoon, Brannoc gliding overhead, past the fisherwoman who caught Emrys's arm as she passed.

"For what it's worth," the woman said quietly, "my boats leave for the southern marshes Thursday, running eels. If a person were mad enough to go knocking on a drowned kingdom's door." She shrugged. "Eels don't ask questions."

Emrys stared at her. Then, slowly, she smiled—the first real smile Torv had seen from her in three days, tired and fierce and bright as a struck flint.

"Thursday," she said.

"Thursday," Torv echoed weakly, once the woman had gone. "We're leaving Thursday. Of course we are. Why would we rest? Resting is for people whose plans haven't been rejected by a committee." He sighed and looked up at the falcon wheeling against the clouds, heading, he noticed, not toward the inn but south, out over the rooftops, toward the marshes—as if the bird had heard everything and needed no vote at all. "Brannoc?" he called. "Where are you going?"

But the falcon only cried once—a sharp, carrying note that rolled down over the city like a horn-call—and beat away southward with the wind, and Emrys watched him go with an expression Torv had learned, over these long weeks, to recognize and dread in equal measure.

It was the look of someone who had just stopped asking permission.

"Get your maps ready," she said. "We ride at first light. Before the envoys do."
