## 14. Betrayal at the Tide Gate

The morning it all came apart began, as so many disasters do, with porridge.

Torv had insisted on buying breakfast at a dockside stall near the Rivergate fish market—*"You can't plan a war on an empty stomach, Emrys, my mother said that, and she was wrong about everything else"*—and so they sat on upturned crates with tin bowls, watching gulls argue over herring heads while Torv sketched the route east in spilled salt on the tabletop.

"The tide flats," he said, tapping the salt into a long pale smear. "Old Sablemark's causeways. They surface twice a day for perhaps three hours. The rest of the time there's nothing there but water deep enough to drown a horse and cold enough to make it grateful."

"And the Door of Salt is at the end of them."

"At the end of *what*," Torv corrected gently, "because officially there are no tide flats and no Door, and I am officially a man who draws pictures of places that don't exist." He smiled, but it didn't reach his eyes. They hadn't reached his eyes much since Rivergate.

Emrys stirred her porridge. Brannoc perched on her shoulder, feathered and patched and healing well, his talons gripping her coat like a small judgmental hand. She'd grown used to the weight of him. She was already dreading the day he'd fly without it.

"Three days' walk along the flats," she said. "Then what? We knock?"

"Then we find out whether my family's maps were lies or merely cursed." Torv swept the salt off the table into his palm and let it fall back to the earth, which seemed somehow ceremonial. "I've been thinking about the beasts."

"You're always thinking about the beasts."

"Because you talk about them in your sleep." He said it lightly, but his ears went pink. "Emrys. If this works—if we open the Door—the beasts come through into a world that burned their country and salted the memory of it. What makes you think they'll fight *for* us?"

"I don't know," she admitted. It was the truth, and the truth had become a habit between them somewhere on the drowned road. "But I know they'll listen. That has to be worth something."

He looked at her then—really looked, the way he did when he thought she wasn't noticing—and opened his mouth to say something that was clearly not about beasts.

That was when the ox bellowed.

Not an ordinary bellow. Emrys knew ordinary bellows; she'd spent her life elbow-deep in livestock grievance. This was a scream of alarm, and it came from the draft herd in the market yard behind them, and beneath it she heard something worse: boots. Many boots. Moving in formation.

Brannoc went rigid on her shoulder. *Soldiers,* he said, though no sound left him. *At the gate. Sixteen. And the one who smells like ink and iron.*

Wren.

Emrys's spoon clattered into the bowl. Across the stall, a rat-faced man in a fish-seller's apron was already slipping away down an alley, walking quickly, not running—the particular walk of someone who has just been paid and does not want to be seen spending it.

"The informer," she breathed. "The one from the stampede. Torv—he sold us. He sold us yesterday, probably. Maybe the moment I touched those oxen."

Torv was already standing, coins abandoned, hand closing around her wrist. "Back way. Through the net-menders' lane. Don't run—running looks guilty."

"We *are* guilty."

"We're *fleeing*, which is different and much more dignified."

They slipped into the alley as the first red-and-gold cloaks entered the market square. Behind them, a crier's voice rose over the stalls, flat and official: *"By order of King Aldric, surrender the beast-speaker and the traitor cartographer, and the city of Rivergate will be spared the full measure of the crown's justice—"*

"The full measure," Torv muttered, hauling her past drying nets that smelled of brine and old rope. "Charming. As opposed to what, the partial measure? A light burning? A moderate imprisonment?"

"Torv."

"Yes?"

"He said *surrender*. Not *capture*." Her stomach had gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with the morning fog. "They're not trying to take us alive."

Torv glanced back once. Whatever he saw in the lane behind them made his jaw set. "Right. New plan. Less dignity."

* * *

They ran.

Rivergate woke around them in confusion—shutters banging, a fruit cart overturned, a grandmother shouting curses after the soldiers with the fluency of long practice. Emrys kept hold of Torv's sleeve and let Brannoc be her second pair of eyes, the falcon's sight pouring through their strange bond: *Two on the left street. Clear by the chandler's. The tall one circles back—no, he stops. He stops.*

She felt it too, then—a stillness in the chase, a held breath. She risked a look over her shoulder.

Hunter Wren stood at the mouth of Fishbone Lane, alone, his hounds sitting at heel in perfect silence. His crossbow was slung, not raised. His face, when she met his eyes across thirty feet of wet cobblestone, was not the face of a man about to loose a bolt.

It was the face of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

"Go," Wren said. Just that. Quiet enough that only she could have heard it.

And then, louder, for the soldiers spilling into the square behind him: *"They went toward the harbor! West gate! Move!"*

Emrys stood frozen for half a heartbeat too long. Torv grabbed her arm. "Don't question gifts," he gasped. "Run!"

They ran. But she carried Wren's voice with her out of the city like a stone in her shoe, turning itself over and over: why would a hunter spend his reputation buying them minutes? What had he seen in the market yard—her hands on the oxen, her lips moving in the old tongue—that had cracked something behind those grey eyes?

There was no time to wonder. The west road was crawling with cloaks. Which meant the only way out of Rivergate was down.

* * *

The tide flats began where the city's refuse ended: a gray stairway of ancient stone descending into brown water, slick with weed, each step carved with letters worn nearly smooth. Old Sablemark's script. Torv knelt and pressed his palm against one for a moment, the way another man might touch a grave-marker.

"My grandfather helped bury these stairs," he said. "Put false survey lines on every map, bribed three harbormasters, told sailors the flats ate ships. And now I'm using his lies to save my life. There's a lesson in that, probably. I'm too busy to learn it."

"How long until the tide turns?" Emrys peered eastward. The flats stretched away before them like a road made of mirror and mud, pools catching the pale sky, channels threading silver between hummocks of samphire. Somewhere far out, at the edge of seeing, the air shimmered oddly—as if the horizon couldn't quite decide what to be.

"Water's already retreating. We have maybe two hours before it comes home hungry." Torv checked the straps of his pack, rolled his shoulders, and managed something almost like a grin. "After you, heir of voices."

"Don't call me that."

"Why not? It's true."

"It's *heavy*, is what it is." She stepped down onto the first exposed causeway stone and felt it hum faintly under her boot—not sound exactly, more like recognition. The flats remembered footsteps. The flats remembered *everything*. That was rather the point of them.

They made good time for the first hour. The causeways ran straight and sure beneath the shallow sheets of water, and the fog of Ashvale felt very far away indeed. Brannoc rode the wind above them in wide, lazy circles, and Emrys let herself imagine—for whole minutes at a stretch—that they might actually make it. That there might be a Door, and a key, and a world on the other side where speaking to goats was not a crime but a kindness.

She was mid-thought, laughing at something Torv said about harbormasters and their flexible morals, when Brannoc screamed.

*Crossbows! On the sea-wall!*

They hit the mud together as the first bolts hissed overhead. Emrys looked back and saw them—soldiers wading along the ridge of the old sea-wall, a dozen of them, red-and-gold cloaks plastered dark with spray. And in front, walking with the unhurried stride of a man who had never once needed to hurry, Hunter Wren.

But Wren's crossbow stayed slung. The others did not.

"They'll outrun us on the wall!" Torv shouted over the next volley. A bolt buried itself in the mud a hand's width from his knee. "We can't make the far shore before they cut us off at the channel!"

"Then we don't go to the far shore!" Emrys's heart was hammering, but her mind had gone strangely clear, the way it did when the beasts spoke. "The flats—the *beasts* of the flats! Torv, there are things living out here, things the fog never reached because the fog doesn't come past the coast—"

"You can't possibly—"

"*Brannoc!*" She lifted her arm and the falcon dropped out of the sky onto her wrist, feathers wild, eyes fierce. She leaned close and gave him the words in the voice she had hidden her whole life, the voice like roots and rain and old stone: *Fly east. Find the marsh-elephants, the wolf-clans, the herons of the drowned orchards. Tell them the heir of voices stands upon the flats of Sablemark and asks for the debt owed to the old kings. Tell them the Door may open again.*

Brannoc's head cocked. For a moment he simply regarded her, and she felt the enormity of what she asked settle over both of them—he was small, and the distance was vast, and the beasts of Sablemark had not answered any human voice in twenty years.

*If I die on the wing?* he asked.

*Then die loudly,* Emrys said. *Make them remember you.*

Something that might have been falcon laughter glittered in his eye. Then he was gone, up into the gray, wings hammering, smaller and smaller until he was a fleck, until he was nothing, until the sky closed over him like water.

A bolt took the space where he had been.

"No—" Torv pulled her down hard. "Stay low! Emrys, listen to me—the channel, the deep channel, we can wade it if we—"

The volley came again, six bolts in a ragged sheet, and Emrys did the thing she swore she'd never do in front of witnesses: she threw up her hand and *spoke*, aloud, in the beast-voice, at the arrows.

Of course arrows cannot hear. Arrows have no ears. But the wind does, and the water does, and the flats themselves do—and the bolt meant for her heart struck the rising mist instead and veered wide, splashing into a pool with a sound like a slapped cheek. Two more twisted aside. One did not.

Time went strange. Later, she would remember it in pieces: the smell of blood, sharp as a struck match. Torv's weight against her side. The exact color of the mud—brown going to rust going to red. His hand finding hers with absurd gentleness, as if he were the one comforting her.

"Oh," he said. He sounded surprised, and mildly apologetic, the way he did about everything. "Oh, bother."

"You're hit." Her voice came out very small. "Torv, you're hit, you're—"

"Bolt. Shoulder. Or possibly chest." He tried to laugh and it turned into something wet and awful. "I'd like to state, for the record, that I always suspected I'd die of drink in a mediocre inn, and this is a deeply unflattering deviation."

"Stop talking. Stop—press here, keep pressing here—" She jammed her palms against the wound below his collarbone and blood pushed up between her fingers, warm, too warm, so much of it. The flats drank it greedily. Somewhere behind them the soldiers were coming down off the sea-wall, and Wren was shouting orders she couldn't parse, and none of it mattered, none of it, because Torv's face had gone the color of old paper and his eyes were losing their focus the way a map loses its ink.

"Emrys." He caught her wrist. His grip was still strong; that had to mean something. "Listen. Listen to me. The Door needs a speaker. You have to leave me and—"

"If you say 'leave me,' I will feed you to a marsh-elephant myself."

"There aren't any marsh-elephants within forty miles, I mapped it—"

"THEN YOU'LL LIVE OUT OF SPITE." Her voice cracked clean down the middle. The tide pools shivered around them. Far off, something vast shifted in the reeds and lifted its head.

Torv smiled up at her. Blood on his teeth. Eyes bright with fever or mischief or something else entirely, something he'd been carrying since a market stall and a bowl of porridge and a sentence he'd never finished.

"I meant to tell you," he said. "On the drowned road. At the orchard with the ghost-geese, and you laughed, and I thought—there's time. There's time." His thumb moved against her wrist, once, like a signature. "There's never time. Emrys Delacroix, I—"

The world answered for him.

Out of the eastern haze came a sound Emrys had heard only in stories and in the bones of old roads: the horn-call of the marsh-elephants, deep enough to feel in the fillings of your teeth. Then wolves, many voices braided into one rising note. Then wings—wings beyond counting, a darkness rolling in off the sea like weather, like nightfall arriving early and furious.

The soldiers on the flats stopped advancing. Stopped breathing, nearly. One of them dropped his crossbow and simply sat down in the water.

Through the parting fog came Brannoc, flying at the head of a hundred falcons in a screaming silver wedge, and beneath him the flats themselves seemed to move—reeds bending back, channels parting, the great gray shapes of the marsh-elephants wading ashore with the patience of mountains deciding to relocate.

Wren stood alone in the shallows between the armies, arms slightly raised, and Emrys would swear until her dying day that the hunter was smiling.

She bent back over Torv, pressing the wound, whispering to his blood to stay, to his heart to keep its rhythm, to the flats to hold him the way they held everything.

"You're not allowed," she told him fiercely. "Do you hear me? You found the map. You don't get to miss the ending."

His eyes found hers, and focused, and stayed.

"Hadn't planned to," he whispered. "Terrible... cartography... dying halfway there."

And the tide, faithful old traitor, began to turn—coming home hungry across the flats of Sablemark, bringing the sea with it, and somewhere beyond the sea, if his family's maps were anything but cursed, a seam in the air that wanted one true memory as its toll.

Emrys put her arms around him and held on, as the horns of the beasts rolled over them like thunder, and the water rose, and the Door waited.
