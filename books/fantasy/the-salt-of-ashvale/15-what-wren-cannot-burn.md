## 15. What Wren Cannot Burn

The cell smelled of wet straw and old fear, and Hunter Wren had been standing outside it for the better part of an hour, working up the courage to do his job.

That was the trouble with courage, he had learned over a long and unlovely career: it never arrived when you wanted it. It came at inconvenient moments—mid-bite of breakfast, mid-prayer, mid-lie—and abandoned you precisely when a man needed it most. Tonight it was nowhere to be found. His supper sat untouched in the guardhouse. His hounds were stabled two streets away because he could not bear to let them smell what was about to happen. And Torv of the Broken Compass was sitting on the other side of that iron door, whistling.

Whistling. In a cell awaiting execution.

Wren pushed the door open. "You're supposed to be terrified."

Torv looked up from the floor where he'd been scratching something into the straw—a map, probably, because the man drew maps the way other men breathed, compulsively and without much hope of anyone thanking them for it. He had a fresh cut above his eye from the arrest and a bruise blooming along his jaw, and somehow he still managed to look like a scholar who'd wandered into the wrong tavern rather than a condemned traitor.

"I tried terror," Torv said. "Found it boring. Mostly I'm annoyed. Do you know they confiscated my boots? The *boots*, Wren. My feet are going to be cold on the scaffold tomorrow and some sergeant is walking around in my good boots."

"They'll hang you, not behead you. You won't feel your feet."

"That's oddly comforting. Thank you." Torv studied him with those ink-stained eyes that saw too much. "You didn't come here to discuss footwear."

"No," Wren said. "I didn't."

* * *

He had filed three false reports now. Three. Each one a small treason folded into official language, each one buying the Delacroix woman and her cartographer another day of freedom. Wren had spent twenty years building a reputation as the crown's most reliable hound—the man who never lost a scent, who never flinched, who turned in his own sister when the edicts first came down and slept soundly afterward, or so everyone believed.

He had not slept soundly since. That was the part nobody knew. Twenty years of sleeping badly, one night at a time, and every night the same dream: Maren at fourteen, standing in their mother's kitchen with frost on the window, saying *I can't help it, Wrennie, the kettle talks to me,* and laughing—laughing, because she was fourteen and thought their family would protect her.

He had reported her by noon.

They said afterward she hadn't struggled. They said she'd looked at him through the crowd, and he'd made himself meet her eyes, because running from it felt worse than facing it, and she had mouthed something at him that he had spent two decades refusing to read aloud in his own head.

Tomorrow they meant to hang Torv on the same scaffold. And tomorrow morning, if the reports reached the capital on schedule, Aldric's clerks would notice that the third report contradicted the second, and then the fourth would contradict everything, and Hunter Wren's long career as a reliable hound would end the way all careers ended under King Aldric.

So really, the choice had already been made. He was just here to catch up to himself.

"Delacroix got out," Wren said. "Crossed the marshes two nights ago. My hounds lost her at the tide flats—the salt kills scent, which I suspect is why your Door is where it is. Clever people, your ancestors."

"My ancestors were cowards with excellent penmanship," Torv said. "But go on."

"The falcon carried word ahead. She's beyond my reach now." Wren leaned against the wall, arms crossed, because if he kept moving he was going to do something irreversible, and he wanted a moment longer to pretend he might not. "Which leaves you. And leaves me with orders to execute you publicly at dawn, as an example to the city. The heralds are already painting the placard. They spelled your name wrong, incidentally."

"Of course they did. It's a difficult name."

"It's four letters, Torv."

"And yet."

Wren laughed. It startled him—he couldn't remember the last time he'd laughed, and it came out rusty, like a gate hinge in winter. Torv grinned up at him, and there it was again, that maddening quality the man had, that total absence of dignity in the face of doom. Emrys Delacroix must have seen it too. Wren had watched them together across three cities, always at a distance, always through the lens of the hunt, and he had catalogued the way they orbited each other the way he catalogued everything: carefully, thoroughly, and with no idea what any of it meant until far too late.

"You love her," Wren said.

Torv went very still. For a moment all the humor drained out of him, leaving something raw underneath. "Is that in my file?"

"It's not in anybody's file. Files don't have room for it." Wren rubbed his face. "Twenty years ago I put my sister in a cell like this one. She was gifted. Small gift, harmless gift—kettles, spoons, nothing that could hurt a fly. But the edict didn't care about size. Neither did I. I told myself the law was the law, and a man who picks and chooses which laws to keep isn't a man at all, he's just a criminal with better manners." He paused. "I've had twenty years to consider whether that was wisdom or cowardice dressed up in a uniform. I've reached a conclusion. I won't share it. It's embarrassing."

"Wren—"

"Don't thank me yet." He pushed off the wall and crouched, producing from inside his coat a ring of keys, a leather pouch, and a folded paper, which he laid out on the straw between them like a merchant displaying wares of dubious provenance. "Keys. Coin—less than you deserve, more than I can spare. And this." He tapped the paper. "A name. Not mine. Hers."

Torv's breath caught. "Maren."

"You know it?"

"She mentioned it once. In Rivergate. She said—" Torv hesitated, choosing his words the way he chose contour lines, precisely and with visible care. "She said a hunter once gave her a horse and a name, and that she'd spend her life trying to be worth it. She uses it as a password among the hidden folk. There are safehouses, Wren. Whole networks. Say *Maren* in the right kitchen in the right city and doors open that stay closed for everyone else." He looked up. "You built that. With her name. All these years."

"I built a network for the people I helped destroy," Wren said flatly. "It's not absolution. It's bookkeeping." He stood. "The horse is bayed in the south stable, third stall, gray mare named Bess. She bites, but only people she doesn't respect, so you should be fine."

"Genuinely unsure how to take that."

"Take it however you like. Dawn comes early."

* * *

The problem with freeing a prisoner, Wren reflected as he walked Torv through the back corridors of the garrison, was that it required an astonishing amount of lying to people whose entire profession was noticing lies.

He'd handled it by the simple expedient of telling the truth sideways. To the night sergeant: *transferring the prisoner for questioning.* To the gate watch: *special assignment, crown business, don't ask.* Men rarely questioned Wren; that had been the great convenience of being reliable, and tonight it was the great irony. Every lie he'd ever refused to tell had earned him the credit to tell these.

At the stable door he stopped.

"There's a guard rotation change at the fourth bell," he said. "After that, the south road is clear to the river. Follow it west, then north through the fens. Don't take the causeway—it's watched. There's a ferryman at Miller's Bend with a crooked left hand; tell him Maren sent you and he'll take you to the coast for free and complain about it the whole way, which you should consider a form of hospitality."

Torv shrugged on the coat Wren had brought him—one of the hunter's own, dark and unremarkable, the kind of garment that made a man invisible in exactly the way a fugitive needed. "And you? What's your road?"

"I don't have one." Wren said it lightly, but the weight of it settled into his bones as he spoke, and he found—to his considerable surprise—that it didn't frighten him. It felt almost like setting down luggage carried too long. "By the time they figure out what happened here, I intend to be standing in my own quarters, asleep, with my report on the desk and my conscience wherever I left it. Let them come. I've spent twenty years waiting for this particular knock at the door. It'll be nice to stop flinching at all the others."

"That's a terrible plan."

"It's the only plan I have that doesn't involve running. I did the arithmetic a long time ago, Torv. A man who runs spends the rest of his life looking over his shoulder, and I've done enough looking over shoulders for three careers." He opened the stall. Bess pinned her ears at Torv, considered him, and apparently decided he was tolerable. "Besides. Someone has to stand in front of the Delacroix woman's empty cell tomorrow and explain to the king's men how a prisoner escaped from under the nose of the realm's best hunter. If it's going to be anyone, it may as well be someone who's actually good at his job."

"You could come with us." Torv said it quietly, mounting up. "West. There's room. There's always room."

For one absurd moment Wren imagined it: the fen road at dawn, the gray light, the three of them riding out of the country Aldric had made, toward a Door that ate memories and a war that ate everything else. He imagined Maren's name spoken freely, in daylight, by people who smiled when they said it.

"No," he said. "Someone has to stay and be the example. Otherwise the example becomes the escape, and escapes make hopeful people, and hopeful people get whole villages burned." He stepped back. "I can give them a hunter who failed. I can't give them a hunter who fled. There's a difference, and the difference matters to frightened people."

Torv looked down at him for a long moment. Then he extended his hand, and Wren took it, and neither of them said anything, because there wasn't anything that fit, and both of them were men who disliked using words that didn't fit.

"One more thing," Torv said as he gathered the reins. "Your sister. The thing she mouthed at you, on the scaffold day. Have you ever worked out what it was?"

Wren had. He'd worked it out around year nine, alone in a rented room in Greyharbor, staring at the ceiling, and it had broken something in him that he'd spent eleven years pretending was merely bruised.

"She said *thank you,*" he said. "For not looking away."

Torv nodded slowly, the way men nod at things too large for comment. Then he turned Bess south, and the hoofbeats faded into the fog, and Hunter Wren walked back through the empty garrison to write the report that would end his life.

* * *

He wrote it well. That was the bitter joke of the whole affair—he wrote his own ruin with the same care he'd given twenty years of flawless service. *Prisoner removed by persons unknown during the fourth bell. Guard rotation compromised. No pursuit undertaken; trail cold by dawn. The fault is mine.* He signed it, dated it, and left it square in the center of his desk where even the dullest clerk could not miss it.

Then he sat by the window with the last of his whiskey and watched the sky go grey over the rooftops, and he thought about Maren, and about a girl in Ashvale who spoke to goats, and about all the kettles in all the kitchens in the world that talked to lonely children while their brothers pretended not to hear.

The knock came at sunrise. Of course it did. Aldric's men were punctual.

Wren finished the whiskey, set the glass down neatly, and went to open the door himself, because after twenty years of hunting other men, it seemed only courteous to save them the trouble.

Behind him, far to the west, past the fens and the river and the drowned roads of a kingdom the maps had forgotten, a gray mare was carrying a cartographer toward the sea—and somewhere beyond the sea, on salt flats white as bone, a woman with a voice like thunder was learning the names of beasts.

Wren smiled at the soldiers on his doorstep.

"Well," he said. "Come in, gentlemen. Let's not waste the morning."
