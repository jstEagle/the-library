## 16. The Army of Horn and Wing

The marsh ended the way all things in old Sablemark seemed to end: gradually, and then all at once.

One moment Emrys was ankle-deep in brackish water with her boots squelching a complaint she could hear perfectly well without anyone translating; the next, the ground rose beneath her feet in a long, patient swell of salt-grass and stone, and the horizon opened like a book that had been shut for twenty years. Beyond the marshes lay a plain so wide it made the sky look small. And the plain was full.

Not full of men. Full of *everything else*.

Wolves stood in ranks along a ridge to the east—not the ragged, hungry packs she'd imagined from fireside stories, but wolves arranged with the grim orderliness of soldiers who had once been drilled by someone very good at drilling them, and had never quite forgotten how. Marsh-elephants waded out of the shallows on the west, gray as drowned cathedrals, water streaming off their tusks in silver ropes. Overhead wheeled the falcon clans—hundreds of them, maybe thousands, their shadows flickering across the grass like a language written in moving ink.

And everywhere, everywhere, the voices.

They came to her not as sound, exactly. Sound would have been simpler. They came as pressure, as tide, as the feeling of standing in a crowded market where every stall-keeper was shouting about her at once. She caught fragments: *the heir of voices*, *small*, *is that all there is?*, *she smells of fog and goats*, *I heard she opened the Door*, *the Door is closed, fool, she IS the Door now*—

"Emrys." Torv's voice, carefully casual, the voice of a man standing very still because he has calculated exactly which movements frighten elephants. "Emrys, I want you to know that I am handling this extremely well."

"You're gripping my sleeve hard enough to cut off circulation."

"That *is* me handling it."

She laughed—a short, startled bark of laughter—and felt several hundred wolf ears swivel toward the sound. Brannoc lifted off her shoulder, circled once, and rose high against the pale morning sky, and when he cried out, the whole plain answered him. The sound rolled over her like weather. Like the sea deciding, after long consideration, to speak.

"They've been gathering since the Door opened," Torv said quietly. "Word travels fast among the clans. Faster than any rider, faster than any beacon. The falcons carried it north before we'd even crossed the second causeway." He hesitated. "There are stories about what happens next, Emrys. Old ones. About the last time Sablemark's beasts marched under a human banner."

"I know the stories. Everyone knows the stories. That's rather the problem."

Below them, at the foot of the ridge, the grass parted around a single figure. A wolf came forward alone—enormous, gray-shot with white at the muzzle, walking with the deliberate unhurried gait of someone who has never once needed to hurry in her life. She stopped perhaps fifty paces away, sat down with the composure of a magistrate, and looked up at Emrys.

*I am Vesska,* said the pressure of her presence, calm and vast as deep water. *I was whelped in the reign of the last queen. I remember the burning of the capital. I remember what your kind asked of us, and what we gave, and how we were repaid.*

"I know," Emrys said aloud, because it felt wrong to answer a creature like this in anything less than a voice. "Brannoc told me some of it. The rest—the beasts along the drowned road told me more."

*Words are cheap,* Vesska said. *Every human who ever commanded us spoke beautifully.* Around the plain, low rumbles of agreement moved through the wolf ranks like wind through wheat. *You come asking us to die for your cities. Your cities that forgot us existed. Your kings who wrote us out of their maps and then wrote us into their wars. Why should the horn and wing rise for the hand that held the whip?*

It was, Emrys reflected, a fair question. It was possibly the fairest question anyone had ever asked her.

She looked out over the assembled host—the wolves, the elephants standing hip-deep and patient, the falcon clans wheeling in slow concentric rings overhead, and beyond them, at the edges of her sight, shapes she couldn't name: great elk with antlers like winter trees, something long and sinuous moving through the distant channels, a herd of shaggy cattle-beasts watching her with eyes old and unamused. An army. Her army, if she claimed them. If she could claim them.

Her mother's face floated up in her memory—warm, blurred at the edges, humming an old song while kneading bread—and Emrys thought: *she'd have known what to say.* Her mother could talk a tax collector out of a bad mood. Her mother had talked a goat named Bartholomew out of eating an entire hat.

But her mother wasn't here. And the truth was that Emrys didn't know what to say. She only knew what was true, and lately she'd learned that the two things weren't always enemies.

"I'm not going to command you," she said.

Silence. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

Vesska's ear twitched. *No?*

"No. I can't. Not because I wouldn't—I mean, obviously if I could snap my fingers and have you all march off nobly to save everyone, some tired, frightened part of me would be tempted, because I'm human and we're terrible—but I *can't*. That's the honest answer. I don't have that power. What I have is a voice. I can ask. I can tell you the truth. And then you'll do whatever you were always going to do, because you're not mine, and you never were, and the last people who pretended otherwise burned a kingdom doing it."

A ripple went through the plain. Not agreement, exactly. Something more cautious. Interest.

*Pretty words,* said a new voice, deeper, rumbling up from the shallows—an elephant, old and scarred, one tusk broken to a blunt stump. *We have heard pretty words. We have DIED for pretty words. What proof do you bring, little heir? How do we know you will not use us and forget us, as they used us and forgot us?*

Emrys opened her mouth.

Closed it.

Because the awful, humbling truth was that she didn't have proof. There was no token, no bloodline, no royal seal sewn into her coat. She was a goatherd from a valley that smelled like manure and fog, and she had walked into the middle of the oldest assembly in the world with nothing but a voice and a falcon and a cartographer who was currently whispering "don't say anything stupid" under his breath with the desperate intensity of a prayer.

"I don't know," she said.

Torv made a small strangled noise beside her.

"I don't know how you can know," Emrys went on, louder, letting her voice carry the way she'd learned to carry it across a hillside to a stubborn nanny-goat. "Trust isn't the sort of thing you can prove in advance. If I could prove it, it wouldn't be trust, it would just be arithmetic. All I can do is stand here and be exactly what I am, which is nobody special, and tell you everything honestly, including the parts that make me look foolish, and let you decide. You're older than my whole species' habit of lying. If anyone can smell a false note, it's you."

*She says she is nobody special,* Vesska observed to the plain at large, and there was something in the great wolf's tone that might, in a human, have been amusement. *The last heir of voices rode to us on a white stag wearing a crown of river-gold.*

"I rode here on a mule called Duchess," Emrys said. "She bit Torv twice."

*Only twice?* said the old elephant. *He must be likable.*

"He's very likable," Emrys said, and Torv turned an interesting shade of pink and became intensely interested in the horizon. "That's actually part of the point. I'm going to tell you the truth now, all of it, and it starts with the fact that three weeks ago I had never left my valley, and my grandest ambition was to convince Bartholomew not to eat the hat again."

* * *

She told them everything.

She told them about Ashvale, the fog-bound valley where magic couldn't follow, and the childhood spent learning silence the way other children learned letters. She told them about the goats—the audience of her secret, the first creatures who ever heard her real voice—and she made them laugh, or whatever beasts did instead of laughing, which involved a lot of huffing and one elderly wolf somewhere on the eastern ridge making a noise suspiciously like a snort.

*Goats,* said Vesska gravely, *are the most dishonest creatures in creation. My sister was killed by a farmer's goat. It pushed her off a cliff.*

"Bartholomew once faked a limp for four days to avoid being milked," Emrys offered. "So. Yes. Deeply dishonest animals. But they listened to me. That's where it started."

She told them about the Gift-Hunt edict, and Wren, and the hedge-healer whose name she had learned only after watching him burn—Tomas, he'd been called, Tomas who cured fevers with nettle tea and had never hurt anyone in his life. She did not soften that part. She watched the wolves' ears flatten and the elephants' great heads lower and she let them be angry, because she was angry too, and pretending otherwise would have been the first false note.

She told them about the message meant for a dead queen. About the Door of Salt, and the toll, and—here her voice went quiet and strange even to herself—about the memory she'd paid. She told them she had given up her mother's face, and that she now carried a woman in her head whom she loved desperately and could no longer picture, and that she would do it again, because the beasts of Sablemark deserved to walk free in the world more than she deserved to keep one face among hundreds.

The plain went very still.

*You paid the toll yourself,* Vesska said at last. *You did not send another. You did not find a trick around it.*

"There was no trick. Torv checked. Believe me, he checked. He's a cartographer; finding loopholes is practically his religion."

"It's not my *religion*," Torv muttered. "It's a professional interest."

*The Door does not bargain,* said the old elephant slowly, wonderingly. *For twenty years we have circled that coast and felt it sealed like a wound. Many tried. None passed. And this small fog-child walks through by paying love, and stands here offering us nothing but honesty, and asks for nothing.*

"Not nothing," Emrys admitted. "I'm asking. I told you—I'm asking, not commanding. Aldric's army reaches the free cities within days. His soldiers burn what they can't rule. If they win, there will be no corner of this world left where a beast or a gifted child can live unafraid. I think you know that better than I do. You've been hiding longer than we have." She took a breath. "I'm asking you to fight beside the free cities. Not for them. Beside them—as allies, as equals, as peoples who were erased together and can refuse to be erased together. And if you say no—if you decide the risk is too great and the debt too old—then I will thank you for hearing me, and I will go back down that hill and stand in the battle line myself, on foot, with a pitchfork, and probably die there, and none of it will have been your fault."

*A pitchfork,* repeated Vesska.

"It's what I have. Torv has a crossbow he's not very good with."

"My crossbow skills are adequate," Torv protested.

"Torv, you shot Duchess."

"The mule was *startled*—"

The plain erupted. Not in violence—in something else. The wolves threw back their heads and the sound that came out of them was huge and wild and unmistakably, gloriously *laughter*, rolling across the ridge and down into the shallows where the elephants trumpeted until the water shook and above it all the falcon clans screamed their own high delight, wheeling and diving, and Brannoc looped joyfully through the middle of it like a spark through smoke.

*Ohhh,* said Vesska, when the noise had settled to a rumble. *Ohh, little heir. Twenty years we have waited in these marshes, growing old, remembering glory, and the Door opens at last and sends us... a goatherd with a pitchfork and a man who shoots mules.*

"To be fair," Emrys said, "Duchess started it."

*Duchess did not start it,* said a new voice from behind her, dry and deeply offended, and Emrys turned to find the mule in question picking her way regally down the slope, having apparently decided to attend the historic council after all. *The man pointed a weapon at me. I merely expressed my opinion of it. Also, I have been listening to this entire proceedings, and I wish it noted for the record that I am the only member of this company who has never lost a war.*

"She has a point," Emrys said solemnly.

*She is a mule,* said Vesska.

*She is a mule with an unblemished military record,* said Duchess, *which is more than anyone present can claim, present company very much included, gray-muzzle.*

And that—that absurd, undignified, perfect moment—was when Emrys felt it change. She couldn't have said what shifted. Perhaps nothing visible at all. But the pressure of all those ancient minds upon her changed its weight, from interrogation to something almost like curiosity, almost like the way the goats used to crowd close on cold evenings, not because she fed them but because she was warm.

The old elephant waded closer, close enough that she could see the scars laddering his flank, each one a story from a war she'd only heard in whispers. *When we marched for the last queen,* he rumbled, *we marched because we were commanded, and because we loved her, and the commanding and the loving got tangled together until we could no longer tell which was which. That is how they used us, little heir. Not with chains. With love, badly handled.* His small bright eye regarded her. *You offer us the chance to march untangled. To choose. No human has offered that before.*

"I'm not offering it," Emrys said. "It's yours whether you march or not. Choosing is the whole gift."

Vesska rose from her haunches. She walked forward, past the invisible boundary of fifty paces, and sat down directly in front of Emrys, near enough that Emrys could count the silver hairs at her muzzle, near enough to feel the heat rising off her fur like a hearth.

*Then hear our choosing,* the wolf said. *We will come. Not because you asked beautifully—you asked clumsily, child, and thanked a mule in the middle of a war council—but because in twenty years you are the first human to stand before us empty-handed and say 'I cannot prove it.' The liars always had proof. Proof was their favorite weapon.* She tilted her great head. *We will come. On one condition.*

Emrys's stomach dropped. Here it was. The price. There was always a price. "Name it."

*When the fighting is done—win or lose—you will stand before the free cities and their councils and their fine trembling magistrates, and you will tell them what you told us. That we are peoples, not tools. That the erasure ends. You will spend your loud human voice on us, heir of voices, for as long as it takes, which may be the rest of your life.* Vesska's eyes glinted. *Is your pitchfork-arm strong enough for paperwork?*

Emrys thought of Tomas burning. Of her mother's faceless warmth in her memory. Of a lighthouse she hadn't built yet on a coast she hadn't seen, and children she hadn't taught.

"Yes," she said. "That I can promise."

*Then it is done.* Vesska turned her head toward the plain and loosed a single howl, and the whole world answered it—horn and wing and thunder of hooves, ten thousand voices rising off the marsh in a chord that Emrys felt in her teeth, her bones, the hollow place where her mother's face used to be. The Army of Horn and Wing, moving at dawn.

Brannoc landed on her shoulder, breathing hard with triumph. *Well,* he said. *That went well.*

"You think?"

*You thanked a mule in front of the oldest assembly in the world and they joined us anyway. In falcon terms this is considered excellent diplomacy.*

Torv let out a breath he'd apparently been holding since the ridge, sagged against a fence post that had no business existing in the middle of a salt marsh, and looked out at the assembling host with the expression of a man recalculating every map he'd ever drawn. "Emrys," he said faintly. "There are... a great many of them. And they appear to be organizing by clan. And the falcons are arguing about formation. Loudly."

"Can't you map them or something?"

"I map *land*, Emrys, I don't map—" He stopped. Blinked. Went slightly gray. "Emrys. The scouts. The falcon scouts just came in from the east." He turned to her, and all the color had gone out of his face. "Aldric's vanguard isn't marching on the free cities anymore."

"What do you mean? Where—"

"He's turned south. Toward the marshes." Torv swallowed. "Somebody told him where the Door was. Somebody told him *we* were here—and he means to close it forever, with us on the wrong side."

Out on the plain, the horns began.
