## 17. Aldric's Answer

The banners of Aldric came up the Rivergate road in the grey hour before dawn, and they did not stop coming.

Maren Ostell, First Speaker of the Free Cities Council, watched from the council balcony with her hands folded so no one would see them shake. She had counted armies before—theoretically, on maps, over wine—but counting was one thing and watching was another. The column filled the road like water filling a channel. It filled the fields beside the road. It filled the horizon until she stopped counting altogether.

"They brought siege towers," said Speaker Vess of Tarrowmoot, who had a gift for stating the obvious with the air of a man delivering devastating wit. "Three of them. On *boats*. Who puts a siege tower on a boat?"

"A man who intends to use it," Maren said.

"Well, yes, obviously, but—" Vess subsided.

Below them, the army halted in perfect silence. That was the worst part, Maren thought. Armies were supposed to be loud—a sea of shouting men, drums, the braying of mules. Something you could hate. This one stopped as if a hand had closed over it, ten thousand soldiers going still at once, and waited.

A single rider came forward under a white banner.

"He wants to parley," Vess said, brightening. "Good. Parley is good. Parley means nobody burns anything today."

"Parley means he thinks we're already beaten," Maren said, "and wants to enjoy saying so." She pulled her chain of office straight. "Come along, Vess. Let's go find out what mercy costs these days."

* * *

King Aldric received them in a pavilion that was either deliberately austere or simply too new to have accumulated any comfort—a table, three chairs, a map of the coast weighted down with daggers. He stood when they entered, which surprised her. She had expected him to remain seated. Tyrants in the stories always remained seated.

He was younger than she'd imagined. Forty, perhaps, with grey coming into his beard early and tiredness carved deep around his eyes. He looked less like a conqueror than a man who had been awake for twenty years and saw no end to it.

"First Speaker," he said. His voice was quiet, and the quietness made everyone lean in to hear it, which Maren suspected was the point. "Thank you for coming. Please sit."

"We'd rather stand," Vess said.

"Then stand," Aldric agreed pleasantly, and sat himself. "I'll be brief, because I know what you're thinking. You're thinking: every hour this man talks is an hour my walls hold. So." He spread his hands. "Here is my answer to your cities, and I will give it once."

Maren had prepared herself for many things. A list of demands. Humiliations. Hostages. What she had not prepared herself for was that Aldric began by pouring wine for all three of them with his own hands, like an innkeeper.

"You believe I am here for conquest," he said. "You've told yourselves a story about me. A hungry king, an ambitious man, a crown that wants more crowns. I'm not going to insult you by pretending otherwise—I can't stop you believing it. But I want you to understand what you're actually refusing, because you deserve to refuse it knowingly."

He turned the map toward them. The drowned coast. The salt marshes beyond Rivergate. And there, inked in a careful antique hand, a shape Maren had only ever seen in fragments: the old borders of Sablemark.

"Do you know why your grandmothers' maps have a hole in them?" Aldric asked. "Why there's a stretch of coast no chart will name? Not because the sea took it. Because the crown paid to have it forgotten. Every map burned. Every record sealed. Every family sworn to silence on pain of—" he paused, "—well. You've seen my edicts. You know what pain of means."

"The Catastrophe," Vess said. "Everyone knows the story. Magic ran wild, the land drowned, the kingdom fell."

"Everyone knows the story," Aldric repeated softly. "And everyone has it wrong, or rather, everyone has it exactly as my grandfather wanted it told. Let me tell you how it actually went, since I'm the last man alive who was taught the truth."

He drank, set down the cup, and for a moment the exhaustion in his face opened like a door.

"Sablemark didn't fall because magic ran wild. It fell because magic was *everywhere*, and beloved, and trusted, and utterly without restraint. Beast-speakers commanded the marsh-elephants to build seawalls, and the elephants built them, and no one asked whether the beasts wished to. Storm-callers sold good weather to one village and took payment in the bad weather they sent next door. Gifted children were taken from their families to the Tower of Voices and trained, and if their gifts turned strange—if they heard things men shouldn't hear, or wanted things children shouldn't want—no one could say so aloud, because who argues with a miracle?" He smiled, and it was the coldest thing Maren had ever seen on a warm face. "My mother was a storm-caller. My father was a beast-speaker of the old coastal clans. They met at court. They married. They had two sons."

The pavilion was very quiet. Somewhere outside, a soldier coughed.

"When Sablemark broke," Aldric said, "it broke in a night. The Door of Salt—the great working, the one your histories don't even name—was torn open from the wrong side, and everything the gifted had spent centuries calling up came through it or fled through it, and the land between drowned in a single tide. My father held our gate open while the water rose. He was speaking to the horses. Calming them. That was the last thing anyone saw him do—his mouth moving, the water at his chest, talking kindly to animals that were going to die anyway." He turned his cup a quarter-turn, precisely, as though aligning it with something invisible. "I was nine years old. My brother was six. We climbed the tower at Carrow's Rest and watched the country drown, and my mother flew above the flood on a wind she had called herself, trying to turn the storm back, and the storm took her instead. I watched her light go out. From the tower. At nine years old, I understood two things completely: that magic is real, and that magic does not love you back."

Vess, remarkably, had stopped performing. Maren found, to her own surprise, that her eyes were burning. She hated him a little for that—for making her grieve for him, for building sympathy out of a drowned boy the way other men built siege towers.

"I am sorry," Maren said, and meant it. "No child should see that. No person should carry it."

"Thank you," Aldric said, and inclined his head, and for one unguarded moment he looked almost grateful, and Maren thought—*here, here is the seam, here is where mercy might enter*—

"So you understand," he went on, "when I say what I say next, that I have thought about it for thirty years. I did not wake up cruel one morning. I built this edict stone by stone, the way my father's people built seawalls, because I have seen the flood." He stood. "Your cities harbor the gifted. You know you do. The hedge-healers, the charm-sellers, the ones who whisper to your oxen and your hens. Under my edict they are to be surrendered to the crown for testing. All of them. Every city opens its gates, every registry is handed over, every gifted soul comes north—and in exchange, your walls stand, your people live, and no tower burns. Refuse, and I take the cities. And I want to be honest with you about what taking them means, because I promised myself I would never lie about it the way my grandfather lied. If you refuse, I will burn Rivergate, Tarrowmoot, and Saltmere to their foundations, and I will begin tonight."

The silence afterward had weight, like deep water.

"You'd slaughter thousands," Maren said, "to reach a few hundred frightened healers."

"I watched thousands drown," Aldric said, "because a few hundred gifted people believed their power was a blessing instead of a fire. I am not slaughtering thousands. I am choosing which thousands. That is the only choice I have been given since I was nine years old, and I have made it, and I will make it again tomorrow, and the day after, until there is no unwatched magic left in the world to open any door." He gathered his map, his daggers, his terrible tidy grief. "You have until sundown, First Speaker. I'll leave the wine."

At the tent flap Maren turned back. "There's a story reaching us out of the east," she said. "About a woman who speaks to beasts. About an army gathering beyond the marshes. Horn and wing, they say. Does that change anything?"

Something crossed Aldric's face—not fear. Recognition. Like a man hearing, thirty years later, the first far-off sound of the tide that took everything.

"No," he said quietly. "It confirms everything. Sundown, First Speaker."

* * *

In the soldiers' camp, the talk was of supper and nothing else, because Sergeant Dannick Hobb had learned long ago that armies march on rumors and starve on them too.

"Stew again," said young Pell, poking his bowl with the resignation of a philosopher. "Day four of stew. I joined for glory, you know."

"You joined because your father hit you with a shovel," said Dannick.

"Glory-adjacent," Pell admitted.

They were sitting on the wheel of a supply cart within earshot of the command tents, which was against regulations, but Dannick had decided years ago that regulations were for men whose officers didn't cry. Because that was the thing nobody put in songs: the King wept. Not publicly—never publicly—but Dannick had delivered reports at night, twice, three times, and each time the King had turned away to the map table and his shoulders had moved, and each time he'd turned back with his voice level and given orders that made Dannick's stomach hurt.

Tonight the orders had made it hurt worse.

"Burn the cities," Pell said, keeping his voice low, the way everyone in camp said it now, *burn the cities*, like a weather report. "That's really happening? There's folk in Rivergate. Bakers. Kids."

"There's folk everywhere," Dannick said. "That's generally what cities are."

"My nan lives near Saltmere."

"Then pray she owns a fast mule."

Pell was quiet a moment, spooning stew. "Sarge. Can I ask you something and you won't box my ears?"

"Given our history, no promises."

"Back home, when the criers read the edict—there was a woman in our village, Widow Crane, who could make a fever break just holding your hand. Didn't charge. Made soup while she was at it. Everyone knew. Nobody said a word, whole village, years." He stared into the bowl. "Then the hunters came, and everyone said a word. All at once. Like a dam breaking. And they took her north for testing and nobody ever—" He stopped. "Was that right, Sarge? Was that the flood the King talks about? Her hands? The soup?"

Dannick looked out over the camp, at the watch fires, at the silent ranks, at the pavilion where a man who talked kindly to memory was preparing to do the opposite of kind.

"No," he said. "But asking that question is above both our pay grades, lad, so eat your stew."

Pell ate his stew. But later, when the horns sounded the evening watch, he was not the only soldier in ten thousand who looked east, toward the marshes, toward whatever was gathering out there in the dark—and felt something in his chest that was not quite fear, and not quite hope, and would not sit still.

* * *

Hunter Wren heard the order given from outside the command tent, because Aldric had stopped caring who heard it.

"Fire the trebuchets at moonrise," the King was saying to his captains. "Rivergate first. Then Tarrowmoot. Saltmere can watch its sisters burn and reconsider by morning."

Wren stood among the couriers and aides with his hood back and his hands loose at his sides, and inside his chest, something that had been cracking for weeks finally went through.

He had turned in his own sister. He had walked her to the testers himself, eleven years old and certain, *certain*, that law was the same thing as right. He had built his whole life on that certainty the way a house is built on foundations, and now the King—the King whose grief Wren understood better than anyone alive, because Wren had his own small drowned country behind his eyes—was going to feed bakers and children and probably somebody's nan into the fire, and call it the only choice.

*The only choice.* Wren had used those words himself, handing over Liss. He knew exactly how much comfort they bought, and exactly how little they were worth.

"Master Wren." A captain beckoned. "The King wants your hounds on the east road. The marsh track. If the beast-speaker woman tries to come west with her creatures, you're to find her first."

"And if I do?" Wren asked.

"Take her alive. The King wants to test her personally."

Wren bowed. "Alive," he said. "Understood."

He walked back through the camp slowly, past the trebuchets being assembled in the dark, past soldiers' fires where men spoke low, past a young soldier who looked up at him with wet angry eyes and then away. Three days, Wren thought. He had bought them three days with his false report, and the three days had run out like sand, and somewhere east of here a woman with a broken cartographer and a falcon was doing something with the Door of Salt that either meant nothing at all—or everything.

His sister's name sat behind his teeth, where he kept it. *Liss.*

"East road," he told his hounds, and turned their leads away from the city, away from the fires being planned, out toward the marshes and the dark.

Behind him, the first torch touched the first bundle of pitch-soaked faggots beneath the first trebuchet, and the flame stood up straight in the windless night like a thing listening.

Moonrise was four hours off. Wren rode hard, and did not look back.
