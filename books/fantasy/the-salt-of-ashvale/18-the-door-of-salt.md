## 18. The Door of Salt

The coast arrived before we did.

I felt it in my teeth first—a thin ringing, like a wet finger circling the rim of a cup. Torv felt it too. I knew because he stopped mid-stride on the shingle and pressed his palm flat against his jaw, the way a man does when he suspects his own body of keeping secrets from him.

"You hear it," he said. Not a question.

"More like it hears me."

"That's worse." He said it lightly, but his eyes weren't light at all. They hadn't been since Rivergate, since the bolt meant for me had gone through him instead, since Brannoc had carried our message east into the marshes while I rode with Torv's blood soaking my sleeves and a promise in my throat I couldn't finish. Three weeks of healing salves and stubbornness and arguments about who should carry the heavier pack, and the wound had closed to a pale seam across his ribs that he touched now like a man touching an old scar instead of a new one.

"The map's humming," he said, pulling the blank vellum from its oilcloth. It was still blank to any eye but mine—had been blank for two hundred years, waiting for someone who spoke beast-tongue to wake it. Now the ink moved beneath the surface like fish under ice, lines gathering and dissolving, and at the center of it all a single point pulsed where no coastline had ever been drawn.

Because there was no coastline. That was the whole trick of Sablemark. When the old kingdom fell, the victors hadn't just burned it—they'd unmade it, pulled it off the maps and out of the songs, drowned its roads under the tide flats and salted its name out of every mouth. The Door of Salt wasn't hidden. It was *forgotten*, deliberately, thoroughly, by everyone except a family of cartographers who were disgraced for remembering.

Torv's family. Who had paid for that remembering with everything but the map itself.

"It's close," he said. "A day, maybe less."

"Then let's not spend the night arguing over whose fault the fog is."

"There's no fog here," he said, and stopped walking, and I understood what my teeth had been telling me all along.

There was no fog. There had been no fog since dawn. The mist that had wrapped us since Ashvale—the mist that had wrapped my whole life—simply ended behind us, hanging in the valley passes like laundry forgotten on a line, and ahead lay open country: grey shingle, dun grass, wind coming off the sea with nothing between it and us. Magic lived in this place. I could feel it the way you feel sun through a window, warm and enormous and utterly indifferent to whether you deserved it.

I had crossed the fog-line once already, weeks ago, and thought then that I would never get used to it. I hadn't. Every hour outside the valley was still a small astonishment. In Ashvale, magic could not follow; here, it followed everywhere, pooled in the hollows, ran bright in the streams. My gift, which I had spent twenty-two years strangling into silence, sat in my chest like a second heartbeat, loud and unashamed.

Brannoc wheeled overhead and screamed down something in falcon that translated roughly to *hurry up, slow-walking mammals*, which is the trouble with befriending birds. They have no tact whatsoever.

"We're hurrying," I told him.

"You're talking to the bird again," said Torv.

"I'm consulting my scout."

"Your scout wants a rabbit."

"Your scout," said Brannoc, landing on my shoulder with his usual delicacy—that is, claws first, apology later—"wants two rabbits."

* * *

We made camp that night in the lee of a drowned wall. You saw them everywhere as the land tilted toward the sea: walls and gateposts and the stumps of chimneys rising from the turf like bones, all that remained above water of the roads that ran beneath the tide flats. Old Sablemark. Somewhere under our fire, people had baked bread and argued and buried their dead, and then the world had decided to forget them, and the forgetting had been so complete that even the grass grew without guilt.

Torv cooked. Torv always cooked when he was nervous, and tonight he was nervous enough to make three meals' worth of rabbit stew, which Brannoc considered the finest development in human history.

"Eat," Torv said, pushing a bowl at me. "Tomorrow you'll want your strength."

"For opening a door?"

"For paying for one." He stirred his stew and didn't look at me. "My grandfather wrote about it. What's left of his notes. The Door doesn't open for strength or blood or cleverness. It opens for a price."

"I know. You've told me."

"A true memory. One, freely given, whole. And Emrys—" he finally looked up, and the firelight did him no favors, showing me every sleepless night he'd spent since Rivergate doing this same arithmetic, "—you don't get to choose carelessly. Whatever you give it, you lose completely. Not dulled. Gone. As if it never happened to you. Everyone around you will remember it, and you will look at their faces and see strangers grieving something you cannot find."

I put down my spoon.

"And you know this how?"

"Because my grandmother gave hers." His voice went very quiet. "She opened the Door once, when she was young, to smuggle refugees out during the purges. She paid with the memory of her wedding day. She had been married forty years, Emrys. She loved my grandfather until the day she died. But she never remembered loving him. She used to watch him across the table and ask my mother, very politely, why that sad old man kept looking at her like that."

The fire cracked. Out on the dark flats, the tide breathed in and out against the drowned road, patient as a sleeping animal.

"So we know it can be done," I said. "And we know what it costs."

"Yes."

"And you're worried about what I'll choose."

"I'm terrified about what you'll choose," Torv said, "because I know you, and I know that whatever you pick, you'll pick the thing that hurts least for everyone else and most for you, and you'll do it smiling, because that's how you do everything, and I will have to stand there and remember for both of us—"

"Torv."

"—and I am telling you now, before, while you're still you—if you give up anything that has me in it, I will—"

"*Torv.*"

He stopped. His hands were shaking around the bowl. I reached over and took it from him, set it down, and took his hands instead, which were cold, which were always cold, which had been cold the first time he grabbed mine running through the streets of Rivergate with informers shouting behind us, and had been warm exactly twice since: once when he confessed he loved me, badly, in blood, and once last week, when I told him I'd heard him the first time.

"Listen to me," I said. "Whatever happens tomorrow—I'm not giving up you. I spent twenty-two years practicing loss, Torv. I lost my mother, I lost my valley, I nearly lost you. I'm done rehearsing. Tomorrow I pay the Door whatever it asks, but it doesn't get the things I've bled for. It gets something else. Something I can bear to set down."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then, because he was Torv, he said: "You realize that's exactly the kind of sentence that sounds brave now and costs everything later."

"I realize."

"Good. Just so we understand each other." And he squeezed my hands back, and neither of us slept much after that.

* * *

We found it at noon.

Not on the shore—the shore was wrong, the maps agreed on nothing there—but out on the flats themselves, past the last of the standing stones, where the drowned causeway of old Sablemark rose just far enough above the sand to walk. The tide was out, the sea drawn back half a mile like a held breath, and at the end of the ancient road stood a doorway with no wall around it.

That's the only way I can say it. A doorframe, tall as two men, carved from grey stone furred with salt-bloom, standing alone on the causeway with nothing behind it but sky and shining sand. No building. No ruin. Just the door, and the air inside it moving differently than the air around it—a faint shimmer, like heat-haze in winter, like the surface of a pond seen from below.

Beasts had gathered. Of course they had. Word travels fast when the messengers have wings. Wolves sat at the edges of the causeway with the patience of judges. Marsh-elephants stood hip-deep in the channels, grey as the stone, watching with small wise eyes. Falcons ringed the sky above the doorframe in a slow wheel, and among them Brannoc descended to my shoulder, and his weight there steadied me more than I can say.

An old wolf came forward—old the way mountains are old, her muzzle gone silver, her gait stiff—and looked at me for a long time.

"Heir of voices," she said. Her speech was slow, formal, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "You come to open what men closed."

"I come to go through it," I said. "To bring word to those beyond, and bring them back if they'll come. The cities are burning. Aldric's army marches. We need—" I stopped myself. I had learned this lesson in the marshes, kneeling in front of creatures who remembered being ridden as weapons. "I need help. I won't dress it up as anything nobler than that."

The wolf's ears tipped. Something almost like approval.

"Honesty," she said. "Rare, in your kind. The Door will take its toll from you, heir. Do you know this?"

"I know."

"Then say your farewells first. The Door does not wait, and memory does not negotiate."

* * *

Here is the thing nobody tells you about choosing which memory to surrender: you stand there, and you inventory yourself, and every single thing you own turns out to be load-bearing.

I walked away from the doorframe a little way, down the causeway, and Torv gave me the distance without being asked, because he understood. The tide whispered in the channels. Brannoc watched from a gatepost with his head cocked, and for once said nothing, because even falcons know when a creature is doing hard arithmetic.

My mother. I went to her first, because I had to. My mother teaching me goat-song in the barn with the door barred, her voice low, her hands warm on my shoulders. My mother's face—grey-eyed, sharp-chinned, quick to laugh and quicker to worry. My mother dying in the fever winter, holding my hand and making me promise, *never aloud, never where anyone can hear, promise me, little wren.*

If I gave up my mother entirely, I gave up the promise. I gave up the reason for everything. I became a woman who had hidden her whole life for no cause she could name. The Door might as well take my spine while it was at it.

But if I gave up only her face—

The thought arrived quietly, the way important thoughts do, dressed in ordinary clothes.

Her face. Not her voice. Not her hands. Not the barn, not the goats, not the fever winter or the promise or the love. Only the face. I would keep the woman and lose the picture of her. I would keep everything she taught me and forget what she looked like teaching it. Twenty years from now, I would tell children about my mother and reach for her face and find smooth water where a face should be, and I would grieve her fresh every time, and never know why.

It was unbearable.

I stood there and held it up against the alternative—giving up Torv, giving up the fog-line crossing when he caught my hand, giving up the bad confession and the good one, the stew, the arguments, the scar I'd cleaned and re-bandaged a hundred times—and found that it wasn't unbearable at all next to that. It was merely terrible. Terrible was a thing I could carry. I had been carrying terrible since I was nine years old.

That was the trick of it, I realized. The Door didn't ask for what hurt most. It asked for a true memory, freely given, whole—and the cruelest mercy in the world is that you get to keep everything else, including the knowledge of what you gave. Grandmother Kavanagh had known that. She had walked past her husband's grave for thirty years knowing exactly what she'd lost and feeling none of it.

I turned and walked back up the causeway. Torv was waiting where I'd left him, arms folded against the sea wind, trying to look calm and failing in the specific ways I had memorized: the jaw set too tight, the thumb worrying the edge of his cuff, the eyes that found mine and stayed.

"Well?" he said.

"I've chosen."

"Do you want to tell me?"

"No," I said honestly. "Because you'll argue."

"I'll argue."

"And I'd win."

"You'd win," he admitted, and something in his mouth almost became a smile. Then it didn't. "Emrys. If it takes something of mine too—if losing it changes how I look at you—you'll know it isn't real, what's left. Remember that. Whatever's left is the truest part."

"Come here," I said, and kissed him, salt wind and all, in front of wolves and elephants and forty falcons who absolutely had opinions and mercifully kept them to themselves.

* * *

The doorframe hummed when I stepped close. Up close I could see the carving: interlaced beasts, wolf and eagle and serpent, worn nearly smooth, and words in old Sablemark script that the blank map's ink rose eagerly to read aloud inside my head:

*A door remembers what men forget. Pay it truth, and pass.*

I put my hand flat against the stone. It was warm. Everything else out here was cold, and the stone was warm as a living thing.

"What do you want?" I whispered.

The shimmer in the doorway thickened. And the Door answered—not in sound, exactly, but in showing. It reached into me gently, terribly gently, the way you'd lift an egg from a nest, and it turned my life over and looked at it, all of it, the goats and the fog and the crow on market day and Wren's cold eyes and the hedge-healer falling and Torv bleeding and my mother's voice singing low in the barred barn—

and it paused, on that last one, the way a merchant pauses over the coin he likes best.

"No," I said aloud. My voice cracked on it. "No. Not that. Not all of that."

The Door waited. Patient as the tide.

I closed my eyes. I gathered it up carefully, the way Torv had described his grandmother must have done: one memory, whole, entire. My mother's face. All of it—the grey eyes, the sharp chin, the laugh-lines, the way her brow folded when she worried, her face in lamplight, her face in the last grey light of the fever winter. I lifted it out of myself like a stone from a riverbed, and I felt the cold rush in around the space it left, and I offered it up with both hands, the way you offer anything you cannot keep.

"This," I said. "Take my mother's face. Let me keep her voice. Let me keep her hands. Let me keep the promise. Take only the picture of her."

For one moment—one long, salt-slow moment—nothing happened. And I understood, with a horror that will stay with me longer than her face would have, that the Door was considering refusing. That it wanted the whole woman. That bargains with doors are not bargains at all unless the door is generous.

Then the warmth of the stone deepened under my palm, almost like a hand closing over mine, almost like pity, and the Door accepted.

I felt it go.

There is no describing it well. It was not pain. It was subtraction. A page lifting cleanly out of a book. My mother's face—gone. I stood on the causeway of a drowned kingdom with tears running down my face and searched for her face and found, where it had always lived, a soft grey absence shaped exactly like a face, and around it, intact, untouched, her voice: *never aloud, never where anyone can hear, promise me, little wren.*

I promised, Mama. I don't remember your face anymore. I remember everything else.

The shimmer in the doorway parted like a curtain of rain.

Behind it—not sky, not sand. Green country going gold in a low sun, hills rolling away into distances that smelled of thyme and woodsmoke and old, old magic. And figures standing in the light, waiting: tall shapes and small ones, some with horns and some with wings, faces I knew from the beasts at my back and faces no one in this world had seen in two hundred years.

They had been waiting a long time. They were looking at me.

"Emrys," Torv said softly, beside me. He had come up without my noticing. His hand found mine, and I gripped it, because the ground ahead was unfamiliar and the ground behind was gone. "Is it open?"

I wiped my face with my free sleeve, took one breath, another, and stepped forward to the threshold of everything.

"It's open," I said. "And they know my mother's name."

Behind me, the gathered beasts began, very softly, to sing.
