## 19. The Battle of the Shallows

The tide was going out when the army came.

Emrys stood on the shingle ridge above the shallows and watched them appear out of the morning haze like a bruise spreading across the land—rank upon rank of Aldric's soldiery, pikes catching the grey light, siege wagons groaning over the wet sand, banners snapping in a wind that smelled of iron and salt. Ten thousand men, perhaps more. Against them: four thousand refugees, fishermen, dockhands, and free-city militia who had never held anything sharper than a filleting knife until last month.

"Cheerful odds," Torv said beside her. His arm was bound where the crossbow bolt had gone through, but he stood straight, and his ruined compass hung at his neck like a talisman that had finally decided what it wanted to point at. "I've seen worse."

"No, you haven't," Emrys said.

"No," he admitted. "But it's good for morale to lie."

She almost smiled. Almost. Her hands would not stop shaking, so she pressed them flat against her thighs and made herself look at the line below—the ragged line of people she had helped convince to stand here instead of kneeling. Old Marta from Rivergate with her kitchen spear. The twins from the fish market who were sixteen if they were fourteen. A one-eyed ferryman who had volunteered by shouting *someone has to* and then looking embarrassed about it for three days straight.

They had come because she had promised them beasts.

And the Door of Salt had opened two nights ago, and since then—nothing. Silence from the marshes beyond the erased coast. Only the falcon clans wheeling high overhead, watching, carrying no word back.

"They're coming," Torv said quietly. "Brannoc reached them. I know it in my bones."

"Your bones also told you the shortcut through the fen was solid."

"That was my knees, and they've always been traitors."

Below them, horns sounded—not Aldric's deep war-horns but the thinner, braver bleat of the free cities' signal trumpets, cracked and off-key. The ragged line locked shields, which is to say they locked pots and door-boards and one remarkable amount of furniture, and waited to die or not die.

Emrys closed her eyes and reached out with the voice she had spent her whole life strangling.

*Hold,* she said, into the ground, into the wind, into every listening ear for ten miles. *Hold, little brothers. Hold, sisters of the reeds. We are here. Come.*

The fog answered her with silence.

Then, far off, something answered too.

* * *

It began as a sound she felt in her teeth before she heard it—a low rolling thunder beneath the earth, wrong for hooves, too slow, too vast. The soldiers in the vanguard of Aldric's army stopped. Even at this distance Emrys could see their formation ripple, men craning toward the eastern marshes, officers screaming them back into place.

Torv grabbed her shoulder. "Emrys."

"I feel it."

"No—I mean *look.*"

She looked.

The marshes were moving.

Not flooding. Not burning. *Moving*—the whole drowned landscape of old Sablemark rising up in sections, water sheeting off gray flanks the size of cottages. Marsh-elephants. She had read of them in Torv's forbidden books and thought them half legend: tusked giants bred by the old kingdom to walk the sunken causeways, thought extinct these twenty years, thought *erased*, like everything else Sablemark had been. There were forty of them. Sixty. Behind them came wolves the color of wet slate, running not as packs but as battalions, and above it all the sky darkened and darkened as the falcon clans came down off the high thermals in a spiral that blotted out the morning.

"Oh," said Torv, very softly. "Oh, you beautiful, terrifying woman. It worked."

"It hasn't worked yet," Emrys said, because her heart was doing something complicated between terror and joy and there wasn't room in her chest for both, so they were sharing.

The first marsh-elephant hit Aldric's left flank like a hillside deciding to take a walk.

* * *

Afterward, Emrys would remember it all in fragments, the way a storm is remembered—one bright shard at a time.

A siege wagon lifted entire in a curving gray trunk and set down gently, almost politely, fifty feet from where it had been, its crew scrambling out the back and simply *running*. Wolves flowing through gaps in the pikelines like water finding cracks, not killing where they could disarm, nipping hamstrings and herding men into knots that could do nothing but stand together and shout. The falcons diving in shrieking spirals that broke formations before the wolves ever reached them—and Brannoc at their head, her wing healed strong, screaming a cry that Emrys understood perfectly because it was beast-speech pitched for war:

*For the heir of voices! For the Door!*

And Emrys rode among them.

She had been given a mare by the ferryman—an actual horse, which she had only ridden twice before and badly—but the mare seemed to understand that today was not a day for being difficult. Emrys came down the shingle slope with the second wave, and the moment her boots touched the flooded sand, she stopped hiding.

She had hidden for twenty-five years. She had spoken to goats in whispers behind closed barn doors. She had swallowed words until her throat ached and bitten them back until her jaw hurt and taught herself, syllable by syllable, to be silent in a world that would burn her for her voice.

She opened her mouth on the field of the Shallows, in front of four thousand frightened neighbors and ten thousand enemy soldiers, and she spoke.

"*Stand!*"

The word went out of her like light. She felt it leave—felt the gift blaze up her spine and out, felt it strike the battlefield the way a struck bell strikes the air. Every animal within earshot turned toward her. The wolves' heads swung as one. The elephants rumbled assent deep enough to shake the standing water. And the *people*—the ragged line of Rivergate and the dockhands and old Marta with her kitchen spear—they heard it too, not the words but the weight of them, and Emrys watched terror transfigure itself in real time, watched fear turn to fury, watched four thousand people decide in the same heartbeat that they were not going to kneel after all.

"*Hold the line, children of the cities!* she cried. *Your grandmothers did not build walls so you could hide behind your own fear! And you—*" and now she turned to the enemy, and her voice changed, softened, became the voice she used on frightened animals, "*you are not wicked, most of you, you are only led. Drop your pikes. Go home. Nobody here wants your blood—we want our country back.*"

Some of them dropped their pikes. Not many. But some, and each man who turned and walked away took three others with him, and the wolves let them go, herding only those who stood.

She rode through it all, visible, blazing, singing the beast-speech aloud like a woman who has spent her life underwater finally breaking the surface—and she wept while she shouted, and no one could tell the difference, and it didn't matter, because both were true.

* * *

By midmorning the tide had turned, literally and otherwise. Aldric's center still held—the crown's regulars were professionals, and professionals die hard—but the left had folded entirely and the right was dissolving into the marshes, pursued not by wolves but by their own panic. The siege train was a graveyard of abandoned engines sinking slowly into the flats.

Which was when Emrys saw him.

He was easy to find, even across the churned chaos of the battlefield: a figure in the king's black-and-gold riding at the head of the reserve, unhurried, immaculate, watching the ruin of his left flank with the stillness of a man doing arithmetic. King Aldric. She had never seen him before. She had expected a monster, and what she saw instead was a broad, tired-looking man in his fifties with a commander's posture and an expression she recognized, horribly, because she wore it herself sometimes in mirrors—the face of someone carrying a grief so old it had fossilized into purpose.

Their eyes met across the field. She would have sworn to it later: he looked directly at her, at the small blazing figure on horseback surrounded by wolves, and something in his face *broke*—not with hatred. With recognition. With an old, exhausted sorrow, as if he were seeing not her but something he had buried long ago, and it had climbed out of the grave wearing new flesh.

Then he raised his hand, and the reserve began to advance, and there was no more time for reading faces.

"Hold them here," Emrys told the slate-colored wolf pacing at her stirrup—a pack-mother, scarred down one side, who had taken to guarding Emrys's flank with proprietary ferocity. "Buy the line time. Don't follow past the stakes; their archers will have range."

The wolf huffed something unprintable about humans and their endless rules and flowed away.

Torv appeared at her side, leading his horse through the shallows, soaked to the waist and grinning like an idiot. "Did you see? Did you see the wagon?"

"I saw the wagon."

"*Gently.* It set it down gently. Marsh-elephants have manners, Emrys. Nobody tells you this."

"Torv." She caught his sleeve. "Aldric's reserve is coming, and it's fresh. Our people are tired. The beasts can break them, but—" She hesitated. Saying it aloud made it real. "If we push them to rout, Aldric dies or runs, and either way thousands more die in the mud. If we *break* them without breaking their hearts—"

"You want to win mercifully."

"I want to win *winningly*," she said. "Mercy is just tactics with better manners."

He laughed—actually laughed, on a battlefield, which was so perfectly Torv that she nearly kissed him then and there, and decided to save it for a moment when neither of them was bleeding. Probably. "So how does a shepherd girl rout a king gently?"

"She talks to his horses," said Emrys.

* * *

Warhorses, it turned out, were the most miserable creatures on any battlefield. She had known this abstractedly—goats gossiped, and even Ashvale goats had opinions about cavalry—but knowing and *hearing* were different things. When Emrys opened her gift to the enemy's mounts, she was nearly knocked backward by the sheer volume of their complaint. The noise. The smells. The dead men they'd carried. The screaming. Horses, she learned, hated war with a purity that would have embarrassed the poets.

*I know,* she told them, gentling her voice, spreading it along the enemy lines like oil on water. *I know, brave hearts. This is not your fight and never was. Your riders are afraid, and afraid men grip reins too tight. But the tide is turning, and the gate home is open, and no one needs to carry anyone else into the mud today.*

And the warhorse line—two thousand strong, the hammer of Aldric's reserve—shivered, stamped, and began, unit by unit, to refuse.

It was not mutiny exactly. Horses are too honest for mutiny. They simply stopped going forward. They backed. They sidled. They turned with the calm immovability of creatures who have decided, collectively and irrevocably, that the marsh is that way and they are going to stand near it now. Riders cursed and spurred and got nothing for it but flattened ears. Officers screamed. The reserve's perfect advance dissolved into a milling, squealing, immovable traffic jam of cavalry, and into that jam the wolves poured, disarming, herding, unhorsing men who mostly seemed relieved to be unhorsed.

Old Marta, fighting somewhere on the left, would later swear she saw a royal knight sit down in the shallows, pat his own horse on the neck, and say, "Fair enough."

By noon it was over. Not won—Emrys knew the difference. Aldric himself had cut free with his household guard and fallen back east along the coast road, unhurt, undefeated in his own mind, gathering stragglers as he went. But the assault on the Shallows was broken. The free cities held the field. The refugees held the field. The beasts of erased Sablemark stood ankle-deep in the tide among the men they had saved, steaming in the pale sun, and nobody quite knew what to do next except that everyone, somehow, started cheering.

Emrys sat her mare in the middle of it and let the sound wash over her—the human cheering, the elephant thunder, the falcon cries, the wolf-song rising eerie and glad—and felt something she had no name for. Terror transfigured. Fear burned down to ash and something green growing in it.

"You're crying again," Torv observed, reining up beside her.

"You're crying too."

"Yes, but mine are dignified."

* * *

The celebration lasted exactly as long as it took a falcon to spot the rider coming up the coast road under a white cloth on a broken lance.

It was one of Wren's scouts—or had been; Emrys hadn't seen Wren since the tide flats, since Torv's blood on the sand, since whatever happened in the city that Torv wouldn't fully explain and Wren had paid for with everything. The scout reined in at the edge of the camp, gray-faced, and delivered his message to anyone who would listen, which rapidly became everyone:

"The king has turned south. He's not retreating." The scout swallowed. "He's marching on the Shallows Gate. The old toll-house on the causeway—the one the beasts came through. He means to seal the Door of Salt. He's got fire-oil, sappers, the whole train. And he's ordered Hunter Wren to hold the gate against all comers until it burns."

Silence, of the kind that has a roar inside it.

Emrys looked at the marshes, where the Door of Salt stood open behind its veil of salt-fog—the wound in the world she had opened at the price she still couldn't quite count, the road home for every creature of erased Sablemark. If Aldric sealed it, they would be stranded here forever, exiles twice over. If Aldric *burned* it—

"He'll kill everything trying to come through," she said. "The Door doesn't close gently. Torv, how fast can the elephants march?"

"Faster than a king expects," Torv said grimly, already turning his horse. "Everyone always underestimates the pace of things that size. Cartographer's secret."

"And Wren?" Someone asked it—old Marta, maybe. "What about Wren holding the gate? Whose side is he on?"

Emrys thought of the hunter's cold eyes, and of the horse he'd given them, and of a sister's name whispered as a password, and found she was smiling in a way that scared her a little.

"Wren's side," she said, and put her heels to the mare. "Which means ours. Ride!"
