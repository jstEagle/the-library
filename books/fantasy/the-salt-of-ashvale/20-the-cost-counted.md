## 20. The Cost Counted

The tide came in over the Shallows carrying things it had no business carrying: a broken pike, half a banner with Aldric's sun stamped on it, and enough blood to turn the foam the color of old wine.

Torv stood knee-deep in it, because someone had to be standing somewhere, and his legs had chosen this spot without consulting him. Around him the battle was ending in that strange, unfinished way battles end—not with a horn or a cry but with a slow realization passing through ten thousand men at once, like cold through a wet boot: *it's over. We lost. Or we won. Nobody's sure which.*

He wasn't sure either. He was looking for Emrys.

* * *

He had last seen her an hour before dawn, riding a marsh-elephant the size of a chapel, her hair whipping out behind her, shouting in the voice-that-was-not-a-voice while the whole horizon came alive with wing and horn and hoof. She had looked, in that moment, like something off one of his grandfather's maps—the ones Torv had spent thirty years trying to prove were lies. He remembered thinking, absurdly: *I should have drawn her. No cartographer would believe the scale.*

Then the tide flats had swallowed everything in spray and screaming, and he had lost her, and now he was walking through the aftermath calling her name in the smallest voice he'd used since childhood.

"Emrys. *Emrys.*"

A wolf loped past him, gray as sea-fog, its muzzle dark with work Torv did not want described. It paused. Looked at him. And spoke—not in words, exactly, but in the pressure of meaning against his skull, the way all of Sablemark's creatures spoke to anyone who would listen.

*She breathes,* the wolf said. *Follow the salt smell. Not the blood salt. The other.*

"The other salt," Torv repeated. "Wonderful. That narrows it down on a battlefield."

The wolf made a sound that could only be described as a laugh, if you were generous, and trotted off toward whatever wolves do after wars.

Torv followed the smell anyway. He had followed worse leads with less confidence—his entire career had been worse leads with less confidence—and eventually, near the shattered stump of the old tide gate, he found what he was looking for.

Emrys sat on a fallen beam of white stone, salt-crusted from head to foot, holding her left arm against her chest at an angle arms are not meant to hold. Brannoc's descendant—a young falcon, fierce-eyed and ridiculous—was perched on her shoulder, screaming at anyone who came within ten feet. Behind her, visible through the settling haze, stood the Door of Salt: a seam of pale light in the air, tall as a cathedral door, and even as Torv watched, it narrowed. Narrowed. And closed with a sound like the sea exhaling.

Not a boom. Not a crack. Just—gone. The way a kingdom can be gone. The way a name can be gone.

"No," Emrys said. She said it very quietly, to the place where the Door had been, and her voice cracked down the middle. "No, wait—I didn't—the beasts aren't all through yet—"

"They're through." Torv crouched beside her, careful, the way you approach a wounded animal, except he'd spent three weeks learning she hated being approached like a wounded animal. "Emrys. They're all through. I saw them come off the marshes at first light—wolves, the elephants, the falcon clans, the marsh-cattle, all of them. Every last one. The Door held until they crossed and then—" he gestured at the empty air, helplessly, "—then it kept its own counsel. Doors do that. My family wrote the book on doors keeping their own counsel."

"It shouldn't have closed." Her voice was flat with shock. "It was supposed to stay open. Wren said—Wren thought—"

"Wren," Torv said gently, "is still holding the gate."

And then he told her. Because someone had to, and because she deserved to hear it from a friend rather than from rumor wearing a soldier's mouth.

Hunter Wren—the man who had tracked them across two hundred miles, who had put a bolt meant for her into Torv's shoulder instead, who had freed them both and handed over his horse and his sister's name like a man paying off a debt older than himself—that man had walked back to Aldric's lines that morning with his head up, knowing exactly what was waiting. And when the king's officers ordered him to stand aside so the reserve companies could flank the free cities' ragged line, Wren had stood instead. In the gatehouse of the old Sablemark fort, alone, with a borrowed sword and the stubbornness of a man who had finally found something worth being stubborn about.

He had held the gate for forty minutes. Long enough for Emrys's beasts to come through the Door and break the flanking maneuver like a wave breaking on rock. Long enough for the battle to turn.

He had not survived the turning.

"He bought us the hour," Torv said. His voice did something embarrassing halfway through and he let it. "Forty minutes, they say. The soldiers who saw it—they're already telling stories. He died holding the gate, Emrys. That's how it'll be written. I intend to write it myself, actually, on a map, in the margin, where no king can burn it."

Emrys didn't answer. She leaned sideways until her forehead rested against his good shoulder, careful of his wound, careful of hers, and they stayed like that while the falcon screamed at the gulls and the tide slid in over the Shallows, patient as arithmetic.

* * *

They carried him through the streets of the nearest free city—Vessany, all crooked lanes and fish-smoke—at dusk, on a bier built from a door somebody had pulled off its hinges. Torv thought that was right, somehow. A man who had opened a gate should go through a doorway.

Half the city turned out. Half the city was celebrating; the other half was grieving; the halves overlapped completely, which Torv decided was simply what victory looked like when you counted the cost honestly. He watched a woman set a candle on a windowsill and then, three steps later, throw her arms around a stranger and laugh until she cried. He watched a baker give away every loaf in his shop and then sit down in his own doorway and weep into his flour-dusted apron. He watched children chase each other between the mourners, shrieking, alive, unaware, which was the whole point of the day, though nobody could quite feel it yet.

Someone started singing—one of the old Sablemark songs, the kind the fog villages still hummed without knowing why—and by the second verse the whole street had it, thousands of voices, off-key and ragged and unstoppable.

Torv walked beside the bier with Emrys on his arm. She'd refused the healers' tent until Wren was laid to rest. Her arm was bound in a sling now, splinted, the bone knit crooked by a field-medic who apologized more than he worked. She was gray-faced with pain and exhaustion, and she walked the entire way anyway, because some debts you pay on your feet.

At the grave—shallow, hurried, marked with a piece of the broken gatehouse that a mason had already begun carving—Emrys knelt and said something too low for Torv to hear. He caught only the last of it:

"—and I'm sorry it took me so long to learn your name properly. Hunter Wren. It's a good name. Your sister would be proud. I think maybe you finally were."

She laid a single gray feather on the earth. Brannoc's line had offered it; she had accepted on his behalf. Then she stood, swayed, and Torv caught her before the ground could.

"I've got you," he said.

"You've got me," she agreed, half-asleep already. "That's going to be a problem for my independence."

"Noted. Filed. Ignored."

* * *

The healers' tent smelled of comfrey and burnt rosemary and the particular sourness of too many frightened people in too small a space. Torv sat beside Emrys's cot for six hours while a healer with ink-stained fingers—she'd been pulling double duty as a scribe, apparently; everyone in Vessany had three jobs now—reset what needed resetting and salved what needed salving.

Emrys slept like the dead and woke like the drowned, gasping, once an hour, always reaching for something on the pillow beside her hand. The first four times, Torv pretended to be asleep and let her find nothing. The fifth time, he took her hand.

"You're in Vessany," he said. "The battle's won. The Door is closed. You're safe. All three facts confirmed personally by a professional geographer, whose entire trade is knowing where things are."

She blinked at him in the lamplight. Then, slowly, the terror drained out of her face and something worse replaced it.

"Torv." Her voice was very small. "My mother."

"What about her?"

"I can't—" She stopped. Started again. Stopped again. He watched her reach inside herself the way he'd seen her reach for beast-speech, that inward turning, and come back empty-handed. "Her face. Torv, I can't see her face. I know I knew it. I know I loved her. I remember loving her the way you remember—you know, the shape of where a thing was. But the Door took—" Her breath hitched. "It asked for one true memory and I gave it hers. I chose—God forgive me, I *chose*—"

"Yes," Torv said. "You did. And it was the bravest thing I've ever watched a person do, and I once watched a marsh-elephant carry a burning siege tower on its back out of pure spite."

That startled a wet, broken laugh out of her, which had been the entire point.

"Listen to me." He leaned close, and his voice went low and steady, the voice he used for reading maps aloud to frightened clients in bad weather. "You paid the toll so that every gifted child in this country could grow up without hiding. So that no one else ever has to make a choice like yours. That memory isn't gone, Emrys. It's spent. There's a difference. A coin spent on something worth buying isn't lost—it's just not in your pocket anymore. It's out in the world doing its work."

"But I don't know what I paid."

"You know what you paid. You just told me: her face. You'll always know that much. The rest—" he shrugged, and winced at his shoulder, and kept shrugging anyway, because dignity, "—the rest is mine to hold for you, if you want it. I remember her, you know. From the market days. The way she hummed when she bartered. The blue scarf. The goat named after a saint she swore she didn't worship."

Emrys stared at him. "You never met my mother."

"I met her twice. She called me 'that map boy.' She was right to." He smiled, and found, to his surprise, that the smile was real. "Here's what I propose. Until you're old and gray, whenever you wonder about her, you come ask me. I'll tell you what I remember. It won't be the same as having it. But it will be *something*, and it will be true, and it will be given freely, which is more than most memories ever get."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then she squeezed his hand—careful of the bandage, careless of everything else—and said, "That map boy is going to be insufferable about this."

"Already am."

* * *

Three days later, the counting began.

It happened in the harbor square, on a platform of crates, before a crowd of free-city delegates and soldiers and refugees and one extremely opinionated falcon. The tally was read aloud, because the free cities believed grief should be public or it curdled. Dead: nine thousand of the cities' defenders. The Sablemark beasts: eleven, names given in full, including a marsh-elephant called Old Mercy whom the crowd had clearly loved. Wounded: uncounted. Saved: everyone else.

"And the gatekeeper," the reader finished, "Hunter Wren of the Crown's Gift-Hunt, who turned."

The silence that followed lasted long enough for Torv to count his own heartbeats. Forty-one of them. Then someone in the crowd—Torv would swear forever it was the baker—began the old song again, and this time nobody sang it sadly.

Afterward, on the platform's edge, Emrys stood looking out at the harbor, her splinted arm tucked against her like a folded wing. The falcon sat on the rail. The evening light did something golden and shameless with the water.

"They keep asking," she said. "The delegates. When I'll ride east and take the crown."

"What do you tell them?"

"That I'm a goatherd with a broken arm and no interest in thrones." She glanced at him sideways. "What would you tell them, cartographer? You're the one who knows how countries get made."

Torv considered this with the seriousness it deserved, which was considerable, since his family had buried one country and helped unbury another.

"I'd tell them," he said, "that countries aren't made by crowns. They're made by maps. And maps are made by people who walk the ground. And the ground east of here—" he nodded toward the horizon, where the drowned road ran, where the fog-bound valleys waited, where a hundred hidden villages full of hidden children were just now hearing the news and daring, for the first time in twenty years, to open their windows—"that ground has never been walked honestly. Not once. Not by anyone with eyes that see what's really there."

Emrys was quiet a moment. Then: "You want to go walking."

"I want to go *surveying*, thank you, there's a difference, and it involves instruments."

"With a broken-armed goatherd."

"With a legendary beast-speaker, actually, whose testimony every animal from here to the Salt Coast will vouch for. Do you know what a map drawn with *their* help would show? Roads no human eye has seen in a generation. Springs. Safe passes. Every hiding place, every hollow, every child in every cellar—found, and named, and *on the record*." He heard his own voice rising and didn't care. "No more erased kingdoms, Emrys. No more Ashvales. That's how we count the cost. That's how we make it worth—"

"Torv." She was smiling. It was the tired, crooked, real one. "Yes. Obviously yes. I was going to make you ask properly, but you've gone red in the face, so."

Behind them, on the rail, the young falcon screamed once—a sound like laughter, if you were generous—and spread its wings against the last of the light, facing east, where the drowned road waited, and the fog-bound valleys, and all the names that had not yet been written down.
