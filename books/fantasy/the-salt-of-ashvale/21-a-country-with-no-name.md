## 21. A Country With No Name

The morning after the battle, the tide came in pink.

Emrys stood on the shattered seawall of the Shallows and watched the water carry away what it could and keep what it couldn't, and she understood that this was how a war ended—not with trumpets or treaties but with gulls picking through the aftermath and the sea doing its slow, indifferent arithmetic. Somewhere behind her, in the camps along the dunes, free city soldiers were discovering they were alive. Some of them were laughing about it. Some of them would never laugh quite the same way again.

Brannoc's great-granddaughter—her name was Sere, and she had her ancestor's imperious eye and none of his patience for being thanked—landed on the broken stone beside Emrys and folded her wings.

"He is awake," Sere said.

"Who?"

"The king." The falcon said the word the way one might say *maggot*. "The one who came to burn us. He has been standing at the waterline since before dawn. He does not move."

Emrys looked down the beach. And there he was.

King Aldric of Ashvale, the usurper whose edicts had driven her into the fog for twenty-five years, whose criers had announced the Gift-Hunt, whose hunter had nearly caught her twice over—standing barefoot at the edge of the surf in a mud-stained coat with no crown on his head and no guards within a mile of him. He was looking out at the water as though it might tell him something. Or perhaps as though he were waiting for it to finish him.

"I should go," Emrys said.

"You should," Sere agreed, and did not offer to come with her, which was its own kind of courtesy. Falcons understood, better than most, when a conversation needed to happen between only two creatures who could lie.

* * *

She walked down to the waterline slowly, giving him time to see her coming, time to run if running was what he wanted. He didn't run. When she stopped an arm's length away, he didn't even turn fully—he just glanced at her sideways, the way you look at weather.

"You're the beast-speaker," he said.

"People are calling me that. I'm also called Emrys."

"Aldric," he said, as though introducing himself at a wedding. Then, after a moment: "They could have killed me yesterday. Your wolves had me. I was on my knees in the shallows and the big grey one had my arm in his mouth, and he let go. They all let go. Why did they let go?"

It wasn't really a question, so Emrys answered the question underneath it instead. "Because I asked them to."

"Why?" His voice cracked on it. "I burned the hedge-healers. I hunted children. I marched on Rivergate to burn it because it sheltered people like you. If our positions were turned, I promise you I would be dead already."

"Yes," Emrys said. "You probably would be. That's rather the point of everything that happened here."

He made a sound that might have been a laugh in some other life, in some other man's chest. The surf came up around their ankles. Out past the breakers, a marsh-elephant surfaced and sank again like a small gray island deciding whether to exist.

"My wife was from Sablemark," Aldric said suddenly. "Before it fell. Before—" He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "Everyone thinks I hate magic because it's power and I wanted all the power for myself. Let them think it; it's simpler. But I built the edict because magic ate my family. It swallowed the whole country in a night, and I woke up in a fishing boat with my hands empty and the horizon full of ash. I spent thirty years telling myself that if magic never existed again, no one else would wake up in a boat."

"And now?"

"Now I stood in the shallows with a wolf's teeth at my arm," he said, "and your beasts looked at me—and I swear on whatever's left of my soul, girl, they looked at me the way my daughter used to look at thunderstorms. Curious. Not cruel. Curious." His hand dropped. He was crying, quietly, without any apparent interest in hiding it. "And I thought: thirty years. Thirty years of boats and ash and edicts, and the thing I was so afraid of was curious about me."

Emrys thought of her goats, who had once been deeply curious about Torv's bootlaces and had eaten two of them. She decided not to share this.

"What will you do?" she asked.

"That's the other reason I'm standing in the sea." He turned to face her properly then, and up close she could see how much the war had taken from him—the hollows under his eyes, the white that had claimed half his hair since the autumn criers. "A king who abdicates in wartime is a coward. A king who abdicates in peacetime is a traitor. So I'm going to stand here until the war feels properly finished, and then I am going to walk back to the capital, and I am going to stand in the Hall of Voices and give the crown to whoever wants the miserable thing. There will be a line. I'll wait in it myself if I have to."

"They'll want to try you," Emrys said. "For the hedge-healer. For Wren."

Something moved across his face at the hunter's name—grief, or guilt, or the place where those two things lived together. "Yes. They should. I won't argue for mercy. Mercy is yours to spend, not mine." He looked at her then, directly, and for the first time she saw not the king from the proclamations but simply a tired man with salt on his boots. "But I'd ask one thing, beast-speaker. Whatever they do with me—don't let them make a lesson of it. Don't let them put my head on a wall and call it justice and teach the next frightened generation that fear answered with fire is the natural order. You've seen where that road goes. Both of us have."

Emrys looked at him for a long moment. The sea filled the silence, patient as ever.

"No walls," she said finally. "But you'll answer for the healer. Publicly. In front of the villages."

"Yes," he said, and bowed his head, and it seemed to Emrys that the bow cost him more than the crown ever had.

* * *

Torv found her that evening sitting on the ruins of the tide gate with a bowl of fish stew she hadn't touched, staring at nothing in particular. He sat down beside her without asking, which was one of the ten thousand reasons she loved him, and stole a piece of fish from her bowl, which was another.

"So," he said. "The king's abdicating. Half the council's fainted. The other half are fighting over who gets to write the history. How are you?"

"Tired. Confused. My ribs still hurt when I breathe."

"I meant about the crown."

"There is no crown," Emrys said. "There's a very heavy hat that someone's going to have to wear, and everyone keeps looking at me like I've already got it on."

Torv winced sympathetically. "Ah. Yes. I heard. Councilor Vess cornered me by the cookfires. She was extremely casual about it. 'Oh, Torv, by the way, the council wishes to offer the Lady Emrys the protectorship of the coast, purely as a formality, do mention it to her, no rush.'" He took another piece of fish. "She mentioned it to me three separate times and once via a note delivered by a boy who was clearly paid to run fast."

"It's absurd."

"Mm."

"I fought a war. I didn't fight a war to become a *government*."

"Mm," Torv said again, with the particular serenity of a man who agrees with you completely and is waiting for you to hear yourself.

"I'm a goat-girl, Torv. I spent twenty-five years learning to be invisible. I hid in fog. I whispered to animals behind barn doors. The single greatest skill of my entire life is *not being noticed*, and these people want to put me on a platform."

"Mm-hmm."

"Stop doing that."

"Do what?" He was grinning now, the wretched cartographer, ink still permanently stained into the creases of his fingers despite everything. "I haven't said a word."

"You're saying whole paragraphs with your eyebrows."

"All right." He set the stew aside and turned to face her, and his voice gentled. "Then say it plainly, love. What do you actually want?"

And that was the trouble, wasn't it. Because she'd spent the whole ride south, and the whole terrible bright week of the battle, imagining an afterward, and in every version of the afterward she was somewhere quiet. A valley, maybe. Goats. A door she could leave open. But every time she pictured it, she found she couldn't picture herself *in* it—not honestly. Every time she imagined the quiet valley, there were children in it. Gifted ones, hiding their voices the way she'd hidden hers, flinching at loud noises, learning early that the truest thing about them was the most dangerous thing about them. And every time, in the imagining, she opened her mouth and taught them anyway. Quietly. One child at a time. Behind barn doors, if necessary.

"I don't want a country," she said slowly. "I don't want armies or councils or a title with *protector* in it. I want—" She laughed, surprised at herself. "I want a school. A ridiculous little school on the drowned coast, where nobody's afraid. Where a child can speak to a crow in the market and the worst consequence is that the crow follows them home and steals their breakfast."

"That sounds like an excellent school," Torv said. "Terrible attendance policy regarding crows."

"Vess will be furious."

"Vess will be furious for exactly four days and then she'll take credit for suggesting it. That woman could take credit for the sunrise." He bumped his shoulder against hers. "So we'll decline the protectorship."

"We?"

"Well, someone has to draw the maps of a country that doesn't exist yet," Torv said. "And I happen to be the only disgraced cartographer on the continent with experience mapping erased places. Also I love you, and also you cannot cook, and also someone has to protect the school's bootlaces from the goats."

"We're getting goats?"

"Emrys. Of course we're getting goats."

* * *

The council session where she refused the crown took place in Rivergate's old harbor hall, which still smelled faintly of smoke and heroism. They had cleaned it up for the occasion, hung banners, arranged the benches in a semicircle facing a raised chair that someone had draped in blue cloth to make it look less like a chair and more like a throne. Emrys stood at the bottom of the three steps leading up to it and felt every eye in the room land on her like a physical weight.

Councilor Vess—a small, sharp woman with a voice like a well-sharpened pencil—rose and made the offer formally. Protector of the Coast. Guardian of the Reconciliation. First Voice of the Free Accord. They'd clearly stayed up late inventing titles, and Emrys suspected several of them had enjoyed it far too much.

She let the applause die. She let the hopeful silence stretch. And then she said:

"Thank you. No."

A rustle went through the benches. Vess's pencil-voice cut in smoothly: "Lady Emrys, with respect, the accord requires—"

"With respect," Emrys said, "the accord requires a leader, and I'm not one. Listen—I stood in front of the beasts of Sablemark and they didn't follow me because I commanded them. They followed me because I told them the truth and let them choose. If I take a throne—even a good throne, even a blue-draped chair in a smoky harbor hall—then the first thing this new world teaches its children is that the gifted rise by becoming rulers. That the reward for surviving the hunt is to become the hunter's opposite, which is to say, the hunter with better manners." She shook her head. "I don't want gifted children growing up believing they must either hide or rule. Those aren't the only choices. They never were. We just pretended they were, because both of them are easier than the third one."

"Which is?" someone called from the back.

"Living," Emrys said. "Plainly, publicly, among neighbors. Speaking to a crow where anyone can see. Being strange in the daylight." She smiled, and to her surprise the smile was returned in more than a few of those watching faces. "So I'll decline the protectorate. But I'll make you a counteroffer. Give me the lighthouse on the drowned coast—it's ruined, it's worthless, no one's lit it since Sablemark fell. Give me paper and chalk and enough coin to feed a dozen mouths. And I will build a school there, for the gifted children of every city and village that will send them. Not to train soldiers. Not to train advisors. To teach them that concealment need not be a life sentence. That's my price for the beasts' alliance holding—which, Councilor, between friends, it will hold better with a teacher on the coast than a queen on a chair."

Silence. Then Vess, slowly, began to smile, and it was the smile of a woman recalculating everything. "The lighthouse," she said. "Of course. The Lighthouse Accord. Oh, that will *sing* in the histories." She was already turning to her clerks. "Someone fetch me a fresh quill—"

"She's taking credit," Torv murmured in Emrys's ear. "Told you. Four days ahead of schedule."

* * *

They left Rivergate a fortnight later, on a mild gray morning with gulls shouting overhead and half the city turned out to see them off, which embarrassed Emrys so thoroughly that she spent most of the procession studying the cobblestones. Sere flew escort for the first league, then wheeled once around their heads—falcons do not say goodbye; they say *I may return, provision accordingly*—and turned north toward the falcon clans.

The lighthouse, when they reached it, was worse than promised and better than hoped. Its tower leaned slightly, as if listening. Its lamp room was full of gull nests and forty years of unkind weather. Its keeper's cottage had a roof that was mostly theoretical. Torv stood in the doorway of the ruin, hands on hips, surveying it with the expression he usually reserved for deliberately misleading antique maps.

"It's perfect," he said.

"It's falling down."

"Everything worth building starts by falling down." He was already pacing out the cottage with his stride, muttering measurements, and Emrys left him to it and walked out onto the drowned causeway below, where the tide was low and the old Sablemark stones showed dark and weed-slick beneath the shallow water.

The sea said her mother's name.

It always did, now, when the tide drew back—some trick of current over stone, a sound like *Maren, Maren* repeated soft against the rocks. She had stopped flinching at it months ago. Now she just listened, the way you'd listen to a neighbor calling from a distant hill: present, beloved, no longer requiring an answer.

"Come look at this!" Torv shouted from the cottage. "There's a hearth! An actual hearth! And something living in the chimney that I've chosen to believe is a badger!"

Emrys laughed and turned back toward the light. Behind her, far down the coast, a rider appeared on the drowned road—a young girl on a shaggy pony, alone, with a bindle tied to her saddle and the wary posture of someone who had been traveling a long way on hope. As the girl drew closer, Emrys saw her lips moving, saw a starling rise from the pony's mane and circle her head, and saw the girl talking to it openly, unhidden, in broad daylight, because no one had yet taught her that she shouldn't.

The girl reined up at the foot of the causeway and stared at Emrys with wide, terrified, hopeful eyes.

"Are you," the girl began, and swallowed, and started again. "Are you the one who speaks? They said—on the road they said—if we can find the coast, there's a lady who—"

"Yes," Emrys said, and held out her hand. "Come in out of the wind. School's just starting."

Behind her, in the leaning tower, something scraped and clattered, and Torv yelled, "It is NOT a badger!"
