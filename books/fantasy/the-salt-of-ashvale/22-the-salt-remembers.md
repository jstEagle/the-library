## 22. The Salt Remembers

The lighthouse had been a ruin when they found it, and in some ways it still was. Torv liked to say that about everything he loved—the map, the boat, himself—*still is, mostly*, as if ruin were not an ending but a habit one grew comfortable with. Emrys had stopped arguing the point years ago. The light at the top of the tower burned every night from Duskwatch to Dawnbell, and no ruin she'd ever heard of could manage that.

Below her, in the round stone room that served as schoolhouse, kitchen, and on market days something dangerously close to a theater, eleven children sat in a crooked half-circle and tried very hard to look patient.

They failed. Children always did. That was rather the point of them.

"Magistra," said Pell, who was nine and built entirely of elbows and questions, "did it hurt? Opening the Door?"

Emrys set down the slate she'd been cleaning. Outside the salt-glazed windows, the drowned coast lay flat and shining under the afternoon sun—the old causeways of Sablemark rising from shallow water like the ribs of something enormous and sleeping, gulls wheeling over them, marsh-grass combing the wind.

"I don't know," she said honestly. "I don't remember."

"You *say* that," Pell said, with the exquisite skepticism only children and cats can achieve. "You say it every year. But you must remember *something*. You walked through it. You opened it with—" he dropped his voice into the hushed register children use for the sacred and the scandalous alike— "*with your own memory.*"

"That's what the stories say."

"But you're the story!"

"I'm the magistra," Emrys said. "Stories get to be more interesting than I am. It's one of the few advantages of being made of words instead of knees."

Laughter rippled down the half-circle, and even Pell grinned despite himself. At the back of the room, Torv looked up from the great table where three students were bent over his blank vellum maps—blank to everyone but them—and gave Emrys the small, wry tilt of the head that meant, after twenty years, *you're doing fine, and also there's tea.*

She loved him for that head-tilt more than she could reasonably explain to anyone, including him.

"The thing is," she said, turning back to the children, "the Door took exactly one true memory. Not the whole person. Not all the memories of a kind. Just one, chosen by the one who paid." She spread her hands. "And I chose carefully. I chose the one I thought I could bear to lose."

"And now you can't remember what it was."

"No."

"So you lost something and you don't even know what it was." Pell's voice cracked with genuine horror, the way it might have if she'd confessed to losing a limb. "Doesn't that frighten you?"

Emrys considered the question properly, because Pell deserved that much. She considered it every time he asked it, which was every year, which was perhaps why he asked it—to hear whether her answer changed, whether the hole in her had grown teeth.

"It used to," she said. "Now I think of it this way. Somewhere in me is a door I locked myself, and behind it is something true. And every morning I wake up, and I make tea, and I teach you lot, and I argue with Torv about the roof, and the door stays shut and nothing leaks out of it except—" she paused— "except a shape. A warmth. Like standing near a fire in another room. I know there was someone I loved who isn't here. I know her name."

"Maren," said little Sefa, softly, from the front row. Sefa, whose gift was hearing what the sea carried, and who sometimes repeated things the water told her without understanding their weight.

"Maren," Emrys agreed. Her throat did its small familiar tightening and released. "I have her name. I have her voice, a little—I kept her voice, the Door didn't want voices, only faces, or so I've decided, since deciding things is how we survive our choices." She smiled at the class. "So no, Pell. It doesn't frighten me anymore. It's more like—carrying a letter I'm not allowed to open, addressed in handwriting I almost recognize."

"From whom?" asked Wrenna—not named for the hunter; the children had simply arrived at the name independently, and Emrys had decided long ago that names come back around because they're needed, not because they're haunted.

"Maybe from me," Emrys said. "Maybe from whoever I'll be in another twenty years. Maybe the sea. The sea writes a great many letters nobody reads."

* * *

School let out at the low bell, and the children scattered along the causeway toward the fishing villages—half walking, half riding, because the marsh-elephants' grandchildren still came up from the shallows at noon to ferry the smallest ones dry-shod across the tide flats, and because no child raised on this coast had ever once been afraid of anything larger than a crab.

Emrys watched them go from the tower gallery, Torv beside her, both of them doing the thing they pretended wasn't a ritual: counting heads until the last small figure vanished into the dune-grass.

"Eleven," Torv said.

"Eleven."

"Good year. Only two of them bit anyone."

"Three," Emrys said. "You weren't awake for the third."

He laughed, that dry rolling laugh she'd first heard in a valley inn over a cup of bad wine, when he'd been a disgrace with a broken compass and she'd been a girl with a secret, and neither of them had known yet that disgraces and secrets are just futures wearing poor clothes.

Below them, the lighthouse lamp room waited for evening. Torv went up to trim the wick—he insisted on doing it by hand, though the flame answered Emrys's whistled requests perfectly well, and they had argued about this for two decades in the gentle, endless way married people argue about things that are actually about love—and Emrys stayed on the gallery, leaning on the rail, watching the tide come in over the Door road.

The Door itself had closed forever the night the beasts passed through. Everyone knew that. The seam in the air had sealed like a wound healing, and where it had stood there was now only sea, and above the sea, at dusk, sometimes a light that was probably a star and probably wasn't.

But the salt remembered. Salt remembered everything. That was the trouble and the treasure of it—it held what water carried and never gave it up cheaply.

Emrys put two fingers to her lips and whistled—a falling three-note phrase, old Sablemark speech, the way one says *anyone out there?* to the horizon.

The horizon considered it.

* * *

He came out of the west at golden hour: a falcon, broad-winged, dark as wet iron with a single white feather at the throat, and Emrys knew before he'd crossed the first mile of air that he was Brannoc's line—not the grandson; the great-great-grandson, four generations deep, carrying the family memory of this rail the way Torv's family had carried the blank map. Falcon memory was a stubborn country. She'd taught enough of them to know.

He landed on the gallery rail with a thump that meant *I have flown far and you will feed me immediately,* and then, softening, dipped his head in the old salute: *heir of voices.*

"Oh, sit down," Emrys said fondly, in beast-speech, aloud, in full daylight, without a flicker of fear—the ordinary miracle of her ordinary evenings. "You and your ancestors and your appetites. There's mutton inside and you know it."

*I know it,* the falcon agreed. *But first, news.*

Falcons always had news. It was their currency, their pride, their excuse.

"The northern clans have settled the high passes for good," he reported, while she fetched the dish. "The wolves send word that the last of Aldric's soldiers' children came to them unarmed, asking to learn the speech, and the wolves—grudgingly, with much complaint, as is the wolf way—said yes. The marsh-elephants have a new calf. The free cities have voted to put beasts' names on the council rolls, actual names, in ink." He ruffled his feathers, pleased. "Also, a dog in Rivergate has learned to sing and is insufferable about it."

"Tell him we said well done and please stop."

*I will convey it in exactly those words.* He ate, then grew quiet in the way falcons did before saying the thing they'd actually flown all this way to say. *There is a child in the eastern valleys. Gifted. Beast-speech, strong, untrained. Her village doesn't know what she is yet, but the crows do, and the crows are talking.*

Emrys's hand stilled on the falcon's back. Twenty years ago, that sentence would have been a death warrant read aloud. Now it was simply—an errand. A child. A letter worth opening.

"In spring," she said. "We'll go in spring, when the causeways are dry."

*The crows say autumn.*

"The crows," Emrys said, "have never once had to walk the drowned road in rain. Tell the crows that magistra outranks crow."

*No one outranks crow,* said the falcon serenely, *but I will tell them anyway, because watching your face when they refuse is my inheritance too.*

Torv came down from the lamp room wiping oil from his fingers and stopped in the doorway, taking in the scene—his wife, a royal falcon's descendant, the last light of the sun coming gold across the water—with the expression of a man who had once been certain his life would end in a tavern and still hadn't fully recovered from being wrong.

"News?" he asked.

"A gifted child in the east. We ride in spring."

"We ride in autumn," Torv said, "if we leave the teaching to a substitute, and we both know what happens when Maren-of-the-dunes substitutes. Last time the children unionized."

"They were right to," Emrys said. "Their demands were reasonable."

Torv laughed and came to stand beside her at the rail, and the three of them—cartographer, magistra, falcon—watched the tide fill the Door road, the water going rose and copper, the first star finding its place above the place where a seam in the world had been.

"Pell asked again today," Emrys said quietly. "About the Door. What I paid."

"And?"

"And I still don't know. Twenty years, Torv. I've rebuilt a kingdom's memory out of other people's stories, I've taught two generations of children the old speech, I can recite Sablemark's royal line backward. But that one room in me stays shut." She turned her hand over on the rail, palm up, as if the answer might land in it. "Sometimes I think that's the cruelest part. Sometimes I think it's the kindest. I genuinely cannot decide."

Torv was silent a moment. Then he said, "My grandmother used to say the sea takes what it takes, and gives back what it gives, and the trick is knowing those aren't the same day."

"Your grandmother," Emrys said, "was a wise woman who drank too much and buried maps for a living."

"All three at once, usually."

The falcon, fed and philosophical, had tucked his head under his wing. Below, the water slid silver-dark into evening, and Emrys felt it—that thing she'd never found better words for than *a shape, a warmth*, the fire burning in a room she couldn't enter. Love with the face worn off. Grief with the teeth pulled. Memory kept the way the salt keeps: dissolved, dispersed, and somehow, impossibly, still itself.

She began, idly, without thinking, to whistle—the falling three-note phrase, then a fourth note she didn't plan, a fifth, a melody assembling itself out of her faster than she could question it, a tune she did not know learning itself through her mouth.

Torv went still. "What's that?"

"I don't—" She stopped. Started again. The tune knew where it was going even if she didn't. "It's nothing. Something the sea—"

And then the sea said it.

Not in words. The sea didn't use words; it used pressure and light and the long slow grammar of tides. But the wave that came up the causeway just then came in a rhythm that matched her whistle, and the foam wrote and unwrote a pattern on the stones that her eyes read before her mind could stop them, and the wind off the water carried a sound that was almost, almost, almost—

"*Maren.*"

Her mother's name. Spoken in her mother's cadence, in the voice Emrys had kept when the Door took everything else—kept, all these years, without knowing she was keeping it, the way the salt keeps.

The wave withdrew. The foam smoothed. The coast went quiet and ordinary and vast.

Emrys stood at the rail with tears running freely down her face and discovered, to her complete lack of surprise, that she was smiling.

"What happened?" Torv asked gently. "What did you hear?"

"I heard—" She laughed, wiped her cheeks with the heel of her hand. "I heard my own name, called the way mothers call it. From the other side of a shut door." She looked out at the darkening water, at the star above the place where the Door had been. "Torv. I think the salt's been holding it for me. All this time. Keeping it safe until—until I was far enough past the grief to hold it again without breaking."

"And now?"

"Now I know what I paid." She said it slowly, tasting each word like the first sip of a long-awaited tea. "I paid her *face*. And the sea has kept it for me, all these years, out past the bar, in the deep salt where nothing is ever truly lost—" her voice caught, steadied— "and tonight it sent me back her voice saying mine."

Torv put his arm around her, and they stood together while the lighthouse lamp bloomed overhead, throwing its long wheeling beam across the water—out and out and out, over the causeways, over the drowned kingdom, over the whole remembering dark.

The falcon lifted his head from under his wing, considered the two of them, the lamp, the sea, and delivered the evening's last dispatch:

*The crows,* he said, *will want to know everything.*

"Of course they will," Emrys said, still laughing, still crying. "Tell them autumn, then. Tell them autumn."
