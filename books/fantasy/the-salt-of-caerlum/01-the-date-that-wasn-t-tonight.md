## 1. The Date That Wasn't Tonight

The trouble with carving a dead woman's hands, Hazel thought, was that the dead insisted on commenting.

*Too bony,* murmured the voice that lived in the whalebone blank. *She had kind hands. Round them out or she'll haunt you proper.*

"They're already going to haunt me," Hazel said to their workshop, which was empty of the living and crowded otherwise. "That's rather the point of a memorial."

The lamplight caught the shavings curled on the bench like pale little sleeping things. Outside, Caerlum's harbor wind worried at the shutters, carrying its usual cargo of salt and gossip. Somewhere below in the lanes, Aunt Sef's smugglers were no doubt arguing about tide tables and whether young Piet had watered down the rum again, and Hazel was glad—genuinely glad—to be up here above it all, doing the one thing they did better than anyone on the coast.

Hazel carved bone. And bone talked.

Not loudly. Never loudly. That was the whole trick of surviving forty-odd years as the strange child the Villalobos clan kept anyway: be useful, be quiet, be unremarkable. The dead who whispered through antler and ivory and whalebone wanted small things—a name remembered, a debt acknowledged, once memorably a recipe for plum brandy finished before the widow could get it wrong. Hazel obliged. The living never noticed. A bone-carver who mumbled to their work was just a bone-carver being eccentric, and eccentricity was practically currency in Caerlum.

Tonight's commission was a pair of hands, clasped, for old Maren Ostrey's coffin lid. Maren herself was supplying the commentary with the patience of someone who had waited eighty years to be carved and could wait another hour.

*The left thumb. You've given me my sister's thumb.*

"Your sisters' thumbs are identical," Hazel said, paring a curl away. "I checked the family hands I did last spring."

*Mine has a scar. Piet's dog. Ask anyone.*

Hazel asked the bone, and the bone agreed, and Hazel added a fine hairline groove across the thumb, and Maren settled into satisfaction like an old cat into sun.

It was, all in all, shaping up to be a good evening. There was tea. There was bread and hard cheese. There was the particular pleasure of work going well, which Hazel trusted about as far as they could throw the workshop, but enjoyed anyway.

Then came the knock.

Three raps, precise as a ledger line. Not the smugglers' knock—that was four, then two, then a scrape, because Aunt Sef believed any code worth having should be annoying enough that nobody used it casually. This was a stranger's knock. Strangers did not climb the cliff path to Hazel's workshop after dark. Strangers didn't climb it at all; the path had a reputation, and Hazel had spent years carefully not feeding it.

*Don't answer,* said Maren.

*Answer,* said three other voices at once, and Hazel frowned, because the dead rarely agreed on anything except that living people were endlessly amusing.

Hazel opened the door.

The stranger was tall in the way of someone who'd been tall too early and never quite made peace with it—all elbows and folded-in posture, spectacles fogged from the walk, a satchel clutched to their chest like a rescued infant. Wind had done something criminal to their hair.

"Hazel Villalobos," the stranger said. It wasn't a question, which was the first alarming thing. Nobody sought Hazel out by name. Hazel was the one Villalobos who appeared on no manifest, owed no debts, attended no gatherings. Being named felt like being doused in cold water.

"You have the advantage," Hazel said.

"Wren Corvane." A pause, as if expecting recognition. Receiving none, Wren pressed on with the air of someone stepping off a ledge. "Scholar. Late of the University at Vell, currently of nowhere, because the University withdrew my stipend on account of what I'm about to show you, which they called 'an embarrassing forgery,' which it is not." The satchel was held out. "May I come in? It's about tonight."

"Tonight," Hazel repeated.

"The date," Wren said. "Today's date. Specifically this evening." They glanced past Hazel into the workshop with the look of someone who had read about such rooms and disbelieved half of it. "Also specifically your face, though I confess the book got the nose wrong."

"My nose is fine."

"Your nose is *distinctive*. The book flattered you." Wren hesitated. "Please. I walked nine days. I would very much like to sit down, and I suspect you would very much like to argue with me, and both are easier indoors."

Every sensible instinct Hazel owned—the ones polished by decades of invisibility—said *close the door.* But the dead had gone quiet, and that silence had a texture Hazel knew. It was the hush of a room waiting for a verdict.

Hazel stepped aside.

* * *

Wren sat on the good stool without being offered it, drank the tea without being poured any—no, that was unfair; Hazel poured it, because hospitality had been drilled into them alongside lock-picking and tide-craft—and laid the satchel on the bench beside Maren's unfinished hands with a reverence that made Hazel's skin prickle.

"I need you to understand," Wren said, "that I checked the binding. Fourteenth century, southern vellum, chain-stitch spine. The pigments are period. The marginalia are period. Whoever faked this faked it so well that faking it becomes more miraculous than believing it."

"Believing what?"

Wren drew out a book.

It was heavy, swollen with age, its covers dark leather gone soft as old ear. When Wren opened it, the smell rose up—dust and tallow and something underneath, something mineral and cold, like the air off a deep mine. Hazel's hands went still on their own knees.

The page Wren turned to was an illumination. Ink and faded vermilion, a border of thorned vines, and in the center a figure standing at the mouth of a cave, holding a chisel aloft like a torch.

Hazel looked at the figure.

Hazel looked at their own reflection, faint and warped, in the window glass beside it.

The figure wore Hazel's face. Not approximately. Exactly—the sharp jaw, the crooked set of the brow, even the little notch in the left ear from a chisel slip at fourteen, which Hazel had told exactly no one outside the family, because who brags about a chisel injury.

"That's—" Hazel began, and found their voice had gone somewhere else.

"Below it," Wren said gently, "the date."

Below it, in a careful old hand, letters that had been waiting centuries:

*On this night the carver comes down. On this night the last fire gutters.*

"This is a joke," Hazel said. "A very expensive, extremely well-researched joke. Did Sef put you up to this? Because if this is about the Ostrey commission—"

"Why would your grandmother buy roads?"

The question dropped into the room like a stone down a well. Hazel heard it fall a long way before anything answered.

"I don't know what you mean."

"Page ninety," Wren said. "The bargain. I'll spare you the middle verses—they're mostly the sort of doom-poetry that gives prophecy a bad name—but the substance is this: a century ago, someone sold a soul to the king beneath Thornfell. A great soul. In exchange, safe passage through the mountain—roads no storm, no wreck, no revenuer could close. Your family's roads, Hazel. Your family's fortune." Wren's eyes behind the fogged spectacles were steady and sorry. "And the soul they sold belonged to Maerin. The last dragon."

The lamp guttered, though there was no draft.

Hazel laughed. It came out thin and wrong. "Dragons are stories. Dragons are what we put on figureheads to scare customs men."

"So are bone-carvers who hear the dead," Wren said quietly. "Yet here we sit."

And that was when the workshop spoke.

All of it. Every piece. The whalebone blanks, the antler-handled tools, the finished keepsakes on their shelf—Maren's mother's lark, the fisherman's charm, the tiny carved dog that had belonged to a girl sixty years drowned. Hazel had spent a lifetime keeping the voices to a murmur, a background hush they could lean against or ignore. Now every carving in the room spoke at once, and the word they spoke was one word, over and over, in dozens of voices layered like tide over tide:

*Descend. Descend. Descend. Descend.*

Maren's unfinished hands said it too. Kindly, the way she'd said everything. *Descend, dear. You've been seen.*

Hazel gripped the edge of the bench. The vertigo was total—not of heights, but of exposure. Forty years of being nobody, of being the quiet one, of walking through Caerlum like smoke through fingers—and some dead scholar's ink had peeled all of it back in a single evening. A book with their face. A date with tonight's name on it. A grandmother's bargain they'd never been told of, priced in dragon.

"I can't," Hazel said. "You don't understand what you're asking. I'm not—I'm no one. That's how I've survived. That's how everyone I love has survived."

Wren closed the book and rested their hand flat upon it, and looked at Hazel with an expression that was not pity, which Hazel would have resented, but recognition, which was infinitely worse.

"The book says the carver comes down tonight," Wren said. "It doesn't say why. It doesn't say what happens after. Believe me, I've read the marginalia; the author didn't know either." A breath. "But I know what the dead around you are saying, because you've gone white as your own shavings and your lips haven't stopped moving. So. One question, Hazel Villalobos, and then I'll leave if you want me to, and we can both pretend the world is ordinary."

Outside, the wind dropped all at once, the way it does before something arrives.

"What happened," Wren asked, "a hundred years ago, that bought your family those roads? Ask your matriarch. Tonight."

Down in the lanes, faint through the floorboards, Hazel could hear Aunt Sef's household lighting lamps against the dark—ordinary lamps, ordinary people, the family that had taken in a strange orphaned child and asked nothing but silence in return.

The dead whispered: *descend.*

Hazel reached for their coat.
