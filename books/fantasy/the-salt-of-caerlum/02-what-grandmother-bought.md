## 2. What Grandmother Bought

The Salt Cellar smelled the way it always did at dusk: tar, tallow, and Aunt Sef's terrible stew, which she insisted was a family recipe and which everyone else insisted was a punishment. Hazel sat at the long table with their hands still trembling from the morning's carving, watching steam rise off a bowl they had no intention of eating.

The prophecy book lay between them like an unexploded shell.

Wren Corvane had wanted to come along. Hazel had said no. Wren had argued — of course Wren had argued, the woman argued with furniture — and Hazel had finally said, "This is family business," which was true, and also the only weapon they'd ever needed to end a conversation.

Now, alone in the kitchen where Hazel had learned to hold a knife, they turned the book toward the door and waited.

Aunt Sef came in on the smell of the stew, wiping her hands on her apron, already talking. "If you're here to complain about the mackerel shipment, your uncle already took the blame, and rightly so, the man could lose a boat in a bathtub—"

She saw the book. She stopped talking.

Hazel had spent twenty-nine years learning to read faces — smugglers' faces, customs men's faces, the faces of clients who wanted keepsakes carved from their dead and lied about how the dead had died. They had never once seen Aunt Sef look afraid. Not during the wreck of the *Kestrel's Mercy*. Not when the revenue cutter boarded them off Thornfell Point and Sef had laughed at the captain until he apologized.

She looked afraid now.

"Where did you get that?" she asked. Her voice was steady. Her knuckles were not; they'd gone white around the apron cloth.

"It found me," Hazel said. "A scholar named Corvane. She says it's a hundred years old. She says—" Hazel stopped. Started again, because saying it made it real, and real things had weight, and this one had the weight of a mountain. "She says there's a picture of me in it. Drawn before I was born."

Sef sat down across from Hazel. Slowly, the way you sit when your knees have opinions. "Open it to the page."

Hazel opened it to the page. The ink portrait looked back up at both of them: the same narrow jaw, the same scar through the left brow that Hazel had gotten falling out of a rigging at nine years old — except the drawing predated the rigging, the fall, the scar, the whole improbable business of Hazel Villalobos.

Below the face, in a hand like frost forming on glass, the words Hazel knew by heart now:

*On the last night of the waning moon, the bone-carver goes down. What was sold shall be sought.*

"And the rest of it?" Sef asked quietly. "The part about what was sold?"

"You tell me," Hazel said. "You're the one who taught me never to sign anything I haven't read."

That got a huff out of her — half laugh, half wound. Sef Villalobos had raised Hazel from the age of six, fished them out of grief the way she fished everything else out of the water: competently, without ceremony, and without ever explaining what she'd found. "Your grandmother," Sef said, "was the best woman I ever knew."

"I know."

"She was also desperate." Sef turned the bowl of stew aside as if it offended her personally. "You know the story we tell. That Gran won the smugglers' roads from the mountain in a card game."

"The story has a lot of detail for a card game," Hazel said. "There's a song."

"There's always a song. Songs are how we bury things." Sef folded her hands. For a long moment she just breathed, and Hazel realized she was deciding something she'd been deciding for a hundred years' worth of nights. "It wasn't cards. It wasn't even a bargain, not properly. The roads were failing, Hazel. Before you were born, before your mother, before any of us — the passes closed one winter and never truly reopened. No roads, no smuggling, no food that didn't come through customs at prices meant to starve us polite. Your grandmother went down into Thornfell with nothing but her pride and a lantern."

"And bought the roads open."

"Bought the *roads*, yes." Sef's voice went very careful. "What she paid with—"

"Don't." Hazel heard themselves say it, sharp and small. "Don't say it. If you don't say it, I can keep pretending the picture is a coincidence and the date is a printer's error and my whole life isn't a receipt."

Sef reached across the table and put her hand over Hazel's — the hand with the carving calluses, the hand that had been shaking since morning. "You were six years old," she said, "when they brought you to us. Do you remember why?"

Hazel did remember. Everyone said they shouldn't. A fever, they said. The village that had raised them first had simply — thinned. And then a stranger had carried a silent child down the coast road and handed them to Sef with instructions nobody would repeat.

"They told me you were touched," Sef said gently. "They told me you heard things. And I watched you, those first months, waiting for the madness, and it never came. You just listened. And the dead liked you, and the living trusted you, and I thought: well. Whatever this is, it's ours now."

"Aunt Sef."

"What she paid with," Sef said, "was a soul. Not hers." Her grip tightened. "The pale king under Thornfell collects souls the way I collect debts — patiently, and with interest. Your grandmother owed him passage for the roads, and the coin he wanted wasn't silver. It was the last dragon. Maerin, who used to sleep in the high passes when the passes still existed. Who was old, and tired, and ready to be done — but not *sold*, Hazel. There's a difference between ending and being ended, and your grandmother crossed it to keep us fed."

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere outside, gulls argued over the mackerel.

Hazel thought about the roads. Every road they'd ever walked. The salt routes, the hidden paths, the whole circulatory system of a family that had taken in a strange, silent child and never once made them feel like cargo. All of it paved with a dragon.

"I can't burn it," Hazel said.

"Hazel—"

"You want me to burn the only honest document our family owns." Their voice cracked and they let it. "Everything else is songs."

"If you go down there, you'll die, or worse, you'll be *kept*. The king doesn't forget a debt just because the debtor's grandchild shows up with good intentions. He'll hang your soul next to hers and light his court with you both." Sef stood, and her fear transmuted, as it always did, into ferocity. "Stay invisible. It's kept you alive. Small life, small shadow, safe. Burn the book. Carve spoons. Grow old annoying me."

And because Hazel loved her — because love was the whole problem, braided through everything like rope through a net — they almost did it. Their hand actually moved toward the hearth.

* * *

*We remember her going down.*

The dead do not speak all at once; they speak the way tide comes in, filling every low place. Hazel had not invited them. They came anyway, through the grain of the table, through the marrow-deep hum of the knife in Hazel's pocket, through the old whalebone pendant Sef had given them at fifteen.

*A cold night. A lantern with a crack in it. She sang the walking song to keep her courage up and her voice broke on the second verse, the way everyone's does.*

*The king asked her what she loved. She said: my family. He said: then pay in what you love least. And she thought of the dragon sleeping in the pass above her village, the old gray one who had once, in the flood year, held back the water with his wing so the children could be carried out —*

*She did not hate him for it. That is the worst part. She wept, and she paid, and she came home and wrote the song herself, so that no one would ever have to sing the truth.*

*We have been holding the door for you, carver. Since you were six years old. Did you think a fever takes a whole village and leaves one child?*

*Did you think you survived the wreck of the Kestrel's Mercy because you are lucky?*

*Descend.*

Hazel came back to the kitchen with tears on their face and their hand nowhere near the fire. Sef was watching them with the expression of a woman who has lost an argument before it started.

"They talked to you just now," she said. Not a question.

"They've been talking to me since I was six." Hazel closed the book. "You knew. You all knew what I am, and you let me believe I was just strange."

"We let you believe you were *safe*."

"No." Hazel stood. The chair scraped back, loud as a verdict. "You let Maerin believe he was forgotten."

They kissed Sef's forehead — she smelled of tar and tallow and thirty years of covering for the dead — and took the book, and the knife, and the whalebone pendant, and walked out into the waning-moon dark, where Wren Corvane was waiting by the sea wall with two packs and an expression that suggested she had packed for an argument and brought snacks.

"The passes close at dawn," Wren said.

"I know," Hazel said. "I hear they reopen for family."
