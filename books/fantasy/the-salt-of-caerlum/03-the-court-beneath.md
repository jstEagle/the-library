## 3. The Court Beneath

The mines began where the smugglers' maps ended, which Hazel had always understood to be the edge of the world. Wren Corvane, naturally, had other opinions.

"This is a ventilation shaft," they said, peering down into a black throat of stone that smelled of rust and old water. "Ventilation implies air circulation. Air circulation implies someone breathing."

"Or something," Hazel said.

"I'm so glad you said that out loud. I was trying very hard not to."

Hazel almost smiled. Three days of travel together had taught them things about Wren: that the scholar's fear arrived as footnotes, that their hands shook unless they were holding a book, and that when the shaking got bad enough, they recited dates under their breath like rosaries. Hazel had also learned—though they would have died before admitting it—that Wren hummed when she thought no one could hear, an old salt-song from the coast, and that hearing it made something in Hazel's chest go soft and strange.

They climbed down.

* * *

Wren counted the rungs to keep from counting the dark. Four hundred and eleven, four hundred and twelve—the iron bars slick beneath her palms, cold as a dead man's promise. Below, the flooded galleries spread out in black mirrors, so still that her own reflection looked back up at her with the patience of something waiting.

"You're quiet," Hazel said behind her.

"I'm composing my obituary. It's going well. 'She descended for scholarship and was eaten by geology.'"

Hazel snorted—a real laugh, brief and startled out of them, and Wren treasured it the way she treasured all rare texts: secretly, and with the certainty that she'd be ruined if caught.

Then the light changed.

It came up through the water first—a pale glow, sourceless, like moonlight that had forgotten the moon. The flooded tunnels brightened around them, and Wren saw what the light touched: doors. Hundreds of them, set into the rock at every level, some drowned, some dry as dust, all of them carved from a single seamless piece of bone-white stone.

"Hazel," she whispered. "These are mine entrances. This whole mountain is—" She stopped, because the word that rose in her throat was *ribs*, and she did not want it to be true.

"The mountain isn't hollow," Hazel said quietly. Their hand found hers in the dark, calloused and steady. "It never was. Stay close to me. And whatever hangs in there—don't touch it. Don't look at its face too long."

"What hangs in—"

They rounded the gallery's elbow, and Wren stopped composing her obituary, because she suddenly wanted very much to live.

Souls hung from the cavern ceiling like lanterns.

That was the only word for it. Small lights, each one wrapped in a cage of pale bone lattice, swaying gently in a wind that did not exist, shedding their soft white glow over courts of carved stone below. There were hundreds. Thousands. Each one flickering in a rhythm almost—but not quite—like breathing.

"Oh," Wren said. Her voice came out very small. "Oh, that's what we're doing here."

"That's what we're doing here."

* * *

Hazel walked through the court of the pale king with their eyes on their boots, because looking up meant seeing faces in the lanterns, and seeing faces meant hearing names, and hearing names was how you ended up owing the dead favors.

But they heard them anyway. They couldn't help it. A century of whispers pressed against their skull like rain on a roof: *cold, so cold, the king keeps us lit but never warm, tell the carver, tell the carver.*

*I hear you,* Hazel thought, hating that it was true. *I'm here for one soul only.*

The throne room opened like a wound in the mountain—a vast chamber walled in that same bone-white stone, and upon a dais of fused vertebrae sat the pale king.

He was beautiful the way frost on a window is beautiful, which is to say: briefly, and at the cost of everything behind the glass. Tall, slender, crowned in a circlet of knucklebones, his skin the color of candle-wax, his smile patient as erosion.

"Little carver," he said, and his voice was the sound the mines made when they settled. "And the reader of prophecies. How kind of the surface world to send me both a knife and its handle."

Wren stiffened beside Hazel. Hazel stepped half in front of her without deciding to.

"I've come about Maerin," Hazel said. "The dragon. Your contract's due to expire."

"My contracts do not expire. They are merely renewed." The king rose, and the lantern-souls above dimmed toward him like flowers toward sun. "You know what holds them burning? Not my will. The bargain. Signed by your grandmother's grandmother's hand, sealed in bone. The last dragon's soul, purchased fair, to buy your family's roads." He tilted his wax-pale head. "Would you like to see it?"

He gestured, and a table of stone slid from the floor between them, and upon it lay the contract—and beside the contract, a knife.

And beside the knife, blocks of bone, cut smooth and blank, exactly the size Hazel used for keepsake carvings.

"No," Hazel breathed.

"You've already guessed," the king said, delighted. "Of course you have. You hear the dead; surely you can hear a bargain's shape. Here is renewal's price: a soul for a soul. Bring me one, freely given, carved by your own hand, and Maerin burns another century, and your family keeps its roads, and everyone goes home." He spread his long fingers. "Or. Refuse, and I take the reader instead."

Chains moved in the dark before Hazel even turned—bone links, whispering fast, and then Wren was hauled off her feet and held suspended, kicking, her book tumbling from her satchel and skidding across the floor.

"Hazel!" she called, and her voice cracked on the second syllable, and something in Hazel's chest tore cleanly down the middle.

"Don't," Hazel said to the king. "Don't you touch her."

"Then trade," the king said softly. "Carve me a false soul. You're good enough—I've watched your work come down with the smuggled goods for years. Little birds, little boats, little names. Carve me a soul so fine the dead themselves would envy it, and I'll release the reader, and we needn't speak of Maerin again until the next renewal."

* * *

Wren hung in the chains and watched Hazel pick up the knife, and understood—with the terrible clarity that comes to scholars only at the moment all their research becomes flesh—that Hazel was going to do it. Of course they were. That was who Hazel was. They would carve their hands bloody before letting anyone be taken on their account.

*Good*, thought Wren. *Be predictable. Be loyal. I'll get us out of this; I always get us out of—*

No, she didn't. She had never once gotten anyone out of anything. She got people *into* things. She had gotten Hazel into a prophecy, into a mountain, into this.

"Hazel," she said. "Look at me."

Hazel didn't look up. The knife was moving. Bone curls fell away from the block, and even now—even here—Hazel's cuts were lovely, sure, tender. Wren watched them carve the shape of a small bird, and felt her heart break in a scholarly, footnote-riddled sort of way.

"There's no shame in it," the king murmured, drifting closer to Hazel, his voice dropping into honey. "But since you kneel at my table regardless, let me offer a better bargain. My final offer, carver."

Hazel kept carving.

"Stay," the king said. "Not as a prisoner—as a guest. As *ordinary*. I can give it to you, you know. I hold ten thousand souls, and every one of them once wished to be unremarkable, and I granted every wish. No voices. No prophecy with your face in it. No destiny hanging over you like weather. Just quiet. A workshop. A life where no one looks at you twice." His smile widened by a single tooth. "Isn't that all you've ever wanted? To be invisible, safely, forever?"

The knife stopped.

Wren saw it stop. Saw Hazel's shoulders curve inward, saw the bone bird tremble in their grip, and understood in one lurching moment that the king had not offered a temptation. He had offered a *diagnosis*. Every invisible year, every unremarkable day Hazel had built like a wall—here was someone offering to make the wall permanent, and calling it mercy.

"Hazel," Wren said again, gentler now. "Hey. Bone-carver. Look at me."

Slowly, Hazel raised their head. Their eyes were wet and furious and more alive than anything else in the entire mountain.

"I see you," Wren said. "I've seen you since the night I knocked on your door with a book full of your own face. And I am telling you, as a scholar, with citations: you were never invisible. Someone has been keeping you safe this whole time, and I don't think it was luck."

Something shifted in Hazel's expression—something that was not fear, and not surrender, and made the pale king take a half-step backward for the first time in a century.

"Your heart's wish," Hazel said quietly, setting down the bird. "You asked if carving a false soul was my price. It isn't."

"Then name yours," the king said, wary now.

Hazel reached down and picked up Wren's fallen book. Held it up so the king could see the page—their own face, tonight's date.

"My price," Hazel said, "is your signature."

And the lanterns overhead—all ten thousand of them—flickered at once, like a held breath.

* * *

The king went very still. "Explain."

"You bought Maerin's soul from my grandmother. Fair purchase, sealed in bone—your words." Hazel laid the open book on the stone table, next to the contract, next to the knife. "But a contract needs two signatures, pale king. And I've read your law in the margins of this book, because Wren reads *everything*, even the parts she thinks are decorative." They glanced up at Wren, chained and pale and grinning despite herself. "Tell him what happens when living bone touches a seal."

Wren's grin sharpened into something fierce. "Living bone voids it. Living bone can't be counterfeited, can't be coerced, can't be *carved by the dead*. It's the one material your whole court runs on that you can't touch."

"And whose bones," Hazel said, lifting the knife, "are more alive than anyone's, down here?"

The king's waxen face did something it had clearly not done in a hundred years.

It feared.

"Little carver," he said, and for the first time his voice was only a voice. "Think carefully. What you propose doesn't free the dragon. It frees *everything*. Every lantern. Every debt. Every road your family ever bought."

"I know," Hazel said.

Above them, ten thousand souls began—very faintly, very beautifully—to sing.

And Hazel Villalobos, nonbinary bone-carver of Thornfell, invisible for twenty-nine years, took the knife in their right hand and turned it toward their own left, and said:

"Hold still, Wren. This part I have to do myself."
