## 1. The Ledger Room

The ledgers of the Guild of Impossible Things were kept in a hall that smelled of tallow, dust, and old rain—three scents that had soaked so deeply into the stone that no amount of scrubbing could ever pry them loose. Vesper Fairweather had tried once, in his second year, when he still believed that effort was noticed here. He had scrubbed the flagstones of the archive on his hands and knees with lye and sand until his knuckles cracked and bled, and when he finished, Aldous Greywater had walked through the room without a glance, trailing mud from the street, and sat down at the long table to complain about the price of candles.

Vesper had learned since then. The guildhall did not want cleaning. It wanted keeping, in the way a grave wants keeping: quietly, without expectation of thanks.

He worked by lamplight even in the afternoon, because the windows were tall and narrow and let in only a gray, salt-thinned light that made the ink look like water stains. There were forty-one ledgers on the shelves behind him—forty-one, counting the one open beneath his hands—and each was bound in hide he preferred not to think about too closely, though he knew precisely what it was. Calfskin, Aldous always said. *Calfskin, boy.* And Vesper always nodded, because the alternative was to say what he sometimes suspected, which was that some of those covers were older than any calf born in the free cities, and smoother than leather had any right to be, and warm on cold mornings in a way that made the hair on his arms stand up.

The ledger open before him tonight—the thirty-seventh, covering the years of the Long Frost—was the worst sort. Debts of memory. He turned a page and read:

*To Madoc Thorn, cooper, of Saltmere Row: two years of childhood, taken against harvest-fail, repaid in grain that rotted ere spring. Balance outstanding: one summer, aged seven.*

The handwriting was not his. It belonged to a keeper dead eighty years, a woman named Ilse who had written with a beautiful, terrible economy, every letter like a nail driven flush. Vesper had spent long hours studying her hand, teaching himself its rhythms, because someone in this building ought to know what was written here. Someone ought to carry it. The elders certainly did not.

Below Ilse's entry, in a different ink—a brown-black, almost rust—someone else had added a note: *Called due by the court. Paid in full. Witnessed.* No name. No seal.

Vesper copied the entry into the working index, his own hand small and precise beside hers, and felt the particular ache that came with this work. Not grief, exactly. Something adjacent to grief. When you wrote down what a man had lost—when you gave the loss a page and a number and a line in an index—you made it real, and making it real meant admitting that no one would ever give it back. The guild's whole business lived in that gap between entry and repayment, and most of the entries, he knew now after six years of cataloguing, would never close. They sat in their books like seeds in frozen ground, waiting for a spring that had stopped coming sometime before he was born.

From the hall above, muffled through the floor, the elders had begun arguing again.

He could track the argument's shape without hearing the words. There was the low grinding bass of Corvo Hale, who had been Master of Accounts back when there were accounts worth mastering; the dry flutter of Maren Ostwick; the occasional sharp crack of Aldous's voice cutting through both like flint on steel. Three voices. Once there had been eleven elders, and before that—according to the portraits in the stairwell, all of them darkened to near-invisibility by centuries of smoke—there had been a full chapter, robed and ringed, whose names were carved into the mantel in letters that visitors were meant to recognize and be awed by.

Nobody visited anymore. Nobody remembered what the carvings said. Vesper knew, because he had copied them out one winter for something to do: nine founders, nine names, and beneath them a motto in the old script that no living member could read fluently except Aldous, and Aldous pretended he couldn't.

*We hold what others cannot carry.*

That was the motto. Vesper had translated it alone, cross-referencing three dictionaries and half a grammar, sitting in this same chair through the whole of a February. He had wanted, absurdly, to announce it—to stand in the great hall and say *I have read our founding words; I know what we are*—and he had gotten as far as the bottom of the stairs before the sound of the elders' voices stopped him. They were laughing about something. He couldn't remember what. He had gone back up to the archive instead and written the translation in the margin of the index, where no one would ever see it, and that, he understood later, was the truest act of his life so far: carrying the guild's meaning to the only person in the guildhall willing to receive it, who was himself.

The lamp guttered. He trimmed the wick and kept writing.

* * *

"—and I am telling you, Corvo, that there is nothing left to administer." Maren's voice came thin and sharp down through the boards. "Three clients in a year. Three. The cooper's widow paid out her husband's balance last month and wept doing it, but she is the first in four years to settle anything."

"Then we adjust," Corvo rumbled. "We have adjusted before."

"To what? We cannot sell memories to people who have none to spare. The free cities have been picked clean, Corvo. Look around you. Half the houses on Guildsman Street stand empty, and the other half are full of families who sold something to get through the famine years and have been walking around with holes in themselves ever since. You want to sell to them? Sell them what—their own losses?"

"There is always the court," Corvo said, and the silence that followed had a texture Vesper knew well. It was the silence of men standing at the edge of a thing they did not name.

It was Aldous who finally spoke, and Vesper leaned back in his chair to listen better, the way he always did when Aldous talked about the court, because the old man's voice changed—not softer, exactly, but more careful, each word set down like a foot placed on river ice.

"The court does not buy anymore," Aldous said. "The court collects. There is a difference, and those of you too young to remember it should thank whatever gods you don't believe in."

"We are not *young*—"

"You are younger than me, which is the same thing." A chair scraped. "I will say this once more and then never again. The mountain has a new king. Whatever arrangement the founders struck, whoever sat beneath Highmoor when the seals were cut—that reign is over. And the thing that replaced it has sent no heralds, made no demands, collected nothing in eleven years. Either it has forgotten us—" a dry sound, almost a laugh, "—or it is saving us for later. I do not care for either possibility, and I especially do not care for gentlemen who propose going to knock upon its door to ask for work."

"The guild is dying, Aldous."

"The guild has died before. It is a habit of ours."

Vesper smiled despite himself, alone in the lamplight, and then felt foolish for smiling, and then felt angry at feeling foolish, and put all of it away and went back to the index. This was the trick he had learned: to fold everything—hurt, hope, the strange warmth of eavesdropping on people who would not speak to him directly—into the work. The work received it all without complaint. The work never looked through him.

* * *

By the time the argument upstairs collapsed into its usual stalemate—heavy footsteps, doors, the clink of decanters—the light through the high windows had gone the color of pewter and the first rain had started against the glass, a fine salt-hazed drizzle off the flats. Storm weather. The marshes beyond the city wall would be drowning, and the gutters of Guildsman Street would run white with brine, and anyone caught out in it would come home looking like they'd been weeping seawater.

Vesper rose, stiff from hours in the chair, and made his rounds.

This was the part of the day no one had assigned him and no one had ever thanked him for. He went shelf by shelf, pressing each ledger's cover flat with his palm, checking the wax seals on the thirteen volumes that were supposed to stay sealed—thirteen, always thirteen, and he had never once been told why, and had stopped asking. He checked the iron box under the floorboard where the founder's signet slept in its nest of wool. He checked the window latches against the wind. The hall breathed around him, settling, creaking, alive in the way old buildings are alive, and he moved through it with the lamp held high, casting his own enormous shadow ahead of him across the shelves.

His shadow was taller than he was. Most things about him were smaller than they should have been. That was the joke of his position in the guild, if it was a joke: the keeper whom nobody saw, cataloguing debts that nobody remembered, in a guild whose entire purpose was to make sure nothing was forgotten. The irony had teeth. He had stopped mentioning it years ago, after the third time Corvo had blinked at him blankly across the supper table and said, *And you are... the Fairweather lad, yes. The nephew of—someone. Someone.*

Not even his family name had survived intact here. Just *the Fairweather lad*, filed under *someone*, in a mind that could recite the interest terms of a century-old soul-contract without hesitation.

He paused at the thirteenth sealed ledger—it sat apart from the others, on its own shelf, at the height of his chest—and rested his hand on it a moment longer than he needed to. The cover was warm. It was always warm. On nights like this, with rain ticking against the glass and the storm gathering its breath out over the salt flats, he liked to imagine it was warm because somewhere inside it, something was still alive.

Then he took his hand away, because that thought led to worse thoughts, and worse thoughts led to the margin-notes and the translations and the growing private suspicion that the guild he served was not a guild at all but a wound that had learned to keep accounts of itself.

* * *

He ate supper alone in the archive, as he did every night, on a stool drawn up to the end of the long table, with the thirty-seventh ledger for company. Bread, hard cheese, a pear going soft. From the great hall came the sounds of the elders' evening: decanter, dice, Maren's laugh, brief and rusty, at something Corvo said. They were not cruel men. That was the hardest thing to hold onto, some nights. They were not cruel. They were simply full, the way a house is full—with their own histories, their own losses, their own long memories of better decades—and there was no room left in them for a quiet boy with ink-stained fingers who asked sensible questions and got answers like *ask Aldous* or *it's not your concern* or, worst of all, nothing at all.

Loneliness, Vesper had decided, was mostly a matter of bookkeeping. It was a debt carried on the wrong side of the page. So he entered it nowhere, acknowledged it to no one, and balanced it nightly against the one asset he possessed outright: the ledgers themselves, all forty-one, every entry, every seal, every rust-dark marginal note in Ilse's careful hand. No one else alive could say that. Whatever else the guild had lost—its clients, its wealth, its meaning, its name in the mouths of the free cities—the memory of the memory-trade itself lived now in one place, and that place was him.

They thought he was invisible. Let them. Invisibility was a kind of access.

He was copying the final entry of the night—*to the estate of Sera Vane, one name, freely given, balance unredeemed, debtor deceased*—when the storm arrived properly.

It came off the flats like a wall, slamming rain against the high windows hard enough to rattle the lead, and the lamps in the archive dipped, and somewhere below, the guildhall's ancient door boomed in its frame. Vesper looked up, quill suspended, a drop of ink trembling at its nib.

The door boomed again. Then a third time—but the third was different. Not the wind. Not a fist. Small. Quick. Desperate. The knocking of someone who had been knocking for a long time already, out there, in all that dark and all that salt.

The elders' noise from the great hall had stopped entirely.

Vesper set down his pen, marked his page with a ribbon, and stood, heart beating strangely, listening to the rain and to that small persistent sound—knock, knock, knock—coming up through the stones like something rising from under the world.

No servant would answer. There were no servants. Corvo's knees were bad, Maren was deep in her cups, and Aldous—Aldous was nearest the door, but Aldous had gone silent in a way that Vesper, after six years of listening to the man through floors and walls, had learned to distrust profoundly.

The knocking came again. And beneath it now, faintly, threaded through the roar of the storm, Vesper heard something else: a voice. High. Young. Speaking words in a language he did not know—and yet the sound of it went through him like a hook through cloth, snagging on something old and deep, pulling him toward the stairs before he had decided to move.
