## 4. What the Guild Sells

The rain had not stopped in three days, and neither had the child's singing.

Vesper heard it through the floorboards of the Ledger Room as he worked by lamplight, that wordless melody in the old tongue threading down through the guildhall like water finding cracks in stone. Above him, muffled by beams and plaster, Aldous had sealed the boy in the east attic with a warding chalked on the door — a ward so old its script had been forgotten by everyone but the eldest members, and perhaps even by them. The song came anyway. It always found its way down.

He tried to concentrate on the ledger open before him: *Account of Mirelle Ostrehan, weaver, aged sixty-one years. One summer of her girlhood, sold to redeem her husband's gambling debt. Interest accrued: two springs, one first snowfall.* The ink had faded to the color of weak tea. Someone had pressed a flower between these pages once — he could see the ghost of it, a brown stain shaped like a five-petaled star — and Vesper found himself wondering, as he often did, whether Mirelle had ever missed that summer. Whether she had felt a hole in her life the shape of a meadow and never known why.

*You're cataloguing stolen goods,* said a voice in his head that sounded uncomfortably like his own.

He turned the page. He was good at turning pages. It was, perhaps, the only thing he had ever been truly good at.

Three days since the child arrived. Three days since Aldous had gone grey as ash at the sound of that language and barked orders like a man watching a fire start in his own house. The elders had met twice behind closed doors, and both times they had fallen silent when Vesper passed, conversations folding up like moths' wings. He told himself it meant nothing. He told himself a great many things.

Tonight, unable to sleep, he had come down to the Ledger Room because it was the one place in the world where he felt competent, and competence was a kind of comfort. Here he was not the youngest member, the least gifted, the Fairweather nobody could quite explain keeping on the rolls. Here he was the keeper of the ledgers, and the ledgers were everything.

Or so he had believed, until three days ago.

He lit a second lamp and pulled the oldest volume from the iron cabinet at the back — the Founder's Ledger, bound in hide no tanner would claim, its spine cracked like riverbed clay. Guild law said this book was to be read only by the First Chair. Guild law said a great many things. But Aldous was sleeping, or pretending to, and the rain covered all sounds, and Vesper had spent thirty years obeying rules written by people who never once explained them.

The first page bore the founder's seal: a circle bisected by a vertical line, stamped deep enough to dent the parchment beneath. *Halric Vane, First of the Guild of Impossible Things.* Below it, in a hand so elegant it looked less written than grown:

*Let it be recorded that the Guild trades in what cannot be weighed, and therefore cannot be missed until it is gone.*

Vesper read that line four times. As a boy, learning his letters from the guild's copybooks, he had thought it beautiful — a credo, almost holy. *What cannot be missed until it is gone.* Now, with a mute child singing above his head, the words curdled. What could not be weighed. What could not be missed.

A soul.

He kept reading. The lamplight guttered; the rain drummed; and page by page, year by year, the true history of his beloved guild unspooled before him like something rotten being peeled.

* * *

**One hundred and ninety years ago**, the ledger said, in an entry dated the third of Thawmonth:

*A delegation from the Court Beneath Highmoor attended the hall under flag of parley. Their envoy wore gloves of grey silk and removed them only once, to demonstrate the nature of our trade. For one memory — freely surrendered, witnessed, and sealed — the Court offered gold beyond the guild's then-annual revenue. The memory chosen for demonstration belonged to a volunteer of the congregation: a fisherman who gave up the drowning of his brother. He left the hall smiling. He will not remember why he cannot stand near deep water.*

*The vote to formalize trade with the Court carried, nine hands to two.*

*The two dissenters are recorded here by name, per their request, that history might know the guild was never unanimous.*

Their names were Ilse Corvane and Tam Broadstairs. Vesper searched the rest of the volume for those names and found them nowhere else. Dissenters, it seemed, did not linger. He wondered if they had resigned, or been resigned, or simply stopped appearing at meetings until their chairs were given to others. The guild had many ways of making people disappear without ever touching them. He knew that better than anyone.

The entries grew denser after that. Memories, mostly — small ones at first, then vast. A widow selling the last year of her marriage because grief was more than she could carry. A soldier selling the face of every man he'd killed. A mother, and Vesper had to set the book down when he reached this one, a mother selling the memory of her daughter's laugh because the daughter had died and the laughing ghost of it woke her every night at the hour of the death.

Where did the memories go? He had assumed, in his innocence, that the guild destroyed them. That they were dangerous things, best burned. But the entries were meticulous about provenance, chain of custody, final disposition. And the final disposition was always the same.

*Transferred to the Court Beneath Highmoor, per standing contract.*

The court bought memories the way other courts bought grain or timber. Not occasionally. Constantly. Season upon season, decade upon decade, the caravans went out from the guildhall loaded with casks of other people's lives, and gold came back, and the guild grew rich, and the free cities spoke of the Guild of Impossible Things with reverence, and no one — no one in any record Vesper could find — asked what a king beneath a mountain wanted with ten thousand strangers' childhoods.

Until the names started.

* * *

**One hundred and forty-two years ago**: *Sold to the Court: the name "Aveline," borne by a cooper's daughter of Saltmarsh, together with all answering of that name. The girl is recorded henceforth as "the woman formerly Aveline." She has agreed to answer to nothing.*

Vesper read that entry aloud just to hear how mad it sounded. A name. They had sold a *name*. And the entry was initialed by the Second Chair of that era — Wren Kavanagh, his own great-great-grandfather, whose portrait hung in the front hall with a look of mild, kindly disappointment, as if perpetually observing that the younger generation wasn't reading enough.

He had loved that portrait as a child. He had stood beneath it and promised it things.

The names became a regular commodity. Whole villages, it seemed, sometimes sold their names collectively during famine years — traded their identities for grain, becoming nameless places that maps eventually lost track of. Vesper had always wondered, idly, why the salt marsh settlements had such strange blank stretches in the historical record. Now he knew. The blanks were inventory. The blanks had been *shipped*.

And then, near the middle of the volume, in an entry dated the winter of the Long Frost, the handwriting changed. It became smaller. Careful. Almost ashamed.

*This night the Court made its gravest offer, and the Chair accepted.*

*They ask no longer for memories alone, nor names, nor years of life, though these we continue to supply. They ask for souls entire — held in trust, they say, not consumed, merely collateral against debts too great for mortal surety. The Court swears the held souls suffer nothing. That they dream, and are tended. That they may be redeemed.*

*I do not believe them. I write my disbelief here where no auditor will look, because the alternative is the guild's ruin, and I am a coward, and the gold keeps our doors open and our members fed.*

*God forgive us. We have begun selling people.*

Vesper sat very still. Somewhere above him, faintly, the child's song changed key.

He understood, suddenly, why Aldous had gone pale. Why the ward on the attic door. Why the elders met behind closed doors and fell silent at his footsteps. The child was not a stray. The child was *stock*. Held collateral, three centuries old, called due — and the guild, which had spent generations pretending its ledgers were a sacred trust, was going to have to decide what its sacred trust was actually worth.

But that horror was still ahead of him. There was worse in the book. Worse, and older, and closer to home.

* * *

The candle had burned down two fingers when he reached the section headed **Debtors of Standing** — clients who had borrowed rather than sold, who owed the guild impossible things. He skimmed the familiar merchant houses, the minor noble lines, the occasional archmage's apprentice who'd pawned a decade of his training. Routine. Then a name caught his eye not because it was grand but because it was *his*.

*Fairweather.*

His hand stopped moving.

*Fairweather, Edmun, cartographer of Greyharbor. Debt incurred: purchase of one (1) memory of the sea, sold to fund passage inland. Debt outstanding: the memory itself, unredeemed at time of debtor's death. Per contract clause nine, the debt passes to bloodline.*

Edmun Fairweather. His great-grandfather. Vesper knew the name from the family Bible — knew that Edmun had come inland from Greyharbor, had founded the branch of the family that stayed with the guild, had died young of lung-rot in the map rooms. Vesper had grown up on stories of Edmun the cartographer, who drew coastlines so lovingly you could smell the salt off them.

Except Edmun had sold his memory of the sea. Every coastline he ever drew was drawn from hearsay and longing, a man mapping waters he could no longer remember — and the guild had held that memory in its vaults, collecting interest on it, generation after generation, while his descendants served the institution that owned it. While *he*, Vesper, dusted its shelves and polished its locks and called it home.

His fingers were shaking. He turned pages faster now, hunting, dreading, unable to stop.

*Fairweather, Sela. Debt: one name, partial ("the second name," usage restricted). Outstanding.*
*Fairweather, Joss and Mirren. Debt: three years of shared life, held jointly. Outstanding.*
*Fairweather, Vesper, infant. Debt incurred at birth: [entry incomplete]. Surety pending. Recorded by order of the First Chair.*

He read that last line six times. *Recorded by order of the First Chair.* Aldous. Aldous had entered Vesper himself into the ledger before he could walk — before he could *do anything* — with a debt so secret the amount hadn't even been written. An incomplete entry. A blank where his life should be itemized.

The inheritance he had treasured — the family tradition of service, the pride of being guild-born, the sense that he belonged somewhere even if no one noticed him — it rose up in his chest like floodwater and turned black. He had never belonged to the guild. The guild had *held title* to him. He was not a member. He was collateral that had learned to keep books.

Above him, the singing stopped.

The silence was worse. It dropped through the floorboards like a stone through still water, and in it Vesper heard, distantly, the knocker at the guild's front gate — not the polite triple-rap of clients but a slow, ceremonial pounding, iron on iron, spaced like a funeral bell.

He closed the Founder's Ledger. His palms had dampened the ancient cover, and the seal of Halric Vane gleamed wetly in the lamplight, the circle and the line, the mark he had traced a thousand times as a boy and never once questioned.

*We are not a guild,* he thought, rising on numb legs. *We are a warehouse. We are the fence for a thief beneath a mountain, and I have spent my life guarding the loot.*

The knocking came again, patient and enormous. Upstairs, doors were opening; voices stirred along the corridors; someone was calling for Aldous. Vesper snuffed the lamps and stood a moment in the dark among the shelves of other people's stolen lives, listening to his heart.

Whatever came through that gate, he already knew one thing with perfect certainty: he was done believing the ledgers were sacred.

He climbed toward the light of the front hall — and found the elders gathered there, every face lit orange by torchlight, every gaze fixed on the gate, where through the iron grille a figure in storm-grey waited with a banner folded under one arm. Even from the stairs, Vesper could see the device stitched into the silk.

A circle, bisected by a vertical line.

The seal of Highmoor.
