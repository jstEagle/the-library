## 7. The Ferryman's Price

The salt marshes ended where the Greywater began, and the Greywater began where the maps grew honest about knowing nothing.

Vesper had never seen the river before. He had catalogued it, of course—*Greywater, tidal, unnavigable above Tarn's Reach, ferry rights disputed since the Salt War*—but ink is a flat country, and he had not understood until now that a river could look like a wound that had healed wrong. It lay wide and brown-silver beneath a sky the color of old pewter, breathing with the tide. Reeds hissed along its margins in long sighing waves, like an audience shushing itself. Somewhere out in that grey expanse, fish moved. Somewhere further, the mountain waited.

Ashen stood at his hip, her small hand wrapped in two fingers of his, watching the water with the particular stillness she saved for things that mattered. Since the marshes, since the flats where she had begun to murmur those fragments of the old tongue—words like *sorrow-of-long-standing* and *the-taste-of-iron-in-a-promise*—she had spoken little else. But she watched everything. She was learning the world again, he thought, or learning it for the first time; he no longer knew which.

"There," he said. "That must be her."

A boat was coming downriver against the current, which should have been impossible and was therefore exactly what they needed. It was a low, black-hulled thing with a patched lateen sail the color of a dead man's lips, and it moved with the unhurried certainty of something that had never once asked permission of the water. At its stern stood a broad figure in oilskins, one hand lazy on the tiller, a clay pipe trailing smoke behind her like a fuse.

The boat came about with a groan of canvas and slid into the reeds as gently as a knife going back into a sheath. The woman looked them over without stepping ashore. She was perhaps fifty, weathered to the exact brown of her own hull, with a face like a cliff that had decided, after long argument, to remain standing. One eye was pale grey and sharp. The other was milk-white and fixed on nothing—which somehow made Vesper feel more observed, not less.

"You're the guild boy," she said. Her voice was gravel rolled in honey. "The one the enforcers are asking after. Three coppers a head for word of you, five for the child." She sucked her pipe. "Ten if someone brings you in breathing."

Vesper's mouth went dry. "Then I'll pay more than three coppers for the crossing."

"Everyone pays more than three coppers for my crossings, love. That's rather the point of me." She spat placidly over the side. "Brannoc. You'll have heard worse said of me, most of it true. And you're Vesper Fairweather, ledger-keeper, youngest of the Impossible Things, which these days is a title like being the tallest dwarf. And this—" the pale eye swung to Ashen, and something in Brannoc's face shifted, some deep keel adjusting, "—is the held soul herself."

Ashen looked up at the woman and said something in the old language. Four words, soft, almost conversational.

Brannoc went very still. The pipe stopped smoking.

"What did she say?" Vesper asked.

"Never you mind." But Brannoc's voice had lost its performance, just for a moment, and underneath it was something older and wearier. She looked at Ashen a long time. Then she looked at Vesper, and the sharp eye was sharp again. "Right. Business. You want the Greywater crossed and the high road toward Highmoor, which is mad, but mad clients pay best. My price isn't coin. Coin I can steal. My price is a memory."

"I know," Vesper said. "I asked after you in Tarn's Reach. They said you take payment in the past."

"They say it like it's a scandal. It's simpler than coin, love. Coin can be counterfeited, stolen, taxed. A memory is only ever yours." She tapped her temple with the pipe stem. "I've got a chest below decks full of other people's yesterdays. Some nights I open it and live whole lives that were never mine. It's how I keep from being lonely, and how I make sure I'm never only myself. So. What have you got worth carrying?"

Vesper had prepared for this. He had spent two days in the marshes preparing for this, cataloguing himself the way he catalogued everything, laying out his memories like wares on a stall, weighing each one. He was a guildsman. He knew how trade worked. You never offered the first thing you loved.

"The day I was accepted into the Guild," he said. "I was nine. My father carried me through the snow on his shoulders to the guildhall door. It was the proudest day of his life."

Brannoc considered him with something close to pity. "That's a trinket. I'll carry you across the river for that and not a mile further."

"The night I first balanced a ledger perfectly. Every debt matched."

"A trinket."

"The storm last winter when Aldous told us stories of the founder, and we all sat up past midnight drinking spiced wine—"

"Boy." Brannoc climbed down onto the muddy bank at last, boots sinking, and stood before him. Up close she smelled of tar and tobacco and river-mud and, faintly, inexplicably, of lavender. "You're doing what your guild taught you. Offering the pretty things off the top shelf, the ones you can spare." The white eye seemed to look through his skull at the shelves themselves. "But I can see what you're actually holding behind your back. Everyone holds something behind their back. So let's not insult each other. What won't you give me?"

And Vesper found, to his horror, that his eyes went—took only half a heartbeat, barely a flicker—to the memory he had been guarding hardest. The summer he was eleven. The loft above the chandler's shop. The smell of tallow and rain through the slats. A girl named Merren who had shown him that a person could be seen, wholly seen, by another person, and that it would not kill him. Who had laughed at his seriousness until seriousness became a game instead of armor. Whose family had moved away in autumn, and whom he had never gone after, because the guild did not go after things, and because he had already begun, even then, to file his life instead of living it.

"No," he said aloud, before he could stop himself.

Brannoc smiled, slow and terrible. "There it is."

"It's not for sale."

"Everything is for sale. Your own ledgers taught you that, didn't they? Isn't that the whole rotten catechism?" She stepped closer. She was shorter than him, but she took up more space than any person he'd ever stood near. "Here's what I'll do, guild boy, because the child looked at me kindly and I'm a sentimental old wreck under the salt. I'll name my real price, and you can refuse, and I'll row you both to the free cities instead and wish you luck dying there. My price is one memory. Any memory. But it has to be a *load-bearing* one—one of the beams your house stands on. Not ornament. Structure. Because where you're going, love, is under a mountain that eats people who travel light in the soul. You'll need to be lighter than you've ever been. So give me the beam."

The tide sighed. The reeds hissed. Ashen's small hand tightened around his fingers.

"Why?" Vesper asked. His voice came out cracked. "Why does it matter to you what I give?"

Brannoc looked at him for a long moment, and the performance fell away entirely, and what was underneath was so tired and so kind that it frightened him more than the sharpness had.

"Because thirty years ago I sold a memory to a guild broker to buy my mother a coffin," she said. "And the broker was polite, and fair, and wrote it all down beautifully in his ledger, and I signed, and I walked home lighter, and it wasn't until years later that I realized the hole where that day used to be was shaped exactly like my mother's face. I don't remember her, boy. I remember that I don't remember. So I take memories from people like you—not for cruelty. Because every one I take, I understand a little better what your guild has been doing to the world for three hundred years, and every one I take makes me angrier, and the anger keeps the boat moving." She knocked out her pipe against her palm. "Now. The beam. Or the free cities. Choose."

* * *

He chose on the bank, in the mud, with the river breathing beside them, and it did not feel like a choice at all—it felt like the ledgers had always known, like somewhere in the sealed pages of the guildhall there was a line with his name on it and this moment written in advance in a fine antique hand: *Item: one summer, surrendered.*

Brannoc built the ritual right there on the shore, because she refused to do it aboard—"bad luck to take a man's past over deep water"—and because, he suspected, she wanted him to see every part of what he was agreeing to. She drew a circle in the mud with the edge of an oar. She set a small tin box in its center, plain and dented, its lid scarred with hundreds of scratches, tally marks or names or both. From her coat she took a stoppered vial of something that glittered like water at dusk.

"Hold the box. Think of it. All of it. Don't flinch from the details—the details are what makes it yours, and the details are what makes it worth anything. When it's ready, it will come loose on its own. Like a tooth."

"And then I won't remember."

"And then you won't remember. You'll remember that there's a gap. You'll remember the shape of the hole, because the mind hates a gap and will worry it forever, like a tongue finding a missing tooth. But the summer itself goes in the box, and it comes with me." She paused. "Last chance to run for the free cities."

Vesper knelt. Ashen knelt beside him and put her hand flat on his knee, and whether it was comfort or witness he couldn't say, and didn't deserve to wonder.

He thought of the loft. He made himself think of all of it. The rain ticking the slats. The tallow smell. Merren's laugh, which started low and broke upward, like something taking flight. The afternoon they mapped the constellations visible through the broken roof tile and she invented a constellation of her own, a lopsided thing she called the Ferryman, because she said someone ought to be paid for carrying people across dark places. He thought of the last evening, her family's cart loaded at dawn, and how she had pressed a carved wooden fish into his hand and said *come find me someday*, and how he had said yes and meant it and never gone, because the ledgers were always waiting, because diligence was safer than longing, because he had chosen the guild the way cowards choose anything—by staying.

The grief arrived ahead of the loss, which he hadn't expected. He was mourning it while he still had it, holding the whole summer in his mind's hands and feeling it grow warm, then lighter, then strange, the way a word repeated too many times goes hollow. Merren. *Merren.* The name was beginning to sound like a sound and not a person.

"Don't fight it," Brannoc murmured. "It knows the way out."

It came loose. There was no better word. Something behind his ribs unhooked, and the summer poured out of him through his palms into the tin box, and he felt it leave the way you feel blood leave a cut—warmth, then wet, then a cold draft where the warmth used to be. He heard, distantly, his own voice say something—he would never know what—and heard Brannoc answer softly, and heard the lid of the box scrape shut.

Then he was kneeling in the mud by a grey river, and there was a gap in him.

It was exactly as she had promised: he remembered that there had been something. A season. A warmth. He could feel the shape of the absence with perfect clarity, the way you can feel the exact contour of a limb that is no longer attached. Someone had been there, in that season. Someone had laughed. He had been, briefly, seen. The knowledge remained like the impression left in a bed after the sleeper rises—evidence, but empty. He reached for a name and found a smooth wall where the name should have been, and his hand, without his instructing it, closed around a small carved object in his coat pocket—a wooden fish, worn smooth by years of thumbing, whose origin he suddenly could not recall at all though he had evidently carried it long enough to polish it with grief.

"What did I lose?" he asked. His voice sounded far away, like someone calling across water.

"A summer," Brannoc said quietly. She was capping the vial, not looking at him. "You'll thank me or hate me later. Sometimes both. Come on—the tide turns in an hour and it waits for no one, least of all fools bound for mountains."

She lifted Ashen aboard first, gentle as handling cargo marked fragile, and the child turned at the rail and looked back at Vesper with those old, old eyes, and said one word of the elder tongue. Soft. Almost tender.

"What did she say?"

Brannoc hesitated at the tiller. For a moment Vesper thought she wouldn't answer. Then: "There's no clean translation. Closest I can give you—and don't ask how I know, that's another story and another price—is *'you are poorer now, and kinder.'*" She pushed off from the bank. The black sail caught the wind with a crack like a whip. "Sit down, guild boy. Hold the child. And whatever you do, don't lean over the side tonight. The Greywater remembers everyone who's ever paid it, and it likes to show them."

The river took them. Behind them the marshes sank into the haze; ahead the water ran brown and silver toward the distant smudge of the high country, where a mountain stood up against the clouds like a debt waiting to be collected. Vesper sat in the bow with Ashen tucked against his side, her weight warm and real, and turned the wooden fish over and over in his fingers, trying to remember why his chest ached, why the ache had a name-shaped hole in it, why he could not stop touching the smooth worn wood as though it were the only proof remaining that some part of him had once been rich.

By the time the stars came out, Brannoc had changed course without announcement, swinging the boat north of the high road toward a landing she called the Weeping Stair, and it was only when Ashen stiffened against his side and spoke a single urgent syllable of warning—the same one, he would realize too late, that she had spoken on the bank—that Vesper saw the lantern-light of three boats keeping pace with theirs in the darkness downstream.

Enforcers' boats. Flying no colors at all, which was how the Guild of Impossible Things preferred its violence done.

"Ah," said Brannoc, around her pipe, steering calmly into the dark. "There's the rest of your fare, love. Knew you'd be good for it."
