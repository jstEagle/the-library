## 8. Roadside Confessions

The river had a name once. Brannoc was almost certain of it.

She stood at the tiller of her flat-bottomed ferry, watching the brown water slide beneath the hull, and tried to remember what the fishing villages downstream called this stretch—the slow bend where the current went quiet and the willows leaned in like gossips. There had been a word for it. A good word, round in the mouth. Her father had used it every morning of her childhood, pointing with his pipe before he cast his nets: *There goes the—*

Gone. The word was gone, and the place where it had lived in her was smooth and empty as a stone from a streambed, and she knew—she *knew*, the way you know a scar—that she hadn't lost it. It had been taken. Sold. Carried off in a ledger by men who smelled of ink and camphor, and paid out to some gray-faced client beneath a mountain who wanted to know what it felt like to love a river enough to name it.

"Sandbar ahead," Vesper said behind her. "Port side. Or—I think port side. I've been wrong about rivers before."

"I know about the sandbar." She didn't turn around. "I've run this crossing since before you could write your own name, ledger-keeper."

"That's probably true," he admitted. She could hear him moving among his bundles, checking on the child. Ashen slept in the bow under an oilcloth, curled small, one muddy hand visible at the edge of the tarp like something washed ashore. The boy had barely made a sound all day. He watched everything, though. Brannoc had felt those eyes on her back for six hours, weighing her the way you weigh a stranger's knife.

The ferry scraped, shuddered, slid free of the bar. Brannoc leaned into the pole and thought about turning the boat around. She had done the arithmetic three times already and it never came out any better: one guild clerk, one impossible child, half her fee paid in advance, and the other half sitting inside Vesper Fairweather's head like a coin in a locked box. Memories were risky currency. They leaked. They changed hands in ways that couldn't be witnessed or sworn to. And guild clerks, in her experience, were exactly as honest as their ledgers required them to be and not one drop more.

But the guild was dying. Everyone said so. And a dying guild might do anything to refill its coffers—including selling a child twice.

"You keep looking at me like I'm going to push you overboard," Vesper said.

"The thought has crossed my mind."

"I'd float," he said. "I'm mostly paper."

Despite herself—a thing that happened more often around him than she liked—Brannoc snorted. She turned then and looked at him properly for the first time since they'd shoved off at grey dawn. He was younger than she'd first taken him for, twenty-five maybe, with the kind of face that made itself forgettable on purpose: plain brow, ink-stained fingers, eyes that flinched away from direct attention the way a debtor flinches from a knock. But there was a stubbornness in the set of his shoulders that didn't match the rest of him. He sat with Ashen's bundle like a man guarding a fire in the rain, hunched over it, refusing to acknowledge the weather.

"You paid me half in memories," she said. "I want to know which ones."

Vesper's hands went still over the oilcloth. "Why?"

"Because I need to know what you can still afford to lose. Because if the enforcers catch us and offer you your life back for the child, I want to know how much of your life is even left to sell." She let the pole drag, letting the ferry drift with the current, giving the question room to breathe. "And because I'm curious what a man keeps when he thinks no one's counting."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed—a short, startled sound, like a bird flushing from cover.

"No one's ever asked me that. Not which ones. The elders take what they take and initial the page and that's the whole ceremony." He settled back against the gunwale. "A winter morning when I was nine. My mother singing in the kitchen—just the song, not my mother; they were careful about that, they always are. And the smell of bread baking. Apparently someone below the mountain wanted to know what hunger satisfied feels like from the inside, and my ninth winter was... sufficient."

Brannoc kept her face still, but something in her chest pulled tight, the way rope pulls when a knot slips.

"They took the song but left your mother."

"They're precise. That's the horror of it. If they were sloppy, you could tell yourself you'd just forgotten, the way people do. But it's surgical. There's a hole shaped exactly like bread-smell and singing, and everything around the edges is untouched, and you spend years running your thumb along it wondering what fit there." His voice stayed level, recitation-flat, the voice of a man reading his own inventory aloud. "I catalogued the debt myself last year. Entry four thousand and eleven. I initialed my own theft and filed it under *satisfied*."

The river ran on. Somewhere downstream a bittern boomed in the reeds, that hollow pumping cry like a heart too large for its body.

"My brother sold his wedding day," Brannoc said.

Vesper looked up.

"To your guild, thirty years ago, when the herring failed and we owed the salt-tithe and there was nothing else in the house worth a copper." She found she was gripping the pole harder than the current required. "He came back with a purse full of guild-mint and a smile, and he thanked them. Thanked them, Vesper. Because from his side of the table it was a kindness. They'd bought the worst day of his life—no, don't look like that, that's the trick of it, that's the genius—they only buy the sweet part. The vows, the dancing, her face in the candlelight. They left him the marriage. They left him waking up next to a woman he loved with no memory of why, no memory of choosing her, just this terrible unearned tenderness with no root to hold it. He spent ten years being devoted to a stranger. She spent ten years married to a man who looked at her like a gift he couldn't remember opening."

"What happened to them?" Vesper asked quietly.

"He drowned himself in the spring floods. She runs the ferry license office in Corrow now. Signs the papers that let boats like mine operate." Brannoc turned back to the water. "I've been meaning to ask her, someday, whether she remembers being happy. Whether the shape of it is still there even with the middle scooped out. I've been meaning to ask her for thirty years and I have never once managed to walk through her door."

* * *

They made camp that night on a shingle bank where the river widened and slowed, under a stand of black poplars that rattled in the wind like a room full of people whispering. Brannoc built a small fire—dry reed-matting and driftwood, smoke thin and blue—and cooked eels she'd netted at dusk, skewered over the coals. Ashen ate his share in careful, silent bites, watching the flames with an expression Brannoc couldn't read: not wonder, exactly. Recognition. As though fire were an old acquaintance met again after a long separation.

Vesper sat apart, as he always did, with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth across his knees—not reading it, just holding it, the way a man holds a sleeping animal he's afraid will wake.

"You should eat while it's hot," Brannoc said.

"In a moment."

"You've said 'in a moment' four times since noon. Your moments are very long."

He glanced up, and in the firelight she saw how worn he looked, the shadows under his eyes gone the color of wet slate. "I'm trying to decide whether you're telling me the truth."

"About the eels?"

"About your brother. About—" He gestured vaguely at everything: the fire, the marsh, the child, the whole crooked road they were fleeing down together. "You took our coin. Half our fee. You knew what the guild was, what it does, and you signed on anyway. Why help us? Why now?"

It was a fair question, and she resented him for asking it fairly, without accusation, in that mild bookkeeper's voice that made every hard thing sound like an entry pending verification.

"Because I hated your guild before you were born," she said, "and hating something for thirty years is its own kind of loyalty. You get attached to the hate. You arrange your life around it." She fed a stick to the fire. "When you walked onto my deck with that child and that ledger and that look on your face—the look of a man who's just read his own family name in the debt column—you weren't a guild clerk anymore. You were a man the guild had robbed, same as everyone. I could carry that. I couldn't carry the other thing."

"The other thing?"

"A guilty man buying passage with stolen goods. I've ferried those. They always come back." She pointed the skewer at him. "You haven't come back yet. Three days running from your own order, sleeping rough, eating my cooking without complaint—which, for a guild man, is practically sainthood—and every mile south you get poorer-looking and more determined. It doesn't add up. Guild men don't spend themselves down for strangers."

Ashen spoke from beside the fire. Not words—sounds, low and rising, the old language moving through him like wind through a ruined house. He was looking at Vesper when he did it, and Vesper listened with his whole body, leaning forward, lips slightly parted, as though the sounds were water and he was dying of thirst.

"Do you understand him?" Brannoc asked.

"Almost." Vesper's voice had gone strange. "That's the worst part. It's like hearing a conversation through a wall—every third word comes through, and the ones that do are..." He shook his head. "Tonight he said something with *salt* in it. And something I think means *before*. And a word I've seen in the oldest pages of the ledger, the pre-founder entries, in the margins where nobody signs. I think it means *home*. But home the way a mountain means home. Underneath."

The poplars whispered. Ashen finished speaking and lay back against his bundle, and within minutes his breathing slowed into sleep—but his hand, Brannoc noticed, had found the hem of Vesper's coat and closed around it, and Vesper either didn't notice or pretended not to, and did not move away.

* * *

Second watch, past midnight. Brannoc sat up alone by the embers while the other two slept, and allowed herself the ritual she permitted once a year, on the anniversary of the sale, and apparently also now, on a gravel bank in the middle of nowhere, because a soft-spoken clerk with ink on his fingers had pried her open like an oyster and now she couldn't shut.

She tried to remember her brother's wedding.

This was the discipline of it, the penance: not to reach for the memory, but to reach for the hole. To map its edges precisely, the way Vesper described, the way all the sold-to did. She knew the church had been St. Verany's-on-the-Marsh, because the building was still there and she'd walked past it a thousand times. She knew the month, because the tithe records survived. She knew there had been rain, because Tam had complained about it for weeks afterward—or had he? Was that a real memory or a story the family built around the absence, scaffolding over the gap? This was where the exercise always came apart: you could not trust anything adjacent to the wound. The guild's knife was clean. Everything near it was suspect.

What she remembered—what remained—was this: standing in the doorway of the nave, nineteen years old, watching her brother at the altar, and feeling absolutely nothing. Not grief, not joy, not boredom. Nothing, where something enormous should have been. A congregation of faces all wet-eyed with happiness, and her dry as salt flats, thinking *why am I here, why do these people matter to me,* and being frightened of the nothing, and hiding the fright behind a yawn.

She had told no one for years. Then one night, drunk on plum brandy, Tam had confessed his own nothing to her—his own blank door in his own head—and they'd sat up until dawn comparing absences like two men comparing scars, laughing and weeping by turns, and that night was the only memory she had of her brother that felt wholly hers, unsold, unbought, entire.

And she had hated the Guild of Impossible Things with a purity of feeling she otherwise reserved for nothing in this world.

Except.

Except that tonight, listening to Vesper talk about his mother's song, she had caught herself thinking: *the usurper has my brother's wedding day. Right now, under Highmoor, some gray courtier wears Tam's happiness like a coat.* And the hatred had shifted, tilted, become something else—something with a direction and a purpose, aimed south toward the mountain instead of inward at herself.

She didn't trust it. Hope was a currency too, and she'd seen what it did to people.

Behind her, in the dark, Vesper stirred. She heard him sit up, heard the soft scrape of the ledger's oilcloth, and then his voice, low, not meant for her:

"I'm sorry. I don't even know if you can hear me. But I'm sorry. Whatever they took—it wasn't theirs. None of it was ever theirs."

A pause. Then Ashen's voice answering in the dark, sleep-thick, in the old tongue: two syllables, gentle, unmistakable even without translation.

Brannoc had grown up on the water, and she knew every kind of yes the world contained—the yes of a rope coming taut, the yes of a tide turning, the yes of a passenger stepping aboard. She knew what that sounded like.

She banked the fire and lay back and did not sleep.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of tin. Brannoc was pushing off from the shingle when Vesper stopped mid-motion, one hand on the mast, staring north along the river road where it climbed toward the distant smudge of the free cities.

Smoke. Not hearth-smoke—too much of it, too many columns, rising from the direction of Corrow and the waystation towns beyond, a dark stitchwork against the pale sky.

"Refugees," Brannoc said, following his gaze. "Or fires. On this river, this time of year, it's usually both."

Vesper's hand tightened on the mast until the knuckles went white. "Corrow," he said. "Your license office. Your sister-in-law."

Brannoc said nothing. She pushed the pole down into the brown water, and the ferry swung its nose south, toward the smoke, because there was nowhere else to go—and because, though she would have cut out her tongue before saying it, some buried and treacherous part of her needed to see what the mountain's war looked like when it arrived in a town that had once, long ago, sold something precious to make ends meet.
