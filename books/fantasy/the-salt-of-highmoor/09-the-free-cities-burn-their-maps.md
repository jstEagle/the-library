## 9. The Free Cities Burn Their Maps

The waystation city of Corvel's Rest sat at the meeting of three roads like a knot in a rope, and by the time Vesper and Ashen reached its gates, the knot had begun to tighten.

They heard it before they saw it: the sound of too many people in a place built for a quarter as many. A low tide-noise of voices, the creak of overloaded carts, a child crying somewhere with the flat, exhausted persistence of a child who had been crying for days. The smell reached them next—woodsmoke and wet wool and the sweetish rot of vegetables left too long in barrels—and beneath it all, faint but unmistakable to a nose raised among ledgers and lamp-oil, the mineral bite of salt.

The same salt they had crossed. It clung to everything now, Vesper thought. His boots. His hair. His dreams.

Ashen's small hand found his without her looking up from the road. She had not spoken since the marshes—not in her own tongue, not in anything—but she did not let go either, and that was its own kind of sentence.

"Corvel's Rest," Brannoc said behind them, spitting into the dust. "Half the smugglers I know were born in that ditch. Other half were caught there." She shifted the pack on her shoulders and squinted at the walls. "Gate's open. That's either generous or desperate."

It was both. The guards at the gate barely looked at them—two more mud-caked travelers in a river of them—and waved them through with the weary indifference of men who had stopped counting days ago.

Inside, the city was drowning.

Not in water. In people.

* * *

They found lodging the only way it could be found: Brannoc bullied a tavern-keeper whose name was Ostrec, an old client of hers, or perhaps an old debtor—the distinction had blurred somewhere on the road—into giving them a room above the stable that smelled of hay and horse and, underneath, of the particular staleness of a space where frightened humans had slept in shifts. Vesper paid with coin he did not have to explain. He was learning that coin bought less here than stories, and that he had fewer of both than he'd believed.

He went down to the common room that evening to listen.

Listening was, he realized, the one skill the guild had actually taught him well. Thirty years of keeping ledgers meant thirty years of hearing what debtors said around their debts—the pauses, the deflections, the way grief arranged itself into respectable sentences. He knew how to sit in a corner with a cup of thin cider and become furniture. It was the closest thing to his old life he could still perform: invisible, diligent, taking inventory.

Except this time the ledger filled itself with horror.

The common room was full of easterners. He could read it in their coats, cut in the style of the river valleys; in the vowel-shapes of their speech; in the particular quality of their silence when a stranger approached. They were farmers, mostly, and millers, and a family of tanners whose hands were stained the color of autumn oak-bark even after whatever washing the road allowed. They had come west ahead of something, and they spoke of it the way sailors speak of weather—constantly, compulsively, in fragments, because the whole of it would not fit in a mouth.

"The soldiers came through Merrow's Ford on the ninth day of harvest," a woman was saying to anyone who would hear. She said it like a prayer, or like a line she needed to keep exactly right, as though forgetting a word might lose the place itself. "On the ninth day. There were four hundred souls in Merrow's Ford. My father kept the toll-book. I can tell you every name."

"Then write them down," said a man near the fire, not unkindly. "That's how you keep a place alive. Names and records."

And the woman's face did something terrible. It didn't crumple. It emptied.

"There is no record," she said. "I have been to the archive at Dunmark. I have been to the parish chest at Sallow Bridge. There is nothing. No toll-book. No parish rolls. No gravestones—the churchyard is grass, smooth grass, like sheep have grazed it for a hundred years. My father kept the toll-book." Her voice climbed and steadied itself with visible effort. "I held it. I inked my own marriage in it. And the clerks looked at me with such pity, such *kind* pity, and told me there had never been a Merrow's Ford."

The fire popped. Somewhere a cart axle screamed against stone outside.

"They didn't burn the books," she said, quieter now, which was worse. "Burning you can see. Burning leaves ash, and ash means someone was angry, and anger can be answered. This was—" She searched for the word and found one that made several listeners flinch. "This was *tidy.*"

Vesper set down his cider. His hands were cold.

* * *

He should have gone upstairs. He knew that even then, some part of him standing apart with a quill behind its ear, noting: subject distressed, exit advisable. Instead he crossed the room and asked, gently, whether she would tell him about the roads—which passes were watched, which wells were safe—for he was traveling toward the mountains with a child.

She told him. Of course she told him; kindness buys information cheaper than gold ever will. Her name was Maren Ostvane, late of Merrow's Ford, and she drew the roads in spilled ale with a blunt finger while her children slept against her sides like moored boats. And because she was generous with her knowledge, and because grief makes people honest past all prudence, she also told him the rest.

"It isn't only the papers," she said. "It's the remembering. You'll think I'm mad."

"I keep accounts," Vesper said. "Madness is a trade I respect."

Something in his tone must have reached her, because she laughed—a single dry note with no floor under it.

"People forget us," she said. "Not in the ordinary way. I mean my cousin Tolla, who has known me forty years—we shared a bed through three fevers—when I found her in Dunmark she looked at me the way you look at a beggar who claims kinship. Polite. Pained. She said, 'You remind me of someone.' Forty years, and I was a *reminding.*" Maren's finger stopped moving in the ale. "But here's the thing that keeps me awake. She remembered everything else perfectly. The price of wool. Her husband's debts. The flood year. Every fact of the world stayed nailed down except the ones that held us. As if someone came along with careful fingers and lifted out only our stitches."

Vesper's mouth had gone dry. He thought of pages in the guild ledgers—entries he had catalogued ten thousand times without once reading them properly. *One village's remembrance, sold. One town's tenure in the world's memory, transferred to vault, Highmoor. Terms in perpetuity.*

He had thought those were figures of speech. Guild shorthand. He had filed them between a man's sold childhood and a widow's purchased good name, and never once wondered where a village's memory *went*, or who carried it, or what it weighed.

"How many," he heard himself ask. "How many places?"

"That we know of? Nine." She counted them off on stained fingers, and each name landed like a stone in still water. "Merrow's Ford. Sallow Bridge. Cold Harrow. The two Fenwick villages—they went together, same night, the witnesses agree. Thistlewaite. Marle. Osterlin. The ferry-town at Graywater." She paused. "And Highmoor Undercliff, though that one was long ago, before my grandmother's time. The first one, the old men used to say. The pattern-setter."

*The first one.*

Vesper stood very carefully, thanked her, pressed his last silver into her hand over her protests, and walked up the stairs with his body moving on rails while his mind tore through thirty years of cataloguing like a flood through a paper warehouse.

* * *

Ashen was awake. She was always awake when he came back; he suspected she did not sleep so much as wait, in the patient way of things that had waited a very long time already. She sat cross-legged on the narrow bed with the blanket pooled around her, watching the candle with eyes the color of deep water over slate.

He sat down across from her. For a while neither of them moved.

"I need to check something," he said. "Will you stay here?"

Her chin dipped. Then, after a moment, she reached out and touched his wrist—once, lightly, the way you steady something you are afraid will blow away—and withdrew. It was the longest conversation they'd had in words.

The ledgers were in his pack, wrapped in oilcloth, because he had not been able to leave them. He had told himself it was evidence, or leverage, or simple habit—the guild's books were the guild, and he was the guild's keeper, and a keeper does not abandon the keeping. Now he unwrapped them with hands that shook only a little, and turned to the oldest volume, the one bound in grey hide no tanner would claim, and found the section he had always called *Anomalous Transfers* because calling it anything truer would have required questions.

There it was. Entry 114 in the founder's cramped, beautiful hand:

*Received of the Court Beneath, in exchange for the continuation of our compact: one town entire, its tenure in the world's remembering. The place shall stand; the world shall not hold it. Terms: perpetual, renewable at each century's turning. Witnessed and sealed.*

And beneath it, in a later ink, a clerk's annotation—his own predecessor's, maybe his own teacher's, neat as a hem:

*N.B. — The Court calls these "withdrawals." We have always recorded them as sales. Determine which is accurate before the next renewal falls due.*

Determine which is accurate. Three hundred years of clerks, and not one had dared.

Vesper closed the book and sat in the dark with it against his chest, listening to the horses stamp below and Maren Ostvane's voice still ringing in his skull—*burning you can see*—and understood, finally, in the place where understanding stops being information and becomes a weight you carry in your spine:

The usurper had not learned erasure. The usurper had *bought* it. Wholesale, centuries ago, from the guild—from Aldous and his elders, from the founder with his beautiful hand, from every generation of clerks including the boy who had grown up proud of the ledgers' elegance. Every tidy village. Every smooth churchyard. Every cousin who looked at you with polite pity. Not conquest. *Inventory.* The court beneath the mountain was furnished with the remembering of nine towns, and it had paid for them with whatever coins the guild had wanted, and the guild had wanted to live forever, and so it had.

He was complicit. Not by act—by inheritance, by salary, by the thousand small diligences of a man who kept the books that made the trade possible. He had sharpened the instrument daily and called it stewardship.

Ashen was watching him. He hadn't heard her cross the room. She put her hand flat on the ledger's cover, over the entry he couldn't make himself reread, and looked at him with an expression no child should have had cause to wear: recognition. She knew this book. Perhaps she knew this *entry.*

"Ashen," he said quietly. "Highmoor Undercliff. Was that—were you—"

The door banged open.

Brannoc filled it, backlit by the corridor lamp, and for the first time since the salt flats Vesper saw fear on her face—not the sharp working fear of pursuit, but the slower kind, the kind that has had time to settle in and pay rent.

"You need to come downstairs," she said. "Now. And bring your fancy reading, because there's a man below telling the room what happened to Graywater ferry-town, and Vess—" She swallowed. "He says the soldiers didn't do it. He says they just arrived to collect what was already owed. He says there's paper. Old paper. With a seal on it."

Vesper felt the ledger grow heavy as an anvil under Ashen's hand.

"What seal?" he asked, though the cold in his gut had already answered.

"Three towers and a closed eye," Brannoc said. "Ring any bells?"

It rang every bell the guild owned. It was stamped into the leather of every ledger Vesper had ever kept.

The founder's seal. Here, three hundred years and half a kingdom away from the hall where it belonged, riding at the head of a debt-collection notice for a town that no longer existed—except, Vesper understood with a lurch, it did exist, it was sitting in a vault beneath a mountain, and somewhere tonight the usurper's clerks were drawing up the next renewal.

The next century's turning had fallen due. And the guild's paper was marching east ahead of the armies.

"We leave at first light," Vesper said. "Before. Before they post it."

"Post what?" Brannoc asked.

He looked down at Ashen, whose hand still rested on the grey cover as if holding shut a door.

"A call for the return of stolen property," he said. "And child—I'm beginning to understand exactly what they'll say was stolen, and why they'll be right."
