## 10. The Child Speaks

The waystation city of Corrow's Reach announced itself by smell before it showed itself: woodsmoke and wet wool and the particular sour tang of too many frightened people pressed into too few streets. We had been three days on Brannoc's cart since leaving the ferry at the Saltwash crossing, three days of juddering wheels and the child's silence, and I had begun to think of that silence as a kind of weather—permanent, atmospheric, something we would simply have to live beneath.

Then, on the morning we came down out of the hills and saw the city's smoke smeared against a low gray sky, she spoke.

Not the old language. I had grown almost fond of the old language in its way—the clicking consonants like stones shifting in a riverbed, the vowels that seemed to come from somewhere deeper in the chest than any word I knew. It was music without meaning, at least to me. But this was different. This was a single word, rough-edged and misshapen as a knapped flint, spoken in a voice so small I nearly missed it under the axle-groan.

"Hungry."

Brannoc hauled the reins so hard the cart slewed sideways and nearly put us in a ditch.

I will not pretend I handled it well. I dropped the ledger satchel I'd been holding—I carried it everywhere now, even to sleep, even though Aldous was dead and no one was left alive who would punish me for letting it out of my sight—and I scrambled around the canvas flap and stared at her as though she had produced the word from some conjurer's hat. She sat among our bundled blankets with her knees drawn up, mud still dried in the whorls of her ears from the marshes, looking back at me with an expression of perfect, reasonable expectation. As if she had been speaking all along and I had only just learned to listen.

"You—" My voice cracked. I tried again. "You said hungry."

She considered this. Her head tilted, the way a bird's does when it is deciding whether a thing is food or threat. Then, carefully, laying each sound down like a person crossing ice:

"Yes. Hungry."

*Yes.* She had *yes*. She did not, apparently, have *bread*, or *please*, or anything else in the vast architecture of the common tongue, but she had *yes*, and she had *hungry*, and she had assembled them for us on a rutted road outside a refugee-choked city while the usurper's shadow crept westward across every map in the free lands.

I laughed. I couldn't help it—it came out of me high and strange, half sob, the laugh of a man who has been holding his breath for weeks without knowing it. Brannoc looked at me as if I'd gone mad, and perhaps I had, a little. Madness is a reasonable response to impossible things; it is practically the guild's founding principle.

"She talked," I told her. "Did you hear? She *talked*."

"I heard." Brannoc's voice was flat, but I had learned by then to read what lived underneath the flatness—the wariness of a woman who had sold pieces of herself to the guild once and trusted nothing that arrived wrapped in wonder. "Words are dangerous things coming out of that particular mouth, Vesper Fairweather. You'd do well to remember it."

But she stopped at the next farmstead anyway, and traded two silver bits for bread and hard cheese and a jar of honey, and when she handed the bread back to the child, she watched with the same careful attention she gave to loading a crossbow.

The child ate like someone who had recently remembered eating was possible. And between bites, she practiced.

That was how I thought of it afterward—not speaking, exactly, but practicing, the way a musician works through scales. She would point at a thing and try its name: *cart*, *wheel*, *horse* (she called the mule *horse*, and the mule, to its credit, did not object). She would point at me and say *Vesper*, which came out of her as *Fes-fer*, and I found I liked the mangling of it more than I could explain. Nobody had ever shortened my name before. In the guild hall I was Fairweather, or the boy with the ledgers, or nothing at all. *Fes-fer* was mine. She had made it hers.

We camped that night in a hollow above the road, within sight of Corrow's Reach's walls, because Brannoc refused to enter a walled city after dark with a child whose face might be worth more than either of our lives. The fire was small. The wind came off the salt flats behind us carrying that mineral dryness that gets into everything, into your teeth, into the stitching of your coat. Highmoor salt. Even here, days east of the mountain, the land tasted of it.

I was showing her the fire—pointing, saying the word, having her shape her mouth around it—when Brannoc spoke up from where she sat sharpening her knife against a whetstone.

"Ask her about the mountain."

I looked over. The whetstone had gone quiet.

"Why?"

"Because everyone in that city tomorrow is going to be asking each other about the mountain, and most of them won't like the answers." She nodded at the child. "And because she knew things in the marshes, Vesper. Don't tell me you've forgotten. She pointed the way past the sinkholes when the mist came down. She knew the ferry landing was burned before we saw the smoke. Whatever they did to her down there—or whatever she was, before they did it—she remembers Highmoor. Ask her."

I turned back to the fire. The child was watching me, firelight pooling in her eyes, waiting with that bottomless patience of hers. I realized suddenly how young she might be—six, seven years old by the size of her, though the guild's ledgers had taught me long ago that age and souls keep poor company. Three centuries ago her soul had been signed away, and yet here she sat, small enough to fit inside a rain barrel, warming her hands at a smuggler's fire.

"Ashen," I said.

I don't know where the name came from. It rose in me the way the word *hungry* had risen in her—unbidden, inevitable. Perhaps it was the salt flats, the ash-gray light of them. Perhaps it was something older than either of us, some thread in the sealed page of the ledger that had wound itself around my sleeping mind. But the moment I said it, her whole body changed.

She went very still. Not the stillness of fear. The stillness of a locked door hearing its key.

"Ashen," she repeated. And then, in her own tongue—a soft cascade of those river-stone syllables, but gentler now, almost tender, as if she were greeting someone she had missed for a very long time—she said it again, and again, touching her own collarbone, her own throat. "*Ashen.*"

"That's you," I whispered. "That's your name."

She nodded. And then she did something I will carry to whatever grave is allotted me. She reached across the fire, took my hand in both of hers—her fingers were cold, always cold, cold as ledger-paper—and pressed my palm flat against her chest, over her heart, and held it there.

Under my hand, faint as a moth's wingbeat, I felt something pulse. Not quite a heartbeat. Something underneath the heartbeat. A second rhythm, slower, deeper, like the tide under waves. Like a bell heard through a wall.

The soul. The held soul, the collateral, the debt three centuries due. I was feeling it. It was real, and it was *hers*, and it had been sitting in a vault beneath a mountain while a little girl walked the world wearing it like a coat that didn't fit.

"Gods," Brannoc breathed. She had come around the fire without my noticing. Her knife lay forgotten on the stone beside her. "What was that? What did I just see?"

"I don't know." My voice came out wrecked. "I don't know."

The child released my hand and looked at each of us in turn, and I saw her struggling with something—the shape of a thought too large for the few words she owned. Her brow furrowed. Her hands moved, sketching shapes in the air, and she slipped back into the old language, rapid and frustrated, then caught herself and forced it down.

"They," she began. Stopped. Started again. "They know."

The fire popped. Somewhere off toward the city a dog barked, thin and distant.

"Who knows?" I kept my voice gentle, though everything in me had gone taut as wire. "The people in the mountain?"

She nodded. Then she shook her head, frustrated, and tried another angle. She pointed east, toward Highmoor, toward the dark shoulder of the horizon where the mountain would stand invisible beyond a hundred miles of salt and scrub. She pointed at the ground. At the fire. At her own eyes, and then at mine.

"*They know,*" she said again, and this time the words had weight behind them, the weight of something rehearsed, something she had been turning over in her mouth for days, saving. "They know Fes-fer. They know Ashen. Coming."

Her grammar collapsed there, but the meaning landed like a hammer. I felt Brannoc go rigid beside me.

"How does she know that?" Brannoc's hand had drifted back toward her knife. "Vesper. How does a child on a smuggler's cart know what's happening under a mountain we haven't reached?"

"The soul," I said slowly, working it through as I spoke. "Aldous said the vessel was still in the vault. If the court holds her soul, and she's walking around out here wearing the rest of her—" I touched my own chest, mirroring where she'd pressed my hand. "Maybe the connection runs both ways. Maybe they can feel her the same way I just felt her. Maybe they've felt her every step since the marshes."

"Then they've felt her since the day you took her out of the guild hall."

"Then they've known where we are the whole time," I finished. The words tasted of salt and iron. "Every mile. Every ferry crossing. They let us come."

The wind shifted, and for a moment the fire leaned hard sideways, shadows wheeling across the hollow like startled birds. The child—Ashen, Ashen, I would not stop saying it to myself, hoarding it—watched us argue with the composure of someone who had already grieved this argument and moved on. When we finally fell silent, she spoke again, and her voice had steadied, found its footing.

"Fes-fer." She pointed at me. Then at the eastern dark. "You go. They wait."

"No," I started, "we both—"

"They wait *you*." Her jaw set. She had never used that tone before—had never had the words to use it—and there was something almost funny in the sight of a seven-year-old scolding me in her fourth day of English, except that nothing about it was funny at all. "They want Fes-fer. Ledger-man." She patted the satchel at my hip, and I understood, with a lurch, that she had known what I carried all along. Had maybe known it before I did. "They want book. Want Ashen." She drew her small fist closed, slowly, and held it up between us. "Take. Both."

*Both.* The ledgers and the child. The record of every bargain the guild ever struck beneath that founder's seal—including, somewhere in the later pages, in a hand I had not yet learned to recognize, my own family's name. The usurper hadn't sent armies after a runaway girl. He had sent armies after his paperwork.

"He's right, then," Brannoc said quietly. "The herald. He wasn't bluffing. He wants the ledgers as much as the child—maybe more. A man who holds every contract the guild ever signed holds every debtor in the free cities by the throat. Half the old families east of the Wash sold something once. Names. Years. Memories. If he calls those debts—" She didn't finish. She didn't need to. I had spent my life cataloguing exactly what the guild held over the world, entry by entry, in a room nobody visited. I knew better than anyone what those pages weighed.

Ashen was watching me. Waiting. And I understood, in that moment, that she had not brought me a warning at all.

She had brought me an invitation.

*They wait you.* Not as prey. Or not only as prey. The usurper's herald had stood in the guild hall and offered terms, and terms meant conversation, and conversation meant a door. Everything in me that had survived twenty-six years of being overlooked—the clerk's instinct, the ledger-keeper's eye for the fine print, the small stubborn pride I took in knowing every line of every impossible debt—rose up and insisted there was a reading of this that ended differently than the one Brannoc feared.

Or everything in me was a fool constructing a beautiful lie out of desperation. The guild had made a science of that, too.

"We sleep," Brannoc said at last, into the silence, and her voice brokered no appeal. "In shifts. Tomorrow we decide whether we go around Corrow's Reach or through it, and either way we're moving before the sun clears the hills. And Vesper—" she jabbed the whetstone at me like a scepter, "—if that child says one more word about mountains waiting for you, you wake me. Immediately. I'm not burying you out here. The ground's too salty. Nothing rots proper."

She rolled into her blanket with the finality of a slammed door.

Ashen did not sleep. She sat beside me at the fire's edge, close enough that her shoulder rested against my arm, and together we watched the coals breathe. After a long while, she took a stick and drew in the ash at our feet: a shape like a mountain, crude but unmistakable. Beneath it she scratched lines—rows of them, orderly, deliberate—and I caught my breath, because I knew those lines. I had spent ten years of my life among them.

Ledgers. Rows upon rows upon rows.

Then, below the mountain and the rows, she drew two small figures. One tall, one small. Standing together at the mountain's foot. And around them both she drew a circle, pressing hard, grinding the stick into the earth until the circle was deep and black and unbroken.

She looked up at me. In the firelight her face was grave and certain and impossibly old, the way it had been the first night in the doorway of the guild hall, mud to the knees, speaking the language before kingdoms.

"Together," she said. The word was new—she must have gathered it from our talk on the road, hoarded it the way she hoarded all of them—but she placed it perfectly, like a jewel in a setting. "Together, Fes-fer."

"Together," I agreed.

And far to the east, beyond a hundred miles of sleeping salt, something beneath Highmoor turned a page.
