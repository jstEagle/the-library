## 12. The Ascent to Highmoor

The mountain did not grow larger as we approached it. That was the first wrongness.

I had expected the opposite—the way a tower swells as you walk toward it, or a storm builds itself against the horizon. But Highmoor kept its own counsel, gray and vast and patient, and each morning when we woke on the road it seemed no nearer than the morning before, though our legs told us otherwise. Ashen walked between Brannoc and me with her small hand in mine, and sometimes she would stop and look up at that mass of stone and cloud and make a sound—not a word, not quite a hum—that made my teeth ache. The old language lived in her throat like weather. It was older than the mountain, Aldous had said, and I was beginning to believe him, because the mountain seemed to know it too.

The road climbed through what had once been mining country. We saw the evidence of it everywhere: rusted rails vanishing into bracken, the mouths of adits sealed with timber gone soft as bread, great spoil-heaps furred with moss like sleeping animals. And towns. Towns with their windows shuttered, their wells capped with stone, their names worn off the signposts—except for one word, freshly painted, white as bone, on every wall we passed.

*Vhalen.*

That was the usurper's name. We had heard it first from refugees in the waystation city, spat like a seed-pip or whispered like a prayer, depending on who was speaking. Now we heard it nowhere else at all. Not once in three days of walking did anyone say another name aloud. Not the name of the town. Not their own.

* * *

The first inhabited village sat in a fold of the hills where two streams met, all slate roofs and low chimneys breathing woodsmoke into the drizzle. We came down into it near dusk, hungry, Brannoc's ferrywoman's instincts reading the settlement the way she read water—where the current ran, where it eddied, where it might swallow us.

"Keep your hood up," she murmured to me. "And the little one close. If anyone asks, we're salt-buyers out of the flats."

"No one will ask," I said. "No one asked in the last village."

"That's what worries me." She shifted her pack. "In the last village they didn't ask because they didn't care. Here—" Her eyes moved along the street, and I followed them. Doors stood open. People stood in them. Not crowds, exactly. Just figures, one to a doorway, watching us come with expressions that were not unfriendly and not anything else either. The look of people waiting for a coach they have already decided will be late.

An old woman stepped from the well-house as we passed, offering us bread without a word. It was good bread, dark and dense, and I ate because refusing felt more dangerous than accepting. When I thanked her, she blinked at me slowly, twice, like an owl in lamplight.

"You're welcome," she said. And then, with the mild contentment of someone reciting a lesson learned so long ago it has become a comfort: "All things belong to Vhalen."

Brannoc's hand found my sleeve. I felt her fingers tighten there, and then, deliberately, loosen.

"Yes," I said carefully. "So we've heard."

"He is very generous." The woman smiled. Her eyes were the pale blue of skimmed milk. "He gave us back our work when the mines closed. He gives us sleep. He gives us forgetting, when remembering hurts." She patted my arm as if I were a child at a funeral. "You'll understand soon. Everyone understands, coming up the mountain."

Ashen pressed herself against my leg. The old woman looked down at her, and something flickered across her face—a twitch, almost pain—and then it smoothed away again like water closing over a dropped stone.

"Pretty child," she said, but her voice had gone strange, thin as paper over a flame. "Where did you find a pretty child with no name?"

"She has a name," I said.

"No she doesn't." Gentle. Absolute. "I can see. There's nothing written on her. Nothing written on you either, salt-man. You're all blank as new pages." She laughed then, a small dry sound, and shuffled back toward her door. From its threshold she called after us, cheerful as a market bell: "All things belong to Vhalen!"

We did not stop in that village. We walked through the middle of it with our heads down and came out the far side into the dark, and only when the last chimney-smell had faded did Brannoc speak.

"They're not afraid of us," she said.

"I noticed."

"That's worse. Frightened people lie to you, or chase you off, or rob you. These ones—" She shook her head. "My mother came from mining country. Ironhold, up north, before the pits went under. She used to say a company town has two kinds of silence: the silence before payday and the silence after. This is neither. This is a silence with a name painted on it."

Behind us, faint on the wind, we could hear them still: the whole village saying it together as they banked their fires, unhurried, unafraid, a lullaby in reverse. *All things belong to Vhalen. All things belong to Vhalen.*

Ashen pulled my hand until I knelt. In the darkness her eyes caught some light that wasn't there, and she touched my chest, over my heart, and then drew her finger slowly down through the air in front of me—a long vertical stroke, like a line being ruled in a ledger.

She had never done that before. I did not know what it meant. But I thought of the ledgers in the guild hall, the sealed page under Aldous's floor, my family name written among the debtors three centuries dead, and I thought: *she can see the writing on me too.*

I took her hand and we walked on.

* * *

Higher up, the villages emptied out entirely—or rather, they did not empty, which frightened me more. We passed a hamlet on the fourth day where every house was furnished, every hearth swept, every table laid for a meal no one would ever eat. A cradle by one window held a carved wooden horse. Boots stood paired by doors, toes pointing out, as if their owners had intended to come back. Dust lay thick as snowfall, undisturbed except where foxes had crossed the main street in neat diagonal lines.

"They left in a hurry," Brannoc said.

"They didn't leave at all," I said. "Look at the boots. Look at the table. Nobody packs for leaving and sets the table besides." I turned a slow circle in the empty street, and my voice came out smaller than I wanted. "There's nothing here, Brannoc. No bodies, no graves, no packed carts. They're just—gone. Lifted out like stitches pulled from cloth."

"Or remembered out." She said it flatly, but I heard what moved underneath it. "Back in the city, those refugees. The ones whose villages weren't erased from the maps—they said the maps were fine. It was the *remembering* that got taken. People who'd traded there, married there, buried kin there, waking up with holes where those years used to be." She kicked a rusted rail spike into the grass. "Maybe this is what's left when the taking finishes. Maybe these people are still alive somewhere, wearing other people's childhoods, serving wine in a palace under the rock."

The thought settled over us both like the drizzle. We camped that night in a barn whose stalls had been mucked out and never refilled, and I lay listening to Ashen breathe and trying not to imagine the subterranean court waiting for us, lit with stolen dawns, staffed by miners' wives and smiths' sons who no longer knew their own hands.

Somewhere past midnight I woke to find Brannoc sitting up, sharpening her belt-knife with slow, unnecessary strokes.

"Can't sleep?" I asked.

"Thinking about turning back."

I sat up. The words landed in my stomach like stones dropped down a well. "Brannoc—"

"Don't." The whetstone paused. "Don't argue yet. Just listen, because I've earned the right to say this and you've earned the right to hear it." She did not look at me. "I carried you two onto my boat because you paid me fair, and because I owed the guild a debt of my own, and because—gods help me—it felt good to point my prow somewhere that mattered. I've held the gate for you. I've lied to enforcers with your face two feet from mine. I'm not sorry for any of it. But I keep counting, Vesper. Two of us, and whatever's under that mountain. An army at Highmoor's skirts. And a guild behind us that wants you dead for running, which means even if we win, there's nowhere to go home *to*."

"There'll be a home if we win. That's the point of winning."

"Is it?" Now she looked at me, and her eyes in the dark were bright and hard as wet riverstones. "Because here's what I see. I see a man carrying a child up a mountain full of people who've forgotten their own names, to steal back a soul from a king who buys memories by the barrel. And I keep asking myself: what does he think happens after? Does he think the ledgers burn? Does he think the guild says thank you? Does he think he walks out of that court with the child and his own name intact and everything square?" She shook her head slowly. "Nothing's ever square with traders, Vesper. You taught me that yourself, the day you paid me for passage with a piece of your childhood. You don't get things back. That's the whole shape of the world. You pay, and pay, and what you buy gets smaller every time."

I wanted to be angry at her. I reached for anger the way you reach for a coat in the dark, and it wasn't there. What was there instead was the memory of the memory I'd sold her—the smell of my mother's kitchen, gone now, a hole in me shaped like cardamom and rain—and the knowledge that she was right in every particular except the last one.

"You're right that I don't have a plan for after," I said quietly. "But you're wrong about the shape of the world. Or if you're right about it, then it's the shape because people like the guild made it that way, trading pieces of everyone until everyone was small enough to pocket. Someone has to try paying differently. Even if I don't know how yet. Even if it costs everything I've got left." I looked at Ashen, asleep against my pack, her breath stirring the dust. "Aldous died buying us an hour. Brannoc, he was ninety years old and he'd served that order since before my grandfather's grandfather, and he spent his last hour telling me the guild was worth saving and then proving he meant the *child*, not the guild. I can't make his death mean less than that."

The whetstone had stopped moving altogether. For a long moment the only sound was Ashen's breathing and the wind working at the barn's loose boards.

"Ironhold," Brannoc said at last, so softly I nearly missed it. "My mother's town. I went back once, ten years ago. Couldn't find the street she grew up on. Couldn't find anyone who'd heard of it. I told myself it was just time, just ordinary forgetting, the kind that eats everything eventually." She slid the knife home in its sheath. "It wasn't ordinary, was it."

"No," I said. "I don't think any of it was."

"All right." She lay back down, facing away from me, and her voice came rough over her shoulder. "I'm still scared. Don't mistake me staying for courage. But I'm staying. Get some sleep, ledger-keeper. Tomorrow we start the real climbing."

I did not tell her that I was more afraid than she was. I did not tell her that doubt had been gnawing at me since the herald's trumpets, that some nights I dreamed of the usurper's offer already made—the restoration of everything, the guild golden again, my name spoken with respect in halls that had forgotten it—and woke ashamed at how sweet the dream had tasted. Some confessions are a kindness to withhold. She needed me steady, the way a passenger needs a ferryman steady, even when the ferryman's hands are shaking on the oar.

* * *

Above the last of the towns, the trees gave out. The road became a stair cut into living rock, switchbacking up a face of black stone slick with meltwater, and the air thinned until each breath tasted of iron and old cold. We climbed for two days through cloud, seeing nothing but the next twenty steps ahead, hearing nothing but wind and our own hearts.

On the evening of the second day the cloud tore open without warning, as if a curtain had been snatched aside, and there it was: Highmoor's crown, bare and white-capped, and below us, spilling down its southern flank, the army.

I had imagined armies as noise. This one was silence. Ten thousand tents in perfect ranks, cookfires burning smokeless, horses stabled, sentries motionless at their posts—and not one voice, not one drum, not one hammer on an anvil. From this height it looked less like a camp than like a painting of a camp, something rendered by a careful and joyless hand. Even at this distance I could feel the discipline in it, the terrible hush of ten thousand people who had given up every name but one.

"They're not marching," Brannoc breathed beside me. "Why aren't they marching? The free cities are weeks away. Every day they wait—"

"Maybe they're not waiting for orders," I said. My mouth had gone dry. "Maybe they're waiting for something to be brought *down* to them."

Ashen tugged my sleeve. She was pointing—not at the army, but lower, at the mountainside itself, where the stair we climbed ended at a shelf of rock jutting out over the void. Set into that shelf, half-hidden by the last rag of cloud, stood a door.

Not a mine entrance. Not a fortress gate. A door, tall and narrow, banded in a metal that gleamed dull gold even in the failing light, carved over its lintel with characters that hurt to look at—the old language, the language before kingdoms, cut deep enough to read from a mile away. Ashen read it aloud in a whisper, and though I understood none of it, I understood the shape of her voice: reverence and grief braided together, the way you speak over a grave.

"What does it say?" I asked.

She considered me for a long moment, hunting for common words in that deep well of hers. Then she touched the air above her heart, and below it, and spread her small hands wide.

"*Under,*" she said. "*Everything under.*"

The wind rose. Below us, all at once and without any signal we could see, ten thousand fires guttered out together, and the army began, silently, in perfect order, to strike its tents.

Brannoc's hand found mine in the dark. Her grip was ice and iron, and her voice, when it came, was barely more than breath.

"They're going inside," she said. "Vesper—the door's opening. Whatever waits under that mountain, we're about to be standing in front of it."

The first torches were already flaring to life along the stair below us, climbing fast.
