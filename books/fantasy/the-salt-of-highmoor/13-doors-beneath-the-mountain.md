## 13. Doors Beneath the Mountain

The door was not a door.

That was the first thing Vesper understood about it, standing in the dark with Ashen's small hand wrapped around two of his fingers and Brannoc's lamp guttering in the wind that came from nowhere and everywhere beneath Highmoor. The door was a slab of stone set into the mountain's root like a tooth in a jaw, and carved across its face—not carved, he corrected himself, *grown*—were images that shifted when he looked at them directly. A wedding feast became a funeral. A woman's laughing face became an old man's weeping one. A field of wheat ripened, was harvested, lay fallow, ripened again, all in the space of a breath.

"It's made of memories," he said, and his voice sounded wrong down here, flat and close, as if the mountain were swallowing each word before it finished leaving his mouth.

"Everything under here is," Brannoc muttered. She held the lamp high, and its light slid across the stone like water over glass. "I've run cargo past this mountain twenty years. Never once seen this door open. Half of me hoped it didn't exist."

"Then why come?"

"Because you'd have walked into it without me anyway." She spat, a habit she performed with more tenderness than contempt these days. "And because someone has to keep you from signing anything."

The words landed harder than she likely intended. Vesper touched the ledger inside his coat—the old ledger, Aldous's ledger, the one they had carried out of the burning guildhouse like a relic from a fire. It had not left his chest since the marshes. Some nights he dreamed it grew roots into him.

Ashen tugged his fingers.

He looked down. The child was staring at the door with an expression he had never seen on her face before: recognition. Not wonder. Not fear. The weary familiarity of someone returning to a house they had been stolen from. Her lips moved, shaping syllables in that language older than kingdoms, and though he still could not translate it—though the words slipped through his understanding like fish through reeds—he found he could feel their shape now, the way a man blind from birth learns to read weather on his skin. Grief. Warning. And underneath both, terribly, *longing*.

"You lived here," Vesper said quietly.

Ashen did not answer. She never answered questions like that. But her grip tightened until her nails pressed crescents into his knuckles, and she stepped forward, toward the door, pulling him with her.

The stone parted.

There was no hinge, no groan, no grinding of ancient mechanisms. The surface simply ceased to be solid where Ashen stood, opening around her small body like water accepting a dropped stone—and then it opened for Vesper too, and for Brannoc and her lamp, and the cold breath of the deep mountain rolled out to meet them, carrying smells that stopped Vesper mid-stride.

Bread baking. Rain on hot stone. Cut grass. Woodsmoke. His mother's kitchen—or *a* mother's kitchen, some mother's kitchen, decades dead, its ghost exhaled from the palace's throat.

"Oh," Brannoc breathed beside him. "Oh, that's foul."

The corridor beyond was lit by nothing and yet not dark. Light came from the walls themselves, or from what was *in* the walls, because as Vesper's eyes adjusted he saw that the stone was not stone at all, not entirely. It was dense with images, layered like sedimentary rock: faces, landscapes, moments, compressed and stacked and pressed until they hardened into something load-bearing. A child's birthday. A soldier's last sunrise. A farmer watching hail destroy his harvest, frozen forever at the moment of his looking up. The palace was built from traded memories the way other palaces were built from quarried granite, and every block had been somebody's whole life once.

"They sold this," Vesper whispered. He heard his own voice shake and did not bother steadying it. "My guild sold this. Every stone."

"Don't touch the walls," Brannoc said. Her voice was rough but her hand on his shoulder was gentle, and he was grateful for both. "Whatever you do. I've heard stories about what happens to people who touch the walls."

"What happens?"

"I only heard the stories, Kavanagh. That's rather the point."

They walked. The corridor descended in long, shallow sweeps, spiraling inward and downward, and the light changed as they went—not brighter or dimmer but *warmer*, then colder, then scented, so that walking felt like passing through seasons, through years, through other people's afternoons. At one bend Vesper caught the smell of salt marsh and his own childhood slammed into him so hard he staggered: the smell of the flats behind the guildhall, low tide, gull-cries, the specific rot-sweetness of weed drying on pilings. It lasted three steps and then was gone, replaced by pine smoke and snow, and he stood gasping in the sudden winter of a stranger's memory.

"The whole place is leaking," Brannoc said grimly. "Or bleeding. Pick your word."

Ahead, Ashen walked without hesitation. She turned corners before the corners arrived. She paused at junctions where there was no sign of direction and chose correctly, always correctly, as if the palace remembered her and was making way. Watching her, Vesper felt the dread in his stomach take on a new and sharper edge. They were not sneaking into this place. They were being *expected*. Somewhere far above, a usurper who knew their names was perhaps already pouring wine.

The corridor ended at a balcony.

Vesper stepped through an archway grown from a single memory—a wedding, by the look of it, roses and rain and a bride's face caught between joy and terror—and the court beneath Highmoor opened below him, and for a long moment he simply forgot how to breathe.

It was a cavern, but that word was too small, the way *pond* is too small for the sea. The vaulted dark overhead rose farther than lamplight could follow, hung with chandeliers that burned without flame, each crystal catching and holding some small scene—a dance, a death, a first kiss—so that the whole ceiling glittered like a sky made of everyone's best days. Below, terraces of pale stone descended in tiers toward a floor of black glass that mirrored everything and showed nothing true. Staircases climbed in impossible geometries. Bridges arched over drops into glowing mist. And everywhere, everywhere, *people*.

Hundreds of them. Courtiers, servants, guards, musicians, children. They moved through the vast space with unhurried grace, and the music—there was music, strings and something like a hammered dulcimer, coming from nowhere in particular—rose and fell around them like breathing.

"They're beautiful," Vesper said, and hated himself for saying it.

"Yeah," Brannoc said. "Now look closer."

He looked.

A servant crossed a terrace below them carrying a tray of fruit, and as the light moved across her, her face changed. Not transformed—*shifted*, subtly, the way a landscape changes when clouds pass. For a moment she wore the round-cheeked delight of a seven-year-old seeing the sea for the first time; a moment later, the wary hunger of a boy stealing bread; a moment later still, something serene and middle-aged and unbearably sad. She wore these faces the way a woman wears shawls, layered and interchangeable, and none of them appeared to be her own.

Another servant stood at a balustrade nearby, and his hands—his hands were a child's hands, soft and unmarked, attached to a tall man's body. He was admiring them, turning them in the light, flexing fingers that had never worked a day in anyone's life. A guard by the far stair laughed at some joke, and his laugh was an old woman's laugh, cracked and wheezing and full of genuine delight, and he seemed not to notice, or to have forgotten there was ever any other laugh available to him.

"They wear them," Vesper said. The revulsion rose in his throat like bile. "Childhoods. First loves. Whole lives. They put them on like clothing."

"That's the trade, isn't it?" Brannoc leaned on the balcony rail, her scarred forearms bare, deliberately plain amid all this splendor, an anchor of the real. "Your guild sells a memory. Someone buys it. Down here, apparently, they buy enough of them to dress themselves head to foot in other people's living." She shook her head slowly. "Twenty years I figured the memory-trade was sad old folk buying back their youth. Never imagined *this*."

"No one imagines this. That's how it survives." Vesper watched a pair of courtiers drift across the black glass floor, arm in arm, and saw that they were sharing a single memory between them—one of them wearing a young man's first heartbreak while the other wore the same young man's first joy, so that together they composed a complete afternoon neither had ever lived. "You don't survive centuries of doing this by letting people see the warehouse."

Ashen tugged his sleeve. She pointed down, along a colonnade at the cavern's edge, where the architecture grew stranger—walls less orderly, memories packed rawer, like a house built by someone running out of materials. There, half-hidden behind a screen of translucent stone, a figure sat on the floor. Small. Hunched. Rocking slightly.

As Vesper watched, the figure lifted its head, and its face was a blur—literally a blur, features smeared like wet paint, because it wore no memories at all. Nothing. An empty person, smooth as an egg, dressed in a plain gray shift.

"A hollow one," Brannoc whispered. "Saints' teeth. I thought those were stories too."

The blurred face turned toward them. Toward Ashen. And though it had no eyes worth the name, Vesper felt its attention land on the child like a physical weight—and felt Ashen go rigid against his leg.

She raised one small hand. Pressed it flat against her own chest. Then extended it, palm up, toward the hollow figure below: an offering, or a greeting, or—Vesper understood with a lurch—an *identification*. *I am like you. I am also empty. I am also waiting.*

The hollow one raised its hand in perfect mirror. Held it there. And somewhere in the chandeliered dark above, a bell began to toll—slow, sweet, and utterly wrong, a sound like a memory of a bell rather than a bell itself—and every face in the court beneath the mountain turned upward at once, hundreds of borrowed expressions going still as painted masks.

"They know we're here," Brannoc said.

"They knew before we got here," Vesper said.

Down on the black glass floor, the crowd was parting. Silently, gracefully, the courtiers drew back from a central aisle that led from the great stair toward a dais Vesper had not noticed before, half-hidden behind cascades of hanging crystal. On the dais stood a throne—or what had probably been a throne once, before it was buried under accumulated gifts, offerings, tributes of the traded and treasured: crowns, letters, locks of hair, a child's shoe, a wedding ring fused to a signet, layer upon layer, centuries deep, a mountain of everything people had ever been willing to give away.

And descending the great stair, unhurried, alone, came a figure in robes the color of deep water, whose face—

Whose face Vesper recognized.

Not from life. From the ledgers. From the sealed page bearing the founder's seal, from the portraits etched in the guild's oldest records, from three hundred years of entries written in a hand Vesper had spent his career learning to read.

"That's impossible," he said. His voice came out very small in all that vastness. "That's the founder. That's *Merrin Vale*. She died before the guild had walls. She's in the ground under the guildhall, I've swept leaves off her marker my whole life."

"Kavanagh," Brannoc said quietly, "look at her hands."

The founder of the Guild of Impossible Things reached the bottom of the stair and looked up—directly up, across all that luminous air, straight into Vesper's eyes—with a smile of such warmth, such welcome, such perfect and intimate recognition that his blood ran cold.

Her hands were a child's hands. Soft, unmarked, seven years old, and reaching upward as if to pull him gently, patiently, down into the dark.

"Come down, little ledger-keeper," she called, and her voice was every voice at once—young and old, male and female, alive and otherwise. "We've kept a page open for you since before you were born."

Beside him, Ashen began, very softly, to sing—in the language older than kingdoms—and every chandelier in the cavern flickered at once, like a sky remembering how to be afraid.
