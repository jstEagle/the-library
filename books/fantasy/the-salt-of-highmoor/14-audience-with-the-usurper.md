## 14. Audience with the Usurper

The doors of the court beneath Highmoor were made of doors.

Vesper understood this on the third look and refused to understand it on the fourth. Each panel in the vast twin leaves was a smaller door, complete with hinges of pale bone-white metal, a handle worn smooth by hands that had never existed, a keyhole that led nowhere—or that led everywhere, depending on what you believed about the place you were standing in. Some of the little doors were painted with scenes: a kitchen at dawn, a harbor under fog, a child's bedroom with paper stars on the ceiling. Others bore only numbers. One, near the bottom where a child might reach it, was scratched all over with tally marks, as if someone had been counting days behind it.

"They were collected," Brannoc said quietly beside him. She had not let go of her knife since they entered the antechamber, though she held it low, against her thigh, the way a woman holds a rosary rather than a weapon. "Doors. The usurper's collectors brought them back from houses that no longer exist. Whole streets' worth. He likes to be able to close things."

Ashen's hand was small and cold in Vesper's. She had not spoken since they passed the last of the mining towns, since the man at the well had smiled at them with empty eyes and said, *All praise the King Below*, in a voice like water finding a drain. Now her fingers tightened around Vesper's, and he felt through her palm a tremor that was not fear exactly. It was recognition. She had been here before—not with these feet, not in this body, but some part of her had walked these floors when they were new, three hundred years ago, before anyone thought to call the guild's business impossible rather than monstrous.

*We come to return what was taken,* she had signed to him in the salt marshes, teaching him the shapes with infinite patience, laughing silently at his clumsy fingers. *Not to beg. To take.*

He held on to that now. It was the only dry thing left in him.

"Names," said a voice from the far end of the antechamber.

A steward stood there—Vesper assumed it was a steward, because it wore the gray livery of a servant and carried a silver tray bearing nothing at all. Its face was pleasant and forgettable in the specific way of faces assembled rather than born: eyes set a half-degree too symmetrically, a smile calibrated to reassure without inviting confidence. Vesper had seen that craftsmanship before, in the guild's oldest portraits. These were made people. Bought people, or parts of them, stitched into service.

"Names," the steward repeated, patient as tide. "The court requires them. Yours, for the record. The child's is already known."

"I don't give my name to furniture," Brannoc said.

The steward's smile did not waver. "Then the court will provide one for you. It has so many spares."

Something in Vesper's chest went tight and cold. He thought of the ledger room at home, the smell of iron-gall ink and beeswax, the way he used to run his thumb down columns of names and feel that he was holding something sacred. He had spent his whole life believing that a name written carefully was a name protected. Standing here, he understood for the first time that a name could also be a leash, and that somewhere below their feet, in vaults cut from the mountain's living stone, his own name hung on its hook among thousands of others, waiting to be claimed by whoever paid.

"Vesper Fairweather," he said. "Journeyman of the Guild of Impossible Things. I'm expected."

"You are awaited," the steward corrected gently, and turned, and the great doors opened inward without being touched.

* * *

The throne room had been a cathedral once. Vesper knew cathedrals; he had copied their floor plans into the guild's archives during the years he catalogued architecture nobody remembered building. This one still showed its bones: pillars ribbed like whalebone rising into darkness, a rose window high above with its colored glass replaced by sheets of cloudy quartz that diffused the torchlight into something like weather. But where an altar should have stood there was a dais of black salt—the same salt that crusted the marshes a hundred leagues south, hauled here grain by grain, Vesper did not doubt, by hands that had forgotten why.

And upon the dais, in a chair that was less a throne than an accumulation—of armrests added over centuries, of cushions dyed in colors that had gone out of fashion before the free cities had names, of a footstool upholstered in leather that Vesper very much did not want to think about—sat the King Below.

He was smaller than Vesper had imagined. That was the first obscenity of him. Stories made usurpers vast; this man was narrow and neat, dressed in dove-gray, his white hair cropped close, his hands folded in his lap like a scholar's between chapters. His face was old but not ancient, kind but not kind, and his eyes—

His eyes were the problem. They were brown, warm, ordinary, and utterly awake in a way that no one else in this place was awake. Everyone Vester had met beneath the mountain wore their borrowed memories loosely, like coats that didn't fit. This man wore his the way a miser wears gold: close to the skin, counted nightly, never enough.

"Journeyman Fairweather." The voice was dry and precise, an archivist's voice, and it went through Vesper like a hook through soft flesh, because he knew it—he had never heard it, and he knew it. Three centuries of the guild's correspondence lived in his head; he had transcribed the old letters himself, squinting at faded ink, and every letter from the court's greatest client had been signed in a hand that slanted just so. *Yours in perpetual gratitude, M.* "You have your founder's shoulders. Did you know that? I met him only twice. The second time was the last time anyone saw him weep."

"I've come for the child," Vesper said. His voice came out steadier than he deserved. Beside him, Brannoc shifted her weight, and Ashen pressed closer to his leg, and he felt the small fierce heat of her like a coal in his pocket.

"Yes." The usurper did not rise. He studied Vesper the way Vesper studied ledgers—line by line, looking for the entry that didn't balance. "You've come a long way to make a bad withdrawal. Sit. Please. Hospitality is the one virtue I have kept honestly." He gestured, and servants Vesper had not noticed materialized with chairs—real ones, oak and wool, absurdly homely in the cathedral dark. "Your ferrywoman may sit as well. And the child." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "The child will sit whether she chooses to or not. I would prefer she chose."

They sat. The chairs were more comfortable than any Vesper had known, and he hated how much that mattered, how the warmth of the cushion crept up his spine and loosened the argumentative knot in his shoulders. Everything in this room was designed to do exactly that. He realized, with a lurch, that the temperature was perfect, that the light was flattering, that somewhere unseen something burned that smelled faintly of bread and woodsmoke and every hearth he had ever loved. The court was not threatening him. The court was *remembering at him.*

"Ashen," the usurper said, and the child flinched as though struck. "Do you know what your name cost? No—of course not. You weren't there when it was coined. Your mother chose it from a list my agents provided. Seven names, each one priced. *Ashen* was mid-range. There was a dearer option. *Ember.* We thought it too provocative." He smiled, and it was almost gentle. "I have wondered for three hundred years whether we were wrong."

"What do you want?" Brannoc asked flatly.

"Directness. Good. The guild never learned it; that was always their tragedy." The usurper leaned forward, and the torchlight moved across his face and made him, briefly, look like everyone—a grandfather, a teacher, a tired clerk at the end of an honest day. "I want, Journeyman Fairweather, to make you an offer. Not a threat. Threats are for those who lack imagination, and I bought most of mine fair and square." He laughed at his own joke, softly, and waited until Vesper's traitorous mouth almost answered it.

"The Guild of Impossible Things is dying. Do not insult us both by denying it. Nine elders rattling around a hall built for two hundred, arguing over clients who can no longer afford to remember anything worth selling. Your vaults are thin. Your enforcers moonlight as debt collectors for fishmongers—I know; two of them owe me. In a generation the guild will be a story told badly in taverns, and in two it won't even be that." He spread his neat hands. "I can restore it. All of it. The hall rebuilt in living stone. The ranks refilled—you have no idea how many men and women in the free cities would kneel for a purpose, real purpose, if someone offered it wrapped in mystery. Your ledgers restored to their full authority, enforceable in every market from here to the sea. And the guild's founding prerogative—" his voice dropped, and the whole cathedral seemed to lean in— "its claim upon its members' continuance. The thing that lets your elders walk out of their own funerals. I will honor it. I will fund it. The Guild of Impossible Things will endure as long as this mountain does, which is to say forever, and you, Vesper Fairweather, youngest and least regarded, will be the one who saved it. They will carve your name beside Aldous Wren's. Higher, probably. Aldous was always better at dying for things than living with them."

The silence after was enormous. Somewhere above, wind moved through the rose window and made a sound like distant surf.

"And in exchange," Vesper said.

"The child returns to her vault. And the ledgers come to me—all of them, the sealed pages especially. I'll even leave you copies." The usurper's tone did not change, but the room did; the warmth curdled by a single degree, the way milk turns. "You see, Journeyman, I am not the monster your elders' nightmares invented. I am a client. The guild's best client, its most faithful, and I have simply been waiting three hundred years for the guild to honor its side of our arrangement. Every soul in my collection was sold willingly—sold by sellers who wanted something more than they wanted themselves. That is not theft. That is commerce." His eyes flicked to Ashen, and for a moment something moved behind them that was older and hungrier than kindness. "She was collateral. Collateral comes home when called. All I ask is that the institution that profited from her sale for three centuries not pretend, at the end, to have clean hands."

And there it was. The hook inside the hook. Because Vesper *did* have unclean hands—he had known it since the night he found the Fairweather name in the debtor columns, since Aldous told him what the sealed pages meant—and the usurper had seen it in him from the moment the doors opened, the way a moneylender sees a desperate man's gait across a crowded square.

"You're offering me everything my guild ever wanted," Vesper said slowly, "in exchange for becoming exactly what it always was."

"I'm offering you the chance to stop apologizing for it." The usurper sat back, and the warmth returned like sun after cloud, and Vesper caught himself leaning toward it and despised his own body for its honesty. "Think of the child's life under your care. Hiding in marshes. Running from men with knives. Speaking a language no one alive can answer. Think of her life here. Fed, schooled, safe behind walls no army has breached. She would be a treasure of the court, honored—" 

"She'd be a jar on a shelf."

"She'd be *kept.*" For the first time, the usurper's composure thinned, and underneath it Vesper glimpsed the thing that had eaten a kingdom: not cruelty, but need, vast and bottomless, the need of a man who owned every memory in the world except the ones he'd sold to get them. "Everything ends, Journeyman. Kingdoms, guilds, names, loves. I have watched it all end for three hundred years and I have built the only ark that floats, and you would scuttle it for a child you've known a month, because she looked at you with wet eyes in a doorway." His voice softened to something terrible. "You are not the first guildsman to stand where you stand. Your founder stood here. He bargained. He wept, as I said. And then he signed, and he went home immortal, and he slept soundly for two hundred years. Be like him. It is so much easier than being good."

Vesper looked down at Ashen. She was watching the usurper with an expression Vesper had never seen on her face—not fear, not anger. Pity. She raised one small hand and signed, slowly, deliberately, knowing he would understand:

*He forgot what he traded away. That's why he needs the rest of us.*

And Vesper began, very quietly, to laugh.

The usurper's eyes narrowed. "You find something amusing."

"No," Vester said. "I find something *familiar.* My whole life I've been the least remarkable member of my guild—the boy who keeps the books while the elders do the impossible. And you're offering to make me remarkable." He stood. His legs shook. He let them. "But here's what I've learned from the ledgers, Majesty, that you seem to have missed in three hundred years of reading them: every entry balances. Everything sold is owed. And the guild's founding secret—the one thing your offer conspicuously doesn't ask me to sell—is sitting in an archive under this very floor, and I think it's the one page in the world you've never been allowed to read."

The usurper went very still.

"Guards," he said, without raising his voice, and every door in the cathedral—the bone-hinged doors, the painted ones, the little door with the tally marks—opened at once.
