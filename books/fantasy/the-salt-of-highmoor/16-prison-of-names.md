## 16. Prison of Names

Before there were kingdoms there were names, and before the names there was the salt.

Ashen remembers the salt. She remembers it the way a river remembers its source—not as thought, but as gravity. It pulls her down through sleep into the cold bright country where she was born whole, and every night she walks its white shores again, and every morning she wakes with grit between her teeth that is not grit at all but memory, fine-ground, unswallowable.

*The water was loud then. The water had a name too, and she knew it.*

She knew everything's name then. That is what being whole meant. Not that she was unbroken—she had been broken many times, small breakages, the ordinary weather of a life—but that every broken thing could be called by its true word and would answer. She would stand on the salt shore and speak to the tide, and the tide would come to her ankle like a dog that loved her. She would speak to the gulls and they would wheel down carrying her words in their beaks like silver fish. The mountain far away had a name, and the mountain answered in its slow stone voice, and she was not afraid of it, because a named thing cannot be a stranger.

Her own name—

Here the remembering goes dark, the way a lamp goes dark when a hand cups around the flame. Her own name was not a sound. It was older than sound. It was a *shape*, a thing like the space between two waves, and she wore it under her ribs the way other children wore their hearts, and when she breathed out fully, on cold mornings, you could almost see it hanging in the air, brief and bright, before it went back inside her.

She does not remember selling it.

This is the mercy of it, or the cruelty—she has never decided which. She remembers the men who came to the salt shore with ledgers bound in grey leather, and she remembers that they were kind, in the way of people who have practiced kindness until it sits well on them. They did not come with ropes. They came with questions. *What do you fear? What would you give so your village never knows famine? What is your name worth, little one, against a hundred winters of plenty?*

She was young. The winters were hard. Her mother coughed red into a cloth and said nothing about it, which is how the poor say *everything* about it.

She remembers signing—or rather, she remembers pressing her palm to the page, because her language had no writing then, only handprints and songs, and the guild men were patient with this. They let her choose the song. They told her the song would keep her name warm while she was without it. They told her she could take it back whenever she wished.

They did not lie, exactly. That is what she understands now, here in the dark beneath the mountain, in the vessel that is not a vessel. You can take your name back whenever you wish. But first you must remember that you gave it. And they had sold, along with the name, the *remembering*. That was the finest clause in all their contracts, the clause written in no ink at all: *the debtor shall forget the debt.* Clean transactions. No grief to sour the trade.

She remembers the moment after, though. Oh, she remembers that.

* * *

It was evening. The tide was going out and the light was going with it, and she stood on the shore and opened her mouth to call her mother home for supper—and nothing came. Not silence; silence would have been something. What came was a *hole*, a shaped absence where the calling-word should have been, and she felt the shape of herself slide loose under her ribs like a fish slipping a net, and she put her hands over her chest and pressed, and pressed, and there was nothing there to press against anymore. Just smooth skin over an ordinary heart doing its ordinary work, dumb as any animal's.

Her mother came up the shingle anyway, drawn by supper-smell and habit, and looked at her, and Ashen tried to say *I am frightened,* and what came out was crying, plain human crying, the kind any child makes, the kind that means nothing particular.

"That's better," the guild man said gently, behind her. "That's how children ought to sound."

*That's how children ought to sound.*

She has carried that sentence three centuries through the dark. It is the one thing they could not buy, because it was never hers—it was theirs, the buyers', and so it stayed with the merchandise, stamped into her like a maker's mark.

After that the years go strange. She remembers them as weather, not as time. There was a ship whose timbers sang in a language almost like hers, degraded, worn smooth from disuse, and she lay in its hold listening and understood perhaps one word in ten, and each word she understood hurt more than the ones she didn't, the way a half-remembered face hurts worse than a stranger's.

There was the road. There were cities that had forgotten the old tongue so thoroughly that when she spoke it, people crossed themselves or laughed or threw stones, and she learned to be quiet, and quiet became another prison built inside the first one. A soul without its name can still make noise. She made noise for a long time. Then she stopped.

There was a woman in a fishing town who took her in for one winter and taught her knots, and the woman's hands were quick and kind, and Ashen loved her in the wordless way the wordless love: entirely, uselessly, with no way to say so. In spring the woman's son came home from the wars and found a mute child in his mother's kitchen eating bread he thought was his, and Ashen left in the night rather than be a debt in someone else's house. She had learned that much about debts by then, even without knowing she carried one.

And then—the mountain. Always, eventually, the mountain.

* * *

The court beneath Highmoor did not take her all at once. Nothing in that place takes anything all at once; taking things slowly is the whole of its architecture.

She remembers arriving as a payment, a line-item transferred from one ledger to another, carried down through the mining shafts in a cart among ingots and salt-blocks, because that is what she was to them: freight with a pulse. She remembers the doors. Everyone remembers the doors. They are made of things people gave up—a door of first kisses, a door of fathers' approval, a door made entirely from the last words of dying sailors, and if you put your ear against that one you can hear the sea saying goodbye forever. She put her ear against every door as they brought her down, hungry as ever for any voice, even borrowed ones, even dead ones. The doors whispered to her. None of them spoke her language. Even here, in the deep place where languages were currency, hers was too old to be legal tender.

In the court she learned what she was for.

Not a servant—servants there wear stolen childhoods like silk gloves and are envied for it. Not a prisoner—prisoners have names, and names are leverage, and she had none to lever with. She was *stock*. Reserve capital. She was the oldest asset in the vault, the founding collateral, the soul that anchored every contract signed since, the keel-stone of the whole grey economy. The usurper himself had once come down to look at her, in the early years, before he was usurper, when he was only a client grown greedy. He had circled her vessel the way a merchant circles a pearl.

"Pre-kingdom," he'd murmured, in that voice like a coin spun on marble. "Do you know what that means, little one? It means you predate the law. It means no court above can claim jurisdiction over you. It means you are the single most valuable thing in my collection, and you don't even know enough to be proud."

She hadn't understood his words then. She understands them now, the way she understands everything now—slowly, sideways, in the long dreaming the vessel allows her. For the vessel is not sleep exactly, and not death, and not imprisonment in any way the free world uses the word. It is *being kept*. Milk kept cool. Meat kept salted. A name kept warm, the guild men promised her, three hundred years ago on a cold shore.

Kept. Yes. Like a knife kept sharp. Like a prisoner kept.

Time in the vessel moves like sap. She dreams the salt shore on a loop, and each loop the dream erodes a little, the way real shores erode, until sometimes the water forgets to be loud and the gulls fly upside down and her mother's coughing turns to birdsong and back again, and she knows the dream is wearing out, wearing thin, and some night soon there will be nothing left of the shore but white noise, white like salt, white like bone.

But lately—lately something new has come into the loop.

A boy.

He is not from her dreams. She knows the difference; she has had three hundred years to learn the geography of her own mind, and he walks in it like a man walking into a house he's only heard described, touching the walls, apologizing to the furniture. He is tall and stooped and carries ink stains on his fingers the way other men carry scars, and he looks at her ledgers—her *ledgers*, the pages where her sale is written—and his face does what no face in the court beneath the mountain has ever done.

It breaks.

Everyone down here wears other people's feelings, and none of the feelings fit, and she has watched a thousand faces arrange themselves into pity and desire and wonder like servants trying on their masters' coats. But this boy's face breaks *from the inside*. Something in him is coming apart at the sight of her name on a page, and the something is grief, real grief, unpaid-for grief, and she has not seen a feeling that wasn't invoiced in longer than kingdoms have existed.

He says her name.

Not her true name—he cannot; no one living can, it sleeps in the vessel beside her heart-shape like a sword in a stone. But he tries. His mouth makes the shapes of the old tongue clumsily, a child's drawing of a mountain range, and the sound is wrong, wrong, wrong—and it is the most beautiful thing she has heard since the tide went out with her voice inside it.

Because wrongness means effort. Wrongness means someone standing outside the prison, hammering.

* * *

Now, tonight—or what passes for night in the lightless vault—she dreams the shore again, and the boy is there, standing where the guild men stood, except he is holding out no ledger. His hands are empty. Empty hands. She has almost forgotten what they look like.

And in the dream she finds she can move her mouth again, and what comes out is not the hole, not the shaped absence, but words—her words, the old words, surfacing one at a time like drowned things rising.

*They will tell you I am property,* she says, in the tongue before kingdoms, the tongue the mountain itself forgot. *Tell them a name cannot be owned. Tell them a name can only be given, and mine was taken, and taken is not given, and the difference between those two words is the width of the world.*

The boy doesn't understand her. Of course he doesn't. But he leans closer, the way you lean toward a fire, and she thinks: *he will come down here. He is already coming. I dreamed him before he chose me and now he has chosen me and the choosing runs backward through time like a crack through ice.*

She presses her hand flat against the inside of the vessel, against the wall of her keeping, and somewhere far above—through three hundred feet of mountain, through courts and corridors and locked grey doors—she feels an answering pressure. Faint. Human. Ink-stained.

Someone is knocking.

And under her ribs, in the dark where her shape used to live, something stirs and turns over like a sleeper hearing footsteps on the stairs, and the first syllable of her true name rises toward her lips—

—and far above her, iron begins to scream.
