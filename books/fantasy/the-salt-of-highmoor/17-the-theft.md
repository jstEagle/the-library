## 17. The Theft

The vault opened only at the hour when the court slept, and even then it did not sleep deeply. Vesper learned this from a servant with a borrowed childhood—a woman who wore someone else's memory of learning to swim the way another might wear a shawl, draped and slightly too large. She had told him in a whisper, in exchange for nothing more than his attention, that the vault breathed. That between the second bell and the third, the great doors exhaled a slow tide of cold air, and in that exhale the locks grew drowsy.

He did not ask how she knew. In this place, everyone knew things they could not say they knew. Knowledge down here was like water in a flooded cellar; it rose around your ankles no matter how carefully you stepped.

Now he crouched in the shadow of a buttress carved from a single memory of winter—Vesper could feel it radiating cold that had never belonged to anyone in this court, cold remembered by some farmer three hundred years dead—and watched the vault doors.

They were vast. Not grand; vast. The difference mattered. Grandeur was made to be seen. This was made to be endured. Black stone shot through with veins of something that glimmered faintly gold, like light trapped in amber, or like names written in ink that had not yet dried. As he watched, the veins pulsed once, slowly, and Vesper felt the pulse in his own chest answer it, an arrhythmia of dread.

Beside him, Brannoc was very still. She had been still for a long time. He had learned on the road north that her stillness was not calm—it was the stillness of a drawn bowstring, all her fear compressed into readiness.

"Tell me again," she murmured, not moving her lips, "why we didn't simply walk out the way we walked in."

"Because Ashen's soul isn't in Ashen."

"Yes. You've said. It remains the stupidest sentence I've ever heard."

"It's the truest one." Vesper kept his eyes on the doors. "The guild sold her whole. Body to the mountain's edge, soul to the deep vaults. What walks around wearing her face is—" He searched for a word and found none adequate. "A vessel. An empty house with the lamps left burning so no one notices."

"And the lamps are in there." Brannoc tilted her chin toward the doors. "In a box. On a shelf. Like salt pork."

"Like everything else here."

The corridor beyond the buttress lay silent. Above them, the palace of traded memories went on dreaming its borrowed dreams: corridors that rearranged themselves when unobserved, chandeliers made from a thousand people's recollections of starlight, servants gliding past with faces that flickered—the original owners' griefs surfacing like drowned bodies before being pressed back down. Vesper had spent four days learning its rhythms. Four days of walking slowly with his eyes lowered while his heart hammered out the shape of the ledgers back home, every page of which had led here.

Somewhere above, Ashen waited in the room Brannoc had secured for them, under pretense of seeking an audience for a petition of trade. The usurper believed the guild had come crawling back to negotiate. Aldous's death had bought them that assumption—the old man had died loudly, publicly, protesting his order's loyalty even as his hidden letter laid bare its treachery—and the usurper was vain enough to believe that grief made men pliable.

Vesper had stood in the audience hall yesterday and let the usurper look at him. Let the great dark figure lean forward on its throne of fused memories and say, *You have your founder's eyes. I remember when those eyes were young.* And Vesper had bowed, and smiled, and inside himself he had felt something close over like a hand closing over a coal.

Tonight the hand would open. Tonight he would either hold the coal or be burned by it.

"The bell," Brannoc whispered.

Far off, muffled by stone that remembered being quarried, the second bell tolled. Its note hung in the air a moment too long, as if reluctant to finish.

The vault doors exhaled.

Vesper felt it against his face—a long, slow breath of air so cold it seemed older than cold, carrying a smell like snow on iron, like the space inside a locked book. Along the black stone, the golden veins dimmed. Somewhere within the doors' thickness, mechanisms that were not entirely mechanical sighed and slackened.

"Now," Vesper said. "Before the third bell. Before they breathe in again."

They crossed the corridor in the open, because hiding was finished now, because from this point forward everything was committed motion. Vesper had expected his legs to shake. They did not. That frightened him more than shaking would have—he had imagined fear as trembling, but this was fear as clarity, terrible and clean, the world sharpened to a single point of purpose.

The vault doors bore no handle. Of course they didn't. They were not meant to be opened from outside by anyone with a right to enter; the court's treasures went in and stayed in. Instead, set into the stone at the height of a man's heart, was a recess shaped like an open book.

Vesper drew the ledger page from inside his coat.

He had cut it from the sealed volume two nights ago, working by the light of a candle stub, with a knife Brannoc sharpened for him without asking what it was for. The page bore the founder's seal—that broken circle of interlocking letters that Vesper had grown up believing was merely decoration, the way children believe the bars of their crib are merely furniture. The seal was the key. Aldous's last letter had told him so, in a hand gone shaky with age and urgency: *The vaults below Highmoor were built to our design. Our seals open what our hands closed. This is the shame of the guild, Vesper—we built the cage, and so we alone hold the key.*

The page fit the recess exactly.

For a moment nothing happened. Then the golden veins in the stone began to move—not pulsing now but flowing, streaming toward the recess like water finding a drain, and where they touched the paper the founder's seal drank them in, letter by interlocking letter, until the page glowed like a coal wrapped in silk.

The doors split along a seam Vesper hadn't been able to see, and swung inward without sound.

Cold poured out. Beyond lay darkness, and in the darkness, shelves.

The vault of the court beneath Highmoor was not large. That was the first thing Vesper understood, standing on its threshold, and the understanding arrived with the force of revelation. He had imagined caverns, endless ranks of treasure, the accumulated plunder of centuries. But the room was modest—a long chamber, maybe forty paces, lined with shelving of the same black veined stone.

It was small because souls do not take up much room.

Each shelf held vessels. Vesper walked the aisle slowly, and the cold walked with him. Some vessels were jars of cloudy glass, some were boxes banded in lead, some were things he had no name for: a folded length of grey cloth that whispered; a bird's skull with light behind its eye sockets; a stoppered gourd that rocked gently, endlessly, as if something inside were trying to rock itself to sleep. Each bore a tag. Not paper tags—he should have known better than to expect paper—but strips of some pale material, and on each strip, in script that shifted subtly as he looked at it, was written a name.

He read a few. *Merrin Hale, aged nine years, sold by her father against the famine winters.* *Tobias Wren, given freely, payment rendered: one kingdom's gratitude.* *Unnamed child, recovered from the salt marshes, year of the second founding.*

His hands had begun to shake now. Here, among them, the trembling finally came, and he welcomed it, because the alternative was to feel nothing at all, and feeling nothing in this place would have been a kind of consent.

"They're all here," Brannoc said quietly behind him. Her voice had lost its edge. She stood in the doorway, unwilling—unable—to come further in. "All these years. All the trades. They're just... shelved."

"Not shelved." Vesper's voice came out rough. "Stored. Like the guild stored grain, like it stored wine. Like it stored me." He touched a jar gently, and through the glass he felt—or dreamed he felt—a faint pressure, the way you feel a moth tap against a window from the inside. "Brannoc. There are hundreds."

"There are thousands," she corrected, and her voice cracked on the word. "Look at the dates."

He looked. The oldest tags were written in the founder's own hand—Vesper recognized it from the ledger's first pages, that beautiful severe script—and the newest were fresh, the pale material still bright. The court beneath Highmoor had never stopped collecting. Even now, even with its armies marching, somewhere a father was trading a daughter's name for bread, and somewhere a clerk with Vesper's training was entering it into a ledger with neat, careful strokes.

"We can't carry them all," Brannoc said. It was not cruelty. It was arithmetic, and he loved her for daring it. "We came for one."

"I know."

"Say it, then. Say we take one and leave the rest, and mean it, because if you stand here much longer reading names you'll read yourself to death and Ashen will stay an empty house forever."

"I know," Vesper said again, and made himself turn from the shelves, made himself walk the aisle with his eyes fixed forward, though the tags pulled at his gaze like hands plucking at his sleeves. *Merrin Hale. Tobias Wren.* He passed them. He would spend the rest of his life passing them.

Ashen's vessel was where Aldous's letter had promised: the inner alcove, the oldest section, guarded by a case of glass thicker than the rest, because the thing inside was the oldest thing in the room.

It was a lantern.

Not ornamental—plain, almost crude, the kind a fisherman might carry onto a night boat. But within its glass burned a flame that gave no heat and cast no proper light; instead of illuminating the alcove, it illuminated memories. Vesper looked at it and saw, unbidden, a girl of perhaps six years running through a field of salt-grass with her arms spread wide, laughing in a language that predated laughter's naming. He saw her mother's hands braiding her hair. He saw a door, and a stranger in the doorway, and scales being produced, and the specific terrible silence of a child understanding she is being weighed.

The flame bent, as if toward him. As if it knew him.

"Oh," Vesper whispered. "Oh, Ashen."

"She can see us?" Brannoc had crept closer despite herself, drawn by the light that wasn't light.

"I think she sees everything. I think she has seen everything for three hundred years, from inside this glass." He knelt before the case. His reflection stared back at him from the thick glass, warped and doubled, and beside it, impossibly, a smaller reflection: a girl's silhouette, standing very straight, waiting.

The case was locked, and the lock was guild-made, and the key was guild-made, and Vesper had brought both. The founder's seal again, pressed to the clasp; the mechanism that his order had built three centuries ago to cage a soul yielded to the same sigil that had built it. The irony was so complete it circled back around to grief.

The case opened with a click no louder than a knuckle cracking.

And the vault screamed.

Not the doors, not the alarms he had steeled himself for—bells, horns, running boots. The vault itself. Every vessel on every shelf began to sound at once: the jars hummed, the whispering cloth shrieked aloud, the bird's skull clattered against its shelf, the rocking gourd slammed itself against the glass walls of its prison in frantic rhythm. Three hundred years of stolen souls, sensing one of their number about to go free, crying out in whatever voices remained to them.

The sound was unbearable. It was also, Vesper realized with a lurch, audible far beyond this room. The golden veins in the walls blazed; somewhere above, he heard the great palace convulse—a crash, shouting, the particular thunder of many feet on stairs that had not existed a moment before.

"They know," Brannoc said. She had her knife out, her back to the alcove, watching the aisle. "Vesper. They *know*. Take it and run."

He lifted the lantern from its cradle.

It was warm. After all that cold, after three centuries in the frozen dark, the little lamp was warm as a sleeping child's forehead, and the flame inside leaned toward his chest, and through his coat, over his heart, he felt it settle—felt it *recognize* him, the way you recognize a voice heard once, long ago, across water.

*You,* the warmth said, without words. *I know your name. It was written before you were born.*

"Hold on," Vesper whispered to it, to her. "Hold on, Ashen. I'm taking you home."

Then the first guards rounded the end of the aisle—servants with flickering faces, soldiers whose armor was stitched from other people's courage—and Brannoc stepped past him into the narrow mouth of the vault with her knife low and her voice raised in the smuggler's cant of the free cities, cursing them with fluency and joy.

Vesper ran.

Behind him the vault roared with the voices of the shelved, and ahead the corridors of the memory-palace were already rearranging themselves, walls sliding like cards reshuffled by a furious hand, and above it all the third bell began to toll—not the sleepy hour-bell now, but the alarm, deep and getting deeper, a sound like the mountain clearing its throat to speak.

The lantern flame burned steady against his heart.

*This way,* it seemed to say, in the language before kingdoms. *I remember the way out. I remember everything.*

Vesper followed the light.
