## 1. The Ninth Dish

There are nine dishes at a royal feast in Ironhollow, and I am the reason there are nine.

The first eight are for the court. The ninth is for the queen — and the ninth is always mine. That is the arrangement that has kept me alive for nine years, which sounds generous until you understand that keeping me alive is not kindness but inventory management. A taster who dies is a taster you must replace. And replacing me would mean finding another orphan with the right blood, the right silence, and the right constitution to swallow murder every morning before breakfast.

I was that orphan once. Now I am furniture with a pulse.

On the morning of the Queen's Harvest Feast, I woke before the bells as always, dressed in my grey taster's coat as always, and walked the cold corridor from my cell to the kitchens beneath the palace as always. Routine is a kind of armor too. I have worn it longer than I have worn the coat.

The kitchen was already roaring when I arrived. Feast days turn Cook Maren's domain into a small, sweating kingdom of its own — copper pots gleaming like armor, apprentices darting between them with the wide eyes of soldiers who have been told the battle starts at noon. The air smelled of roasting quail, honeyed pears, wine reduced with blackcurrant, and underneath all of it, the smell I knew best: fear.

Every cook in Ironhollow fears the ninth dish. Not because they might poison the queen by accident — though that terror exists, and I've seen two sous-chefs faint over a mislabeled jar — but because they might fail to. Every feast, somewhere in the palace's web of enemies, someone tries. It is my job to catch their attempt with my tongue, and it is the kitchen's job to make sure the attempt, when it comes, arrives beautifully plated.

"Beatrix." Maren found me by the door the way she always does, wiping her hands on an apron that had given up being white sometime during the last reign. She's a broad woman with forearms like bread loaves and a face that has forgotten how to do anything except look tired. "You're late."

"I'm early. You're just earlier."

"Don't get clever with me today. Her Majesty wants the tasting done before the noon bell. There's a new dish." Something flickered across her face when she said *new* — quick, gone again, like a fish under ice. "Ninth position. Venison in a dark sauce. You'll take it last, after the others settle."

I had eaten eight dishes at every feast for nine years. I knew the order the way I knew my own heartbeat: the cold sorrel soup, the oysters, the little pastries with the mushroom filling that the Duke of Vael considers his personal assassination vector because he believes everyone else poisons as a matter of course. The order never changed. The order changing was, itself, a kind of alarm bell.

"A new dish," I repeated.

"The queen requested it." Maren said this the way people say *the weather happened*, which is how everyone in this palace says things that are lies. The queen requests nothing on feast days. The queen eats what tradition puts in front of her, because tradition is the only bodyguard she trusts more than me.

But I said, "Of course," because that is what furniture says.

* * *

The tasting room is a narrow stone chamber off the main kitchen, windowless, warmed by a single brazier. There is a table, a chair, a ewer of water for rinsing, and a small brass bell I ring if I begin to die. In nine years I have rung it twice. Both times it was nothing — a bad oyster, a spice that didn't agree with me — and both times the kitchen staff looked at me afterward with something close to reverence, which is the closest thing to affection this palace offers anyone.

I worked through the first dishes with the methodical calm of long practice. This is the part no one understands about my work: it isn't courage. Courage is for people who have a choice. What I have is technique. You take a spoonful no larger than a walnut. You hold it on your tongue and count — one, two, three — letting the front of your mouth report. Bitterness that blooms late rather than early. Numbness. A metallic edge like biting a coin. Heat where there should be no heat. Then you swallow, count to sixty, drink water, wait, and move on. The whole ritual takes most of an hour, and I have performed it so many times that my body does it without consulting me, the way a sailor's hands knot rope.

The sorrel soup: clean. Oysters: clean, if you can call anything an oyster does to the mouth clean. Pastries: clean, though the mushrooms were older than they should have been, and I made a mental note to tell Maren, because even a taster can be murdered by sloppiness.

Dish by dish, hour by hour, the morning wore down toward the ninth plate.

And that is when the strangeness began — small at first, the way all catastrophes are small at first. The apprentices stopped coming near the tasting room door. Then they stopped coming near the corridor outside it. By the time I finished the eighth dish, a honeyed quail that sat in my stomach like a well-behaved stone, the entire kitchen had developed a sudden fascination with tasks on the far side of the building.

Maren brought the ninth dish herself.

She carried it on both arms like something that might wake up. The venison lay in slices under a sauce so dark it was nearly black, glossy, smelling of wine and juniper and — underneath, faint as a whisper through a wall — something else. Something green. Not herb-green. Green like moss on old stones, like the inside of forests, like the smell of the valley floor after rain in places where the salt never reaches.

"Venison in a dark sauce," Maren said. Her voice was steady. Her knuckles were not. "Her Majesty's request."

"You already told me what it's called. Tell me where it came from."

"A gift. From a village in the low valleys. A hunting party brought it to the gates this morning with their compliments." She set the plate down in front of me with exaggerated care, the way you'd set down a sleeping animal. "They asked that it be served first to the queen's own table. As an honor."

"And the Steward approved a dish from outside the walls?"

Maren's jaw tightened. "The Steward approved it," she said, in a tone that told me the Steward had done no such thing, or had done it in a hurry, or had done it while looking slightly gray around the mouth. "Taste it, Beatrix."

I looked at the venison. The venison looked back at me, insofar as dead meat can, and I had the distinct and ridiculous impression that it was waiting.

Here is what nine years of poison teaches you that no manual will: poison has a texture. Arsenic sits sweet and shy. Nightshade flirts. The compounds our alchemists brew have manners; they announce themselves politely to a trained tongue like guests presenting cards. Even crude poisons — rat-bane, drain-water, the desperate brews of servants with grievances — have a rough honesty about them. Poison *wants*. Poison reaches for you.

This sauce did not reach. It simply *was*, dense and patient, and that green smell wound through it like a thread pulled from somewhere very deep underground.

"Taste it," Maren said again, softer. "Please."

I took up the spoon.

One, I thought, holding the first bite on my tongue. Two. Three. Nothing reported. No bitterness, no numbness, no coin-taste. Four. Five. The flavor opened slowly — venison, juniper, wine, and that green note strengthening until it filled my whole mouth, and with it came a sensation I have no better word for than *attention*. As though the sauce were noticing me. As though I were not tasting the dish at all, but being tasted.

Six. Seven. My heart picked up its pace, and I want to be clear: in nine years, my heart has never picked up its pace. Fear is a luxury my work cannot afford, and I have trained it out of myself the way you train a dog out of barking. But something in that dark sauce reached past all my training and knocked on a door I did not know I had.

Eight. Nine. Ten.

I swallowed.

The sensation sank down through me like a stone dropped into a well — down past my chest, past my stomach, into some floor inside me I had never found before — and settled there. Warm. Patient. *Waiting.* Like something that had arrived after a very long journey and intended to stay.

Then the taste faded, and I was sitting in a cold stone room with an empty spoon in my hand and Maren watching me with an expression I had never seen on her face before. I would spend a long time trying to name it later. The nearest word is *pity*.

"Well?" she asked.

"It's clean," I said. My voice came out strange, distant, as if someone else were using it. "No poison."

Something in her shoulders loosened and then immediately re-tightened, because we both understood the arithmetic. If the dish was clean — truly, verifiably clean — then the ninth dish had failed at its only purpose. Someone had sent the queen an offering instead of an attempt, and the offering had gotten through, and the system that governed my entire existence had just blinked.

"Ring the all-clear," Maren said. "I'll inform the Steward."

"Maren." I caught her sleeve. Under my fingers her arm felt like iron wrapped in cloth. "What village?"

"They didn't give a name. They gave—" She stopped. Looked at the door. Looked at me. And lowered her voice to almost nothing, the way people speak in this palace when they are about to hand you something dangerous. "They left a message for whoever tasted first. Written instructions, sealed, that the dish be tasted by the royal taster *before anyone else in the palace*, and that the taster speak the words aloud: *the mountain remembers its debts.*"

The brazier crackled. Somewhere far away, a bell rang the half-hour, indifferent as ever.

"I didn't say them," Maren added quickly. "I wasn't going to say them. That's not — we don't say things here, Beatrix. You know the law."

I did know the law. Every child raised in Ironhollow knows it before they know their letters, because it is carved over every gate in the valley in letters taller than a man: **NO GOD MAY ENTER. NO SOUL MAY LEAVE.** We learn it the way children in other kingdoms learn nursery rhymes, and we dismiss it the same way, because the gods are old stories and the salt line is just a customs border and nobody, in nine years of swallowing death for the crown, had ever given me reason to think otherwise.

But something was now sitting warm and patient in the floor of my chest, and it had come through my own mouth, past my own immunity, into the one place in my body that no poison had ever touched because no poison had ever bothered to try.

"The mountain remembers its debts," I said aloud, because apparently I am the kind of fool who reads the cursed inscription out loud.

The words left my mouth and the air changed. Subtly — nothing so vulgar as thunder. The brazier flames leaned, all together, toward me, like a crowd turning to look. The water in the ewer shivered though nothing had struck the table. And deep in that inner floor of me, the warm patient thing turned over in its sleep and *answered*, a pressure like a hand pressing gently against the inside of my ribs, and a single syllable of something vast and slow moved through my mind without bothering to become language.

Then everything went still again, ordinary, stone-cold, and Maren was staring at me with her hand pressed over her mouth.

"Get out," she whispered.

"Maren—"

"*Get out of my kitchen.*" Her voice cracked on the second word. "Not — no, listen to me, boy, listen." She gripped my shoulders, hard enough to bruise, and her eyes were wet and terrified in equal measure. "Go straight to the Steward. Tell him exactly what happened, every word, before anyone else hears it. Don't eat anything else today. Don't pray. Don't—" She stopped herself, swallowed, and finished in a voice gone flat and careful: "Don't answer if anything asks you a question."

"Nothing asked me a question."

"Not yet," said Maren, and crossed herself the old way, palm to sternum, the way people did before the founding — the way that is forbidden now — and pushed me out of the tasting room into the corridor beyond.

* * *

I walked to the Steward's office the long way, along the gallery above the great hall, because my legs wanted the extra time and I have learned to trust my legs more than my thoughts.

Below me, servants were dressing the feast tables. Gold cloth. Candles the length of my forearm. The eight dishes arriving in procession under silver lids, each one guarded by a footman whose entire career depended on nothing happening. Everything precisely as it has been every year of my service. Everything precisely as it will be for the next four hours, until the queen takes her seat and lifts her fork and the world continues being safe and poisoned and ordinary.

Except that the ninth dish was already tasted. Except that the words were already spoken. Except that there was a warmth in the floor of me like a banked fire, and every few steps it pressed again against the inside of my ribs — gently, almost courteously, the way a guest knocks before entering — and each time, unbidden, untrained, unwelcome, something in me pressed *back*.

That was the worst part. Not the dish. Not the words. Not even the leaning flames. The worst part was the pressing back — the discovery that after nine years of being a vessel for other people's murder, there was room in me for something else, and that the something else knew it, and that some traitor part of me was glad.

I stopped at the gallery rail and looked down at my hands. Grey sleeves. Steady fingers. The taster's badge stitched at the collar: a cup and a serpent, the crown's promise that what kills the taster would never reach the throne.

Nine years I had believed I was the shield. Standing there with a god's attention coiled warm beneath my ribs, the thought arrived — quiet, complete, and colder than any poison I'd ever swallowed — that perhaps I had never been the shield at all.

Perhaps I had always been the chalice.

The Steward of Tasters received reports in a room at the end of the east wing, behind a door with no decoration at all, because the man himself considered ornament a security risk. I raised my hand to knock.

From inside, faintly, I heard him speaking to someone — and though I could not make out the words, I recognized the shape of the sentence he was saying, the rising, urgent cadence of a man giving orders he hoped he'd never have to give.

Then a second voice answered him. It was very quiet, and very old, and it did not come from anywhere in that room.

It came from under the floor.
