## 2. Ironhollow Rules

There are two ways to tell a man his life's work has failed. You can hand him the evidence gently, like passing a knife handle-first. Or you can let him find out in front of the whole court, which is what I did, because Beatrix Dravenmoor has never once been accused of tact.

The Steward of Tasters kept his office in the east wing, behind a door of black oak carved with the valley's sigil: an open hand over a closed mountain. I'd walked past that door nine hundred times and never wondered what the hand was holding back. That morning, with the offering dish wrapped in my napkin like contraband, I finally did.

I knocked twice — our code for *routine* — and entered without waiting, because routine was exactly what I needed it to be.

Merrick Halloway sat behind his desk looking like a man who had slept in his clothes and dreamed badly in them. He was fifty, gray at the temples, precise as a ledger line. In nine years he had never raised his voice at me and never once told me anything true. We had a fine arrangement built on mutual fiction: he pretended I was a servant, I pretended to believe him.

"Taster Dravenmoor." He didn't look up from his inventory. "The queen's feast went smoothly, then."

"Mostly." I set the covered dish on his desk between us, careful as a surgeon laying out instruments. "The quail in ash-berry glaze. Fourth course."

"Delicious, I'm told."

"It was." I lifted the napkin. "That's the problem."

He looked up.

I'll give Merrick this much: he was not a stupid man. I watched the sequence cross his face — confusion first, because the dish looked untouched; then recognition of the dish itself; then the arithmetic every taster learns before he learns his own name. Ash-berry glaze. The one preparation the kitchens are forbidden to make. Not because it's poisonous.

Because it isn't.

"Where," he said quietly, "did this come from."

"The royal kitchen. Cook Ellery made it. She said—" and here I hesitated, because saying it aloud would make it real in a way the dish alone couldn't, "—she said she dreamed the recipe. Woke up with her hands already moving. Said someone was *hungry*, Merrick. She said the words felt old in her mouth."

The color left his face the way water leaves a cracked bowl — slowly, completely, and all at once. He stood so fast his chair went over backward and neither of us moved to catch it. He crossed to the door, threw both bolts, and drew the inner curtain across the little window, and only then did he turn around, and I saw something I had never seen in nine years of swallowing death for breakfast.

Merrick Halloway was afraid.

"You ate none of it," he said. It wasn't a question, but I answered anyway.

"Not a crumb. I spat the first mouthful into the sardine tin. The rest is here." I nudged the dish toward him. "Which raises an interesting question, doesn't it? If it's harmless enough for the gods to eat—"

"*Do not.*" His voice cracked like green wood. "Don't say *that word*. Not in this room. Not in this wing." He pressed both palms flat against his desk and breathed, and when he spoke again he had rebuilt himself into something resembling the Steward of Tasters, though the seams showed. "Beatrix. Sit down."

"I'm standing fine."

"*Sit down.*"

I sat. It seemed suddenly important, in that small curtained room, to do exactly what the frightened man asked.

He remained standing. He paced once, twice, the length of his office, and then stopped at the shelf behind his desk where he kept the ledgers — the tasting logs, the dosing schedules, the meticulous records of everything that had ever passed through my body on its way to the crown's. But he didn't take down a ledger. He took down a book I had never seen: thin, iron-clasped, bound in leather gone dark as dried blood. There was no title on the spine. There was no spine, really. It was less a book than a relic someone had decided to disguise as one.

"This stays in this room," he said. "It has stayed in this room for two hundred years. Every Steward reads it on the day of his appointment, and every Steward wishes he hadn't." He set it down but did not open it. "You reported an anomaly. Procedure says I log it, flag the cook, and send you back to work. Procedure also assumes the anomaly is poison. This is not poison."

"No," I agreed. "It's an invitation."

His jaw tightened. "You know what it is."

"I know what it *isn't*. It isn't arsenic or nightshade or any of the forty-one compounds you've trained my tongue to recognize. It's a meal somebody's grandmother would recognize. And it came through a woman who can't cook like that sober, let alone asleep." I leaned forward. "Merrick. Someone answered her prayer. Through the kitchen. Through *my table*. So either you tell me what's happening, or I start asking louder people."

For a long moment he just looked at me, and I understood that he was doing arithmetic too — weighing what I already knew against what I might survive knowing. Nine years of trust, or whatever we'd called it, balanced against two hundred years of law.

Then he opened the book.

The pages were vellum gone soft as cloth, the ink faded to the brown of old wounds. The first page held three lines in a hand so formal it barely qualified as writing:

**NO GOD MAY ENTER THE VALLEY OF IRONHOLLOW.**

**NO SOUL MAY LEAVE IT.**

**WHAT IS BOUND BELOW STAYS BELOW.**

"That's it?" I said. "Two hundred years and the founding document is three sentences?"

"Three sentences ended the Godswars," Merrick said. "Do you know why they built the valley here? Not the farms, not the palace — the *valley*? Salt in the soil. Iron in the bedrock. Cold springs running through limestone. They found the one place in the world where the old things cannot reach, and they built a kingdom on top of it like a cork in a bottle." He tapped the second line. "No soul may leave. You've heard that all your life as piety. It's not piety. It's plumbing."

"Plumbing."

"A seal has two directions, Beatrix. Nothing gets in. Nothing gets out." He closed the book with exaggerated gentleness, as if it might bruise. "Every wall in this palace is salted mortar. Every wellhead is blessed with iron filings. The choir sings the Dampening at dawn and dusk, and half of them think it's tradition. Your whole life, every ritual you've ever rolled your eyes at — it's containment. All of it."

"And the food?" My voice came out steadier than I felt. "What does dinner have to do with any of this?"

"Nothing. That's the point. Dinner should have nothing to do with anything. The seal holds or it doesn't, and if it holds, no power in the world reaches inside to whisper recipes into a sleeping cook's ear." He sank into his chair at last, and the fear came back, and this time he let me see it. "Unless the seal is failing. Unless something below is pushing hard enough that it's leaking *upward* now."

Below. The phrase snagged on something, some half-heard nursery warning — *be good or the folk beneath will hear you* — that every Ironhollow child absorbs with their mother's milk and forgets by school age. I'd thought it was a bogeyman story. Looking at Merrick's face, I revised my opinion of bogeymen considerably.

"What's below?"

"Older than the crown. Older than the wars that made the crown necessary. When the founders sealed the valley, they didn't destroy what they were hiding from. You can't kill a god, Beatrix. You can only make somewhere it can't follow." He rubbed his eyes. "We bound it under the mountain. And now, apparently, it's hungry enough to start ordering off the menu above."

"So report it upward. To whoever's above *you*."

"There is nothing above me in this matter." He said it flatly, and I believed him, which was somehow worse. "The Steward of Tasters answers to the Crown for poisons. For *this*—" he touched the iron-clasped book, "—the Steward answers to the Founding Law. Alone. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Two hundred years of Stewards, each one the sole keeper of this knowledge, each one hoping to die before it matters."

"And today it matters."

"Today a cook dreamed a recipe that died out with the Godswars, and served it to the queen's table, and the vessel assigned to protect Her Majesty tasted it and lived." His eyes came up and fixed on mine, and there was something in them I couldn't read — grief, or guilt, or both wearing each other's coats. "Lived, Beatrix. An offering passes through the intended recipient untouched. Do you know what that means?"

"That I'm not the intended recipient."

"It means the gods don't consider you a guest at that table." He said it very softly. "They consider you furniture."

I laughed. I honestly couldn't help it — one short, ugly bark of a laugh, the kind that escapes when the alternative is screaming. Furniture. Nine years of service, nine hundred dishes, a tongue trained finer than any jeweler's scale, and the verdict from upstairs was: *not on the list of living things worth feeding.*

But even as I laughed, some colder clerk in the back of my mind was filing the detail away. Untouched by the offering. Immune to everything. Merrick had spent nine years building that immunity into me, dose by measured dose, and I had always assumed the purpose was the obvious one. Sitting in that curtained room with the Founding Law open between us, I felt the assumption shift under me like ice on a spring river.

An immune body is useful against poison.

An immune body is also useful against other things.

"Merrick," I said carefully. "Why me? Out of all the orphans in this valley, why did they pick me for the tasting?"

Something flickered behind his eyes — the look of a man watching a door he meant to keep shut swing inward on its own. "Because you had the constitution for it."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the answer I'm authorized to give." He stood, abruptly, all business again, the Steward reassembling himself piece by piece. "Here's what happens now. The cook goes on the punishment roster — standard infraction, unauthorized recipe, flogging. Public enough to satisfy procedure, private enough that nobody asks why a seasoning error earns ten lashes. You'll log the incident as contamination and taste tomorrow's meals as usual. And you will speak of this to no one."

"Flogged." The word sat wrong in my mouth. "She didn't poison anyone, Merrick. She fed something that wasn't even aimed at us. She's a victim of the same anomaly I am."

"She's a leak." He said it without heat, which made it worse. "And leaks get sealed. That's not cruelty, Beatrix, that's the Founding Law working exactly as designed. No god may enter. No soul may leave. No crack may spread." He met my eyes. "You're a taster. You know better than anyone how much of safety is just deciding what to swallow."

* * *

They flogged her in the courtyard at dusk, because the crown believes punishment improves with an audience.

I stood in the ranks of household staff with my hands folded and my face arranged into the mild boredom of a man watching weather. Ten lashes with a short whip, administered by a bored sergeant to a woman half again my age who did not cry out. That was the worst part. Not the sound of the lash — I'd heard it before, everyone in the palace had — but the silence. Cook Ellery took each stroke the way she'd taken my news that morning: with the terrible calm of someone who has already been somewhere worse in her dreams.

Her eyes found mine over the heads of the crowd. Once. Just once, in the middle of it, and there was no accusation in them, which was somehow more unbearable than hatred would have been. She looked at me the way you look at the last person who'll understand what you're about to say.

I told myself it meant nothing. I told myself she was delirious with pain. I told myself a great many efficient lies while the sergeant counted seven, eight, nine.

On the tenth stroke she sagged in the restraints, and the crowd murmured its satisfied murmur, and the ceremony dissolved the way ceremonies do — into gossip and cold air and the ordinary machinery of a palace evening. I hung back until the courtyard emptied, until it was just the two of us and a guard who had discovered urgent business elsewhere, and I helped the physician's assistant walk her to the infirmary, because I was her taster and she had fed me for nine years and there are debts even the Founding Law doesn't itemize.

She smelled of salt and smoke and the particular copper scent that follows a flogging. Halfway down the corridor, leaning most of her weight on my arm, she spoke for the first time since the courtyard.

"They got it wrong," she rasped.

"The sentence? Aye, ten was harsh—"

"No." She stopped walking, and her grip on my arm tightened with sudden, desperate strength. "The *law*. The Steward's law. No god may enter." Her breath whistled through her teeth. "Master Dravenmoor, the seal doesn't keep them *out*."

"Ellery—"

"It keeps them *down*." Her eyes were bright and wet and utterly lucid, and I felt the floor of the world tilt a degree beneath my feet. "There's a difference. There's all the difference in the world. Things that are kept out stay outside. Things kept *down* press *up*. And they've started pressing, child, and they remember the old doors, and one of those doors—" she swallowed, wincing, "—one of those doors is standing right in front of me, wearing your face."

My mouth went dry. "I don't understand."

"No," she agreed, almost gently. "Not yet. But you will, and when you do—" She fumbled at her collar, and pressed something small and smooth into my palm, closing my fingers over it with hers. Bone, worn soft by handling. Carved. A whistle. "When you do, go where the hum is loudest. And ask for Vespera."

"Who's Vespera?"

But her eyes had already drifted past me, down the long corridor toward the kitchen fires, toward whatever dream had carried a dead recipe into her sleeping hands. She smiled — small, tired, and certain as a signed confession.

"Hungry," she murmured. "Someone down there is so terribly hungry. And they know your name, Master Dravenmoor. They've known it far longer than you have."
