## 3. What the Cook Knew

They flogged Marrow Tam at noon, in the yard where the palace kept its woodpile, and by evening he was dead of nothing at all.

I want to be precise about that, because precision is the only prayer I know. The Steward of Tasters did not order him killed. The Steward ordered him flogged — twenty strokes for the crime of serving an unsanctioned dish to the queen's table — and then everyone went about their business, and Marrow Tam went and died. Natural causes. That was the phrase on the ledger, written in the neat grey hand of the infirmary clerk: *death by natural causes.* As though his back had simply opened like a ripe fruit. As though flesh had opinions.

Twenty lashes does not kill a man who has stood over a hot stove for thirty years. Twenty lashes kills a man who is old, or sick, or whose heart has already decided it is finished with the world. Or — and I have had two days now to turn this over, and I still don't like what my hands keep finding — twenty lashes kill a man when someone in the infirmary has a steady hand and a small vial and a reason.

I was not present for the flogging. Tasters are not permitted to watch punishments; we are instruments, and instruments are kept clean and apart. But I heard it. Sound carries strangely in Ironhollow, up through the valley's stone throat, and every stroke came to me in my cell as a soft wet knock, like someone knocking politely at a door from inside a coffin. Twenty knocks. Then quiet. Then, much later, the sound of a body being moved, which is a sound I knew before I could write my own name: the particular drag-and-settle of dead weight handled by men who want their shift to end.

Here is what I did instead of watching: I ate my supper. All of it. Cold mutton, bread gone stiff at the edges, a cup of small beer. I chewed each mouthful forty times, the way they trained me, and I swallowed everything, and nothing in any of it fought me, and I sat there in the lamplight hating how easy it was. Nine years of swallowing death for the crown and the one thing I cannot make myself choke down is guilt. It sits wrong. It sits heavy. It has no antidote I've been taught.

* * *

The infirmary smelled of vinegar and boiled linen, which is the smell of everywhere in this palace pretending to be clean.

I told myself I had no business going there. Tasters report anomalies; we do not visit them. I had reported mine, hadn't I? I had walked into the Steward's office that first morning with the honeyed quince still on my tongue and said, in my flattest voice, *the seventh dish was not poison.* And I watched a man who has never once in nine years shown me anything but bored competence go white to the lips, whiter than the ledger paper he gripped, and say, "Say that again."

"The seventh dish. Quince in saffron honey, garnished with candied violets. It should have been dosed — every dish from the queen's table passes through the taster's kitchen, that's protocol — but this one wasn't dosed. It was something else. I don't have the word for what it was." I paused, because the next part felt like stepping off a ledge in the dark. "It tasted like being prayed over."

The Steward looked at me for a long moment. Somewhere behind him a clock ticked with the patience of a thing that outlives everyone. "You will not repeat that," he said. "Not to me. Not to anyone. There was no anomaly. The dish was dosed. You are tired."

"I'm never tired," I said. "That's the point of me."

He wrote something in a book. He ordered the cook flogged. He did not look at me again, and I understood, with the slow cold certainty of ice forming on a pond, that I had seen something I was not built to survive seeing — and that the Steward knew it too, because the Steward knows everything that enters or leaves this valley, including, I suspect, me.

So yes. I had no business in the infirmary. I went anyway.

Marrow Tam lay in the last cot against the wall, a mountain of a man brought low, his broad back bandaged in strips gone rust-brown at the seams. His breathing made a sound like a bellows with a tear in it. The infirmarer, a woman named Hesk with forearms like a blacksmith's, glanced up from her mortar and gave me the flat look she gives everyone.

"He's dying," she said, before I asked.

"Of what?"

"Of natural causes." She ground her pestle harder. "That's what I'm instructed to write, so that's what's true today. If you're here to question him, question him fast and quiet. And if you're here to bring him water, bring it yourself; my hands are full."

I crossed to the cot. Up close, Marrow Tam was less a man than a landscape — weathered, creased, mapped with old burns from a lifetime of leaning over fire. His eyes were closed, but his lips were moving, shaping words too soft to catch. Praying, I thought, and the thought landed in my stomach like a swallowed stone, because praying was illegal in Ironhollow. Not discouraged. Illegal. First law of the founding charter, carved over the valley gate where every child learns to read it before they learn their letters:

***No god shall enter. No soul shall leave.***

I had always assumed the second half was about death. Everyone assumes that. Souls leave at death; the law just means you die here, you're buried here, your bones belong to the crown. It had never once occurred to me, in nine years of walking these halls, to ask what else might count as *leaving.*

His eyes opened when my shadow fell across him. They were pale, almost milk-colored, and they found my face with terrible accuracy for a dying man.

"You're the taster," he rasped. "Dravenmoor. The ninth dish man."

"They call me that?"

"They call you worse." Something that might have been a smile cracked the burnt landscape of his face. "You didn't die. Seventh course. I watched them carry the tray past and I thought, well, there goes another one, and then you didn't die, and I laughed until the flogging started, and laughing while they flog you is a bad idea. Don't laugh while they flog you."

"I'm sorry," I said. "For the flogging. For—" I gestured at all of him, the ruin of it. "I reported it. The dish. I didn't think—"

"You thought reporting was the right thing." He wheezed. "It was. It wasn't. Both. That's the valley, taster. Everything true here cuts both ways." His hand came out from under the blanket — huge, trembling, spotted like an old apple — and caught my wrist with surprising strength. "Come closer. My voice doesn't carry far anymore."

I leaned in. Close enough to smell woodsmoke and vinegar and the sweetish undertone of a body quietly closing its accounts.

"They answered somebody," he whispered. "Understand? The gods. Somebody in this valley prayed hard enough, long enough, secret enough, and something under the mountain heard, and it wanted to send word, and it used my kitchen. Used *my hands*. I didn't choose it. I woke up three nights ago with flour on my arms and a recipe in my head that I never learned and couldn't stop humming. Saffron. Honey. Violets." His grip tightened. "I cooked a god's letter, taster. And you read it. You're the only one alive who read it."

"The Steward says there was no anomaly."

"The Steward would say the sun was a rumor if the charter paid him enough." A cough took him, long and wet, and when it released him he sagged into the cot like a puppet with cut strings. But his hand didn't let go of my wrist. "Listen. Listen, because I haven't got the time to say it twice and you haven't got the sense to believe it once. Your immunity isn't training. I've fed this palace thirty years. I've watched tasters come and go — they burn out, they sicken, they get reassigned somewhere quiet. You don't burn. Nine years and you don't burn. That's not a constitution, boy. That's a *design*."

"I was selected for tolerance. Everyone knows—"

"Everyone knows what they're told." His milk-pale eyes fixed on mine, and I swear to every outlawed god that there was pity in them. Pity, aimed at me, from a dying cook. "There's a priestess. Under the stones. Old foundations, older than the charter. She'll explain what you are better than I can — I only ever held the bread." His other hand fumbled beneath the blanket, at his side, and came out holding something small and pale. "Take it. Take it *now*, before Hesk turns around."

"What is it?"

"A whistle. Bone. Carved it myself from a shinbone I probably shouldn't name." He pressed it into my palm and folded my fingers over it, and the bone was warm, which bone should not be, and faintly it hummed against my skin like a bee trapped in a jar. "Follow the hum. It knows where she sleeps. Tell her—" and here his voice broke, actually broke, a crack running through it like the bellows-tear in his breathing, "tell her the kitchens remember. Tell her the bread got through. Every week. Thirty years. Tell her it mattered."

"Mattered to whom? Who is she? What do I tell her—"

"*Vespera,*" he breathed.

And then he died.

I mean that plainly, without drama, because it happened plainly. One breath he was looking at me with those milk-pale pitying eyes; the next breath the looking stopped, mid-word, as though someone had simply lifted the man out of his body and left the body as a courtesy. His hand slid off my wrist and thumped against the cot rail.

Hesk turned around. She looked at the cot, at me, at the cot again, and sighed the sigh of a woman adding a line to a ledger. "Natural causes," she said, mostly to herself, and reached for her ink.

I stood there with a dead man's warmth still trapped in my fist and a bone whistle humming against my palm like something alive, like something *waiting*, and I did the only thing my training equipped me to do.

I thanked the infirmarer for her time and walked out with steady steps and an empty face, past the vinegar and the boiled linen, into a corridor lit grey with evening, and I did not shake until I was three doors away, alone, with my back against cold stone.

* * *

Here is the arithmetic that kept me awake that night, and I set it down now the way I set down dishes, methodically, one at a time, tasting each for what's hidden in it:

One. A cook died of natural causes within six hours of telling a taster that gods answer prayers in Ironhollow. In a valley where the founding law forbids gods. Where the Steward of Tasters controls the infirmary's ledgers.

Two. Marrow Tam was old, but he was not dying. I have eaten beside this court long enough to recognize a scheduled death when it's served to me. This one was plated beautifully.

Three. The whistle hums. It has not stopped humming since he pressed it into my hand. Quieter now, patient, the way a cat purrs when it has decided to wait you out. Bone does not hum. Whatever Marrow Tam carved that whistle from, it is listening.

Four. He called me *boy*. Nobody calls me boy. To the court I am a function — the ninth dish, the instrument, the vessel-shaped vacancy in a livery uniform — and functions don't get called boy by old men who feed palaces.

Five — and this is the one I keep circling back to, the pit at the center of the fruit — he trusted *me*. A man who had spent thirty years surviving this valley, who knew enough to hide prayers in bread for decades, who must have known exactly what happens to people who talk — that man spent his final breath handing his most dangerous secret to the crown's own poison-eater. Why?

Because I read the letter. Because I didn't die. Because somewhere under thirty years of caution, an old cook looked at the queen's taster and saw not an instrument of the crown but a fellow prisoner of it — the only other person in Ironhollow who had been touched by the same impossible thing and lived.

He trusted me, and his trust got him flogged, and his flogging got him killed, and none of it would have touched him if I had kept my mouth shut about the seventh course. I could have swallowed that quince in silence. Swallowed it, logged it as routine, carried the anomaly in my chest like everything else I carry, and Marrow Tam would be stirring pots tomorrow with flour on his arms and a hymn stuck in his head.

Instead I did the dutiful thing. The correct thing. The thing I was *made* for — and I am beginning to hate that word, *made*, because Marrow Tam used it too, and dying men don't choose words carelessly.

*That's not a constitution. That's a design.*

I lay in my cell with the whistle cupped against my sternum, feeling it hum its small stubborn note against my skin, and I grieved for him the way I know how: precisely, silently, with no antidote. And underneath the grief, quieter and colder, something else was waking up. Curiosity, maybe. Or its uglier twin, the one that looks like curiosity but tastes like defiance.

Because here is the truth I keep arriving at, the taste that won't wash out: for nine years I have believed I was safe. That was the whole bargain — I swallow poison so the queen can eat, and in exchange the valley keeps me, feeds me, shelters me, keeps me *safe*. Safe from blades, safe from toxins, safe from everything the world could throw at a motherless boy with no family to miss him.

But a valley with a law against gods is not a shelter. It's a wall. And walls are not built around things that are safe.

Walls are built around things that are *dangerous*.

The whistle pulsed against my breastbone, once, twice, patient as a heartbeat that wasn't mine. Outside my cell window, the mountain that anchors the north of the valley stood black against the stars, and I found myself staring at it the way you stare at a stranger across a feast table — aware, suddenly, that it has been watching you the entire meal.

Tomorrow, I decided. Tomorrow I would follow the hum.

Somewhere beneath the stones of Ironhollow, a woman named Vespera had been fed smuggled prayers for thirty years by a dead man's hands, and she knew what I was.

I very much wanted to know too.
