## 4. The Priestess in the Wall

The whistle hummed.

Not a metaphorical hum, not the hum of guilt or conscience or any of the other things I'd have preferred it to be. It sat on my palm in the dark of my cell and vibrated against my skin like an insect with opinions, and when I closed my fist around it, I could feel the note travel up through my wrist bones, into my elbow, into the hollow behind my sternum where nine years of poison had carved out whatever soft thing used to live there.

I should have thrown it into the privy. That was the sensible move, the move that kept tasters alive: swallow what's given to you, taste nothing, report nothing, want nothing. Nine years of discipline told me exactly what to do with a dead cook's bone whistle.

Instead I put it in my mouth.

Don't ask me why. Or rather — ask, and I'll give you the answer I gave myself at three o'clock that morning, standing barefoot on cold flagstone like an idiot: *Marlo died pressing this into your hand, and the least you owe him is finding out why.* It's a good answer. It has grief in it, which makes it respectable. The truer answer was simpler and less flattering: for nine years I had been a man whose entire purpose was detecting things hidden in other things, and here was a thing hidden in a thing, humming at me, and I had never once in my life been able to leave a locked door alone. Poison immunity doesn't come from obedience. It comes from being the kind of man who tastes the stew twice.

The whistle tasted like marrow and salt and, faintly, of bread.

And it pulled. There was no better word for it. The moment my breath moved through it, the sound went somewhere — not out into the room, but *down*, the way water goes down, the way roots go down — and something down there answered with a second note, faint as a pulse under a wrist, and the whistle leaned in my grip like a compass needle finding its north.

North, in Ironhollow, meant the mountain. But this pull didn't go toward the mountain. It went *under*.

* * *

Ironhollow was built on its own foundations the way old cities are: stone stacked on older stone stacked on stone so ancient no one bothered to remember laying it. The valley floor wasn't floor at all but a lid, and every building in the lower quarter — the granary, the tannery row, the barracks, the kitchens where Marlo had bled out on his own scrubbed tiles — stood on top of whatever the founders had buried first.

I followed the hum through streets still gray with pre-dawn, past the night watch dozing at their post, past the baker's window where the first loaves were going in, and the whole time the whistle sang its thin secret note against my ribs, and the note said *down, down, down.*

It led me to the tanner's yard, which was the least romantic destination imaginable, and then past it, to a stretch of foundation wall where the stones were fitted so tightly you couldn't have slid a knife between them. Except that the wall was breathing.

I want to be clear about how much I did not want that wall to be breathing. Walls are supposed to be the one reliable category of thing. In a life where the queen's soup might kill you and the king's smile might mean anything and the Steward of Tasters could order a man flogged to death over a plate of pheasant, a wall ought to hold still.

But there it was: a faint expansion and contraction in the mortar line at knee height, slow, patient, rhythmic as sleep. And threaded through the rhythm, almost too quiet to catch, a voice.

Not words. Not yet. A voice doing the work of words the way a candle does the work of the sun — same principle, smaller scale. Someone behind or beneath or inside that wall was murmuring, and each murmur made the stones shift by the width of a hair, and each shift let out a thread of warmth that smelled, unmistakably, of fresh bread.

Prayers smuggled in bread. I wouldn't understand that until later. At the time all I knew was that the smell made my eyes sting, because it smelled like the orphanage kitchen in the years before the palace took me, and grief is a scent before it's ever a feeling.

I knelt. I put my mouth near the mortar line. I said, with all the authority of a man in his nightshirt kneeling in goose filth at the base of a wall:

"I know you're there."

The murmuring stopped.

For a long moment there was nothing but my own heartbeat and the distant, ordinary sounds of a valley waking up that did not know yet what was living in its bones. Then, from somewhere inside the stone, dry as paper and twice as tired:

"Then you know more than the ones who built it. Come around the east face. There's a gap behind the rain barrel. Mind the third step; it's been rotting since before your grandmother."

* * *

The gap was real. Behind the rain barrel, where two foundation stones had settled apart sometime in the last century, there was a slot barely wider than my shoulders, exhaling warm air that tasted of yeast and lamp oil and something older underneath both — the smell of a place that had never seen weather. The rotten step turned out to be a slab that tipped under my weight and dropped me into a short slide of scree and old rubble, and then I was standing in a space that should not have existed.

A chamber. Low-ceilinged, no bigger than a decent cell, built into the valley's foundations from stones that bore markings I recognized without knowing how: spirals, open hands, a circle bisected by a single vertical line. The old signs. The signs that appeared nowhere above ground anymore, because above ground was the point — above ground was scrubbed clean.

In the middle of the chamber, on a pallet thin enough to be a rumor, sat a woman.

She was younger than her hands and older than her eyes, if that makes sense. It probably doesn't. Her hair was white — not aged-white, but white the way frost is white, deliberate about it — and she wore robes that had once been magnificent and now consisted mainly of patches holding other patches together, embroidered along every seam with tiny stitched prayers in no alphabet I knew. Around her neck hung a pendant: a circle bisected by a line, worked in tarnished silver.

Between her teeth, half-eaten, was a heel of bread.

She swallowed it with dignity, brushed the crumbs from her lap into her palm, and poured them into a clay bowl beside her with the care of a priestess making an offering. Which, I would learn, was exactly what they were.

"You're the taster," she said.

"And you're the reason the kitchens answered back."

Her eyebrows rose a fraction. "You know about that?"

"A dish at the queen's feast tasted like a prayer instead of a poison. My cook got flogged for it. He died last night giving me a bone whistle and a name." I held up the whistle. "Vespera."

Something crossed her face — not surprise. Recognition, maybe, of a bill arriving that she'd known was coming. "He remembered it, then. All the way to the end." She looked at her hands. "Bread goes in every morning through the baker who owes me his daughter's fever-broke. Prayers go in folded inside it. Words written small, wishes whispered over the crust, griefs pressed flat between the crumb. For six years I've eaten this valley's smuggled devotions, and I've carried them down to the sleeping gods beneath us, one crumb at a time." She lifted her chin. "Three nights ago, someone prayed so hard and so true that the gods woke enough to answer — and the nearest open channel was a royal kitchen. So yes. The feast dish was an offering. The gods sent it up through the flour and the fire, and your cook was simply the poor fool standing closest when heaven knocked."

"He died for a knock."

"He died for a law." Her voice hardened, and the chamber seemed to tighten around the words, the way a drumhead tightens before a beat. "You've served the crown nine years and you don't even know the founding law of the place that owns you? No god may enter Ironhollow. No soul may leave it. Those are the twin pillars everything else stands on. Every warding stone, every boundary of salt, every rule of every office — including yours. The valley isn't a fortress, taster. Fortresses keep armies out. This is a *seal*. And seals are only ever built around one thing."

"Which is?"

"Whatever the kingdom was most afraid of losing." She studied me then, properly, the way I studied dishes — looking for the poison in me. "Or most afraid of letting go."

I laughed. I'm slightly proud of that; it came out steady. "I'm a taster. I was an orphan. I have a cell, a stipend, and a set of very expensive kidneys. If the kingdom sealed an entire valley to guard me, someone wasted an extraordinary amount of masonry."

"Did they?" Vespera reached beneath her pallet and drew out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, unwrapping it with the reverence of someone handling relics, which — I understood soon enough — was precisely what she thought she was doing. Inside lay papers. Old ones. Some so old the ink had gone brown and shy, retreating into the vellum. "Six years I've been down here, and I haven't been idle. The founders left records. The priests who built the seal left *more* than records. They left the terms."

"Terms of what?"

"Of the sale."

The word landed in the chamber and didn't echo, because stone that thick doesn't echo; it just absorbs, the way it had absorbed her voice for six years, the way it had absorbed — I began to suspect — considerably more than that.

"Sale," I repeated. "Of what?"

"Before you were born," she said, gently, the way you speak to a man standing on ice that's already cracking, "there was a bargain struck between the crown of this valley and the court beneath the mountain. The crown wanted protection — the old gods rising, the borders failing, wars coming that magic would decide. The court below wanted a vessel. Something bred pure, raised immune, hollowed by degrees until it could hold what needed holding. The crown searched its foundlings and found an infant whose blood already ran strange. And they signed him over." She turned a page toward me. Brown ink. A signature I didn't recognize and one I did — a title, not a name, but a title that sat in my chest like a swallowed coal.

*Steward of Tasters, for the Crown, witnesseth—*

"The soul of the child," Vespera read aloud, quietly, "is conveyed unto the court beneath, to be held in trust until the vessel be ripe. The body shall remain above, and be prepared by gradual means. Immunity shall be built into the flesh, brick by brick, that the vessel close itself against all intrusions save the appointed one."

The chamber was very quiet. Somewhere above us, Ironhollow was waking — cart wheels, a dog, the baker's boy singing off-key — and all of it sounded like it belonged to someone else's life.

"That's not possible," I said.

"No?"

"I have a soul. I can feel it. It's right—" I gestured at my own chest, at the hollow behind the sternum, and stopped, because my hand knew the shape of that hollow the way a tongue knows a missing tooth. Nine years I'd assumed the poisons had carved it. Nine years I'd worn the emptiness like a professional scar, a cost of service, a thing to be quietly proud of.

"What you feel," Vespera said, "is the walls of a house. Not what lived in it."

I sat down. I don't remember deciding to; my legs simply resigned. I sat down on the floor of a hidden chamber beneath the valley that had raised me, holding a dead man's whistle, looking at a page that said I had been sold before I could walk, and I did the arithmetic I'd been doing all night and all my life without knowing it.

Nine years of dishes. Nine years of doses measured to the grain, escalating on a schedule I'd been told was tradition. Nine years of the Steward's careful, approving nods — *good, Beatrix, very good, you barely felt that one at all* — and the small cold pride I'd taken in barely feeling anything at all.

They hadn't been protecting me. Protection leaves marks of care. This had been construction. Every immunity a brick. Every meal a lesson in closing. Every year another layer laid down over the empty place, until the house stood finished and waiting, and the only question left was *when*.

"They were ripening me," I said.

"Yes."

"For a god."

"For the returning one. At the eclipse." She said it without drama, which somehow made it worse. Drama would have let me argue. Flat fact gives you nowhere to sit except on top of it.

I looked at my hands. Taster's hands, scarred and steady, hands that had never trembled over a dish in nine years. They were trembling now, and I hated them for it, and then I hated myself for hating them, because what had those hands ever actually been *for*? A vessel doesn't get a purpose. A vessel gets a function, and functions are always someone else's.

Here is the vertigo nobody warns you about. It isn't falling. Falling has direction. This was discovering that the floor you've stood on your entire life was installed by other people, for purposes of their own, over a pit — and that the vertigo isn't fear of falling. It's the suspicion that there may be no *you* left to fall. If my soul was sold before I could walk, then who had spent these nine years tasting? Who was sitting here grieving? Some clerk of flesh, some caretaker running the property until the owner returned?

"Taster." Vespera's voice cut through, sharp as a bell. "Look at me."

I looked.

"You're thinking that there's nothing under the bricks. That they hollowed you so thoroughly there's no one home." Her eyes were fierce and very bright. "I have spent six years carrying prayers to gods who were asleep, and I will tell you the one thing I know for certain about sealed things: a seal is proof of contents. Nobody builds a vault around emptiness. Nobody breeds a fortress around a vacant lot. Whatever they sold, whatever they think they own — it's still *in there*, Beatrix Dravenmoor, or they'd never have gone to such lengths to keep you fed and closed and safe."

My name in her mouth did something the ledger couldn't. It landed like a key in a lock I'd forgotten I had.

"How do you know my name?"

"Your cook prayed for you," she said simply. "Every day, folded into the bread. *Keep the boy safe. Keep the boy whole. Let the boy out.*" She picked up the bone whistle from where it had rolled against my knee and closed my fingers around it, hers cool and certain over mine. "Marlo knew what you were before either of us did. He tended the vessel and he prayed for the man, and he trusted the man to the priestess. That's why the whistle sings to you. It's not mine. It never was. Bone remembers who it was given to."

Above us, distantly, a bell rang the hour. Sixth bell. In ninety minutes I was due in the tasting chamber with my report on the feast anomaly, standing before the Steward of Tasters — the man whose signature sat brown and patient on a page six feet underground, conveying me, before my birth, to a court beneath a mountain.

I thought about what I would say. *The dish was an offering, sir. The gods are answering prayers in the royal kitchens, sir. Also, sir, you sold me.*

I thought about Marlo bleeding out on clean tile while the valley slept.

I thought about the hollow behind my sternum, and whether it was a wound or a doorway, and which of those I'd rather it be.

"Vespera," I said. "How long do I have before they notice the cook talked to me?"

She glanced at the whistle in my fist, at my face, at the gap in the wall where daylight was beginning to leak in gray and thin.

"Longer than you'll want," she said, "and shorter than you'll need. Go make your report. Lie beautifully. And taster—" I was already turning for the gap, already rehearsing the lie, when her voice caught me like a hand on the collar. "When the Steward looks at you tomorrow, look back. Look at his *hands*. Men who sell children sign away their own steadiness. You'll see it. You've spent nine years learning to read what people hide."

I climbed out through the wall of the world I'd grown up in, into the gray light and the baking smells and the off-key singing, and everything above ground looked different now — thinner, like paint over stone, like crust over crumb.

I had a report to give and a lie to build and a signature to look at.

I walked toward the palace, and beneath my boots, all the way, the valley hummed.
