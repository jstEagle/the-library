## 5. Nine Years of Poison

I did not sleep that night. I lay on my narrow bed in the tasters' wing and watched the ceiling beam and tried very hard not to do arithmetic.

I failed, of course. I have always been good at sums. It was one of the things they liked about me.

Nine years. Two hundred and eighty-one dishes a year, by the palace calendar of feasts and fast days — more, if the queen was melancholy, because melancholy queens eat constantly, the way some people pray. Call it twenty-six hundred dishes. Twenty-six hundred small deaths rehearsed in my mouth and swallowed down like communion. And every single one had been a brick.

That was the word Vespera used, there in the dark behind the wall, her voice dry as old mortar: *bricks*. They were building something out of me, dish by dish, dose by dose, and they had been building it since before I could walk.

You cannot hear a thing like that and simply lie down. Or you can — but first you have to take the whole edifice apart, brick by brick, and count them. So I did. All night. Here is what I found.

* * *

The first brick was laid when I was six years old, though I didn't know it was a brick then. I thought it was soup.

I remember the orphanage at Corren's Ford only in pieces now: the smell of lye soap, the sound of sixty children breathing in one long room, Sister Marle's habit dragging wet hem-marks across the flagstones every morning. I remember being hungry in the ordinary way, the way everyone was hungry, which is to say permanently and without drama.

And I remember the man who came in winter. He wore grey, unremarkable grey, and he carried a leather case, and he asked Sister Marle for the children who never got sick.

Not the strong ones. Not the clever ones. The ones who *never got sick*.

I had gone four years without so much as a fever. While other children coughed through the damp months like dying birds, I stayed stubbornly, boringly well. Sister Marle called it God's favor. The grey man wrote it in his book as though it were a figure in a ledger, which, I understand now, it was.

There was a test. I remember it better than almost anything else from those years, because it was the first time anyone had ever told me I was special, and I was six, and I drank it up like the sweet milk it came with. He gave each child a spoonful of something bitter and watched what happened. Most children vomited. One boy went grey and had to be carried to the infirmary. I sat there tasting pennyroyal and wormwood and something else, something cold and green-tasting that I would later learn was a distillation of widow's bane, and I felt nothing at all except disappointment that the milk afterward was such a small cupful.

The grey man smiled at me. I remember thinking his smile was kind. It took me nineteen years and one priestess-in-a-wall to understand that he had smiled the way a mason smiles at a good stone — checking the grain, tapping for flaws, pleased with the weight of it in his hand.

"You'll come with us," he said. "There's work for a boy with your constitution."

My constitution. As if I were a building. Which, of course —

Well. Brick by brick.

* * *

They did not tell me I was to be a poison-taster. They told me I was going to school.

In a sense this was true. The tasters' wing had lessons: languages, heraldry, the heraldry especially, because you must know at a glance whose arms are on the seal of an unopened letter if someone might have ground glass into the wax. We learned botany from a bent old herbalist who made us identify forty-one poisonous plants by leaf alone, blindfolded, and rapped our knuckles when we erred. We learned the taste profiles of arsenic (almond, faintly, and sweetness that sits wrong), of hemlock (numbness starting at the tongue's tip), of aconite (a tingling like bees under the skin).

We learned all this as if we were learning to *detect* poisons. That is what we believed. That is what we were told. A taster's job, we thought, was to stand between the crown and death, alert as a hound, ready to fall so the sovereign might live.

Here is what no one said aloud, because it was never true: a taster who can be killed is a liability. A taster who falls tells you only that the dish was poisoned — after the fact, after the panic, after the queen has already half-risen from her chair. What the court actually wanted, what the court had wanted for generations, was a taster who could eat anything and rise, unbowed, and nod, and let the feast go on.

So they dosed us. Small doses at first, in our evening tea — we were told it was a tonic, bitter with iron and herbs, "for the constitution." Then larger doses, disguised in the training meals. Widow's bane on Mondays. Salt-of-tombwater in the pottage. Once a month, the grey man — Master Quillon, though we only learned his name years later — drew our blood into little vials and made notes in his book, and if our numbers pleased him we got an extra honey cake.

I want to be clear about how it felt from the inside, because from the outside it must look like atrocity, and from the inside it looked like — nothing. It looked like childhood. It looked like warm beds after Corren's Ford, and enough food, and lessons, and Master Quillon's rare smile, and the honey cakes. They built their vessel out of a child and the child thanked them for it. There is a cruelty so complete that it wears the face of rescue, and I lived inside its mouth for nine years believing I had been saved.

* * *

There were others, at first. Four of us arrived that winter: me; a girl named Odile who could eat spoiled meat without ill effect; twin boys, Perrin and Aldous, whose gift was subtler — they never burned fevers, never swelled from stings, their bodies declining to react to anything whatsoever.

Odile lasted three years. She caught a chill — a real one, an honest winter chill — and it settled in her chest and would not leave, because whatever made her proof against poison had apparently used up all her body's argumentativeness. I held her hand while she coughed. I was eleven. I did not understand yet that she was the first draft of my own ending: the vessel discarded when it cracked.

The twins lasted longer. They were dosed harder than any of us — their bodies simply shrugged everything off, and Quillon grew fascinated, escalating month by month, chasing the edge of what they could survive. I remember the night Perrin stopped shrugging. He ate his supper, all of it, asked for seconds, and by morning his heart had simply decided it was finished. No convulsion, no warning. Aldous was moved to another wing after that. I saw him once more, years later, across a courtyard, and he looked through me the way you look through furniture.

And I remained. The last stone still standing after the weather has taken the rest of the wall. I told myself for years that this meant I was the strongest. That I was the best, that Quillon had chosen rightly, that my survival was excellence.

Vespera disabused me of that in about eleven words. "A vessel that survives," she said, "is not a vessel that was loved. It is a vessel that was needed."

Brick by brick. Every immunity a course of masonry. Every honey cake a trowelful of mortar.

* * *

Some bricks I can date precisely.

Seven years ago: the first dish served at a royal table. Queen Isolde's midwinter feast, and I, fifteen, standing behind her left shoulder in my first proper livery, sweating in a way that had nothing to do with heat. The boar in wine sauce tasted of nothing dangerous, and I swallowed, and nodded, and the hall exhaled, and I walked back to my cell that night feeling ten feet tall. The most important boy in the valley. The shield of the realm. I lay awake replaying the moment the queen glanced back at me — one glance, half a heartbeat — and I built an entire identity out of it.

Six years ago: the night I ate the dish that should have killed me and knew, absolutely knew, that something in it had been meant for the queen — and realized, standing there while the court watched, that I could not tell anymore whether my body's calm was courage or chemistry. That was the night I started keeping my own private ledger of tastes, in a cipher of my own devising, hidden under the loose floorboard. Not evidence. Just — proof to myself that my mouth belonged to me. That somewhere under all the tonics and the training doses there was still a Beatrix doing the tasting, and not merely a well-built jar.

Four years ago: Master Quillon retired. Or "retired." He was replaced by the current Steward of Tasters, a soft-spoken man with ink-stained fingers and a memory like a tax roll, and the tonics changed recipe — subtly, once, just once — and my blood vials began to be collected twice monthly instead of once. I noted it in my ledger. I even wondered about it, idly, the way you wonder about weather. Why would they increase the dosage schedule of a fully-trained taster? You build a wall to a certain height and then you stop. Unless the wall isn't the point. Unless the wall is a mold, and you keep pouring because the casting isn't finished.

Three years ago: the dreams began. I have not told anyone this, not even Vespera — not yet, anyway, and writing it here in my own head at three in the morning is as close to telling as I can bear. Dreams of standing in a great dark hall full of mirrors, wearing a face that wasn't mine. Dreams where something vast and patient looked out through my eyes and found the view agreeable. I woke from those dreams with my mouth full of a taste I had no name for — not poison, nothing the herbalist's forty-one plants could account for — a taste like cold iron and incense. Like a temple that had been shut for a very long time.

I told myself they were indigestion.

I am a professional liar to kings and queens. I know all the classic techniques. And I lied to myself for three years using every one of them, and it worked beautifully, right up until a bowl of stewed quince in the royal kitchens turned out to be a prayer instead of a poison, and the whole beautiful structure of my life came down at once, bricks and all.

* * *

Near dawn, I gave up on the ceiling and sat at the little table by my window, and I took out my ledger — the private one, the cipher one — and read it through start to finish for the first time as a document rather than a diary.

Do you know what it reads like? It reads like a construction report. Entry by entry, dose by dose, tolerance confirmed, resistance holding, subject stable. My own handwriting, recording my own ruin, in obsessive detail, because I wanted to feel ownership of my own mouth. Nine years of meticulous self-surveillance by a boy who thought he was guarding himself, when all along he was inventorying himself for someone else's use.

I laughed. I will admit it — alone in my cell at dawn, I laughed until it hurt, because the alternative was the other thing, the wet loud thing, and I had a duty shift at noon.

Because here is the arithmetic that finally broke me open, the sum I'd been refusing since Vespera said the word *sold*: they had spent nine years and two other children's lives building me. You do not spend that on a servant. You do not flog cooks over an anomaly in the kitchens, quietly, before dawn, unless the anomaly touches something worth more than a cook. Whatever I was, I was expensive. And expensive things, in my experience of the palace, belong to whoever paid — and are kept exactly as long as they remain useful.

Sold before I could walk. That was what the priestess said. Before I could walk. Which means the choosing at Corren's Ford wasn't a choosing at all. Quillon hadn't discovered a promising orphan; he had come to collect a purchase. Sister Marle's God's-favor boy, already spoken for, already entered in some ledger beneath the mountain, already — oh, this is the part that makes my hands unsteady even now, hours later, even laughing — already *paid for*, the way you pay for a cask of wine or a length of silk. My soul had a price and a buyer and a delivery date, and the delivery date, if the priestess's fear was accurate, is written in the sky. An eclipse.

I set the ledger down. I looked out my window at Ironhollow in the early light — the terraced fields, the smoke rising straight and domestic from the kitchen chimneys, the mountain standing over it all like a patient creditor — and I felt grief arrive, finally, properly, the way a tide arrives: not all at once but everywhere at once, unavoidable.

Not grief for danger. I have lived with danger so long it's practically a spouse. Grief for the self. For the boy in the grey man's leather case of spoons, the boy who got a small cup of milk and thought he'd won something. For the fifteen-year-old in new livery glowing under half a heartbeat of the queen's attention. For every version of Beatrix Dravenmoor who believed his survival was his own achievement, his immunity his armor, his service his honor. None of them existed. They were scaffolding around an empty form, and the form was never meant to stay empty, and the men who filled the scaffolding with poison and praise knew it the entire time.

Every suffering retroactively meaningless. That is the cruelest trick of all. If the poisoning had been cruelty, I could at least hate it cleanly. But it was craftsmanship. Patient, careful, budgeted craftsmanship, and I was the work, and the work was nearly done.

* * *

The bell rang for noon. I washed my face, dressed, pinned the taster's badge over my heart — a little silver dish, of all the jokes, a tiny dish on a chain — and went down to the tasting chamber to report for duty.

The Steward was waiting for me inside the door. Not at his desk, where he belonged. Inside the door, directly, as though he had calculated to the minute when I would arrive, which knowing him he had.

"Beatrix." He smiled. It was his ledger-smile, the one he used for balanced columns. "You look tired. Bad night?"

"Bad stew," I said. "It talked to me."

Something flickered behind his eyes — quick, there and gone, but I have spent nine years reading faces for trace flavors of intent, and I caught it. Not surprise. Confirmation.

"I imagine it did," the Steward said softly. "We should speak. Alone. There are arrangements to discuss — yours, specifically. Arrangements that were made a long time ago, by people whose names you don't yet know you ought to fear."

He held the inner door open for me, courteous as a headsman.

And I stood there on the threshold of the tasting chamber, in the smell of bread and silver and nine years of quiet poison, understanding that the last brick had just been set, and that the next voice to fill this vessel — his, or the god's, or mine — would depend entirely on what I did in the next ten seconds.
