## 6. The Steward's Bargain

I have been summoned by better messengers. The boy who came to Vespera's crack in the wall was maybe eleven, wore the livery of the Steward's office two sizes too large, and delivered his summons with the flat solemnity of a child reciting a lesson he doesn't understand: *"Master Taster. The Steward of Tasters requires your presence at the fourth bell. In his chambers."*

Not *requests*. *Requires*.

Vespera's hand found my wrist before I could move — her grip surprisingly strong for a woman who'd spent years living on smuggled prayers and bread crusts. "He knows," she said.

"Everyone knows something," I told her. "It's practically a civic duty in Ironhollow."

"Beatrix." My name in her mouth sounded like a warning bell. "The Steward doesn't send children unless he wants to be seen sending them. He wants witnesses to where you're going. He wants a record."

She was right, of course. She'd been right about everything since I'd pressed that bone whistle to my lips in the cook's cold room and felt the hum of it travel through my teeth like a second heartbeat. Right about the sold soul. Right about the vessel. Right about the valley being less a home than a lockbox with pretty mountains painted on the lid.

But knowing she was right and doing anything about it were separated by the width of the whole valley, so I did what any sensible man does when doom sends a polite invitation: I straightened my collar, checked that the whistle was hidden against my ribs where the skin had learned its warmth, and went.

* * *

The Steward of Tasters kept chambers above the tasting hall, which meant he lived directly over the room where men had died for the queen's dinner for nine generations. I used to think it was convenience. Now, standing on his threshold while an usher announced me, I understood it was theology. You keep your god close. Or your god's food.

Master Corvane had been Steward since before my selection. He was a long man folded into a short chair behind a desk of black ironwood, and he looked up from his ledger the way a spider looks up from the center of its web — not startled, not even particularly interested. Just noting coordinates.

"Taster Dravenmoor," he said. "Sit."

There was one chair facing the desk. There is always exactly one chair facing the desk of a powerful man; the architecture of authority is a language and it never says *welcome*.

I sat.

"Do you know why you're here?"

"You've read my quarterly reports and found my handwriting wanting."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not amusement. Recognition. He had known me nine years, trained me, dosed me, watched me swallow death on schedule like a good little investment, and I think in all that time this was the first moment he'd ever registered me as a person rather than a procedure. It lasted half a breath, and then it was gone, filed away as another anomaly.

"The dish at the feast," he said. "Course seven. Report."

I gave him the truth, or the shape of it. "It wasn't poison. It was structured like an offering — layered, intentional, addressed. Someone prayed through the kitchens. Something answered."

"And you tasted it anyway."

"It's my job."

"It's your *function*," Corvane corrected softly. "Your job would have been to flag it and let the guards burn the kitchen to the foundations. Your function was to take it into your body and tell us what happened inside you." He closed the ledger. "What happened inside you?"

"Nothing happened inside me."

"That," said the Steward of Tasters, "is what frightens me."

He rose then, and crossed to the window — the narrow one, slit-thick glass, looking out over the valley toward the mountain that we were all taught to call simply *the mountain*, the way you call a wound *the wound*. When he spoke again his voice had changed register. Lower. Almost gentle, which from Corvane was more alarming than shouting.

"Do you know the founding law of this valley?"

"No god may enter," I said. "No soul may leave."

"Recited beautifully. Do you understand it?"

"I understand that the cook who made that dish was flogged for it."

"The cook was flogged because the cook was the *vector*," Corvane said, still facing the window. "Prayers need a mouth. Offerings need a hand. We flogged the hand. But a prayer that reaches a god once will reach again, and next time it won't be through soup." He turned around. His face was grey in the window light. "Do you know what you are, Beatrix?"

Here it was, then. The question I'd been circling since a dying cook pressed bone into my palm and whispered a dead priestess's name. Every answer I might give was a trapdoor. *A taster. A vessel. A sold thing.* I chose the smallest lie available.

"A loyal servant of the crown."

"Yes," he agreed. "You are. And loyal servants are promoted." He came back to the desk, opened a drawer, and set something on the ironwood between us: a letter bearing the privy seal. "There is a position in the capital. Master of the Royal Cellars. Triple your stipend. A house with a garden. Distance from—" he gestured vaguely eastward, toward the mountain, toward the wall, toward everything "—from the eastern districts entirely. You'd leave within the week. Quietly. No records transferred."

No records transferred. Meaning no record that Beatrix Dravenmoor had ever served here. Meaning the vessel would be moved somewhere the gods couldn't find it, or somewhere they could, depending on what the contract actually required — and I thought of Vespera's voice telling me *your soul was sold before you could walk*, and I understood that this generous promotion was either a cage or a delivery.

"In exchange for?" I asked.

"Nothing. That's the beauty of it. You simply go."

"And if I decline?"

The gentleness left him like water out of a cracked bowl.

"Then the physicians examine you. Routine, of course. And the physicians are very thorough, and they are very good at finding taint, and once taint is found there are procedures." He sat back down and laced his fingers. "I am offering you survival, Beatrix. I've known you since you were a scrawny orphan who didn't flinch at hemlock. Take the cellar keys and live to be old. It's more than most men in your position get."

"And her?"

I hadn't meant to say it. The word just walked out of my mouth wearing my voice.

Corvane didn't pretend not to know who I meant. He'd never pretended anything; that was somehow the worst thing about him. "The woman in the foundation stones has been dead for six years as far as the temple records are concerned. If she stays dead, she stays alive. If she surfaces—"

"She surfaces with me or not at all."

"Then she doesn't surface," he said, "and neither do you. Think carefully. Loyalty is a fine quality in a taster. But loyalty has a direction, Beatrix, and right now yours points the wrong way, and I cannot promote a man whose loyalty points wrong. Betray her — tell me where the entrance to her hiding place is, give me the whistle, sign the transfer papers — and by tomorrow night you'll be drinking wine in the capital while someone else worries about gods."

The offer sat between us on the black desk like course seven at the feast. Structured. Layered. Addressed.

And underneath the sweetness, poison — except it wasn't poison, and that was the horror of it. He meant every word. He would genuinely rather shuffle me off to cellars than feed me to procedures. Nine years of swallowing death for these people and the Steward of Tasters was, in his terrible ledger-keeping way, trying to save my life. All it would cost was a dead woman in a wall and the last trust a dying cook had placed in my hands.

I looked at the transfer papers. My name was already written at the top in a clerk's careful hand. *Beatrix Dravenmoor.* The signature line waited below it, empty and patient.

They say a man's whole life passes before his eyes when he's about to die. What passed before mine was smaller and stranger: a kitchen boy's hands folding bread around scraps of prayer. The hum of bone against my ribs. Vespera saying *they built you* in a voice like a struck bell. Nine years of dishes, each one a brick.

"I have a counteroffer," I said.

Corvane raised an eyebrow a fraction of an inch, which for him was open laughter.

"You declare me tainted," I said. "Right now. Tonight. Procedures, physicians, whatever you like. And I'll be gone before the ink dries, and you can write in your ledger that the vessel escaped, unassisted, through no negligence of the Steward's office. No betrayal. No promotion. Nothing to sign."

For a moment the only sound was the wind worrying the slit window.

"That's not how bargains work," he said finally.

"I know. That's how *running* works."

I stood. He didn't call the guards — I'd wondered if he would, and later I'd understand why he didn't, though understanding it took me a long way down a dark road under a mountain — and when I reached the door his voice stopped me, quiet and stripped of all its office:

"They'll catch you, Beatrix. The salt line isn't a suggestion. Whatever you think is waiting out there — it's worse than what's in here."

"With respect, Master Steward," I said, "you've fed me worse things every morning for nine years. I've developed a tolerance."

And I ran.

* * *

I ran the way I'd been trained to move through the palace — unnoticed, unhurried until hurry cost nothing, a shadow among shadows. Through the servants' stair. Past the laundry courts where the steam hides everything. Out through the gate the carters use before dawn, bribing no one because the gate guard owed me his life from a bad batch of eel pie three winters past, and men remember who spat poison for them.

The city fell away behind me. Then the terraces, the vineyards, the last stubborn farms clinging to the valley floor like barnacles. The moon rose fat and indifferent over the eastern ridge, and beneath my ribs the bone whistle grew warmer with every step, humming its low note against my skin, pulling me east like a compass needle made of guilt and hope in equal parts.

Vespera was waiting at the wall — the true wall, the foundation stones older than the kingdom, where the valley's law was written in mortar and salt and older things. She had a satchel packed, a knife at her belt, and an expression that managed to be both furious and unsurprised.

"He offered you the cellars," she said.

"How did you—"

"You're alive, and you're running. Only the cellars make a man do both." She looked me over, quick and clinical, checking for damage the way I checked dishes for poison. "What did you pay?"

"Nothing. I chose neither door."

Her face did something complicated. "That was stupid."

"Almost certainly."

"It was also," she admitted, turning toward the dark fields beyond the wall, "the first intelligent decision anyone's made in this valley in a century." She glanced back at me, and moonlight caught the edge of something in her expression I couldn't name — fear, maybe, or its cousin, hope. "The salt line is a mile east. Once we cross it, everything changes. Everything. Are you ready for that?"

I put my hand flat against my chest, over the hidden whistle, over whatever hollow thing the crown had left where my soul used to be.

"Vespera," I said, "I've been ready since I was four years old and didn't know it."

We ran together, then, out of the valley that had made me safe, and behind us — faint, distant, unmistakable — I heard the first horn of the alarm rise from the palace towers, thin as a prayer.

By morning, I knew, every hunter the crown could loose would be on our trail.

But that was a problem belonging to the daylight hours.

The night belonged to us — and, somewhere far beneath the mountain, to something else that had just felt a vessel start to move.
