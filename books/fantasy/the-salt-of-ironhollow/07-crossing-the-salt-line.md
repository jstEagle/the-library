## 7. Crossing the Salt Line

We left by the corpse gate, because Vespera insisted the dead tell fewer lies than guards.

"It's not that they're honest," she said, easing the iron latch with a strip of bone she'd been sharpening for three days against her own heel. "It's that nobody bothers to bribe them."

The corpse gate was a narrow door in the valley's western wall, set into stone so old it had stopped being architecture and started being geology. It had been sealed since before my grandmother's grandmother, according to no one, because no one remembered it existed. That was the thing about Ironhollow: it didn't keep its secrets in vaults. It kept them in the shape of ordinary things — a wall with a bump in it, a road that curved just slightly too much, a taster who could swallow anything and smile about it afterward.

I had spent nine years being an ordinary thing.

Now I stood in the dark with a stolen cloak, half a loaf of prayer-bread, a dead cook's bone whistle, and a disgraced priestess who looked at me like I was a door she'd been knocking on for years.

"You're stalling," Vespera said.

"I'm appreciating," I said. "There's a difference. Stalling is what cowards do when they're afraid of what's ahead. Appreciating is what fools do when they're afraid of what's behind."

"And which are you?"

"Both. I contain multitudes. Mostly dread."

She almost smiled. It was the closest she'd come in the six days since we'd fled the Steward's study, and I found I wanted to earn another one, the way you want to earn anything rare — badly, and without admitting it.

The gate opened onto a passage that smelled of cold iron and older dust. We went down, because everything in Ironhollow eventually went down; the valley was built like a cup, and cups are made to hold things, and I had lately learned exactly what kind of thing I was.

* * *

The tunnels took most of the night. Vespera navigated by touch, reading the walls with her fingertips the way other people read scripture, and once, when I asked how she knew where we were going, she said, "I helped build this place."

"You helped build a cage."

"I helped build a church." Her voice did something complicated in the dark. "It's the same stones. The difference is what you're keeping in, or out, or—" She stopped. "Ask me again when we're past the salt. Things sound different on either side of it."

"What is the salt, exactly?"

"Law," she said. "Salt remembers. Salt keeps its promises. Your founders poured a boundary of it around the whole valley and bound it with words nobody living can pronounce anymore. No god may enter. No soul may leave." A pause, the length of a held breath. "You were born inside it, Beatrix. Everything you are has been marinating in that law since your first cry. Crossing over is going to be—"

"Exhilarating? Transformative? The start of a grand adventure?"

"Loud," she said.

* * *

We came up out of the tunnels an hour before dawn, into a gully choked with juniper and frost, and there it was: the salt line.

I had imagined a wall. Something visible, something you could point to and say *there, that's the edge of my life.* But the salt line was nothing. It was a low ridge of pale earth running along the top of the gully like an old scar, whitish-grey in the starlight, unremarkable as any stretch of bad soil. Sheep probably grazed near it. Children probably dared each other to touch it and came back fine, because children aren't souls anyone wants yet.

"That's it?" I said. "That's the great containment protocol? It looks like a drainage ditch."

"The most effective prisons never look like prisons," Vespera said. "Look again. Not with your eyes."

I looked. And because I had spent nine years learning to taste things beneath their taste — poison under honey, intent under courtesy, fear under a queen's smile — I did see it.

The stars above the ridge were stars. The stars below the ridge, on the valley side, were *arranged*. Subtly. The way a table is arranged. Nothing so crude as patterns — just a tidiness, a sense that the sky over Ironhollow had been set for company and was waiting, politely, for someone to arrive. Nine years I'd slept under that sky and thought it merely beautiful. It had been *expectant.*

"Oh," I said. My mouth had gone dry, which for me was nearly a religious experience. "Oh, that's foul."

"Now you see it." Vespera crouched at the ridge and pressed her palm flat against the pale earth, and I watched her whole body flinch — a small, practiced flinch, the flinch of someone touching a stove they've touched a thousand times on purpose. "Still lit. Still watching. It will feel us coming."

"Can it stop us?"

"No. But it can *report* us." She stood, brushed the salt from her hand like shaking off a sin. "By dawn, whatever watches this valley will know two souls walked out through the front door instead of dying quietly in their assigned places. So we don't dawdle. When we cross, we cross all the way."

"And if I change my mind halfway?"

"Then you'll be a very interesting stain," she said, "and I'll have wasted excellent bread."

* * *

Here is what I want recorded, in whatever ledger ends up holding the account of my life, which knowing my luck is probably kept underground by a king with poor lighting: I hesitated.

Not from fear. Or not only. I stood on the valley side of that pale scar with the arranged stars overhead, and I understood — really understood, for the first time since the cook put the whistle in my hand and died with a name on his lips — that everything I knew of myself might be furniture. Placed there. Arranged. An orphan selected, dosed, seasoned, prepared. Nine years of dishes, each immunity a brick, each brick laid toward some purpose I'd been bred for like a hound is bred for the hunt.

What crossed that line with me? What part of Beatrix Dravenmoor was mine, actually mine, purchased honestly with my own living?

I didn't know. That was the terror. Not death — death I'd flirted with professionally for nearly a decade, three times a day, with cutlery. The terror was that I might step over the salt and find there was nothing on my side of my own skin.

"Beatrix." Vespera's voice, quiet. "Whatever happens on the other side, whatever touches you — it cannot take what you refuse to give. That's the oldest law. Older than salt. Older than gods." A beat. "Mostly."

"*Mostly?*"

"Cross."

* * *

I stepped over the salt line.

For the first three steps, nothing happened, and I allowed myself the luxury of thinking Vespera had oversold it, that the great threshold of my life was a ditch after all, that I would walk out of Ironhollow the same man who walked in, merely colder and better informed—

And then magic touched me.

I say *touched* because there's no better word, though touch is a child's word for it. It was more like being *read.* Like standing in a dark room and feeling eyes move across you, except the eyes were everywhere — in the frost, in the juniper, in the wind that came down off the mountains carrying a smell like rain on hot stone, rain that hadn't fallen here in years. The world outside the salt was *awake*, and it noticed me the way a sleeping house notices a floorboard creak.

It slid across my skin like a hand. It pressed, gently, curiously, at the edges of me — and I felt every one of those edges, felt the exact shape of myself from the outside for the first time in my life.

Do you know what I found?

A hollow.

Not emptiness — worse. Emptiness is just absence. This was a *shaped* absence, a space built to specifications, a cavity in the center of me sized precisely for something that wasn't me yet. Every immunity I'd earned, every dish I'd survived, had carved it: nine years of poison chiseling out a vessel, and I had called the chiseling *a career.*

Something inside me answered.

That's the part I'll never manage to explain properly, not even to myself at three in the morning, which is when I usually try. There was a hollow in me, and the waking world poured itself against that hollow like water finding a mold, and the hollow *sang back.* Not with sound. With recognition. With a horrible, homecoming gladness, the way a lock feels about its key, the way a wound feels about the knife.

Somewhere far beneath the mountain, something stirred in its sleep and turned toward me like a compass needle finding north.

My knees went. I caught myself on a juniper trunk, needles biting my palms, and I was dimly aware that I was making a sound — a low, involuntary noise, half laugh, half something else, the noise a man makes when he's terrified and exhilarated in the same breath and his body can't decide which to do about it, so it does both, loudly.

"There it is," Vespera said softly, beside me. She'd crossed right after me — I hadn't even heard her, but then, the world was being very loud in a way that had no sound in it. "Breathe. Breathe, taster. It passes. Or rather — it doesn't pass, but you learn to stand in it. Like cold water."

"It *knows* me." My voice came out wrecked. "Vespera, it knows me. It's — I'm — there's a shape in me, I can feel the shape, it's been *waiting*—"

"Yes."

"How long?"

"Since before you could walk," she said, and did not soften it, because she was the sort of person who believed mercy was a full truth told at the worst possible moment. "Come on. Up. We need distance between us and that ridge before the sun does."

I got up. I don't remember deciding to; my legs simply remembered that I was, despite everything, still a man with somewhere to be. We climbed out of the gully onto open ground — real ground, unsalted, unarranged — and behind us the valley lay in its cup of law, small and neat and expectant, and ahead of us the land rolled black and silver toward the mountain, toward the court beneath it, toward the king of ash who held the deed to my soul in some ledger I intended to burn.

And the whole way, the hollow sang.

That's the confession I'll make only here: it wasn't all horror. That's the filthiest part. As terrified as I was — and I was terrified down to the marrow the poisons never reached — some traitorous chamber of my heart thrilled to it. To be *known*, after nine years of being a function, a palate, a chair that sat at the queen's left hand and swallowed on command. The waking world looked at me and saw not a taster, not a tool, but a *door*, and doors are at least wanted. At least awaited.

I hated myself a little for the thrill. I carried it anyway, the way you carry a coal in your bare hand — carefully, aware of the burn, unwilling to drop it.

* * *

Dawn found us in the ruins of a shepherd's cot, sharing the last of the prayer-bread. Vespera chewed slowly, watching the light come up over the mountain, and I sat with my back to a fallen wall and tried to be one man instead of two.

"You felt it answer," she said at last. Not a question.

"It answered. Yes. Loudly enough that I suspect things three kingdoms away heard it."

"Things did." She tore the bread. "Which means we've lost our head start. Whatever holds your contract will have felt a vessel wake up and start walking toward it. It won't send a polite invitation."

"Wonderful. What will it send?"

She considered the horizon. Frost smoked off the grass in the new light, and far away — too far to be reasonable, too distinct to be mistaken — a line of birds rose off the mountain's shoulder all at once, as if something had passed beneath them.

"Hunters," she said. "Or worse. Debt collectors." She handed me the larger piece of bread, which I chose to interpret as affection. "Eat, taster. You'll need your strength, and for once in your miserable life, nothing in your food is trying to kill you."

I ate. And beneath the taste of bread and freedom, faint as a bell rung in another room, the hollow went on singing — patient, glad, and certain that sooner or later, everything walks home to the shape that was made for it.

I meant to prove it wrong.

The mountain, I suspected, meant to prove otherwise.
