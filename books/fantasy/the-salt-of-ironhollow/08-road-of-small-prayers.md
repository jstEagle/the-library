## 8. Road of Small Prayers

The first thing you learn as a priestess is that the gods are not listening. The second thing you learn — if you are unlucky, or honest, or both — is that they were never listening, and then one day they start.

I was eleven years old when I was given to the temple of the Ninefold Quiet in the city of Marrowgate. Not chosen; given, the way one gives a coat to charity when the fashion changes. My mother had seven other children and a talent for arithmetic, and she calculated that a daughter in temple whites cost less to feed than a daughter at home. I have never entirely forgiven her for being right about the sums.

The Ninefold Quiet taught that the old gods were dead, or sleeping, or — my favorite formulation from my instructors, delivered with the serene confidence of people reciting a lie they'd been paid to memorize — *withdrawn*. That they had turned their faces from the world after the founding of Ironhollow, and that our duty was to keep the candles lit anyway, out of courtesy. Courtesy to whom? I asked once, aged thirteen, and was set to scrubbing the crypt floor for a week. It was a fair question, though. You do not light candles for the dead unless you suspect they can see.

For nineteen years I lit candles for things that could not see me, and I was good at it. I learned the litanies, the fasts, the hundred small courtesies of an empty house. I learned to read prayers the way a physician reads a pulse — not for what they say, but for what trembles under them. And on the night the gods woke beneath the mountain, I was the only priest on watch who did not assume the shaking walls were an earthquake.

Everyone else ran. I knelt down in the falling plaster and listened, because I had spent nineteen years waiting to be wrong about something, and there is no feeling in the world like it.

They excommunicated me within the season, naturally. Heresy has always moved faster than miracle.

* * *

The road east of Ironhollow is not a road so much as a disagreement between mud and gravel that the mud is winning. Beatrix walked it like a man walking on a floor he suspected might be a lid. He had not spoken above a dozen words since we crossed the salt line two nights ago, and I understood why. When you have spent your whole life in a sealed jar, the first breath of outside air tastes less like freedom than like the moment before someone decides whether to put the lid back.

"You're staring," he said, without turning around.

"I'm assessing," I said. "There's a difference. Staring is rude. Assessing is professional."

"What's your assessment?"

"That you walk like a man expecting the ground to invoice him."

That earned me half a glance over his shoulder. Progress. "And?"

"And nothing. Yet." I caught up with him, which took some doing; he had the long stride of a man whose legs were the only part of him ever allowed to make decisions. "You crossed a boundary last night that kills most things that touch it. You felt magic move through you. Most people would be asking questions."

"I'm saving them up."

"For what?"

"For someone who answers honestly."

I laughed. It surprised us both — his shoulders flinched as though the sound had come from behind him. "Then save your questions, taster. Honest people are rarer than miracles, and I've seen three miracles since dawn."

He stopped walking. Ahead of us, the valley of a stream opened into a village: a scatter of slate roofs, smoke standing straight in the windless morning, and — this was the part worth stopping for — a well in the square with water rising out of it in a slow, impossible spiral, turning gently like cream stirred by a patient finger.

"Three?" Beatrix said.

"The spiral well. The barley field back along the ridge that ripened overnight — midwinter, mind you, and gold as a bride's dowry. And the dog." I nodded toward the well, where a brindled hound sat with the stillness of furniture. Around its neck hung a collar of woven straw, and the straw was green. Growing. Alive. "Somebody prayed for that dog to live. It died anyway. Somebody prayed again, and whatever answered didn't raise it — it just won't let it finish dying. Look at its eyes."

Beatrix looked. I watched him look, and I saw the taster in him take over: the flat, cataloguing attention of a man trained to find poison in anything, including mercy.

"It's in pain," he said quietly.

"It's alive. Those aren't the same thing anymore. That's rather the point."

A woman came out of the nearest house with a pail, saw us, and stopped dead. Not frightened, exactly. Worse than frightened — *careful*, the way one is careful near a hole in the ice. She dipped her pail from the spiraling water without looking at it, the way you don't look at a debt collector, and went back inside without a word.

"They're used to strangers now," I said. "Pilgrims come through for the well. They drink and their fevers break, and they leave coins in the bowl by the church — except there's no priest here anymore, so the bowl just fills up, and every full moon the bowl is empty again, and nobody will say who takes the coins."

"Who does take them?"

"No one knows. That's the third miracle, if you're counting. Something drinks the offerings. Something with an appetite." I started walking again, and after a moment he fell in beside me. "This is what waking looks like, Beatrix Dravenmoor. Not thunder. Not burning bushes. A well that turns. A dog that won't die. Small prayers getting small answers, and each answer taking a little more than it should."

"Taking what?"

"That's the question everyone on this road is afraid to ask out loud. The fever breaks but the dream doesn't come back. The barley ripens but the farmer can't remember his mother's face. The god answers wrong — or answers right, which amounts to the same thing, because the old ones never promised to be kind. Only to listen. And now they're listening very, very hard."

We walked through the village without speaking. At the edge of it stood the old shrine — a squat stone thing older than any kingdom, its carved god worn faceless by weather and by hands. Someone had left fresh bread on the altar. Someone had also left, neatly folded, a child's shoe. Just the one. I did not explain it to Beatrix. Some sermons preach themselves.

* * *

We made camp that night in the lee of a fallen waymarker, one of the ancient boundary stones from before the valley, its script so old even I could only read fragments. Beatrix built a fire with the competence of a man who had been permitted exactly one skill at a time and had mastered all of them out of spite. Then he sat across from me and looked into the flames, and I realized with a small, unwelcome tenderness that he was waiting for me to talk. He had been waiting for days. He simply hadn't known how to ask, because no one had ever taught him that asking was allowed.

So I talked. It seemed the least I could do, and also the most dangerous, which is usually how the least and the most arrive together.

"You want to know how a priestess ends up living in a wall," I said.

"I wasn't going to—"

"You were going to spend a week not asking, and then ask badly, and then feel guilty about it. I've met men before, Beatrix. Let me save us both the month." I pulled my knees up. The fire cracked and settled. "I told you I heard the mountain wake. What I didn't tell you is what I did next. I wrote to my superiors. A full report. Seismic anomaly consistent with vocalization. Liturgical resonance detected in the bedrock. I used the polite words, the ones that mean *a god is stirring and we should discuss it like adults*."

"And?"

"And the Ninefold Quiet has survived four centuries precisely by never discussing anything like adults. Three days later I was called before the Provost of Silence himself. Do you know what the Provost said to me? He didn't deny it. That's what I can't get past, even now. He looked at me with these tired, kind eyes and he said, *'Sister Vespera. The valley holds. The valley must hold. Whatever wakes beneath it must go on not-waking, and your report, and you, and anyone who repeats either, are obstacles to that holding.'*" I turned my hands over in the firelight. "Then he offered me a choice. Recant, forget, keep my vows. Or be defrocked, named, and handed to the crown's inquisitors as a madwoman. He gave me until dawn. Very generous. Very clerical."

"What did you choose?"

"I chose neither, obviously. I stole the archive key off his belt while he was monologuing — priests love the sound of their own gravity, it makes them careless — and I took everything the Quiet had on Ironhollow. Founding records. Containment protocols. The original covenant, written in a hand that shook." I watched Beatrix's face across the fire. I had been carrying this next part for months, waiting for the right ears, and I still almost couldn't say it. "Your valley isn't a valley, Beatrix. It's a seal. The salt line, the law that no god may enter and no soul may leave — those aren't customs. They're stitches. Someone sewed the mountain shut, and the crown has been maintaining the sutures for four hundred years, and the thread they used—" I made myself say it plainly, because he deserved plain. "The thread was souls. Bought souls, bound into the foundation. Yours among them. Sold before you could walk."

He didn't flinch. That was the terrible thing. He sat there with the firelight moving on his face and absorbed it the way stone absorbs rain — completely, silently, with damage you wouldn't see for years.

"The cook," he said finally. "Tomas. He knew."

"Tomas had been smuggling prayers to me in bread for six years. Little notes in the crusts, written in flour-paste. *Is it true they sleep? Are they dreaming? Can they hear us?* I answered what I could. He was the one who told me about you — the taster who never sickened. He'd figured out what you were before you had, God rest him, and it got him flogged for feeding a heretic, and then the Steward decided a flogging was too survivable." I looked into the fire. "I've buried enough people for knowing things. I'd hoped you'd be the exception."

"He trusted you with a whistle and a name."

"He trusted you with them. I was just the address." I glanced up. "Why did you come find me, Beatrix? Truly. You had a whistle, a name, and a functioning life. Most sensible men would have eaten the whistle."

"Most sensible men weren't sold before they could walk." He said it evenly, without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "You were the only person in the world who knew what I was. When your whole life turns out to be a cage, you go find the person holding the map of it."

"Well," I said, after a moment. "That's the least flattering reason anyone's ever needed me for. I'll take it."

Something happened to his mouth then. It took me a second to identify it, because I'd only ever seen it on statues: he smiled. Briefly, crookedly, gone as soon as it came — but real. Wary companionship, I thought. We were becoming wary companions. It wasn't much. On this road, in this year, it was beginning to feel like wealth.

* * *

The next village had no well. It had a chapel instead — or what had been a chapel, until somebody had burned the altar and then, judging by the scorch pattern, apologized to it. The villagers wouldn't speak to us. Doors closed as we passed, one after another, soft as eyelids. In the square stood a young man sweeping ash that had long since finished being ash, sweeping it anyway, over and over, his eyes fixed on nothing.

Beatrix approached him. I let him — he needed practice being a person, and the doomed generosity of talking to the broken is as good a place to start as any.

"Friend," Beatrix said. "What happened here?"

The sweeper didn't stop. "Our reeve prayed," he said, in the tone of a man reporting weather. "Drought, three years. He prayed to the old one under the hill. Rain came."

"And?"

"And the reeve's daughter stopped speaking. Not mute. She talks constantly — lovely voice, sings hymns, recites scripture. But none of it's hers. Ask her her own name and she smiles and says, *'It rains.'*" He swept. "The reeve tried to pray her back. The god's price for that was the reeve's shadow. He walks crooked now, leaning like a man in a strong wind, on clear days, indoors." He finally looked up at Beatrix, and his face was the worst thing I'd seen in weeks: not grief. Bookkeeping. "We're learning to pray smaller. That's the lesson of the age, traveler. Pray small. Pay exact. Count your children before and after."

We walked out of that village faster than we'd walked into it. For a mile Beatrix said nothing, and then, in a voice carefully stripped of everything:

"This is what's coming to Ironhollow. If the seal breaks."

"Yes."

"Every village on this road. Every prayer. Every price."

"Yes." I hesitated, and then decided he'd earned the rest. "Which is why the court beneath the mountain buys vessels, Beatrix. Think it through. A god needs a door into the world. A vessel is a door with hinges — something shaped, over years, to fit. Your immunity, your nine years of poison — you weren't being protected. You were being *fitted*." I watched his hands close slowly at his sides. "I'm sorry. You deserve to hear it from someone who hates saying it more than you hate hearing it."

"Do you hate saying it?"

"More than you know. Because the woman who tells you what you are becomes part of the cage, and I swore an oath once — a real one, underneath the candle nonsense — that I would spend my life opening cages." I kicked a stone off the track with more force than it required. "Oaths have a sense of humor."

He was quiet a while longer. Then: "Vespera. Why did the Quiet really expel you? The truth, not the report. You found the covenant. You knew what I was before Tomas told you. You'd known for years." His eyes came around to mine, and there it was — the taster's precision, tasting my words before swallowing them. "What did you do that was worse than writing a letter?"

I should have lied. I am, historically, quite good at lying; it was practically a sacrament where I was raised. But he had crossed a killing boundary for me, had run from the only home he'd ever known, had carried a dead cook's whistle like a relic — and lies, I have found, are a poor foundation for the only kind of friendship worth having, which is the kind built between two people the world has already discarded.

"When the mountain woke," I said, "it spoke. Not in words. In *names*. It said names for three days — thousands of them, the sold ones, the bound ones, rolling through the bedrock like a tide. And one of them was mine."

His step faltered. "Yours. But you were never—"

"I was never sold. No. But my mother gave me to the temple, remember? Given. And a gift freely given, to an order that maintained the covenant..." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "The Quiet registered its holdings, Beatrix. All of them. Including the novice Vespera, entered into the ledger the week I arrived, aged eleven, in my mother's handwriting. I wasn't a priestess. I was inventory. And when the mountain read its accounts aloud, it read me."

We stood on the road in the failing light, two entries in a ledger, walking toward the bookkeeper.

"So when they offered me recantation," I went on, softer now, "what they were really offering was to keep pretending. To go back to lighting candles for the dark and calling it devotion. I couldn't. I'd *heard my own name in the mouth of a god*. There's no unhearing that. So I took the archive, I found the covenant, I found the entry — *one soul, female, infant, sold by the crown's Steward of Tasters, held against the return* — and I went looking for the person it belonged to. It took me six years. Tomas found me first."

The wind came down off the ridgeline, smelling of snow and far-off iron. Ahead of us the road bent north, toward the black shoulder of the mountain, where a light was burning that I told myself was a farmhouse.

Beatrix looked at that light for a long time. Then he said, in a voice with no wryness left in it at all:

"There's a village ahead. I can smell the prayer-smell on the wind — that sweet-rot stink under the woodsmoke. And Vespera... the light isn't flickering. Whatever's burning down there has been burning at exactly the same height since we crested the ridge." He started walking. "Someone down there is praying right now. And something is answering."

I followed him into the dark, and I did not tell him that I recognized the shape of that flame — steady, colorless, hungry — from the oldest pages of the stolen archive. I told myself I'd tell him tomorrow.

Tomorrow, I have since learned, is the currency with which the gods prefer to be paid.
