## 9. The God Who Answers Wrong

The god lived in a well.

I want to be precise about that, because later—when everything went wrong in ways I couldn't have imagined—I kept coming back to the fact that our first real encounter with the waking gods was with something that lived in a hole in the ground and spoke through the mouths of frogs. Nine years as a royal taster, and I had expected divinity to have better posture.

The village was called Miren's Ford, or claimed to be; the sign at the crossing had been repainted so many times the letters had gone soft, like a word repeated until it stops meaning anything. It was the third settlement we'd passed since the salt line, and each one had been stranger than the last. In the first, the barley grew overnight whenever anyone wept near it. In the second, a shrine to some nameless hearth-mother cured fevers but took, as payment, the memory of the fevered person's mother's face—which meant the whole village walked around loving a woman none of them could picture. Vespera had called these *roadside miracles*, said them the way a physician says *festering*.

"The gods are waking up hungry," she told me. "Nine centuries of fasting under your valley's law. They'll take what they can get."

"And people pay?"

"People always pay." She adjusted the frayed gray hood she wore against more than weather. "That's what prayer is, when you get down to it. A transaction with the invoice on the back page."

I was learning to read Vespera's moods the way I'd once learned to read dishes—the faint bitterness of fear, the metallic tang of guilt, the cloying sweetness of hope left too long uncovered. That morning her mood tasted of all three, which should have warned me.

* * *

We'd stopped at Miren's Ford because our food was gone and my coin, it turned out, was minted by a kingdom that considered the road east of the salt line foreign territory. The innkeeper—a broad woman named Dagny with forearms like bread loaves—took one look at Vespera's priestess's knot, half-hidden under the hood, and went pale in a way I recognized. I'd seen it on the Steward's face, back when the kitchens served me a dish that should have killed me and didn't.

"You're one of the waking ones," Dagny said. Not a question.

"I'm no one," Vespera said. "Disgraced. Struck from the rolls."

"That's worse," Dagny said, "or better. Depends who you ask. Depends who *answers*." She wiped her hands on her apron, hesitated, then leaned across the bar. "You'll be wanting to know about the well, then. Everyone asks eventually. Folk come from two valleys over to ask it things." Her voice dropped. "It answers wrong, mind. Never lies. Just answers wrong. Like it only heard half the question and filled in the rest with whatever it had lying about."

"What does it charge?" Vespera asked.

Dagny looked at her for a long moment. "Depends what you ask. Small questions cost small things. You don't notice till later. Old Tam asked it where his lost ox was, and found the ox that same hour—but he never again could taste honey. Just ate sweet things like a man eating warm water. Little Hesta asked if her boy would live, and he lived, and she forgot his laugh. Remembers that he laughed. Can't hear it anymore, not even in her head." She shrugged, an enormous gesture. "Folk keep asking anyway. That's the thing about gods. They don't have to trick you. You trick yourselves and call it faith."

* * *

I want to say I had no intention of going anywhere near the well. I want to say that nine years of professional suspicion had burned the curiosity out of me like poison burns out of a body built to refuse it.

But here is the truth, which I've promised myself I'd write down honestly even when it shames me: I lay awake that night in the inn's loft, staring at the rafters, and I did arithmetic. Somewhere behind me stood a Steward who had either sold my soul or known it had been sold and said nothing. Somewhere ahead stood a court beneath a mountain that held the deed to everything I was. And between those two facts stretched a question I could not stop chewing: *who signed?*

Vespera thought the crown itself. I thought—no. I'd watched the Steward for nine years. He checked my dishes himself. He knew every poison by smell, every antidote by heart. Men don't guard an investment that carefully unless they understand its value. Whoever sold me hadn't done it carelessly. They'd done it *lovingly*, the way you'd sell a horse you'd spent years training, and that distinction mattered. It mattered like a knife matters.

And there was a well down the road that answered questions for a price.

I told myself I'd just look at it. This is the lie everyone tells at the edge of every well, I suspect, since wells were first dug.

* * *

The well sat in the village square, which was less a square than a widening of the road where three crooked houses agreed to stop arguing. It was old—older than the village, older than the stonework around its lip, which had clearly been added the way you put a collar on something. The rope and bucket were ceremonial; nobody drew water. Nobody drank from it. You leaned over the lip, you asked, and something below answered.

Frogs first. There were frogs in it—white ones, eyeless-looking, and they arranged themselves on the wet stones below in patterns too deliberate to be chance. Then the water moved without wind, rising slightly, like a chest drawing breath. Then the voice came, and it came from everywhere the water touched: the stones, the moss, the frogs' open mouths, and, unpleasantly, my own teeth.

*"Ask."*

I gripped the rim. "You're not one of the mountain gods," I said. "The ones under Ironhollow."

A pause. Water sounds. *"No. We are the small ones. The field ones. The ones the law forgot because we fit in cracks."* The frogs shifted. *"But law is cracking everywhere now. Ask your question, taster. I know what you are. You taste of iron and withheld things."*

My mouth had gone dry. "What do you charge?"

*"For little questions, little prices. For true questions—" * the water rose another inch, lapping at the inner stones— *"true prices."*

"Who sold my soul?"

There it was. Out loud. The question I'd carried since a dying cook pressed a bone whistle into my hand and gave me a name—*Vespera*—and a burden. The words fell into the well and I swear the dark swallowed them audibly, the way a deep cave swallows a dropped stone long before the splash.

The water went very still.

Then the god laughed. I felt it in my fillings. *"Oh,"* it said, delighted, *"oh, that is a good question. That is a question with roots. Do you know what I could tell you? The hand. The seal. The room where it was done. The words spoken over your cradle. I could give you the whole shape of it, taster—the name of the seller, freely spoken, which is worth more than you know."*

My heart was doing something unsanctioned. Because this was it—this was everything. No ledger to steal, no court to infiltrate, no king of ash to bargain with. One answer, one well, one moment, and I would finally know whose face to hate.

"And the price?" I made myself ask.

*"One year,"* the god said, *"of your memories. Any year. Chosen by me."*

The square went quiet. Somewhere a dog stopped barking. Even the frogs held their arrangement.

"Chosen by you," I repeated.

*"I am fair,"* the god said, and the terrible thing was that it sounded fair. Sounded like a merchant quoting an honest price. *"Not kind. Fair. One year out of twenty-six. You would not miss what you do not remember. You would wake tomorrow lighter. Everyone speaks well of being lighter."*

And here's what saved me, though it didn't feel like salvation at the time. It felt like cowardice. I thought about the second dish of the spring feast, four years ago—the one I'd refused to eat because the smell was wrong, and how I'd spent a week unable to explain *why* the smell was wrong, only that some part of me that wasn't intellect had flagged it. I thought about the cook's hand, cold and urgent around mine, pressing the whistle into it. I thought about Vespera telling me my soul was sold before I could walk, and how the horror of that wasn't the loss—it was that someone had decided what I was worth before I'd ever gotten to decide anything myself.

This god was offering me knowledge in exchange for letting it choose which piece of me to delete.

Every deal that shaped my life had worked exactly this way. Someone else chose. Someone else weighed. Someone else took, and called it fate, or duty, or investment.

"No," I said.

The water sank an inch. *"No?"*

"No. Not because the price is high. Because you pick the year." I leaned closer, close enough to see my own reflection wobbling in the black water, thin and sleepless and stubborn. "I've spent my whole life being spent by other people. I'm done paying tolls where the toll-keeper chooses what he takes. If I ever sell a year of my life, I'll pick which one goes, and I'll pick the buyer, and I'll shake his hand while I do it."

Silence. Long enough that I began composing my own eulogy.

Then the frogs rearranged themselves, and the god said, almost gently, *"You are going to be a problem for someone. I hope it is the right someone."* And the water simply—stopped. Went ordinary. Well-water. Frogs scattered into the dark crevices, and the presence withdrew like a tide, leaving the square smelling of wet stone and nothing else.

I stood there shaking, and discovered I was grinning. Terror and exhilaration. I was beginning to suspect those two traveled together for me now, sharing a horse.

* * *

Vespera was waiting at the inn's corner, arms crossed, hood back, watching me with an expression I couldn't parse. "Dagny said you went to the well."

"I went to the well."

"You asked it."

"I asked it."

She crossed the distance fast, grabbed my wrist—her grip stronger than her frame suggested—and searched my face. "What did it take? What's missing? Think. What year is it? Who am I? Why are we on this road?"

"Nothing's missing. I refused."

She stared at me. "Nobody refuses."

"I did. Apparently I'm going to be a problem for someone." I tried to make it light. It landed somewhere short of light. "It wanted a year of my memories, its choice. I declined the terms."

Vespera let go of my wrist slowly, and something moved across her face—relief, yes, but underneath it something else, quick and furtive, gone before I could name it. The taster's reflex in me catalogued it anyway: *guilt, masked with relief.* I'd spent nine years identifying what people hid in dishes. Faces weren't so different.

"Why do you look like that?" I asked. "Like you're glad and sorry at once?"

"Because you're right to refuse," she said, too smoothly, "and I'm glad one of us has sense." She turned toward the inn. "Come on. We should be moving. Ashmarket's four days east, and we'll want daylight margins. Roads like these grow teeth after dark."

"Vespera."

She stopped. Didn't turn.

"When did you go to the well?"

A beat of silence, precisely measured, the length of a heartbeat plus deniability. "I haven't been near it."

"Dagny said everyone asks eventually. She said it looking at you."

"Dagny says a lot of things." Now she turned, and her face was composed, priestess-smooth, the mask they must teach before they teach anything else. But her eyes stayed a half-second too long on the middle distance, the way people's do when they're reading something written inside their own skull. "Beatrix. We have a long road. Trust me on the things that matter and don't audit the rest."

The trouble was, auditing was the only skill I had. Nine years of checking every spoonful for the thing that kills you will do that. And here is what I noticed, standing in that crooked square with evening coming down: she'd said *trust me*, not *believe me*. Priests ask for belief. Con artists ask for trust.

I followed her inside anyway. Where else was I going to go? Back through the salt line to the kingdom that owned me? Forward was the only direction with a door in it.

But I lay awake again that night doing different arithmetic. Not *who sold my soul*—that answer was locked in a well that now knew my face, and possibly for sale elsewhere. New sums. Smaller, colder ones. *What did she ask the well, if she asked? What did it take? What has she stopped noticing is gone?*

Because here's the thing about the well's prices, according to Dagny: you don't miss what you don't remember. Which means the only person who'd ever know what Vespera paid was the god that took it.

Or someone who watched her closely enough to catch the gap.

* * *

We left Miren's Ford at grey dawn, ahead of the mist, which Dagny swore behaved strangely east of the village—"moves against the wind, sometimes, like it's following somebody"—and she looked at me when she said it, and I decided not to think about that yet.

Four days to Ashmarket, she'd said. A bazaar where souls traded like coin. Somewhere past it, the court beneath the mountain, holding a contract with my name in it—if I still had a name by then. The well's parting courtesy echoed in my teeth all morning:

*I hope it is the right someone.*

By noon, Vespera had gone quiet in the way of someone carrying a weight they've stopped noticing. By dusk, when we camped in a hollow full of wildflowers that opened only when we spoke aloud, I caught her looking at me—not at my face, exactly, but at the space just above it, the way you look at a book trying to recall a title.

"Something wrong?" I asked.

"No," she said, too quickly. "Nothing. Sleep. Long road tomorrow."

And I closed my eyes and did not sleep, and above me the wildflowers opened and shut, opened and shut, keeping time with voices too small to hear—while somewhere east, in a market built on trade in things like mine, a ledger waited with my signature already drying on it, signed by a hand I'd trusted for nine years.

I just didn't know yet whose hand it was.

I only knew that the next person to offer me a bargain was going to find out what I'd learned at the bottom of a well: I'd rather stay ignorant than be harvested. And I was beginning to wonder whether the woman leading me east had learned the same lesson—or whether she'd already failed it, and paid, and couldn't remember what she'd lost.

The mist followed us out of the hollow at dawn. Against the wind.
