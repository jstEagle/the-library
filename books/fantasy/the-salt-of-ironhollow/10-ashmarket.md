## 10. Ashmarket

You can smell Ashmarket before you see it, which is the first thing they don't tell you about the place. It smells like a bakery that has burned down and been rebuilt on its own ashes every day for a thousand years — warm bread and char, sweetness and ruin, all of it layered so deep into the stone that the mountain itself must taste like it.

The second thing they don't tell you is that you'll want to stay.

We came down out of the high pass at dusk, Vespera walking ahead with her hood up and her chin set in that way she had, as though the road itself owed her an apology. Nine days from the salt line, nine days of villages where prayers were currency and I'd learned to keep my mouth shut when old women pressed charms into my hands — because charms worked out here, that was the thing, they *worked*, and every working made the cage I'd escaped feel less like cruelty and more like sense.

"There," Vespera said.

I looked where she pointed and saw, at first, nothing but a bowl of darkness cupped between two ridges of the mountain's skirt. Then the darkness moved. It resolved into lights — thousands of them, lanterns strung on wires of what I would later learn was hair, bonfires burning colors fire shouldn't burn — and above it all hung a haze that glowed faintly gold, like the market was lit from inside by something digesting.

"That glow," I said. "What is it?"

"Trade," said Vespera, and started down.

* * *

Ashmarket is built in tiers, like an amphitheater for a play no one admits to watching. The lowest tier is honest goods: turnips, tin pots, secondhand boots, a man selling live eels out of a barrel he claimed was his grandmother. You could walk that ring and believe you'd stumbled into any market in any valley, if you ignored that every third merchant had too many joints in their fingers, or eyes the color of pond ice, or a smile that arrived a half-second before the rest of their face decided to join it.

The middle tier is where it starts.

A woman there sold luck. Not charms, not tokens — *luck*, ladled out of a cauldron by the spoonful, and you could watch the customers' fortunes change as they swallowed. A farmer drank and wept and bought a second spoonful for his son. The price wasn't written anywhere. The woman just touched each customer's wrist before they paid, and whatever she found there, she took some of. I watched a young man pay for a spoonful of good fortune and walk away slightly *less* than he'd been. I couldn't say what was missing. That was the horror of it. Nothing you could point to.

"Don't stare," Vespera murmured.

"I'm not staring."

"You're staring like a taster who's just realized the whole world is a dish."

That stopped me, because it was exactly right, and because she'd known enough about my life by then to say it. Nine days on the road will do that. Nine days of her teaching me the shape of the waking gods and me teaching her, without meaning to, every detail of my childhood like a confession neither of us had asked for.

We climbed to the third tier, and that was where the souls were.

* * *

I want to describe it properly, because what struck me was not the wickedness of it. Wickedness I could have understood. I'd grown up in a palace; I knew wickedness the way farmers know weather.

It was the *bookkeeping*.

There was a hall on the third tier lined with counters, and behind each counter sat a broker with a ledger, and the ledgers were beautiful — vellum and calfskin and one bound in something pale that I refused to look at twice. And the trade was conducted in the tone people use for haggling over fish. A tired man sold "the summer I was seventeen" — literally that, a season of memory and joy, boxed and labeled — to buy medicine for his daughter. Two sisters split their grandfather's courage between them because neither could afford a whole measure and both needed to cross a river they feared. A soldier bought back a single hour from a battle and paid with his ability to weep at funerals, and he signed the receipt with a steady hand and walked out lighter, whistling.

Nobody screamed. Nobody wept, except the ones buying back things they'd already lost. There were lamps, and tea, and a broker who chided a client gently about reading the terms before signing. There was a *queue*. Souls stood in a queue.

"They're not trading the whole soul," Vespera said quietly. She'd gone the color of tallow. "Mostly. Fractions. Memories, loves, griefs — pieces. A soul's divisible if you're careful."

"And if you're careless?"

"Then you get the wholesale end of the street." She nodded toward a wide archway at the far side of the hall, beyond which the light went strange and the crowd thinned as though the air itself discouraged company. Above the arch, in letters that seemed to be made of cooled cinders rather than paint, ran a legend: THE COURT BENEATH BUYS IN BULK. ASK FOR THE LEDGER OF LOTS.

"Bulk," I repeated.

"The court beneath the mountain doesn't buy memories or summers." Vespera's voice dropped further, until it was mostly breath. "They buy vessels. Whole containers, empty on purpose. Bodies raised and seasoned and prepared to hold something larger. They've been doing it for centuries. Every kingdom that built walls against magic was really building inventory."

The word *seasoned* did something unpleasant in my stomach. Nine years of poison. Ninety-nine dishes a week. Every immunity a brick.

"Come on," I said. "Let's find the ledger."

"Beatrix—"

"If my name's written in a book somewhere, I want to read the entry."

* * *

The wholesale street was quieter than the rest of the market, the way a chapel is quieter than a tavern, and for much the same reason. The brokers here didn't haggle. They *appraised*. I saw a woman led through a doorway by two attendants, turning back once with an expression I recognized — the look of someone who has agreed to something enormous and is still telling herself it's small.

And at the end of the street, under a canopy of grey silk, sat the ledger of lots.

It was the size of a tomb door, chained open on a lectern, and a clerk attended it — a thin person of indeterminate age whose skin had the matte finish of unglazed pottery. The clerk looked up at us with polite, bottomless patience.

"Inquiry?"

"A contract," Vespera said. "Held by the court beneath. For a vessel bred in the valley of Ironhollow."

Something shifted behind the clerk's clay-smooth face. Not alarm. Interest. The interest of a man who has finally sold the last item in a long-catalogued collection.

"Names are required for searches."

Vespera hesitated. So did I — but only for a heartbeat, because the truth was I'd already given my name away a hundred times since the salt line. To innkeepers. To priests. To the god in chapter nine's village who had wanted a year of my memories and gotten nothing but my refusal. My name was the last coin I owned and I was tired of clutching it.

"Beatrix Dravenmoor," I said.

The clerk turned pages. The sound they made was wrong — not paper, but ash settling. Past entries for Ironhollow, dozens of them, decades of them, vessels listed like livestock with breeding notes in the margins. *Robust constitution. High tolerance. Unremarkable features — desirable.* My whole region of the world was a farm, and I was reading the feed schedule.

Then the clerk stopped turning.

"Here," the clerk said, and turned the lectern toward us.

The entry was brief. Contracts always are; the horror lives in the brevity. *Lot 7749-Dravenmoor. Vessel, prepared, nine years' seasoning complete. Immunity profile: total. Holder of record:* —

and there it was, the seller's signature, a name I knew, a title I knew, inked neat as a grocer's tally —

and beneath that, in letters that were not ink at all but a darkness that moved when I blinked:

*Holder of debt: HIS MAJESTY OF ASH, KING UNDER THE MOUNTAIN, FIRST AND FINAL.*

"Not a court broker," Vespera whispered. She had both hands flat on the lectern as if the floor might tip. "Not a minister. Beatrix — the King himself holds your contract. Personally. Do you understand what that means? Kings don't personally hold anything unless it matters more than kingdoms."

I understood something else, something smaller and colder. I understood that the entry used the past tense. *Seasoning complete.* Not *in progress*. Complete. As far as the ledger was concerned, I was finished goods. Ripe. Ready for collection.

"When," I heard myself ask, "is delivery due?"

The clerk smiled with perfect courtesy, and the smile had no warmth in it at all, and behind us, all down the wholesale street, every lamp guttered at once — as though the market had heard the question and flinched.

"Eclipse," said the clerk. "Naturally."

* * *

We didn't speak until we'd pushed our way back into the noise of the middle tier, where the luck-seller's cauldron bubbled and the eel man shouted and everything was loud enough to hide in. Vespera pulled me into the shadow of an awning stitched from moths' wings and gripped my arm hard enough to bruise.

"An eclipse. How long?"

"Ask your gods. They keep better calendars than I do."

"Beatrix." She never used my name lightly anymore; we'd both learned on the road what names cost here. But she used it now, twice in an hour, and her eyes were bright and frightened. "If the King of Ash holds your debt personally, then no broker can void it, no priest can annul it, no amount of running breaks it. There is exactly one way a contract held by a king ends, and it isn't escape. It's *settlement*. Payment. Or—"

"Or what?"

"Or the holder releases it. And kings don't release."

Somewhere behind us, in the quiet street of appraisals and lot numbers, a bell rang once — soft, unhurried, the sound a shopkeeper makes when a customer finally comes to claim something long since paid for.

Vespera heard it too. We looked at each other, and I saw her reach the same arithmetic I did: we had come to Ashmarket to learn the terms of my captivity, and we had learned them, and learning them had told the seller exactly where his merchandise was standing.

The bell rang again. Closer. Or perhaps just louder, as though it were ringing inside my own ribs.

"We need to move," Vespera said. "Now. Out the low gate, before—"

But even as she spoke, the crowd around us began to part, politely, the way water parts around a keel — and down the lane they made came footsteps that did not hurry, because nothing that certain ever needs to.
