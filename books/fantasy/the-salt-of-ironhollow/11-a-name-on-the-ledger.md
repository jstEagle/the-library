## 11. A Name on the Ledger

I had read holy texts in seven dead languages, deciphered the dream-scrawls of prophets who'd eaten nothing but ash and honey for forty years, and once spent an entire winter translating a hymn that turned out to be a recipe for cheese. I tell you this so you understand I am not easily defeated by old handwriting.

The ledger of Ashmarket defeated me anyway — for about two hours, until I remembered that merchants, whether they trade in turnips or in souls, are lazy above all things. The fancy script was for the entries meant to impress. The real accounting lived in the margins, in a cramped merchant's hand, in numbers.

And Beatrix Dravenmoor was, above all else, a number.

* * *

We had rented a room above a dyer's shop at the edge of Ashmarket because it was cheap and because the fumes made the broker downstairs assume no one sober would choose to sleep there, which discouraged questions. The walls were stained violet. Everything I owned smelled faintly of mordant now. There are worse smells to carry into exile. There is always something worse; Ashmarket exists to teach you this.

Beatrix slept badly. He had slept badly since the bazaar, since we'd learned whose hands held his contract, and I don't blame him. I slept badly too, but for a different reason: I couldn't stop reading.

The copy of the ledger page had cost me three favors and a small lie, which in my experience is the standard exchange rate for anything true. The broker who sold it to me thought I was researching inheritance claims. In a sense he was right. Somewhere under the mountain, something had inherited a soul, and I wanted to know from whom.

The entry itself was elegant, infuriatingly so. The court beneath the mountain kept beautiful books. Every clause of the contract was rendered in that flowing script, all flourishes and restraint, the way a knife is restrained inside a sheath right up until it isn't.

*One vessel, unclaimed at time of sale. Male child, valley-born, valley-bred. Immunities installed per schedule. Delivery upon majority or upon eclipse, whichever conveniences the buyer.*

*Installed.* Like hinges. Like locks.

And then, at the bottom, where the seller signs:

*Executed by the Steward of Tasters, Ironhollow, by authority of the Crown.*

I read it eleven times. The eleventh time changed nothing except my hands, which had begun to shake, so I set the page down on the violet-stained sill and looked out at the market instead, at the stalls closing up for the evening, at the soul-merchants banking their lanterns and their inventory both, and I let myself feel what I felt, which was not surprise.

That was the worst part. It wasn't surprise.

* * *

You have to understand what I was before I fell. I was not a village hedge-priestess muttering over birthing mothers. I served in the high sanctuary at Corvenholt. I could recite the Litany of Sealing from memory before I could write my own name — the founding law of the valley, carved into every temple lintel in the realm: *No god may enter. No soul may leave.*

As a girl, I thought it was a protection. That was the genius of it, really. The whole kingdom thought it was a protection. The salt lines, the tasters, the careful little godless valley — we were told it was a wall against the dark, and we were grateful for our wall, and gratitude is the most obedient of all emotions.

But laws have two faces, like gods do. *No god may enter* keeps the divine out. And *no soul may leave* — well. A law like that doesn't protect souls. It contains them. It makes them stock. You cannot export what may not leave, but you can sell the rights. You can sign a contract in one valley and let the delivery happen wherever the buyer likes. The salt line wasn't a wall around the people of Ironhollow.

It was a fence around a pasture.

I had suspected this for years, in the way you suspect a thing you cannot afford to know. It was why I fell, if I'm honest with myself, and I try to be, these days, when honesty costs less than it used to. I asked one question too loudly at a convocation: *If no soul may leave the valley, whose souls are counted there?* They stripped my vestments within the month. Not for heresy. For arithmetic.

So no, seeing the Steward's name on that line did not shock me. What shocked me was the date.

* * *

The contract bore two dates. The date of execution — twenty-three years past — and a date of amendment, nine years back, initialed in the margin by the same hand.

Nine years. Beatrix has been the queen's taster for nine years. He told me so himself, on the road, with that dry flat way of his, as if nine years of swallowing poison were a trade like any other, as if he'd chosen it the way a man chooses carpentry.

He hadn't chosen it. He'd been *delivered*. Twenty-three years ago, a steward signed away a child not yet born. Nine years ago, that same steward amended the contract — or fulfilled a condition of it, or confirmed the goods were as described — and sent the boy to stand beside the throne with a spoon in his hand.

Every dish he survived. Every poison his body shrugged off. I had thought, when he first told me, that it was a miracle of endurance, the kind of grim human stubbornness I had built my whole broken faith around. It wasn't. It was *specification*. The immunities were installed per schedule. He was not a man who had survived poisons. He was a vessel being fired in a kiln, and every dose was another pass through the flame.

And the crown knew. The crown didn't just know. The crown held the pen.

* * *

Beatrix woke near midnight, the way he always does — all at once, no drifting, as though even his sleep knows better than to dawdle. He sat up in the dyer's-smelling dark and looked at me sitting by the window with the ledger page, and I watched him do the math on my face. He's very good at math on faces.

"What is it," he said. Not a question. He never wastes question-marks on things he already knows.

"You should read this."

"I'd rather not." He said it mildly, almost pleasantly. "I've been avoiding it for nine years. I've gotten quite skilled."

"Beatrix."

"That's the trouble," he said. "That's exactly the trouble. Everyone keeps calling me by that name as if it answers to me."

It was the closest thing to a joke I'd ever heard him make, and it landed like a dropped stone. I held out the page anyway, because I am, whatever else they took from me, still a priestess, and a priestess's whole vocation is handing people truths they'd rather not hold.

He read it standing, by the light of the guttering lamp, and I watched him the way I used to watch converts read their first forbidden text. His face did very little. That was the horror of it. Most people, reading the record of their own sale, would crack somewhere — rage, tears, denial, anything. Beatrix read the terms of his own damnation the way he tasted the queen's soup: carefully, completely, and without any visible reaction, cataloguing each ingredient.

"The Steward," he said finally. "Old Maren. She taught me to hold the spoon." He set the page down with exaggerated gentleness, the way you set down something you're afraid your hands will crush. "She used to check my tongue afterward. Said it was for swelling. She'd hold my chin and look in my mouth like a horse trader."

"She was checking her investment," I said, and hated how it sounded the moment it left me, but there was no kinder phrasing available. There never is, for this particular truth. "She was confirming the immunities held."

"I know," he said. "I worked that out around the word *installed*." He turned toward the window. Below, Ashmarket was settling into its night noises, the particular hush of a place where everything for sale is alive in a way goods shouldn't be. "Twenty-three years. She signed before I was born."

"Yes."

"So the orphanage. The selection. The 'aptitude for service.'" He said the words like he was tasting them, hunting for poison, finding none, finding that worse. "None of it was chance. I wasn't chosen. I was *ordered*. Like cloth."

"Like cloth," I agreed softly.

"And the crown protected me all those years. Fed me. Housed me. Kept me safe from every god and toxin in creation." His voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse to listen to. "Because I wasn't theirs to risk. I was already spoken for."

There it was. I had been circling it all evening, and he walked straight to it in four sentences, the way he does everything — no wasted motion, no drama, just the shortest path through the dark. The kingdom didn't protect Beatrix Dravenmoor. The kingdom *invested* in him. Every kindness was maintenance. Every honor was packaging. The salt of the valley wasn't poured out to keep him holy; it was poured to keep him *unspoiled*, a vessel kept clean for someone else's use.

"They will come for him," I said. "For you. Before the eclipse. The contract says delivery, and contracts here are enforced — you saw what the bazaar does to defaulters."

"I saw." He was quiet a moment. Then: "Vespera. The signature. Maren signed *by authority of the Crown*. Whose authority, exactly? Who gives a steward of tasters power to sell souls?"

I had known this question was coming too, and I had been dreading it more than any other, because I had gone looking for its answer while he slept, and found it in the marginalia, in the little initials and seals that scholars learn to read the way farmers read weather.

"A crown," I said carefully, "is worn by a head. But crowns outlive heads. The authority cited here is the Founding Authority. The same one that sealed the valley. Whoever holds the seal of Ironhollow holds the right of sale over everything inside the salt line." I made myself say the rest plainly, because he deserved plain. "It's not one traitor, Beatrix. It's the throne itself, going back generations. This wasn't corruption. It was *policy*."

He nodded slowly. I had expected anger. I realized, watching him, that I'd been bracing for anger the way you brace for weather, and its absence unsettled me more than a storm would have.

"Good," he said.

"*Good?*"

"It means I can stop looking for the one decent person who might have stopped it." He said it without self-pity, which is the only way that sentence can be said without drowning. "I've spent nine years assuming there was someone above me who didn't know. Someone who'd have intervened. Some good minister, some merciful clerk. It means there wasn't. It means the machine worked exactly as designed, and I was a part that fit." He picked up the ledger page again, folded it along its creases with terrible precision, and handed it back to me. "That's useful knowledge. Rage needs a target. Despair needs an exception. I have neither, so I can get on with it."

"Get on with what?"

"Breaking the contract." He looked at me then, and in the lamplight his eyes had the flat shine of a blade laid down but not put away. "You said the court beneath the mountain holds the debt. So we go under the mountain. You wanted a pilgrimage, priestess. Congratulations. You've got one."

* * *

He slept again after that, or pretended to, and I sat by the window with the folded page in my lap and let the betrayal finish crystallizing, because that's what betrayal does — it starts as a wound and hardens into a tool. I thought about old Maren teaching a boy to hold a spoon. I thought about the convocation that stripped my vestments for asking who counted the souls. I thought about every hymn I'd ever sung praising the godless safety of the valley, every note of it financed by a child sold before his first breath.

Here is what nine years of exile had taught me that my vestments never did: institutions do not sin. Institutions only invest. The sin is distributed so finely across ledgers, signatures, schedules, and spoonfuls that no single hand is ever bloody, and the blood pools instead in the lives of people like Beatrix Dravenmoor, who are told all their lives that they are protected, honored, *safe*.

Well. I had been a priestess of gods who had gone silent, serving a church that served a crown that served a king of ash, and every layer of that hierarchy had been feeding on the layer below it. I had thought my fall was the end of my faith. Sitting in that violet room, holding the proof of a twenty-three-year-old crime, I understood it had only been the beginning. Faith that survives its institution stops being obedience and becomes something with teeth.

I would get him to the mountain. I would get him through the court beneath it. And when the King of Ash opened his beautiful books to collect, I would be standing there with everything I knew about how contracts break — because I had spent my life studying the divine, and one thing I knew for certain about anything that feeds on worship, on names, on souls:

They all believe themselves untouchable. That belief is the flaw in every one of them.

The lamp guttered out. Down in the market, a bell rang once — not the hour bell. The other one. The one I'd been told rang only when a hunter's writ arrived in Ashmarket, authorizing seizure of assets.

Assets.

Through the floorboards, I heard boots in the dye shop below. More than two pairs. Moving quietly, which is the loudest thing soldiers ever do.

"Beatrix," I whispered into the dark. "Get up. They've found us."
