## 12. The Hunt Begins

I had been in Ashmarket for two days before I understood that the stalls selling souls were the honest ones.

Everything else was lies with better signage. The spice merchants sold ash that dreamed of being cinnamon. The cloth-sellers draped you in colors that changed when your mood did, which sounds delightful until you realize they were also reporting your mood to anyone who paid. Even the money was a lie — coins stamped with faces that winked at you when you weren't looking, as if to say *we know what you spent to get here*.

But the soul-stalls? The soul-stalls told you exactly what you were looking at. A vial of a farmer's courage, going cheap because he'd sold it to pay a debt and hadn't needed it since. A jar of a child's first wonder, labeled with a date and a warning: *fades in sunlight.* A shelf of names, alphabetized by someone with a cruel sense of humor, each one tagged like a cut of meat.

Vespera hated the place on principle. I hated it because it felt familiar, though I couldn't have said why until she'd decoded the ledger and shown me my own signature-shaped hole in the world. Now every stall looked like a mirror shop, and I kept walking faster to get past them.

"We need a ferryman," Vespera said for the fourth time that morning, ducking under a canopy strung with bottled regrets. "The entrances to the deep court don't take coin. They take weight."

"Weight of what?"

"Whatever you can't put down." She glanced at my satchel, where the cook's bone whistle rode against my ribs like a second heartbeat. "You'll have no shortage."

It was the closest thing to a joke she'd made since Ashmarket, and I was deciding whether to be flattered or insulted when the market went quiet.

Not all at once. Quiet in Ashmarket spread the way frost does — from the edges inward, one hushed voice at a time, until the silence arrived at the center and stood there wearing armor.

Six figures came down the main aisle. They wore no livery, which in Ironhollow's capital would have meant nothing, but here it meant everything: they had bought anonymity off a stall somewhere, and it fit them badly, the way borrowed clothes always do. Their boots were palace-issue. Their swords were palace-issue. And the man leading them —

I knew his walk before I saw his face. Everyone who'd spent nine years carrying dishes down palace corridors knew Captain Aldous Renn's walk. He moved like a man who had never once had to hurry and never once been late, which is its own kind of threat.

"Vespera," I said, very quietly. "Don't look left."

She looked left. Priests. Nine years dead gods and she still couldn't resist a direction she'd been told not to go.

"That's a royal hunter," I breathed. "That's *the* royal hunter. Renn. He trained half the palace guard. He taught me how to fall without spilling the tray."

"He taught you well?"

"Well enough that I'm about to disappoint him."

Renn stopped three stalls away, beside a merchant selling bottled laughter, and picked up a vial as if he were shopping. He turned it in the light. He was letting us see him see us. That was courtesy, of a kind. Renn believed in giving a man time to make the stupid choice first.

"The crown knows," Vespera murmured. "Of course they know. The moment we crossed the salt line we were — "

"Ledger entries," I finished. "Everything's ledger entries out here."

He set down the vial, thanked the merchant with a nod that would have looked gracious to anyone who didn't know him, and began walking toward us at his never-hurried pace.

Here is what nobody tells you about panic: it isn't loud. It's small and precise, a voice that says *you have nine years of training in surviving palaces and none whatsoever in surviving open ground.* In the palace, danger came with corridors. You knew the corners. You knew which doors locked from which side. Here there was only a bazaar full of liars and six men whose salaries depended on bringing me back intact.

Intact. That word mattered. They wouldn't kill me. The crown had invested too much — I could hear the Steward's oiled voice saying it — *an asset of considerable development.* They wanted me breathing, willing or otherwise, before the eclipse let something else claim the lease.

Which meant, for once in my life, I had an advantage. They had to be careful with me. I didn't have to be careful at all.

"Stall," I said.

"What?"

"*Stall.* Talk to him. You're a priestess; priests talk. It's your whole thing." I was already sliding sideways, into the crowd, becoming the kind of problem markets are designed for. "Meet me at the drowned fountain. Two minutes. If I'm not there, keep going and don't be interesting."

"And if he comes after *me* instead?"

"Then run toward the soul-stalls and knock over as many as you can. Nothing scatters hunters like loose inventory."

She gave me a look that said she intended to survive this specifically so she could lecture me about it later, and then she turned, lifted her chin, and walked straight into Renn's path with her arms open like a woman greeting a nephew at a wedding.

I heard her start talking — something about pilgrims' rights, about sanctuary law older than the valley, her voice climbing into the register that could hold a congregation breathless — and I melted backward through the market.

Ashmarket's aisles are narrow on purpose. Everything here is narrow on purpose. Narrow aisles mean you brush against strangers, and brushing against strangers means touching their wares, and their wares remember. By my third stall I could feel the market starting to know me — a tingle along my arms like static before lightning, little hooks of attention catching in my sleeves. A bottled envy tried to climb onto my shoulder. A hanging dream of someone else's wedding snagged my hair.

"Sorry," I muttered, to a jar of grief I knocked askew. "Sorry. Not today."

Behind me, Vespera's voice rose and cracked beautifully — I could hear Renn's patience fraying against it — and then, sooner than two minutes, sooner than any reasonable negotiation should end, it stopped.

The silence that followed was the frost-quiet again, spreading fast.

I stopped moving. Bad instinct. Standing still in a crowd makes you a shape; moving makes you a smear. But some animal part of me, the part that had survived nine years of tasting death in small doses, went rigid and listened.

"...found her at the salt crossing," a voice said, not Renn's. One of the six. "She'd already sent word ahead. There's a letter."

A pause. Then Renn, calm as a man reading a menu: "To whom?"

"To the court beneath. Asking for asylum."

My stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

She hadn't stalled him. She'd *traded* herself — given him a story, a delay, a reason to take her alive and carry her slowly — while I ran. And the letter, whether it existed or not, whether she'd written it days ago or invented it on the spot, did exactly what she'd intended: it made me the prize they'd chase, and her the bait they'd dangle.

*Don't come for me,* she'd say later, if they ever let her speak again. *I knew the price when I knelt.*

But that was later. Right now the market around me was peeling open like fruit, hunters fanning down the side aisles with the unhurried competence of men who had done this in worse places, and I was pressing myself behind a stall of bottled courage wondering why the one thing I'd never tasted was fear, and whether it was too late to start.

* * *

They nearly had me twice.

The first time was my own fault. I doubled back for Vespera — of course I did, don't tell me you wouldn't have — and got four steps toward the sound of her captors' boots before the bone whistle hummed against my chest. Not a warning. A pull. The way a compass needle doesn't warn you north, it simply insists.

*Down,* the hum said. *Down is the only door.*

I hated it. I stood in an alley between a memory-broker and a woman selling bottled second chances, hating a dead cook's whistle with my whole heart, because it was right and I knew it was right and Vespera had known it was right when she stepped into Renn's path. She hadn't been caught. She'd been *spent*, the way you spend the last coin in your hand to buy the rest of the purse time to run.

The second time was their fault, and nearly fatal, and taught me something about Ashmarket I should have learned on day one: nothing here belongs to itself. When a hunter grabbed my collar near the name-stalls, the names objected. Dozens of sold souls felt a free man being dragged past their jars, and the jars *shrieked* — a sound like a choir of hinges — and the broker came out of his booth swinging a broom at whoever was disturbing his inventory. The hunter let go of me to deal with the broom. I let go of Ashmarket's main aisle entirely.

After that I ran the way water runs, downhill and without dignity, through gaps I found rather than chose, over a bridge made of stacked prayer-rugs that complained under my weight in forty languages, past a child selling luck by the pinch who grabbed my sleeve and said, "You smell like the mountain's business," and let go before I could decide whether to be frightened.

By the time I reached the drowned fountain, the market noise had closed over my wake like water over a stone, and I permitted myself eleven seconds of leaning against the fountain's rim and shaking.

The fountain had drowned centuries ago and never been drained. Its basin was full of black, motionless water, and offerings floated on it — letters to the dead, mostly, weighted with coins, curling at the edges. People wrote to the drowned dead here because the drowned dead were considered better listeners. Cheaper, too.

I watched my reflection do the same thing I was doing, and I thought: *this is what they see. A vessel. A developed asset. Something the crown grew in a cellar and now wants back before the neighbors can move in.*

Nine years of swallowing poison so the queen could eat safely. Nine years, and I'd thought I was a servant. A tool, maybe. A sacrifice, at my most bitter. Never inventory. Never a thing with a holding company.

And now Vespera sat in a palace cell — or worse, a wagon bound for the capital, or worst, already handed across some border to people who dealt in vessels wholesale — because she'd looked at a stranger with a stolen soul and decided he was worth a priestess's freedom.

I fished a coin from my pocket. I had almost none left; Ashmarket's prices were written in things I didn't want to pay. I held it over the black water and realized I had no one to write to. No dead who'd listen. The cook might have, but I'd spent his trust on passage plans, and besides, I'd never known his name. Another man erased before I could save him. The pattern of my life, repeating.

"I'm coming for her," I told the water, because someone ought to say it aloud even if only the drowned heard.

The water did nothing, which from the drowned dead I chose to take as agreement.

* * *

Finding a ferryman, it turns out, is easy. Finding one who'll take you where you're going is the hard part, and finding one *quickly* is apparently impossible, because every ferryman of the drowned dead in Ashmarket had suddenly developed a full schedule.

"They smell the hunt on you," said the third one, an ancient woman with riverweed braided into her beard — don't ask, I'd stopped asking — as she repainted the name on her boat for perhaps the hundredth time that day. "Hunted cargo pays triple or refuses passage. Those are the traditions."

"I don't have triple."

"Then you have a problem and I have a schedule." She dipped her brush. "Come back when you're worth more."

I was composing several replies, none of them useful, when the market's frost-quiet started spreading again.

They'd shaken loose of Vespera's delay faster than I'd hoped. Or Renn had split his hunters — likely; he taught tactics the way other men taught scripture — and posted watchers on the fountain, because of course he had. The fountain was the door to everywhere below, and Renn had carried trays down palace corridors long enough to know that in any building, you watch the doors.

I slipped behind the ferrywoman's mooring post as two hunters worked up the quay, moving apart, unhurried, reading the crowd the way I read a dish. One of them paused at a stall. Bought something. Kept walking.

"They've bought scent," the ferrywoman said conversationally, not looking up from her boat. "From a tracker-stall. Cheap ones last an hour. Good ones last until they find you."

"How much for passage? Honestly."

"Honestly?" She finally looked at me, and her eyes were the gray of deep water. "Passage below costs weight. What are you carrying that you can't put down?"

I touched the whistle through my shirt.

"That," she said. "That's a good start. Dead men's trusts are heavy as anchors. But it won't be enough alone, not for the court beneath. Not during a hunt."

The hunters were closer. I could hear one of them asking questions in the mild, terrible tone of a man who has already been paid.

"Then what else?"

"You." She set down the brush. "Your fear. Your want. Whatever's driving you down that hole — the drowned dead respect a passenger who's going somewhere on purpose. Give them the reason. All of it. Hold nothing back at the threshold, and they'll bear you like a coffin bears a king."

"And if I hold something back?"

"Then they'll bear you like a coffin bears everyone else."

The nearest hunter had reached the mouth of the quay. I recognized his face now — Pellam, one of Renn's younger knives, a boy I'd shared a mess table with for three years. He'd liked me. He'd once covered for me when I broke a decanter. He was scanning the crowd with a strip of scented leather in his fist, and the leather was lifting, pointing, pointing —

"Decision," the ferrywoman said.

I thought of Vespera opening her arms to Renn like family. I thought of the cook's hand closing mine over the whistle, his lips shaping *Vespera* like a prayer he wasn't sure anyone still answered. I thought of a ledger line with my life priced in it, signed by a man who'd tasted my food and called me son sometimes, when the wine was in him.

Everyone who'd ever carried me had ended up spending themselves to do it. I was so tired of being the coin.

"Yes," I said. "All of it. Take me down."

The ferrywoman smiled with too many teeth and began untying the boat. Behind me, Pellam shouted — my name, actually, my old name, the one I still had for another day or so — and the shout went through the market like a crack through glass.

"Cast off," I said.

The black water of the drowned fountain, connected to the quay by channels older than the city, shivered once, all the floating letters turning face-down at the same moment, as if the dead below had heard the hunt beginning and were making room.

The boat slid into the dark. Pellam ran. The leather in his fist pointed straight at me, and then the quay was gone, and the ceiling came down low, and the last thing I saw of Ashmarket was Vespera's absence, shaped exactly like her, going down with me in pieces I hadn't finished paying for.

Below, something vast turned over in its sleep and began, patiently, to listen.
