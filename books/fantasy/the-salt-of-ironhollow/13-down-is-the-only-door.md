## 13. Down Is the Only Door

Grief, I have learned, is a great deal like poison. It arrives in stages. First there is the taste — copper and wrongness on the back of the tongue — then the denial, when you convince yourself it's nothing, a bad meal, a passing chill. Then your hands start to shake, and there is no more pretending.

I had taken Vespera's hand in the crowd at Ashmarket. I had felt her fingers in mine for exactly as long as it takes a man to turn his head and shout *run*. When I turned back, my hand was holding air.

The hunters had been efficient about it. No screams, no scuffle, no trail of dropped belongings for a hero to follow. One moment she was beside me, complaining that everything in the bazaar smelled of tallow and regret; the next she was gone, and the crowd closed over the space where she'd been like water over a stone.

I stood in the market square of a city built from ash and trade, holding nothing, and did what any trained professional does in a crisis.

I catalogued.

One: the hunters were crown-trained, which meant they'd take her up the valley road, not down. The Steward would want her questioned above ground, in a proper room, with proper instruments.

Two: questioning takes time. A day, perhaps two, before they realized she knew less about the vessel's location than they hoped, and began to be creative about it.

Three: the court beneath the mountain held my soul's contract, and the only person who could help me read its terms was currently being carried away in a canvas sack, or would be once they stopped bothering to pretend she was walking voluntarily.

Four: there is a door under Ashmarket, and it goes down.

Everyone in the bazaar knew about the door, the way everyone knows about the thing under their own bed — thoroughly, constantly, and never out loud. You didn't speak of the ferryman. You paid him. Souls crossed in both directions through the flooded dark below the city, and the drowned dead policed the crossing, and the price was always something you'd miss.

*Something you'd miss.* In my line of work, that phrase has teeth.

I found an alley behind a stall selling bottled breath — yes, bottled breath, this was Ashmarket, they also sold Tuesday afternoons and the last words of hanged men — and I sat down on an empty crate to think, because thinking was free and everything else here cost more than it appeared.

Here is what I want to be honest about, since honesty is apparently the one poison I never developed a tolerance for: part of me wanted to go back. Not to Ironhollow, never back to Ironhollow, but *back*, backward, to the morning of the queen's feast when the worst thing in my life was a soup that tasted of incense. Before the cook died pressing a whistle into my hand. Before a priestess in the wall told me I had been sold before I could walk. Before I learned that every bowl I swallowed without dying was another brick laid in somebody else's house.

Nine years I'd told myself I was protected. Immune. Careful. It turns out I was *inventory*. And inventory that runs off the shelf gets retrieved.

Vespera had said that to me once, on the road — "You're not running from them, Beatrix, you're running *late*" — and laughed until she coughed, because she was the sort of person who laughed at true things. Now she was in a sack, or worse, and I was sitting on a crate behind a stall full of other people's exhaled air, and the laugh was nowhere to be found.

So. Determination. Everyone writes about determination as though it's a fire, bright and roaring. In my experience it's colder than that. Determination is what's left after the fire goes out and you get up anyway because lying down means the story ends and you are not finished. I stood up. My legs worked. That would have to be enough raw material.

Down was the only door.

*

Finding the ferryman was easy, which should have frightened me more than it did. Everything in Ashmarket is for sale, including directions, but some things you don't buy — you qualify for. I asked a toothless woman selling luck by the pinch where a man might find the water that isn't water, and she looked at me the way a butcher looks at a cut of meat he hasn't decided on yet.

"You've lost someone," she said.

"Yesterday."

"To them?" She meant the hunters. She meant the crown. In Ashmarket, *them* needs no further specification; it's the oldest commodity on the exchange.

"Yes."

"Then you're already half-ferried." She pinched a measure of luck from her tray and pressed it into my palm without taking coin, which in this city was either charity or a very sophisticated insult. "Follow the smell of old rivers. Not the river smell — the *old* river smell. Like mud that remembers being holy."

Ashmarket sits on pilings above a flooded district that predates the bazaar by a thousand years, streets drowned when the mountain wept or shifted or simply got bored, depending on which vendor you ask. I descended a stair behind the luck-seller's stall that shouldn't have fit in the alley it occupied, and kept descending long past the point where stairs stop making architectural sense, and the air went cold and mineral and wet.

The old river smell found me before I found it. Mud and silt, yes, but underneath it something sweeter and older, temple-smoke and lotus rot, the scent of prayers that had been said sincerely and then abandoned. Water dripped somewhere in the black. Not dripping *down*. Dripping *sideways*, which I decided was a problem for future Beatrix.

"Hello," I said to the dark.

My voice came back wrong. Echoes return what you send. This returned something else — my own voice, but patient, and slightly amused, as if the dark had heard better greetings and worse ones and was grading mine a generous passable.

"You're loud," said a voice that was not an echo. It came from everywhere the water was, which was everywhere. "The dead are sleeping. They sleep badly. What do you carry?"

"Nothing," I said, and then, because lying to a ferryman seemed unwise in the way that stepping off cliffs seems unwise, "a bone whistle, a dead friend's trust, and no name worth stealing."

A lantern lit itself out in the black, greenish, underwater-colored, though there was no flame in it that I could see — only light behaving the way light behaves at the bottom of deep water, which is to say reluctantly. It approached. What carried it had been a man once. The river had kept the shape and traded away most of the rest: skin like wet slate, hair drifting around his head as if he still floated, eyes the pale blind silver of fish. His boat, when it resolved out of the gloom, was black wood furred with moss, and it sat in water that had no surface I could find — it simply was where it was, the way a rumor is.

"The taster," the ferryman said.

I went very still. Stillness is my one athletic talent; nine years at the royal table will teach you to hold your face like a held breath.

"I know the flavor of you," he said. "Every soul that passes this water leaves a taste. Yours passed through nine years ago, going down, in a ledger-man's grip. It tastes of iron and salt and someone else's signature." He tilted his head, hair swaying. "You've come to fetch yourself back."

"My friend was taken by the crown's hunters. The answers I need are in the court beneath the mountain. I need passage down."

"Everything needs passage down." He sounded tired the way only the very old or the very drowned can be tired. "Kings need passage down. Prayers need passage down. Last week a woman needed passage down carrying her father's guilt in a jar. The question is never *whether*. It is *price*."

"Name it."

"That eagerness is why you'll pay too much." But something in the slate-gray face shifted, softened, the way ice shifts before it decides whether to become water or a weapon. "The price is always something missed. For most, that's memory, or love, or the use of their left hand. For you—" he drifted closer, and his blind eyes somehow managed to look directly at the center of my chest, "—for you, the obvious price is the whistle."

The bone whistle sat against my ribs where it had lived since the cook pressed it into my hand with his bloodied fingers and spent his last breath on a name. *Vespera.* It hummed sometimes, faintly, when the gods were near. It was the thread that had led me to her in the first place, the last gift of the last honest man in the palace kitchens, and I had promised him nothing aloud but had promised him everything silently, the way you do.

"No," I said.

The ferryman waited. The water dripped sideways.

"It's not mine to give," I said. "It's a dead man's trust. If I spend it, I spend him. Find another price."

"There is no other price. The crossing costs a treasure." He let the word sit. "And you carry exactly one."

"He was flogged for feeding a god through the kitchen door, and the last thing he did with his hands was give this to a stranger he hoped might matter. If I trade it for my own convenience, he dies twice." My voice cracked on the last word, which annoyed me enormously. Nine years of tasting poison without flinching and a dead cook's memory undoes me. Grief has terrible aim.

The ferryman was quiet a long time. Somewhere far off in the flooded dark, something vast turned over in its sleep.

"You refuse the treasure," he said at last, "but you offer to guard it. Do you understand what you're saying, taster? The crossing must be *paid*. Rules older than rivers. Older than the mountain. I cannot waive them. I am not permitted to be kind."

"Then let me pay differently." I heard the idea form even as I spoke it, the way you hear your own footsteps in an empty hall. "You said my soul passed through here once, going down, in a ledger-man's grip. It's held in the court beneath. When I get it back — *if* — the debt comes due. So take a lien. Take a claim on my soul's crossing. Whatever I recover down there, the ferry gets first passage rights on it. One crossing, owed, collectible whenever you choose, forever if you choose."

The silence that followed had texture. Weight. Somewhere in it, I understood dimly, I had just done something either brilliant or catastrophically stupid, and the difference between those two things has never once been visible to me in advance.

"A lien on a soul not yet yours," the ferryman said slowly, "secured against a recovery not yet made, payable across a threshold not yet reached." His mouth did something that on a living face would have been a smile and on his was closer to erosion. "That is either the worst bargain I have ever heard or the best. The dead will argue about it for a century."

"Which is it?"

"Both. That's why it works." He extended a hand, slate-gray fingers uncurling, and I saw now that his palm bore a brand: scales, old ones, the kind carved into temple doors. "But understand the terms fully, taster, because I am forbidden to lie and forbidden to soften. If you die down there unrecovered, the claim transfers to whatever remains of you. If you succeed and then flee rather than pay, the water will find you. It has found men in palaces. It has found men in deserts. There is no salt line deep enough."

"And if I pay?"

"Then you cross clean, and we are strangers, and I forget your taste." He paused. "Most people find that the hardest term."

Most people, I thought, had souls worth remembering. Mine had spent nine years marinating in arsenic and nightshade and the queen's favorite henbane wine. Whoever eventually ferried it might be doing the river a favor.

"I accept," I said.

He took my hand. His grip was cold in the specific way of things that have stopped pretending to be warm, and where his palm-brand touched my skin I felt a small cold *click*, deep in the sternum, like a lock engaging in a door I hadn't known I had.

"Get in," said the ferryman of the drowned dead.

*

The boat moved without rowing. Or rather, the boat stayed perfectly still and the world — the flooded streets, the drowned doorways, the pale shapes drifting below the surface like thoughts you almost caught — slid past us instead. I decided not to examine the mechanics. There are limits to what a man can productively inspect at two in the morning while grieving.

The dead watched us go. That's the part I wasn't prepared for. Not ghosts leaping wailing from the water — nothing so theatrical. Just faces, upturned beneath the surface, hundreds of them, patient as stones, watching the boat pass the way crowds watch a funeral carriage. Some looked curious. Some looked envious. A few — and these were the worst — looked *relieved*, as if seeing someone head further down meant, for once, it wasn't them.

"Do they all want passage?" I asked.

"They want what everyone wants," the ferryman said. "To be somewhere they're not. To be someone they aren't." He glanced back at me, hair swirling. "You especially. You've been someone else since before you could walk. Down there you'll meet the party of the second part."

"The King of Ash."

"The collector." Something moved through the water around us at the word — not fear exactly. Recognition. The way a dog recognizes a whip's owner. "Mind one thing, taster. He will offer to make the trade simple. He will show you the contract written plain, and it will seem fair, because collectors write fair-seeming things the way spiders weave pretty webs. Read the margins. The margins are where he keeps himself."

"Noted." I hesitated. "Can I ask you something? You said my soul passed through nine years ago. Did it — did it look back? Going down?"

The ferryman was quiet for so long I thought he wouldn't answer. We slid past a drowned chapel, its bell still hanging, crusted white with mineral bloom like frost.

"Everything looks back," he said finally. "That's how I know it was yours, and not merely stock. Stock doesn't look back. Yours pressed its face against the ledger-glass the whole way down." His blind eyes fixed on me. "Whatever they told you about what you are, taster — remember that. It fought. Even then. Even knowing nothing, it fought."

I looked at the water and let my eyes burn, because there was no one down here to see it except the dead, and the dead, I suspected, were the only audience I'd ever had who understood.

We traveled a long time. Time works strangely in the flooded dark — my joints ached as though days had passed, though the lantern never dimmed. At some point the ceiling of the caverns lowered and the water narrowed and the drowned streets gave way to something older, natural, the mountain's own veins, and the temperature dropped until my breath showed.

Then the boat stopped. Simply stopped, as if the concept of *forward* had run out.

"Here," the ferryman said. "The rest is walking. The water won't carry the living into the court — the court pays tolls to keep it out."

I climbed onto a ledge of black stone slick with condensation. Ahead, a corridor sloped downward, and from its throat came light — not the green underwater glow of the lantern but a dry amber light, candlelight multiplied beyond reason, and with it a sound. Music. Distant, elegant, faintly wrong, like a waltz played by musicians who had learned dancing only from descriptions.

"Last counsel," the ferryman said, already turning the boat, already becoming shadow. "The whistle you refused to spend. Keep it close. Bone remembers what contracts forget. And taster—" the lantern guttered, "—when you find what they took from you, it may not wish to come back. Things improve, in captivity. Even souls. Especially souls."

Then he was gone, and the dark folded shut behind him like a book.

I stood alone on the ledge at the top of the world's longest staircase, listening to the music of the court beneath the mountain, and I took inventory one final time, because inventory is what I have instead of courage:

One bone whistle, humming faintly against my ribs, warmer than it should be.
One lien, cold-clicked into my sternum, collectible forever.
One friend in a sack somewhere above me, whose name I whispered now into the dark like a prayer to gods who had finally started listening: *hold on, hold on, I'm coming down.*
And no name of my own worth the taking — which, I was beginning to suspect, might prove the strangest weapon in my possession after all.

I started walking. Down is the only door, and I had knocked.

The music stopped.

In the sudden silence, from everywhere at once, from the candles and the stone and the amber dark ahead, came a voice like ash settling — patient, pleased, and terribly well-informed.

"*The taster,*" it said, "*has arrived. We've kept your seat.*"
