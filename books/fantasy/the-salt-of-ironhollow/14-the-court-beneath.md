## 14. The Court Beneath

The ferryman's boat did not so much arrive at a shore as give up on moving.

One moment there was the black water and the sound of my own heartbeat doing its best impression of a war drum; the next, the keel scraped stone, and the old man with the drowned face gestured with his pole toward a stairway cut into rock. He had not spoken the entire crossing. I had paid him a bone whistle and a dead cook's trust, and apparently that bought silence as standard.

I stepped off. The moment my boots touched the stone, the boat was simply gone — no wake, no oar-creak, nothing. As though the river had decided it had never carried me and would deny it under oath.

"Charming place," I said to no one. My voice came back wrong off the walls. Flattened. Swallowed.

The stairway climbed — which surprised me; I had assumed down was down all the way to whatever passes for a bottom in this world — and with each step the air changed. Above ground, Ironhollow air tasted of salt and iron, the valley's two favorite flavors. Down here the air tasted of candle smoke and cold metal, and underneath it something sweeter, like lilies left too long in a sealed room.

I knew that smell. Every taster does. It's what poison smells like when it's trying to be polite.

* * *

The stairs ended at a door, and the door was the first honest thing I'd seen since leaving the surface: plain, iron-banded, unadorned. Then I pushed it open and walked into the palace.

Not *a* palace. **The** palace. The one I'd served in for nine years, except inverted — every detail flipped like a reflection in dark water. The great hall above had windows of colored glass depicting the founding of the valley; here, the same windows showed the founding from the other side, and I did not care for what the other side looked like. In the glass above, stern settlers raised walls against a wilderness. Here, they raised walls around something that was already screaming.

The throne room above had a dais of white marble. Below, black marble, and on it a throne that I recognized with a lurch: it was the queen's throne, the one she'd sat upon while I knelt and swallowed her enemies' worst intentions every morning of my adult life. Except this one faced away from the hall, toward a blank wall.

I stood in an antechamber where I'd stood a thousand times waiting to report the morning's dishes, and the ceiling above me was the floor I'd knelt on, or some argument of geometry that suggested it. My skin crawled in a way that had nothing to do with poison and everything to do with architecture.

"You're staring," said a voice near my elbow. "Everyone stares. It's considered rustic."

I turned. A courtier stood beside me — or the shape of one. He wore court dress of a fashion thirty years out of date, cut fine, kept immaculate. His hands were correct. His posture was impeccable. His face —

His face was the Steward of Tasters'. Not the man himself; younger, smoother, but the same heavy jaw, the same hooded eyes, arranged slightly wrong, like a portrait painted by someone who'd only ever heard the subject described.

"That's a borrowed face," I said. Vespera had warned me, in the fragments of instruction she'd pressed on me before the hunters took her. *Don't look at their faces too long. Don't answer to your own name if a stranger uses it. And Beatrix — whatever you see, remember the mirrors lie by telling the truth.*

"It is," the courtier agreed pleasantly. "The original grew tiresome. Faces do, eventually. They get set in their opinions." He examined me with those not-quite-Steward eyes. "You're the vessel. You smell like nine years of arsenic and duty. Remarkable. Like walking past an apothecary that's been on fire."

"I'm looking for someone. A woman. They took her in Ashmarket. Grey robes, ruined hands, talks like scripture fell on her and she never fully recovered."

"Mm. Prisoners go to the galleries." He waved vaguely deeper into the palace. "Though I'd hurry, if I were you. The court forgets quickly, but the living forget faster. It's how we keep them." He smiled with the Steward's mouth, and it was the most obscene thing I had ever witnessed. "Do give the King my regards. Or don't. He collects regards too."

He drifted away into the inverted hall, and I noticed then what I should have noticed immediately: the court was full. Hundreds of figures, murmuring, dancing without music, clustering in conversational knots — and every single one wearing a face I half-knew. A chambermaid who'd gone missing from the palace when I was fourteen. A border lord rumored dead these ten years. A child's face on a tall body, worn like a brooch. None of the faces matched their owners. None of the owners seemed to mind.

Above, in the sunlit palace, I had spent nine years learning to read faces for the flicker of poison, the micro-tells of a dish that lied. Down here, every face was a lie wearing the memory of a person, and I realized with a cold settling in my gut that this was what the court beneath collected when it wasn't collecting souls. It collected the evidence that people had existed.

I walked deeper.

* * *

The galleries were cells without doors — alcoves of black stone opening onto corridors, each one furnished with terrible gentleness: a chair, a candle, a small window that showed not the underworld but the surface. Someone's home, seen from an angle they'd never chosen. Vespera had told me once that prayers were how the gods found people. I understood now that these windows worked the same way in reverse. Each prisoner could watch the world that was forgetting them.

I passed a dozen alcoves before I understood the mechanism, and I owe that understanding to an old woman in the third gallery who called me by name before I'd made a sound.

"Beatrix Dravenmoor," she said, in a voice like dry leaves. "Taster to the crown. Ninth of your line to serve the valley."

My whole body went still. "Who told you that?"

"No one tells us anything, dear. We hear the living thinking about us. That's the trick of this place. As long as someone up there remembers you — truly remembers, holds you in their mind like a stone in the hand — you can hear it. Like warmth through a wall." She smiled with genuine sweetness, and it broke my heart more thoroughly than any horror could have. "You've been thought about recently. Fiercely. By a woman praying very badly. She prays like she's arguing with the gods personally. Is she yours?"

Vespera. Alive. Praying. "She was taken in Ashmarket. Two days ago."

"Then she'll be in the deep galleries. They keep the recently taken shallow — easier to ransom, sometimes. But a priestess?" The old woman clucked. "They won't ransom her. The court hates priests. All those years of being told it wasn't real." Her eyes glittered. "Go quickly, taster. And listen to an old woman who has watched her daughter's face grow smooth in her memory — watched her own name wear thin like a coin rubbed between fingers. Being forgotten isn't quiet, whatever they say. It sounds like snow. It falls on everything, softly, and one morning the garden is gone."

I didn't have an answer for that. I gave her the only thing I had, which was the truth: "If I find what I'm looking for, I'll break this place open."

"Oh, they all say that." She settled back in her chair, content as a cat. "But you smell like someone who's already died once and found it negotiable. Go on, then."

* * *

The deep galleries were colder, and the candles burned with flames that bent toward the prisoners instead of away — as though even fire down here leaned in to listen to the last warm thoughts of the living.

I found Vespera in the ninth alcove.

She sat cross-legged on the stone floor, grey robe torn at the shoulder, her ruined hands folded in her lap with the deliberate calm of someone holding themselves together by doctrine alone. Her lips moved. Through the little window above her — showing, I saw with a twist, the road outside Ashmarket, our road, our abandoned campfire — her prayer went up like smoke.

*I don't know his name. I don't know his name. Let that be enough to save him. I don't know his—*

"Vespera."

Her head snapped up. For one naked second her face did something I had never seen it do — cracked wide open, all the priesthood and the wry armor gone, just a woman in a hole seeing a stupid, stubborn man walk into the one place he shouldn't. Then the discipline closed over it like water.

"You absolute fool," she whispered. "You beautiful, catastrophic fool. How are you *here*?"

"Ferryman. Bone whistle. Long story, and you're missing the good parts about the river."

"The river doesn't have good parts." She gripped the bars — there were bars, thin as needles, silver-dark — and her voice dropped to a rush. "Listen to me. You can't stay. The King knows you're coming; the contract pulls at you like a hook. Everything down here bends toward you, do you understand? This whole court is a throat, and you're the meal it's been keeping the plate warm for."

"Then we leave. Together. There must be a way out the way I came in."

"There isn't. Not for me." She lifted her wrists. Around each one was a ring of fine script, inked directly into the skin, and the script was moving, slowly, patiently, like worms through loam. "Prisoner's marks. While these hold, I belong to the ledger. And Beatrix—" her voice caught on my name, and I watched her hate that it caught, "—every hour I'm down here, fewer people up there remember me. My order struck me from their rolls years ago. My mother is dead. The novices I taught will be middle-aged and vague about me. Do you see? The court doesn't need to kill its prisoners. It just waits for the world to misplace them. I have perhaps a season left of being remembered at all. After that, even if I walked out, I'd be a stranger knocking at every door."

The old woman's words came back to me. *Snow.*

"Then I get these marks off you."

"With what? You have no magic. No name anyone down here respects. You have—" she gestured at me, exasperated, "-a body full of poison and a face I'm trying very hard to memorize right now, actually, so stop talking for a moment."

I stopped talking. She stared at me with the ferocious attention of someone copying a text she expects to lose, tracing my features with her eyes like a woman counting coins she knows will be stolen. It should have been uncomfortable. It was unbearable, and I let it be, because I understood exactly what she was doing, and I understood exactly why, and the understanding sat in my chest like a swallowed coal.

She was memorizing me because she expected to be forgotten. And somewhere beneath the fear, colder and quieter, I felt the true dread of this place settle over me — not death, which a taster makes peace with by the second year, but erasure. Nine years ago I'd been an orphan with nothing. I'd built something out of duty, out of routine, out of the small pride of a job done well in the dark where no one thanked me. And this court dissolved such things. It dissolved mothers' faces and daughters' names and the entire architecture of having mattered. Down here I finally understood what the valley above had been protecting, all along, with its salt lines and its founding law.

Not the kingdom. Memory itself. The valley was a jar, and we were all of us pickled in it, safe from a court that ate remembrance — and I had walked out of the jar carrying the one soul the court wanted most.

"They're coming," Vespera breathed. Her eyes had gone to something over my shoulder. "They always come for the vessel. Don't fight them. Whatever happens — don't let them make you angry. Anger is an opening. The King reads anger like a taster reads a dish."

"Vespera—"

"And Beatrix." Her hands wrapped around the needle-bars, and the moving script on her wrists flared faintly red, punishing her, and she didn't flinch. "Whatever he offers you. Whatever price he names. Remember that down here, everything is priced honestly except the things that matter, and those are priced in the only coin the King truly values."

"Which is?"

Behind me, unhurried footsteps on black marble. A great many of them, in perfect unison, like a single creature wearing a procession.

"A name," said a voice that was not a voice so much as a settling of ash, dry and vast and terribly patient. "Come along, vessel. We have kept your seat warm for nine years. It would be rude to make the eclipse wait."

The candles in every gallery bowed at once, flames bending low, and the court beneath the mountain opened its borrowed faces in a thousand identical smiles.

I turned to meet the King of Ash — and behind me, in her cell of soft forgetting, Vespera went on mouthing the only prayer she had left: *I don't know his name.*
