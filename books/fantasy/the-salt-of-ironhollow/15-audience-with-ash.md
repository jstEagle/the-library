## 15. Audience with Ash

They dressed me for the audience.

That was the first insult. Not chains, not a cell — tailoring. Two courtiers with borrowed faces (a young woman's eyes, an old man's jaw, stitched together like a quilt made by someone who hated quilts) measured me with knotted cords and clucked over my shoulders as though I were a bride rather than a debt.

"You'll want to look presentable," one said. Its voice had the texture of paper left out in rain. "His Majesty appreciates presentation."

"Does he," I said. "What does he appreciate second?"

Neither of them laughed. Neither of them could, I suspected. Laughter requires a face of your own, and every face in that corridor belonged to someone else — someone above, someone living, someone who probably missed it without knowing why their reflection had started looking tired.

I thought about Vespera in her cell below the waterline, behind a door of black glass that showed whoever looked into it a version of themselves they'd rather not meet. I'd seen mine. It had been wearing my mother's expression. I'd stopped walking for a full minute before I made myself go on.

The courtiers led me up. That surprised me. Everything about the court beneath the mountain seemed designed to go down — down through drowned galleries, down past markets where grief was sold by weight, down to the ferryman and his boat of nails. But the throne room, when we reached it, was *up*: a cavern so tall the ceiling vanished into dark, with pillars of veined stone rising like the ribs of something that had swallowed a cathedral and died content.

And at the far end, on a dais of compacted ash — actual ash, gray and fine, raked into patterns like a zen garden tended by a very patient nihilist — sat the King of Ash.

He was smaller than I expected. That's always the way with monsters; the imagination inflates them, gives them horns and heights and voices like rockslides. The King was a lean man in a robe the color of cooled coals, with a face so ordinary it took me a moment to understand the horror of it: it wasn't borrowed. It was *generic*. A face like the idea of a face. The default setting of humanity.

"Beatrix Dravenmoor," he said, and my name came out of his mouth sounding used. Secondhand. Like he'd owned it a while and worn it smooth. "The taster. Nine years at the table. Do you know what your service record says? I have it here."

He lifted a ledger from beside the throne. Black binding, pages the color of old teeth. My stomach did something complicated, because I'd seen that book before — in Ashmarket, in the broker's stall, its twin open to entries written in ink that moved when you weren't looking.

"Sit," the King said.

There was no chair. There was, however, a small mound of ash near the foot of the dais, shaped suspiciously like a seat, and when I hesitated it patted itself flat in invitation. I have been poisoned professionally for nine years; I am not easily daunted by furniture. I sat. It was, annoyingly, comfortable.

"Now." He opened the ledger to a page marked with a ribbon of woven hair — mine, I realized, taken at some point I couldn't pinpoint, which is the sort of thing that keeps you up at night. "Let us dispense with the theatrics you've been bracing for. No riddles. No threefold trials. You are a practical man; I will be practical back."

"I'm listening."

"The contract." He turned the book toward me. The script on the page was in a language I shouldn't have known and yet read fluently, the way you understand a nightmare. "Executed before your birth. Parties: the Crown of Ironhollow, represented by its Steward of Tasters, acting under royal seal. And the Court Beneath, represented by me. The commodity—" he tapped the page, and the word *commodity* flared briefly like a coal breathed upon, "—is you. Specifically: the vessel. Body, blood, and the hollow space reserved within."

"Hollow space," I repeated.

"Souls take up room, Beatrix. Yours was removed at the moment of contracting — sold forward, as it were, held in escrow. What has lived in your chest these twenty-six years is a placeholder. Very well-made. We use only the finest craftsmen." He smiled, and the smile fit him the way a stranger's coat fits you: close enough to pass, wrong in every seam. "Your immunity was never protection. It was *construction*. Nine years of poison, each dose a chisel. You were being carved, son. Hollowed for occupancy."

I knew this. Vespera had told me, and I'd spent the road north telling myself I believed her. But there is a difference between hearing a thing and hearing it in a voice that sounds bored, from a king holding your own hair as a bookmark.

"And the god?" I asked. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "The one you're renting me to."

"*Renting* implies I profit twice." He sounded genuinely pained. "I am owed once. The returning one — you'd know it as the Third Woken, the god whose prayers went unanswered longest beneath your valley's salt — comes back at the next eclipse. Eleven days. It requires a body immune to corruption, resistant to every poison of flesh and spirit, unclaimed by any living faith. Your crown spent nine years and considerable treasury funds making sure such a body existed. I simply held the paperwork."

Eleven days. I thought of the sky over the mountain, and how somewhere above it the moon was already beginning its slow approach to the sun, patient as a debt collector.

"And if the debt isn't paid," I said, "the god moves in."

"Pours in," he corrected. "Possession is such a violent word. Think of it as homecoming. The body remembers its tenant. You'll be present for it, incidentally — pressed into the walls of yourself, watching. Most vessels find the first century disorienting."

"Most vessels don't get a choice."

"No vessel gets a choice." He closed the ledger gently, almost tenderly. "Except you. That is why I granted the audience. Because there is a third option, and you've already guessed it, haven't you? Clever boy. The taster's palate. You taste everything before you swallow it."

I had guessed it. Somewhere between the mirrored corridor and the ash that shaped itself into furniture, I had assembled it from the pieces the cook's whistle gave me, from Vespera's half-answers, from the way everyone down here kept asking my name and writing it down.

"My soul," I said. "You'll sell it back."

"Sell? No." For the first time, something moved behind the generic face — not warmth, nothing so human, but interest, the way a lender looks at collateral. "Contracts of this vintage can't be bought out. They can only be *amended*, and amendment requires consideration freely given. Here is the clause." He reopened the book and turned it again, and the words rearranged themselves under my eyes into plain speech:

*The debt shall be discharged in full should the vessel surrender, of its own free will and without coercion, that which it was contracted against: namely, its name.*

"A name," I said.

"Freely given," he agreed. "Spoken into the ledger. Once spoken, it is mine. You become, in law and in memory, unnamed. Unrecorded. Every person who ever knew it will find the knowing slipping away like water through fingers. Your records above will blank themselves. Your cell will stand empty and no one will wonder why. You will walk out of here owning yourself entire — soul returned, contract void, god refused — and no one you saved will remember there was ever anyone to save."

* * *

I asked for time to think. He gave me an hour and a view: a balcony of black glass overlooking the drowned galleries, where the dead drifted like kelp and the stolen faces of the nobles glimmered in the water's reflections. Somewhere below, Vespera sat in a cell forgetting herself by degrees. Somewhere above, eleven days of moonlight remained.

I stood there doing arithmetic.

Here is the trap, laid out plainly, because I want it recorded even if no one reads it: a name is not a word. A name is the handle by which everything else holds onto you. My mother spoke mine over my cradle and it became the first brick of me. The Steward wrote it into a ledger and it became the deed to my body. Vespera learned it across a smuggled loaf of bread and it became the reason she followed me into the dark. Take away the name and you don't lose a sound. You lose the thread. Everyone who loved you lets go without knowing they were holding anything.

And the alternative was worse. The alternative was standing inside my own skull for eternity, watching some starving god wear my hands, immune even to death — because of course I would be, that was the whole design, that was the *point* of me. Nine years of swallowing ruin so that nothing, not hemlock, not despair, not divinity itself, could evict whatever moved in.

They built me safe so that I could be taken safely.

I laughed, actually, alone on that balcony, and the sound came back off the black glass wearing someone else's laugh, and I stopped.

There was one more thing. One thread I hadn't pulled, and I pulled it now, walking back into the throne room with my decision forming like scar tissue.

"The amendment," I said. "It frees *my* soul and voids *my* contract. Does it release the others? The faces in your mirrors. The names in your ledger. All of them."

The King regarded me for a long moment. Around the throne room, the courtiers went very still, in the way of servants hoping not to be noticed during a conversation about money.

"That clause," he said carefully, "is not part of the standard amendment."

"I'm not asking for the standard amendment."

"Then you're asking for something worth considerably more than one name."

"Am I?" I spread my hands. "Look at me, Your Majesty. Look at what I am. A hollow man in a borrowed coat, eleven days from becoming a rental property. What exactly do you imagine I have that you want — besides the one thing you've already named?"

And there it was. The flicker. Small, quickly banked, but I've spent nine years reading faces for traces of poison, and greed leaves a residue like arsenic: faint, metallic, unmistakable.

He wanted more than my name. He wanted me to *offer* it. There is a difference, in the deep law that governs things like him, between a price extracted and a gift surrendered — and gifts bind tighter. If I bargained, haggled, demanded terms, the transaction stayed a transaction. But if I gave freely, gladly, *gratefully*—

He didn't just want my name. He wanted my consent to matter. He wanted the story of the taster who gave up everything willingly, told in his court forever, a jewel in the ledger. He wanted me to make surrender look beautiful.

Fine, I thought. Let's both perform.

"I'll give it," I said aloud. "Freely. Gladly. On one condition: when the contract closes, every name in that ledger goes home. Every face returns to its owner. The whole book, burned clean. Not just me."

The silence stretched long enough that I heard the ash garden settling, grain by grain.

"You understand," the King said softly, "that if I agree, I agree to the destruction of everything I am. A king of collected names, emptied."

"I understand you'd be agreeing to something you think you can wriggle out of later. I've met kings." I nodded at the ledger. "But here's my protection, since yours is contracts: I've tasted everything you serve down here, metaphorically speaking. I'll speak my name into that book with conditions spoken first, witnessed by the drowned dead on your balconies, who owe their faces to nobody and forget nothing. You taught me the value of a witness, Majesty. You keep an entire court of them."

Something happened to his generic face then. I couldn't name it — a shifting, like ash rearranging after a boot print — but I chose, generously, to interpret it as respect.

Or hunger. With collectors it's usually hunger.

"Eleven days," he said. "Return before the eclipse. Speak the words. And bring no priestess, no whistles, no cleverness I haven't already tasted." He smiled again, and this time the coat fit perfectly, and that was worse than anything. "You have grown, taster. Your crown should be proud."

"They were never mine to make proud," I said, and walked out of the throne room with my name still in my mouth, feeling it there like a stone I was carrying to throw — or like a coin I was carrying to spend, and couldn't decide yet which hand to drop it into.

Behind me, soft as a closing book, I heard the ledger open to my page.

And beneath the mountain, faint but unmistakable, something began counting down.
