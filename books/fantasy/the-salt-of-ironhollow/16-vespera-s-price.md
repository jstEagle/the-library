## 16. Vespera's Price

I have lied to gods, to kings, to one very persistent goat in the village of Marrowfen who seemed to know things it shouldn't. But the lie I have told longest, and best, is the one I tell Beatrix Dravenmoor every morning when he asks me how I slept.

*Fine,* I say. *Well enough.*

Here is what actually happens when I sleep: I dream of a name.

It comes to me the way water comes to a dry riverbed — not all at once, but as a pressure, a shape, a certainty that something used to be here. In the dream I am standing at a door made of ashwood, and I know that if I speak the word resting behind my teeth, the door will open. I wake with my mouth already forming it. And then it's gone, the way a name on the tip of your tongue is gone the moment someone says *never mind*, except no one has said never mind. No one said anything. Something was simply *taken*, cleanly, professionally, the way a good thief takes a purse — you don't miss the weight until you reach for your coins.

I know exactly when I paid for it. That's the cruel joke. I remember the transaction perfectly. It's only the merchandise that's missing.

* * *

Let me back up, because a confession without context is just cruelty wearing a cassock.

Before Ironhollow, before the salt line and the taster with his careful hands, I was Vespera of the Ninth Choir, priestess of the Waking Gods, and I was very good at my job. That was the problem. The priesthood doesn't punish incompetence — incompetence is safe, predictable, easily managed. What it punishes is *listening*. A priestess who hears the gods answer is a priestess who might hear them say something inconvenient. Like: *why do you keep us behind walls?* Or: *who taught you to be afraid of us?*

I heard. I asked. I was defrocked with great ceremony and greater speed, and I crawled into the one place in the world built specifically so the gods could not follow — the valley of Ironhollow, whose founding law I could recite in my sleep even after they took my vestments: *no god may enter, no soul may leave.* I thought I was fleeing into a dead place. I did not understand yet that dead places are where living things go to be hidden.

That's where I found him. Not found him — he found me, following the hum of a bone whistle through the foundation stones like a man following a thread out of a labyrinth, except the labyrinth was his whole life and the thread led deeper in. He stood there in the dark under the palace with his taster's pallor and his taster's stillness, and he asked me the question that has kept me up every night since:

*Who sold me?*

And I — fool, fraud, fallen woman of the Ninth Choir — I said, *I'll find out for you.*

* * *

The village god came to us on the ninth day of our road, in a place called Thistlebrook where the old gods had begun answering prayers again, crude and hungry and newly awake. This one lived in a well. They often do. Water remembers everything and holds grudges about none of it, which makes wells excellent vessels for divinity and terrible company at dinner.

This god had grown fat on the village's small desperate prayers — rain, milk, a son home from the war. It spoke in the voice of everyone who'd ever dropped a coin down its throat, which gave its speech an unsettling chorus quality, like a crowd whispering in agreement.

Beatrix asked it nothing. That's him all over. He walks past open doors because he learned young that doors are how cages work.

But I stopped. Because the well-god looked at me — and I use *looked* loosely; it rippled in my direction with intent — and it said, in the voice of a girl who had drowned there forty years ago:

*I know what you're carrying, little priestess. I can smell a debt on you from three parishes away.*

"I'm not carrying anything," I said, which was true in the way most of my best lies are true.

*You owe,* it chorused. *You paid something once and got less than you bargained. I can taste unfinished business. I deal in finished business. Ask your question. Everyone has a question. Yours has been rotting in you since the salt line.*

And here is the thing I need you to understand about me, the thing I have never said aloud to anyone, the thing that will make the rest of this chapter land like a knife between ribs: I wanted to ask. I have always wanted to ask. Asking is my heresy, my signature sin, the reason the Ninth Choir stripped my title and burned my prayer-book. Other priests doubt; I *inquire*. It's practically a medical condition.

So I leaned over the well, and I asked the question I had been carrying since I first read the ledger in Ashmarket and saw the seller's signature — the Steward of Tasters, acting for the crown — and understood that Beatrix Dravenmoor's entire life was a line item in someone else's budget.

"What is his true name?" I asked. "The name he was born under. The name that would void the contract."

Because that's what I'd learned in the bazaar, haggling over soul-ledgers while hunters closed in: contracts written in blood can be broken by blood, but contracts written in *names* can only be broken by the true name, spoken freely by someone who knows it, at the right hour, in the right place. The court beneath the mountain held Beatrix's soul under the name his mother gave him. The Steward knew that name. The King of Ash knew it. Beatrix himself did not — they'd taken it from him along with everything else, left him only *Beatrix Dravenmoor*, a working label, a cage-door painted to look like a window.

If we could learn the true name, we could void the contract from outside. From outside. Without him having to stand before the King of Ash and bargain with the only currency he had left.

I asked the question. The well went quiet in the particular way that means a god is deciding how much you can afford.

*Ah,* said the drowned girl's voice, and the chorus behind it sighed with something almost like pity. *That name. That name is expensive, little priestess. Names within names. You want the key to the vault, but the vault is a person, and persons are the one thing I cannot simply give. I must trade.*

"Name your price."

*Not mine. The price sets itself, according to what you ask. You ask for a name that belongs to another man's soul. The market rate for such knowledge—* the water shivered— *is memory of equal weight. One year. We choose which year. The gods choose. Not you.*

"One year," I repeated. "Of my memories."

*Of yours,* it agreed, pleasantly, the way a butcher agrees about cuts of meat.

Now. A wiser woman would have walked away. A wiser woman would have remembered that she had already lost her title, her gods, her home, and that the only thing she had left unbartered was the contents of her own skull. But wisdom, as I may have mentioned, is not among my vices.

Besides — and this is the part I have turned over four hundred times since, examining it like a jeweler checking for flaws — besides, I thought, *what could a year possibly take that matters?* I had nine years of priesthood, thirty-odd years of a hard and mostly unpleasant life, and the sum total of my time with Beatrix Dravenmoor amounted to a handful of weeks on a bad road. Whatever year the god chose, it couldn't touch what mattered. It couldn't touch the weeks. Those were too new, too recent, too *now*.

Reader, I want you to sit with my reasoning for a moment. Savor it. I genuinely believed that the important parts of a person are the recent parts. I, a priestess of gods older than rivers, believed that memory works like a pantry — newest goods on top, and if you lose the bottom shelf, well, you weren't eating those anyway.

Memory does not work like a pantry. Memory works like a foundation. You don't notice the bottom stones until the house leans.

"I accept," I said.

* * *

What happened next took less time than a heartbeat and cost more than I have finished counting.

The well didn't glow or thunder. It simply *reached*, the way cold reaches through a boot sole, and a year lifted out of me. I felt it go — felt the specific shape of its going, a Beatrix-shaped hollow where a Beatrix-shaped something had been. For half a second I stood there holding the absence, and I thought, absurdly: *that's fine. That's fine. I'll get it back. Debts can be reversed. That's practically scripture.*

Then the god gave me what I bought.

I saw the name.

I *saw* it. It unfolded in my mind like a banner, like dawn over the salt flats, and I understood in that instant why names are currency in the courts below and contraband in the valley above. It wasn't letters. It was a *key cut to fit one lock*, and the lock was a soul, and I held it, I held it, and I opened my mouth to carry it back to Beatrix—

—and the name slid off my tongue like water off oiled leather.

I tried again. Slower. Shaping each sound with a care I haven't given speech since seminary. The name dissolved in my mouth like sugar in hot tea. Not forgotten — *unspeakable*. Do you see? The god hadn't merely shown me the name and charged me a year of memory for the privilege. The price had set itself, it said. Equal weight. And what is the weight of a true name, spoken freely?

A true name is worth exactly one memory: the memory of hearing it. Of knowing it. Of being able to *say* it.

The god hadn't cheated me. That's the worst part. It had priced the knowledge perfectly. To know his true name and be able to use it, I would have had to pay with the capacity to know and use it. The information and the ability to carry the information are the same object, and you cannot hand a person a key made of their own melted-down hands.

The well chuckled — forty years of voices chuckling in ragged unison — and said, gently, in the drowned girl's voice:

*Now you understand why nobody rescues anybody down here, little priestess. Every key is priced at the hand that turns it. Go on. He's waiting for you. Tell him whatever keeps him walking.*

* * *

I walked back to the inn where Beatrix was mending his boots — he mends everything, that man, patches and darns and reinforces, as if he's preparing himself for a long journey he refuses to think about — and he looked up and said, "Did it help?"

"It gave me what I needed," I said.

Which was true. It gave me exactly what I needed. It simply neglected to mention that what I needed and what I could *use* had parted ways at the well's rim.

Here is what I have, and I have inventoried it nightly like a miser: I have the *shape* of the name. I know it has two movements and a falling end, like a bird landing. I know it tastes — and names have tastes, I learned this in the choir, though I learned it fresh at the well — I know it tastes like iron and honey, which is to say like him, like poison swallowed sweetly for nine years. I know that if I ever hear it spoken aloud, by the Steward, by the King of Ash, by anyone who still holds it, I will recognize it instantly. Recognition survived the payment. Only recall was repossessed.

Do you see the trap now? Do you see the beautiful, obscene architecture of it? The one thing needed to void the contract from outside — the true name, known by an ally, spoken freely — sits locked inside my head behind a door I cannot open, waiting for someone else to say it aloud so I can catch it like a falling cup.

Someone else. Which means either the Steward, who wants us dragged back to Ironhollow in chains, or the King of Ash, who wants Beatrix's body filled with a returning god at the next eclipse. Either way, to get the name spoken, I must bring Beatrix face-to-face with the very powers we fled. My sacrifice hasn't saved him from the trap. It has quietly rebuilt the trap around both of us and called it a rescue.

And the alternative — telling him the truth — what would that buy him? *I know your name, Beatrix. I paid a year of myself for it. I simply cannot give it to you, or say it, or write it, or hum it, or carve it, or indicate it by any means whatsoever, because the god priced it at the exact weight of my knowing.* He would look at me with those careful eyes that catalog everything, and he would do the arithmetic faster than I did, and he would understand that his ally spent a piece of herself to purchase a key that opens nothing, and he would — this is the part I cannot bear — he would *blame himself*. He would find some way to make it his fault, the man who apologizes to furniture he bumps into.

So I lie. Every morning. *Fine,* I say. *Slept well.*

* * *

Tonight we camp in the shadow of the mountain itself, close enough now that the air tastes of cinders and old worship. Beatrix is asleep sitting up, the way he does, back against a stone, hands loose, face finally unguarded — and I watch him, because watching is free and everything else costs.

I love him. There. Said it to the dark, where it can't hurt anyone. Not the grand burning love of ballads; the quieter kind, the kind that accumulates like silt, grain by grain, until one day you realize the riverbed has risen and you're standing somewhere new. I loved him before the well. I think — I *think* — the year the god took was not the year I met him, because we hadn't met yet, and surely even the cruelest market wouldn't charge for goods not yet in stock. Surely what it took was something else. Some other year. Some other self.

But I check, every night. I run my fingers along the shelves of my memory like a woman feeling for the dent where a stolen cup used to sit, and there is a gap, a smooth-walled absence, and around its edges the remaining years lean inward slightly, the way books lean when you pull one from the middle. And I cannot see what book it was. That's the horror of it. The theft included the theft of noticing the theft.

Tomorrow we go down. Down is the only door; the ferryman waits; the whistle-bone is spent. Somewhere beneath us, in a mirrored court full of borrowed faces, a king of ash holds a ledger with Beatrix's soul on one line and his true name on another, and at the eclipse the debt comes due.

And I am walking toward the one creature in creation who can say that name aloud — the King of Ash, who wrote it in his book in his own hand — carrying inside me the only key that fits, praying to gods who abandoned me that I'll be fast enough to catch it when it falls.

Pray for me, whoever reads this. Pray loudly. The old gods are answering again, and I have learned the hard way that they always, always quote their prices afterward.
