## 17. The Eclipse Rises

There is a moment, just before a thing you have dreaded your entire life finally happens, when the dread itself becomes almost comfortable. Familiar. A coat you've worn so long you've forgotten you're wearing it.

I was standing in the heart of the Court Beneath the Mountain with my soul in my hand — metaphorically; it had been in the King of Ash's ledger for twenty-six years — and I found myself thinking about breakfast. About the ninth dish. About the taste of honeyed quail that should have killed me and tasted instead like an answer to someone else's prayer.

The eclipse began above us, though we were a half-mile of stone below the world, and somehow everyone in the court could see it anyway. That's the thing about places built by gods and their accountants: physics is less a law than a suggestion they occasionally honor. The mirrored ceiling of the great hall darkened from silver to bruise, and every noble in their borrowed face turned upward at once, like flowers that had given up on the sun.

"Here it comes," said Vespera beside me. Her wrists still bore the marks of her chains. Her voice was steady, but she'd tucked herself half behind my shoulder, which for a priestess who once commanded storms was either tactical positioning or something warmer. I chose not to examine which.

"You're sure about this?" I asked. "The plan. The part where we don't actually have a plan."

"I'm sure the god is coming whether we like it or not."

"That's not a plan. That's weather."

"Beatrix." She used my name the way you'd set down a heavy load, carefully, knowing it might break. "You survived nine years of poison designed to kill kings. Whatever arrives tonight, it's arriving into *you*. Let it find out what that costs."

I wanted to tell her about chapter and verse — about the ledger, the contract, the price of a name freely given — but there wasn't time, and honestly, there never is. Time in the court beneath the mountain moves like a debtor fleeing his creditors: quickly, and only downhill.

* * *

The darkness came down through the stone the way water comes through a ceiling — first a stain, then a drip, then a flood. The nobles began to sing. It was not a hymn. It was more like the sound a lock makes when it recognizes its key: a long, grating, satisfied note that vibrated in my teeth and in the fillings of my fears.

Vespera gripped my arm. "It's early," she breathed. "Eclipses don't start until—"

"The court lies," I said. "Everything here lies. Why would the sky keep honest hours?"

The first of the returning god poured into me through the top of my skull, and I will be honest with you, because at this point in my story honesty is all I have left that isn't mortgaged: it hurt.

Not the way poison hurts. Poison hurts like a betrayal — like your own body turning traitor, your stomach becoming an assassin's accomplice. This was the opposite. This was something vast trying to move into a house far too small for it, and discovering, room by room, corridor by corridor, that every door was locked.

"What—" The voice came out of my own mouth, which is a deeply unpleasant experience I recommend to no one. It sounded like me, but layered, like one man speaking through the echo of a canyon. "*What is this flesh?*"

"Mine," I said. Or tried to. My jaw wasn't entirely under my own management anymore. What came out was more of a grimace with opinions.

The god pushed harder. I felt it then — the full weight of it, a presence old enough to remember when the mountain was a wound in the world instead of a wall around a valley, pressing against the inside of my skin like a prisoner testing bars. And the bars held.

They held because of the aconite they fed me at seven. Because of the hemlock broth at nine, and the arsenic dusting my morning bread at twelve, and the serpent venom in the wedding wine I tasted at nineteen for a duke whose bride he shouldn't have been marrying anyway. Nine years, dish by dish, drop by drop. Every immunity a brick. Every brick laid by hands I'd trusted — the Steward's kind, patient hands, the hands of a man who'd signed away my soul before I could walk and then spent two decades building me into a fortress fit to garrison.

Every suffering, retroactively meaningful. There's a joke in there somewhere, but it's the kind of joke you laugh at alone, at three in the morning, with your teeth gritted, while a god tries to wear you like a glove.

"*Open,*" the god said, and the mirrors along the walls all cracked at once, hairline fractures spiderwebbing outward from a hundred reflections of my face. In each fractured reflection, something else looked back — something crowned in dying starlight, something with eyes like the last coals of a fire that had burned down a civilization.

"No," I managed.

It redoubled its efforts, and I went to my knees on the obsidian floor, and the whole court leaned in with their borrowed faces, hungry as tax collectors.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about possession: it is not violence, exactly. Not at first. It's negotiation. It's a landlord's voice, reasonable and cold, explaining that the lease has lapsed, that the property has stood empty long enough, that surely a sensible creature like yourself can see the arrangement benefits everyone.

*You were made for this,* the god said inside my head, in the voice of every adult who had ever explained my life to me without consulting me. *Bred for it. Purchased for it. Do you imagine all that suffering meant nothing? All those years of poison, wasted on mere survival? I am the purpose, little vessel. I am what it was all for.*

And oh, that was the knife of it. That was the blade turned just so. Because I had spent the last weeks learning that my life was a containment protocol, a ledger entry, an investment — and some tired, starving corner of me had wanted, desperately, for it to mean *something*. Anything. Even this.

*I know what you're doing,* I thought back at it, through the white noise of my own straining bones. *You're offering meaning the way the crown offered safety. Take it and stop struggling.*

*Is it not a fair trade?*

*Nothing in this court is fair. Everything in this court is priced.* I got one knee under myself. Then, slowly, insultingly, the other. *And I have read the ledger now. I know how you people do arithmetic.*

The god laughed, and the laughter shook dust from the ceiling, and three of the nearer nobles fell over, which improved the room considerably.

*You resist because you are afraid,* it said. *Fear is a door, vessel. It opens from the inside.*

"Fear," I said out loud, and my voice was mine again, ragged but mine, "is the least of what's locked in here. Let me show you the rest of the house."

* * *

Because here is the thing I understood in that moment, kneeling on the black glass while an exiled god hammered at the doors of my soul: I was not empty. I had spent nine years being told I was a vessel, a container, a shell waiting to be filled — and I had believed it, the way you believe the water is the cup. But a vessel that has held nine years of poison isn't empty. It's *seasoned*. It's steeped through, saturated, marinated in death down to the marrow.

They had made me immune to everything.

*Everything.*

The word arrived in my mind with the quiet click of a lock I'd been carrying all my life finally being shown its key. Aconite. Hemlock. Arsenic. Belladonna. The gentle chronic poisons and the merciful quick ones, the venoms of six serpents and the distilled spite of plants that grow where nothing else dares. Nine years of the Steward's careful curriculum, written in doses, taught in meals.

And possession — I knew this now, knew it in my marrow the way I knew the taste of cyanide (almonds; disappointing) — possession is a poison. It is the oldest poison. It is a toxin introduced into a living body with intent to override it, to supplant it, to kill the tenant and move in.

And I was immune to everything.

The god felt me realize it. I felt the god feel it — a hesitation, a vast and ancient pause, the sensation of a conqueror reaching the city gates and finding them not merely barred but *built of the one material his armies cannot breach.*

"*What did they feed you?*" it whispered, and for the first time, the whisper sounded afraid.

"Breakfast," I said, and stood up. "Nine years of breakfasts."

I rose off the obsidian floor like a man climbing out of a grave he'd decided he wasn't done using. The pressure against the inside of my skin didn't lessen — if anything it grew furious, frantic, a storm throwing itself against sea-walls — but the storm was outside the walls now, hammering, and the walls were me. All of me. Every meal I'd ever choked down and smiled about afterward. Every night I'd curled around cramps in my iron cot and told myself it was worth it, it was service, it was the price of belonging. Every dish. Every dose. Every single swallowed horror, stacked and mortared into armor I hadn't known I was wearing.

The Steward had built me to hold a god. He had simply never asked the god's opinion of the accommodation.

"It doesn't fit," I told the darkness behind my eyes, conversationally. "You don't fit. You are, if you'll forgive the technical term, rejected."

* * *

What happened next happened fast, and most of it happened inside me, which makes it difficult to narrate with any dignity.

The god stopped pushing and started pouring. If you cannot pick a lock, drown it — that seemed to be the strategy, a flood of presence, of will, of ancient grinding hunger, enough to fill me past any resistance, to dilute whatever stubborn remnant of Beatrix Dravenmoor was holding the deed.

And I let it pour.

I stood in the middle of the cracked-mirror hall with my arms slightly out from my body like a man accepting rain, and I drank it in. Because here is the second thing nobody tells you: immunity is not emptiness. Immunity is capacity. Nine years of training had taught my body to take in poison and render it harmless — to receive the killing thing and make it nothing. That was the whole curriculum. That was the entire art of the taster.

So I tasted it.

I took the returning god into myself the way I had taken ten thousand dishes, and my body — patient, practiced, magnificent in its ruined way — did what it had been built to do.

It rendered him harmless.

Oh, he fought it. You would have too. Imagine spending an age in exile beneath a mountain, gathering yourself shard by shard, riding an eclipse down through the stone into a body purchased and prepared across a generation — and finding that the body treats divinity the same way it treats hemlock. Imagine feeling yourself diluted. Filtered. Broken into components and sorted into harmless sediment.

The scream didn't come out of my mouth this time. It came out of the mirrors.

Every reflection of me in that shattered hall opened its borrowed mouth at once, and the sound that came out was the sound of a god being digested — which, for the record, sounds exactly like what it is: enormous, and undignified, and ending.

The nobles fled their seats. Some tore off their borrowed faces and ran with the raw faces underneath, which were worse. Vespera stood her ground at the edge of it all, arms raised, murmuring in the true tongue of the waking gods — not commanding, not fighting, just *witnessing*, which I suspect is the only sacrament her order ever really practiced.

"*What are you?*" the god asked me, small now, a voice at the bottom of a well.

"A taster," I said. "Occupational hazard."

And then the last of him settled, and the last of him was quiet, and the eclipse-light bleeding down through the broken ceiling guttered like a candle finding its wick again.

I stood alone in my own body — wholly, entirely, for perhaps the first time since I'd crossed the salt line. It fit better than I remembered. Or perhaps I finally fit it.

* * *

"Beatrix." Vespera caught my elbow as I swayed. "Your eyes. They're—"

"Brown? Devastating? Please say devastating."

"They're *normal*," she said, with the particular relief of a woman who has spent her life around miracles and developed strong opinions about them. "Whatever came down with the eclipse — it's gone. Where did it go?"

"Nowhere," I said. "That's rather the point. It went nowhere. It's nowhere now." I flexed my hands. They were mine. They trembled, but they were mine. "Nine years, Vespera. Nine years of poison, and I thought it was all cost. Every meal, every cramp, every year of my life spent being slowly murdered on schedule. And it turns out it was tuition."

She looked at me for a long moment. "You sound almost grateful."

"Don't worry. It'll pass. Gratitude always does." I looked up at the mending ceiling, where the eclipse was rolling off the face of the world above, and felt the exhaustion arrive like a debt collector. "But right now I'd like to enjoy it for one more minute, because somewhere in this mountain is a king made of ash who just watched his investment fail, and kings like that tend to—"

The temperature dropped.

Not gradually. Not the way weather does things. One heartbeat the hall was merely cold; the next, our breath hung in the air like ghosts refusing to move on, and every remaining mirror in the court frosted over from the edges inward, and down the long black corridor behind the throne — the deep road, the road further down, the direction even the dead call *down* — came a sound like a library burning.

Footsteps. Patient ones. Unhurried. The footsteps of a creditor who knows the debt is good and always has been.

Vespera's hand found mine. "He watched," she whispered. "The whole time. From the deep court. He watched his god fail inside you, and now—"

"Now he wants to inspect the asset personally," I finished.

The frost reached our feet. Somewhere ahead, in the dark, something old cleared its throat with the sound of a mountain settling.

I had survived a god.

I was about to find out whether I could survive his bookkeeper.
