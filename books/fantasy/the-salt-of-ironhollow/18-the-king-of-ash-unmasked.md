## 18. The King of Ash Unmasked

The god left me the way a fever breaks.

One moment I was full of Him — of the pressure, the vastness, the taste of every prayer ever whispered into stone — and the next I was only myself again, kneeling on the mirrored floor of the court beneath the mountain, shaking, empty, and *alive* in a way I had never once been in twenty-six years.

The eclipse was still going. I could feel it through a thousand feet of rock, a cold ring of light burning somewhere above us, and the King of Ash was standing at the far end of his hall with his hand still outstretched, fingers curled around nothing.

Around the god that wasn't there anymore. Because there was no room for Him in me. There never had been.

"Nine years," the King said.

His voice had changed. That was the first thing I noticed, in the strange ringing quiet after the god's departure — the way his voice had changed. It had been smooth before, ledger-smooth, the voice of a man who reads contracts aloud for pleasure. Now there was something under it, something with grit in it, like ash sifting down through a burned house long after the fire should be over.

"Nine years I fed you," he said. "Nine years your kingdom dosed you, brick by brick, and nine years *I* held the mortgage. Do you know what you've done?"

I spat. My mouth tasted of cinders and old incense and, faintly, wonderfully, of nothing at all. "I think I just failed to be possessed."

"You think." He began to walk toward me, and with every step the mirrors along the walls dimmed, as if they were flinching. "You were bred, taster. Selected. Dosed. Delivered. You are the single most expensive object in this court, and you have the temerity to stand there and call yourself a *failure* at being used—"

"I don't call myself anything," I said, and found that it was true, and found that it was the freest sentence I'd ever spoken. "That was rather the point."

He stopped three paces from me. Up close, without the ledger between us, without the ceremony, I could see what he actually was: not a king at all. A collector. A magpie with a crown. His robes moved wrong, the way smoke moves when it's trying to remember being cloth, and beneath the borrowed face he wore — a handsome face, a courtly face, a face I realized with a lurch I had seen hanging in the palace gallery above — there was nothing. No features waiting. Just an absence shaped like patience.

And all around us, filling the walls from floor to vaulted dark, hung the mirrors.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. Tall ones and small ones, round ones like moons, cracked ones like ice on a pond. And in every one of them, a face. Not reflections — *faces*. Living, or something near it. Eyes that tracked me. Mouths that sometimes moved, whispering, though no sound came through the silver. Faces of nobles who had come down here generations ago and never come back up. Faces of servants. Faces, I understood suddenly and completely, of everyone this court had ever eaten.

The King followed my gaze and smiled with his borrowed mouth.

"You see them," he said. "Of course you do. You're nearly one of us now — unclaimed, unnamed, a loose soul rattling around my mountain. Tell me, Beatrix Dravenmoor—" and he said my name the way a man cracks a whip, savoring it, testing whether it still obeyed him "—do you know why a collector keeps mirrors? Why not jars? Why not bottles, like any sensible trader of souls?"

"Mirrors show you what you look like," I said. "Even when you don't have a self to look like. They must be very comforting."

The smile died.

It happened all at once, the way a mask doesn't slip so much as shatter. The handsome gallery-face went gray, then translucent, then simply *gone*, and underneath there was the absence again, but larger now, hungry, and the temperature of the whole hall dropped as if we had descended another mile into the mountain. The ash-voice came from everywhere at once, from the walls, from the floor, from inside my own skull where a name used to live.

"*You will kneel,*" said the King of Ash, "*and you will sign.*"

And he reached for me.

I want to be honest about what happened next, because honesty is the only thing I own outright now. I did not stand heroically. I did not have a plan. What I had was nine years of swallowing poison, and the animal reflex of a man who has spent nearly a decade learning that everything offered to his body is a lie.

I threw myself sideways.

His hand closed on the air where my shoulder had been, and where his fingers touched the floor, the mirror-tiles frosted white and cracked like thin ice under a boot. The cold of him poured off in waves. I scrambled back among the standing mirrors, knocking one with my elbow, feeling it hum against my spine like a struck bell — and heard, for the first time, a sound from inside the silver.

A voice. Thin, cracked, human.

"*Run,*" it whispered. Or maybe it said something else. Maybe it said *help*. When you've spent nine years tasting other people's food for them, you develop an ear for the thing someone means underneath the words, and what I heard underneath that whisper, from whatever was pressed against the inside of that glass, was the same thing I'd heard in the cook's last breath, in Vespera's voice through a wall, in every prayer the old gods had ever half-answered:

*I am still here. Remember me.*

The King turned, unhurried now, terrible now, and the frost spread out from his feet in a slow black bloom across the mirrored floor. "You cannot leave," he said. "There is no door up. The ferryman is mine. The salt line is a week's climb and my hunters run faster than grief. And even if you reached it, taster — even if you crawled to the light — there is nothing above ground that knows your name anymore. You are already halfway forgotten. Kneel, sign the ledger, take back the debt, and I will let you keep a *face*. One of the good ones. I'll even let you choose—"

"No," I said.

And here is the thing about saying no after twenty-six years of yes — after nine years of opening your mouth on command, swallowing on command, living on command because your life belonged to a contract signed before you could walk — here is the thing:

It echoes.

"No," I said again, louder, and the word went bouncing down the mirrored hall, from glass to glass, and in each mirror it landed, something stirred. Faces pressed closer to their silver. Hands — I saw hands now, ghost-pale, palm-flat against the inner curve of the glass — appeared where there had been none. All around the hall, the stolen dead of the court beneath the mountain were turning to look at their keeper, and their keeper had just learned, visibly, publicly, that his newest acquisition would not hold still.

"They told me you collected names," I said. My heart was hammering, but it was hammering *mine*, such as it was — the one piece of property I had left, beating in its little bone-and-blood vault, refusing foreclosure. "They didn't tell me you kept them in *glass*."

"The glass is mercy," the King snarled, and lunged.

I dodged — I'm getting better at dodging, which is a miserable skill to be proud of — and my hand shot out for balance and slammed flat against the nearest mirror.

It was warm.

Warm, and trembling, like the flank of a frightened horse, and beneath my palm the face inside leaned into my touch the way a prisoner leans against a cell door when someone walks the corridor. A woman. Gray-streaked hair. Court dress two centuries out of fashion. Her lips moved, soundless, urgent, and I read the shape of it more than heard it:

"*Break it.*"

The King was almost on me. Behind him the whole hall had gone black-frosted, and the ledger lay open on its pedestal, pages riffling in a wind that had no source, and somewhere above us the eclipse was sliding toward its end, and my window — my one impossible window of being immune to everything, including him — was beginning to close.

So I broke it.

I broke it with my elbow, which is not a poetic implement, but I have since decided that most true things are done with elbows and knees and desperation rather than swords. The mirror did not shatter like glass. It shattered like *ice on a river in spring* — a great cracking boom that ran outward through the floor and up the walls, mirror to mirror to mirror, a hundred cracks answering a hundred cracks — and the face did not fall out of it.

She stepped out.

She came apart into light as she came, unraveling into ribbons of pale fire, and the last expression on her freed face was not gratitude. It was fury. Two hundred years of fury, aimed past me like an arrow loosed at a target she had stared at through silver since before my grandmother was born.

At the King.

"Oh," I said. "Oh, *that's* how it works."

The second mirror I broke with the woman's own momentum behind me — I simply turned and drove my shoulder into the next pane, and a young man in a servant's livery burst free in a spray of sparks, screaming, screaming, screaming with joy. The third I kicked. The fourth fell on its own, sympathetic fracture running through the frame, and by the fifth I wasn't breaking them anymore.

They were breaking themselves.

All down the hall, faces surged against the insides of their prisons, and the prisons gave way — some bursting outward in gouts of pale flame, some splitting neatly down the middle like eggs, some simply dissolving into motes that rose instead of fell, because down here, in this inverted court, even gravity had been part of the lie. And every freed soul, every one, turned — before it rose, before it scattered, before it went wherever the erased go when someone finally says their existence out loud — and *looked* at the King of Ash.

He had stopped chasing me.

I don't think I can convey what that moment felt like, standing in the middle of that collapsing hall amid a storm of liberated light, watching a thing that had worn a king's manner and a gallery's worth of stolen faces try to make itself small. He had backed against his throne — against the one mirror in the hall that was still intact, a vast black pane behind the seat of ash, and I understood in one cold flash what that mirror held. Not a face. A *collection*. Every name in the ledger, pressed flat behind the glass, layered like leaves pressed in a book, and he had backed himself against it the way a miser backs against his chest.

"Stop," he said. His voice had lost the grit and found something worse underneath it: whine. "Stop them. Stop — you are *contracted* — you are MINE, the ink is not dry, the debt stands, the eclipse—"

"The eclipse ends," I said, "in minutes. And I'm still immune."

"You'll die down here."

"Probably," I agreed. "But I'll die *unowned*, and you will die *surrounded*, and I know which of those I'd rather be."

The freed souls closed in.

They didn't touch him. That was the terrible elegance of it — they didn't need to. They simply stood around him in a tightening ring of pale fire, hundreds of them, thousands, the whole hoard of his long collecting, and they *looked* at him. That was all. Looking. But when you are a creature made entirely of what others see in you, being seen truly is the only wound there is. Where their gaze landed, his borrowed grandeur smoked and sloughed away — the robes, the crown-shape, the suggestion of shoulders — until what stood in the ring was small and gray and shapeless as a heap of sifted ash, clutching at itself, whispering numbers. Ledger entries. Prices paid. Debts owed. As if reciting the arithmetic of a lifetime would save him from the sum.

The great black mirror behind the throne began to crack.

Not from anything I did. From the inside. The pressed-flat names — the whole crushed collection of him — had felt their siblings go free, and now they pushed, all together, against the silver from within, and the cracks spread across it like frost climbing a window, and the King of Ash turned and screamed at it, actually screamed, "*No — those are MINE — those are SIGNED —*" and clawed at the glass with hands that were barely hands anymore—

I should have watched it end. Some part of me wanted to; some part that kept a tally of its own, nine years deep.

But the hall was coming down with it, and somewhere under the thunder of splitting mirrors I could hear another sound, fainter, muffled, coming through the wall of the antechamber where I had last seen her.

Vespera.

* * *

I found her cell door buckled inward, warped by the same convulsion that was shaking the mountain's roots, and beyond it a corridor of falling dust and blue-white motes drifting upward like snow in reverse. She was on her feet, one hand pressed to the wall, her chains gone slack — the links crumbling, I saw, into ordinary rust, because whatever power had forged them had just been broken into ribbons two rooms away.

"You're alive," she said. Her voice was raw. Her eyes went over me the way she checked bread for smuggled prayers — thorough, disbelieving. "The god — I felt it pass through you like a flood through a culvert — Beatrix, what did you *do*?"

"Broke some furniture," I said. My legs chose that moment to inform me they were finished, and I caught myself against the wall beside her. "Long story. Several centuries long, actually. We need to go *up*."

"The ferryman's toll is spent. The door down was one-way."

"The door down," I said, "was his. Everything down here was his. Which means—" I looked up, at the ceiling shedding its stone skin, at the drifting lights of the freed dead streaming past us toward some vent, some crack, some chimney of escape older than the court that had imprisoned them — "everything is coming apart in the direction of *out*."

She understood before I finished. Of course she did; she'd spent her life listening for gods in the walls. She grabbed my wrist, and we ran — two small figures swept up in a migration of the erased, carried through splitting galleries and drowning halls on a tide of pale fire, past ledgers burning themselves blank, past thrones dissolving into ordinary ash that finally, at the end, smelled of nothing but hearth-smoke and rain.

Behind us, deeper in, the last great mirror gave way with a sound like a name spoken for the first time in centuries.

Above us — impossibly, blessedly far above — I saw a coin of gray daylight widen in a crack in the mountain's crown.

And rising toward it on a river of the freed, I thought about names, and debts, and the price still stamped unpaid on my soul — because a contract ended by rebellion is not a contract ended, and the King of Ash had taught me one lesson thoroughly before he burned: *what is given freely can never be repossessed.*

There was one thing left in me that had never been taken.

I was going to have to decide whether to give it away.
