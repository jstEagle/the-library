## 19. A Name Freely Given

The ledger lay open between us like a wound that had learned to read.

I want to tell you I hesitated. I want to tell you some part of me flinched at the edge of that decision, the way a man flinches at a high window even when he means to jump. But the truth is stranger and quieter than that: I felt *relief*. Nine years of swallowing death on schedule, nine years of being a lock someone else kept the key to, and here, finally, was a door I could open myself.

"You understand what you're doing," the King of Ash said. It wasn't quite a question. Nothing he said was ever quite a question; everything was an invoice with the numbers left blank for you to fill in.

"Better than you do," I said. "You think a name is a handle. Something to grip. But I've been nobody's for a long time now — I just didn't know it yet." I turned my hand over. My palm still ached where the mirrors had cut me, though the cuts were already closing. Immune to everything, even my own blood's habit of leaving. "A name freely given. Those are your terms."

"My terms," he agreed, "are always fair." The King sat upon his throne of compacted ash — not cinders, understand, but ash as in *what remains*, the fine grey residue of every promise ever burned in his court — and he did not smile, because his borrowed face had been taken from a man who'd forgotten how. Around him the nobles of the court beneath stood very still, wearing their stolen visages, watching us the way debtors watch a fire spread toward their own street.

Behind me, Vespera knelt in her cage of forgetting. She had looked at me twice since I broke her cell. Twice she had studied my face with the particular concentration of someone trying to place a stranger at a funeral — certain he matters, certain she should know why, and hollow where the knowing should be. She had paid for something with my name without knowing what she bought. Or rather: she had paid with the one coin that could have saved me, and neither of us could spend it now.

That was the tragedy of it. That was also, I decided, the way through.

"Before I sign," I said, "I want the contract voided. Not amended. Not renegotiated. Voided — the vessel clause, the eclipse clause, all of it. His soul returned" — I gestured vaguely at myself, since apparently we were speaking of me in the third person now, which felt appropriate; I'd never owned the fellow — "and the girl freed from your ledgers entirely."

"The priestess owes nothing here," the King said. "She paid her price."

"She paid *your* price, which is different from owing nothing." I took a step closer to the throne. Somewhere behind me, the shattered mirrors were still bleeding light — the freed faces drifting in the air like moths that had finally remembered they were people. A few had gathered near Vespera's cage, hovering, as if they remembered being forgotten too. "Here's how this goes, your Ashness. You can take my name as agreed and keep the contract, and then explain to your court why the god you invited into my body found the door locked and the house immune. Or you can take my name, void everything, and let me walk out of here carrying the only witness who knows what you look like when you lose."

Silence, of the kind that has weight. In the court beneath the mountain, silence isn't absence of sound; it's the sound itself — a low hum of held breaths and unspent bargains.

"You would give up your name," the King said slowly, "the thing men kill for, the thing prayers are addressed by, the whole of what you are —"

"I've seen what happens to names here." I nodded at the mirrors, at the drifting faces, at Vespera kneeling in the wreckage of her own memory. "They're currency. And I have spent nine years being spent. So yes. Take the last coin. But take it *all* — or take nothing, and I will stand in your court until the next eclipse comes and find out what else I'm immune to."

Something moved behind the King's borrowed eyes. I want to believe it was respect. It may only have been accounting.

"Speak it, then," he said, and opened the ledger.

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about your own name: you don't say it so much as it says you.

I had rehearsed nothing. There was no ceremony, no grand declaration — I simply opened my mouth to speak, and Beatrix Dravenmoor came out of me the way water comes out of a cracked jar, all at once, unstoppable, taking pieces of the jar with it.

And oh, what came with it. Every time my mother — whoever she had been — must have whispered it over a crib she wouldn't live to see emptied. The first time the orphan-mistress barked it across a yard of frozen mud, and I turned, small and hopeful, because a child thinks being named means being wanted. The day the Steward of Tasters wrote it into a ledger of his own, in ink mixed with salt from the valley line, and beneath it wrote a number that was not a price but a *yield*. Nine years of it called down corridors before poison. Nine years of it spoken over me while I convulsed and sweated and learned, cell by cell, to stop dying.

It all went into the book. All of it. I felt each memory of my own name lift out of me like splinters leaving flesh, and the strange thing — the thing I will carry into whatever I am now — is that it didn't hurt. It *itched*. It itched like a healing, like skin knitting over a wound so old I'd stopped noticing it was there.

The ledger drank. The pen — no, there was no pen; the words simply sank into the page the way rain sinks into ground that has waited years for it — and the King of Ash closed the book, and the sound of that closing was the quietest sound I have ever heard, and the loudest.

"It is done," he said.

And then, because I am who I am — or was, or am again, nameless and therefore newly made — I said: "Voided? All of it? Say the whole sentence, your Ashness. I've been poisoned by professionals. I know how partial doses work."

For the first time in the long history of that grey court, the King of Ash laughed. It was a terrible sound, like a library burning politely. "Voided," he said. "The vessel is empty. The contract is ash. The girl walks free. Are you satisfied, *no one*?"

"Not yet," I said, and turned to Vespera.

* * *

She was standing by then — the cage had come apart when the contract did, its bars unraveling into threads of grey silk that drifted up and vanished — and she was looking at me with that terrible, gentle puzzlement. The faces of the forgotten orbited her like patient birds.

"You did something," she said. Her voice was hoarse. She'd been in that cage three days, and in the court beneath the mountain, days drink you faster than anywhere else. "I don't — I know you saved me. I know it the way you know a scar is yours. But I can't find *how*, and I can't find —" She pressed her hand flat against her sternum, hunting. "There's a hole in me shaped like a word. What was the word?"

"Doesn't matter," I said.

"It matters. It mattered enough for me to pay —" She stopped. I watched her arrive at it, watched the arithmetic complete behind her eyes, and saw the horror bloom. "*Oh.* Oh, no. No, whatever it was, I'll pay it back, I'll bargain, there must be —"

"There isn't," I said, as gently as I knew how, which has never been very. "And even if there were, I'm out of things to trade. Spent it all just now. Bought our freedom with the last coin in my purse." I shrugged. "Honestly? Best deal I ever made. Terrible value. Would trade again."

She laughed — wet, broken, furious at herself for laughing — and then she was crying, and then I was holding her, which surprised us both. I am not a man built for comfort. I was built for tasting and surviving, two skills that translate poorly to tenderness. But she shook against my chest, and I found that my arms had opinions of their own, and I let them.

"They'll forget you," she whispered into my collar. "Everyone. If you're nameless, the valley won't hold the shape of you. The palace records will slide off you like water off wax. Beatrix, I can't — I won't remember —"

"Then remember this instead," I said. "You hid in a wall for six years eating prayers out of bread, and you never once stopped believing the gods would wake. You were right. That's worth more than my name ever was."

"And what do I call you?"

I thought about it. Truly thought about it, there in the breaking heart of the court beneath the mountain, while the King of Ash watched us with his face that couldn't smile and his book that was finally, mercifully, shut.

"Nothing," I said. "Call me nothing. Call me 'hey, you.' Call me the man who owed you a rescue. Names are handles, and I'm done being carried."

Above us — far above, through miles of stone and root and drowned dark — something groaned. The court had begun to notice its king had lost.

* * *

Because that's the other thing about a ledger full of souls: it holds the room up.

The King of Ash understood it a half-second after I did. I saw him reach for his book — clutch it, really, the way a drowning man clutches driftwood — but the contract was voided, and void contracts don't bear weight. The first crack ran through the ceiling of the court like lightning deciding to travel slowly. Then another. Then the ash of the throne began to sift downward, grain by grain, and every stolen face in the air turned at once toward the exit and toward each other, as if asking: *do we run, or do we finish what we started?*

They finished what they started. God love the erased — they have nothing left to lose and everything to prove. The freed faces fell upon their former keeper like weather. He shrieked in a voice that was many voices, all of them once his victims, and I did not stay to watch the settling of accounts. I had a priestess under one arm and a very strong opinion about stairs.

"Which way?" Vespera shouted, as the mirrored hall began to come down around us in sheets of silver glass.

"The ferryman's door! Down is the only door — except now up is also a door, the architecture down here is a *metaphor* —" I hauled her left, along the corridor the drowned dead had shown me, past niches where the forgotten dead sat patiently waiting to be entirely forgotten, and several of them raised their heads as we passed. One of them — a woman with riverweed in her hair — smiled at me.

"Nameless one," she said, in a voice like water over stones. "You broke the book."

"Somebody had to return it overdue."

"Take the left fork. The old way. The new ways belong to the court, and the court is ending." Behind her, more of the seated dead were rising, slow as dawn, and following. Not chasing — *joining*. An exodus. When the ledger dies, everything it buried gets up and starts walking home.

We ran. The court came apart behind us in slow, thunderous sections, and the dark ahead of us filled with the shuffling footsteps of the returned. Vespera kept pace beside me, her hand locked in mine, and somewhere in the running she stopped being afraid and started being fierce, shouting directions to the dead in a liturgical voice that hadn't been used since the gods went quiet — *this way, children, mind the floodline, keep together* — a shepherdess of the drowned, and if my chest did something complicated about that, well, I was nameless and therefore allowed mysteries.

The ferryman was waiting at his black water, pole in hand, as if he'd known.

"You paid already," he said. "The whistle-bone. The cook's last trust." His hood tilted toward the crowd shambling up behind us. "I only carry the drowned. These are not drowned."

"They're close enough," I said. "Flooded basements. Come on, ferryman. One last crossing, and I'll throw in a story for free."

"What story?"

"The one about the taster who poisoned himself back."

The ferryman considered. Then he stepped aside, and the black water split like a book opening, and stairs rose out of it — actual stairs, stone and slick with spray, climbing up and up into a darkness that was warming by degrees toward grey, toward less-than-dark, toward the rumor of sky.

"Go," he said. "And nameless one —" I paused on the first step. "When you climb out of my country and forget the way back, don't worry. No one finds this door twice. That's not a curse. It's a mercy."

* * *

We climbed for what felt like a season.

Vespera ahead of me, the dead behind us, a river of the returning flowing up through the mountain's throat. Twice the stair narrowed to nothing and twice it remembered itself. Once the whole passage shuddered so hard I thought the mountain meant to swallow its own history, and Vespera reached back without looking and gripped my wrist, and we stood in the shaking dark holding on to each other like the last two facts in a collapsing argument.

At some point she asked, quietly, without turning: "Will it hurt? Being forgotten?"

"Yes," I said. "But I've been hurt on schedule for nine years. Unscheduled pain is practically a holiday."

"That's the worst joke —"

"I know."

"— and I'm going to laugh at it anyway." She did, and it echoed up the stairwell ahead of us like a bird released early.

Near the top, the dead began to peel away — each one arriving, at last, at whatever threshold was theirs. The woman with riverweed in her hair pressed something into my hand as she passed: a smooth grey stone, river-worn, warm as a held hand. "For the garden you'll plant," she said. "Dead things make the best soil. We would know."

And then, at last — light. Not torchlight, not mirror-light. *Daylight*, thin and cold and absolutely real, spilling down the stairwell like an apology. I climbed into it, and hauled Vespera up after me, and we stood together on the mountainside above Ironhollow with the wind pulling at us and the valley spread below — the little grey town, the palace with its kitchens, the salt line a pale seam in the earth like an old scar finally ready to fade.

Behind us, deep in the rock, the last of the court finished falling. I felt it go the way you feel a tooth leave your jaw — relief and absence in the same socket.

Vespera looked at me. Morning light on her face. And I watched her watch me, and I saw it happen: the soft focus, the polite searching, the stranger's arithmetic running behind her eyes. *Who is this man. Why does my chest hurt when I look at him. Why do I feel I've lost something I never had.*

"Ready?" she asked. Kindly. Carefully. The way you speak to someone you're afraid of startling.

"No," I said, and took her hand anyway. "But I hear the valley makes excellent poison. Might as well see what it does to breakfast."

We started down the mountain, toward home, toward forgetting — and below us, faint on the wind, the bells of Ironhollow began to ring, though for whose return, I could not have said.
