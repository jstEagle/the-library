## 20. Homecoming, Unremembered

There is a particular kind of silence that follows a god leaving a room, and I had heard it only once before in my life — the day the priesthood stripped my vestments and told me the gods had never spoken to me at all. It is the silence of a held breath released. Of a debt forgiven so suddenly that your knees forget what they were holding you up for.

I heard it again as we climbed out of the dark beneath Ironhollow, Beatrix's arm over my shoulders and my feet finding purchase on stone that had stopped humming. The mountain above us was quiet in a way it had not been quiet in nine hundred years. Not sleeping-quiet. *Gone*-quiet.

"He's heavy," I said, to no one, because saying things aloud kept me from screaming, and I had done enough screaming underground to last several lifetimes. "The man who just unmade a king of ash is heavy."

He didn't answer. He hadn't answered much since the ledger took his name. I want to be precise about this, because precision was the last discipline they let me keep: he could speak. His voice worked. His mind worked. When I asked him questions he answered them, patiently, in that dry tone I had come to know on the road of small prayers. But there was a hollow space where his name used to sit, and when anyone addressed him directly — by *him*, I mean, by the shape of him standing there — something behind his eyes flickered like a candle in a draft, reaching for a wick that wasn't there anymore.

I knew his name. That's the joke. That's the cosmic jest the old gods apparently find hilarious. I knew it once. I paid it away in a village god's shrine without understanding what I was buying, and now I carried the shape of its absence around with me like a coin purse turned inside out.

*Vespera,* he'd said to me, in the collapsing dark, before we climbed. *Don't try to remember. It won't come back, and you'll only hurt yourself.*

And I had said, because I am apparently incapable of accepting grace when it's handed to me: *Try to stop me.*

* * *

We came up through the salt flats at dawn, which felt appropriate. All the best endings happen at dawn; the priests taught me that too, along with the liturgies and the lies.

The salt line itself was dying. I don't know how else to describe it. For centuries the boundary of Ironhollow had been a scar of white crystal running rim to rim of the valley, wide as a road, sharp enough to cut, humming faintly with the law that no god may enter and no soul may leave. We had crossed it fleeing in the other direction, and Beatrix had made a sound I still hear sometimes when I'm trying to fall asleep — half terror, half the first gasp after drowning.

Now the crystals were going gray. As we walked the line north toward the valley gate, whole stretches of it crumbled at our approach, slumping into ordinary salt, then into ordinary dust, then into nothing much at all. The wind came down off the mountain and moved through the valley without meeting anything that argued with it.

"It's dissolving," I said. "The law."

"Everything has a keeper," Beatrix said. "Even laws." He paused. "Especially laws."

"And when the keeper—"

"Goes into a ledger," he finished, "the law goes with him."

I looked at him sideways. This was another thing about him since the court: he spoke about his own erasure the way a farmer speaks about weather. Dry. Practical. With an undercurrent I couldn't read, because reading people was my trade once and every tool I owned had been blunted against the smooth blank place where his name had been.

The valley opened below us. Ironhollow in morning light: terraced fields going gold, smoke rising from chimneys, the river doing its patient silver work. The palace sat on its hill with its pale towers, looking exactly as it always had, which struck me suddenly as obscene. Everything above ground was unchanged. Everything below it had burned.

"They'll notice," I said. "Won't they? The salt, the mountain, all of it?"

"People notice what hurts," Beatrix said. "When it stops hurting, they mostly feel lighter and call it providence."

"You have a terrible opinion of people."

"I was bred by them." He said it mildly. Mildly! As if he were remarking on the quality of the road gravel. "It's earned."

* * *

The first village took us in without question, which should have warned me.

It was Saltmere, the same village where a god had traded miracles for years of memory, where I had knelt at a crude stone altar and paid a price I didn't understand to buy something I couldn't name. The god there was gone now — I could feel the absence the way you feel a missing tooth, tongue going again and again to the gap. But the people were still there, and they were celebrating. They didn't know what they were celebrating. They'd woken that morning feeling lighter and decided it was a festival day, and by noon there were ribbons on the doors and someone had dragged a fiddle out of storage.

An old woman pressed bread into my hands — real bread, smuggled prayers baked in, except the prayers had gone inert, little knots of devotion with nowhere to go. She smiled at me with the uncomplicated warmth of the very old.

"Bless you both, travelers," she said. "You've come from the mountain way? They say something happened up there. Nobody can say what. Old Marta dreamed her dead husband came home and told her the door was open, and then he left again, polite as anything."

"What did the door open onto?" I asked.

She looked at me as if I'd asked a foolish question, which perhaps I had. "Onto morning, dear. What else?"

Beatrix stood a little apart while she talked to me, and I noticed — I had gotten good at noticing — how the villagers' eyes slid off him. Not with fear or dislike. Just a gentle, universal failure to hold on. A woman handing out stew looked straight through him to offer me a bowl. A child playing in the lane ran past him without a glance, though children notice everything. He stood in the middle of human warmth like a man standing in rain wearing oilcloth: touched everywhere, wet nowhere.

I hated it. I want to be honest, since honesty is the one offering I have left to give any god: I hated it with a fury that surprised me. Nine weeks on the road together. Nine weeks of shared fire and shared bread and shared terror, of him teaching me to walk quietly and me teaching him to laugh — badly, reluctantly, but teaching him — and these people looked at him and saw weather. Saw a stranger. Saw *nothing*, because that is what he had bought their salvation with, and the purchase had cleared.

"You're staring," he said quietly, when the old woman moved on.

"You're invisible."

"I'm free." He took the bowl from me, finally seen enough to be fed. "There's a difference."

"Is there?" My voice came out sharper than I meant it to. "Because from where I stand it looks like the same thing with better lighting."

He ate his stew. He let me be angry, which was its own unbearable kindness.

* * *

I should tell you what I know about him, since knowing things about him is now my sole occupation and I am catastrophically bad at it.

I know he was nine years a poison taster. I know he swallowed death every morning for a crown that sold him before he could walk. I know he was immune to everything except the truth, and the truth cost him more than any venom ever brewed. I know he gave away his name — freely, willingly, into the King of Ash's ledger — to close a contract that would have put a returning god inside his skin, and that the giving broke the court beneath the mountain and freed every stolen face in it, including mine, because yes, I had begun to be forgotten too, up in the world above, and he carried me out through the breaking dark when he could have run faster alone.

I know all of that the way you know the plot of a story someone told you. What I don't know is why, when he sleeps by the fire, I find myself memorizing the exact angle of his jaw. Why I catalogued, somewhere in the weeks of walking, that he takes his tea bitter and pretends not to care. Why, when he laughs — that rusty, suspicious sound, like a door opening in a house long shut — something in my chest pulls tight as a bowstring drawn to the ear.

I owe him everything. I know that the way you know your own hands. And I cannot say why. There is a hole in me shaped exactly like his name, and I fell into it nightly.

Once — this was two nights out of Saltmere — I tried. I sat across the fire from him and I gripped the edges of the absence and I *pulled*, the way Vespera of the Waking Order pulled truth out of unwilling mouths, which was the talent that got me defrocked. I reached for the name.

I got nothing. Worse than nothing. I got the sensation of a stair that isn't where your foot expects it, that lurching vertigo of a world briefly untrue. And Beatrix set down his cup and looked at me across the flames with those grey eyes and said, gently:

"Don't."

"I almost had it."

"No," he said, "you didn't. You had the hurt of it. Those are different things." He stirred the coals. "You paid for something once. In a village with a stone god. Don't make the payment twice."

"How do you know about that?"

"Because whatever you bought," he said, "it saved my life. I've had a lot of time to work backward from effects to causes. It's most of what I do now." A pause. The fire cracked. "Whatever it cost you — thank you. I can't remember being grateful for anything specific. But I remember being grateful."

*Grateful.* The word sat between us like a guest who had come to the wrong house.

* * *

Ironhollow's gates, when we reached them, stood open.

Not broken open. Open the way doors are open in summer, propped with stones, an invitation rather than a wound. The guards at the wall looked at us with mild interest and waved us through. One of them yawned. I nearly laughed — nearly wept, it's hard to tell the difference in me — because I remembered the stories of this valley's walls. No soul may leave. The founding law carved over the gate in letters deep as knife cuts.

The letters were still there. I stopped and read them, and read them again, and understood that they had become what all abandoned laws become: decoration. The stone under the carving had gone soft-looking, weathered, like a face relaxing after long tension. Someone had hung laundry over part of the inscription. A pair of trousers flapped gently against the word *soul*.

"Charming," Beatrix said.

"Shut up."

"You're crying at a wall."

"I am *not*—" I wiped my face. "It's the wind. The valley wind is notorious. Everyone says."

"Nobody says that."

"The valley wind is notorious," I repeated, "and I will not be mocked by a man who has legally ceased to exist."

That landed differently than I intended. He went quiet, and I wanted to bite my own tongue off, and the guard by the gate watched us both with the pleasant vacant attention of a man watching two strangers argue about wind.

Inside, the valley was waking to its own freedom without knowing it. I saw it in small things as we walked toward the palace town: a woman praying at a roadside shrine and getting an answer — nothing grand, just a sense of being heard, the candles burning a shade steadier than physics strictly allowed. A child skipping rope to a rhyme about the mountain that made the older women look up sharply, not with fear but with recognition. The old gods withdrawing, yes — the mountain was empty, the great presences gone wherever gods go when their debts are paid — but small holiness seeping back into the cracks like water into drought-cracked earth. Prayers working again. Crude, local, imperfect. Mine, once. Before I fell. Before I hid in the foundation stones of the very cage I now watched dissolve.

"Do you feel it?" I asked him.

"I feel it the way you'd feel someone else's bathwater." He glanced at me. "It doesn't touch me. Nothing does. That was rather the point of me."

"That's the loneliest sentence I've ever heard."

"It's efficient."

"Beatrix—" I caught myself. Caught the name in my mouth like a fish I'd somehow hooked from an emptied pond, and stared at it, stunned. "I just said your name."

He stopped walking.

We stood in the market square of the lower town, carts rolling past, and I turned the word over again. *Beatrix.* It came easily. Too easily. It came the way a song comes when you can't remember learning it.

"That's not—" He frowned, and I watched him reach inward for something and find the same smooth blank wall I found. "That's not mine. Or — it might be. It sounds like it might be. Where did you get that?"

"I don't know." My heart was pounding absurdly. "I just know it. The way I know I owe you. The way I know—" I stopped. The way I know your jaw by heart. The way I know you take your tea bitter. The way I know that if you died I would burn down whatever remained of the world, and I cannot tell you why, and neither of us can say whether that's love or just the ghost-shape of something a ledger stole.

"Keep it," he said at last.

"It's not mine to keep."

"You're the only one who has it." Something moved behind his eyes — the candle-in-a-draft flicker, but slower this time, steadier, a flame deciding whether to commit to the wick. "One person remembering a thing makes it half-true. I've decided to take half."

* * *

The palace received us without ceremony, because palaces are built to receive everything without ceremony; it's how they stay standing.

We went in openly, through the front, past servants who stepped aside for the grey-eyed man and the travel-worn woman beside him without a flicker of curiosity. And here is where I must record the thing that has kept me awake since, the thing I turn over at night like a stone looking for what lives under it.

We asked — I asked, I did the talking — after the Steward of Tasters. Justice, I wanted. The man who signed the contract, who sold a child before it could walk, who ordered a cook flogged for a kindness. The guards directed us pleasantly to the records hall. And in the records hall, a clerk with ink-stained fingers paged through the rolls of service and looked up at us with honest bafflement.

"Taster?" he said. "There's no taster by that description. The office of royal taster has stood vacant for some months. Since the spring, at least. There was a fellow before that — old Aldous, retired to the coast — but no one named..." He waited politely for a name.

I didn't give him one. I looked at Beatrix, and Beatrix looked at the open ledger page, at the clean unbroken column of entries, at the vacancy where nine years of service should have been written — and he smiled. Small. Unreadable. A smile like a door closed gently from the inside.

"Thank you," I told the clerk, because someone had to say something and it clearly wasn't going to be him.

In the corridor outside, I rounded on him. "They erased your service. Your whole life's work. Doesn't that anger you?"

"Would it change anything?"

"Yes! Yes, it would change — it would matter—"

"Vespera." And he said my name carefully, tasting it, the way a man tastes the last food of a country he's leaving. "I spent nine years being written down. Measured, dosed, recorded, contracted. Every part of me that was written down belonged to someone else." He looked back toward the records hall, at the emptiness that had been his life, and there was grief in his face — real grief, I'll swear to that — but underneath it, unmistakably, relief. "This is the first thing that's ever been unwritten. Let me have it."

I had no answer. I am a priestess; answers are my trade; I had none. So I said the only true thing available to me:

"You're still hungry. You haven't eaten since Saltmere. Come on — the kitchens are this way. And don't taste anything first. Whatever else happens, you're done tasting for kings."

Something happened to his face then. Not a laugh. Something before a laugh, or after one — the place laughter comes from when it's been locked up for nine years. He followed me down the corridor toward the smell of bread.

Behind us, in the records hall, the clerk blew out his lamp. And far off, in the direction of the taster's chambers, I thought I heard — though I have never managed to confirm it, and I have tried — a chair scrape, softly, as if someone had just sat down in a seat that everyone else believed was empty.
