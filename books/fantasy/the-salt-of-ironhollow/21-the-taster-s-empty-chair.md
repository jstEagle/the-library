## 21. The Taster's Empty Chair

The palace kitchens smelled the same. That was the first cruelty of it.

I had imagined—during the long climb out of the dark, with Vespera's weight across my shoulders and the King of Ash screaming somewhere behind us in a language that made the stones bleed—that when I came home I would come *home*. That the walls would know me. That the cookfires would lean toward me the way they once did when I was small and hungry and pretending I wasn't.

But the kitchens smelled of bay leaf and rendered fat and the particular scorched-onion grief of a sous-chef who'd been yelled at before dawn, and none of it knew my name. None of it could have known my name. Names were what I had traded away, and the palace was only keeping its records honest.

I stood in the shadow of the flour store with ash still ground into the creases of my hands and watched a boy of perhaps seventeen taste the queen's morning broth.

He did it beautifully. Spoon at the correct angle, sip held on the tongue for the full count of ten, eyes closed as though in prayer rather than concentration. He wore the grey-and-silver livery of the Steward of Tasters, which meant the Steward of Tasters still existed, which meant either the crown had never learned how deep its Steward's signature went into the ledgers below—or it had learned, and decided some debts were cheaper than the scandal of collecting them. Knowing courts, I'd have bet on the second. Courts are not sentimental. They are merely patient in ways that look like sentiment from a distance.

The boy swallowed. He opened his eyes. He announced the broth safe, and his voice cracked on the word *safe* in exactly the way mine had cracked nine years ago, on my ninth dish, when I still believed the trembling was fear of dying rather than the beginning of an education in everything else.

"He's new," said a voice beside me, and I nearly put a knife through Vespera's ribs out of pure habit.

She had a talent for appearing at my shoulder without sound, a relic of her years hiding inside foundation stones, sustained on prayers smuggled in bread. She looked better than she had any right to. The court beneath had starved her of being remembered, and memory, for a priestess of the waking gods, is a kind of food. But she'd been eating again since we came up—strangers' kindnesses, small ones, the crude miracles of ordinary people—and it showed in her face the way rain shows on stone.

"I can see he's new," I murmured. "I'm not blind."

"You're staring like a man at a funeral."

"Whose?"

"That," she said gently, "is rather the question, isn't it."

* * *

We spent three days in Ironhollow before I could make myself walk up to the palace, and even then I told myself it was reconnaissance. The valley's founding law—no god may enter, no soul may leave—had dissolved along with the gods' withdrawal; the salt line was just salt now, white and inert, and children already dared each other to step over it and reported feeling nothing but cold feet and disappointment. The cage was open. The bird had gotten out by ceasing, in every way that mattered, to exist.

That is the arithmetic nobody warns you about. Freedom costs exactly one name. Not a finger. Not a year. A name—the whole of it, root and branch, spoken willingly into a ledger that ate it the way the palace drains ate waste. And so the palace had no taster named Beatrix Dravenmoor. It had never had one. The cell in the tasters' wing stood empty, made up with fresh linen, waiting for whoever came next. My nine years of service were unrecorded—not expunged, which would at least have been an act with me in it, but simply *absent*, as though the chair had always been vacant, as though poison had tasted itself all those mornings through sheer bureaucratic initiative.

Vespera had tried, in those first days, to be kind about it. She had sat with me in a rented room above a chandler's shop and said, "I owe you everything, and I cannot tell you why, and I am sorry." She said it every morning, like a prayer, because she is a priestess and that is what priestesses do with wounds too large for one saying. She remembered the debt. She remembered the shape of it. She remembered that there had been someone, and that the someone had carried her up through the breaking dark while the court collapsed around them like a cathedral deciding against itself.

She did not remember *Beatrix Dravenmoor*. No one did. The hunters who'd chased us through Ashmarket had stopped mid-stride outside the salt line, blinking, holding warrants for a vessel whose description slid off their minds like water off wax. The Steward of Tasters—I checked, from a distance, through a window, hating myself for checking—had aged a decade in the weeks I was gone and now took his meals with a private taster, a nervous woman with quick hands. Guilt does that to a man. Or fear. With the Steward it was always hard to tell the difference, which was presumably why he'd been so good at his work.

So: reconnaissance. That's what I called it, standing in the flour store's shadow, watching the new boy live.

* * *

His name was Tam, I learned, because names are free to everyone but me. The under-cooks shouted it with the casual brutality kitchens reserve for their youngest. *Tam, the fish sauce. Tam, her majesty wants the almond thing again, god help us all. Tam, you're on dishes tomorrow, don't argue.* Tam, Tam, Tam—a name worn like a hand-me-down coat, unvalued precisely because it had never been at risk.

I followed him for two days. This is either stalking or scholarship depending on who tells it, and I have decided to tell it.

He rose before the bells. He prayed—to whom, I couldn't tell; the old gods were quiet now, withdrawn behind whatever door they'd opened and closed, and the sanctioned church of the valley had never approved of kitchen-floor devotion anyway. He counted his steps between the dormitory and the scullery, forty-one, and touched the doorpost twice, and I recognized each superstition the way you recognize your own handwriting in an old letter. I had done all of it. Forty-one steps, twice on the post, spoon at the correct angle, ten-count on the tongue. Somewhere in the palace archives there was—or wasn't, now—a manual, and this boy had found the same copy, or been shaped by the same Steward, or simply arrived at the same rituals because terror condenses into liturgy given enough mornings.

On the third day he was assigned the queen's feast tasting, alone, in the small stone room with the slit window, and I understood that the palace had learned nothing, because of course it hadn't. Palaces are machines for not learning. The room was mine once. The window framed the same slice of mountain, the same suggestion of the deep places underneath where a mirrored court had kept its borrowed faces until I shattered the glass and let the stolen walk home.

I got into the corridor outside. This required no great skill; the palace guards had been halved since the eclipse, since the night the sky went wrong over Ironhollow and something vast poured into a taster's body and found it shut to them like a locked house—since the night nobody could afterward describe, because the center of it had walked away nameless and the edges had been sanded smooth by official retelling. There had been an eclipse, the records would say. There had been tremors. There had been a regrettable incident involving mirrors in the undercroft, cause unknown. History is what survives the filing.

Through the door's observation slot I watched Tam take the ninth dish of the feast.

It was quail in a glaze of honey and black mustard, and I knew before he raised the spoon what it would taste like, because I had tasted it, years ago, on a morning the kitchens had answered someone's prayer without meaning to—an offering that should have killed me and didn't, the first crack in the routine, the first hint that my whole life was a containment protocol wearing the costume of a career. The gods were gone now. The dish was only food. Tam sipped, held it, swallowed, and pronounced it safe in a voice that cracked on the word.

And I stood in the corridor of the place that had bred me like a crop and felt nothing so useful as anger. Anger had been spent underground, in installments, at the mirrors and the ledger and the king who collected names like a miser collects coins he can never spend. What was left was quieter and worse. What was left was mourning.

Because here is the thing I understood, watching him, and it came to me with the slow certainty of water finding a crack: I had wanted this. Some part of me, nine years poisoned and decades sold, had wanted exactly this—for the suffering to mean armor instead of damage, for the vessel to refuse its cargo, for the boy in the chair to win. And I *had* won. The contract was voided. The god had poured into me and found nothing to pour *into*, every immunity a brick, every brick laid by their own hands, and the King of Ash had learned in the worst hour of his long gray existence that you cannot possess a man who has already given away the only thing possession fastens to.

I won. And the price of winning was that no record remained of the match.

There is a particular loneliness in being the only witness to your own life. Every scar on my body was testimony without a case. The calluses on my tongue from ten thousand tastings. The burn on my forearm from the ferryman's rope, the salt-scars on my palms from the boundary crossing, the bruise-shaped ache where Vespera's weight had ridden my shoulders up out of the dark. All evidence. All pointing at a man who, legally, metaphysically, and now memorially, had never existed.

Tam finished the quail. He washed his mouth with the prescribed water. He wrote his mark in the tasting book—the book I had written my mark in for nine years, except that my marks were gone now too, the ink unchanged, the entries simply never having contained me—and I saw his hand shake slightly as he signed, and I loved him for it, and I hated the palace for making that shaking necessary, and I wanted, with a ferocity that surprised me, to go in and teach him everything. How to read a kitchen's mood. How a prayer smells different from a seasoning. How to know, in your bones, when the tenth dish is not a dish.

But you cannot apprentice a boy to a ghost. And that was what I was now, in every way that touched the palace: a ghost with excellent circulation and a working knowledge of poisons.

* * *

I found Vespera at dusk on the old salt line, because of course she was there. She'd taken to sitting on the boundary at evening the way other people take to chapel—hands open on her knees, face turned toward the mountain, listening for a gods' answer that no longer came and, I think, grieving the silence less than she'd expected to.

"They're telling stories," she said, without looking around. "In the village. About the eclipse."

"What kind of stories?"

"The good kind. The inaccurate kind." She patted the salt-crusted earth beside her, and I sat. "A priestess and an unnamed man broke the court beneath the mountain. The gods woke, saw what had been done in their sleeping name, and withdrew in disgust, dissolving the valley's law as they went. Everyone the court had forgotten is remembering themselves—the drowned dead, the ledger-lost. There's a woman in Ashmarket, they say, who woke up with thirty years of her life returned to her, and wept into her beer for a day straight."

"And the man? In the story?"

"No one remembers him." She said it plainly, without apology, because she had learned that apology was a knife I kept stepping on. "That's rather the point of the story. He isn't in it. Only his works."

"Very tidy," I said. "The kind of miracle that files itself."

"You could tell them the true version."

"And say what? 'I was Beatrix Dravenmoor'? The words don't hold, Ves. I've tried. I stand in front of a mirror—which survived, oddly, the one in the chandler's back room—and I say it, and it sounds like a stranger's coat I'm holding up. Well-made. Not mine." I picked up a pinch of dead salt and let the wind take it. "Do you know what the strange part is? It doesn't hurt the way I thought it would. It hurts the way an amputation hurts after the fever breaks. Present. Bearable. Wrong in a way I'll be renegotiating with the world for the rest of my life."

She was quiet awhile. Below us, the valley lights came on one by one, lamps in windows of houses that had been cages and were now, tentatively, homes. Somewhere a child laughed past bedtime. The mountain kept its silence, and for the first time in the valley's history the silence was only geology.

"There's one record," Vespera said at last. "One place your name might still be written. The King's ledger burned with the court—but the ferryman keeps his own book. The passage you paid. The whistle-bone and the cook's last trust. He writes down what is paid, not what is owned. He might have—"

"Don't," I said.

"It wouldn't restore anything. It would just be proof. A line in a dead man's book, in a language the living can't—"

"*Don't.*" I heard my voice go flat and old, the voice of the taster's room, and I made myself soften it. "If I go back down there, even for a line in a ledger, I'm bargaining again. And I've learned what I am in a bargain, Ves. I'm currency. The moment I stand in front of another collector with a want in my hand, I start spending pieces of myself to get it, and I've spent enough." I looked at her—really looked, at the woman who owed me everything and knew my face only from these last hard weeks, who was learning me the way you learn a language late in life, with effort and love and no childhood vocabulary. "Let them keep it. The name. Let it sit in a burned book at the bottom of the world. It bought the most expensive thing I'll ever own."

"Which is?"

I gestured—at the valley, at the lights, at the salt line going soft and gardenable under our feet, at her.

She didn't say anything to that. She had learned, in her disgrace, the discipline of letting silence carry what words would cheapen. But she moved closer until her shoulder rested against mine, and we watched the mountain not answer, and the evening came down gentle as sifted flour.

* * *

Here is what I did next, and why.

The palace posts for a new Steward of Tasters within the month. Of course it does. The old one has developed a sudden piety and requested transfer to a coastal province where nothing grows worth poisoning, and the crown, needing the office filled before the autumn feasts, sets notice on the palace gates: candidates must possess iron constitution, unblemished loyalty, and no family to grieve them.

No family to grieve them. I read that line four times. Somewhere behind the phrasing was the same logic that had selected an orphan nine years ago, the same investment thesis, the same quiet arithmetic of a kingdom buying a vessel wholesale. The court beneath the mountain was ash and broken mirror. But the habits that fed it were alive and well upstairs, taking applications.

I could not sign my name to anything. I could not testify, accuse, or appear. The Steward's crime lived in a ledger at the bottom of a dead world, witnessed only by a man who did not exist and a priestess who could not remember the crucial entry. Justice, for Beatrix Dravenmoor, was a door permanently locked from a house that had been demolished.

But Tam existed. Tam with his forty-one steps and his cracking voice and his shaking signature. Tam, who in a year or five would be the man in the corridor, or the man signing the contracts, or—if someone reached him early enough—the man who refused.

So I did the only thing a ghost can do. I applied for the position.

Not under my name; I haven't one. Under the name I'd been using in the valley since the salt lost its teeth—*Bex*, a chandler's assistant, no family, references available—and with a constitution I demonstrated, in the trial tasting, by swallowing things that sent three other candidates to the infirmary. Old immunities die hard. It turns out nine years of poison remains the finest credential in the known world, and the examining physicians pronounced me a marvel, and the Steward-elect wrote *iron constitution, unblemished loyalty, no family* in her neat hand, and offered me the post.

I start tomorrow.

Vespera thinks I'm mad. She stood in the doorway of the rented room while I folded my one good shirt and said, "You spent everything to get out of that chair."

"I spent a name," I said. "The chair was never the point. The point was that they chose what went in it. Someone should be in that chair who knows what the palace is for—someone immune to everything, including their gratitude. Nine years, they built me as a weapon against gods." I tucked the shirt into the bag and smiled at her, and it was mostly a real smile. "Turns out I work fine against kingdoms too."

She laughed—the short, disbelieving laugh of a priestess catching her god in an act of mercy—and then she said, quietly, "I'll be near. The chandler's wife likes me. I light candles for strangers; it's practically my vocation."

"Then I'll see you at dinner," I said, "where you can watch me eat anything I like."

The cell in the tasters' wing has fresh linen on the bunk. Tomorrow morning a boy named Bex will sit in the empty chair, at the correct angle, spoon level, and taste the queen's breakfast on behalf of a crown that cannot remember killing him.

I find that I am looking forward to it.

Some things grow back differently. I intend to find out what this one becomes.
