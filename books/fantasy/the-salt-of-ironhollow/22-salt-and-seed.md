## 22. Salt and Seed

The salt line runs through the valley like a scar through a palm, and I have decided to garden on it.

I want that recorded somewhere, in case anyone ever asks — though of course no one will, because asking requires remembering, and remembering requires a name, and I gave mine to a ledger beneath a mountain in exchange for the only thing I've ever truly owned: myself. All of it. The parts I was born with and the parts they built. Even the parts that hurt.

Especially those.

* * *

Spring came late to Ironhollow the year the gods left. They went quietly, which surprised me; I had expected thunder, or at least some theatrical cracking of the sky. Instead, one morning the prayers stopped being answered, and the priests who weren't disgraced found their altars cold, and Vespera stood at the mouth of her cave with tears running down her face and said, "They're gone home," in the voice of a woman whose whole family has moved overseas without leaving a forwarding address.

"They were never here," I said. "That was the point of this place."

"No," she said. "They were *listening*. There's a difference." She wiped her face with her sleeve, briskly, like a housewife shutting a window against rain. "And now they aren't."

I didn't know what to do with grief for gods I'd spent nine years being poisoned on behalf of, so I did what I always do with feelings too large for my hands. I picked up a shovel.

The salt line, as it turned out, was not salt. That's a lie the valley tells itself, the way it told itself a lot of lies for three hundred years. It was ash — old ash, pressed and fused into a gray glassy seam that ran wall to wall across the valley floor where the founders had poured it, burning something at each end that Vespera refuses to describe even now. A boundary. A seal. A cage-wall laid flat, so the prisoners wouldn't have to look at bars.

Nothing grew on it. Nothing had grown on it in three centuries.

Which is exactly the sort of challenge a man with no past and no name and nothing better to do cannot resist.

* * *

The first problem was breaking the ground. Glass does not yield to a spade, and I am not a patient man — a fact I know about myself because I chose it, recently, deliberately, the way you choose a coat off a peg. I am impatient. I am stubborn. I am fond of plums and distrustful of clergy and I sleep on my left side. These are my possessions now, self-declared, and I guard them jealously.

Vespera solved the ground-breaking the way she solves everything: by talking to it.

"You could just ask," she said, watching me sweat and swear at the ash-glass with an iron bar. "It's not a god anymore. It's just dead ritual. Dead things listen better than living ones, in my experience."

"That is a horrifying sentence, Vespera."

"Thank you. It took years of seminary." She crouched, pressed both palms flat to the gray seam, and murmured something in a language that made my teeth ache pleasantly, like biting foil. Where she touched it, the ash softened — not melting, exactly. Remembering being soil. Three hundred years of enforced sterility let go all at once, and underneath the glass the earth was black and rich and smelled like the inside of a promise.

We broke the line in eleven places. Eleven gardens, strung along the valley like beads on a broken necklace, each one planted square on top of what used to keep magic out. Vespera said it was penance. I said it was spite. We were both right, which is the foundation of every good arrangement I've ever been part of.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about having your name eaten: you don't miss it. Not exactly. What you miss is the shape other people's mouths made around it.

I watch Vespera struggle with this daily, and I would feel guilty about it if guilt were still on the list of things I'm allowed to carry without charge. She remembers everything else. Every conversation, every mile of road, every horror of the court beneath the mountain — including, gods rot it, the moment I spoke my name into the King's ledger and the dark came down like a curtain between the man I was and whatever I am now. She remembers *that* I had a name. She remembers it mattered. What she doesn't remember is the name itself, because that was the coin, and coins get spent.

She copes by studying me. There's no better word for it. She watches my hands when I work, my mouth when I talk, the particular way I squint when I'm lying about how much I've eaten of something. Yesterday I caught her sketching — actual charcoal on actual paper, hidden under a seed sack — and when I demanded to see it, she held it against her chest like a schoolgirl caught passing notes.

"It's practice," she said.

"Practice for what?"

"For remembering you." She said it simply, without drama, which is somehow worse. "Names are easy. Anyone can memorize a name. Faces you have to *earn*."

I looked at the paper when she finally let me. It was my own face, done well enough to be unsettling, and beneath it, in her careful priestess hand, a list: *left eyebrow splits from old scar. Smiles with teeth but not eyes unless plums involved. Says 'gods rot it' when angry, 'well' when frightened.* A taxonomy. An act of devotion disguised as field notes.

"I look like a specimen," I said.

"You look like someone worth knowing by heart," she said, and then went very red and very busy with the turnip rows, and I had the good grace not to say anything else, mostly because I didn't trust my voice.

* * *

The naming happened in April, over the third garden, and it went nothing like I'd planned, because I hadn't planned it, because planning is for people with futures they're certain of.

A tax assessor came through the valley — the new crown's bureaucracy reasserting itself over the ruins of the old one, clipboards replacing gods, which may be an improvement. He wanted names for the land registry. He looked at our garden, sitting fat and green astride the broken ash-line, and he looked at me, and he said the question everyone asks everyone: "Name?"

And I opened my mouth to say *no one*, which had become a habit, a small comfortable dishonesty, and Vespera's head came up sharp as a hawk's, and I understood suddenly that she was afraid. Not of the assessor. Of the answer. Because every time I said *no one*, a little more of me agreed with it.

"Beatrix," I said.

The word sat strangely in my mouth. Not because it was wrong — it was the name I'd carried for thirty-one years, the name on the empty cell, the name struck from the palace rolls — but because saying it now was different from saying it before. Before, it had been given to me by people who wanted a vessel and needed a label for the jar. Now it was mine. Chosen. Picked up off the ground of my ruined life like a coin found in the street, and pocketed on purpose.

The assessor wrote it down without looking up. "Surname?"

I thought about Dravenmoor. About the orphanage that manufactured it, the Steward who filed it, the contract that sold it. I thought about the cook with his bone whistle, dying with a stranger's name in his mouth because he trusted me with it. I thought about starting clean.

"Just Beatrix," I said. "One name. Like a cat."

The assessor shrugged the shrug of a man paid by the entry, not the poetry, and moved on down the valley.

Vespera didn't speak until he was out of earshot. Then: "Why keep it? You could have been anyone. You could have been *new*."

I knelt and pressed a plum pit into the black soil where the ash-line used to run. "Because," I said, "they took everything else. The soul, the childhood, the nine years, the memory of me from every person I ever saved. This word is the last thing they gave me that I got to keep, and I'm keeping it *my* way now. On my terms. In my mouth." I tamped the soil down. "Besides. If I became someone entirely new, you'd have to learn two faces."

Her expression did something complicated. "You're impossible."

"I'm told it's one of my features. It's on the list."

* * *

The garden grows the way forgiveness grows: unevenly, with long stretches where you're sure nothing is happening, and then a morning when you come out and the world has quietly rearranged itself overnight.

We planted what the valley had forgotten how to grow. Old grains from Vespera's seminary days. Beans, because beans forgive beginners. And plums — plum pits saved from the palace orchards back when I tasted for a queen who no longer rules, back when my tongue was a weapon I pointed at my own food twice a day and called it service. Those pits sat in my pocket through the underworld and the eclipse and the collapse of a court, rattling against my ribs like a second heartbeat, and I never once considered throwing them away.

The first tree came up in June. Then four more. Then a row of them, crooked and defiant, growing straight out of the broken spine of the valley's oldest law.

People notice. That's the strange part. The valley is full of people the gods withdrew from — farmers who prayed to cold altars, mothers whose crude miracles stopped coming, children born after the salt line broke who will grow up thinking the world was always this soft at its edges. They walk past our gardens on the old ash-road, and they slow down, and they look, and some of them ask for seeds. None of them know why the gardens matter. None of them remember the taster who broke the cage, because erasure doesn't care how heroic you were; it takes the whole ledger entry, ink and heroics alike.

But sometimes a farmer's wife presses bread into my hands — smuggled prayers, Vespera calls it, laughing until she cries, because that's how the priestess ate for seven years in the wall and now the whole valley feeds strangers without knowing why. Habit outlives memory. Kindness, apparently, is muscle memory.

That'll do. That'll more than do.

* * *

Autumn, and the plums come ripe, and I eat the first one straight off the branch without testing it, which remains, even now, the most luxurious thing I have ever done. Nine years of tasting death for other people, and here is the sum of my reward: fruit I don't have to fear. I sit under my crooked tree and eat until juice runs down my wrist and I am, briefly, completely happy, in the plain animal way that no court and no god ever managed to purchase.

Vespera finds me there. She carries her charcoal book everywhere now, and she doesn't hide it anymore, and I pretend not to notice that the recent pages contain fewer sketches and more writing. Lists of my habits. Notes on my moods. Once, folded small, a page I wasn't meant to see, with a single line in her careful hand: *If I learn him perfectly enough, maybe the part of me that knew his name will recognize it coming home.*

I let her believe I haven't seen it. Some hopes need darkness to sprout, and I of all people know how much can grow back differently if you just leave it in the ground long enough.

She sits beside me under the tree and holds out her hand for a plum without asking, because we passed the asking stage sometime during the flight from Ashmarket and never went back. Juice on her chin. Charcoal on her fingers. The valley gold and quiet below us, the mountains bare of gods and better for it, and somewhere far beneath us, a collapsed court full of freed faces learning how to be their own again.

"Do you regret it?" she asks. "The name. The price."

I consider lying, for flavor. I consider the truth, for once.

"I regret that you don't remember me," I say. "The rest of it — the cell, the poison, the ledger — that was theirs. The forgetting is the only thing they actually won. But everything since the salt line?" I take another plum. "Everything since is the first property I've ever owned outright, and I intend to die embarrassingly rich in it."

She leans her shoulder against mine, and we watch the light go long and amber across the gardens, eleven broken seals blooming their defiance into the evening, and she says, quietly, in the voice of a woman memorizing rather than mourning:

"Well, Beatrix. One name, like a cat. What shall we plant next spring?"

And I tell her. And some things grow back differently — but they grow.
