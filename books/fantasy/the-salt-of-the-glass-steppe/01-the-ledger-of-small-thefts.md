## 1. The Ledger of Small Thefts

There are three rules in the ledger room of the Glass Steppe Guild, and I have broken two of them so many times they have worn smooth as river stones.

Rule one: write what you are given, exactly as you are given it. Rule two: never touch a memory with bare skin. Rule three: never ask where the memories come from.

I break rule one on nights like this, when the acquisitions are small and sad and I cannot bear to record them without softening something. I break rule two only in secret, and only ever downward — taking nothing, giving back slivers, like a man slipping coins into beggars' cups when no one is watching. Rule three I keep absolutely, because rule three is the only thing standing between me and knowing things that would get me killed.

The lamps were lit. The ink was mixed — memory-ink, ground from saltglass and bound with tallow, the kind that holds a recollection the way amber holds an insect. And the acquisitions had come in at dusk, as they always did, carried up from the weighing room in their little stoppered vials, each one labeled in Corvane's cramped hand: *one courage, soldier's, partial; one grief, widow's, whole.*

Whole griefs are rare. Most people sell grief by the slice, the way you'd sell a ham. You keep enough to know why you're crying. This widow had sold all of it, every drop, which meant she would wake tomorrow remembering her husband's funeral the way you remember a stranger's: a date, a name, a fact with no weight to it. She would be able to say *my husband died* without her voice breaking, and she would probably think herself cured.

She was not cured. She was hollowed. But try explaining that to someone who has just paid off a debt with the only coin she had left.

I uncorked the vial and held it to the lamp. Grief looks different depending on whose it is. A child's grief is bright and thin, like a struck bell. A soldier's is dense, settled at the bottom of the vial like silt. This one was gray-blue and slow-moving, the way deep water moves, and it smelled faintly of bread — which told me, before I wrote a single word, that this was a woman who had baked for her husband for forty years and could not separate his dying from the smell of her own kitchen.

That is the part the guild does not record. We record category, quantity, provenance-in-broad-strokes, price. We do not record the bread. The bread is nobody's business but hers, and after tonight it will not even be hers.

*One grief, widow's, whole,* I wrote. *Acquired from M— of the Lower Terraces, against debt of eleven crowns, four shillings. Quality: exceptional. Clarity: undimmed. Notes: seller wept during transfer, then thanked us.*

Then she thanked us. That was the part I hated most, the thanking. People always thank you. They thank you because the pain is gone and they do not yet understand that the pain was load-bearing, that it was holding up something in them they haven't noticed missing yet. You can take the grief out of a woman and leave the shape of it behind, a shadow pressed into her like a fossil, and she will spend years running her hand over the hollow wondering what used to live there.

I blotted the entry. I did not soften it. Some nights I manage to leave the entries alone entirely, and tonight was almost one of those nights, until I got to the soldier.

* * *

The courage had come in three days ago and Corvane had been saving it, the way he saves everything, for a buyer he wouldn't name. A merchant's son, I heard through the weighing-room wall, some boy who flinched at loud noises and needed to stand up in front of creditors and lie with a straight face. Courage is our steadiest seller. Everyone wants it and no one wants to admit wanting it, so they come in dressed as something else — buying *confidence*, buying *nerve*, buying *what my brother had*. The guild obligingly relabels the jar. Same courage. Nicer label.

This particular courage had been taken whole from a sergeant at the border garrison, or nearly whole — the transfer-marks said ninety percent, which is the guild's way of saying the man kept a thimbleful, just enough to hold a sword and follow orders, not enough to volunteer for anything. Ninety percent of a brave man's courage leaves behind a very specific kind of person: obedient, punctual, quietly terrified. He'll stand where he's put. He will never again step forward.

I wrote the entry. *One courage, soldier's, ninety parts of one hundred. Acquired from Sergeant D—, Third Border Company, against arrears of pay owed to the Guild since the autumn levy. Quality: high. Temperament: steady under fire, poor under scrutiny.* That last bit was me editorializing again — the sergeant, whoever he was, had apparently once frozen when an officer shouted at him, and the buyer's agent had dickered the price down over it, and now the flaw lived forever in my neat little hand, a man's worst hour recorded in a ledger for eternity while his best hour got poured into a merchant's son.

Somebody should write the other ledger. Somebody should sit down across from these people and record what they gave instead of what they paid. *Sergeant D—, who stood a wall at the fords for six hours. Widow M—, who loved a man for forty years and baked him bread.* That ledger would be worth reading. It would also be worthless as collateral, which is the only kind of worth the guild recognizes.

Corvane came in at the second bell, the way he always does, on soft shoes, because he believes I don't hear him and I have decided to let him believe it. There is a détente between us, Corvane and I. He pretends I am merely meticulous. I pretend not to notice that he checks my work afterward, page by page, looking for — what? Embezzlement? Sentiment? He has never found anything, because there is nothing to find, because I am exactly what I appear to be: a person who writes down thefts in a beautiful hand and feels nothing about it.

Or that is the story. Let me be honest, since honesty costs nothing here in the dark where no one reads: I feel everything about it. That is my affliction. Not the gift everyone fears — well, that too, but we'll come to it — but the feeling. Twenty-two years in this room and I still have not learned to transcribe a stolen childhood without my chest going tight. Corvane thinks my value is my penmanship. My actual value, though he'd die before admitting it, is that I remember everything I write, and I write it accurately, and accuracy is rarer than penmanship in this business.

"Kavanagh." His voice, dry as the salt flats outside the windows. "The courage. Is it entered?"

"It's entered."

"And the widow."

"Entered."

He drifted along the shelves the way he does, trailing one finger along the spines — thousands of them, vials racked behind glass-fronted cases, each case labeled by decade, each decade labeled by type. *Courages, 1189–1194. Childhoods, summer, 1201. First loves, assorted.* Somewhere on those shelves is a vial containing my grandmother's voice singing a lullaby in a language the maps have forgotten, sold by my mother in a bad winter before I was old enough to understand what was being taken out of our house. I have looked for it. I have never found it. I suspect Corvane knows exactly where it is and suspects that I know that he knows, and neither of us has ever said a word about it, because saying the word aloud would make it real, and both of us prefer it as a ghost.

"The buyer comes Thursday," Corvane said, stopping at the case where the courage sat. "For the merchant's son. Have the transfer papers drawn."

"They're drawn."

"Good." He looked at me a moment longer than the conversation required. Corvane's silences are invoices. Every extra second he spends looking at you is a second he expects to collect on later. "You've been quiet lately."

"I'm always quiet."

"You've been quieter." He let that hang. Then he left, soft-shoed, and I listened to his footsteps fade down the corridor and counted to sixty before I moved, because rule zero, the unwritten one, the one that governs all the others: never move while Corvane might still be listening.

* * *

Here is the thing I have never written in any ledger, including this one, which is not technically a ledger but is the closest thing I have to a confession booth.

I can lift a memory with a touch.

Not buy it. Not trade for it. Take it — or give it — skin to skin, in the space of a breath, no vial, no ink, no fee. The gift runs in families; mine came down through the grandmother whose lullaby sits somewhere on those shelves in a jar. The country we came from — the country that no longer appears on any map, the erased one, the Salt Steppe itself, though the guild's clients call it nothing at all because they cannot remember it — produced people like me the way orchards produce fruit, and then the borders were redrawn and the orchards were declared weeds and cut down, and the ones of us who remained learned quickly that the safest thing a gifted person can be is invisible.

So I became invisible. I became the most invisible kind of invisible: useful. A guild that buys and sells memories needs a keeper of records who cannot be bought, because he already owns the most dangerous inventory in the building — the knowledge of what every client sold and why. Corvane pays me well, trusts me completely within the narrow lane of my duties, and has never once, in twenty-two years, asked me to shake his hand.

I noticed that in my first year. The handshake. Or rather the absence of it. Gloves in winter, gloves in summer, the careful half-bow that substitutes for touch, the way he hands me documents by setting them on the table rather than passing them. I thought, at fifteen, that it was fastidiousness. It took me three more years to understand it was fear. Corvane suspects. Corvane has always suspected. He built this guild on the backs of people like me; he knows what we can do; and he keeps me close the way you keep a wolf close — fed, warm, watched.

Which is why rule two exists, and why I break it anyway, carefully, in the dark, after the second bell, when the corridor is empty and the lamps are low.

Tonight's breaking was small. It usually is. The widow's vial was still open on my desk — I had been re-reading my own entry, which I do sometimes, the way other people re-read letters — and I did the thing I do, the thing I have done perhaps four hundred times now and never once been caught doing. I pressed two fingers to the glass.

It doesn't work through glass, properly speaking. What works through glass is a seep, a leak, a fraction of a fraction. Enough to taste. Enough to know. The grief came through cold and slow and it tasted of bread, and underneath the bread, faint as a watermark, there was a name — I felt it rather than heard it, the way you feel a word printed on the far side of a page — and the name was *Alo*, and Alo had died in the month of the Falling Leaves, and for about four seconds I knew what forty years of marriage felt like from the inside, and then I took my fingers off the glass and it drained away and I was myself again, alone in a room full of other people's lives.

Four seconds. That's my tithe. That's what I pay, secretly, nightly, to balance whatever books the gods keep on this place. I take a sip of what they sold and I carry it for them, because someone should carry it, because grief that goes nowhere curdles into nothing, and I refuse — this is the one vanity I allow myself, the one piece of pride I keep under the floorboards — I refuse to let these things go nowhere.

The soldier's courage I did not sample. Courage tastes of iron and cold water and it makes me want things, specifically it makes me want to walk into Corvane's office and say the true thing, whatever the true thing is, and I cannot afford the true thing. The widow's grief is safe. Grief only makes me sad, and sadness I can hide. I have had twenty-two years of practice hiding sadness. I could teach a seminar.

I closed the vial. I racked it. Case nine, shelf three, between a fisherman's patience and a bride's nervous joy, and I noted the location in the index in my best hand, the hand Corvane brags about to buyers, *our archivist's script is famous throughout the Steppe*, as if handwriting were an achievement and not a disguise.

Because that's what it is. That's what all of this is. The penmanship, the precision, the quiet, the punctuality — every virtue I own was selected for concealment. A loud man with my gift lasts a season. A silent man lasts a lifetime, or believes he will, right up until the night the world changes and silence stops being protection and becomes evidence.

* * *

I finished the last entries near dawn. A tanner's steadiness, acquired against a debt. A midwife's calm hands, sold voluntarily, which is rarer than you'd think — most sellers are paying something, but every few months someone walks in off the street and sells a memory for cash, no debt, no duress, just a person who wants to be lighter, and those are the ones I hate recording most, because the guild gives them half what the memory was worth and calls it charity.

The lamps burned low. The salt wind came through the shutters and made the flames lean east. Outside, the Glass Steppe did what it always does before sunrise: went briefly, perfectly silent, the silence of a place that has been scraped empty and polished smooth, and then the light came up the color of weak tea and caught the flats and turned them into a mirror, and for ten minutes every morning the whole world doubled itself, sky below and sky above, and if you stood at the window at exactly the right moment you could not tell which way was down.

I stood at the window. I always stand at the window. It is the one indulgence Corvane permits without comment, perhaps because he cannot imagine what I see there, or perhaps because he can.

What I see, most mornings, is home. Not the guild — the guild is a workplace, a fortress, a trap, depending on the hour. Home is further out, past the flats, past the smugglers' road, in the direction where the maps stop pretending. My grandmother came from a country that was erased. Not conquered — erased. There is a difference, and the difference is ink. Conquered countries get new names on new maps. Erased countries get nothing. The cartographers drew around it. The census-takers skipped it. Within a generation, children born fifty miles from its border could not have told you it had ever existed, and the adults who remembered had learned to hum instead of speak, to sing their directions into lullabies, to hide a whole nation inside melodies because melodies could not be confiscated.

My grandmother sang me one such melody. I have it still — it is the single item of contraband I carry on my person at all times, folded flat inside my skull where no warden can search. Fourteen notes, repeating, and if you know how to listen, the notes are a route: ridge, pass, salt pan, door. A door. Even as a child I understood that lullabies don't usually contain doors, and I asked her once, and she looked at me for a long time and said, *some doors only open for people who remember the way, and the way is a song, and songs survive when maps burn.*

She died when I was nine. The guild bought her funeral-grief from my mother the following spring — a fair price, my mother said, though fair prices in this business are like fair weather on the Steppe, brief and suspicious. And I stayed. Because staying was invisible, and invisible was safe, and I was seventeen and wanted nothing more than safety.

Twenty-two years later I want something else, and I could not tell you what, and that not-knowing is its own kind of dread. It sits under my competence the way water sits under ice. Everything on the surface is smooth. Everything beneath is moving.

* * *

The third bell rang. The day staff came up the stairs — Osk with his tray of bread and salt tea, setting it on my desk without meeting my eyes, because Osk is one of maybe three people in this building who have guessed at me and the other two are dead. I ate. I sharpened the pen. I opened the ledger to a fresh page and ruled the margins, because today's acquisitions would arrive at dusk, as they always do, and the ledger does not care that I slept four hours, and the ledger is the only master I have chosen freely, which either makes it the best job in the world or the loneliest, and I have stopped trying to decide which.

Loneliness, for the record, is not the absence of people. The guild is full of people — weighers, appraisers, the transfer-men with their soft gloved hands, the clients coming and going with their jars and their debts and their thanks. Loneliness is being the only person in a building who knows what the building actually is. Everyone else sees a trade house. I see a granary of souls, racked and indexed and appreciating in value, and I am the only one who lies awake counting them.

Dusk came. The acquisitions came. Nothing unusual — a cooper's patience, a sailor's sense of direction, a young wife's first year of marriage, sold by the husband, which I recorded without comment because commenting is how archivists disappear. I wrote. I blotted. I racked. And then, near the end of the tray, I found a vial that stopped my pen.

It was unlabeled except for Corvane's mark and a single word in his hand: *provenance unknown.*

Provenance unknown means the memory came in sideways — not through a client, not through a debt, but through one of the guild's other channels, the ones I am not supposed to ask about (rule three). Sometimes those are poached from rival houses. Sometimes they are taken. *Taken* is a word that appears in my ledgers perhaps twice a year, always obliquely, always as *provenance unknown*, and every time it appears I feel the floor tilt a degree.

I held the vial to the lamp. Inside: a childhood. Summer, by the color of it — gold and green and the specific brown of a river in late July. A child's memory, five or six years old, whole and unspoiled, the kind of acquisition that fetches triple price from certain buyers because a whole childhood can be worn like a coat by someone who never had one.

And through the glass, faint, leaking, the way memories seep for me whether I will it or not — a song.

Fourteen notes. Repeating. Ridge, pass, salt pan, door.

I set the vial down very carefully, the way you set down something when your hands have stopped being fully yours. I told myself it was coincidence. Grandmothers sing similar songs; the old routes were common knowledge once; fourteen notes is fourteen notes. I told myself the guild had bought this honestly from some other family of the erased country, another scattered orchard, another hidden melody, and that the child who lost it had been paid fairly and would miss it only the way you miss a thing you never knew you had.

I told myself all of that. And then I looked at the entry line, and at Corvane's mark, and at the word *unknown*, and I felt the quiet dread that lives under my competence shift its weight, the way ice shifts before it decides whether to hold.

I wrote the entry. *One childhood, summer, whole. Provenance unknown. Quality: pristine. Notes: none.*

No notes. For the first time in twenty-two years, I wrote *no notes*, and my hand did not shake, and that frightened me more than the vial did.

Outside, the moon was coming up over the flats — thin, early in its turn, one of thirteen turns it would make before the year closed. Thirteen. An unlucky number everywhere else in the world. On the Steppe we counted by it anyway, because the old calendar of the erased country had thirteen months, and the thirteenth moon was the one my grandmother's song was waiting for.

I racked the vial. Case twelve, shelf one, alone, because provenance-unknown acquisitions get isolated pending review, and I told myself the review would be routine, and I walked to the window, and I stood there a long time watching the doubled world, and somewhere in the middle of standing there I understood — quietly, completely, in the place where dread lives — that the routine reviews were over.

Something was coming. I didn't know what. I only knew that the guild had just bought a piece of my grandmother's country off a tray with no questions asked, and that Corvane had marked it himself, and that men like Corvane do not mark things without a reason, and that the reason was going to arrive the way reasons always arrive in this business:

with paperwork.
