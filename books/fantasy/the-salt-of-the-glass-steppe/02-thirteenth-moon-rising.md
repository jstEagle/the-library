## 2. Thirteenth Moon Rising

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in the Royal Cartographic Office after midnight, and I have spent eleven years learning to love it the way you love a dog that bites strangers: fondly, and with one eye on its teeth.

The office occupied the top floor of the Surveyor-General's wing, which meant it had the best light in the capital and the worst heating, because whoever designed the building had decided that mapmakers should suffer for their art in winter and squint at it in summer. The great tables ran the length of the room like the ribs of some beached whale, each one littered with dividers, pens, pots of ink graded by permanence, and the small brass weights we used to hold down corners of paper that had opinions of its own. Forty-one lamps burned there most nights. Mine was usually the last to go out.

I am Vespera Lune. I was, at the time this story properly begins, the royal cartographer — not an assistant cartographer, not a junior draughtsman, but *the* cartographer, with my name embossed on the title plates of atlases that sat on desks in every ministry and every merchant house worth bribing into. I could draw a coastline so beautiful that admirals wept over it, or at least so they told me, though I suspect admirals weep over most things if you catch them between drinks.

And I was good at my work in a way that mattered more than beauty, because cartography in our kingdom was not really about land. It was about agreement. The crown did not want maps that showed where things were. It wanted maps that showed where things were *permitted* to be. Every border I inked was a sentence passed. Every blank space was a verdict. I had redrawn the eastern marches four times in six years as treaties died and were reborn, and each time I moved a river by a finger's width, some village on the far bank stopped existing in law. Nobody wrote to tell me what happened to the villages. You learn not to ask, in the same way you learn not to ask why certain names vanish from the census rolls between printings, or why whole districts of old city maps get replaced with clean white rectangles labeled RECLAIMED FOR CROWN USE.

You learn not to ask. That is the first thing they teach you here, before projection or scale. They don't say it aloud. They simply stop answering when you do.

* * *

The atlas on my table that night was the Grand Imperial Edition, fourth revision, the one that would carry the kingdom's face to every chancellery abroad. Two hundred and forty leaves. My job was to correct the coastal soundings — the navy had finally admitted their charts were thirty years stale, which in naval time is roughly since yesterday — and to re-etch the marginal medallions, because the new Lord Treasurer wanted his crest where the old Lord Treasurer's crest was, politics being the only science practiced with total consistency.

It was tedious, holy work. I loved it the way you love anything that asks nothing of you but precision.

I was three-quarters through leaf one hundred and ninety — the northern provinces, all pale blues and patient hatching — when I found the pocket.

Every old atlas has them. A binder's slip, a folded scrap left behind by some apprentice a century dead, pressed flat between leaves until it became part of the book's body. Usually it's a receipt or a doodle of something unprintable. I'd found forty-one such scraps over the years and kept them in a tin labeled BINDER'S LEavings, which is the sort of joke that sustains a person through a career in government.

This one was different. This one crackled.

Not paper-crackle. Wax. I set down my pen — always set down your pen, ink waits for no discovery — and worked the thing free with a bone folder, easing it out of the gutter where it had been pasted flat against the binding itself. Not slipped in. *Pasted.* Someone had wanted this hidden well enough to glue it into the spine of a state atlas, inside the official copy, the master from which every other copy in the country was struck.

Which raised the obvious question: hidden from whom? An atlas is not read. An atlas is consulted, by people who already know what they expect to find. If you wanted to hide a document in a nation's collective memory, you could do worse than tuck it into the one book everyone trusted and nobody opened.

I unfolded it under the lamp.

It was a proclamation. I knew the format instantly — the heavy header, the double rule, the seal impression stamped in red wax gone brown with age. But the seal was wrong. Not the Privy Seal, not the Great Seal. A third seal I had never seen: a circle bisected by thirteen lines, and at the center, where a king's face should be, a small closed eye.

And the date. The date made my hands go still on the desk.

It was dated for the coming spring. Three months hence. A proclamation written in the future tense of law — *be it enacted, be it known* — sitting in the spine of an atlas like a knife laid on a pillow beside a sleeping man.

I read it twice. Then a third time, slowly, the way you reread a letter that has just rearranged your life, checking each word for mercy and finding none.

*By order of His Majesty, and in accordance with the Rite of Thirteen Moons, let there be proclaimed the Thirteenth Hunt.*

*Let it be known that within our borders there dwell persons bearing the Taint of Gift — who steal the memories of honest subjects, who wear the faces of the dead, who traffic in the souls of others as merchants traffic in cloth.*

*Let every such person present themselves at a Warden's station within ninety days of this proclamation to receive the Brand of Registry.*

*And let those who do not present themselves be sought, and taken, and —*

I stopped reading aloud in my head at that point. There is reading, and there is the moment when the words start reading you back. The clause that followed was short. Short clauses are the most frightening ones; long clauses mean someone argued. Nobody had argued over this.

The Thirteenth Hunt. I turned the phrase over. I had heard of hunts, plural, in the way you hear of old wars — the First through the Twelfth, a litany of purges scattered across three centuries, each one officially concluded, each one officially successful, each one followed, decades later, by the quiet reappearance of the thing supposedly exterminated. Children's rhymes still carried the bones of them. *Thirteen moons, thirteen fires, count your fingers, count your liars.* I had sung it skipping rope. We all had. That was the genius of it, I understood suddenly, standing alone at midnight in a room full of maps of everything except the truth: they didn't hide the hunts. They put them in nursery songs. Nothing buried that deep gets dug up.

But this hunt was different. The old ones had been reactive — sparked by scandals, by a gifted courtier exposed, by a famine blamed on someone's hex. This proclamation had no scandal attached. It had arithmetic. *In accordance with the Rite of Thirteen Moons.* As if the purge were astronomical now. As if the sky itself had been entered into evidence.

I looked up, absurdly, at the window, half-expecting to see the moon in question hanging there with a thirteenth shadow. What I saw instead was my own reflection superimposed on the capital's lights, and my own face doing something I didn't recognize.

Then I thought of Serei, and everything got very simple and very terrible.

* * *

My sister lives — lived, then — two streets from the Tanner's Canal, in a rooms-over-a-dyer's-shop that smelled permanently of indigo and boiled wool. She is four years younger than me and has never once let me forget it. She embroiders. She argues with landlords. She has her mother's laugh and our father's stubbornness and a habit, when she's concentrating, of touching things — doorframes, cups, the backs of hands — with two fingers, lightly, the way other people tap a pen.

I had noticed the touching years ago. I had explained it to myself as a nervous habit. You can explain almost anything as a nervous habit if you love the person doing it. That is the second thing they teach you in the cartographic office, though again, never aloud: a map is a lie you have agreed to keep telling.

Because there had been other things. The winter she was nine, she described our grandmother's kitchen to me — the blue tiles, the crooked shelf, the smell of salt and smoke — a kitchen that burned down before Serei was born, that she had never seen, that existed nowhere but in my own childhood memory. I had laughed and said, you dreamed it. She had looked at me with an expression I spent twenty years misreading as guilt, and said, "I'm sorry, Vess. It just came." And I had said, "Well, don't let it come at dinner," and we ate, and I filed the moment away in the same drawer where I kept the vanished census names and the white rectangles.

Serei had never stolen anything in her life. Except, apparently, with her fingertips. Except the memories that came to her unasked, leaking off the people around her like heat off a stove. She bore the Taint of Gift. She was exactly the person the sealed proclamation had been written for, in the future tense, in red wax, with a closed eye where a king's face should be.

I stood in that cold office holding a piece of paper and understood that I was holding two loyalties, and that they no longer fit in the same hand.

Here is what no one tells you about treason. It doesn't arrive with trumpets. It arrives with a bone folder and a bit of glued-in wax and the sudden, nauseating clarity that the beautiful thing you have served for eleven years has been using your beautiful work as a weapon, and that you helped sharpen it, leaf by leaf, border by border. Every map I had ever drawn was a list of places the Wardens could search. Every census overlay I maintained — and yes, we maintained census overlays, "for administrative accuracy" — was a register of addresses. The hunters would not need to find the gifted. The gifted were already mapped. By me. In copperplate. With decorative sea-monsters in the margins.

I sat down on my stool because my legs had opinions of their own, and I did what any trained professional does with unbearable information.

I checked it against the archive.

* * *

The reference vault is below the drafting floor, down a spiral stair worn concave by two hundred years of clerks' feet. It smells of vellum and camphor and, faintly, of the lamp oil that has soaked into everything since before anyone living was born. The keys hang on a board by the door, and the board is labeled, with the office's characteristic gift for euphemism, HISTORICAL SERIES.

Historical Series, shelf twelve: the survey records of the Salt Steppe.

Everyone knows the Steppe. It's the glass-colored waste east of the Verrik mountains, the one the school atlases show as a pale wash with the note UNPRODUCTIVE — SALINE. No towns marked. No roads. A century ago it had towns, and roads, and a name of its own that wasn't a description of its soil, and then one year the atlases came out changed, and the change was never explained, because the office does not explain. The office revises.

I pulled the oldest folio in the series. The comparison took me less than an hour, and I will spare you the details except for this: the erasure was thorough, but erasure is a craft like any other, and it leaves tool-marks. A road drawn and scraped and drawn again along a slightly different line. A river renamed twice. And on the earliest sheet, in faded sepia, a cluster of little squares where the later sheets showed nothing at all, annotated in a hand I recognized — everyone in the office recognizes it, it's on the founding plates — with a single word.

*Salhame.*

And underneath, smaller, in the same hand: *the Door of Salt lies at the ridge where the two moons cast one shadow.*

Two moons. Everyone knows there is one moon. The phrase sat there in sepia like a joke told a hundred years too early, waiting for the night I would look up from my lamp and understand it.

I copied the coordinates onto a scrap of my own, folded it small, and put it somewhere no inventory would ever record. Then I climbed back up to my table, where the sealed proclamation lay waiting under the lamplight with its closed eye, and I made the decision that split my life into before and after.

I would not report the finding. Discoveries in state documents went to the Surveyor-General, who went to the Privy Council, who went wherever proclamations are born. Eleven years of service bought me that much: nobody questioned what I chose to flag. So I flagged nothing. I smoothed the proclamation flat, and I pasted it back into the gutter of leaf one hundred and ninety with fresh paste, pressing it down until it was part of the book's body again, hidden in the one place in the kingdom guaranteed to survive every fire and flood — because the atlases are always saved first. Always. We save the maps before we save the people. I know. I helped decide the evacuation priority lists.

Then I blew out my lamp, and I walked home through the sleeping capital with my collar up, taking the long way, past the Tanner's Canal, past the dyer's shop with the dark windows above it where my sister slept with her dangerous fingers curled under her cheek.

Ninety days, the proclamation said, once it was proclaimed in spring. Ninety days for the gifted to brand themselves or be hunted. And the machinery that would do the hunting was already oiled and waiting — I had drawn half of it myself.

I stood across the canal from her window for a long time, doing arithmetic no atlas contained. One sister. One set of coordinates to a Door at the edge of a country the crown had spent a century pretending did not exist. One moonrise per month between now and the thirteenth turn.

Loyalty, I had learned in this office, is mostly a matter of scale. Draw the kingdom large enough and a single life disappears into the hatching, a rounding error, a village on the far bank of a moved river. Draw it small enough — one window, one sleeping girl, one scrap of paste-hidden paper warming in your coat against your heart — and the borders stop meaning anything at all.

I turned for home. Behind me the capital lay spread under its one obedient moon, mapped to the last chimney, every street a sentence I had helped write.

In three months, the hunters would come for the words. I intended to make sure that when they arrived, the most important name on their list would already be ink I had burned.

I just needed a map out. And there was only one kind of establishment in this country that traded in things that weren't supposed to exist.

They said the guild on the Glass Steppe would buy anything.
