## 3. What the Guild Buys

There is a particular silence that settles over the ledger room at midnight, and I have come to think of it as the sound of the guild digesting.

The day's acquisitions had been laid out on the weighing table by the time I descended from my garret: eleven vials in their velvet cradle, each stoppered with wax the color of old teeth, each labeled in the appraiser's neat hand. I lit the green lamp — the only light permitted near memory-stock, because ordinary flame makes recollections flicker, or so Corvane claims, and because green flatters nothing, which is the point. Under a green lamp a memory shows you exactly what it is: diminished, already dying, worth precisely what someone was desperate enough to pay for it.

I uncapped the first vial and held it over the open ledger.

*A soldier's courage, taken whole from a deserter of the Third Marches.* It rose out of the glass like steam off a winter pond — I could not feel it, mind you, not through the vial, that requires skin, and this is the one fact about me that has kept me alive — but I could *see* it, the way you can see heat shimmer without being burned by it. A grey-gold haze with something bright thrashing at its center. Courage looks different depending on who's selling. This man's courage had been small and stubborn, the kind that gets you through a flogging rather than a battle, and he'd sold it because his creditors had threatened to sell his daughter instead. The guild took the courage. The daughter stayed home. Everyone called it mercy.

I wrote: *Acquisition 4,417. One (1) courage, personal, mid-grade. Seller: name withheld per standard terms. Provenance: Third Marches garrison. Condition: intact, lightly worn.* And then, as always, the line I was hired to write and no one else could: *Residual value assessed.*

The residual value is the ghost in every entry. When you pull a memory out of a person, they don't lose just that one thing. They lose everything that touched it. Sell your wedding day, and you lose the courtship that led there, the reason you said yes, the shape of your own happiness. Sell your courage and you lose every morning you got up anyway. The guild prices memories high because the interest compounds invisibly — every buyer gets more than they paid for, every seller loses more than they sold. I am the only person in the building whose entire job is writing down how much was actually taken. Corvane calls it diligence. I have privately come to think of those lines as obituaries for things still walking around.

The widow came at dusk. That's typical; grief keeps business hours like a drunk keeps a schedule, which is to say it arrives whenever it likes and usually after dark. She was perhaps sixty, dressed in her best black, which had gone shiny at the elbows from years of wearing. She sold her husband's death — not the man, note, the *death*. She wanted to keep loving him. She just couldn't afford to keep grieving him, and grief, unlike love, can be extracted cleanly if you take the moment itself rather than the person.

The appraiser drew it out through her wrist while she sat very straight with her hands folded like a woman in prayer. What came up the tube into the vial was the color of deep water with iron in it. She wept the entire time without changing expression, which is a thing I've seen maybe forty times now and never gotten used to: tears moving down a face that has already forgotten what they're for.

*Acquisition 4,418,* I wrote. *One (1) bereavement, spousal, terminal event, high-grade intact.* Residual value: she would keep the love and lose the loss. She would remember being married and forget why she stopped setting two cups out. In six months she would stand in her kitchen holding a second cup and feel a hollowness with no edges, and some buyer in the capital would pay four hundred crowns to feel her husband die so vividly they'd need a week to recover.

I finished the entry. I blotted it. I told myself what I always tell myself, which is that the ledger does not buy or sell anything, the ledger only remembers, and somebody in this rotten house has to do the remembering honestly.

That used to be enough. Lately the sentence has started to taste like a lie rehearsed until it passes for truth.

* * *

The client with the daughter arrived past two in the morning, which should have been my first warning, because honest debtors sleep and desperate ones don't.

He gave his name as Merrick Voss, though I'd have bet the ledger itself it was false — too smooth, no burr on it, a name put on that morning like a coat. He was maybe fifty, a big man going soft, with the hands of someone who'd done physical work and then spent years doing something else he didn't want to talk about. He smelled faintly of river water and cheap tallow. His boots were good boots gone bad at the soles, resoled twice with the wrong leather.

"Debt," he said, when the intake clerk asked his business. "To the house."

Corvane ran a lending side to the guild that he kept off most books — mine included, though I read everything eventually, because paper cannot hide from a man who files it. Borrow against a memory, default, and the house collects. The collections were supposed to be voluntary surrenders. The word *voluntary* was doing work in that sentence that no word should ever be asked to do alone.

The clerk consulted a slim folio. "Voss. Yes. Principal outstanding: three hundred and eighty crowns, plus accrual." He looked up with the flat courtesy of a man reading someone's death warrant in recipe form. "Standard satisfaction is accepted tonight. The item to be collected is designated in your original instrument."

"I know what I signed." Voss's voice was steady. His hands were not. They were big hands, and he kept them flat on the counter like he was holding down a map in wind.

They took him into the extraction room. I followed, because acquisitions require the ledger-keeper's presence for valuation — that's the rule, rule nine, written in Corvane's own hand: *No memory enters stock unwitnessed.* Corvane believed in rules the way other men believe in gods: sincerely, selectively, and mostly when they benefited him.

The extraction room is the one place in the guild I hate. Everything in it is white — walls, ceiling, the padded chair — because white shows contamination, contamination ruins provenance, provenance is money. Under the white lamps the appraiser looked like a priest and Voss looked like a sacrifice, and nobody in the room would have appreciated the comparison, which somehow made it worse.

"The instrument specifies," the appraiser said, consulting the loan paper, "one (1) episodic memory: the subject's daughter. Face and presence. To be drawn at full depth."

"Not at full depth," Voss said quickly. "You can't— the instrument says the *memory*, it doesn't say all of it. Take the recent ones. Take last year. Take the birthday, take the—"

"The instrument specifies full depth," the appraiser repeated, and there it was, the whole guild in one sentence. The instrument specifies. Not cruelty. Specification.

Here is what full depth means, since I'm the one who writes the valuations: a memory isn't a coin, it's a well. You can draw a bucket or you can draw until the rope runs out. Full depth on a daughter's face means taking not just her face but the first time he saw it, the sound of her before she had words, every morning she'd appeared in his doorway, the accumulated weight of twenty years of looking at someone and being glad. What remains afterward is a man who knows intellectually that he has a daughter the way you know the capital has a king — true, distant, unfelt. He'll pass her in the street someday and feel nothing, and she'll know her father looked at her like a stranger, and neither of them will ever learn why.

Three hundred and eighty crowns. That's what a daughter costs when the house sets the price.

I stood by the wall with my ledger open and did my job. I watched the appraiser strap Voss's wrist to the armature and fit the drawing-cup over the vein. I watched Voss close his eyes and move his lips — praying, or saying her name, or both; with sellers it's usually both.

And here is where I must be honest, because the whole point of me, the whole *function*, is honesty of record, and if I lie even here then nothing I've written in fifteen years is worth the ink.

I moved.

Not much. The room was small and everyone's attention was on the armature, on the rising column of color in the glass — hers, whatever color she was, I never learned, the memory came up pale gold shot with brown like beer held to a window, warm and ordinary and unbearably specific. I stepped to the table where the day's filled vials waited in their cradle, and I let my bare hand pass across them the way a tired man steadies himself, palm down, two seconds, no more.

Skin. That's all it takes. Skin and wanting.

Eleven memories entered me at once and I will not pretend I chose it gracefully; it was like falling downstairs into other people. The deserter's stubborn little courage. The widow's iron-dark grief, which hit me hardest and last, a cold wave with a man's dying in it. And underneath, threaded through everything, the pale gold thread of the daughter — because the vial from the extraction room had already joined the cradle, and my hand had crossed *all* of them.

I kept my face stupid and my breathing even, the way you do. Fifteen years of practice: the gift doesn't show on the outside unless you let it. Inside, I was drowning politely in a dead man's courage and a living woman's grief and a child's laughter I had no right to.

Then I did the thing I have never done in fifteen years of keeping honest books.

I pressed my thumb, briefly, against the stopper of the daughter-vial, and I *pushed*.

Taking is easy. Taking is a door opening. Giving back — that's like pouring wine uphill, against everything the gift wants to do, and I've never once managed it, never practiced it, never dared. But I didn't try to give back the whole memory. Just a fragment. A splinter off the gold. Her voice, maybe. One morning. One instance of *her*, returned through the glass by touch, the way water finds a crack.

Whether any of it reached him I couldn't say. The science of it — if you can call what I have a science — ends at my own skin. I felt the fragment leave me like warmth leaving a cup. Where it went after that was between the vial, the vault, and whatever god keeps books for people like me.

* * *

The extraction took less than an hour. They always do; that's part of the horror. Twenty years of a daughter, drawn off in the time it takes to eat supper.

Voss walked out under his own power. That's the protocol — sellers leave upright or the neighbors talk. He paused at the intake desk while the clerk stamped his release, and I watched him from behind my ledger, pen moving on an entry I was no longer reading.

He stood very still. Then he frowned, the way a man frowns at a word on the tip of his tongue. He raised one hand halfway to his chest — to the place, I realized, where a person keeps the thing they can't find — and held it there.

"She liked," he said, to no one, to the clerk, to the lamp, "she liked it when it rained. I think she liked it when it rained."

The clerk, who processes forty sorrows a night, said, "Sign here, sir," and Voss signed, and the fragment — my fragment, stolen back from the house and smuggled inside his forgetting — went out the door with him into the dark, one small gold splinter of a girl who liked rain, saved from the fire like a single book carried from a burning library.

I wrote the final entry with a steady hand:

*Acquisition 4,419. One (1) familial memory, filial, full-depth extraction per instrument. Residual value: total. Seller retains knowledge of relation without affect. Note:* — and here my pen stopped, because the next word was going to be either *none* or *something*, and only one of those was true, and only one of those was safe.

I wrote *none.*

First falsified line in fifteen years of ledgers. I sat looking at it for a long time. It looked like any other line. That's the thing about treason; it's typeset exactly like duty.

* * *

I climbed to my garret as the sky went the grey of dishwater, carrying the green lamp because I didn't trust myself to walk up in the dark. I sat on my cot with the ledger of residuals — my private one, the true one, the one that would get me rendered down into stock if Corvane ever found it — and I added the night's weight to the running total I keep in the back.

The widow's grief. The deserter's courage. A girl who liked rain.

I thought about how the gift works, how it's supposed to work, how I'd spent half my life believing that touching memories made me the guild's finest appraiser and the other half believing it made me its filthiest accomplice, and how both were true and neither was the whole of it. Because here is what I felt when Voss said *she liked it when it rained* and walked out knowing it and not knowing why: I felt *him*. The joy came up through the fragment like heat through a floor — his joy, the joy of a man handed back one piece of his own child, and it passed through my hand into me on its way out of the world, and I sat on a cot in a guild garret at dawn crying over a stranger's happiness because I had caused it and because I would cause its opposite tomorrow, and the next night, and the next, for as long as I kept these books and kept this secret and kept telling myself the ledger only remembers.

Neutrality, it turns out, is not a position. It's a debt. And the house collects.

I closed the private ledger. Below the window, the Steppe was turning pink at the edges, salt flats catching the first light like the scales of something enormous asleep. Somewhere out there was a girl who would grow up with a father who looked at her like weather. Somewhere in the vault below me, in row nine, shelf three, the rest of her sat in glass waiting to be sold to some capital buyer who wanted to know what fatherhood felt like without paying for it.

I had touched the vials. I knew what I could do. And I understood, sitting there with my own guilt sharp enough to shave with, that the question was never again going to be whether the guild's trade was wrong. The question was how long I intended to keep being its steadiest hand.

The bell in the yard rang the hour. Six. Corvane's household stirred below — and with it, footsteps on the stairs, unhurried, familiar, stopping outside my door.

A knock. Two soft beats.

"Ledger-keeper," Corvane's voice said through the wood, pleasant as banked coals. "You're wanted downstairs. There's been a development regarding our records."

In fifteen years, Corvane had never once come to my door before seven.
