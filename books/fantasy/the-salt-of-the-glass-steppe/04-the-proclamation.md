## 4. The Proclamation

There are three ways to read a decree from the crown, and I have read all of them in my sixty-one years.

The first way is as a citizen: with dread. The second is as a merchant: with arithmetic. The third—the one I have come to prefer, the one that has kept the Glass Steppe Guild alive through four reigns and two purges—is as a man reading weather. A decree is a storm front. You do not argue with it. You do not stand in the open field and shake your fist at clouds like some idealist poet. You count your livestock, you mend your roof, and if the storm looks bad enough, you sell your neighbor's grain before the price collapses.

The proclamation arrived on a Tuesday, folded inside a shipment of salted herring from the coast, which was fitting, because it stank.

I knew what it was before I broke the seal. The wax was royal crimson, not guild grey, and no honest merchant uses crimson. I carried it up to my study above the ledger room, poured myself two fingers of plum brandy—medicinal; my hands have had a tremor since the winter of the double fever, though I confess the brandy does not seem to cure it—and sat by the window where the light of the thirteenth moon's waning quarter fell across the desk like spilled milk.

*By order of the Crown,* it began, in that calligraphic hand they train into their scribes until the letters look like little soldiers standing at attention. *In recognition of the contagion of unlawful gifts among the populace, His Majesty declares the Thirteenth Hunt. All persons found to bear such gifts shall present themselves for lawful branding within ninety days of this proclamation. Those who conceal themselves or others shall be subject to fire.*

Ninety days. Generous of them. The last hunt gave the provinces forty.

I read it twice more, because a good merchant reads a contract three times, and because there was a passage near the end I wanted to be sure of:

*Further, any house or association possessing knowledge of gifted persons—whether by record, confession, observation, or trade—shall deliver such knowledge to the Office of the Warden within thirty days. Compliance shall be compensated. Concealment shall be prosecuted.*

*Compensated.* There it was. Not "rewarded"—the crown does not reward, it compensates, the way you compensate a man whose ox you've requisitioned. But compensation meant coin, and coin meant the guild could live through another winter, another decade, another century. And *prosecuted* meant that if I did not sell, I would burn alongside the people in my ledgers.

You will forgive me—I hope someone, someday, will forgive me—for how quickly the arithmetic completed itself.

* * *

Here is what the ledgers contain, for those who imagine them to be mere account books.

The Glass Steppe Guild deals in stolen memories, bottled names, griefs sold by the ounce, courage decanted from dying soldiers into vials the size of a thumbnail. Every transaction passes through my office. Every client who sells us something impossible signs—or thumbs, or bleeds—a page in my registry. And every client who *buys*, every noblewoman purchasing her dead mother's voice, every general wanting the battle-memories of a man he executed, leaves behind a name and a want. Wants tell you things. A man who wants courage is afraid. A man who wants forgetting is guilty. And a surprising number of men and women in this country want gifts—want them bought, borrowed, tasted—and the crown would very much like to know who they are.

But more valuable than either buyers or sellers is a third category, one that exists only in the margins of my books, in the shorthand only I can read: the clients who came in *bearing* gifts without knowing their own worth. The widow who sold her grief and wept tears that glowed faintly in the dark of my office, weeping without knowing why the candles leaned toward her. The ferryman whose touch calmed storms. Children brought in by mothers who wanted the gift *removed*, as if it were a rotten tooth.

Those pages are a map of everyone the Thirteenth Hunt wants. And I hold the only copy.

I sat with the decree until the brandy was gone and the moonlight had crossed the whole width of the desk, and I told myself the story I am still telling myself, the one that goes: *This is survival. This is what a steward does. Corvane, you have fed these people's secrets to no one for thirty years; you have kept the guild clean and quiet and invisible, and now invisibility is a crime, and the choice is the ledgers or the guild, and the guild is three hundred souls under this roof who did nothing but buy and sell honestly—*

Honestly. There's a word that deserves its own brand.

* * *

The first page I sold was a test. My own.

A courier from the Office of the Warden came on Thursday—not a soldier, worse luck, but a clerk with soft hands and softer eyes, the kind of man who apologizes while he ruins you. He set a purse on my desk, heavy enough to make the wood creak, and said, "Master Corvane, the Warden Ilsette extends her compliments and her confidence that a house of your... discretion understands the times."

"I understand the times," I said, "the way a fisherman understands the tide."

"Then you'll understand that discretion, going forward, is best exercised in cooperation."

I gave him one page. Just one. The ferryman, dead eleven years now, his gift long since decayed in whatever vial holds it. Useless information, verifiably useless—I made sure the death record sat prominently in the same file—and worth a fortune precisely because it proved compliance without costing anyone anything. A merchant's masterpiece: sell the buyer a thing he cannot check, at a price that buys his trust.

Ilsette's clerk counted the purse out again in front of me, nodded, and left. That night I slept badly, which surprised me. I had expected to sleep like a stone. Instead I lay awake listening to the guild breathe around me—the night-clerks in the scriptorium, the vial-room boys laughing over dice somewhere below, old Marta the bottler singing a Steppe lullaby to herself as she sealed jars, a song in a language the crown erased off the maps a hundred years ago and never thought to erase off the tongues of grandmothers.

Three hundred souls. I kept counting them like coins. It helped. It almost helped.

* * *

Over the following fortnight I built the machine, because guilt is easier to carry when it is efficient.

A second registry—unnumbered, unwitnessed, written in my own hand alone—began to grow in a locked drawer beneath my strongest wards. Page by page, I copied out the names of the gifted: the widow with the glowing grief. The midwife whose hands could find pain and pull it out like splinters. A boy in the salt-flats settlements who dreamed tomorrow's weather. Eleven names the first week. Nineteen the second. Each one priced, each one sold, each payment logged in the official books as "archive maintenance fees," because even treason should balance against the quarterly ledger.

I told myself: *only adults. Only those whose gifts are known already in their villages—what use hiding what every neighbor whispers about? Only those who can run, who have coin, who have warning enough.* I told myself the compensation would buy the guild's silence later, when the hunts reached the Steppe proper. I told myself that a burned guild shelters no one.

All true. All lies. Both at once—that is the special craft of an old man's conscience.

And Silas watched me do it.

I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger heaven keeps: Silas Kavanagh, my clerk of nine years, sat across from me every morning with their ink-stained fingers and their careful, unremarkable face, and entered my payments into the books, and said nothing. I caught them looking at the drawer once. Only once. They looked away the way clerks do, professionally, completely, and I chose—*chose*—to believe that look was boredom.

You hire a person to keep accounts, and after nine years you forget that keeping accounts means keeping track of everything. Including you.

* * *

It was on the twenty-second day after the decree that I noticed the missing page.

I had gone down to the ledger room at dusk—my knees objecting to the stairs, my conscience objecting to everything else—to verify the registry against my copies before the next sale. The ledger room is the heart of this house: iron shelves, salt-warded doors, air so thick with preservation-charms that it tastes of copper and old rain. Silas had finished the evening entries and gone; the lamps were banked low, the way they leave them.

I pulled the Registry of Acquisitions, volume thirty-eight, and ran my thumb down the index to the Vellin transaction—the widow, entry 2211, the first name I'd copied for the crown. I wanted to see exactly what evidence sat against her beyond my shorthand.

The page was gone.

Not torn—cut, close and clean along the spine, by someone who knew binding and knew blades. In its place, pasted flat, a replacement leaf aged to match, bearing a forged entry in a fair imitation of my own clerk-hand: *Entry 2211 — transaction voided. Client declined sale. No acquisition.*

Someone had un-sold the widow Vellin. Someone had reached into thirty years of my records and quietly, competently, committed forgery against *me*—against the guild, against the crown's purchase, against everything—and done it so neatly I might not have found it for months if I hadn't come hunting that specific page.

I stood in the cold of that room for a long time, holding volume thirty-eight, feeling the shape of the trap I had built change around me.

Because here is the arithmetic I had missed: if a page can vanish, then my sales are built on sand. If Silas—or whoever—can forge one entry, the Warden's verification of any future purchase could unravel. Worse: if someone inside my walls is protecting the gifted, that someone knows about the drawer, or soon will, and every name I sell makes that someone more dangerous—to the crown, yes, but far more immediately, to *me*. A traitor in the archive is a blade lying flat on a table. Invisible until you reach across it.

Or perhaps—this thought arrived late, unwelcome, wearing my grandmother's disapproving frown—perhaps the page was not protection at all. Perhaps it was a message. *We see you, old man. We know what you're doing.*

I locked volume thirty-eight back in its case. I climbed the stairs slowly, one hand on the rail, an old merchant climbing toward his bed with the day's accounts unsettled, and I passed Silas in the corridor with a lamp, going the other way, toward the ledger room, no doubt, to fetch some forgotten pen.

"Late work?" I asked. My voice was perfectly level. Sixty-one years of practice.

"The Aldous appraisal needed closing," Silas said, calm as still water. "Goodnight, Guildmaster."

They passed me. The lamp threw their shadow long down the wall ahead of them, and I watched it go, and for the first time in nine years I looked at my clerk the way a merchant looks at a contract he suspects has been altered—hunting the small print, the changed figure, the cut page.

Silas Kavanagh, walking away from me down that corridor, gave me nothing. No tremor. No glance. Nothing but competence, and the smell of lamp oil, and the faintest trace—though the ward-scrubbed air of this house should permit no such thing—the faintest scent of salt.

Salt, on a night with no wind, from a direction where there is no sea.

I stood in my corridor, an old man with a full strongbox and a hollow chest, and understood at last what kind of house I had been living in. Not a sanctuary. Not a fortress. A trap with very good shelving—and I had spent a fortnight baiting it with other people's names, never asking who else was stocking the larder.

Tomorrow I would sell nineteen more pages. Tonight, I went to my study and took out the decree and read, for the fourth time, the clause I had skimmed past in my eagerness to survive:

*Any servant of the crown found harboring or abetting a gifted person shall be tried as gifted.*

Tried *as* gifted. Not merely punished beside them—tried *as* them. As if aiding the marked made you one of them. As if mercy were contagious.

I laughed. I will admit that. I sat in my chair with the plum brandy and laughed until the tremor in my hands shook the glass, because the crown had finally written a law I understood completely, and it described, with perfect bureaucratic precision, the exact position I now occupied: a man selling out the gifted while sheltering one under his own roof, whether he wished to or not.

Whether he wished to or not.

I drained the glass. Below me, faintly, I heard the ledger room door open and close—one soft click, careful as a cut page—and the sound of a pen beginning to move.
