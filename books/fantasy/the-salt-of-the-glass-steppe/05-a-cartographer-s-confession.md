## 5. A Cartographer's Confession

There is a particular smell to burning vellum that no one warns you about in the Cartographer's Guild. Not smoke, exactly—smoke is honest. This is something sweeter and more obscene, like roasting a thing that was once alive and still remembers it.

I learned this at three o'clock in the morning, crouched over a brazier in my own office, feeding the census roll into the flames one name at a time so the fire wouldn't spit and wake the night clerks two doors down.

*Marisol Vane. Marisol Vane. Marisol Vane.*

My sister's name, entered eleven times across eleven districts because Marisol had never in her life stayed where she was put, had to be unmade eleven times. I'd chosen the order carefully: the oldest entries first, so that if anyone checked—and someone always checks—they'd find the most recent record intact for just long enough to assume the earlier ones were transcription errors. Cartography runs on the comfortable assumption that mistakes are old and corrections are new. I was counting on it the way a drowning woman counts on driftwood.

The third copy curled and blackened. My sister's name became a curl of ash shaped, briefly, like a bird, before it collapsed.

"You're doing it wrong," said a voice from my doorway, and I nearly went into the brazier after her name.

It was Pellam, the junior ink-mixer, seventeen years old, chronically under-slept, and holding two cups of chicory he was apparently delivering at an hour no sane person delivers chicory.

"Pellam." My voice came out admirably level, given that my heart was attempting to exit through my ribs. "It's three in the morning."

"Couldn't sleep either." He set both cups on my desk, which meant he now had a reason to linger, which meant I had a problem. His eyes went to the brazier, then to the roll in my hands, then back to me with the terrible clarity of the young. "That's a census page."

"It's a misprint."

"Misprints don't get burned at three in the morning by the Royal Cartographer herself. Misprints go in the waste bin." He picked up one cup of chicory and drank it, watching me over the rim. "You should crumple the vellum first. Loosens the fibers. It burns faster and doesn't smell."

I stared at him. He shrugged, the universal gesture of a boy who has grown up poor in a capital city and therefore knows how fires work.

"I saw nothing," he added. "Obviously. I brought you chicory and left. That's what happened here."

"Pellam—"

"My cousin's got the gift," he said, conversationally, terribly. "Little thing. She can make bread rise just by humming at it. We don't tell anyone. You'd be surprised how many people don't tell anyone." He set down the cup, gave me a small bow that managed to be both mocking and sincere, and walked out.

I sat there for a long moment with a half-burned census roll and a full cup of chicory going cold, revising every estimate I'd ever made of the capital's population. Then I crumpled the remaining vellum the way he'd suggested, and fed it to the fire, and it went quickly, cleanly, without smell, and I chose not to think about what it meant that the boy had known exactly how to destroy evidence of a gifted person's existence.

* * *

Here is what they don't put on maps: the blank spaces aren't empty. They're decisions.

Every cartographer learns this in her first year, usually from a master who says it casually, the way you'd mention the weather. *The crown doesn't pay us to draw what's there. It pays us to draw what's useful.* And then the master taps the eastern edge of the kingdom, past the Salt Steppe, where the parchment goes pristine and white, and doesn't explain further, because further explanation would be treason and everyone has chores waiting at home.

A century ago, the Steppe had been a country. I knew this the way I knew the names of dead kings—from documents I wasn't supposed to find interesting. The old atlases in the restricted vault showed coastlines and roads and a place called the Door of Salt, drawn once, in ink that had gone brown with age, and then never again. Somewhere around the reign of Aldric the Tidy—the historians' nickname, not mine; mine was less printable—an edict went out. Borders redrawn. Roads erased. Names struck. The country didn't fall. It was *edited*.

And I had spent nine years redrawing the edit. Every season, a fresh survey of the eastern flats, confirming nothing there, nothing there, nothing there. I'd won commendations for the elegance of my nothing. There's a special kind of shame in being decorated for your precision at erasing people.

I hadn't thought much about it, before. That's the confession part. People imagine traitors are made in dramatic moments—torches, speeches, betrayals witnessed through keyholes. Mostly they're made slowly, in well-lit rooms, by people doing their jobs. I had done my job beautifully for nine years, and the job was making a country stop existing, and I had slept fine.

Then, four days ago, I'd been sent to redraw the master atlas of the eastern reaches—a ceremonial honor, the kind of tedium they give you when you've earned a rest from real work. And tucked into the binding of the 1742 edition, sewn beneath the spine where only someone re-sewing the whole volume would ever find it, was a loose sheet in a hand I didn't recognize.

It wasn't a map, exactly. It was a *correction*. Survey notes. Bearings. A route across the glass flats marked with distances and hazards, written by someone who had clearly walked them, ending at a ridge line annotated in a cramped, urgent script:

*Door of Salt — opens at the thirteenth turn of the thirteenth moon. Seals after. Do not be late.*

And beneath that, smaller, as if the writer had hesitated:

*If you found this, the Hunt has started again. Run.*

I had sat in the archive with that paper in my hands while the dust settled around me, and I had thought of my sister.

* * *

Because here's the other thing, the thing underneath the thing. Marisol has the gift. Has had it since she was six, when she told our mother, matter-of-factly, that she could taste what people were feeling—"like soup," she'd said, "everyone's a different soup"—and our mother had gone white as unbleached linen and made her promise never to say it again, not even as a joke, not even alone, and Marisol had kept that promise for twenty years the way she kept almost nothing else.

Tasting feelings isn't the dangerous gift. It isn't lifting memories or lighting candles with a look. But the Thirteenth Hunt doesn't distinguish. The last proclamation—sealed, archived, and dutifully catalogued by me, God help me, eight years ago, filed under Proclamations, Royal, Suppressed—had been specific on this point: *all manifestations of the unlicensed gift, however minor.* Branding for confession. Fire for refusal. The crown had been patient for a hundred years, letting the fear compost quietly, and now the garden was ready.

I'd known, when I read the hidden note, that the Hunt was coming. Everyone in the archives knows; we file the paperwork of persecution the way other people file tax receipts. But knowing a flood is coming and watching it take your street are different animals entirely.

So: the census rolls. Eleven entries. One sister, currently living under her fourth false name in a boarding house in the Tanner's Quarter, expecting me for supper on Thursday.

There would be no Thursday.

* * *

By dawn I had done three things I could not undo.

First: the census. Eleven burns, plus the index card, plus the cross-reference in the district registry, which required picking a lock I'd sworn an oath to protect, which tells you something about how oaths fare against sisters.

Second: the letter. I wrote to Marisol in the cipher we'd invented as children—we'd had a governess who read our correspondence, and we'd hated her with a unity we never achieved over anything else—telling her to leave the city tonight, telling her the route, telling her where to meet me. Three days east, at the last waystation before the flats, the one with the crooked sign. I paid a smuggler I trusted slightly more than the post to carry it, and watched him disappear into the pre-dawn fog, and felt the peculiar vertigo of having committed yourself to a plan that now exists outside your own two hands.

Third: I stole from the crown.

Not gold—gold is heavy and traceable and besides, I'd never been good at spending money, a failing my sister mocked relentlessly. I stole paper. The correction sheet went inside my coat lining, flat against my ribs like a second heartbeat. And from the restricted vault, while signing out materials I legitimately needed, I lifted two things more: a coastal chart of the western shallows, because if the Steppe failed me I wanted to know what lay beyond it, and—this is the part I'm least proud of, or proudest of, I haven't decided—my own resignation papers, pre-dated, forged with my superior's signature, which I have gotten very good at forging, because forging signatures is ninety percent of cartography anyway.

Let the record show, whenever some future clerk finds it, that Wren Okonkwo—that Vespera Ilmaari, Royal Cartographer, First Class—resigned her commission with regret, citing ill health. The ill health is real. It began the moment I decided to become a criminal, and it presents as a permanent cold feeling behind the sternum.

* * *

They noticed by noon.

Of course they noticed. The census registry runs reconciliation audits every twelve hours—it was designed, I now understood with the clarity of the freshly damned, precisely to catch people like me, except the designers assumed the thieves would be outsiders. Nobody builds locks against the locksmith.

I was in the map room, ostensibly correcting a river's course, actually standing very still and listening to the two junior clerks whisper by the door, when I heard the word "discrepancy" and understood that my morning had a deadline now.

Here is the thing about fleeing the capital: it's easy. Cities are built for leaving—that's why they have gates. What's hard is leaving without looking like you're leaving. So I didn't pack. I wore my survey coat, the one with the deep pockets, carried my field satchel the way I carried it every day, and walked out through the map room's side door, past the guards who nodded to me because I was the Royal Cartographer and Royal Cartographers belong everywhere, down the servants' stair, through the records annex, and out into the coal-smoke afternoon through a door I'd propped with a wedge of oak three weeks ago, on a day I'd told myself I was being paranoid.

Paranoia, it turns out, is just foresight that hasn't been vindicated yet. Mine was vindicated at approximately four o'clock, when I looked back from the top of the eastern road and saw riders at the gate. Not many. Four. The crown doesn't send armies after one woman; it sends four quiet men with a warrant and a strong opinion about rope.

They'd ride to my office first. Find the cold brazier, maybe, if they were clever—the ash of a census roll is distinctive if you know vellum, and the men they send for cartographers are usually clever. Then the vault log, showing what I'd signed out. Then the forgery, which would insult them. Then the hunt proper.

Good, I remember thinking, walking faster. Be insulted. Insulted men ride badly.

* * *

The eastern road is three days of farmland pretending nothing is wrong, and I spent all of it rehearsing conversations with my sister.

In the first rehearsal, Marisol laughed at me for the drama of it all—burned records, stolen charts, a flight into legend—and packed one bag with infuriating calm and came with me because she trusted me, because she'd always trusted me, because trust between siblings is a debt you incur at birth and spend your whole life repaying in installments.

In the second rehearsal, Marisol was frightened and angry, and asked why I hadn't warned her sooner, and I had no good answer, because the true answer—I didn't believe it would happen until it was happening—is the answer of every person who watches a catastrophe arrive on schedule.

In the third rehearsal, she didn't come at all. She'd already run. She'd smelled the soup of the city changing, as she'd say, and left before my letter arrived, and I stood in an empty waystation holding a piece of paper that mattered too late.

The waystation with the crooked sign appeared on the evening of the third day exactly as advertised, its sign hanging at a drunken angle, its stable smelling of hay and horse and the particular despair of places that exist solely so travelers can briefly stop being in motion.

She wasn't there.

I checked the common room. The stable. The well. I stood in the yard with dusk coming down and the wind off the flats beginning to carry salt, and I let myself feel, for exactly sixty seconds, the full weight of the third rehearsal.

Then I saw the stable boy watching me, and something about the quality of his watching—too interested, too quick to look away—made me walk over and ask, in the bored voice of a government official, whether a woman had come through asking after me.

"Left this," he said, and handed me a scrap of paper like it was nothing, like boys handed scraps of paper to strangers every day.

The cipher. Marisol's hand, disguised as a laundry list. *Saw riders on the west road two days ago. Not yours—crown colors. Going ahead on foot along the flats. The salt hides footprints. Meet where the old song says. You know the one. Don't be stupid. —M*

The old song. Our grandmother's lullaby, the one about walking where the water went, which our mother sang without thinking and which I had catalogued years ago, professionally, compulsively, as containing bearings that matched nothing on any map I was allowed to draw.

Marisol, who had never stayed where she was put, was already on the Steppe.

I stood there holding the note while the stable boy pretended enormous interest in a bucket, and the salt wind came off the glass flats in long low breaths, and somewhere behind me, three days west, four insulted men were reading my forged resignation and getting back on their horses.

Everyone knows the rumors about the Steppe. Even the erased countries leave shadows. There's a trade house out on the glass, the stories say—a guild that buys anything. Courage. Grief. Names. Memories. Things that can't be weighed and things that weigh too much. Sensible people call it a fable. But sensible people also believed the eastern reaches were empty, and I had personally, elegantly, for nine years, made sure they believed it.

Fables are just maps nobody's finished drawing.

I went inside, bought a meal I couldn't taste, slept four hours on a bench with the correction sheet under my coat and my hand on my knife, and at first light I walked east onto the salt, following a lullaby, toward a guild that bought anything, praying to every erased god of an edited country that rumor was cheaper than truth and I could afford it anyway.

Behind me, faint on the western road, I heard hoofbeats.

I did not look back. Looking back is for people whose plans are going well.

I walked faster.
