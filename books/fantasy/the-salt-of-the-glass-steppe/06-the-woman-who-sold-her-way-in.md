## 6. The Woman Who Sold Her Way In

The woman came in at the hour when the guild's lamps burn lowest and the ledgers smell most like money, which is to say she came in like trouble dressed as a client. I knew it before she said a word. Trouble always has good boots.

She stood in the receiving room with her travel cloak still dusty from the road and her hands folded with the particular patience of someone who has rehearsed this moment on the way down from the capital. Her eyes went around the room once—quick, professional, cataloguing exits—and then fixed on me with the pleasant blankness of a woman deciding how much of me to believe.

"I'm told you buy anything," she said.

"Anything," I agreed. "Though we pay better for some things than others."

"And what do you pay for maps?"

That was not the opening line I'd expected. Most clients lead with grief or greed; she was leading with geography. I set down my pen—the ledger open before me to Tuesday's acquisitions, a cooper's fear of deep water, three vials' worth—and gave her my best approximation of a man who has nothing to hide.

"Depends on the map," I said. "Road maps are common. Coastlines are dearer. Maps of places that don't exist—" I let the pause sit there, fat and inviting, "—those depend entirely on who's asking."

Something moved behind her eyes. Recognition, maybe. Or the pleasure of a card player watching another card player shuffle.

"Vespera," she said, offering no surname, which told me she had one and disliked it. "I've come a long way."

"They usually have." I rose and lit the second lamp, because I wanted to see her face properly and because lamplight makes liars look sincere, including mine. Up close she had the wind-scoured look of someone who'd ridden hard for days and slept badly, and under that, something more interesting: the specific tension of a woman carrying something she couldn't afford to be caught with. "Silas Kavanagh. Guild record-keeper. If you're selling, I appraise. If you're buying, I quote. If you're neither—" I smiled, "—you should know Corvane doesn't waste lamp oil on sightseers."

"I'm selling." She reached into her cloak and drew out a leather case, and her hand hesitated over the clasp for exactly one heartbeat longer than carelessness would explain. Then she opened it.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a piece of a map. Not a whole map—a fragment, roughly the size of two spread hands, its edges ragged where it had been cut or torn from something larger. The paper was old, heavy stock, the kind the royal cartography office used back when the crown still bothered surveying things honestly. And across its face ran lines of ink so fine they looked less drawn than grown, the way frost grows on glass.

I looked at the fragment. And then I stopped breathing, briefly, in the way one does when a stranger says your grandmother's name in a crowded market.

* * *

Let me tell you what I saw, because I need to be precise about this even now, especially now.

The fragment showed a coastline—but not our coastline. Our coast, the one every charted atlas agrees on, runs smooth as a closed fist along the western edge of the kingdom. This coastline was broken. It showed a bay shaped like a cupped hand, a river delta forked like a heron's foot, a range of low hills marked with a symbol I had only ever seen in one place: the margins of my grandmother's embroidery, stitched into the hems of her aprons in thread the color of rust, while she hummed a song about salt and shadows and told me it meant nothing, *nini, kichwa changu*, it means nothing, little head, go to sleep.

Across the center of the fragment, in faded cartographer's ink, ran a single word, half effaced as though someone had begun scraping it away and lost their nerve:

**SAL—**

Salt. Salt Steppe. Salt Country. Whatever the rest of it had been before the erasure crews got to work a hundred years ago and unmade a nation with pen knives and fresh vellum.

Every official map in the kingdom shows glass flats there. Empty ones. The Glass Steppe, they call it—not because anyone named it, but because the surveys report nothing but cracked silica pan to the horizon, worthless, waterless, *empty*. A country deleted. Not conquered—that would have required admitting it existed—but erased, the way you erase a debt, the way the guild erases a memory when the client pays enough: cleanly, professionally, leaving only smooth paper where a life used to be.

And here was a piece of the original, carried into my guildhall by a woman with capital dust on her boots and a liar's excellent boots.

"Well?" she said.

"It's a fake," I said.

"No, it isn't."

"It's a very good fake. The paper's right. The ink oxidation is right. Whoever made it knew their trade." I turned it toward the lamp without touching the surface again—I had already let my fingertip graze the corner during the unwrapping, an accident I'd arranged to look accidental, and gotten nothing, which was itself informative. Memory-ink resists the touch of those it hasn't been fed by, sometimes. Or the map simply held no memories, which would make it ordinary paper, which would make it worthless, which would make everything simpler. "But it's a fake."

"You didn't even look properly."

"I looked exactly properly. That's my trade." I slid the fragment back toward her. "Come back when you have something real. Grief sells well this time of year. Courage, if you have any. We pay scandalously little for nostalgia."

She didn't reach for it. She sat down instead, uninvited, in the client's chair, and laced her fingers, and looked at me with the expression of a woman revising an estimate upward.

"You touched the corner," she said. "When you unfolded it. You think I didn't see?"

My stomach performed a small, controlled maneuver, the kind of thing you train it to do over years of keeping your gift a secret in a house that trades in gifts. "I handle every acquisition. Hygiene of commerce."

"That's not what I mean, and you know it's not what I mean." She leaned forward. "You flinched. Just barely. Right here—" she tapped the corner of the fragment, "—and then you covered it by turning it to the light. I spent eleven years drawing maps for men who read faces the way you read ledgers. You don't survive that by missing flinches."

"The light was better."

"Silas Kavanagh." She said my name like she was checking its weight against coins. "I rode nine days to get here. I burned things I can't unburn to get here. I am not leaving because the clerk is frightened."

"I'm not frightened."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "You're terrified. It's just very well organized."

* * *

Here is what I should have done: called for Hesk, the doorwarden, and had her escorted out. Told Corvane a woman came asking after fantasy geography, watched him make his small cold assessment, filed the evening in the ledger under *wasted lamp oil*. Gone to bed. Woken the next morning still invisible, still safe, still the man nobody looks at twice because he is only the pen behind the guild's fortune, not the fortune itself.

Here is what I actually did: I looked at the word **SAL—**, and I thought of my grandmother's hands in the rust-colored thread, and I heard the lullaby start up in the back of my skull where it has lived, patient as a taproot, for thirty years. And I heard myself say:

"The receiving room has thin walls. My office doesn't."

Her eyebrows rose a fraction. "Is that an invitation?"

"It's a professional courtesy. Corvane will want to know everything you bring us eventually. But he's at the weigh station until tomorrow night, and—" I paused, choosing the lie with a craftsman's care, "—and some acquisitions lose value when too many people look at them. If your fragment is genuine, the fewer witnesses between you and payment, the better your price."

It was a good lie. It had a flaw built into it, the way all my best lies do, because a flawless story is suspicious and a slightly greedy one is believable. Greed explains a great deal of human behavior convincingly, which is fortunate, since it explains so much of it actually.

She gathered the fragment, and we walked down the corridor together, past the acquisition vaults with their triple locks, past the shelf where the bottled names sleep in their felt cradles, and I was aware of her awareness of all of it—the quick flick of her eyes reading the guild the way she'd read my face—and I found, to my mild alarm, that I didn't entirely mind being read.

In my office, I shut the door, and did not light a lamp, because moonlight through the salt-glass window was enough, and because darkness makes confessions cheaper.

"Now," I said. "Tell me who you are, what that fragment is, and why a woman with a capital accent is selling national secrets to a trade house in the middle of nowhere. And before you construct your answer—know that I will check it. I have ways of checking that you wouldn't believe."

"Oh, I'd believe them," Vespera said. "I've heard rumors about this place. About people here who can take a memory straight out of your head with a touch." She said it lightly, testingly, the way you toss a stone onto ice to hear how thick it is.

"Rumors are the guild's best advertising," I said. "They keep the prices up. Answer the question."

So she answered it. And I will give her this: she lied to me only twice, and both times about things that didn't matter, and by the end she'd stopped lying altogether, which in my experience is rarer than honesty and considerably more valuable.

She had been the royal cartographer. Principal chartist to the Office of Roads and Boundaries—she said the title the way you spit out a pit. Eleven years drawing the kingdom truer and larger, and prouder of it than she admitted, until the month she was assigned to redraw the master atlas for the new reign and found, sewn into the binding of the old one, a supplement she was never meant to see. The proclamation. The Thirteenth Hunt. The order that every bearer of the gift be registered, branded, and—where registration proved inconvenient—burned. With appendices. Census extracts. Lists.

"One of the names on the list is my sister," she said. Her voice stayed level the whole time, which is how I knew what it was costing her. "Sera. She lights candles with her fingertips when she's sad. She has done it since she was four. My mother taught her to hide it before she taught her letters." A breath. "The census lists her as *gifted, unlocated*. Unlocated. As if she were a coin dropped behind a cushion."

"And you took the atlas fragment."

"I took the fragment because the fragment is the only surviving piece of the pre-erasure survey of the Salt Steppe. It was misfiled a century ago—filed under *K* for a surveyor's name instead of under *S*, and the erasure crews worked alphabetically, and they ran out of time, or nerve, or budget. It has been sitting in a drawer in the Office of Roads for a hundred years waiting for someone lazy enough to misfile it and honest enough to steal it." She almost smiled. "I was both."

"Why bring it here? Why not run? Take your sister and ride west past the border garrisons and never look back?"

"Because there is no west." She laid the fragment flat on my desk and turned it so the broken coastline faced me. "Look at it. Look at the shape of the bay. Now think about every map you've ever seen of the western reaches. The coast doesn't match. It can't match. Because the coast everyone charts isn't the coast—it's the edge of the erasure. The kingdom didn't conquer the Salt Country. It redrew itself to exclude it. The borders were moved *on paper* and the paper is the law, and everything beyond the paper—" she tapped the fragment, "—is unmapped. Ungoverned. Off the books. Which means it's the only place in the world where the Thirteenth Hunt can't follow. No jurisdiction. No roads. No names on any list, because officially there's no there there."

I looked at the scrap of hundred-year-old vellum, at the heron's-foot delta, at the hills my grandmother stitched in rust-red thread, and I did the arithmetic I had been avoiding since she walked in.

"You want the Door of Salt," I said.

"I want the Door of Salt," she agreed. "And rumor says the Glass Steppe Guild buys anything, knows everything, and has been quietly trading across the flats for decades despite them being officially empty. Rumor also says the guild keeps records going back further than the crown's own archives. So." She spread her hands. "Sell me the way through. Name any price."

* * *

Now. Here is the difficulty, and I want it recorded accurately, whoever ends up reading this account—if anyone does, which is a wager I wouldn't take.

The honest answer was: I don't know the way through. The guild deals in the Steppe's edges, its smuggler tracks, its weigh stations and salt roads. The Door itself is a story grandmothers tell. My grandmother told it. *The Door opens for those who carry the country inside them*, she sang, right there in the lullaby I thought meant nothing, and I had assumed it was the usual grandmother nonsense, the same genre as the embroidery.

The profitable answer was: sell her to Corvane. A disgraced principal chartist with stolen crown property and knowledge of the Hunt's proclamation? Corvane would pay me a finder's fee that would buy my independence twice over. He might even protect her—Corvane protects assets; it's his religion—or he might sell her name straight to the hunters, because the times were changing and I'd seen the new ledgers appearing in his office, ledgers with different columns than ours, columns like *locatable* and *value to crown*. Either way, it wasn't my problem, and I would be richer and safer for having made it his.

The answer I actually gave was none of these, because I am apparently the sort of fool who, offered safety and profit, chooses a third thing with no name yet.

"Here's my counteroffer," I said. "You don't sell me the fragment. You don't sell me anything. Corvane comes back tomorrow night. When he does, you are a client who came to sell a minor curiosity—an old coastal chart, damaged, dubious provenance, worth perhaps forty crowns, which I will pay you from my own purse to keep the fiction clean. He'll glance at it, dismiss it, dismiss you. He's distracted lately. He's counting things he shouldn't be counting." I watched her absorb that. "Meanwhile, the fragment stays out of the guild records entirely. And I will find out everything the guild knows about the Door of Salt. Every smuggler's song, every weigh-station rumor, every entry in ninety years of ledgers. In exchange—"

"In exchange?"

"In exchange, when you find your door, I'm on the other side of it with you. Passage for two." I heard myself say it and marveled slightly, the way you marvel at your own signature when you're very tired. "I have my reasons. They're written in rust-colored thread, if that means anything to you."

Something crossed her face then—not trust, nothing so generous, but recognition. One liar clocking another, and finding the workmanship sound.

"It means," she said slowly, "that you know more about the Salt Country than a guild clerk should. It means you're hiding from something too. And it means this bargain is either the smartest thing either of us has ever done or the last." She put out her hand. "No contracts. No witnesses. If you betray me, Silas Kavanagh, I will draw you so precisely into the margin of a map that hunters will find you by candlelight."

"If you betray me, Vespera-no-surname, I won't need to lift a finger. The crown will do it for me. All I'll do is stop standing in the way."

We shook on it. Her grip was dry and strong and did not flinch, and neither did mine, and if either of our pulses was hammering, we were both too well-trained to mention it.

She left by the salt-door before dawn, fragment hidden in the false bottom of her saddlebag, forty crowns lighter in my purse and heavier in my chest. I watched her ride east toward the flats until the thirteenth moon's early light took her, and then I went back inside, sat at my desk, and opened the ledger to a fresh page.

For a long time I wrote nothing. Then, in the narrow margin where I record things that never officially happened, I entered a single line:

*One map, unsold. One bargain, unrecorded. Two liars, solvent.*

Then I blew out the lamp, and went to find out what my grandmother's lullaby had been trying to tell me for thirty years—and whether, somewhere in ninety years of guild ledgers, Corvane had already sold the Door of Salt to somebody willing to kill for it.
