## 8. Corvane's Price

There is a particular sound a guildhouse makes at three in the morning when it knows something you don't.

I'd been keeping the ledgers long enough to know every voice the Salt House had. The settling of the salt-beam floors as the night cold worked its way in. The tick of the memory-jars cooling on their racks in the vault below, a sound like a thousand tiny clocks disagreeing about the hour. The creak of old Maren's boots two floors up, because Maren had never in forty years learned to sleep through the hour of her own regrets.

But this was new. A silence with weight to it. The kind of silence that has been arranged.

I was three lines into recording the week's acquisitions—a cooper's fear of deep water, sold to pay a brewing debt; half a soldier's courage, the left half, which he swore was the braver half anyway—when I heard the door of the ledger room open without knocking.

Nobody opened that door without knocking. Not because it was locked. Because the rules said knock, and the rules were the only religion the guild had ever practiced with any devotion.

Guildmaster Corvane stood in the doorway holding a candle, and behind him the corridor was dark in a way that suggested he'd dismissed the night watch on his way down. He was not a large man. He had never needed to be. He occupied space the way ink occupies paper—completely, and leaving no part of himself unaccounted for.

"Silas." My name in his mouth always sounded like an entry in a ledger. Neat. Filed. Owed.

"Guildmaster." I set down my pen with the careful slowness of a man who has nothing to hide and would very much like the universe to notice how carefully he's doing it. "The week's acquisitions are nearly—"

"I didn't come for the acquisitions."

He came in. He closed the door behind him, which was worse than the not-knocking. Then he did something I had never once seen him do in eleven years: he sat down across from me like a customer. Folded his hands on the scarred oak of my worktable, between the inkwell and the jar of a dead man's wedding day, and waited.

When a man like Corvane waits, he is not waiting. He is letting you discover what he already knows, at your own pace, so that the discovery feels like your fault.

"The cartographer," he said.

"Vespera Lune." I kept my voice level. "Her payment cleared Tuesday. The map fragment. Appraised and shelved."

"Mm." He turned his head slightly, the way a hawk adjusts for wind. "And where exactly did you shelve it?"

"In the restricted case. Third drawer."

"Third drawer is empty, Silas."

The candle between us did something theatrical with its flame. I watched it rather than his face, because his face was a document I'd spent years learning to read and I knew better than to let him see me read it now.

"Then it's been moved," I said. "I'll check the transfer log."

"You'll find no entry in the transfer log. You'll find nothing anywhere, because the fragment hasn't moved. It's simply *gone*, the way things go when someone with clever hands and a sentimental streak decides that certain inventory shouldn't be inventory at all." He leaned back. "You've been careless. Or you've been brave. In my experience the two look identical from the outside, right up until the moment they don't."

I want to tell you I had a plan in that moment. That some part of me, the part that had survived eleven years in a house that traded in stolen souls by maintaining a personality like unsalted porridge, produced a flawless lie on demand.

What actually happened was that I said, "I don't know what you mean," in a tone that convinced absolutely no one, least of all me.

Corvane smiled. I had seen him smile at clients before, usually just before naming a price they couldn't afford. It was the smile of a man doing arithmetic.

"Eleven years," he said. "You came to me at nineteen, with your grandmother's letters and no history I could verify past the ferry landing, and I took you in anyway. Do you ever wonder why?"

"You needed a ledger-keeper who could read Old Steppe script."

"I needed a ledger-keeper who could read Old Steppe script *and* had reason never to leave." He tapped the table once, softly. "Everyone in this house is running from something, Silas. That's not a flaw in my hiring. That's the entire design. A guild of contented people would be a guild of traitors-in-waiting. But a house full of the frightened—" he spread his hands, presenting the architecture of the thing, "—a house full of the frightened stays bought."

"And what am I frightened of tonight?"

"That depends entirely on what happens in the next ten minutes."

* * *

Here is what you need to understand about Guildmaster Corvane, or at least here is what I understood at nineteen and believed until tonight: he was not cruel. Cruelty is inefficient, and Corvane wasted nothing—not coin, not memories, not people. He was the fairest monster I have ever known. He paid on time. He honored contracts to the letter. He once returned a widow's deposit because the memory she'd brought him wasn't hers to sell, and he'd done it without being asked, and everyone in the house spoke about it for a month as if it were sainthood instead of bookkeeping.

Fairness, I understood now, watching him sit in my chair with my candle between us, is a much better leash than cruelty. You can hate a cruel man. Hatred gives you somewhere to stand.

"Let me tell you a story," Corvane said. "A client comes to us. Frightened, hunted, carrying the one valuable thing she owns. She offers it for passage out of the country. And my ledger-keeper takes the payment, records it, and then—" he paused delicately, "—negotiates a second arrangement behind my back. Passage for herself and the client both, using guild routes, guild smugglers, guild coin. Using, in short, *my* house to buy *her* way free. Am I close?"

The room was very warm suddenly. Or I was. Sweat does terrible things to a liar's composure; I could feel it starting at my hairline like a confession.

"She came to me," I said. Which was true, and also the last refuge of a man with nothing else true left to say.

"She came to the house. You are the house's face after midnight. The distinction matters." He drew something from his coat and laid it on the table. I looked down at it and felt the floor tilt gently, the way it does on the ferries when the current turns against you.

It was a page. Ledger paper. My ruling hand—the fine, even script I'd been trained into, the script that was supposed to be as anonymous as water. An entry dated four days ago. Vespera Lune. One map fragment, received. And beneath it, in my handwriting, a line I had never written: *Buyer requests private consultation re: Door of Salt. Approved.*

"Forgeries take me about an hour," Corvane said mildly. "Your hand is good but it's regular. Regularity is a weakness in a forger's target and a strength in a forger, and unfortunately for you, Silas, I taught you everything you know about regularity." He slid the page toward me. "This goes to Warden Ilsette's office tomorrow unless we reach an understanding. The warden is currently paying thirty crowns a name for verified gift-holders. She pays more for the gifted themselves, of course, but that requires delivery, and delivery requires—"

"Stop." My voice cracked on it, which I hated. "Whatever you think you know—"

"I know that you touched her hand at the appraisal table and she went white as bleached linen, and afterward neither of you could look at each other for a full minute, and I have run this house long enough to know exactly what that means." He said it without heat. Without triumph, even. That was the horror of him—he might have been reading me a shipping manifest. "I know that for eleven years I have chosen not to know it, because a keeper who can lift a memory with a touch is worth three ordinary clerks and costs nothing extra in wages. And I know that the Thirteenth Hunt has changed the arithmetic, and arithmetic always wins."

The candle guttered. Somewhere above us Maren's boots crossed her floor, and I thought absurdly: *she doesn't know. None of them know. By morning the whole house will know, or none of them will, depending on what I say next.* Your whole life narrows to a doorway like that sometimes, and you don't get a warning. You just find yourself standing in it.

"What do you want?" I asked.

"Ah. There it is. The right question, finally." He folded his hands again, and for a moment—and I will swear this to my grave—for a moment he looked tired. Old. Like a man carrying something heavy up many stairs for many years. "The hunt is coming here, Silas. Not eventually. Imminently. Ilsette's wardens reached the Weigh Station road yesterday. When they arrive, they will want the ledgers—all of them, including the client rolls, including every name of every gifted soul who ever trusted this house with the worst day of their life. And I can burn those ledgers and die beside them, or I can give them up and live, or—" the pause was surgical, "—I can put a man inside the hunters' confidence before they ever arrive. A man whose loyalty they can verify. A man who delivers them something *precious*."

The word landed where he wanted it to land.

"No," I said.

"You haven't heard the offer."

"There's no version of 'deliver Vespera' that I'm going to say yes to, so spare us both the architecture."

"The offer," he continued, in the tone of a man who had never once been interrupted successfully, "is this. Deliver the cartographer to the wardens at the Weigh Station—alive, unharmed, with her map intact. She is a fugitive and a thief of crown property; her fate is the crown's business, not ours, and frankly her fate was sealed the moment she fled the capital with stolen vellum in her coat. What happens to her is not your sin. Hand her over, and the wardens owe the guild a debt of standing, and I retire within the year, and the guild passes to you. The keys, the vaults, the ledgers. Everything I built. Everything your grandmother's people lost, preserved under new management." He leaned forward. "Or refuse. And tomorrow morning this page"—he touched the forgery lightly, one fingertip—"goes to the capital, and your name goes into the very ledgers you keep, entered in your own hand as *gifted*, and you discover precisely how fast thirty crowns travels down the Weigh Station road."

* * *

I have thought since about what I felt in that moment, and the honest answer is: nothing you'd expect. Not rage. Not fear, exactly, though fear was there, sitting quietly in the corner like a well-trained dog.

What I felt was clarity. Terrible, clean clarity, the kind I imagine drowning men get. Because Corvane had made one mistake, and the mistake was thinking he'd built a trap around me when what he'd actually built was a mirror.

*Duty weaponized*, Vespera would later call it, and she was right, though I didn't have her vocabulary yet. He hadn't threatened me with pain or death. He'd threatened me with *betrayal*—and then offered betrayal as the escape. Deliver her, inherit the house. Refuse, and be delivered myself. Every road out of that room ran through someone else's ruin. He'd designed it so that whatever I chose, I would choose it knowing the cost, and knowing the cost was the whole point, because a man who betrays knowingly belongs to you forever after.

"Say something, Silas."

"You've thought about this," I said. "For a while."

"Since the proclamation arrived hidden in the atlas shipment, yes." He almost smiled. "You think I didn't notice the cartographer's fragment showed the Door? That woman walked in here carrying the single most dangerous piece of paper in the kingdom and you shelved it like a receipt. No. Don't apologize. It's the first interesting thing you've ever done."

"Why offer me the guild at all? If I'm compromised, why not just sell me with the rest of the roll and be done with it?"

And there—there—the mask slipped. Just a hair's width. Something moved behind Corvane's eyes that I had never seen there in eleven years, and it took me another decade and a burning cellar to understand what it was: grief. Brief, buried, immediately mortgaged.

"Because I built this house to keep things safe," he said quietly. "Whatever it became, that was the founding. And I am too old to burn it down myself, and the crown will not preserve it, and the only person in this building who has ever once put a memory back where it belonged instead of pocketing it is sitting across from me sweating into his collar." He rose. The audience was ending; the sentence was passing. "Deliver her by moon-twelve. The Weigh Station. Tell her whatever story keeps her walking. After that, Silas Kavanagh keeps the ledgers of the Glass Steppe Guild, and the page in my coat finds a fire, and no one—no one—ever learns what you are."

"And if I warn her instead?"

"Then you'd best be very sure she gets further than you do." He paused at the door, candle in hand, and did not turn around. "One more thing. The fragment. I want it back on my desk by dawn, untouched. Consider it a down payment on whichever choice you make."

The door closed behind him with the soft click of a lock engaging in a cell you'd already been living in.

* * *

I sat alone in the ledger room until the candle drowned in its own wax, and I did the thing I do, the only thing I have ever known how to do: I recorded.

Not in the guild ledger. In the small black notebook I keep sewn into the lining of my coat, the one with the grandmother's letters pressed flat against the spine. I wrote down the terms. I wrote down the deadline. And then, because my hands needed occupation or they were going to start shaking, I wrote down everything I could remember about the Weigh Station road—the guard rotations, the weighmaster's habit of drinking, the stretch of glass flats where a rider could cut off the official path and lose an hour in exchange for losing pursuit.

An escape route. Drawn in my own neat, regular, betrayable hand.

That was when I understood which way I'd already chosen. Corvane thought he'd handed me a fork. He'd actually handed me a map, and maps only point one direction for people like me and people like her.

The problem—the problem that was going to get us both killed—was that moon-twelve was nine nights away, the fragment was due on his desk by dawn, and somewhere out in the dark of the Steppe, in a tavern called the Brine Glass, a woman named Odile who owed me nothing was sheltering a child the hunters hadn't found yet.

Nine nights. And the first brandings were scheduled for market day.

Which was tomorrow.

I blew out the candle, sewed the notebook back into its lining, and went upstairs to wake a cartographer who was going to be very unhappy to learn that her passage had just doubled in price.
