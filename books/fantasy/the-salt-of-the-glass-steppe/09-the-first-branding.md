## 9. The First Branding

I have poured ale through three coronations, two famines, one comet that everyone swore meant something and none of them could agree on what, and the entire slow rot of a kingdom pretending it wasn't rotting. So believe me when I tell you I know crowds. I know them the way a fisherman knows weather — by the weight in the air, by which way the birds turn.

The crowd that gathered in the Steppe market square on the morning they branded Marta Voss was the heaviest I'd ever stood inside. Not because it was large. Because it was quiet. Two hundred people holding their breath at once has a sound, if you've got the ears for it. It sounds like a tavern three minutes before closing time on the last night of a bad month: everyone still there, nobody wanting to be.

Marta mended nets. That's all. Forty years she'd mended nets down at the salt-flats landing, fingers like knotted rope, and if her knots held better than any other woman's in the district — well, we'd all put that down to practice, hadn't we. We'd all been very comfortable putting things down to practice.

Then a tinker had come through in autumn with his little tray of charms and his gift for knowing where lost things were, and he'd looked at Marta across the market and gone pale as whey, because you can't lie to a man who finds what's hidden, and what was hidden in Marta Voss was this: when she tied a knot, she knew whether the net would hold before the net touched water. She knew it the way you know your own name. And she'd known it for forty years and told no one, because telling no one is the whole of the law as most of us live it.

Someone had reported her. I won't say who, though I'll note that Tam Wickerstock bought his winter grain on credit that week from a man who'd never extended him credit before, and I'll note it loudly enough that anyone with ears drew the correct conclusion, and I'll note also that Tam stopped coming to my taproom, because my taproom has a long memory and shorter patience.

* * *

They built the platform out of salt-pine, same as they build everything here, and by noon the sun had bleached it white as bone. The Warden who'd come up from the coast was a young one, pink-cheeked, with a voice like a schoolmaster's — gentle, carrying, the kind of voice that makes you feel scolded even when it's praising you. He read the decree aloud. The Thirteenth Hunt, it was called, which sounded grander than it deserved, since hunts are supposed to involve some risk to the hunters.

*"By order of the crown,"* he read, *"all persons bearing unregistered gifts shall present themselves for branding within thirty days, that they may live in lawful service, or be taken for burning."*

Branding or burning. The crown does love its rhymes. You could set half the proclamations of the last century to music, if you had a cold enough heart to sing them.

"Brand or burn," said the Warden again, conversationally, folding the decree. "That's the whole of the law, friends. Nothing in it hates you. The fire doesn't hate you either. Fire just wants feeding."

I want you to understand that he seemed like a nice young man. That's the worst part. If he'd been a monster, we could have hated him and gone home clean. Instead he smiled at us, and waited, and the crowd did what crowds do when the alternative is being next.

They brought Marta out with her hands bound in front of her, which was unnecessary — she was sixty-one years old and had the shoulders of a wet dishrag — but procedure is procedure, and procedure exists so that the men following it can sleep. They sat her in a chair on the platform. An iron brazier had been heating since dawn. I could smell it from the tavern steps: hot iron and salt-pine smoke and underneath it, faintly, the sweetish stink of the salve they'd already rubbed into her forearm, the one that keeps the wound from killing you. The crown is thorough about keeping you alive. A dead example teaches nothing.

Marta didn't scream. I'll give her that, and I'll give her more than that: she looked out at us. All two hundred of us. She looked at us the whole time, while the iron came up glowing, while the Warden murmured something about service and gratitude, while the iron went down onto the soft inside of her forearm with a sound like a steak hitting a pan — and she looked at us, and we looked everywhere else.

At our boots. At the sky. At the awning of the fruit stall, at a child, at nothing. Two hundred pairs of eyes performing the finest sleight-of-hand ever managed on the Glass Steppe: making an old woman disappear while standing in full view of her.

I looked too. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger the dead keep. I looked at the ground between my own feet and I counted the cracks in the flagstones — eleven, twelve, thirteen, always thirteen that day, I swear the square had thirteen cracks per flagstone, the world mocking me with arithmetic — and I listened to the sizzle, and I felt the crowd flinch around me like a single animal, and I did nothing.

When it was done, the Warden held up her arm — gently, so gently — and showed the mark to the crowd. A circle with a bar through it. The brand of the lawfully gifted, the mark that says *this one is owned now, this one is accounted for, move along.*

"She will report monthly to the garrison," the Warden said warmly. "Her gift will be catalogued and placed in lawful service. She should be congratulated, friends. She chose life."

And Marta Voss, with sweat running down into her collar and her forearm cooked to the bone in one stripe, looked at him with an expression I will carry to my grave, and she laughed. One dry bark of a laugh, like a door slamming.

"Chose," she said. "Aye. That's the word for it."

They took her away to the garrison to be "processed," which is the word the paperwork uses, and the crowd broke apart fast as spilled water, everyone suddenly urgent about errands, about bread, about anything. In under four minutes the square was empty except for the platform, the brazier, the smell, and me.

I stood there a while longer, because someone ought to. Because a square shouldn't get to forget that quickly. Then I went back to the Salt Cellar and opened early, and I didn't charge for the first round, and I didn't explain why, and nobody asked, because that's how we do grief here: on the house, without commentary.

* * *

Here's what you need to know about the Glass Steppe, since the maps won't tell you — the maps being, famously, useless, which is the one mercy this country ever granted itself. The Steppe is salt flats and glass sand for fifty miles in every direction, and the town sits where a river used to be before the river was "reallocated," which is cartographer-speak for stolen. We trade in salt, obviously, and in glasswork, and — less officially — in memory. Bottled recollection. Stolen hours. The Guild up on the ridge buys anything a person is desperate enough to sell, and half the town has sold something, and the other half is saving up the price of something they want to forget.

So yes, we're a town of small corruptions. I'm not going to pretend otherwise. I've sold memories myself — two of them, both cheap ones, a hangover and a funeral, and I'd sell them again. We are not noble people. We are people who live far from anywhere that would notice if we vanished.

But here is the thing about a town of small corruptions: we know each other's faces. Every face. And when the crown sends a boy with a schoolmaster's voice to teach us that some of those faces are contraband — well. Fear is contagious, but so is the other thing. The other thing spreads slower. It spreads the way a fire spreads through wet wood, which is to say stubbornly and all at once.

It started, that night, with a knock at my cellar door.

* * *

Now, a word about my cellar door, because geography is destiny in smuggling towns. The Salt Cellar backs onto the old drainage channel that used to serve the river, and the channel runs out under the glass flats toward the smugglers' causeway, and every keg of untaxed spirits and every bundle of unlicensed goods that has passed through this town in thirty years has passed beneath my floorboards. I am not proud of this. I am also not ashamed. Pride and shame are both luxuries for people whose neighbors aren't starving.

The knock came at the hour when honest people are asleep and dishonest ones are busy, three short raps and one long — Odile's knock, it's called among the trade, because I taught it to half the runners on the causeway and the other half stole it, which is fitting.

I opened it with the lamp hooded and found Brannick the ferryman, dripping, breathless, and holding a bundle that turned out not to be a bundle.

"It's Neme," he whispered. "Widow Sarr's grandchild."

I knew Neme. Bright kid, nine years old maybe, all elbows and questions, forever hanging around the landing asking the ferry passengers where they'd come from and then — this was the part I'd always found odd but never thought hard about — answering before they did. *"Oh, the coast road, past the toll bridge, and you argued with the toll-man."* Everyone laughed. Everyone assumed the child was quick-eared, had heard it secondhand, had a knack.

"You know what happened," Brannick said. "After the branding today. The Warden's men went door to door with the census rolls. Names against ages against—" He stopped. Swallowed. "They were asking about gifts, Odile. Asking neighbors to name neighbors. And Widow Sarr sent the child down the drain channel before they reached her street, and the child swam part of it, and—" He lifted the bundle slightly. It shivered. "There's nowhere else. Her grandmother's taken for questioning. They'll come for the child tomorrow once they've finished frightening the old woman."

"And you brought it to me."

"You've the only door in town that opens outward," Brannick said. "And you're mean enough that no one suspects you of kindness."

"That," I said, "is the nicest thing anyone's said to me all year, and I intend to bill you for it."

I let them in. Of course I let them in. But I want it understood that I stood in that doorway for the length of one full breath doing arithmetic — the fine for sheltering the unbranded, the loss of my license, the fire, the rope, all of it weighed against a wet nine-year-old shaking in a ferryman's arms — and the arithmetic came out wrong, came out cowardly, and I opened the door anyway, because sometimes the sum being wrong is how you know the answer's right.

* * *

Neme sat by my kitchen fire wrapped in two of my good blankets, looking smaller than any child has a right to look, drinking cocoa I make for paying guests at triple price and getting it free, which tells you something about where my principles actually live.

"They took Marta Voss's knot-knowing," the child said. It wasn't a question.

"You saw?"

"I see everything twice." Neme said it flatly, the way children state terrible things they haven't yet learned to apologize for. "Before it happens. Small things mostly. Today I saw the iron coming down before they carried her out, and I couldn't—" The cup rattled against the saucer, and the child steadied it with both hands, deliberately, the way you'd calm a frightened animal. "I couldn't think of anything to do with seeing it. Seeing isn't stopping."

"No," I said. "Seeing never was. Stopping is a different trade entirely."

"I heard the soldiers say they're coming back tomorrow. For me." Those eyes came up, gray as the flats before a storm. "You should know I saw that too. Three days ago. I saw a kitchen fire and cocoa and your face, and I didn't know whose face it was until tonight." A pause, and then, with the terrible precision of children: "You looked angrier in the seeing. Are you angry now?"

I crouched down to the child's height, joints cracking like green firewood, and I thought about what to say. I thought about Marta Voss's laugh, dry as a slammed door. I thought about two hundred people studying their own boots. I thought about my cellar door, and the channel beyond it, and the fact that a door that opens outward is only worth having if, once in a while, you use it that way.

"Aye," I said. "I'm angry. But anger's just fuel, child. Question is what you cook with it."

Neme considered this with the gravity of a guild assessor. "What are you going to cook?"

"First? Cocoa. Second?" I glanced toward the shadowed mouth of the drain channel, and beyond it the flats, and beyond the flats the ridge where the Guild kept its ledgers, and beyond the ledgers a horizon that no map in the kingdom admitted existed. "Second, I'm going to find out who in this town still remembers how to open doors."

The child drank the cocoa. Outside, the thirteenth moon was climbing — thin and strange and doubled, as it had been all season, as though the sky itself couldn't decide which version of events to show us — and somewhere down the channel, water moved steadily toward the smugglers' causeway, patient as anything that has all night and knows exactly where it's going.

They'd come for Neme in the morning. Let them. By morning, the drain would be empty and the rumor mill would be full, and I'd stand in my taproom with my arms crossed and my face arranged into its best expression of ignorant grievance, and I would lie to the crown's officers with the serene professionalism of thirty years in the hospitality trade.

But that night, after Neme finally slept, I took a scrap of paper and wrote four lines in the shorthand we use for untaxed goods, and I sealed it into a hollow keg-stave headed for the causeway runners:

*Child of sight needs passage north. Knows things before they happen. Send word to anyone who owes me. Send it fast.*

Because here's the truth I arrived at somewhere between the thirteenth crack in the flagstones and the thirteenth moon: fear curdles, right enough. But curdled cream is how you start the next batch. Somewhere in this town, in this Steppe, in whatever unmapped country the maps pretend ends at our salt line, there were others like Marta Voss, others like this child, others who had spent forty years telling no one. And somebody — a guild clerk with careful hands, a disgraced mapmaker, a runner, a fool, I didn't know yet who — somebody was going to need to know that the ordinary people were done looking at their boots.

I banked the fire, checked the channel door twice, and sat up till dawn with a poker across my knees, listening to the water run outward, and waiting to see which way the world would break.
