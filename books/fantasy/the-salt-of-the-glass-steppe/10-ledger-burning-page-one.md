## 10. Ledger Burning, Page One

There is an art to copying a ledger without looking like you're copying a ledger.

I learned it young, back when my only crimes were arithmetic errors and an unfortunate fondness for guild ink. You don't hunch. Hunched shoulders are a confession. You sit exactly as you always sit—spine straight, pen moving at its usual pace, expression arranged in that particular boredom that says *this man would rather be anywhere else, but duty is duty*. Boredom is a cloak. I have worn it so long I sometimes forget what's underneath.

The trouble with tonight was volume.

Corvane had retired to his rooms two hours past midnight, which gave me perhaps five before the night porter made his rounds. Five hours to copy four hundred and eleven names out of the Red Ledger—the one kept behind the false shelf, the one that wasn't inventory at all but a menu. Four hundred and eleven people whose names, faces, addresses, and gifts Corvane had already sold or was preparing to sell to the crown's hunters. Four hundred and eleven entries in neat columns, each one a small, tidy sentence of doom.

*Odile Marchetti, tavern-keeper, Salt Row. Gift: mending broken things with her hands. Value: moderate. Disposition: pending.*

Pending. As if she were a shipment of salted cod.

I had found the page missing three nights ago—a gap where a client's record should have been, cut clean with a razor, the stub still glued to the spine. That was how Corvane did business now. He didn't burn anything; burning left ash, and ash could be counted. He razored. One name at a time, folded into his coat, carried down to the Warden's men like a man delivering love letters.

So I was going to steal them all back. Or rather, I was going to make copies of copies, which in the ledger trade is the only honest kind of theft.

* * *

The archive room at this hour has a sound like breathing. The memory-vials on their racks tick softly as the wax seals contract in the cold—I've never decided whether it's chemistry or whether the memories themselves shift in their sleep—and the paper smell is thick enough to chew. Grandmother used to say paper remembers everything written on it, even after you scrape it clean. She said it about the Steppe, about the maps that had erased her country, about how erasure is just writing you haven't read closely enough.

I hadn't thought of her in weeks. Tonight her voice came back the moment I unhooked the false shelf, and it didn't leave.

The Red Ledger was where Corvane always left it, because Corvane believed no one would dare. That was always his genius and his blindness in one: he built a house where fear did the locking for him. I took it down, set it beside a stack of genuine inventory pages—bottled courage, third quality, six vials, awaiting appraisal—and opened both. Then I began the oldest trick in the scribe's book: two pens, two inks, alternating lines between the real work and the stolen one, so that any glance through the door would find me doing exactly what I was paid to do.

Four hundred and eleven names.

I told myself I would not touch them.

You have to understand what that means, for someone like me. Every ledger entry carries residue. Names soak into paper the way blood soaks into bandages, and I don't need a vial or a ritual—skin against ink, skin against the thing remembered, and it comes up through my fingers like water through chalk. Most of the time it's nothing. A flicker. The ghost of a transaction. But these weren't transactions. These were people, written down by the people who meant to destroy them, and grief soaks deep.

I got through eleven names before I broke my own rule.

It wasn't deliberate. My hand slipped—or my hand knew better than I did—and the pad of my thumb dragged across the line *Marta Vey, weaver, gifted: seeing the dead in thread patterns* and Marta Vey arrived in my skull entire. Not her face—I'd never seen her face—but her hands. Hands working a loom by candlelight, weaving her dead husband's coat pattern over and over because as long as the pattern continued he wasn't finished dying. Twenty years of it. I sat there with my own hands curled around the pen, feeling wool under fingers that weren't mine, and when it released me I discovered I had been crying long enough to spot the inventory page.

Eleven names. Four hundred to go.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about other people's lives: they weigh something. Not metaphorically. There is mass to a stranger's sorrow, the way there's mass to wet sand, and I was carrying it by the handful.

*Hallow Fen, lamplighter. Gift: flames remember songs sung to them.* When I touched his line I heard a fire on a winter street singing a lullaby in a cracked, crackling voice while children pressed close for warmth, and I understood that Hallow Fen had spent thirty years giving away warmth and had been repaid with a column in a traitor's book.

*Sera Ivo, midwife. Gift: taking a sliver of a mother's pain during birth, keeping it.* Sera's entry was thick with residue because Sera had done it hundreds of times, and touching her name was like putting my palm flat on a sea. I gripped the desk until the wood bit.

*Aldous Renk, tax clerk. Gift: knowing when a person is lying by the taste of their sweat.* Aldous, poor bastard, had tasted Corvane's lie a hundred times and never known what it meant.

Name after name. I copied each one onto smuggler's paper—thin, tough stuff that folds into a boot heel—and beside each name I wrote what I could of the gift, because a warning that doesn't tell a person what they're running from isn't a warning, it's just bad news delivered late. And every few names my discipline failed and I touched instead of skimmed, and every touch deposited more weight.

By the second hour I understood I was doing something dangerous beyond the crime of it. A ledger-keeper who absorbs what he records stops being a keeper. I could feel myself becoming an archive of the hunted—not a list of them, a vessel. Somewhere around name two hundred I lost track of which griefs were mine. I have very few of my own; I've hoarded them carefully, like coins too valuable to spend. Now they were jumbled in with a fisherman's terror of open water, a schoolteacher's shame at a student she'd failed, a boy of maybe twelve whose gift was making bread rise perfectly and whose only crime had been telling the wrong neighbor about it.

That boy's entry had a note in Corvane's hand. *Young. Malleable. Consider holding for Warden's use.*

I put the pen down. I put my head in my hands. I let the room breathe around me, tick and settle, tick and settle, and I thought about the mathematics of what I was doing. Four hundred and eleven warnings, copied and distributed, might save some fraction of those people. It might also get every one of them killed faster, if the copies fell into the wrong hands. It would certainly get me killed if Corvane caught me, and Corvane was not a man who needed much catching these days—he had hunters to do his walking.

And underneath all of it, quieter than the rest, the question I'd been refusing since the missing page: why was I still here? Loyalty? To what—to a man who razored names from books and sold them like bacon sides? Habit? Habit is just fear that's learned to sit still.

Grandmother crossed the Steppe with nothing but a lullaby and spite, according to the family legend, and the spite was mostly mine by inheritance.

I picked the pen back up.

* * *

Around name three hundred, the door latch moved.

I didn't freeze—that's the fastest way to die in this house. I turned the page of the inventory ledger with my left hand, kept the right pen moving on a legitimate appraisal note, and said, to the darkness beyond the door, in the flat tone of a man interrupted over figures: "If you're the porter, the count's short because Corvane took six vials of grief Tuesday and nobody entered it."

It was Vespera.

She came in the way she does everything—like the room had been expecting her and ought to be grateful. She'd taken to working late in the map alcove since our bargain; the guild slept badly these days and so, apparently, did its guests. She looked at me, at the two ledgers, at my face—which I can only imagine had the texture of a wrung cloth—and she closed the door behind her without being asked.

"You look," she said, "like a man carrying furniture."

"That's accurate."

"How many?"

"Three hundred and six." My voice cracked on the number, which I hated. "Of four hundred and eleven."

She didn't say *stop*. She didn't say *you'll kill us both*. She pulled the second chair to my side of the desk—the chair reserved for clients, for the people selling pieces of themselves—and sat down at my elbow, and held out her hand for a pen.

"I can write fast," she said. "Cartographers' training. We letter whole coastlines before breakfast."

"You don't know what's in it."

"No," she agreed. "But I know what's in your face, and I'd rather share the weight than watch you drown politely." She paused, and then, wryer: "Also, if they catch us, I intend to claim you coerced me. You have a very coercive way of crying."

So we worked together, the disgraced cartographer and the invisible accountant, side by side in the breathing dark, passing names across the desk like contraband. She copied; I verified against the original; and when the residue rose too high in me—as it did, again and again, a tide that didn't care about schedules—she noticed. I don't know how. She'd simply set down her pen, take my wrist in her cool dry grip, and hold on until whatever stranger's life finished pouring through me and drained away.

"Whose was that?" she asked once.

"A woman who could hear the Steppe singing under the glass. Her whole entry is one line long because Corvane couldn't price her. Too strange to sell." I swallowed. "He wrote *disposition: consult Warden*."

Vespera was quiet for a moment. "My sister hears things like that," she said finally. "Wind that talks. I always thought she was making it up to annoy me." She looked at the ledger with a new expression, one I recognized because I'd been wearing it for hours: the look of a person counting names and finding, in the columns, the outline of everyone they love. "Page forty," she said. "Third entry. Read it to me."

I read it. It wasn't her sister—it was a cooper from two provinces away—but she nodded anyway, as if confirming something, and wrote the next name with a steadier hand.

That's the thing I learned tonight, somewhere between the lamplighter's singing fire and the boy with the perfect bread: the weight doesn't lighten when you share it. It distributes. It becomes bearable the way a roof beam is bearable—because something else is standing under the other end.

We finished at the grey edge of dawn. Four hundred and eleven names, four hundred and eleven gifts, four hundred and eleven small biographies of imminent ruin, copied onto smuggler's paper in two different hands and bundled into oilcloth packets sized for boots, saddlebags, and one hollow salt-cod barrel Odile Marchetti wouldn't miss. Pending, I thought, looking at her entry. We'd see about pending.

Vespera stood, stretched until her spine popped, and surveyed the desk with the satisfaction of a woman who has just redrawn a coastline.

"Now the hard part," she said. "Getting them out."

"The smugglers run salt Thursday—"

Which is when we both heard it: footsteps in the corridor outside. Not the porter's shuffle. Measured heels, unhurried, the walk of someone who owns the corridor and everything in it, and who has stopped—directly outside the archive door.

Vespera's eyes met mine. The false shelf stood open. The Red Ledger lay exposed between us like a body.

And through the door, in a voice like silk drawn slowly over a blade, a woman said: "Don't stop on my account. I do so admire thorough bookkeeping."
