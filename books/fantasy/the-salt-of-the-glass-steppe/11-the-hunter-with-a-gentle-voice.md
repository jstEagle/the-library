## 11. The Hunter with a Gentle Voice

I have found in my long career that the most dangerous people are almost never the ones who raise their voices. The ones who shout want something obvious: your purse, your confession, your head on a pike. You can plan around shouting. It is the gentle ones you must fear—the ones who pour your tea themselves, who remember the name of your mother's village, who ask after your health as though your health were a matter of genuine national importance.

I am, of course, one of them.

The Guildmaster of the Salt Steppe trade house received me in his counting room at the top of the guild tower, where the light comes through glass tinted faintly amber, so that everything and everyone in the room appears preserved in honey. A deliberate choice. Corvane was not a man who made accidental choices about anything.

"Warden Ilsette." He rose from behind a desk of black saltwood that had probably cost more than the street it overlooked. "We're honored. I'd heard the Thirteenth Hunt had reached the Steppe, but I hadn't dared hope the crown would send its—"

"Chief Warden," I supplied, because I find it is kinder to let people finish their flattery than to make them struggle through it. "You're too generous, Guildmaster. The crown sends whom the crown sends. I'm told the roads were terrible."

"They were," he agreed gravely, as though we had established a fact of mutual significance. "Will you take tea?"

"I will."

He poured it himself. Of course he did. A man who deals in stolen memories knows precisely what a gesture is worth, and he had calculated that this one—a man of his station pouring for a woman of mine—would buy him an hour of my goodwill at minimum. He was wrong by a factor of roughly an hour, but I let him believe otherwise. Goodwill is cheaper when the other party thinks they've purchased it.

The tea was excellent. That, too, was calculated. Everything in that room was calculated except, I suspected, the dust on the ledgers, and possibly not even that.

"You'll want to see our records," Corvane said, before I had finished my first cup.

I set the cup down and smiled at him. It is important, when hunting, to smile early and often. It puts the quarry at ease, and it gives you practice for the moment when smiling is all that stands between you and what you actually want to do.

"Guildmaster," I said, "you misunderstand why I've come. I don't want to see your records. Anyone can write anything in a record. I could write a record saying I was born in the capital to a family of respectable bakers, and it would be every bit as true as half the entries in any ledger in this country." I paused, letting the silence do a little work. "What I want is to understand how your records are *made*."

Something moved behind his eyes—quick, controlled, gone. A man who has sold memories professionally learns to keep his face like a locked drawer. But even locked drawers rattle.

"Our methods are proprietary," he said mildly.

"Naturally. And protected, I'm sure, by various charters and ancient privileges." I took up my tea again. "Which is why I'm here rather than a junior warden with a warrant. Charters burn, Guildmaster. Ancient privileges burn faster. But I'd much prefer not to burn anything. Burning is so final, and I am a patient woman."

"Patient," he repeated.

"The crown's chief virtue," I said. "Impatience gets innocent people branded, and then the paperwork alone takes months. No—I prefer to be *sure*. Surety requires time. Time requires cooperation. Cooperation requires tea." I lifted the cup slightly, in salute. "So. Tell me how a memory comes into your keeping."

And he did—or, rather, he told me a version of it so smooth it might have been lacquered. Clients came to the guild burdened by things they wished to be rid of. Griefs, shames, loves that had curdled. Trained appraisers assessed the goods. Extraction was performed by licensed practitioners under strict safeguards. The memory was decanted into glass, catalogued, and stored or sold according to contract. All consensual. All clean. All, I noted, described entirely in the passive voice, as though memories extracted themselves and simply drifted into jars out of loneliness.

It was a very good performance. I complimented him on it without saying so.

"And your appraisers?" I asked. "How does one qualify?"

"Talent, training, and discretion," said Corvane. "In descending order of scarcity."

"Mm. And the talent itself. This touch-work. It's a gift, isn't it? Like the other gifts. The ones the proclamation names."

The honey-colored light did nothing to warm the room just then.

"The extraction craft is a discipline," he said carefully. "Learned. Regulated. Nothing like the wild talents the Hunt concerns itself with."

"Nothing like," I echoed pleasantly. "Except that it involves touching another person's mind and taking something out of it."

"That is—" He paused, recalibrating. "That is a reductive description."

"All accurate descriptions are reductive. It's what makes them accurate." I leaned back in my chair. "Guildmaster, I'll be plain with you, because plainness saves us both the trouble of pretending. The Thirteenth Hunt exists because the king's astrologers have confirmed what the old texts always said: the thirteenth turn of the moon completes a cycle, and gifts that have slept for a hundred years will wake. Every gifted soul in this country will grow stronger between now and then. Some will discover gifts they didn't know they had." I watched his face as I said it—that last part—and was rewarded with the tiniest stillness, the kind a deer achieves when it hears a twig snap and hasn't yet decided which way to run. "The crown's position is simple. Branded, registered, and supervised—or burned. There is no third door. But there is a great deal of room inside those two doors for men of practical intelligence. Men who cooperate fully. Men whose institutions become instruments of the crown rather than obstacles to it."

"You're offering the guild protection."

"I'm offering the guild *relevance*," I corrected. "Protection is for things that deserve protecting. Relevance is for things that are useful. I think you know the difference. I think you've built your whole life on knowing the difference."

For the first time, Corvane laughed—a short, dry sound, like a ledger being closed. "They said you were courteous."

"They say many things about me. Most of them are attempts to make me sound less frightening than I am. It never works, because courtesy frightens people far more than cruelty. Cruelty they can predict."

"What do you want from me, Warden?"

"Three things," I said, and held up fingers, because enumerating makes demands feel reasonable, and reasonable demands get met. "First, complete access to your client registers. Names, debts, acquisitions. Second, the names of anyone in your employ whose gift extends beyond your 'licensed disciplines'—and yes, Guildmaster, I include yourself in that inquiry, should the question ever arise, though I note you haven't answered it." His face did not change; that was answer enough, and we both knew I would file it away accordingly. "Third—and this is the small one, really—a table near your appraisal floor. Somewhere discreet. My people will need to observe routine operations."

"Observe," he said. "Not interfere."

"Observation is interference conducted politely. But yes. Observe."

He considered me across the desk, and I gave him the courtesy of being considered back. He was weighing something, and I already knew what: how much of himself to sell me today versus how much he could sell me again tomorrow. A trader to the marrow. Men like him imagine that because everything has a price, everyone is purchasable. They are usually right, which is the tragedy of their lives, because they extend the principle to themselves and eventually meet someone who prices them honestly.

"The registers will be brought to you within the day," he said.

"Today, if you please. Within the day means tonight, and tonight means someone has spent the intervening hours deciding which pages to remove."

Another flicker. This one lasted longer. I began, privately, a list of the pages he would remove. I have a good imagination for other people's dishonesty. It is the single most valuable talent I possess, and unlike the talents the Hunt pursues, mine required no moon to wake it.

"Today, then," he conceded.

"Wonderful." I stood, and he stood with me, and we bowed to each other with the precise mutual courtesy of two people who each intend to survive the other. At the door, I paused, as though the thought had only just occurred to me—which is a lie, since I had composed the sentence on the road three days ago and polished it twice. "Oh—one more thing. A curiosity. Your grandmother, Guildmaster. Was she from the Steppe originally? The old families here so often were."

"Why do you ask?"

"Because the erased country remembers its children," I said gently. "Even after a century. Even through glass." I let him hear the smile in it. "Good day, Guildmaster. Do send the registers promptly. I do hate to be kept waiting—it brings out a side of me that no one enjoys, least of all me."

* * *

My second-in-command, Warden Brech, met me in the hired rooms above the weigh station tavern, where I had established my temporary headquarters on the theory that a hunter works best among ordinary noise. He had laid out the morning's reports with his usual soldierly tidiness, and he wore his usual expression of a man perpetually braced for instructions he will regret receiving.

"Well?" he said.

"He'll cooperate," I said. "Fully, promptly, and treacherously. In that order."

"The registers?"

"Will arrive missing several pages. We'll inventory against the guild's own duplicate seals—every entry in their books carries a wax stamp, and stamps crack in patterns. If he removes pages, the seal sequence breaks. We'll know exactly how many names he's hidden and can infer their shape from the gaps." I unbuttoned my gloves finger by finger. My hands ached; they always ached in cold country. "He'll hide the names of clients he believes are already beyond saving, and the names of employees he intends to sacrifice. Watch for both. The sacrifices tell us who he fears, and what he fears tells us what's true."

Brech wrote this down, because Brech writes down everything, which is why I keep him. "And the guild staff? You think the gift's among them?"

"I think," I said, "that a house which buys and sells the contents of human minds cannot help but attract people who can hold a mind in their hands. It's like asking whether a fishmonger's shop smells of fish. The question is not whether. It's which ones, and how strong, and whether they know it yet." I flexed my fingers, watching the ache travel. "The last one matters most. A gifted person who knows what they are hides. A gifted person who doesn't know yet is merely waiting to be surprised, and surprised people make mistakes."

"You have someone in mind."

"I have a shape in mind," I admitted. "Corvane keeps a private ledger-keeper. Quiet one. Never named in the public rolls, handles the sensitive acquisitions. Every guild of this kind has one—a person whose whole function is to know everything and appear to know nothing." I thought of the brief report my scouts had assembled: a figure seen at odd hours in the archive, careful hands, careful face, no vices worth blackmailing and no friendships worth exploiting. An absence shaped like a person. Those interest me more than presences do. "When the registers come, I want to know whose handwriting fills the gaps. Handwriting is a confession that repeats itself."

* * *

The test was Brech's idea, which I noted with approval and did not tell him, as praise makes subordinates comfortable and comfort makes them slow.

"If the guild is riddled with the gift," he said that evening, "we need a way to make it show itself. You can't search for a touch-taker directly. But you can offer one something worth taking."

"Go on."

"A false client," he said. "Someone new to town, no history anyone can check. Carrying a memory so tempting that a thief of memories couldn't leave it alone. Baited. If anyone in that guild touches the client's skin during appraisal—if anyone lifts so much as a crumb of it—we'll know, because we'll have laced the memory with something traceable."

"Laced how?" I asked, genuinely curious. "We don't have gift-workers. That's rather the point of the entire exercise."

"We don't need one." He produced a small stoppered vial, dark glass, unlabeled. "Old wardens' trick, from before the Hunts went quiet. A memory steeped in griefroot extract. Anyone who absorbs even a fragment of it will carry the marker in their blood for days. Painful, but harmless. Detectable with a simple prick-test."

"Griefroot," I said. "The stuff they used to use on oath-breakers."

"The same."

"How painful?"

"Painful enough," said Brech, in the tone of a man who has decided not to think further about a thing he has already decided.

I looked at the vial for a while. Somewhere below us, the tavern noise rose and fell like weather. I am often accused—by the branded, by the bereaved, by my own reflection on certain nights—of being without conscience. This is imprecise. I have a conscience. It simply files things correctly: the suffering of the guilty under *necessary*, the suffering of the possibly-innocent under *regrettable*, and both folders sit on the same shelf, and I open whichever the workday requires. What I do not permit myself is the folder marked *unexamined*. Everything must be examined. That is the whole of my creed, and the whole of my damnation, depending on who is doing the damning.

"One condition," I said finally. "The baited client volunteers knowing what the memory contains. Not the marker—the content. If we're going to hang a temptation in front of hungry people, the bait should be real. A real memory, freely given, of something worth stealing. Otherwise we learn nothing except that people steal trash."

"And what memory qualifies?"

I turned the vial in my fingers. "Find me someone who has recently lost someone they loved, and who wants to stop carrying it. That is the guild's bread and butter, Brech—they've built an empire on grief. So we offer them grief. Fresh, authentic, unbearable. Something no appraiser with a scrap of the talent could brush against without wanting more." I set the vial down. "And we steep it in griefroot, so that whoever tastes it will carry the taste home with them. Then we prick every hand that handled the appraisal. Politely. As a health measure. The Steppe has fevers, after all."

Brech looked at me the way he sometimes looks at me—with the expression of a man watching a machine do exactly what it was built to do and wishing it had been built differently.

"It'll take a few days to find the right volunteer," he said.

"We have until the thirteenth moon," I said. "But not much past it. Find me someone grieving, Warden. The Steppe is full of them. It's the one crop that grows everywhere."

* * *

The registers arrived the next morning at the ninth bell, carried by a clerk with damp hands and delivered with a bow so deep it was nearly an apology. I counted the folios against the seal inventory before the clerk had straightened. Four pages removed. Four gaps in the wax-crack sequence, neat as pulled teeth.

Four names Corvane believed I did not need.

I sat with the ledgers for most of the day, reading not the entries but the hand that had made them. It was good work—disciplined, even, elegant in a restrained way—but handwriting betrays what faces conceal, and this hand betrayed care. Not the care of a scribe. The care of someone who understood exactly what each name meant to the person it belonged to, and recorded it anyway, and hated the recording, and did it perfectly regardless. I have read ten thousand confessions in my career. Most of them were signed at the bottom. This one was signed all the way through, line by line, by a person trying very hard to be invisible and failing only in the places where conscience leaks.

By dusk I had narrowed my suspicion to two candidates among the guild's unnamed staff, and by lamplight to one.

I was still reading when Brech came in with the evening report and stopped short, looking at the ledgers spread around me like a fallen deck of cards.

"You haven't slept," he observed.

"I'll sleep when the moon finishes turning." I tapped the page before me. "Look at this. Entry forty-one in the acquisition roll. A widow's grief, purchased at standard rate, decanted and shelved. Routine. Except the marginal notation—see it? The appraiser's mark is doubled. Two passes over the same memory. That's against procedure. That's someone going back to look at a thing they'd already catalogued." I sat back. "People don't revisit merchandise, Brech. They revisit wounds. Whoever made this mark wanted one more moment with a stranger's grief, and then made themselves write the perfect entry so no one would ever know."

"You think it's your quiet ledger-keeper."

"I think the quiet ones are always guilty of something. It's only a question of whether the something is avarice or mercy." I smiled, and felt my own smile the way one feels a key turning. "Avarice I can price. Mercy I have to hunt."

Below, in the town, the lamps of the Steppe came on one by one, ordinary lights for ordinary people preparing for an ordinary night, none of them knowing that somewhere in their midst a vial of griefroot waited to become a stranger's worst hour, and that a woman with a gentle voice had already decided how the story would end for everyone who touched it.

"Send word to our false client," I said. "Tell her we're ready. And Brech—"

"Yes, Warden?"

"When she goes in tomorrow, watch the ledger-keeper's hands. Not the face. Hands confess faster." I returned to the page, to the small doubled mark, the tiny signature of a conscience I intended to catch red-handed. "Tomorrow we learn who in that guild cannot keep their fingers to themselves."

I did not yet know, as I bent over the ledgers in the honey-colored lamplight, how completely the answer would undo me—not the catching, but the caught. Hunters rarely do. We write the trap, spring the trap, and stand astonished when the thing inside it turns out to have a face.

But that is tomorrow's chapter, and I am a patient woman.
