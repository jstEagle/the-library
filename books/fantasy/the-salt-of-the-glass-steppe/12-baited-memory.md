## 12. Baited Memory

The client arrived at the ninth bell, and everything about him was wrong in the way that only expensive things are wrong.

His coat was good wool, but it had been creased deliberately—folded and pressed to look like the clothing of a man who slept in it, a costume of poverty that cost more than my year's wages. His boots were scuffed at the toe but clean at the seam. He had calluses on his hands in the places a rope would put them, except the calluses were too evenly distributed, like someone had studied a laborer's palms and then commissioned them.

I have kept the ledgers of the Glass Steppe Guild for eleven years. I know what honest desperation looks like. It looks like Widow Sarr's hands, cracked from salt-work. It looks like the soldier who sold his courage and couldn't stop weeping for three days afterward, though he didn't know why. It does not look like this man, who sat down across from me in the appraisal room and said, with the careful flatness of a rehearsed sentence:

"I want to sell a memory."

"Most people do," I said. "That's rather the point of the establishment."

He flinched at my tone, and the flinch was also rehearsed—I'd seen Corvane coach new appraisers through their first clients with exactly that instruction: *look wounded, they trust wounded*. This man had been coached by someone better than Corvane.

"The memory," he said. "It's of my mother. Her kitchen. I want it gone."

Gone. Not sold—*gone*. Clients who wanted memories erased paid triple, and said things like *I can't bear to carry it anymore*, not *I want it gone* in the voice of a man reciting an inventory number.

I wrote his name in the intake ledger—Tomas Bell, which was almost certainly false, because there were four Tomas Bells in the Steppe census and all of them were children or dead—and I felt the old cold slide down my spine, the one that has nothing to do with gift and everything to do with eleven years of reading people who lie for a living.

"Appraisal fee is standard," I said. "If the memory proves valuable enough, the fee comes off your payout. If you'll place your hand on the glass."

The appraisal pane sat between us, a slab of smoked crystal the size of a hymnal. A client presses their palm to it, thinks of what they're selling, and the glass drinks a taste of the memory—a scent, a color, a single note of sound—which we use to price it. I never touch the pane myself during intake. Rule three, written in Corvane's own hand above the ledger room door: *The keeper does not sample the stock.* Because I could do more than sample. Because one brush of my skin against a held memory and I could lift the whole thing out, whole and warm, and no one would ever know it was gone.

Eleven years without breaking rule three. Eleven years of pressing my hands behind my back like a schoolboy while grief and joy passed under glass an arm's length away.

Tomas Bell placed his palm on the pane.

The glass clouded, then bloomed: a kitchen, yellow light, bread smell, a woman humming—

—and underneath it, threaded through it like wire through wax, something else. Something that did not belong in any mother's kitchen.

I have touched perhaps two hundred stolen memories in my life, most of them by accident, some by choice I'm not proud of. Memories have textures. Grief is wet wool. Courage is hot iron. Love, when you find it bottled honestly, is warm bread—which is why the guild pays well for kitchens and mothers, and why this particular bait was so cleverly chosen. The forger had built a real memory as the frame. Someone had *lived* this kitchen once; you don't fake bread-smell that precisely. But around its edges, woven in, was a second thing, and the second thing was watching.

Memories aren't supposed to watch back.

I knew what it was before I let myself name it. Warden Ilsette had sat in Corvane's office six days ago with her gentle voice and her patient eyes, and she had set a test, and here it was, delivered to my desk like a package. Somewhere in this guildhouse, right now, she was waiting to see whose hand flinched. Waiting to see if anyone in this building could feel what ordinary appraisers cannot: the hook inside the bait.

Rule three existed to protect me from exactly this moment, and rule three was about to fail me, because the pane was still drinking, and the watching-thing inside the memory had turned its attention fully on me, and it was *waiting* too.

Here is what I should have done: finished the intake, priced the memory low—baited traps are always priced low, because the crown doesn't waste good coin on tests—logged it into the vault, and walked out of the room with my heart beating like a normal frightened clerk's. Then packed a bag and vanished, because a test this elaborate meant Ilsette already suspected someone specific, and if the suspicion had a shape, that shape was me.

Instead I did something worse than touching it. I hesitated. One heartbeat, two. And in that hesitation, my sleeve rode up, and the edge of the appraisal pane brushed the bare skin of my wrist.

The world went yellow.

Bread. The kitchen, entire, pouring into me—the woman humming off-key, flour on her knuckles, a boy at the table carving something useless out of soft wood, the specific afternoon light of a country I had never stood in. And beneath it, coiled at the center like a snake in a basket: the watcher. Not a person. An *instruction*. A binding laid over the memory like a snare laid over a rabbit run, keyed to respond to exactly one kind of touch. The kind of touch that lifts instead of samples.

The kind of touch that mine was.

The trap didn't spring, precisely. It *recorded*. That was the horror of it—it wasn't designed to catch me in the act. It was designed to remember the act, to carry the impression of gifted contact back to whoever had crafted it, and I felt it take my print the way wax takes a seal, and then the whole construction went quiet and satisfied, like a ledger entry completed.

I pulled my hand back. My wrist burned where the glass had touched it, though the skin showed nothing.

Tomas Bell was looking at me. "Is something wrong?"

"No," I said. "The memory is... unusual. Kitchen memories fetch well. I'll need to consult the pricing tables." My voice came out steady. Eleven years of practice being invisible will do that; you learn to speak from a part of yourself that feels nothing, a clerk-shaped shell you leave standing at the desk while the rest of you runs for the hills. "Return tomorrow at the tenth bell for your valuation."

He rose, thanked me, and left. At the door he paused—just half a heartbeat—and I understood that the pause was also scripted. *Note the time of the appointment,* the script said. *Be here when the fish returns to the hook.*

When the door closed behind him, I sat very still and counted to sixty, which is how long it takes a disciplined person to stop shaking. It took me twice that.

Then I got up, locked the appraisal room, and went to find Vespera.

*

She was in the map archive on the third floor, where she'd spent the past week ostensibly cataloguing Corvane's collection and actually memorizing every route out of the Steppe that didn't pass a crown checkpoint. She worked with her sleeves rolled and her hair pinned with whatever was nearest—an ink-stylus today, which gave her the look of a woman who had been stabbed lightly in the head and decided not to make a fuss about it.

"We have a problem," I said, and closed the door, and told her all of it in the flat voice I save for bad news: the client, the coat, the bait, the touch, the taking of my print.

When I finished, she was quiet for a moment, stylus still poised over the chart in front of her.

"How long," she said, "do you think we have?"

"Until tomorrow, tenth bell, certainly. Probably less. If the trap reports to her directly through a courier, she could be reading my signature tonight." I leaned against the shelf of atlas spines and felt the whole wall of them press against my shoulders like a jury. "Vespera. I've been careful for eleven years. Eleven years of never sampling, never lingering, wearing gloves through the vault inventories, letting Corvane think I was merely meticulous. And I threw it away in one second because a fake kitchen smelled like bread and I *hesitated*."

"You hesitated because you're human," she said. "And because the trap was built to be hesitated at. Ilsette is not testing whether you're gifted. She's testing whether you're careful. Careful people fail her test too—they just fail it slower." She capped the ink bottle. "What did the binding feel like? When it took your print."

"Wax taking a seal."

"Then she has your touch-signature, not your name. Touch-signatures can't be read by just anyone—she'll need a reader, probably herself, possibly something worse." Vespera stood, and I watched her do the arithmetic I couldn't: distances, moons, checkpoints, the weight of a pack. "So the question isn't whether we run. We ran the day I walked in with a map fragment and you made a bargain behind Corvane's back. The question is what we carry and what we burn."

"Burn," I repeated. My voice did something strange on the word.

"The ledgers can't come with us, Silas."

"I know what the ledgers can't do." I heard myself sharpening, and hated it, and kept going anyway, because fear makes clerks of us all—turns everything into inventory to be argued over. "But the book of the hunted names—the copy I've been making, the warnings—that's not guild property. Those are people. If I leave it here, Ilsette reads it as a shopping list."

"Then we take the copy and burn nothing yet," she said. "You don't get to torch the whole archive tonight out of guilt. Guilt burns carelessly. Save the fire for when fire buys something." She was already pulling charts from the shelf, selecting with quick, ruthless economy—the Door of Salt fragment, the smugglers' tide-tables, a coastal survey old enough that no living cartographer would miss it. Watching her work, I understood that she had packed for flight in her head long ago, maybe every day since the capital, maybe every day since the census rolls. Fugitives keep their bags mentally packed. It's the settled ones who are unprepared.

"I need one hour in the ledger room," I said.

"You have forty minutes. Corvane takes his evening brandy at half past nine, and Ilsette dines late—she likes the dining hall empty, I've watched her. Forty minutes, Silas, and then we go out through the salt cellar and you never see this building again."

I looked around the archive—at the shelves of the world drawn by dead men, at the window with its view of the Steppe market going gold with lamplight, at the little brass instrument Vespera used to check the moon's angle, still sitting on the sill where she'd left it. Eleven years I had lived in this building. I had a chair worn to the shape of me. I had a mug with a chipped rim that I liked. I had a system for filing that I had invented and no one else understood, which meant that after tonight the guild's records would decay into chaos within a month, and some petty, vicious corner of me found that thought deeply comforting.

Forty minutes.

*

The ledger room at night was always my favorite version of itself—lamp turned low, dust hanging in the light like slow snow, the shelves breathing that smell of vellum and cedar oil that I will now, presumably, smell nowhere else for the rest of my life. I moved through it fast and quiet, and grief kept trying to interrupt, and I kept declining the invitation.

The copy of the hunted names was hidden where I hide everything: inside the binding of a volume titled *Tariffs on Dried Fish, Consolidated*, because no hunter in the history of crowns has ever opened a tariff schedule voluntarily. I drew it out. Two hundred and six names in my smallest hand, each one a person the guild had once served and would now sell, each one carrying a fragment of some stranger's life in their blood like a splinter. I had copied it over three nights, touching each name as I wrote it—not lifting, just *reading*, the way you might read the spine of a book you can't afford—and I had carried home fragments of two hundred strangers' lives, and some nights they talked to each other in my sleep.

Tonight I wrapped the pages in oilcloth and put them inside my shirt, against my skin, where a knife would have to negotiate.

Then I stood in front of the master ledgers themselves—the real ones, the acquisitions, eleven years of small thefts recorded in my own handwriting—and I will be honest, since honesty costs nothing in an empty room: I wanted to burn them. Not for strategy. For *relief*. Every widow's grief priced by the dram. Every soldier's courage sold to a merchant who wanted to feel brave without bleeding. Every child's first snowfall bought from a father deep in debt and resold to some collector in the capital who kept childhoods in a cabinet like butterflies. I had written every entry. My hand was on every theft. And the temptation was not righteousness—the temptation was erasure, burning the record of my own complicity and calling it mercy.

Vespera's voice in my head: *guilt burns carelessly.*

I closed the cabinet. Locked it. Put the key back on its nail.

What I did instead took nine minutes. Nine minutes, and then I was crouched at the intake ledger with a pen, writing the last entry I would ever write in this room. I entered Tomas Bell, his false kitchen, his false calluses. I dated it. I noted the appointment: *client to return, tenth bell.* And then, beneath it, in the tiny marginal shorthand I used for private notes—the shorthand Corvane had never bothered to learn, because learning his clerk's habits would have required thinking of me as a person—I added four words:

*Baited. Trap sprung. Gone.*

Let Ilsette read that. Let her know that the fish saw the hook, named the fisherman, and swam. It was spite, and I knew it was spite, and I wrote it anyway, because a man fleeing his only home is entitled to one line of graffiti.

I was blowing out the lamp when I heard footsteps in the corridor.

Not Corvane's tread—he walks like a man arriving everywhere slightly ahead of his reputation. Not Ilsette's either; hers I'd learned this week, soft and even, a lullaby rhythm. These steps were hurried, uneven, and they stopped outside the ledger room door, and then came the scrape of paper sliding under the door.

A folded note. I picked it up. The hand was Corvane's, but degraded, the letters leaning like tired sheep—his brandy hour come and gone badly, apparently. It read:

*She knows. Not the clerk—the other one. She has readers in the archive. Leave now. Leave the girl if you can; she is worth more to me alive. — C.*

I stood there with the note in my hand and felt several things at once, which is the trouble with having a memory-gift: feelings arrive in batches, whole lifetimes of them, unlabeled.

Corvane knew about the bargain. Had known, maybe, all along. And his warning was not kindness—it was inventory management. *Leave the girl if you can; she is worth more to me alive.* He was still trading. Even now, even warning me, he was pricing us both and telling me which of us he'd prefer to keep on his shelf.

I burned the note in the lamp flame and watched the ash curl, and I thought: *eleven years.* I thought: *he taught me the shorthand of loyalty and then sold me by the page.* I thought, briefly and uselessly, of the chipped mug.

Then I opened the door and went down through the sleeping guildhouse, past the dormitories where the junior clerks breathed in shifts, past the vault door with its three locks I would never lock again, out through the kitchen, down into the salt cellar where the pickled herring hung like pale chimes and the floor drain hid the passage Odile's runners used when the customs men drank too much.

Vespera was already there, hood up, pack settled, holding the lantern low. She read my face in one glance.

"He knows," I said. "He knows and he warned us and he wants to keep you."

"Sentimental of him."

"He says you're worth more alive."

"To him." She lifted the grate over the drain passage, and cold air came up out of it smelling of salt and night and distance. "Silas. Once we go down there, we're fugitives until the border or the grave. There's no appraisal fee for coming back."

"I know," I said. And then, because it was true and because no one was grading my composure anymore: "I left a note. Under the intake entry. Told her the trap was sprung."

Vespera looked at me for a long moment, and then she laughed—quietly, so the sound wouldn't climb the cellar stairs, but really laughed, her eyes crinkling over the lantern light. "Of course you did. Eleven years invisible, and your last act in the building is to sign your work." She shook her head. "Get down the hole, ledger-keeper."

I took one last breath of guild air—vellum and cedar and herring brine and eleven years of a life built carefully on top of a lie—and then I went down into the dark, with two hundred and six names against my chest and a warden's seal drying somewhere with my touch printed on it, toward the glass flats, the double-shadowed moon, and whatever waited on the far side of both.

Behind us, faint through the grate, I heard the guildhouse clock strike ten.

We had a night's lead. Perhaps.

Perhaps not even that.
