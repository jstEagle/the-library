## 13. Salt on the Wind

The glass flats do not forgive mistakes. I know this because I made three of them before moonrise and spent the fourth hour of our flight watching Silas pretend not to notice.

The first mistake was wearing my good boots. Cartographers spend their lives indoors, hunched over vellum, and we develop a weakness for footwear that announces we are people of consequence. My boots had brass eyelets. They sang against the salt crust with every step, a bright little treason with each stride.

The second was assuming the flats were flat. They are not. They are a sea that died standing up, every wave fused mid-curl into ridges of green-grey glass, and if you cross them in the dark without knowing where the ridges run, you will break an ankle or worse.

The third — and this is the one I still wince at — was trusting the road. There is no road. The maps say there is a road, because I helped draw it, because the crown pays for roads whether they exist or not, because a country that has erased its own borders will cheerfully invent its own highways. I had drawn the Salt Road myself, a confident black line running east out of the Steppe market town, and I had drawn it from a source who had never been further east than the tavern where he told me about it.

So when Silas said, quietly, "There's no road here," I felt something in my professional pride crack like river ice in spring.

"I drew it," I said.

"I know," they said. "I've read your map. It's very beautiful."

*It's very beautiful.* From anyone else that would have been a compliment. From Silas Kavanagh, ledger-keeper of the Glass Steppe Guild, it was a diagnosis. We stood on the dead sea under a sky bruising toward moonrise, two liars with one lamp between us, and I understood that the next hundred miles would be negotiated, not walked.

"Which way, then, cartographer?" Silas asked. And there it was — not mockery exactly, but the flat courtesy of someone deciding how much to trust you, weighing it the way they must weigh everything, in columns, debit and credit.

I pointed east by north, away from the invented road, toward a ridge I knew only as a smudge on a stolen fragment of vellum. "Toward the Door," I said, which was a lie, because I did not know if the Door existed, and neither did they, and we both knew both things, and we started walking anyway.

That is what fugitives are, I've decided. People who agree to share a lie for the length of a journey.

* * *

The thirteenth moon rose around midnight, and I finally understood why the old smugglers called its light *the honest lantern.*

Every other moon casts one shadow. The thirteenth casts two — a dark shadow and a pale one, thrown in slightly different directions, because its light arrives twice, once direct and once refracted through the salt haze off the glass. Navigators used to kill each other over less. On the flats, the double shadow is a compass: the true shadow points along the old salt routes, the ghost shadow leans always toward water, even water that fell out of the world a century ago. A cartographer should have known this. A cartographer trained by the crown should never have known this, because the crown's atlases show the thirteenth moon's phases but never its shadows. Erasure, I have learned, is rarely loud. Mostly it is a blank space where a fact used to live, drawn so cleanly you mistake the absence for completeness.

Silas walked with their eyes down, reading the glass the way I'd read contour lines, and when the moon cleared the eastern ridges and our shadows split and stretched across the crust — one ink-dark, one silver-pale — they stopped so abruptly I nearly walked into them.

"There," they said. "Do you see it?"

"I see it." Two shadows, mine and theirs, four shadows total, laid out on the glass like a family portrait of people who hadn't yet decided whether they were related. "My grandmother used to sing about it," I heard myself say, and then wished I hadn't, because I had never told Silas about my grandmother, and telling them felt like handing over a hostage.

But Silas only looked at me. In the doubled light their face was half warm, half cold, like a coin mid-flip. "Sing it?" they said.

"Not here."

"When, then? When we're somewhere safe?" They smiled, thin and dry. "I've been fleeing since sundown, Vespera. I don't think 'somewhere safe' exists anymore. I think we're spending the rest of our lives in the space between safe places. So sing it here."

I sang it. Quietly, badly, my voice cracking on the high line the way it always had. It's a counting song, the kind you sing to children — *one for the salt, two for the glass, three for the door that lets none pass* — and I had sung it to my sister a thousand times in a house in the capital that no longer had anything to do with me. When I finished, the flats were so silent I could hear the salt crystals ticking as they cooled.

"My grandmother sang that too," Silas said. "Different words. Same tune."

We looked at each other across four shadows, and neither of us said the obvious thing, which was: *the same song does not grow in two houses a thousand miles apart unless someone carried it, and someone carried it before the maps went blank.* Some truths you hold in your mouth until they're ready. Like a hot coal. Or a confession.

We walked on, following the dark shadow east by north.

* * *

Near dawn we sheltered in the lee of a glass ridge — a curl of the dead sea maybe thirty feet tall, hollowed underneath like a shell, floor carpeted with salt so fine it poured like sugar when you sat in it. Silas shook out the lamp, and we ate hard bread and dried fish from Odile's parting bundle, and I thought: now. If there was ever going to be an honest conversation between us, it would be here, in a seashell on a dead sea, with the hunters' dust plume still visible behind us whenever one of us checked.

"You first," Silas said, before I'd worked out how to begin. They have that trick — seeing the shape of a thing before you've admitted it has one. Ledger-keeper's instinct, probably. You can't hide a missing page from a man who keeps ledgers.

"All right." I turned the bread crust over in my fingers. "You want betrayal? I'll give you betrayal. I didn't burn my sister's name from the census rolls because I'm brave. I did it because she asked me to, and I couldn't look at her while I said no. That's the whole architecture of my courage, Silas. It's load-bearing guilt."

They didn't interrupt. They had a way of listening that made silence feel like attention instead of judgment, though I suspected it was judgment too, patiently filed.

"I spent eleven years drawing maps for the crown," I went on. "Beautiful maps. Accurate maps. And every year, accurate meant *less*. A village here that stopped being recorded. A valley there. An entire coastline — you've seen it, you grew up on the wrong side of it — gone from the atlases, replaced by a decorative border and a note: *here be salt wastes, uninhabited.* I redrew the erasure every single year and called it cartography. And I was *good* at it. Do you understand? I wasn't some coerced clerk grinding her teeth. I won awards. The Provost's Medal, twice." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "There's a medal in a drawer in the capital with my name on it, and it might as well be a brand. I earned the Thirteenth Hunt. People like me drew the country that made it possible."

"The proclamation was hidden in your atlas," Silas said. Not a question; they remembered, or they'd guessed, or — more likely, knowing them — they'd simply watched my face long enough to read the entry.

"Between the index and the foldout of the northern provinces. Sealed orders. *By decree of His Majesty: let every bearer of the unsanctioned gift be marked, and every harborer counted as bearer.* I found it because I was the one renumbering the pages." I hugged my knees. "And my first thought — my genuine, unedited first thought — was *thank the saints it's well hidden.* Not *this is monstrous.* Not *my sister.* *Thank the saints nobody will find it.* That's who I am, Silas. That's what I fled with. A woman whose conscience files things alphabetically."

Silas was quiet for a long moment. Out past the ridge, the flats were turning from grey to rose, the sun coming up on a landscape that looked like the bottom of the sky.

"I returned a memory to a client once," they said at last. "Stolen goods. Restitution. By guild law that's the worst crime there is — worse than theft, because theft feeds the guild and restitution threatens it. A man sold his daughter's face to pay a debt. Sold the *memory* of her face, I mean. Kept the daughter. Woke up loving a stranger he fed breakfast to." Their voice stayed level, but their hands had gone still in a way that struck me as expensive, controlled. "I touched his hand when I took payment and gave him back ten seconds of her laughing. Ten seconds. He cried into his sleeve and thanked me for the kindness of strangers, because he didn't know where it came from." They looked at me. "I did it because I couldn't stand the ledger being true. Not because I was brave either. So we're even."

"We're not even," I said. "I need to finish confessing. You'll want the rest when you hear it."

"Then say it."

"I stole the map fragment from the Crown Archive. The Door of Salt. I stole it, I burned my sister's name, and then—" I made myself keep going, because the whole point of confession is that you don't get to stop at the flattering parts. "When Corvane offered me passage for the fragment, I agreed *before* I met you. I was prepared to walk into the Steppe Guild and trade the only map to freedom for a guide and a head start and nothing else. And then I met you, and you had your grandmother's lullaby in your mouth, and I changed the terms without telling you. The secret bargain behind Corvane's back — you think that was strategy. Half of it was. The other half was that I liked you, and liking you made me dishonest in a new direction."

Silas tilted their head. "Dishonest toward me?"

"Dishonest *for* you. Which is worse. Ask any court cartographer — favoritism gets you hanged faster than forgery."

Something moved through their face then, quick as the refraction on the glass — amusement, I think, actual amusement, the first I'd seen survive contact with the open air. "Noted," they said. "For the record, I also lied. About the ledgers. About why I stay at the guild. I told you it's the only home I've ever known, and that's true, but it's not the reason. The reason is that the ledgers are the last place those memories exist. Every name, every sale, every stolen face and courage and grief — if the archive burns, those people lose those pieces forever. I stay because I'm the only one keeping the receipts of a robbery." A pause. "Kept. Past tense. As of two nights ago I am also a fugitive from my own filing system."

"And Corvane knows what you are."

"Corvane wrote down what I am," Silas said softly, "twenty years ago, the day he took me in. Page one of a ledger he thinks I've never seen. He's been holding my name over me so long I'd forgotten it was a threat. It took a royal proclamation to remind him to use it." They brushed salt from their palms. "So. Two betrayers, one seashell. Anything else we should declare before the hunters catch up?"

I considered. The sun was clearing the ridge now, and the glass caught it and threw it back in sheets, and for a moment the whole world was light with a few sharp edges in it.

"One thing," I said. "When we get to the Door — *if* we get to the Door — I don't intend to go through alone. Whatever else happens. I want that on the record."

Silas regarded me for a long moment with those ledger-balancing eyes. Then they reached into their coat and produced the stub of charcoal they'd taken to carrying, and a scrap of packing paper, and wrote, in their small exact hand: *Vespera Ilane, formerly Royal Cartographer, declares her intentions honestly for the first time in her career.* They handed it to me gravely. "Witnessed and entered," they said. "It's not official until it's in a ledger."

I laughed — really laughed, the sound skidding out across the flats — and tucked the scrap into my breast pocket, where it rode against my heart for six days, through everything that followed.

* * *

We made nine miles that day, and fourteen the next, learning each other's pace the way you learn a language: badly, then suddenly. Silas walked like someone who had spent twenty years being quiet in a building full of dangerous people, heel-to-toe, weight centered, a walk you could do in a library or a war. I walked like a woman who had measured distances all her life without ever crossing one, and Silas corrected my footing on the glass ridges without commentary until, on the fourth day, I realized they'd stopped correcting it, because there was nothing left to correct.

On the fifth night, camped in the shadow of a formation the smugglers' songs call the Weeping Arch — a natural bridge of glass that drips condensed brine even in the dry season — we saw the fire.

It was far to the west, back along our own trail, a bloom of orange against the dark like a wound opening. Too organized for lightning. Too large for travelers.

"That's the market road," Silas said. "Odile's tavern is three miles off that road."

We stood together at the mouth of the arch, two small silhouettes against a distant burning, and I watched Silas do arithmetic I recognized — the same cold, guilty arithmetic I'd done in the capital, *what can I afford to care about, what will caring cost, who else dies if I turn back.*

"They knew we'd come this way," I said. "Ilsette isn't chasing us. She's herding us. Every fire between here and the Door is a fence post."

"I know."

"She wants us running scared and straight, right into whatever she's built at the end of the route."

"I said I know, Vespera." But their voice cracked on my name, just slightly, and I understood that the arithmetic hurt them more than the fire did. "The question isn't what she's doing. The question is whether we're the kind of people who let Odile's tavern burn behind us while we walk toward a door that may not exist."

I looked west. The fire had settled into a steady glow, patient as a held breath. Somewhere out there was a tavern keeper who had packed dried fish into a fugitive's bag and said *go, and quickly,* and asked nothing.

Somewhere closer than that — much closer, if the smoke-shimmer under the rising thirteenth moon meant what I thought it meant — was a second light. Smaller. Moving. Following the dark shadow east by north, the way we were.

"Silas," I said. "Look east."

They turned. Far out on the flats, a lantern swung in a rhythm I knew from a lifetime of reading terrain: not a patrol's march, not a hunter's sweep. Someone stumbling. Someone carrying something heavy, or someone heavy carrying someone.

And behind that lone lantern, faint but gaining, a line of mounted lights, fanned out across the glass like the teeth of a comb.

Silas blew out our lamp without being told. In the sudden doubled moonlight we looked at each other, and I saw my own decision already forming in their face, arriving in mine, shared before either of us spoke — the first truly joint entry in whatever ledger the two of us were becoming.

"Well," Silas said, pulling their coat tight. "So much for walking straight."

We turned east, toward the stumbling lantern and the closing teeth, and ran.
