## 14. The Child Who Remembered Everything

The trouble with kindness, I've always said, is that it doesn't keep. Milk doesn't keep. Bread doesn't keep. Kindness least of all. You set it out on the sill overnight and by morning something's gotten into it, and usually the something has a warrant.

My name is Odile Marchand, and I keep a tavern at the ragged edge of the Salt Steppe market, which means I keep the accounts of half the smugglers in three counties and the secrets of most of them. It also means, as of this season, that I keep a child.

I didn't mean to. Nobody means to. Children arrive at taverns the way cats do — underfed, suspicious, and already convinced they own the place. Neme came in off the flats one evening last autumn, small for ten or old for six, hard to say, with dust in her braids and a look about her like she'd read the last page of every book in the room. She ate two bowls of my barley stew without speaking, then looked up at me and said, "You're going to give me the third bowl too. You just don't know it yet."

Reader, I gave her the third bowl.

That's how it starts. That's always how it starts.

* * *

Here's what I knew about the girl before everything went to ash: her mother had been a salt-cutter out past the Weigh Stations, and had died — or left, or been taken, the story changed depending on who told it and how much they'd drunk — and the girl had drifted into town the way driftwood does, and I'd let her sleep in the loft over the taproom because the alternative was letting her sleep in the street, and I am many things but I am not that.

Here's what I learned after: the girl remembered things.

Not ordinary remembering. I remember my husband's face, God rot his charming bones, and I remember the price of rye in the bad years, and I remember exactly what I was doing when the census men came through and asked everyone their grandmother's birthplace. Ordinary memory sits in you like water in a well. Neme's memory sat in her like a well that other people kept dropping buckets into.

She'd touch your hand — casual, nothing to it, passing you a cup — and she'd know where you'd buried your still. She'd know the name you hadn't spoken since the last Hunt. She'd know, once, sitting cross-legged on my counter while I tried to count the till around her, that my sister Marta hadn't drowned at all like we were all told, but had walked east onto the glass flats with her shoes in her hand, and that she'd smiled when she went.

I put down the coins. I sat down on a barrel. I said, "How."

Neme shrugged, the way children shrug when they've been asked an enormous question and have decided it's beneath the dignity of the answer. "It's just there," she said. "Things that happened. Things that are going to." She picked a dried currant off the counter and ate it. "Mostly going to."

"Going to," I repeated.

"Mm. Tomorrow it rains by noon. Thursday a man comes in wearing green and lies about a horse." She frowned at me, or rather through me, at some middle distance only she had keys to. "You shouldn't sell the brown mare."

"The brown mare is sold."

"Not yet," she said, and went back to her currants.

The mare threw a shoe on the north road two days later, lamed herself, and cost me forty marks in farrier's bills. I did not sell the green man's horse-lie short either; he came in Thursday, wore green, and lied about a horse. After that I started keeping a list. Not of what she said — I'm not a fool, paper burns — but of how often she was right. The list got long. The list got so long I stopped keeping it, because a list like that in a tavern on the Steppe is a thing that gets you visited.

And here is the part I will answer for at whatever door finally asks me: I knew what she was. I knew what the Thirteenth Hunt meant, knew the proclamations nailed to the market post, knew the brand-irons glowing in their little portable furnace like a traveling hell. Knew all of it, and kept her anyway, and told myself it was charity, and knew in the honest cellar of myself that it wasn't charity at all. It was greed. She was the best company I'd had since Marta died, and I was not going to give her up to a law I'd never agreed to, written by men whose grandmothers' birthplaces I could have told them if I'd wanted.

Love outside the law. That's the phrase Silas uses later, and I'll steal it now, because it fits. Everything worth loving on the Steppe is outside the law anyway. Salt itself is outside the law. Water is outside the law, in the dry months. You learn to love contraband or you learn to love nothing.

* * *

They came on a grey morning in the week the thirteenth moon was waxing toward full, and they came politely, which is the worst way.

Warden Ilsette's people didn't kick the door. They opened it. A sergeant with a face like a closed ledger stood in my taproom at an hour when the only customers were two smugglers pretending to drink coffee, and he unrolled a paper and read names from it in a flat voice, and the third name was Neme's — though they had it wrong, had it as *Nemera Vasht*, which isn't even her name, which is the name somebody gave the census men years ago, and I thought, wildly, absurdly, *they can't take her, that's not who she is,* which shows you the quality of thinking grief does.

"She's not here," I said.

"No," the sergeant agreed pleasantly. "But she will be. We'll wait."

They were courteous the way frost is courteous. They bought nothing. They sat. They asked questions with the shape of conversation and the weight of testimony. Where does she sleep. Who feeds her. Is she your daughter, mistress? No relation? Curious arrangement. And all the while Neme was up in the loft, awake — I knew she was awake, I could feel the floorboards thinking above me — and she did not come down, and I did not call her, and between us we performed the oldest play on the Steppe, the one called *Nobody Here But Us Honest People*, and we both knew how it ended because she'd probably seen it.

She came down at noon. On her own. That's the detail that lives in me like a splinter: they never found her, never cornered her, never tricked her. She washed her face, braided her hair fresh, came down the stairs with her chin up, looked at the wardens and said, "You'll want the left wrist. Everyone wants the left wrist," and held out her arm.

"Neme—" I said, and my voice did something humiliating.

"You were going to fight them," she said, matter-of-fact, taking her hand back long enough to pat my knuckles, and her skin against mine was cold and sure and knowing. "With the iron poker behind the bar. You'd have hurt one and then there'd be two of us dead instead of none." She turned to the sergeant. "Also, your lieutenant cheats at cards. You should check the tally on the wagon."

I want to tell you I did something. I want to tell you there was a moment where I moved, where the poker came up, where history bent. But the truth is the truth: four wardens, a market full of people suddenly fascinated by their own shoes, and a child holding out her wrist and telling them which one to brand. They took her out through my door into the grey light, and the crowd watched the cobbles, and the cobbles watched nothing, and the wagon rolled east toward the Weigh Station with my whole heart chained in the back of it.

The last thing she did was turn around in the wagon bed and look at me — not frightened, that's the devil of it, she wasn't frightened, she was *sorry for me* — and mouth something. I've gone over it a hundred times. Three words. I think it was *the tall smuggler.* At the time I thought it was nonsense. Grief makes gibberish of prophecy.

* * *

I gave myself one hour. That's all I allowed. One hour to sit at my own table with a bottle I don't remember opening and hate every person in my empty taproom including the chairs.

Then I got up, because the hour was done, and because there is work in hating and I am a working woman.

The poker stayed behind the bar. What I took instead was ink, paper, my riding coat, and forty years of favors, and I spent them like a drunkard spends a legacy.

First: the tall smuggler. Because she'd said it, and I'd learned not to argue with the girl. There's exactly one tall smuggler whose routes cross mine — a long, laconic salt-runner named Ferro Casque who owes me for the winter I hid his brother's ledgers inside my hymnals, and who has the useful habit of being findable precisely when he shouldn't be. I found him at dusk at the drover's well outside town, watering mules that were mostly theft.

"Ferro."

"Mistress Marchand." He touched his hat. He always touches his hat. If the world ends, Ferro will touch his hat to it.

"I need the channels. All of them. Tonight."

He studied me the way he studies weather. "That's a big spend."

"It's a child." My voice cracked on the word like ice on a pond, which I hated. "A gifted child, Ferro. Taken east to the Weigh Station on Ilsette's own order, and you know what happens at the Weigh Station, and you know what the moon's doing, and you know there's a man keeps ledgers for the Guild who — who buys anything, who knows anything, who — " I heard myself and stopped, and made my voice be a tavern-keeper's voice again, level, priced, reasonable. "There's a man at the Guild named Silas. Get word to him. Tell him Odile sends it, and tell him the words she sent: *the ledger has a living page.*"

Ferro's hat-hand stopped halfway to his brim. "That's guild-speech."

"That's her speech. She said it would mean something to him. She said — " and here my throat closed, and I pushed through it, because a message is a message — "she said *he'll understand, he touches everything.*"

Long silence. Wind off the flats, tasting of salt and coming change. Somewhere east the thirteenth moon was climbing, wrong-colored, doubled at the edge like a coin rubbed thin.

"I'll ride tonight," Ferro said. "Cost you the hymnal debt."

"Cost me whatever you like. Bring her home and I'll sing the hymns to you personally."

He almost smiled. Then he didn't, because neither of us is stupid enough to smile about the odds. He swung up onto the mule, gathered the second one's lead, and paused. "Mistress. The Station's no place to send hope."

"No," I said. "Hope goes where it likes. That's the whole trouble with it."

He rode east. I stood at the well until the dark took him, and then I went back and lit every lamp in my tavern, all of them, wasteful and defiant, because the crowd that wouldn't watch a child taken deserved to at least see one window burning where she used to sleep.

* * *

Three days. I'll give you the three days honestly, because everyone else in this story gets their heroics written down and I get the waiting, and waiting is the truest work there is.

Day one I fed the wardens. Yes — they'd left a rear guard, two men rotating through my taproom watching the road west in case the girl's "associates" came sniffing, and I fed them stew and kept my face pleasant and poured their beer slow, and listened. Wardens talk when they're fed. They talked about the Station's holding pens being full, about a Warden Ilsette who was "not like the others, fair, you know, proper," about quotas and moon-turns and a rumor that the Guild itself was selling names, which made my blood go cold and then hot, because if the Guild was selling names then the man I'd pinned my hope to kept the very book doing the selling, and I lay awake that night doing arithmetic with that and arriving nowhere.

Day two the rain came, the one Neme had promised me weeks ago by noon, right on schedule, and I stood in the doorway of my empty taproom and laughed until it turned into something else, and my pot-girl Petra — good girl, deaf in one ear, loyal in both — put her hand on my shoulder and left it there a while, and that was day two entire.

Day three a boy in a fish-oil coat came in off the east road, ordered nothing, and set a salt-glazed stone on my counter on his way to the privy. Inside the stone, wrapped in oilcloth: a strip of paper in a cramped clerk's hand, smelling faintly of ink and lamp-smoke.

*Received your message. Understand it better than you know. Do nothing alone. Keep her name out of every mouth. I am coming, and I am not coming alone. — S.*

And beneath that, smaller, pressed hard enough to tear the paper, as if the pen had been gripping it like a hand:

*Tell her the ledger remembers her too.*

I read it four times. I burned it in the stove. I stood there watching the curl of it and understood exactly two things: that help was coming from a direction I hadn't dared hope in, and that the man sending it was more afraid than I was, which takes some doing.

Then I went upstairs, took down my father's old fowling piece from behind the loose panel where it had slept for twenty years, and began, with a housekeeper's thoroughness, to plan a rescue I had no business planning — because the girl had told me a tall smuggler, and the tall smuggler had carried the word, and the word was coming back armed, and I am Odile Marchand, and I do not leave my children in the hands of the law.

Outside, eastward, the thirteenth moon rose full and doubled over the glass, and somewhere between here and its light, a wagon full of stolen children was rolling toward a Door that only some of them would ever reach — unless the rest of us moved faster than our fear.

I saddled the mare. Not the brown one. The brown one's luck had taught me better.
