## 15. Smugglers' Liturgy

The smugglers' camp announced itself by smell before anything else—brine and woodsmoke and something sweeter underneath, like honey left too long in the sun. Then came the singing.

I want to be clear about what I expected. Odile's letter had promised "salt-runners who ask no questions," and I had imagined grim men with knives in their boots, communicating in grunts and hand signals, the sort of people who made Corvane's guild look like a knitting circle. I had not imagined forty-odd weathered faces around a fire, passing a clay jug, singing in three-part harmony about—well, I couldn't tell yet. The words were old Steppe-tongue, half-swallowed vowels my grandmother had used for scolding and blessing, and I hadn't heard them spoken aloud since I was nine years old.

"They always sing at moonrise," Vespera murmured beside me. She'd gone very still in the way she did when she was memorizing something. "Every night. The outriders said it was superstition."

"It might be."

"No." Her eyes narrowed at the fire, at the singers, at nothing. "It's not. Listen. They repeat the same phrases in the same order, but the middle verse changes every night. That's not a hymn. Hymns don't update."

Only Vespera would stand in a stranger's camp, exhausted, hunted, her sister's name still fresh as an open wound behind her ribs, and diagnose the folk music. It should have been annoying. Instead it felt like the first warm thing all week.

* * *

The smugglers' matriarch was called Sefa, which I later learned meant "the one who weighs," and she received us sitting on a salt-crusted chest that probably contained either the camp's entire fortune or its entire food supply. Possibly both. She looked at me the way appraisers look at unmarked silver: politely, thoroughly, and with a running estimate of what I'd fetch if things went badly.

"Odile's mark," she said finally, turning the token over in her palm—the tavern-keeper's little stamped tin bird. "She vouches for you?"

"She does."

"She vouches for the cartographer too?" A jerk of her chin toward Vespera. "Royal ink is royal ink, even when it's run away."

"I burned my own name out of the census," Vespera said evenly. "You're welcome to check the ash."

Something moved at the corner of Sefa's mouth—not quite a smile, more like the memory of one. "Sit. Eat. You'll work tomorrow. Nobody rides free on the flats; the glass cuts tiresome."

We ate. Stewed goat, dense black bread, tea brewed from something that tasted like pine needles had opinions about us. Around us the camp talked in low voices about routes and prices, and beneath it all ran the current of that song, still going, the middle verse turning over like a page.

It was an old woman two fires down who finally explained—or started to. She caught me listening, tapped her ear, and said, "You hear it right. You've got the accent under your silence."

"My grandmother," I said carefully. "She was from here. Before the erasure."

"Then you know the songs."

"I know a lullaby. Maybe two. She sang them when she thought I was asleep."

The old woman laughed, a dry rustle like wind through reeds. "Nobody sings a child asleep in this country, boy or girl or otherwise. We sing them awake. Come here at second moonrise. Bring your lullabies. We'll see what your grandmother left you."

* * *

Second moonrise came gray-green and doubled, the way it had all season—the thirteenth moon hanging fat and strange above its paler twin, throwing two shadows off everything, including me. I sat by the old woman's fire feeling like a man reporting to an appointment he doesn't remember making.

Her name was Maret. She spread a strip of hide across her knees, blank except for a scatter of stitched beads, and began to sing.

Low, unhurried, the same song from the fires—and as she sang, she touched the beads. One, then the next, then a third, in time with certain syllables. When the middle verse changed—a different phrase tonight than last night, exactly as Vespera had noticed—her fingers skipped two beads and landed elsewhere.

"Now you," she said.

"That's not a song," I said slowly. "That's—"

"That's the Long Road, north fork, water at the third stone, avoid the white hollow because it froze wrong this year." She shrugged. "Tonight's verse says the hollow's safe again by the next dark. The singers change it when the scouts come back. Every verse of every song we have is a road, or a well, or a place the ice lies thin. Why do you think the crown never found our caches? You can't torture a map out of someone who only knows how to sing it."

"And if they arrest the singers?"

Maret smiled with most of her teeth. "Everyone here can sing. Everyone. The cook, the children, the man who repairs saddles. Take one of us, ten of us, the map keeps walking around on its own legs. Your guildmaster kept knowledge in books so he could own it. We keep ours in throats so nobody can."

I thought of the ledger room. Rows of bound volumes, chained, keyed, mine. Knowledge as property, knowledge as leverage, knowledge with a price list. And here: knowledge poured into melody and handed to anyone patient enough to listen, owned by everyone, therefore by no one.

My grandmother's lullaby rose up my throat before I decided anything. I hummed it first, half-certain, then gave it words in her voice, in her accent, the half-swallowed vowels Maret had smelled on me.

She stopped me four lines in.

"Again. Slower."

I sang slower. Her fingers moved, touching nothing now, tracing shapes on her knee. By the end of the first verse she had gone very quiet. By the second verse, other people had drifted close—Sefa among them, and Vespera, who had appeared from nowhere with the silent competence she applied to everything, including eavesdropping.

"Do you know what you just sang?" Maret asked.

"A lullaby. About a boat. Or a bird. I was never sure which."

"It's the Door approach." Her voice had dropped, gone careful, the way voices go near sleeping things. "From the south ridge. The 'bird' is the light on the salt—the way the Door glimmers before it opens. The 'boat' is the walker. The third verse—" she looked at me almost gently, "—the third verse tells you where to stand so the Door sees you coming."

The doubled moonlight felt suddenly heavier on my shoulders. Somewhere behind my sternum, grief and recognition arrived simultaneously, the way they always do, wearing each other's clothes.

My grandmother hadn't been soothing me to sleep. She'd been hiding a map inside a child's drowsy head, tucking it in among dreams where no hunter would think to search. She'd known—I would bet the guild's whole vault she'd known—that the official maps were already being scrubbed, that paper could be confiscated, that names could be struck. So she put the country somewhere they couldn't reach and waited thirty years for it to surface.

"Vespera," I said. My voice came out strange. "Your fragment. The stolen piece of atlas. Where does the Door sit relative to the south ridge?"

She was already sketching in the frost-dust by the fire, quick strokes, cartographer's shorthand. "If the ridge runs there, and the salt pan slopes east—" Her chalk-stained finger stopped. "The third verse position would be..." She looked up at me. "It matches. Silas, it matches the geography. Your grandmother's lullaby agrees with a map drawn by royal surveyors a century ago."

"Then she wasn't singing fiction."

"She was singing survey data," Vespera said, and there was wonder in it, naked and unguarded, the first unguarded thing I'd seen her wear. "In a cradle song. Do you understand what this means? If enough fragments survive—if enough families hid pieces in songs, in games, in counting rhymes—we could reconstruct the whole route. Not from archives." Her hand flattened over her heart, briefly, unconsciously. "From people. From the things they refused to stop knowing."

Maret snorted. "Royal ink discovers what grandmothers have known since the burning. Welcome aboard."

* * *

I didn't sleep that night. I walked the edge of the camp instead, past the picketed goats, out to where the glass flats began their pale shine under the double moons, and I let the tide come in.

That's the only word for it. Tide. All evening, things had been surfacing—not memories exactly, but the sediment under them. The way my grandmother's hands moved when she kneaded bread, always three turns left, one right, and how I'd assumed it was habit rather than instruction. The counting rhyme she used for buttoning my coat: five buttons, but she always named six. Six, when there were only five. I'd thought she was getting old. She was giving me a bearing. Button six was the well that isn't marked, the spring under the dead tree, and I had stood in front of it once, aged seven, and drunk from it without knowing I was navigating.

I pressed the heel of my hand against my eyes and laughed, or something adjacent to laughing, because the alternative was crying and I'd done my month's ration of that already.

A gift like mine makes you believe knowledge is something you take. Touch a wrist, lift a memory, carry it away. Taking, always taking, and the guilt of taking, and lately—the ledger copies hidden against my ribs—the terrible arithmetic of what I've taken and why. But my grandmother had never taken anything from me. She had planted. Quietly, in the dark, in the shape of tenderness, she had planted a whole country in a child and trusted the soil.

What else is buried in me? What else did she sow that hasn't bloomed yet? What did the neighbors carry, the aunts, the cousins scattered across a kingdom that erased them—how many lullabies are currently being sung to oblivious infants from here to the capital, every one of them a page torn from the atlas and folded into a cradle?

The hunters burn books. The hunters brand wrists. But you cannot brand a melody. You cannot confiscate the inside of a mother's mouth. The crown won a century ago and has been losing ever since, one bedtime at a time.

I turned back toward the fires. Vespera sat apart from the others, working by lantern-light on the map fragment, cross-referencing it against notes she'd taken during Maret's lesson—already stitching the sung country and the drawn country together, thread by thread. She looked up as I approached, and in the doubled moonlight her two shadows lay side by side on the salt, and I thought: hers and mine, running parallel all the way to the horizon.

"You're awake," she observed.

"You're observant."

"One of us should be." But she shifted, making room on the flat stone beside her—an offer, not an order. I took it. For a while neither of us spoke. The singers had gone to bed; the songs stayed anyway, humming faintly in my skull, roads unwinding in the dark.

"The third verse," she said eventually. "Where to stand so the Door sees you coming. You'll teach it to me properly? My tongue is terrible. I flatten the vowels."

"You'll learn. You learned census codes fast enough."

"Census codes didn't come with my grandmother's hands around them." She said it lightly, but her pen had stopped moving, and she was looking at the moons instead of the map. "Silas. When we get there—when the Door opens—do you think anyone will remember why? After a hundred years of erasure, will there be anyone left who actually knows what they're walking toward?"

I thought of Neme, out there somewhere in a hunter's wagon, remembering things that hadn't happened yet. I thought of the ledgers burning in a dream I kept having. I thought of my grandmother's six buttons.

"I think," I said, "that a hundred years is nothing to a song. Songs don't need anyone to remember why. They just need someone to sing them forward. The why comes back with the tune."

She nodded slowly, and wrote something in the margin of her notes—small, private, angled away from me. Cartographers and their margins.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of skimmed milk, and with it came trouble, because dawn usually travels in that kind of company.

One of the outriders came in fast from the west, horse lathered, and spoke low and urgent to Sefa. I caught only fragments—my Steppe-tongue was good enough for lullabies and bad enough for everything else—but I caught the word for "crown," and the word for "wagons," and the word for "weigh station," and I saw Sefa's face do something complicated and cold.

She crossed the camp to where Vespera and I stood. Behind her, quietly, the camp was already moving—striking tents, banking fires, the whole settlement folding itself up like a fist.

"Your friend Odile sent word ahead of you," Sefa said. "There's a child. Gifted, small, taken north two days ago with a wagon-load of others. They'll pass the weigh station at Grey Ford within three days." Her eyes went from me to Vespera and back, weighing, the one who weighs doing what she was named for. "The station holds maybe eight wardens. The smugglers know the ground better than the wardens' own mothers. Some in this camp say go. Some say the risk is death for everyone."

"And what do you say?"

"I say," she said, "that the child's grandmother taught me the same song she taught you, a long time ago, on a night much colder than this one. So I am not neutral. But you two—you've been running from the crown for weeks. Running is sensible. So before I spend my people's lives, I'm asking: are you runners? Or are you something else?"

The doubled morning light fell long and pale across the flattening camp. Vespera's hand found mine without looking, without ceremony—just grip, and question, and answer all at once.

I thought of a lullaby about standing where the Door could see you coming.

"We're something else," I said.
