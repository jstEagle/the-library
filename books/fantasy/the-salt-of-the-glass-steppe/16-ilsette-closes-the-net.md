## 16. Ilsette Closes the Net

I have broken more men with a cup of tea than other wardens have broken with thumbscrews. This is not a boast; it is an observation about the economy of cruelty. The state provides me instruments, and I use almost none of them, because fear does its own work if you simply wait in the room long enough and pour politely.

Guildmaster Corvane had been waiting for three days when I finally granted him the interview he'd been begging for since my arrival at the Salt Steppe outpost. Three days of petitions delivered by increasingly nervous clerks, each one promising cooperation, transparency, the full weight of the Guild's resources behind His Majesty's Thirteenth Hunt. I let the requests pile up on my desk like snowfall, and then I invited him to tea.

He arrived looking exactly as I expected: a tall man gone soft at the edges, silver-streaked beard trimmed with the precision of someone who believes grooming is a form of armor, hands folded on his knees as though they might otherwise wander toward something incriminating. He looked at my teapot the way a condemned man looks at the rope—wondering how personal this was going to become.

"Warden Ilsette." He inclined his head. "The Guild stands ready to assist the crown in every particular."

"Of course it does." I poured. "Sugar?"

"No, thank you."

"More's the pity. It's the only honest thing left in this province." I set his cup down and opened the ledger I'd brought—the Guild's own client registry, the one his clerks had surrendered two days ago with suspicious promptness. "Tell me about page forty-one."

Something moved behind his eyes. Small, quick, professional. A man adjusting to bad news he'd been bracing for.

"Page forty-one," he repeated.

"The client listed as *Marrow, J., salt factor*. Recorded purchase: one memory of a brother, sold in autumn." I turned the page toward him. "Except your clerk wrote the entry in ink that didn't exist until spring. Someone recopied this page, Guildmaster. Badly. The hand trembles on the descenders."

"Faded entries are refreshed routinely," Corvane said. "Preservation of records is—"

"I know what preservation is. I have made a career of it." I sipped my tea. "Here is what interests me. The original entry for Marrow, J. described a different sale. We recovered fragments from the carbon press beneath your archive floor—did you know paper leaves ghosts? Everything written above it presses down into the sheets below. Your archive remembers what you've tried to make it forget." I smiled at him, and I confess I enjoyed it. "The ghost says Marrow, J. was gifted. And that you sold his name to a crown assessor eleven days before the Thirteenth Hunt was even proclaimed."

The silence stretched. Corvane's hands stayed folded, but the knuckles went white as salt crusts.

"You misunderstand," he began.

"I rarely do. It would be convenient if I did more often." I closed the ledger. "Let me tell you what I understand, and you can correct me where I'm wrong. You learned the Hunt was coming before the proclamation went public—you have friends in the chancery, or you bought them. You understood immediately what a list of gifted clients would be worth. So you began editing. Selling names quietly, one at a time, to buyers who could keep their purchases discreet. And when the official decree came down, you presented yourself as the crown's most devoted servant, offering the very records you'd already been bleeding dry." I refilled his cup without asking. "Have I missed anything?"

"You paint survival as treason," he said quietly.

"I paint nothing. I merely note what's on the canvas." I leaned back. "Here is my problem, Guildmaster. I believe you're lying to me now—not about the past, but about the present. You've given me the ledgers, yes. But a man who sells his clients once will sell them twice, and a man who edits pages will edit the ones I need most. So I find myself wondering what else is missing."

"There is nothing else—"

"Marrow, J.," I said. "Where is he?"

Corvane's pause was answer enough.

"He fled," I said. "Or someone warned him. Which means either your operation leaks, or you wanted him gone before we could ask him questions. Either way—" I stood, and he flinched, which pleased me less than it should have. There was no triumph in it. I want to be clear about that. There never has been. But pleasure is a hound too, and it hunts alongside duty whether you whistle for it or not. "Either way, I'm done taking tea."

* * *

They found Marrow, J. in a drainage culvert outside the salterns, half-frozen, carrying forged papers so incompetent that I felt a brief, tired sympathy. My second-in-command, Captain Dree, wanted to take him straight to the branding post. The law was clear. The Thirteenth Hunt did not concern itself with circumstances.

I interviewed him first. Alone. This is my habit, and Dree thinks it's sentiment. It isn't. It's method. Every branded person is a page torn out of a book, and I have spent thirty years learning to read the tears.

Marrow sat across from me with his wrists bound and his breath steaming, and he looked at me with something worse than hatred. He looked at me with recognition.

"You're the one they talk about," he said. "The gentle voice."

"People say many things."

"They say you caught your own brother." His bound hands twitched. "That you signed the report yourself. That you watched him burn and went home and slept."

Ah. There it was. Even here, at the edge of the map, in a country whose name had been scraped off every official document a century ago, they told the story of Warden Ilsette and her brother Tam. Stories travel faster than warrants. They travel faster than regret.

"Is that why you ran so badly?" I asked. "Hoping to meet the famous monster? To see for yourself?"

"I ran badly," Marrow said, "because the guildmaster sent word I had a day to disappear, and a day is not enough time to become someone else." His voice cracked on the last word, and he hated himself for it—I could see the hatred, precise and immediate, the way a man hates his own flinching. "You want to know if I'm gifted. Yes. I carry grief like a lamp. I can hand it to people. I've done it at funerals, for free, for years. That's the whole crime. That's the whole *empire-toppling crime*."

I wrote it down. My hand did not shake. I note this because it should have.

"Take him to Dree," I said to the guard at the door.

Marrow stopped at the threshold. "Do you ever think about him?" he asked. "Your brother."

Every day. Every single morning, in the space between waking and remembering who I had chosen to be. But that was not an answer I could give, so I gave the one I had practiced for thirty years.

"I think about the law," I said. "It's kinder than memory. Memory lies. The law at least tells you the same lie every time."

He looked at me for a long moment with those grief-lamped eyes, and then he said, softly, "No wonder you hunt us. We're the only ones who can see you."

Then the guard took him away, and I sat alone in the cold room with my notes, and I did not sleep that night, and I did not write that down either.

* * *

The falsification charge against Corvane I handled privately, because scandal serves the Hunt poorly. I summoned him to the weigh station at dusk, laid the ghost-pressed papers on the table between us, and watched a proud man understand that he had been weighed.

"Treason against the crown's records carries the brand," I told him. "You know the statute. You've profited from it often enough."

"Warden—" His composure finally cracked, and underneath it was not dignity but arithmetic. I could nearly hear the sums running: assets, allies, exits, all of them closing. "I have served the Hunt. Every name I gave you—"

"Was bought and paid for. Do not dress commerce as service. I've met too much of both to confuse them." I slid a document across the table. "However. The crown prefers working instruments to decorative examples, and I prefer a hound that knows the scent. Sign this. Full authority to act under my warrant, in exchange for your continued usefulness—and your skin."

He read it twice. I watched hope and humiliation fight for possession of his face, and watched humiliation win, because it always does; hope is a poor tenant, always behind on rent.

"And if my usefulness ends?" he asked.

"Then we revisit the statute." I offered the pen. "Sign, Guildmaster. Sentiment is for the branded. You and I are still on the right side of the iron."

He signed. His hand shook on the descenders. I noticed, and said nothing, and filed the trembling away with all the other evidence of who Corvane was when no one was grading his performance.

"Now," I said. "There is a clerk in your house. Quiet one. Keeps the ledgers. Kavanagh—Silas Kavanagh."

"What of them?" Careful, that. Not *him*, not *her*. Corvane was a careful liar, and careful liars reveal themselves in their care.

"My people have been through your archives twice. On the second pass, a page went missing—the register of acquisitions for the week Vespera Lys arrived. The same week your new cartographer-client struck her bargain. The same week, I am reliably informed, that Kavanagh worked late three nights running." I folded my hands. "A clerk who removes pages is either protecting someone or protecting themselves. In my experience the second thing is usually the first thing wearing gloves."

"Kavanagh has served the Guild faithfully for—"

"So did you. Look how faithfully." I stood. "You will watch them. You will report everything—the hours, the visitors, the moods, the mistakes. Especially the mistakes. Gifted people are careless with touch; it's the one sense they can't help using. Watch their hands."

"And if I find nothing?"

"Then you'll report that too, with equal diligence, and I will admire your loyalty to a clerk over your loyalty to your own skin, and we will both learn something about the other." I walked him to the door. At the threshold he paused—a small human hesitation, the kind I catalogued rather than pitied.

"You had a brother," he said. It wasn't a question. It was a gambit, the last card of a desperate man, played clumsily. "People say—"

"People say a great deal. Go and hound, Guildmaster. It's what you're for now."

After he left, I sat with the lamp turned low, and I took out the little tin I keep at the bottom of my dispatch case, and I held the token inside it without opening the lid. A brand-token. Iron, palm-sized, stamped with the sun-and-circle of the Thirteenth Hunt—or rather with the Twelfth Hunt, the one before, the one where I learned the trade. It had come off Tam's wrist after the burning. The surgeon gave it to me because I was family, and family, the law said, deserved a keepsake.

Family deserved a keepsake. I have thought about that sentence for thirty years and I still cannot decide whether it is the cruelest or stupidest thing the crown ever taught me.

Tam had the gift of finding things. Lost keys, lost children, lost roads—he could put his hand on a map and feel where the missing thing was, the way you feel heat from a stove. He found a drowned girl once, in the canal district, and pulled her body out so her mother could bury her instead of waiting for the river. He did it for free. He did it *always* for free. When the Twelfth Hunt was proclaimed, I had a choice, and I have spent three decades telling myself it wasn't a choice—that I reported what the law required me to report, that the alternative was losing my commission, my standing, my ability to protect anyone at all, that a warden who shields the gifted is a warden who gets replaced by one who doesn't, and the replacements are always worse.

All true. Every word of it true, and none of it mattering, because Tam stood on the platform and looked for me in the crowd, and I was there, and he found me—he could always find things—and what he did with his face in that last moment was not accusation. It was worse. It was *understanding*. He understood why I did it. He forgave me while the irons were still heating, and I have carried that forgiveness like a stone in the boot ever since, a small wrongness that turns every step into a question.

We are the only ones who can see you, Marrow had said.

Yes. That was the wound and the whole of it. Not guilt—guilt would have been simpler, guilt can be confessed, absolved, archived. What I carried was visibility. Somewhere out there were people who could look at me and know exactly what I was, and I had built a life on the premise that no one ever would.

So I hunted them. Of course I did. You hunt what shames you. Every warden knows it; none of us says it. We call it duty and we polish it every morning until we can see our faces in it, and the faces we see are calm, and calm is just shame that has learned posture.

I put the tin away. Tomorrow there would be work.

* * *

Work came quickly, as it does when you feed it.

Corvane proved a better hound than I'd expected—desperation improves most animals' noses. Within six days he had assembled a portrait of his clerk that was almost admirably thorough. Kavanagh kept irregular hours. Kavanagh received a visitor in the map room after midnight, twice. Kavanagh had requested copies of the salt-road toll schedules, which no bookkeeper needs. And on the night the fugitive Vespera Lys vanished from the Guild house along with a stolen map fragment, a stable boy saw Kavanagh's lamp burning until the fourth bell—and saw, more damningly, two horses saddled in the Guild's rear yard, one of which returned riderless at dawn.

"Riderless," I repeated.

"The horse came back lame. Or frightened." Corvane stood in my office with his new brand-scar hidden under a glove he must have thought I didn't notice. The iron I'd threatened him with I had never actually used—but I'd had the documents drawn, and Corvane knew the documents existed, and fear writes itself on a man more permanently than any stamp. "The boy couldn't say more. He was paid not to."

"By whom?"

"Kavanagh, presumably. Or the smugglers. The salt roads crawl with them this season."

I looked at the map spread on my table—the Glass Steppe, rendered in the crown's confident ink, all its borders neat and fictional. Somewhere on that clean paper, two people were moving through territory that officially did not exist, heading toward a door that officially was a myth, guided by a woman whose cartographic skill I had once commended in writing. I remembered Vespera Lys's file: brilliant, insubordinate, dismissed for *doctrinal rigidity regarding border verities*. I had liked that phrase. Doctrinal rigidity regarding border verities. The empire punishing a mapmaker for noticing that maps lie.

"They'll go for the Door," I said. "Lys knows the way; that's why she stole the fragment. And Kavanagh—" I paused. "Kavanagh is the interesting one. A bookkeeper who runs, who saddles horses at night, who pays witnesses. Bookkeepers don't run unless the books contain them."

"You suspect the gift."

"I suspect everything. It's the job." I traced the route east with one finger, across flats marked only with surveyor's blanks. "But suspicion is not proof, and the Hunt runs on proof. So we will get some. Send riders to the weigh stations—all of them, from the salterns to the ridge line. Double the toll inspections. Anyone traveling east without harvest papers is detained. And put a price on the pair. Not too high. High prices attract professionals, and professionals bring paperwork. I want hungry people. Hungry people talk."

Corvane hesitated. "And if they reach the Door?"

"Then the Door will do what Doors do, which is nothing. It's a story, Guildmaster. Salt and moonlight and old women's songs. The country beyond it is erased—erased means gone, that is the entire function of erasure. Whatever Lys thinks she's leading them to, she's leading them to a wall of rock and a hundred years of wishful thinking." I said it firmly, because saying things firmly is half of believing them, and I needed to believe it. Because the other possibility—the possibility that the Door was real, that the erased country was real, that everything my empire had scraped off its maps still lived somewhere, breathing, uncounted, free—that possibility was not a threat to the crown.

It was a threat to me. Because if the country beyond the Door existed, then erasure failed. Then the burned could be unbured in memory, the reported could be unreported, and somewhere in the arithmetic of the universe there was an account where Tam's death had been entered wrongly and might yet be corrected. And I did not know what I would do if I stood in front of that correction. That was the truth under the truth: I was not chasing Silas Kavanagh to stop them.

Part of me—small, well-disciplined, locked in its tin at the bottom of my case—wanted to see what they knew.

"Prepare the column," I told Corvane. "Light riders only. We ride at moonrise."

He bowed and withdrew, and I stood a moment longer over the blank spaces on the map, the places where the empire's ink had run out and refused to admit it. Then I closed the case over the tin, over the token, over the brother, and went to give the orders that would send me east, hunting a clerk and a cartographer toward a door I half-prayed was a myth—

—and half-prayed, God forgive me, was not.
