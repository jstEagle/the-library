## 17. Rescue at the Weigh Station

### SILAS

The weigh station sat on the salt road like a tooth in a rotten jaw—a squat stone building where hunters weighed cargo, captives, and conscience with equal indifference. Odile's smuggler contact had drawn us its bones in spilled beer on a tavern table: barracks to the east, holding pens below, a loading yard where wagons came and went at moonrise.

"Twelve wardens," Vespera had said, studying the beer-map like it was an atlas she could correct. "Maybe fourteen."

"Maybe we should wait for a bigger plan," I'd said, which was the kind of thing a sensible coward says, and which everyone at the table had correctly ignored.

So now I crouched behind a salt-crusted wall with my heart doing arithmetic it hadn't agreed to, while the thirteenth moon hung overhead like an unpaid debt. Somewhere in that building was Neme—the child who remembered everything, the child whose rescue Odile had begged of us in a message written in smuggler cipher that took me two hours and one headache to decode. *They took her from under my roof. You owe me nothing, but I'm asking anyway.*

Odile had sheltered us when we were nobody. That was the trouble with kindness: it accrued interest.

Vespera touched my shoulder. In her hand, the stolen map fragment—her old life, folded up small enough to be a weapon. "Yard clears when the wagon leaves," she whispered. "We have maybe four hundred breaths."

"You've counted breaths before."

"I've counted worse." Her eyes caught the moonlight. "Stay behind me until the pens. Then do what you do."

*What you do.* A month ago, what I did was keep ledgers. Now it was this: slipping into a hunter station under a borrowed dark, about to commit a crime so large the crown would need a new word for it.

The wagon rolled out at moonset, wheels grinding salt. The gate swung wide, and we moved through the gap like smoke through a keyhole.

* * *

### VESPERA

I had spent eleven years making maps for people who used them to find other people. Tonight the skill ran backward, and it fit my hands better than it ever had honestly.

The station's layout was standard crown work—I'd surveyed three of them myself, once, telling myself they were customs houses. Barracks east. Weighmaster's office north. Holding pens beneath, accessed through a stair behind the salt stores. I knew the architecture of captivity the way some people know hymns.

Silas moved beside me with the particular grace of someone who has spent their life being overlooked and has finally decided to make a profession of it. We crossed the yard between lantern-pools. A warden coughed thirty feet away and did not look up from his dice.

The stair door was locked. Of course it was locked.

"Salt store," I breathed, pointing. "There'll be a second entrance—they always build one, in case of fire. Nobody burns alive with inventory."

"How do you know?"

"Because I drew them." I didn't tell him how many nights I'd lain awake after those surveys, wondering what went down those stairs. Wondering was cheaper than knowing. Knowing would have required resigning, and I'd had a sister, and a pension, and a talent for not looking.

We found the second entrance behind barrels marked *BRINE — CROWN PROPERTY*, and I picked the lock with two pins and eleven years of guilt.

The smell rose up to meet us first: unwashed bodies, salt rot, fear. Fear has a smell. I learned that tonight and will never unlearn it.

Below, lamplight showed me rows of cages—not cells, cages, iron-barred boxes built for things smaller than people—and in them, shapes. A dozen. More. An old man sitting very straight. Two women holding each other. Children.

And Neme, small and fierce-eyed in the corner cage, who looked up at us with no surprise at all.

"Took you long enough," she said.

* * *

### SILAS

Children are the only honest critics left in this country.

The locks surrendered to Vespera's pins one by one—she worked fast and silent, jaw set, and I kept watch on the stair with my palms sweating against my thighs. The old man came out of his cage upright and dignified, as if he'd been released rather than freed. One of the women started to cry and then stopped herself, because crying makes noise, and everyone down here understood the economics of noise.

Neme slipped out last and stood looking up at me. "Odile sent you."

"Odile sent word. We came ourselves."

"She said someone would come who remembers things." The child studied my face with the unnerving directness of the gifted. "You're scared."

"Terrified."

"That's good. The ones who aren't scared die first." She said it the way other children recite multiplication tables.

Then the stair above us filled with boots.

Later, I would replay this moment many times, looking for the decision point, the fork where everything turned. There wasn't one. There was just the sound of wardens descending, and twelve freed prisoners with nowhere to run, and Vespera's hand finding mine in the dark, and the simple arithmetic of what happened next.

A lantern came around the stairwell. Behind it, a warden—young, nervous, brand-iron still glowing on a rack by the door, which meant they'd been mid-work when our raid began. His eyes went from us to the empty cages, and his mouth opened to shout.

Vespera hit him with a barrel stave. He went down hard, and the lantern broke, and the shout came anyway—from above, from everywhere—and then the whole night came apart.

* * *

### VESPERA

I have been in battles exactly twice in my life, and both times I discovered the same thing: plans survive approximately as long as it takes to draw them.

We got the prisoners moving. That part worked—the old man herded the weak, Neme pulled the children, and Silas and I made a corridor of desperation toward the brine-room stairs. But the yard was already filling with wardens, lanterns blooming like terrible flowers, and the gate we'd come through might as well have been a century away.

"Salt stores," I gasped. "There's a loading hatch—"

"There is?" Silas said.

"I drew this station, remember? I know where the bodies are buried. Literally, there's a pit behind the—" A crossbow bolt took the lantern out of a warden's hand six feet from my head, and I decided the architectural lecture could wait.

What saved us, in the end, was the thing I least expected: the prisoners themselves. The old man—his name was Perrin, though I didn't learn it until later—took the bolt-slit in the salt store wall and widened it with his bare hands and sheer offended dignity, and the women passed children through it into the night like loaves of bread over a market crowd. Ordinary people, branded and starved, doing the work of soldiers because no soldiers had come for them.

I got Neme through. I got three others through. And then I turned around and saw Silas standing frozen in the middle of the chaos, staring at something on the floor.

One of the freed women had collapsed.

* * *

### SILAS

She had fallen trying to lift her sister. Brand-fever, I realized—one of the fresh brands on her forearm gone septic, the skin around it angry and black. She'd walked out of her cage on pure will and her body had finally sent the bill.

Wardens pouring down the stairs. Vespera shouting my name. The woman on the stones, shaking, her teeth chattering out words I couldn't parse.

And I did the thing I have done my whole life without meaning to, the thing I'd hidden in ledger rooms and behind gloves and careful distance.

I knelt and took her hand.

The pain came into me like water finding a crack.

Understand: I have lifted memories all my life, quietly, accidentally, sometimes deliberately. Memory is weightless. Memory slips. Pain does not slip. Pain comes with hooks. Her brand-fever poured up my arm and settled into my bones like molten lead poured into a mold shaped exactly like me. I felt the iron. I felt the day it was pressed to her—felt the square, the crowd, the smell of her own skin burning. Felt her sister's grip on her other hand. Felt every hour since, the fever climbing, the world going hot and narrow.

I heard myself make a sound I have never made before.

Someone screamed. It took me a moment to understand it was me.

And then—it stopped. Not the pain; the hiding. Because when the fever passed out of her and into me, something else passed too: her memory of the branding, whole, and my gift rode it like a rider, and every warden in that room *felt it happen*. They knew that sensation. They'd caused it a hundred times. The pull of a gift working, the air going strange and sweet—

The young warden Vespera had dropped was propped against the wall, watching me with his mouth open.

"You're one of them," he whispered. "You're—you're *gifted*."

The whole room heard it. The whole room would remember it. There was no ledger thick enough, no glove deep enough, no careful invisible life left anywhere in the world.

"Yes," I said, through the fire in my arm, and found, to my astonishment, that saying it out loud felt less like dying than I'd expected. It felt like setting down a load carried for thirty years. "Now get out of my way."

* * *

### VESPERA

He stood up burning. That is the only way I can describe it. The fever had gone into him visibly—I could see it in the sweat on his face, the tremor in his frame—and he straightened anyway, and the young warden scrambled aside, and the other wardens hesitated, because men hesitate before the thing they've been taught to fear.

It bought us ninety seconds. Ninety seconds is a country, if you spend it right.

I dragged Silas through the bolt-slit. The prisoners were ahead of us in the salt flats, a straggling line of shadows under the doubled moonlight, and Odile's smugglers were waiting beyond the ridge with carts, because Odile keeps her promises the way rivers keep their courses—slowly, inevitably, and regardless of obstacles.

Silas lasted until the carts.

Then they folded, and I caught them, and held them while the fever shook through their body like a storm shaking a small house. Their skin was furnace-hot. Their eyes found mine and focused with effort.

"Well," Silas rasped. "That went well."

"You're insane."

"A little. Yes. Probably the fever talking, but—" A shudder took them, and when it passed, their voice came smaller. "Ves. She was in so much pain. All that time. And now it's *mine*, and I don't know how to put it down."

"You don't," I said, because lying to him seemed suddenly obscene. "You carry it. That's what you did. That's the price and the point of it."

"That's a terrible bargain."

"It's the only one anyone's offering." I pushed the hair off their burning forehead. "Welcome to being known, Silas Kavanagh. The whole yard saw. By morning Ilsette will have your description, your name if that boy heard it, and a price on your head that'll embarrass us both."

"Embarrass you how?"

"Because they'll be paying more for you than for me, and I have *renounced a crown*." I meant it as a joke. It came out half-true, which is the best kind.

Somewhere behind us, the weigh station burned—we'd left oil lamps overturned, or fate had, or the wardens' own panic had. Its light stained the sky orange, and against that light the refugees' faces turned back toward it, watching their captivity burn, and Neme stood apart from all of them, looking not at the fire but at Silas.

Looking at Silas the way you look at something in a book you've read before.

She knows something. I saw it then and I see it now, writing this by the next night's small fire: the child who remembers everything looked at Silas Kavanagh with pity, and pity is not an expression you want on the face of someone who can see tomorrow.

But that was a problem for the road ahead. For that moment, there was only the cart jolting under us, the fever slowly breaking, and Silas's hand finding mine in the dark—grip clumsy with pain, but finding it nonetheless.

"They'll come for us harder now," Silas murmured.

"Yes."

"And you're staying anyway."

I watched the burning station shrink behind us and thought about atlases and oaths and the exact weight of a name freely spoken. "Obviously," I said. "Someone has to keep you alive long enough to burn down something bigger."

Ahead, the salt flats ran silver to the horizon, and somewhere past that silver line, the Door of Salt waited with its thirteenth moon rising.
