## 19. The Ledger of the Hunted

There is a particular smell to ink drying on stolen paper. I know this because I have spent the better part of a fortnight hunched over Odile's back table producing it in industrial quantities: lampblack and saltwater and the faintly sweet rot of cheap rag pulp, layered over the sharper scent of fear that seems to cling to everything we do now. The guild's ledgers used to smell of vellum and cedar oil. Corvane insisted on cedar. He said it reminded clients their memories were being kept somewhere dignified.

He never said *somewhere safe*. I should have noticed that. A man who chooses his words that carefully has already decided what he's willing to sell.

The copy I'm making now — the eleventh — is the ledger of the hunted. Forty-one names as of three days ago. Names Corvane sold to the crown before I ever understood what the guild had become: clients who came to us to hide, whose visits we recorded in our own meticulous hand because Corvane believed that thoroughness was next to godliness and considerably more profitable.

Forty-one names. I have touched every one of them. That was not part of the plan. It was simply what happened, night after night, when my fingers moved down the columns and my gift did what it does without asking permission. A cooper from Harrow Fen whose courage was taken at fourteen and sold back to him at thirty, at interest. A midwife named Sefa who traded her memory of her stillborn daughter and then, two years later, tried to buy it back and found the guild had already resold it to a grief-collector in the capital. A boy called Tam, aged nine, listed under *acquisitions* rather than *clients* because children don't sign contracts; their parents do.

I carry all forty-one of them now, in fragments, like stones sewn into the lining of a coat. Some nights I can't tell which griefs are mine. Most nights I've stopped trying.

"You're doing the thing again," Vespera says from across the table, not looking up from her maps. "The thing where you stare at a name until it apologizes."

"Names don't apologize."

"No," she agrees. "People do. Rarely. Usually badly." She sets her pen down. "How many copies?"

"Eleven finished. Four more to go."

"And the distribution?"

"Smugglers' channels. Odile's cousin runs salt east; the ferryman at Weir's Crossing owes me for an appraisal I fudged in his favor; there's a lay-brother at the Salt House chapel who copies documents for the love of God and the price of gin." I blow on the page. "Each copy goes to a different hand. No one holds the whole list. If the Warden takes one courier, she gets one copy and one dead end."

Vespera considers this the way she considers terrain — looking for the pass, the ambush point, the place where the map lies. "And when she does take one?"

"She'll have names." I say it plainly because there's no honest way around it. "That's the cost. The ledger protects by exposing. Everyone on these pages becomes harder to disappear and easier to find. Both at once. Forever."

"That's not protection, Silas. That's a wager."

"Yes." I set down the pen. My hand has the tremor it gets after long copying, or maybe after long carrying; the distinction has blurred. "It's a wager that a person who knows they're hunted will run sooner, hide better, and stand beside their neighbors instead of being taken alone in the dark. The crown's whole method depends on silence. On people vanishing and everyone pretending the shape they left behind was always empty. This—" I tap the drying page, forty-one names in my own careful hand, "—this makes silence expensive. For them and for us."

Vespera is quiet a moment. Then, wryly, because she is incapable of sincerity lasting longer than a breath: "You realize you've invented gossip as a weapon of war."

"I prefer *distributed archival resistance*."

"I prefer gossip. It frightens people less when you explain it."

* * *

The first copy goes out with Odile's cousin Brenna, rolled inside a bolt of salt-cloth, sewn into the axle-grease packet of her cart. The second goes to Weir's Crossing. The third to the chapel. By the fifth, I've started riding along, because a ledger delivered blind is a ledger half-delivered, and because — though I'd never have admitted this to Vespera — I wanted to see faces when the pages opened.

I got faces. Just not the ones I expected.

In Harrow Fen, a woman named Marta took one look at her own name in my copied hand — *Marta Voss, cooper's widow, gift: mending, reported by husband, sold by Guild, price: 12 crowns* — and threw the page into her stove before I could get a word out.

"It burns," she said, watching it curl. "See? Nothing to hunt. Nothing to find."

"Marta—"

"Don't you *Marta* me." She rounded on me, and I saw that the fury in her face had been waiting there a long time, banked like a fire in peat. "Twelve crowns. Is that what I was worth to you people? I came to your guild in good faith. I paid my fees. And you wrote me down like a ewe at market and sold me to men with branding irons, and now you come to my kitchen wanting *thanks* because you've written it all out again for anyone with eyes to read?"

"The copy isn't for the hunters. It's so no one can make you vanish quietly. So there's a record outside their hands that you existed, that you were taken—"

"I don't want a record!" Her voice cracked on it. "I want my life back. I want to mend things in peace and not have my gift be a *price*. You think a piece of paper protects anyone? Paper is what they used to sell me!"

I let her finish. There was nothing else to do; anything I said while she was burning would only have been fuel. When she ran down, breathing hard, I said quietly:

"You're right. Paper sold you. But here's what I've learned keeping the guild's books for eleven years: what's written in one hand can be rewritten in another. Same paper. Different purpose." I didn't offer her another copy. I just left the blank sheets and the ink block on her table, and told her how the smuggler channel worked, and said, "If you ever decide remembering is worth the risk, the road's open. If not — burn those too. They're yours either way. That's rather the point."

She didn't answer. As I left, through the window, I saw her standing over the stove a long time. She didn't feed it the blank pages.

That counts as something. I've learned to count very small somethings lately; otherwise the arithmetic of this work would bankrupt me entirely.

* * *

Weir's Crossing went differently, and worse, and then strangely better, which is the usual order of things in the Steppe now.

The ferryman — Hask, broad as a barge and twice as hard to steer — read the names with his lips moving, then looked up with an expression I recognized from the appraisal room: the look of a man calculating whether he's been cheated.

"My brother's on here," he said. "Dovan. You've got him down as *gift: waterfinding, status: fled.*"

"He crossed south before the Hunt was proclaimed. The guild lost track of him in—"

"Fled." Hask said the word like he was spitting out a fishbone. "Dovan didn't flee. Dovan went to find work on the southern canals and send money home, same as he did every winter. You wrote *fled* because it made the entry tidy." His fist came down on the table, and my tea jumped. "Tidy. That's what we were to you. Tidy entries."

He wasn't wrong, and we both knew it, and there are apologies that fix nothing but must be made anyway, so I made one. He stared at me with wet furious eyes and then did the last thing I expected, which was to laugh — a short, ugly bark with no floor to it.

"Look at you," he said. "The guild's little ghost. Always in the back room, always polite, never once looked anyone in the eye over a transaction. And now you're riding around the country handing out lists of the damned like a priest handing out pardons." He wiped his face with a huge palm. "Fine. Fine. Give me two more copies. One for the canal folk south — someone'll find Dovan if he's alive to find. And one for the mill at Alderbrook, they're not on your list yet but they will be, the miller's girl lights candles with her mind when the lamps run dry and half the village pretends not to see."

"Hask—"

"Don't thank me." He turned away, already done with me, done with all of it. "Just write them down proper this time. Not *fled*. Say what actually happened. People deserve true words even when the words are bad ones."

I wrote the miller's girl into the master copy that night — *gift: flame-tending, status: known locally, unreported* — and under Dovan's entry, in small letters, I added: *went south for winter work. Did not flee. Correction entered by request of family.*

It's not much. It's a true word where a tidy one used to be. But I'm beginning to suspect that's where justice starts — not with the grand burning I keep imagining, but with corrections, entered by request, one line at a time.

* * *

The chapel copy nearly ended the whole enterprise before it began.

The lay-brother, Anselm, took the pages with shaking hands and hid them beneath his cassock before I'd finished explaining, then made me wait while he checked the chapel door, the window, and, as far as I could tell, the confessional, for listeners.

"You understand what happens to me if the Wardens find this," he whispered.

"Yes."

"And you bring it anyway."

"You copy documents for gin," I said. "I assumed your standards were flexible."

Something moved through his face — offense wrestling gratitude and losing — and then, unexpectedly, he sat down across from me and folded his hands like a man preparing to bargain with heaven.

"There's a name I want added," he said. "Not for the list of the hunted." His voice dropped further. "For a second list. The other kind."

I knew what he meant before he said it. Every network develops its shadow vocabulary within a fortnight. The other kind: the collaborators. The informers. The neighbors who pointed, the priests who reported, the guildmasters who sold.

"Anselm—"

"Not for vengeance." He held up a hand. "Hear me. There's a man in this parish, Gerhardt, who told the Wardens about the tanner's son. The boy was branded in the market square — you may have heard. Gerhardt got the boy's cart as a reward. Now he drives it past the tanner's house every morning, slow, so they see it." Anselm's jaw worked. "I am a man of God and I want this man's name written down somewhere that cannot be burned, and I am ashamed of how much I want it, and I want it anyway."

I thought about it the whole ride home. I'm thinking about it still. Because here's the trap inside my own clever ledger: a record that protects the vulnerable can protect the guilty just as well, and a record that punishes the guilty will eventually punish whoever the powerful decide is guilty. The crown keeps lists. That's the entire architecture of the Hunt — lists, and the terrible authority of deciding what goes on them. Build a second list, even a righteous one, and I've built the crown a second time, smaller, wearing my handwriting.

So I told Anselm no. I told him the ledger records what was done to people, and who did it to them when it matters for their safety — but it doesn't sit in judgment, because judgment is a door, and doors open from both sides. He was not satisfied. Neither was I. Justice outside the law turns out to be mostly carpentry: you build the frame strong enough to hold what it should hold and pray it won't hold what it shouldn't, and some nights you lie awake listening to the joints creak.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Because the last delivery — the one I saved for last, though I told myself it was logistics and not cowardice — was Odile's.

* * *

Odile read the ledger sitting on her own tavern steps at dusk, with the market square emptying behind her and the brand-scarred post of the square's whipping rail visible down the street, because Odile has never once arranged her life to avoid looking at things.

She read every name. She read the prices. She read my corrections. Then she closed it, and held it against her chest the way you hold a child, or a wound, and said:

"My sister's in here."

I hadn't known Odile had a sister. That's the thing about the Steppe; you think you know a town, and it turns out you know its surface, and the rest is underwater.

"Betta. Third page." Odile's voice was level in the way of someone spending enormous effort to keep it that way. "Gift: finding lost things. Keys, rings, livestock. Once, a drowned child, alive, pulled from the weir by following the cold spot in the water. Whole town drank to her that night." She looked at me. "Sold by her own husband when the Hunt was announced. Twelve crowns and a pardon for himself. She's in the Salt House cells now, waiting for branding."

"Odile—"

"You knew?" Not accusatory. Worse: genuinely asking, needing to know how deep the rot went.

"The guild recorded her intake. I didn't know what happened after. I didn't ask. That's not a defense, it's a confession."

She nodded slowly, and I watched her fold my confession away somewhere, to be counted later, because Odile accounts for everything; it's why her tavern survives and why she shelters a gifted child in her attic and why she'll probably die poor and beloved, which is more than most of us will manage.

Then she stood up, and pressed the ledger back into my hands, and said the thing that undid me more thoroughly than Marta's rage or Hask's grief:

"Make me a copy. Not for hiding. For reading aloud."

"For—"

"In the taproom. After close. All of it, every name, every week, whenever there's news." She said it the way she'd announce a change in the price of ale: policy, not philosophy. "You want the crown's silence made expensive? Here's how you do it in a town. You say the names out loud where people drink. You say Betta's name and Dovan's and the cooper-widow's and the tanner's boy's, every night, until the town knows them better than the Wardens' faces. Fear needs empty air to breathe, Silas. Fill the room."

I made her the copy. Fifteen in all now, scattered across the country like seeds of a plant nobody's sure will grow.

* * *

It was leaving Odile's, three nights ago, that the strange thing happened — the thing I keep circling and haven't landed.

A woman was waiting in the alley behind the tavern. Old, wrapped in gray, with the particular stillness of someone who has practiced not being noticed for decades. Before I could speak she took my hand in both of hers — skin to skin, deliberate, the first person in years to touch me without flinching at rumor — and gave me a memory.

Freely. The way you'd hand someone bread.

I felt it settle into me: a kitchen, summer light, a woman teaching a small girl to knead dough, and under the flour-smell the unmistakable hum of a gift working quietly — the dough rising faster than it should, the oven heating itself, the small girl laughing because magic, when it's ordinary and loved, is just another word for home. The old woman's gift, given away on purpose, kept nowhere, owed to no one. She pressed my hand once, hard, and said:

"For the ledger-keeper. From someone whose name isn't in it yet, and won't be, because you taught me tonight — I heard through the window — that a thing written down can't be erased, and a thing given away can't be stolen." Her eyes were bright and fierce. "Take the rising of the bread. Remember somebody was glad."

Then she walked off into the dark, and I stood in the alley holding a stranger's joy like a coal, and cried, briefly and furiously, because I am apparently the sort of person who cries in alleys now. Eleven years in the guild's back rooms and I never once received a gift. Stole hundreds. Was given one.

I've written her into the master ledger anyway, despite her wishes — not her name, she asked none, but a line: *one memory freely given, source unnamed, contents: bread, light, laughter.* The archive of the new kind should record its first deposit. Even the anonymous ones. Especially those.

* * *

Tonight Vespera finds me at the table with the sixteenth copy half-finished, and she reads over my shoulder the way she does, uninvited and unrepentant, and goes still at the newest entries.

"These corrections," she says. "*Did not flee.* *Correction entered by request of family.*" Her finger hovers over the ink. "You're rewriting the crown's evidence."

"I'm correcting the record."

"Silas." She sits down across from me, and there's a particular gravity in her face that means cartographer-brain has engaged, the part of her that thinks in borders and claims and the difference between a line on paper and the ground itself. "Do you understand what you've accidentally done? The Wardens' case against every one of these people rests on the guild's records. Corvane's neat, sworn, certified ledgers. And now there exist fifteen — fifteen! — divergent copies in circulation, in smugglers' hands and chapel drawers and tavern taprooms, containing *corrections*. Any Warden relying on the originals now has to prove which version is true. Every prosecution gets slower. Every identification gets disputable." A slow, fierce grin spreads across her face. "You haven't distributed a list. You've poisoned theirs. Every name you've corrected makes the whole document legally unreliable."

I stare at her. Then at the page. Then at her.

"I just wanted people to not be erased."

"Yes, and you've also jammed the machinery of the Hunt with paperwork, which, frankly, is the most accountant act of rebellion in history and I'm a little in love with it."

Before I can decide how to feel about *a little in love*, boots hit the stairs — Odile, two steps at a time, which she only does when the news won't keep. She bursts in gray-faced, and I know before she speaks, the way you know weather.

"They've taken Neme," she says. "Rode through the checkpoint at dawn with a wagon of captives from the river settlements. The child's in the wagon, Silas. Heading for the weigh station on the south road." Her fists are white. "They're weighing them for transport to the capital. Brandings in batches, now. Batches."

Vespera is already pulling the south-road map toward her, her finger moving along the route, counting miles, counting hours, hunting for the pass, the ambush point, the place where the map might lie in our favor.

And I'm reaching for the ledger — not to copy it, not to hide it, but because forty-one names and one freely given memory of bread and light are shouting in my chest, and every one of them is saying the same thing:

Some entries you don't correct. Some you go and rewrite yourself, whatever it costs.
