## 20. Moon Twelve

I used to believe arithmetic was a kindness.

That was the whole appeal of cartography, when I was a girl copying coastlines by candlelight: numbers didn't lie to you the way people did. A degree of latitude was a degree of latitude whether your emperor liked it or not. The distance between two towns was the same on Tuesday as it was on Sunday, no matter what anyone had done in between. I loved maps because they were honest in a country that had made lying into an administrative science.

Then I grew up and learned that maps are only honest if you're allowed to draw them, which is another way of saying I learned nothing at all, except how to do my lying with a compass.

Still. Old habits. When things go wrong, I count.

So here is the counting, done properly, the way my old instructors at the Royal Survey would have demanded — show your work, Cartographer, or your map is worth less than the paper it's printed on.

One turn of the moon remains before the Door of Salt seals itself for another hundred years.

That is the first figure. It is not negotiable. The smugglers' songs agree with my grandmother's almanacs agree with Neme's unsettling certainty, and when three sources that despise each other all give you the same number, you write it down in ink.

The second figure is this: of the four hundred and eleven souls currently camped in the hollows below the ridge, perhaps thirty carry a true memory of the erased country.

I did the census myself. I am good at censuses. I once burned one, but that is a different chapter.

* * *

The night I finished the count, I sat on the cold lip of the glass flats with my tables spread out around me like a fortune-teller's cards, and I let the arithmetic sit on my chest.

Four hundred and eleven people. Thirty who remember. A Door that opens only for those who carry the country in their heads — truly carries it, the way Silas carries their grandmother's lullaby, the way Odile carries the smell of salt-bread from a bakery that was erased before her mother could walk. Not a story someone told them. Not a song learned secondhand. A memory, lived and owned, of a place that officially never existed.

Three hundred and eighty-one people, then, for whom the thirteenth moon will rise over a door that stays shut.

Unless.

I turned the figures over the way I used to turn problem charts, looking for the error, the mislabeled river, the coastline drawn by a drunk. There is always an error. That is the first thing they teach you: the map is wrong until proven otherwise, and even then, mostly still wrong.

But there wasn't. Or rather, there was, and the error was the world, not the work.

The erasure had been thorough. That was its genius, if you want to honor the engineers of atrocity with the word. A century ago the crown hadn't just redrawn the borders; it had redrawn the memory of the borders, buying up recollections the way the guild buys them now, paying widows for their weddings, paying farmers for their fields, paying mothers, gods forgive us all, for their children's first steps — anything that contained the Salt Steppe, purchased and bottled and shelved in vaults, until an entire country existed only in inventory.

The people who remembered were paid to forget. The people who came after were born into the blank space.

And now the blank space has a door, and the door wants to be remembered, and most of the people standing in front of it have nothing in their heads but the shape of the hole.

* * *

Silas found me there sometime past midnight. They have a way of arriving without arriving — I'll turn and they're simply beside me, ledger-quiet, as though they'd been part of the landscape since dusk and had only just decided to be a person.

"You've been out here four hours," they said. "Odile sent me. She said if you didn't come in she was going to come out, and none of us want that."

"Because she'd be right about everything?"

"Because she'd be right about everything loudly."

I should explain the laugh that got out of me, except there isn't much to explain. Exhaustion does that. Fear does that. You spend so long holding your face together that it cracks along the silliest seam.

I showed them my tables anyway. That's the other thing about me — misery makes me pedagogical. I laid out the moon tables, the tide-reckoning from the smugglers' liturgy, the census tally with its columns of names and ages and, in the last column, a mark I'd invented for *remembers*. Thirty marks out of four hundred and eleven rows. I watched Silas read it. I watched them arrive at the same division I had, because their face did the thing faces do when the answer is small.

"One in thirteen," they said quietly.

"I know."

"Is that — is that a joke? Is the universe doing that on purpose?"

"The universe has never once considered me," I said. "But yes. One in thirteen. And the Door doesn't open a crack, Silas. From everything we know, it opens fully or not at all. Either enough of us carry it true, or—" I stopped, because finishing sentences like that aloud is how they become real.

Silas crouched down and studied the tables without touching them. They're careful with my papers. It's one of maybe nine thousand reasons.

"There's something else," they said finally. "Something I keep circling and can't land on."

"Say it badly, then. We're fugitives. Precision is for people with roofs."

They almost smiled. Then didn't. "Corvane. Before he — before the end, he told us the key. Freely surrendered, forever. He hid his own memories of why he built the guild inside his ledgers for forty years, and when I gave them back to him, he remembered the Door. He remembered *standing* in front of it. Which means he carried the country once." Silas looked up at me, and in the moonlight their eyes had that flat shine I've learned means they are about to say something they hate. "Vespera. Where did he put the memories of everyone the crown bought out? A century of purchased recollections. An entire country, bottled. Where would a man like Corvane keep those?"

The cold went through me the way cold goes through glass — instantly, everywhere.

"The vaults," I said.

"The guild vaults. Under the Salt House. Which Ilsette now controls, unless the flooding took them, unless the fire—"

"Which is exactly the kind of sentence we don't get to finish," I said, "because finishing it means going back."

We looked at each other across my little empire of tables, two liars doing what liars do best, which is lie to themselves slightly slower than they lie to everyone else. Because we both knew. Of course we both knew. Four hundred and eleven people down in those hollows, three hundred and eighty-one of them carrying nothing but the shape of the hole where a country used to be — and a hundred years of the country itself, possibly, sitting in sealed bottles under the ruins of the place we'd barely escaped with our lives.

Hope is not a feeling. I want to be clear about that. Hope is a supply line, and ours ran directly through enemy territory.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I went down among the tents instead, because a cartographer who won't look at the ground she's surveying is just a woman with opinions.

The camp at night is a thing I still haven't found words for that don't sound like pity. Four hundred and eleven people pretending to sleep in a landscape sharp enough to flay you. Cooking fires banked low because light travels across glass. Children wrapped in sailcloth. Somewhere a woman singing to a baby — one of the smugglers' navigation songs, actually, though she sang it soft and slow, drained of directions, just a tune to make a small thing stop crying. That's what a hundred years does to a map. It becomes a lullaby. It keeps working, but nobody remembers it's pointing anywhere.

Neme was awake. Of course Neme was awake. The child sits outside their aunt's tent most nights, watching the sky with the patience of a customs official, and when I passed they said my name before I'd said anything, which is their way of telling you they already know what you're going to talk about.

"You counted wrong," Neme said.

My stomach did something unprofessional. "Did I."

"Not wrong-wrong. You counted what's here." The child pointed one small finger at the ridge, black against the stars. "You didn't count what's coming."

"Neme—"

"People are going to start remembering," they said, with the maddening calm of a child reporting tomorrow's weather. "After the market. What got loose there — it didn't all burn off. Some of it stuck. It's in the wind. It's going to land on people like rain, and some of them are going to wake up with a field they never walked in behind their eyes, and it's going to be *true*, because it came from someone who really lived it." They shrugged, a small, terrible shrug. "I don't know how many. I don't see numbers. But you counted the tents and forgot the sky."

I knelt down in the salt dust in front of this child who remembers forward, and I wanted very badly to ask *how many survive, how many cross, do I cross, does Silas—* and I didn't. Not because I'm brave. Because some doors, once opened, don't close again, and Neme's gift is heavy enough without me loading my fear onto it.

"Thank you," I said instead. "For telling me."

"You'll tell Silas?"

"When it's useful."

"It's useful now."

"They're asleep, Neme."

"No," said the child placidly, "they're standing behind you."

They were. They had been for some time, apparently, because being Silas Kavanagh means materializing out of the dark whenever the universe needs a witness. Their expression was unreadable in the way I have privately catalogued as *unreadable because full*.

"So," Silas said. "The sky counts too."

"The sky counts too," Neme agreed, and went back to watching it, conversation concluded, business of childhood resumed.

* * *

Later — much later, the moon well down, the two of us walking the camp's edge because neither of us could perform sleep anymore — Silas said the thing I'd been not-saying for six hours.

"If the memories in the vaults survived, we could open the Door without anyone sacrificing anything. No one gives anything up. No one loses the country to gain it. We just... return what was stolen."

"That would require entering the Salt House."

"Yes."

"Which Ilsette holds."

"Yes."

"With wardens. And Corvane, if he lives, who sold us once already and whose entire remaining personality is grievance."

"I've noticed that about him," Silas said.

"And you're proposing it anyway."

They stopped walking. Out on the glass flats the wind was doing its long slow work, sanding the horizon, and the thirteenth moon — still shy of full, still a coin with a bite taken out of it — hung above the ridge like something waiting to be paid.

"I'm proposing," Silas said carefully, "that we tell the camp the truth. All of it. The arithmetic, the one-in-thirteen, the vaults, all of it. Because right now four hundred people think the plan is *walk toward the ridge and hope*, and hope is a terrible navigator. I spent years keeping books for a man who lied with perfect records. I know what it costs. I'm not going to run this camp the same way."

I looked at them for a long moment. This person who spent a decade making themselves invisible, proposing radical transparency to four hundred frightened strangers, because they'd seen what secrecy builds and declined to build any more of it.

"When did you become the brave one?" I asked.

"About the time you started drawing maps for free," they said. "It's contagious. Apparently."

* * *

So here is where the counting stands, and I record it now the way I record everything, because ink outlives intention:

One turn remains. Thirteen nights, if the smugglers' reckoning holds, and it has held every night so far, which is more than I can say for the crown's almanac.

Thirty carriers in camp, and a sky that Neme says is going to start raining other people's memories, and beneath the flooded cellars of our enemies, possibly, a hundred years of a stolen country in sealed glass — enough, if it exists, if it survived, if we can reach it, to open the Door for everyone without costing anyone the thing they love most.

Four ifs. I have crossed countries on worse margins. I have also buried friends on better ones.

Tomorrow I stand in front of the camp and tell them the truth: that the moon is nearly full, that the door is nearly ready, that most of them cannot pass through it as they are — and that the way to fix it may lie behind us, in the dark, under the house that tried to kill us.

Wish us luck. Or better, wish us arithmetic.

We're going to need both.
