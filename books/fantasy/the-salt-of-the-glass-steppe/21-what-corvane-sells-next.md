## 21. What Corvane Sells Next

There is a particular smell to a brand that has gone wrong. Not the honest stink of a clean burn—that I could respect, in the way one respects an executioner's rope—but something sweeter, riper, like fruit left too long in a ledger room. My own forearm smells of it now, day and night, waking and sleeping. The Warden's iron was not careful. The Warden was not interested in care.

They gave me a cot in my own cellar. That is the joke I keep returning to, the pebble in my boot: thirty years of buying, selling, and appraising other people's pasts, and at the end of it I am housed beneath my own inventory like a mislabeled crate, waiting for someone upstairs to decide what I am worth.

The brand itself is a G, of course. Guild. They might at least have had the imagination to mark me as what I actually am—a thief—but the crown has never been good at taxonomy. To them we are all one letter.

I have decided to live. I mention this because for the first two days it was not a settled question. A man looks at his arm, at the letter eating its slow ring of infection into him, and he does arithmetic he has avoided all his life. What have I purchased with all this? I inventoried. It is what I do. And the inventory came back embarrassingly short: a building full of bottled grief, a reputation among smugglers, a cot, a rotting arm, and a name—Corvane—that I chose myself at nineteen because it sounded like a man who owned things.

I chose my own name. I should start there, perhaps, when I tell this story. But there is no one left to tell it to who would want it.

Except, as it turns out, the woman downstairs from me with the gentle voice and the patient eyes.

* * *

Warden Ilsette came on the third evening. She dismissed my guards—which tells you how thoroughly she owns this operation now—and sat on the single stool across from my cot with the ease of someone who has sat in a hundred cellars and expects to sit in a thousand more.

"Guildmaster," she said.

"Former guildmaster."

"The paperwork hasn't caught up." She unfolded a cloth on her knees: bread, cheese, a small jar of honey. Prisoner's fare, but good prisoner's fare. "I'm told you haven't been eating."

"I'm told you branded half my staff and hanged the appraiser from the loading dock."

"Tomas sold memories to a child of eleven." She said it without heat, the way one notes the weather. "You knew. You took a cut."

I had no answer to that, so I ate the bread instead. It was good bread. That offended me more than the hanging, somehow—the competence of it, the courtesy. Ilsette does everything competently. I have come to believe that is the most frightening thing about her.

"You're dying," she said, watching me chew. "Slowly. The brand went septic; your own physician told my surgeon it's a matter of weeks unless it's properly treated, and proper treatment requires a healer, and every healer in this province with any gift worth having is either fled or in my holding wagons." She let that settle. "You built a market out of other people's needs, Corvane. I'm simply offering you the same terms you've offered everyone else."

"And the price?"

She smiled. It did not reach anywhere near her eyes, which are grey and still and remind me of the salt flats in winter. "The Door of Salt."

I set down the bread.

"Don't perform ignorance," she said mildly. "Kavanagh came to you with a map fragment. Your own ledgers record the purchase—I read them while you were being fitted for your letter. A cartographer's work. Vespera Something-or-other, disgraced. You bought the only reliable route to the Door of Salt in existence, and then Kavanagh stole it back off your desk, and now both of them are somewhere on my steppe laughing at me." Her voice stayed level throughout, which I admired even then. Anger I could bargain with. This patience was another animal entirely. "You know where they'll surface. You know where the Door opens. Sell me that, and you'll have the finest healer in the capital by dawn, and your records will show a cooperative witness rather than a convicted trafficker."

"And if I don't sell?"

"Then you die in this cellar under the letter G, and I find the Door anyway, and everything you spent your life accumulating becomes kindling for my report." She repacked the cloth, unhurried. "I'll leave you with the honey. Consider whether sweetness matters to a dead man."

At the door she paused. "One more thing. The ledgers say you founded the guild forty-one years ago. There's no record of why. No founding charter with a grievance in it, no first entry explaining yourself. Men who build empires always leave a manifesto, Corvane. You left a price list." She looked at me then, just once, with something almost like curiosity. "What were you trying to forget?"

And she was gone, and I lay on my cot with the honey untouched, because she had asked the one question in the world I could not answer without opening a door inside myself that I had nailed shut before she was born.

* * *

Here is what I remember—or here is what remains, which is a different thing, the way a burned house remains a chimney.

Forty-one years ago I was a young man with a different name and a gift I did not understand, living in a border town that no longer exists on any map, because the country it belonged to was erased. I was born on the Salt Steppe. Do you hear how strange that sentence is? I was born on the Salt Steppe, and I cannot tell you the color of its sky. I cannot tell you my mother's face. I know I had a mother the way I know I had a childhood—by inference, by the shape of the absence. Everyone over a certain age carries that absence. We call it ordinary forgetting and pour another drink.

But I remember fragments around the hole, the way you remember the frame of a painting that's been cut out of its canvas. I remember standing in a market square—our market square, glass underfoot, the whole town paved in fused sand that rang faintly when carts crossed it—and watching a man in a merchant's coat pay a starving woman three coins for the memory of her wedding. I remember thinking, with the terrible clarity of the very young: *someone is going to organize this.* Because that is always the thought, isn't it? Someone is going to organize the cruelty. Better it be organized kindly. Better it be me.

I remember a lullaby. Not the words—never the words—but the fact of it, a melody-shaped hole with my grandmother's voice around its edges. I remember deciding, at nineteen, that a man who could not protect one town could at least build a place where the thefts were recorded, priced, and reversible in principle. Reversible in principle. I wrote that phrase somewhere once, in the early years. I believed the ledger was a promise: nothing taken here would vanish. Everything catalogued could someday go home.

And then, some year I cannot name because I cut it out myself, the reversibility became inconvenient. A client with power wanted something kept permanently. A rival needed discrediting. An audit loomed, or a lover betrayed me, or—this is the part that curdles me—I simply found that remembering why I'd started made the daily work unbearable, and a man cannot run a business on unbearable. So I did the one thing a man like me should never do.

I sold myself a memory.

I used my own gift against my own mind. I lifted out the reason—not the whole history, just the keystone of it, the grief and the grandmother and the ringing glass streets—and I put it in a bottle, and I shelved it, and I told myself I would return for it when the guild was secure. When things calmed down. When I could afford the weight again.

Forty-one years, gentlemen and ladies. Forty-one years of walking past that shelf.

Do you know what I did instead of remembering? I grew efficient. I grew respected. I told myself the guild was a mercy—that better we hold the memories safely than let hunters and kings burn them, that a stolen thing in a vault survives longer than a free thing on a pyre. Every lie had a true seed; that's what makes lies load-bearing. And every year the shelf got harder to approach, because approaching it meant admitting what I'd become in the dark where my reason used to be.

Then Silas Kavanagh happened to me. Quiet little clerk, ink-stained, invisible—and carrying the same gift I'd buried, except unburied, uncorrupted, doing with it exactly what I'd sworn the ledger stood for: giving things back. When I learned what Kavanagh was, do you know what my first feeling was? Not fear. Envy. Envy with teeth. Because Kavanagh touched a stranger's grief and returned it, and I couldn't even touch my own.

So when Ilsette asks what I was trying to forget, the honest answer—the answer I will take to whatever grave is coming—is: *everything that mattered, one piece at a time, starting with the piece that made the rest bearable.*

And now she wants the Door. The Door of Salt, the way out, the last unbroken promise of the erased country. And I sit here with a rotten arm and a head full of holes, and I realize I can sell it to her. I know where Kavanagh will run. I know the old smuggler songs point to the ridge above the Glass Steppe like compass needles. I know, better than anyone alive, how badly people want doors opened and how much they'll pay.

I also know—I have known since the iron touched me—that selling it is simply the last item on a very long invoice. First I sold my neighbors' weddings. Then my clients' courage. Then my staff's safety. Then my own past. The Door is merely what's left in the till.

The tragedy is not that I'm a monster. Monsters are useful; monsters make clean stories. The tragedy is that I can stand in this cellar and feel, genuinely, the pull of both choices—the healer's hands and the honey, versus the ridge at moonrise and the crowd of hunted people I'd be feeding to the woman with the grey eyes—and both choices feel like me. That's what erasing your reasons does. It leaves you a creature of pure appetite wearing the costume of a man.

* * *

Ilsette returns on the fifth night. She brings the same cloth, the same bread, a new jar of honey. Ritual, or theater. With her they are the same thing.

"You've been thinking," she says.

"I've been feverish. Thinking happened incidentally."

"Result?"

I look at my arm. The bandage needs changing; the smell has gotten into the wool of my blanket, into my hair, into the back of my throat. Weeks, the physician said. Perhaps fewer.

"Warden," I say, "let me tell you a story about a shelf."

Her stillness sharpens by a degree. She is smart enough to know that men like me open negotiations with stories only when the story is the merchandise.

"There is a bottle in my private vault," I say, "third case, behind the false row of vintage griefs, that contains the reason I built this guild. My own memory, self-taken, forty-one years ago. It is the only acquisition in this entire archive I never sold, never traded, never appraised. And I am prepared to trade it."

"For the Door," she says.

"For restoration," I say. "All of it. The healer, the pardon, the record wiped clean—and the memory back in my head, where it belongs, before anything else changes hands."

Something moves behind her eyes then—not warmth, nothing so generous, but recognition. One collector to another. She understands what I'm offering, and what it costs, and perhaps—this is the hope I hate myself for harboring—she understands because she has a shelf of her own. I saw the brother's token on her desk once, when she interviewed me in my office, in the era when I still had an office. A soldier's brand-token, worn smooth by a thumb. She touches it when she thinks no one is looking. People who carry relics like that are people who have lost someone to a choice they made or failed to make. We are colleagues, she and I, in the guild neither of us names.

"If your bottle is a trick," she says, "you'll die of the fever before my surgeon finishes being disappointed."

"If my bottle is a trick, I'll have tricked the only person in the world whose opinion of me I still fear."

That lands. I watch it land. For a moment the cellar is quiet enough that I can hear the inventory upstairs settling, hundreds of small glass weights shifting on their shelves, the accumulated pasts of a province breathing in their sleep. Somewhere up there is a widow's grief, a soldier's courage, a child's first snow. Somewhere up there is mine.

"One condition," Ilsette says, rising, folding the cloth. "You give me the location tonight. Verbal. The healer comes tomorrow; the memory comes after you've told me everything you know about Kavanagh's route and the Door's mechanism. If the information proves true, you get your past back. If it doesn't—" she shrugs, a small economical motion, "—the fever decides."

"And if it proves true? What will you do at that Door, Warden?"

She stops with her hand on the door. And here is the moment I will replay in whatever time remains to me, because for one instant the patience drops away and I see underneath, and underneath is not a hunter. Underneath is a tired woman in a uniform that fits too well, guarding a wound shaped exactly like a brother.

"What I'm ordered to do," she says.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have left." And then, quieter, almost to herself: "You of all people should know how few answers survive their own keeping."

The door closes. The lamp gutters. I lie back and begin, in the dark, to compose the location of the Door of Salt out of the smuggler songs and the cartographer's fragment and the shape of the hole where my grandmother's lullaby used to be—and I understand, with the last clarity available to a man who sold his own reasons, that I am about to commit the final and purest transaction of my career.

I am selling the way home for the memory of why I left.

It is, I reflect, at least a fair price. Nothing else in this guild ever was.

Tomorrow the healer comes. Tomorrow I talk. And somewhere out on the glass flats, under a moon with one turn still owing, a clerk with ink-stained fingers and a disgraced mapmaker are walking toward the ridge I am about to betray—carrying between them, I suspect, more of the truth than I ever managed to keep.

Let them walk fast.
