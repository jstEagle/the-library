## 22. The Memory Market Riot

Market day in the Steppe always smelled the same: salt brine, frying onions, and the faint copper tang of bottled memories sweating in their glass. I'd been tavern-keeper on Copper Row for twenty-two years, and in all that time I had learned three things about my neighbors.

One: they would haggle over a memory of their own mother's face down to a half-copper.

Two: they would sell that same memory again next month if the winter was hard enough.

Three: they would look away from anything that frightened them, and nearly everything frightened them now.

The Thirteenth Hunt had changed the market the way frost changes a window—same view, new geometry. The memory stalls still opened at dawn, but the sellers kept their hoods up. The buyers paid without haggling, which told you everything about how scared everyone was. Nobody bargains when they think it's the last time they'll touch the thing they're buying.

I'd come down to the square to buy salt and, if I'm honest, to keep an eye on Neme's mother's stall. The girl herself was supposed to be safe at my tavern, minding the ovens, but Neme had a talent for being exactly where she shouldn't be, which is what happens when your gift is remembering things that haven't happened yet. She'd told me at breakfast, very calmly, while buttering bread: "Don't let me near the fountain today."

"And why's that?"

"Because something happens there." She took another bite. "I don't remember what yet."

That's Neme for you. Prophecy as a chore list.

So naturally I spent the morning positioning myself between her and the market fountain like a hen who'd read too many omens. The fountain hadn't run water in years—it was crusted white with salt bloom—but people still called it the fountain, because that's the other thing about the Steppe: we name things by what they used to be.

* * *

The hunters came at noon bell, which was an insult in itself. Noon is when the market is fullest, when the stalls are heaped, when every soul in the district is pressed shoulder to shoulder among goods they can't afford to abandon. You raid at noon if you want a crowd. You want a crowd if you want fear to spread.

There were twelve of them, grey cloaks, white brand-irons slung across their backs like fishing rods. Their captain was a woman with a voice I'd have recognized anywhere, because I'd heard it once through my tavern wall, gentle as a lullaby, asking a man questions until he wept. Warden Ilsette's second-in-command—a man named Dray with a face like a closed ledger—walked ahead of her and unrolled a paper as though reading a menu.

"By order of the Crown," he said, "this market trades in contraband recollection. Every vessel will be surrendered. Every seller named. Compliance will be noted in your favor."

Noted in our favor. As if the crown were a shopkeeper keeping a tab.

The crowd did what crowds do. They went quiet. They looked at their boots. Old Marta, who sells grief by the dram, began gathering her bottles with shaking hands. A fishmonger two stalls from me turned his back with the studied blankness of a man pretending not to exist.

And then a boy—I never learned his name, fourteen maybe, working his aunt's stall—knocked a tray of memories off the table.

I don't know if he meant to. I've thought about it a thousand nights since. Maybe his elbow caught it. Maybe some part of him, some animal part that remembers older than remembering, understood exactly what he was doing. Either way, forty little glass vessels hit the cobbles and burst all at once, and the sound wasn't breaking glass at all.

It was singing.

* * *

You've never heard a memory die in open air unless you've been standing in it. Bottled memory doesn't evaporate—it *escapes*, and it goes looking for somewhere to live. Forty freed recollections rose off those cobbles like moths from a shaken cloak, glittering, wheeling, and then they found us.

They found everybody.

The nearest thing to me was a soldier's memory—the smell of woodsmoke and wet wool, someone's hand on his shoulder saying *we're going home, lad, we're going home*—and it poured into me before I could flinch, warm as soup, alien as a stranger's coat. For one long breath I was a young man on a road at dusk, bone-tired and glad, and the country around me—

The country around me was *green*.

I want you to understand what that means. I was born in the Steppe. I have lived fifty-one years surrounded by glass flats and salt scrub, where the only green is the moss that grows in tavern gutters. And this memory held rolling hills, actual hills, with rivers in the valleys and orchards going gold with fruit, and a village with slate roofs, and bells—bells! We have no bells in the Steppe. We have no rivers. We have nothing that rings.

And I knew—I knew the way you know your own kitchen in the dark—that this was home. Not the soldier's home. *Ours.* The erased country. The Salt Steppe before it was salt, or beside its salt, or under it—someplace the maps had been scraped blank a hundred years ago so thoroughly that even our grandparents' grandparents stopped mentioning it out loud.

Around me, the square was coming apart.

Marta had dropped to her knees amid her spilled wares, hands pressed to her mouth, sobbing words in a language I didn't know and understood completely. The fishmonger had turned back around and was staring at the sky with tears running into his beard, mouthing *the lighthouse, the lighthouse*—there are no lighthouses within four hundred miles. The boy who'd dropped the tray stood in the middle of it all, eyes wide, and I saw him mouth the same word everyone was mouthing, half-swallowed, terrified, exultant:

*Bells.*

A hundred freed memories, then more—because panic spreads and elbows catch trays and whole tables went over—and each one that broke open fed the crowd another piece of a country none of us had ever seen. It moved through the market like fire through dry grass. Strangers gripped each other's arms. An old man laughed and cried at once. Somebody started humming a tune I knew—I *knew* it—my grandmother's lullaby, except it wasn't hers, it was everyone's, it was the song of a place with rivers, and the whole square was humming it by fits and starts like a congregation remembering the hymn halfway through.

And Warden Dray stood in the middle of it with his unrolled paper, watching his compliance order turn into a revival meeting.

* * *

Here is what I believe happened next, and I was close enough to see most of it plainly.

Dray ordered the arrests anyway. Of course he did. Men like Dray do not revise; they repeat louder. Two hunters moved toward Marta, who was still on her knees, still singing, and the crowd—our crowd, the look-away crowd, the haggle-over-your-dead-mother's-face crowd—did not look away.

It was the fishmonger first. He stepped into their path, a big soft-bellied man I'd seen duck his head at tax collectors for a decade, and he said, "No," in a voice like a door closing. When the hunter shoved him, the shove landed, but the hunter staggered too, because forty hands had come up behind the fishmonger, and forty voices said *no* after him, and then the humming turned into the song itself, full-throated, in the old tongue, and I'll tell you something true: there is no weapon made that stands up well against five hundred people singing their own name back to themselves.

The hunters' brand-irons came off their backs. That should have ended it. White iron against flesh ends arguments everywhere else in this kingdom.

But the square had become something else by then. Freed memories were still bursting—stall owners smashing their own stock now, deliberately, feeding the crowd, arming their neighbors with the past—and every break released another flood of *green hills, river water, slate roofs, bells*, and the crowd thickened around the soldiers like wax around a wick. Someone threw salt. Then stones. A bottle of somebody's wedding day shattered against a hunter's chest and he reeled, drenched in a stranger's joy, and I swear on my tavern he lowered his iron and just—stood there, weeping, infected with a happiness that wasn't his and couldn't be contained.

Neme appeared at my elbow. I nearly died of fright on the spot.

"The fountain," she said, tugging my sleeve, calm as Sunday. "Now. This is the part I couldn't remember."

"Neme—"

"It's important. Everyone has to see."

I grabbed her wrist and hauled her toward the dead fountain, because when a child who remembers tomorrow tells you to move, you move, and I'd already decided months ago that she was mine to keep alive.

What came out of the fountain I cannot fully explain, and I have stopped trying to explain it to people who weren't there. The old basin was packed solid with salt bloom, decades of it, white crust over whatever lay beneath. But the square was saturated now with escaped memory, thick as weather with it, and whether the stone remembered being a well, or whether the crowd's borrowed recollection pooled into the basin like rain into a bowl—or both, or neither, or something no one has a word for—the salt crust split with a crack like ice going out in spring.

And the fountain ran.

Not water. Light. A slow seep of pale luminescence rising from the cracked basin, the color of moonrise on the flats, and where it touched the crowd, the flashes of remembering steadied. People didn't get glimpses anymore. They got *knowing*. I watched the fishmonger straighten up and say, clearly, in the old tongue, "This is Vessengard. Our town was called Vessengard," and I watched sixty people repeat it like an oath.

Vessengard. I carry that word now the way you carry a splinter—small, deep, yours.

Dray blew his whistle. His hunters tried to form a line. The line lasted about as long as a sugar shelf in the rain.

* * *

I won't pretend it was a battle, because battles have two sides with plans. It was a drowning. Five hundred people who had just remembered they had a country closed around twelve hunters who represented a crown that had erased it, and the arithmetic did what arithmetic does. No one died—I'll say that proudly until my dying day, because it would have been easy for someone to die, and nobody did. The crowd stripped the hunters of their irons and their papers, tore the arrest warrant into pieces that drifted on the wind like the moths of memory themselves, and then, in a gesture I will treasure into old age, picked Dray up bodily—wailing, dignity gone, ledger-face finally open with pure animal fear—and carried him to the fountain and held his face over the light until he breathed it in.

He came up changed. I saw it happen. One minute he was screaming about sedition; the next he was staring at the light with his mouth open, and what fell out of him was a whisper: "My grandmother sang that." He couldn't have known it was true. He knew it was true. That's the difference between evidence and memory, and the fountain had settled the argument in front of the entire district.

They let the hunters go. Barefoot, cloakless, empty-handed, walking back toward the garrison road past silent lines of townsfolk who hummed as they passed. It was mercy and mockery in equal measure, and both were earned.

By dusk the square was swept, the stalls rebuilt, and the whole Steppe smelled different. That's not poetry. The air itself tasted of green things. Half the town walked around touching walls and weeping; the other half slept in doorways, exhausted from carrying centuries. Marta reopened her stall and gave everything away free, which in a merchant of Marta's temperament constitutes religious conversion.

Neme sat on the fountain's rim while I checked her for bruises she didn't have, and asked, "Did I do it right?"

"You did nothing. A boy dropped a tray."

"No." She shook her head, patient with my slowness the way children are patient with adults, which is its own kind of cruelty. "That was going to happen anyway. I mean the rest. Did I say it right? At the fountain?"

"I don't understand, love."

She looked at me for a long moment, deciding something. Then she said, "Silas and Vespera need to hear what the fountain showed me. Not the town. Them." Her small hand closed around mine, cold despite the summer evening. "Because the Door opens soon, and I've seen what's on the other side of it, and it isn't nothing—but it isn't enough either. Unless Silas does the thing. And Odile—" her voice went thin, younger suddenly, just a scared kid again— "when Silas does the thing, Silas forgets. All of it. Even us."

The lights of the settlement flickered on down Copper Row, ordinary and golden, and above them the thirteenth moon was rising, doubled, sharp as a blade's edge.

"When do we go?" I asked.

"Now," said Neme. "Before tomorrow changes its mind."
