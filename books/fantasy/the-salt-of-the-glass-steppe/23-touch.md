## 23. Touch

### Silas

There is a particular kind of quiet that follows a riot, and it sounds exactly like Vespera tearing linen strips in a room that smells of boiled salt and someone else's blood.

Mine, mostly. The someone else's is incidental.

"Sit still," she said, which was redundant, because I was sitting about as still as a man can sit when his ribs have declared independence from the rest of his torso. She knelt on the floor of Odile's back room with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and an expression I had learned to read the way I once read ledgers: closely, and with growing dread.

"I am sitting still," I said.

"You are narrating at me. That's not stillness."

"In my family, narration *is* stillness."

She tied off the bandage around my forearm where a hunter's blade had opened a line from elbow to wrist—not deep, but honest work—and for a moment neither of us said anything. Outside, the Steppe market was being swept. I could hear brooms. Brooms, after all that. The freed memories had scattered through the town like moths out of a torn coat, and half the people sweeping had stopped mid-stroke in the last two days because they'd remembered something—a harbor, a grandmother's kitchen, a word in a language no one had spoken aloud in a century—and forgotten what they were doing. The brooms kept stopping. It was, I thought, the most beautiful labor dispute in history.

Vespera sat back on her heels and looked at my arm instead of my face, which meant she was working up to something. Vespera did not work up to things often; she was a woman who burned census rolls and renounced crowns between breakfast and noon. But this had the look of a thing she'd been carrying folded up small for a long while.

"The pain," she said. "In the weigh station. You touched Neme's brand and you took it."

"Took some of it," I said. "I'm not greedy."

"Silas."

"It's what I do." I said it lightly, because lightness is the load-bearing wall of my entire personality. "People hand me their worst days and I carry them home like groceries. You knew that when you bargained with me. It was in the contract. Clause four: the archivist absorbs all emotional damages."

"That's not what clause four says."

"It's what it *means*."

She didn't laugh. She moved instead, from kneeling to sitting beside me on the narrow cot, close enough that her shoulder rested against my uninjured one, and the room got very small and very warm and very much like a place I had never been allowed to be.

"When you took the pain," she said quietly, "you didn't flinch away. Most people would have. You leaned in. Like it was—" She stopped, started again. "Like it was yours to carry."

"It wasn't mine," I said. "None of it ever is. That's rather the point of a thief, Vespera. We take what isn't ours."

"And what happens," she said, "when you want to give something back?"

The question sat there between us like a lamp turned up too bright.

Because here is the thing I had never said aloud, to anyone, not even to myself in the ledger room at three in the morning with the ink still wet: every touch of my life has been a theft or an accident. A soldier's courage, lifted in a handshake. A widow's grief, caught like rain on my skin because she gripped my wrist too long. Corvane taught me early—*never* touch bare skin, boy, gloves always, a keeper who keeps nothing of his own is worthless—and I had obeyed for twenty years, and the obedience had calcified into loneliness so complete it had stopped feeling like loneliness and started feeling like architecture. Walls aren't sad. They're just walls.

And now here was a woman asking, in the plainest words she owned, whether I knew how to do the other thing.

"No," I said. Honestly. "I don't. I've taken thousands of memories and given back fragments by accident, by guilt, by—by sleight of hand when nobody was watching. But deliberately? On purpose?" I looked at my bandaged arm. "I don't actually know if I can. It might be like asking a pickpocket to donate."

"Then we find out," Vespera said.

"*We*?"

Her jaw did the thing it does when she's decided something and is daring the world to object. "You took my childhood out of my head without asking, in the archive. Ink that remembers, your fingers on mine, and suddenly I was eight years old and the sea smelled wrong and I couldn't remember why I'd come into the archive at all." Her voice stayed level. It cost her something; I watched it cost her. "You apologized. You gave back everything you'd touched, and then some—I found memories in there I'd sold years ago, returned like letters forwarded. And I forgave you anyway. Do you know why?"

"Charm?"

"Don't ruin it." She took my hand—the bandaged one, gently, turning it palm-up—and held it over her own bare wrist. Not touching yet. Hovering. An inch of air between my skin and hers might as well have been the width of the Steppe.

"Because I've spent my whole life drawing other people's borders," she said. "Lines on paper telling everyone where they may and may not exist. And the first time in my life I felt *seen*—actually seen, down to the sold-off parts—it was your theft that did it. That's obscene, Silas. That should have been a gift." Her eyes came up and met mine. "So let's make it one. Properly. Deliberately. You take something I give you, freely. And you give me something back the same way. No theft. No accident. Trade."

"That's still a transaction," I said. "I'm told I'm bad at those. Ask any client whose daughter's face I smuggled back to him."

"Then call it whatever you like. Call it smuggling. Call it treason against the guildmaster's rules." The corner of her mouth moved. "Call it the last crime either of us commits inside the old country."

Outside, a broom stopped. Somewhere in the swept square, somebody was standing very still, holding a memory of the sea.

I turned my hand over. My fingertips touched her wrist.

### Vespera

I will tell you what I expected, since apparently I'm the one confessing tonight. I expected cold. Some ledger-keeper's trick, a sensation like ink drying, the little mechanical click of a lock opening in my skull. I had built the moment in advance, the way I build everything—measured, drafted, annotated—because if you can predict a thing it can't hurt you.

It was warm.

Not metaphorically. Warm, like sun on stone, like the flat of a hand laid over a fevered forehead, and then the world tilted sideways and I was standing in a courtyard I had not thought of in twenty years, and my sister was eleven, and she was teaching me to whistle through a blade of grass, and the sound she made was terrible, absolutely dreadful, a dying kettle of a sound, and we were both laughing so hard that Mama came to the window—

The memory ended where she had chosen to end it. I understood that immediately, the way a cartographer understands where a mapmaker chose to stop surveying. She had cut it clean at the laughter. Nothing after. No proclamation, no atlas with a decree folded inside it, no night flight from the capital with a stolen fragment and a burned name. Just grass-whistles and laughter, sealed and handed over like bread.

I opened my eyes. Silas was watching me with an expression I can only describe as a man watching to see whether the bridge holds.

"That's it?" I managed. My voice had gone strange. "That whole memory is just—her? Being ridiculous?"

"She asked me to give you the best one I had of her," Silas said. "From the night she sheltered Neme. Odile's kitchen, the child asleep, and your sister talking about you. About how you used to draw maps in the dust with a stick and charge the neighborhood children tolls to cross them."

"I was establishing infrastructure."

"You were a tyrant, apparently. Beloved, but a tyrant." They paused. "The whistling is in there too. I checked."

I laughed, and it came out wet, which was inconvenient, and I decided not to care, which was more inconvenient still. Because here was the arithmetic of it, and I am good at arithmetic: Silas Kavanagh had carried my sister's joy around inside them like contraband, guilty as sin, unable to return it without touching her, and they had kept it safe anyway. Kept it the way they kept everything. Carefully. Reverently. In perfect condition.

"What do I owe you?" I said, and immediately hated the words, because they were crown words, guild words, the whole rotten vocabulary of a country that priced its own children's faces.

"Nothing," Silas said. Then, wryer: "Well. One thing. Fair's fair. You wanted a trade."

They held out their other hand, palm up, an offering so plainly terrified that it broke my heart a little. Because I finally saw it—the architecture. Twenty years of gloves. A man who could hold the grief of strangers but had never once been allowed to simply *be held*, since every touch ran both ways along their cursed, blessed skin.

I put my hand in theirs.

What I gave them was not grand. I considered giving them the Door of Salt, the fragment, the survey of my grandfather—but they'd earn that soon enough on the road, and besides, gifts shouldn't be invoices. Instead I gave them a small thing: the night in the capital, years ago, before any of it, when I had stood on the observatory roof with the twelfth moon rising and thought, *the maps are lies and I draw them and someday I will stop*. Just that. The exact temperature of the resolve. The taste of the air. The precise shade of cowardice-to-courage, that gray hour when a person decides.

Silas made a sound low in their throat when it landed. Their fingers tightened on mine.

"Why that one?" they asked.

"Because you keep thinking your gift is only theft," I said. "And I wanted you to know that I chose this. The way I chose the burning rolls, the stolen fragment, you. Everything worth having in my life, I took against orders." I squeezed their hand. "Consider yourself mapped."

For a long time neither of us spoke. The cot was narrow. Our shoulders were touching. Somewhere beyond the wall Odile was singing to Neme—one of the smugglers' liturgy songs, the ones that are really roads—and the melody came through the boards muffled and patient, a country humming itself back together one verse at a time.

"Vespera," Silas said eventually.

"Yes."

"I'm going to say a thing now, and I'd like to say it without ceremony, because ceremonies are for institutions and we appear to be fleeing one."

"Noted."

"I love you." They said it the way they entered figures in a ledger: plainly, precisely, in a hand that could not be misread. "Not the dramatic kind. The kind that survives clause four. The kind that carries groceries." A breath. "I've loved you since the bargain, if I'm honest, which I'm trying to be, God help me. Since you looked at the erased coastline on that fragment and didn't ask what it was worth. You asked what it *cost*. Nobody asks what things cost. Everybody asks what they're worth."

"I love you too," I said. "Also without ceremony. Also clause four. Though I'll note for the record that you confessed first, which means I win the ledger."

"There's no winning the ledger. There's only balanced columns and unbalanced columns."

"Then balance this one."

I kissed them, and they kissed me back, and if you want to know what it's like to kiss a man who can lift a memory with a touch: it is like being memorized. Every place their mouth went, they were reading me, gently, reverently, taking nothing, leaving everything. My hands found the unbandaged side of their face and I felt them flinch—old reflex, twenty years of gloves—and then felt the flinch dissolve under my palms like salt in water, and they leaned in the way they'd leaned into Neme's pain, like it was theirs to carry, like I was theirs to carry, and the room got small and warm and ours.

Afterward we lay in the narrow dark with our fingers laced together—deliberately, continuously, greedily, two people making up for a combined forty years of gloves—and outside the brooms had all stopped entirely, and nobody in the square seemed inclined to start again.

"We leave before moonset," Vespera said against my shoulder, later, when the candle had burned to a thumb. "Odile's contact at the salt flats. The column gathers tomorrow night."

"Mm."

"Silas." A pause I should have heard coming. "When we cross—whatever the Door asks—we face it together. Whatever it costs. Promise me no heroic arithmetic. No balancing the columns alone."

"I promise," I said, and meant it, and would break it, and could not have told her that any more than she could have mapped the road we hadn't walked yet.

But that was tomorrow's ledger.

Tonight, for the first time in my life, I slept ungloved, with my hand in another person's keeping, and dreamed nothing borrowed at all.

### Vespera

Dawn came gray through the shutters, and with it the smell of woodsmoke that was not breakfast.

I woke alone on the cot, hand curled around the shape of a hand no longer there, and for one stupid second the old architecture tried to rebuild itself—*of course, of course, they always leave, everyone leaves, draw the border and grieve inside it*—and then I heard voices in the front room, low and wrong, the particular hush of neighbors delivering news they wish they weren't.

By the time I reached the doorway, buttoning my coat over yesterday, I had counted three things: Odile standing very straight behind her bar with her arms crossed; Neme sitting on a stool, unnaturally quiet for a child who remembered everything; and the open door, where the morning light lay across a smear of churned mud and bootprints. Many bootprints. Warden-issue.

"They came before first light," Odile said, without turning. "Quiet as thieves. Took him at the well, no struggle—he didn't struggle, Vespera, he *walked* with them, calm as Sunday, like he'd been expecting the appointment." Her jaw worked. "Ilsette herself came. The gentle-voiced one. She thanked me for my cooperation. *Cooperation.*"

Neme looked up at me. The child's eyes were old and terrible and certain, the eyes of someone who had already seen how today ends and was deciding whether to say.

"He let them take him," Neme said. "He made them. He said—" the child's voice dropped into a fair imitation of Silas's dry murmur— "*tell her to check the ledgers, and tell her the columns balance.*"

My stomach went cold and then colder.

Because I knew what lived in the guild's ledgers besides names. I had spent weeks among those pages. And somewhere in the archive of a house now crawling with hunters was a record—Corvane's record, Ilsette's prize—of every memory Silas had ever taken, every touch, every theft, including a certain cartographer's stolen childhood, including last night's freely traded gifts, written in a hand that could not be misread.

A full inventory of everything Silas Kavanagh was. Filed, dated, and waiting for the Warden's gentle voice to read it aloud.

I was moving before I finished the thought—coat, boots, the map fragment already in my breast pocket, my hand remembering the weight of a knife I'd sold in the capital and would need to steal back.

"Neme," I said at the door. "Which direction?"

The child smiled, small and sorrowful, like someone handing over a bill already paid.

"Salt House," Neme said. "Hurry. He doesn't have until the moon sets."
