## 24. Ilsette's Brother

There is a particular kind of silence a prisoner makes when they have decided to stop performing. Most captives never reach it. They weep on schedule, bargain on schedule, confess to whatever you suggest if you suggest it kindly enough. The silence of someone who has simply set down the theater and sat inside themselves is rarer. I had heard it twice in twenty years. Once from a smuggler who died rather than give up his route, and once—though I did not know it yet—from my brother, three days before I reported him.

The Kavanagh person had reached it by their second hour in my cell.

I will use no pronouns for them here, because even now I am not certain which they carried, or whether they carried any at all, or whether that was part of the trick of them. Silas Kavanagh. Guild ledger-keeper. The name came to us clean as a coin from Corvane's mouth, along with a great deal else that was less clean, though it took me another week to understand how dirty his hands were beneath the gloves he wore to hide his brand. What mattered that night was this: the crown's chief hunter had at last, after eleven months of the Thirteenth Hunt, the one gift she wanted most.

Not a firestarter. Not a mindreader. A thief of memory who could also, if the rumors out of the Weigh Station were true, *give* it back.

I sat across the table from them with the lamp turned low, because interrogation is mostly lighting. Too bright and they squint and perform. Too dark and they feel safe enough to lie beautifully. Low light makes people honest the way cold water makes them honest: against their will, and briefly.

"You've been very quiet," I said.

"I've been thinking," said Kavanagh.

"About what?"

"Whether you're going to kill me regardless of what I say. Because if so, I'd like to spend my last hours not talking, and I think you'd find that restful too."

It was, I admit, a good opening move. Disarm the interrogator by agreeing with her power. I have watched prisoners try flattery, defiance, madness. I had never before been offered *companionship*.

"The crown doesn't waste assets," I said. "You're an asset."

"I'm a ledger-keeper."

"You're the only gifted person alive who can return what was taken. Do you understand what that means? Every branded warden carrying pain they can't put down. Every citizen whose grief was sold to pay a debt. You could empty our prisons of sorrow in a season." I let the lamp flicker between us. "You could make yourself indispensable."

"And then the Thirteenth Hunt would need a fourteenth," they said, "because I'd still be gifted, wouldn't I. Indispensable and illegal. That's not a life, Warden. That's a longer rope."

I had prepared arguments for a zealot, a coward, and a merchant. I had not prepared them for someone who had already done the arithmetic and arrived at the correct answer without my help. It unsettled me more than screaming would have.

"Your guildmaster sold you," I said, changing course. "Corvane gave us your name, your habits, the layout of your rooms. Does that not anger you?"

Something moved behind their eyes—not anger. Something older and sadder.

"He sold everyone," Kavanagh said. "I expect it stopped feeling like betrayal to him decades ago. That's the terrible thing about selling people, Warden. The price always feels fairer the second time."

* * *

I should tell you about my brother now. I have told no one, and there is a kind of rot that sets into a thing kept too long, so perhaps I am telling you, whoever you are, whoever reads this, because the alternative was telling Kavanagh, and I was not yet lost enough for that.

His name was Tammas. He was two years younger than me and could not hold a grudge longer than a meal, which in our house was a survival trait. When the first purges came—the ones they didn't call purges yet, just "registrations"—Tammas was nineteen and working the ferry at Coldharbour, and one day a passenger's dropped locket showed him a death he had no way of knowing, and he said so, aloud, helpfully, the way he said everything.

He bore the gift of foreknowing small things. Nothing grand. Lost objects, coming storms, the occasional heart attack. Useless to the crown and fatal to himself.

Our parents were dead. I was twenty-one, newly sworn, already decorated for the Salt Road action that I do not talk about, and the form came to my desk because forms always come to the desk of the family member who can be trusted. One signature. One line: *I attest the above-named bears no gift.*

Tammas stood in my doorway while I read it. He knew what the form was. He had seen it coming, probably—he must have seen it coming—and he stood there anyway with that open face of his and said, "Ilse. Whatever you decide, I'll understand it. That's the whole of what I came to say."

And I signed it. I wrote *no gift* over the truth of him, and then, because a lie unreported is a half-measure and I have never been capable of half-measures, I reported him myself within the week. I told myself it was cleaner this way. Better from me than from a stranger. I told myself he would be registered, not branded, because his gift was small.

They branded him in the market square at Coldharbour on a Tuesday, and the iron was hotter than regulation—I learned this later, during the inquiry I requested and then withdrew—and he looked for me in the crowd and did not find me, because I had arranged to be inspecting a warehouse on the far side of town, which is what cowards call duty when duty lets them look away.

He lived eleven years after that. He never once spoke to me again, and he never once spoke *against* me either, which would have been easier. He died in a border skirmish fighting for the crown that had burned him, and they sent me his effects, and among them was a wooden token—the brand-token, cut from the doorpost of our childhood house, with the sigil scorched into it—and I have kept it on my desk through four promotions because I tell myself it keeps me honest, and because I am lying.

That is what I was when I walked into that cell. Now you know. Now, gods forgive me, does the record.

* * *

Kavanagh did not break on the first night, nor the second. On the third night I brought the file on Vespera Aldemar, the disgraced cartographer, laid out photographs of her sister's registration papers, and said, "She traded herself for this woman's safety. Tell me where the refugee column crosses the Glass Steppe and the cartographer hangs instead of burns."

Kavanagh looked at the photographs for a long time. Then they looked at me with something I recognized too late, having spent a career mistaking it for weakness.

Pity.

"That's the move," they said quietly. "Make the prisoner complicit in a stranger's death so the confession feels like mercy. I've written entries on a hundred interrogations, Warden. I know every page of your book, because the guild sold the book to both sides."

"Then you know how it ends."

"I know how it ends for most people." They flexed their bound hands—rope, not iron; I had learned that much about them. "But you keep touching the table."

I glanced down. My hand lay flat on the scarred wood between us, inches from theirs. Standard positioning. I moved it back.

"There it is," Kavanagh murmured.

"It changes nothing. Skin contact requires duration. You'd have to hold on, and I'd have to allow it."

"Would you?"

I stood then, because standing is what I do when a room starts saying things I haven't authorized. I took the photographs off the table. I told them to think about the cartographer's neck, and I went to the door, and I knocked the signal, and none of that is the part that matters.

The part that matters is that at the door I turned around. Habit, I told myself. Final assessment of the asset. And Kavanagh was standing too—they had worked the rope loose hours ago, I understood this in the same instant I understood everything else, they had been free since midnight of the first day and had stayed anyway—and they crossed the cell in three steps and took my wrist.

I am strong. I disarmed men twice their size for a living. I want that recorded somewhere: that I could have broken the grip, that some part of me chose, in the quarter-second available for choosing, not to.

Then the world came apart like wet paper.

* * *

Here is what returning a suppressed memory feels like, as described afterward by the only person I have ever trusted with the description, which is to say nobody, which is to say you:

It does not arrive. It *unfolds*, as if it had always been in the room, folded very small behind my sternum where the crown taught me to keep nothing, and it opens all at once—Tuesday, Coldharbour, the smell of hot iron and horse and frying onions from the market stalls because the world is obscene and goes on frying onions during anything—

Tammas on the platform. His sleeve pinned up. The warden with the iron counting under his breath the way the manual says, one-two-three-four, press-and-hold, and Tammas looking out at the crowd with his open face, searching, and finding, in the third row behind the fishmonger's stall—

Me. Not a warehouse. Not duty. Me, in my gray coat, watching, because I had come after all, because some part of me that signed nothing and reported nothing needed to see it done, needed to stand witness to my own sentence, and he saw me, and he did not cry out when the iron came down, he cried out *my name*. Just the once. Ilse. Like calling me home for supper.

And then eleven years of letters I never answered. Eleven years of the token on my desk. Eleven years of telling recruits that mercy is a leak in the hull, in my father's voice, with my father's certainty and none of my father's excuses, because he at least had never burned anyone he loved.

I came back to myself on the cell floor. I don't remember falling. Kavanagh knelt beside me, having caught my head before it struck the stone—an instinct of theirs, gentleness, the single most dangerous thing in that room—and their face was stricken.

"I'm sorry," they were saying. "I'm sorry, I didn't mean—that wasn't supposed to happen, I only meant to show you—"

"Get away from me."

They got away. Rope dangling from one wrist like a discarded thought. I got up using the wall. My legs worked. My face was wet and I made no attempt to explain this, because there was no explanation that was not the truth, and the truth was a confession to a capital crime: *I burned my brother, and I remembered it now, and I cannot unknow it ever again.*

"Do you see," Kavanagh said softly, "why I couldn't leave the first night? You carry it folded so small you don't even hurt anymore. I've seen that fold before. In ledgers. People sell the wound itself and walk around with the scar and call it peace." Their voice cracked. "I wasn't going to take anything from you. I swear it on whatever I still believe in. I was going to *give*."

The lamp had guttered low. Outside, the Salt House shifted its old bones in the wind off the flats, and somewhere below, a guard laughed at something in the barracks—onions frying, the world continuing—and I understood with total clarity that I was standing at the exact place my whole life had bent toward, the Tuesday in Coldharbour stretched out twenty years long until it ended here, in this cell, with this thief, and the choice was finally, finally in my own hands instead of on a form.

Every procedure I possessed said: sound the alarm. Say the prisoner attempted assault. Say the touch was forced. No one would question it. No one questions Warden Ilsette. I could have them on the branding platform by dawn and my hands would be as clean as they had ever been, which is to say not at all, which is to say the cleanest they would ever be again.

I picked up their rope coil from the floor. I handed it to them.

"East cellar stair floods at high tide," I said. My voice came out level. Twenty years of practice will do that. "The grate is rusted through at the third landing. The night guard rotates at the second bell and the new man doesn't know your face yet, only mine, and I will not be reporting you missing until morning."

Kavanagh stared at me. "Why?"

"Don't ask me that."

"You could come with—" 

"*Don't ask me that.*"

They went to the door. Stopped with their hand on the latch. Looked back with those ledger-keeper's eyes that had already cost me everything and were somehow, impossibly, about to cost me more.

"For what it's worth," they said, "your brother saw you in that crowd. All those years he never spoke against you. Maybe he knew what you'd just found out tonight."

"And what's that?"

"That it's possible to serve a thing and grieve it at the same time. The guild ran on it. So does the crown." A pause. "So, I think, do you."

Then they were gone, soft as a page turning, and I stood alone in the lamplight with my brother's name in my mouth and did not say it, and did not say it, and did not say it.

* * *

In the morning I filed a report stating that the prisoner Kavanagh remained secure, that interrogation continued, and that early results suggested cooperation. It was the first outright lie I had ever entered into an official record, and my hand did not shake, which frightened me more than trembling would have.

By noon I had reason to wish I had lied better. A rider came up from the Steppe with word that Corvane—branded, broken, hungry for restoration—had given Ilsette's superiors something new: not the refugee column, which had already scattered, but a destination. The Door of Salt. The ridge. The thirteenth moon.

And orders came down with it, sealed in the Lord Protector's own wax: proceed to the Door. Seal it with the fugitives inside. Let the century close over them like water.

I read the orders twice. Then I opened my desk drawer and looked at the brand-token, scorched wood, my brother's careful knife-work, the sigil I had put on his skin with a signature.

I had let one prisoner go and called it a lapse. But orders are just signatures too. I had learned that from the best.

I buckled on my sword, called for my second-in-command, and rode east toward the Door of Salt with a report already composing itself in my mind—one that would declare, in flawless bureaucratic prose, exactly what I decided it needed to declare.

Whether I believed it yet, I could not have said. Whether I believed *anything* anymore, after that cell, after Tammas crying my name through twenty years of folded silence—

The wind came off the glass flats smelling of salt, and I rode into it, and I did not look back.
