## 25. Escape from the Salt House

The cellars flooded an hour before midnight, and I want to be clear about one thing: it was not my fault.

I had, in fact, argued against flooding them. I'd stood in Corvane's study three days earlier and said, with the calm of a man who has balanced books for eleven years and never once cooked them, "Guildmaster, if you open the cistern sluices, you will ruin four hundred years of records that are not stored in memory-ink."

He had looked at me the way he looks at a client who tries to pay in counterfeit grief — patiently, pityingly, as if the failure were mine. "Records can be re-copied," he said. "People cannot be un-branded." Which would have been almost noble if he hadn't meant: *people cannot be sold twice.*

So the water came down through the old salt-channels like a debt collector, black and patient, rising ankle-deep, then knee-deep, then to the third stair, carrying with it the particular smell of the Steppe's deep places — brine and iron and something older, something that tasted in the back of the throat like a word everyone had forgotten on purpose.

And somewhere above us, the Salt House was burning.

Not all of it. Not yet. Corvane had lit the first fires in the west wing himself, I learned later, with his own hands, wearing his guild ring like a man performing a sacrament. The Warden Ilsette was coming up the coast road with thirty wardens and a wagon for prisoners, and Corvane had done the arithmetic every guildmaster does at the end: what remains when everything is spent.

His answer was ashes. If the crown could not have the ledgers, no one would. If he could not own the past, he would drown it and burn it and bury it under the collapsed bones of his own house.

There were eleven of us in the cellar when the smoke came down the stairwell like a guest arriving early. Me. Odile, who had come through the smugglers' gate with two of her people and a lantern she held like a weapon. Neme, small and silent and watching the water with eyes that had already seen this night several times over. And eight strangers from the network whose names I will not write here, because some ledgers should stay honest by staying incomplete.

"Tell me the way out is short," Odile said.

"The way out is through," I said. "Which is worse."

* * *

You should understand something about the Salt House cellars before you judge what happened next. They are not really cellars. They are the underside of the guild — the place where we kept what could not be shelved upstairs among the polite acquisitions. Bottled names in their racks. Confiscated memories sealed in wax and glass. And below even those, behind a door most guildmembers never knew existed, the true archive: the ledger room where I had worked alone for eleven years, keeping accounts of thefts so large they had their own weather.

The floodwater reached the ledger room door just after the first beam fell upstairs. I felt it through the floor — a shudder, a groan, a sound like the building clearing its throat before it spoke its last word.

Odile put her hand on my shoulder. "Silas. We go now or we don't."

"I know which shelf it's on," I said, which was not an answer, and she knew it wasn't, and let me pretend anyway.

Because here is the choice, laid out plainly, the way I would lay it out in any ledger: to my left, behind the false panel in the wine rack, sat the guild's wealth. Not coin — coin is vulgar and portable and Corvane kept that elsewhere. The real wealth. The bottled names. Three hundred years of acquisitions in crystal and wax, each one worth more than the street it sat on, each one a life someone had traded away in desperation and that someone else now owned outright. Enough to buy passage for every soul in the network ten times over. Enough to bribe wardens, buy ships, rent armies. Enough, if I'm honest, to make us rich beyond the reach of anything Ilsette could do.

To my right, behind nothing but a plain oak door with a plain iron lock, sat the book of the hunted.

One volume. Leather gone soft with handling. Four hundred and six names in my handwriting, copied from Corvane's master rolls — every gifted person the guild had ever recorded selling, trading, or merely existing within its walls. Every name Corvane had already begun selling to the crown, page by page, betrayal by betrayal, until I'd started copying faster than he could sell.

Four hundred and six names. Worth nothing to anyone except the people who bore them and the hunters who wanted their skins.

I stood between them while the water climbed my thighs and the smoke thickened overhead, and I thought — I'll admit it, I thought about the wealth. I thought about Vespera waiting somewhere on the flats with the Door of Salt a moon-turn away, about how much easier everything would be with three hundred years of leverage in a crate. I thought about my grandmother's lullaby, which turned out to be a map, and how a map plus money could carry us anywhere.

Then I heard Neme cough. A small sound, swallowed quickly, the cough of a child who has learned that making noise gets people taken away.

I opened the plain oak door.

* * *

Getting the book out was the easy part. It weighed less than a cat. I wrapped it in oilcloth from the smuggling stores, bound it tight against my chest with cord, and turned back to find Odile standing in the doorway of the ledger room with an expression I'd last seen on her face at her husband's funeral — grief and fury sharing a chair.

"The bottles," she said. Not a question. She'd seen the racks.

"They're lost either way," I said. "If we take them, we're a caravan. We die slow."

"If we leave them—"

"We leave them for the fire, which frees them better than we ever could." I said it firmly because I needed it to be firm. Whether it was true, whether a burning releases a stolen memory or only ends its captivity — no one knows. But I chose to believe the smoke carried them somewhere. I still do. You have to choose something, or the water comes up past your chin.

We went out through the smugglers' gallery — a low stone channel cut into the cliff beneath the house, half-drowned since the sluices opened, running with thigh-high water that moved like it had opinions. Odile's people went first with the lantern held high. Then Neme, who refused to be carried, who walked into that black water like a small dignitary inspecting a port. Then me, with the book against my heart and the cold climbing my ribs.

Behind us, the Salt House settled another inch, and the sound it made was the sound of my whole life ending — every routine, every rule, every careful invisible day of recording other people's losses in a fair hand. Eleven years. I did not look back. Looking back is how people drown in channels.

Halfway through the gallery, the ceiling came down.

Not all of it. A section, maybe six feet, brought down by fire eating through the floors above — stone and timber crashing into the water close enough to slap spray across my face, close enough that if Odile hadn't hauled Neme back by the collar we'd have lost the child under the rubble. After that, the way was blocked. Behind us: collapse and rising water. Ahead: darkness, and the sea-gate, and whatever waited there.

"There's a gap," one of Odile's smugglers said. He was a broad, quiet man called Brannic, and he said it the way people say things they don't believe. "At the top of the arch. Maybe."

"Maybe is my favorite word tonight," Odile said. "Go."

They went. Brannic went first, found the gap, wedged himself through it with the grace of a man being born sideways, and reached back for Neme. The child went through. Then Odile. Then me — and here I will confess the moment honestly, because a ledger-keeper who lies in his own book is worth nothing: as I squeezed through that gap, with the water sucking at my legs and the roof grinding above, I thought not of Vespera, not of the Door, not of God or justice or freedom.

I thought: *the book is dry.*

That's what survival had made of me by that point. Not a hero. An archivist with wet boots.

* * *

We came out of the sea-gate onto the shingle beach below the cliffs, and the Salt House was fully alight above us, and it was — I record this without pride — beautiful. Fire took the old building the way a good editor takes a manuscript: ruthlessly, and improving the pacing. Sparks went up off the roof tiles and out over the water, and for a moment they mixed with the stars so thoroughly you couldn't tell which was which, and I remember thinking that Corvane had finally achieved the thing he'd always wanted: a guild too grand to be mapped.

Neme tugged my sleeve and pointed up.

Corvane was on the balcony.

He stood in the firelight in his guild robes, which had caught at the hem and burned upward without him appearing to notice, and he was reading aloud. From the master roll. I couldn't hear the words over the fire and the sea, but I didn't need to. I knew that cadence. I'd written in it for eleven years. He was reciting names — the hunted, the sold, the branded — like a priest reading banns, like a man marrying his own destruction, and as I watched, he lifted the master roll itself and fed it page by page into the flames.

Every name he hadn't yet sold to the crown, dying with him.

"He's mad," Odile said quietly. "Truly mad. Or—" She stopped.

"Or he finally read his own books," I said, "and found out what he was."

A beam gave way. The balcony went with it. I will not describe what I saw after that, because some entries deserve to be left blank, but I will say this: I felt nothing, and the nothing frightened me more than the fire had. A man who keeps accounts learns eventually that some balances can't be settled — they can only be closed.

* * *

We walked inland through the dark, eight of us now, soaked and singed and carrying one book, and the flats opened ahead of us like a held breath. Somewhere out there, Vespera was watching the horizon for our signal. Somewhere out there, a moon was turning toward its thirteenth fullness, and a Door was getting ready to open for people who mostly couldn't remember why.

Brannic broke the silence first. "The bottles," he said. "All that wealth. You just left it."

"Yes."

"Why?"

I stopped walking. The others stopped around me, a small congregation on the glass, and I unwrapped the oilcloth just enough to put my hand flat on the cover of the book of the hunted. Four hundred and six names. Some of them were dead already. Some were in Ilsette's wagons. Some were walking these same flats toward the same ridge, guided by songs that were secretly maps.

"Because," I said, "wealth is a thing you spend, and this is a thing you owe."

Nobody laughed. Nobody argued. Odile looked at me for a long moment, and then she did something she had never done in all the years I'd known her: she bowed her head slightly, the way you bow to a superior officer, or to weather.

We walked on.

And it was Neme — of course it was Neme, it was always going to be Neme — who stopped us at the top of the rise. The child stood looking east, where the sky was going grey with a dawn that hadn't arrived yet, and said, in that flat, certain voice that made your skin crawl:

"He isn't finished."

I followed the child's gaze. Far off across the flats, a line of lights. Lanterns. Many of them, moving in formation, unhurried, the way people move when they already know where you're going.

Ilsette's wardens. Coming not for the Salt House, which was already ash, but for the survivors walking out of it — for the book against my chest, and for every name inside it.

Odile swore with impressive fluency. Brannic checked his knife. And I tightened the cord around the only thing I had saved from the fire, and understood, with the clarity of a man reading a final column of figures, that the escape was not over.

It had just changed venues.
