## 27. Neme's Tomorrow

The trouble with seeing tomorrow is that nobody believes you about today.

I'm nine years old. I have a brand-shaped scar on my wrist that I got before I could write my own name, and I can remember things that haven't happened yet the way other children remember their birthdays. This sounds like a wonderful gift when you say it fast. It is not a wonderful gift. It is mostly waiting.

Here is what I saw last night, and here is why I am going to carry it alone, in these two small hands, for as long as they hold:

The Door of Salt opens on the thirteenth moon. And behind it — nothing.

Not darkness. Nothing is worse than darkness. Darkness is a thing; you can put your hand against it and feel where it ends. What I saw behind the Door was the place where things end before they've begun. Grey that wasn't even grey. A country that had been unwritten so thoroughly that even its absence was missing.

And Silas Kavanagh walking into it anyway, with that look on their face — I know that look, because I've seen it on Odile's face when she gives away our last eggs to someone hungrier — and then Silas falling, quiet as a page closing, and the Door opening wide over them like a mouth, and everyone pouring through to safety while the person who paid for the door stays under it forever.

That's tomorrow. Or the day after. Somewhere between moon twelve and moon thirteen. The visions don't come with dates attached. They come with weight instead, and this one weighs as much as a grown man.

* * *

Let me back up, because if I'm going to keep a secret this size I should at least be organized about it. Odile says secrets kept badly are just rumors with ambition.

We're camped in the hollow below the salt ridge — forty-three of us now, though the number changes every time hunters take someone or someone's cousin catches up. There are the glass flats behind us, silvered over with frost in the mornings, sharp enough to open boots and ankles if you walk carelessly. Vespera has her maps spread out on an overturned cart, weighted down with river stones, and she stands over them muttering arithmetic like the numbers owe her money. Which, I suppose, they do: one turn left on the moon, one door, hundreds of people who were born after the country was erased and couldn't carry a true memory of it if their lives depended on it.

Which they do. Depend on it. That's the whole problem. Vespera says it out loud every evening, standing by the fire, doing sums nobody wants done: *the Door opens only to those who carry the country in them.* Most of us carry nothing but hunger and a story somebody told us secondhand. Secondhand doesn't count. Doors can tell the difference. Doors always could.

Silas stands near Vespera during these speeches with their arms folded and their face arranged into something calm, and I watch them watching the crowd, and I think: *you already know something too.* Gifted people leak. We're all of us cracked cups. You can smell another person's gift the way you smell rain coming, if you've been around enough gifts. Mine comes off me like heat off a stove — Neme, who remembers forward — and Odile does her best to stand downwind of me because what I see makes her face do things faces shouldn't do at breakfast.

But Silas leaks grief. Not their own grief. Grief with strangers' fingerprints all over it. They touch people — a hand on a shoulder, a brush of fingers passing bread — and afterward they go quiet for hours, and their eyes get old in a way eyes on a young face aren't supposed to get.

I've known since the weigh station. Everyone knows since the weigh station. But knowing a thing and understanding it are different animals, and I understood it three nights ago when I dreamed forward into next week and found Silas dying in it.

* * *

You want to know what foresight feels like? It feels like swallowing a stone and having to walk around with it until the day arrives and the stone finally settles somewhere in the world outside you. Some stones are pebbles. Rain coming Thursday. The mule throwing a shoe. A hunter patrol taking the eastern pass two days early — I told Vespera that one, and we went west through the reeds instead, and thirty-one people are alive who wouldn't be, and not one of them knows to thank me, which is fine, which is the job.

This stone is not a pebble. This stone is a boulder wearing a boulder's coat.

In the vision — I've had it four times now, and it's the same every time, which means it's heavy-set, fixed, the kind of future that's already poured and only waiting to harden — we're all standing on the salt ridge under a doubled moon. The air tastes like the inside of a shell. The Door shimmers up out of the ridge like heat-haze made solid, tall as a cathedral, thin as paper, and the crowd goes silent the way crowds only go silent when hope and terror sit down at the same table.

And then Silas walks forward. Alone. Nobody asks them to. That's the part I keep turning over like a coin I can't spend: nobody asks. In the vision there's a moment — I see it clear as noon — where Silas looks back at the crowd, at Vespera, at me, and whatever they're looking for, they find it or they decide they don't need it, and they step through the shimmer.

And the grey takes them. Not violently. That's what makes it unbearable. No hunters, no fire, no blade. Just — subtraction. One moment Silas Kavanagh is a person standing in moonlight with a face full of decision, and the next moment the world has quietly agreed to forget them, and the Door swings open like it's been fed, and the crowd surges through into a country that exists again because someone gave it back.

Everyone lives. Except Silas. And no one remembers what was paid, because the payment *was* the remembering.

Doors are crueler than hunters. Hunters want you dead; doors want you *never to have been.*

* * *

"Stop making that face," Odile said this morning, setting down the porridge pot. "You'll scare the little ones."

"I'm not making a face."

"You're making the face you make when you've swallowed something that hasn't happened yet." She crouched to my level, and her voice dropped into the register she uses for things she doesn't want the camp hearing. "What is it?"

Odile is the only person alive I would consider telling. She took me in after the branding square. She rode after the hunters' wagon with a kitchen knife and a borrowed horse and more fury than sense, and when the smugglers brought word that I'd been freed in the raid — that some ledger-keeper with soft hands had touched my brand and taken my pain into themselves so I could walk — she held me so hard I heard her ribs complain.

But I looked at her flour-cracked hands and her tired eyes and I thought: *if I tell you, you will try to stop it. And if you try to stop it, maybe you break the future wrong, and the Door opens onto nothing for all of us, and everyone dies including Silas, and it'll be my mouth that did it.*

Visions aren't instructions. That's the first rule of my gift, learned the hard way, learned from the shoeing of the mule: when I told the farrier the mule would throw its left front shoe on Thursday, he checked the shoe, and it came loose Friday instead and lamed the mule worse. The future is a river, not a road. You can dam it, but the water goes somewhere.

If I dam this river — warn them, wake the camp, beg Silas not to walk — where does the water go? Maybe nowhere bad. Maybe everywhere bad. I'm nine. The arithmetic of that choice is bigger than I am, and I've done a lot of big arithmetic lately.

"It's just weather," I said. "Rain past the flats."

It wasn't raining anywhere. Odile knew it. She let me lie, which is its own kind of love, and ladled porridge, and I ate mine looking at Silas across the fire and hating every bite.

* * *

Here's the thing that makes carrying it hardest. Silas is *kind.*

I mean it in the structural sense, the way you'd say a bridge is sound. Kindness isn't their mood; it's their architecture. I've watched them give away food they needed. I've watched them sit with the old ones at night, holding their hands, letting them remember out loud — and I've noticed that after those nights, the old ones sleep better, dream sweeter, like something heavy was lifted off them and carried elsewhere. Carried into Silas. I know how that works now, a little. I was on the receiving end once, in a weigh station that smelled of iron and straw, with a brand cooling on my wrist and pain sitting on my chest like a grown man. And Silas touched me, and took it, and I saw them flinch — flinch like a person catching a falling anvil with bare hands — and then smile at me anyway, so I wouldn't be scared.

Who does that? Who volunteers to hurt so a stranger's child won't cry?

Someone who would absolutely walk into a grey nothing to buy four hundred strangers a country. That's the problem with kindness built like a bridge: eventually someone walks across it, and if the someone is Silas, they'll walk across anything, including the edge of the world, if it gets the rest of us over.

So I did the only thing I could think of. I started following them around.

Not obviously. I'm small; smallness is camouflage. I fetched water from their side of the stream. I sat near their fire. I asked questions that sounded like a bored child's questions and weren't.

"What was Grandmother Kess like?"

Silas went very still over the water skin they were filling. "Why do you ask that?"

"The smugglers sing her song. The one that's really a map. I thought maybe you remembered her."

A long silence. The stream made its small arguments. "She was stern," Silas said finally, "and her lap was the safest place in any room, and she smelled of salt and tallow, and she used to say—" and then Silas stopped, because saying it cost something, and I watched them decide to pay anyway. "*The land keeps what the crown cannot carry.*"

"That's nice," I said, and my chest was a fist. Because in the vision, when the grey takes Silas, that sentence goes with them. The grandmother. The lullaby. The whole country, folded up inside one person and handed over like a ransom.

I walked away before my face could betray me, and found a hollow in the rocks, and cried the way children are allowed to cry and adults pretend they've outgrown — loudly, uselessly, with snot — and then wiped my face on my sleeve and went back to work, because the work was: *find the crack in the future.*

Every heavy future has one. A loose nail. A checked shoe. My gift isn't perfect prophecy; it's more like reading a river — you can't stop it, but rivers have shallows, and shallows have stones, and stones turn water. I just had to find the stone.

* * *

I spent two days looking. Here's what I found, and here's what I didn't.

The vision has hinges. Small ones. Places where the picture flexes. In three of my four viewings, the sequence is identical right up until the moment Silas steps toward the Door — and in the fourth, there's something new. A figure apart from the crowd. Someone arriving late, broken, dragging themselves up the ridge on hands and knees, and Silas turning toward them, and the vision going white at exactly the wrong moment, like a page torn out of a book.

I can't see who it is. I've tried. I've sat up three nights running, feeding myself to the vision on purpose — which is miserable, like diving headfirst into cold water over and over — and every attempt tears out at the same place. Whoever comes to the ridge in the last hour, the future doesn't want me reading their face yet. Which usually means the future hasn't decided it. Which means — maybe — that's the loose nail.

Somebody not yet accounted for. Somebody whose arrival might turn the water.

I ran through everyone I know. Odile — no, Odile's already in the vision, standing beside me with her hand on my shoulder, gripping hard. Vespera — in the vision, at the front of the crowd, reaching after Silas a half-second too late; I've watched that reach so many times I could draw it. The smugglers, the refugees, the wardens — accounted for, accounted for, accounted for.

Which leaves the unaccounted. And there's only one name in that column that matters.

Corvane.

Guildmaster Corvane, who sold his clients to the crown and got branded for his ledgers and lost his mind somewhere in the burning of his own archive. Last anyone said, he was dying slowly in Ilsette's custody, or fleeing it, or both. He hid his own memories in his ledger decades ago — everyone knows the story now, the smugglers tell it like scripture. A man who traded his past for power and came up empty-handed on both sides of the bargain.

I don't know if he comes to the ridge. I don't know if he crawls or rides or arrives in a box. All I know is the vision has a hole shaped like him, and holes in futures are the only mercy my gift ever offers, and I intend to find out what fills it.

Because here's my private theory, the one I test against the dark when I should be sleeping: Corvane hid his memories in the ledger. Silas saved the book of the hunted from the fire — but ledgers keep each other company, pages bleed into pages, and if Corvane's founding memory of the Steppe is still sewn into that book anywhere, then there might be *two* people who can pay the Door. Or one person who can pay less. Or a key nobody's turned yet.

Or I'm a nine-year-old telling herself stories to keep from screaming. Both things can be true. Most true things travel in pairs.

* * *

Tonight the moon came up twelve-parts gone, one sliver short of whole, and the camp went quiet in that way camps go quiet when everyone is counting the same thing. Vespera stood at the fire with her charts and said, to no one and everyone, "One turn." And Silas stood beside her, and I watched Silas's face in the firelight, and I saw something that nearly broke my secret loose right there.

Relief. Faint, guilty, carefully hidden — but relief. Like someone who's decided on a hard thing and is almost glad the day is nearly here, the way you're glad when a tooth finally has to come out.

They know. Maybe not everything — maybe not the grey, maybe not the never-having-been — but they know they're the payment. They've known for days. They've been walking around camp giving away bread and holding old hands and memorizing faces, and I finally understood what all that gentleness was: inventory. Silas is taking stock of everything they're about to lose, the way you press flowers before leaving a house forever.

I could have said something. Right then. *Don't. There's a hole in the future shaped like Corvane, there might be another way, wait, wait.*

Instead I looked at Odile's hand on my shoulder — already there, already braced in the vision — and I looked at the crowd, four hundred people who crossed half a kingdom of glass on the strength of one door, and I did the arithmetic that's bigger than me and came out where I always come out.

If I speak too soon and the water breaks wrong, everyone drowns. If I hold the secret one more day, maybe the loose nail shows itself, maybe the torn page turns, maybe tomorrow finally tells me who crawls up the ridge — and whether they arrive in time to matter.

One more day. Dread fits in small hands if you grip it tight and carry it close, the way you carry a coal to light a fire you're not sure you'll reach.

I went to my bedroll. I closed my eyes. I dove.

The ridge. The doubled moon. The crowd's held breath. Silas stepping forward—

—and far below, at the foot of the salt trail, a shape. Broken, slow, on hands and knees, dragging itself upward through the frost. Closer than the vision has ever let it come. Close enough that I could nearly see—

Boots. A guildmaster's ruined boots.

And behind me, down in the dark of the camp, a sentry's voice cracked the night open: *"Someone's coming up the trail!"*

Tomorrow had arrived early. It was limping.
