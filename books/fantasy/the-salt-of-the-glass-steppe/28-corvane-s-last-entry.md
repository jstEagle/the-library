## 28. Corvane's Last Entry

There is a particular indignity in crawling. I had imagined, in the abstract and comfortable way a man imagines his own death—usually after a third glass of plum brandy, usually with the ledgers balanced and the candles trimmed—that I would die standing at my desk, quill in hand, mid-entry, so that whoever found me would have no choice but to conclude I had been indispensable to the last.

Instead I crawled. Across salt flats, on my belly, through the hour before dawn, dragging a leg that no longer answered correspondence and an arm that had gone on strike entirely. The brand on my chest had its own opinions about my posture. Ilsette's irons were elegant work; I will grant her that much. She branded me the way I once priced memories: precisely, without cruelty, without mercy, with a craftsman's indifference to what the thing cost the person paying.

The guildmaster of the Glass Steppe Guild, crawling like a penitent across the flats he had spent thirty years pretending not to love.

I had time to think, is the trouble with dying slowly. A quick death spares you the accounting. Mine came with interest compounded hourly, and I lay in the shadow of a glass ridge while fever did its arithmetic, and every sum came back to the same figure: a ledger page, hidden fifty years ago, folded into the binding of a book no one was permitted to open but me.

I had told myself for half a century that I didn't remember why I built the guild.

That is not quite true. I remembered *that* there was a why. It sat in my chest like a coin swallowed whole—you feel the weight of it, you know which direction it rolled, you cannot for the life of you read what's stamped on it. Every year the weight stayed the same and everything around it grew lighter, until one morning I woke up and realized the only thing left in me of any substance was the thing I couldn't read.

So I built a house for other people's pasts, because I could not enter my own.

* * *

I smelled the camp before I saw it—woodsmoke, and under it something older, mineral, the smell of the Steppe itself when it sweats at night. And under that, faintly, tea. Odile's blend. She'd have sent some ahead with the smugglers, because Odile has never once let a fugitive column march without tea, as if warmth could be issued by ration.

I pulled myself over the last rise on my elbows, and there they were: cookfires banked low, bedrolls in the glass-hollows, sentries wrapped in grey against the moonlight. The thirteenth moon hung fat above the ridge, and beneath it, far off, the Door of Salt stood in its shimmer like a held breath. Hundreds of people slept below it who would cross tomorrow if the key could be found. I knew—I had known since the night Silas restored Corvane-the-man instead of sparing Corvane-the-creature—that none of them could pay it. You cannot surrender a memory of a country you were born too late to remember.

And then, at the edge of the firelight, a shape stirred. Sat up. Looked at me the way one looks at something the tide has brought in.

"Master Kavanagh," I said. My voice had been rehearsing dignity for two days. It came out as gravel anyway. "You always did sleep badly near your work."

Silas came toward me without hurry, which I appreciated. Running would have implied I was dangerous. They crouched, took in the brand, the leg, the state of me generally, with those appraiser's eyes that had once valued a soldier's courage at eleven marks and been exactly right.

"You're dying," they said.

"I'm aware. I wrote the entry myself this afternoon." I managed something that in better light might have passed for a smile. "Dated it and everything. Old habit."

"Why are you here?"

And there it was—the question I had crawled three miles to answer, and I found I couldn't dress it up. Not with Silas. I had spent their entire apprenticeship teaching them that every transaction has a surface price and a true price, and here I was at last, the final client of my own guild, about to name my true price aloud.

"Not for mercy," I said. "Let's be clear about that first. I've done the sums on mercy. I don't qualify."

Silas said nothing. Waiting. They learned waiting from me, God help them both of us.

"Fifty years ago," I said, "I hid something in the ledgers. Page forty-one of the founding volume, folded into the binding, written in my own hand before my hand was worth anything. I sealed it with the guild's oldest rite—the one we used for memories too valuable to sell and too dangerous to keep loose." My voice cracked on *valuable*, and I let it. "It's mine, Silas. It's the only thing in that entire archive that was ever mine. I put it away myself. I paid someone—I don't remember who, that's rather the point—to take it out of me and write it down. And then I built a vault around it and called the vault a business."

The fire popped. Somewhere behind us, the Door shimmered on, indifferent as arithmetic.

"I need you to touch it," I said. "Before the moon turns. Before I go somewhere even I can't keep records."

* * *

They made me rest first. Doctor's nonsense from a person who wasn't a doctor, delivered in the tone of an accountant refusing a fraudulent entry, and I obeyed, because obedience to competence is the last luxury I have ever allowed myself. Someone—Odile's influence again—put a blanket over me. A blanket. On the Glass Steppe. For the man who sold the hunted to the hunters.

I earned the blanket the way I earned everything else: by being useful. While the fever worked me over, I talked. I told Silas where the founding volume sat—not in the burned archive, thank whatever gods traffic in irony; I had moved it years ago to the salt cellar under the weigh station, the one place Ilsette never searched because she believed I'd never hide anything from myself—and I described the seal precisely enough that Silas could break it without destroying the contents. Memory-ink is delicate. So am I. We had that in common.

"You could have come back for this yourself," Silas said, turning the brittle folded page in the firelight. "Any time in fifty years. One touch. Why now? Why wait until you're—" They gestured at all of me.

"Because I was afraid of it," I said. That word again. I kept having to say it. "Do you know what I told myself, all these years? That I'd sold my past for power. Clean trade. Man walks into a memory-broker's back room with his childhood under his arm and walks out with capital. Everyone in this country makes that trade in some form; I just did it wholesale." I watched the page catch the firelight, my own younger handwriting marching down it in columns. "But that's the story a man tells when he can't afford the true one. Here's the true one, since I'm paying by the word: I gave it away because keeping it hurt. That's all. No strategy. No empire-building. Some grief came into me when I was young and I could not hold it, so I poured it out onto a page and locked the page in a box and spent fifty years building a fortress around the box and calling the fortress commerce."

"What's in it?" Silas asked quietly.

"I don't know," I said, and laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing turned into the brand lighting up along my ribs like a festival lantern, and when I could speak again I said, "That's the joke, isn't it? Fifty years as keeper of the largest archive of stolen memory in the world, and the one entry I can't read is my own."

* * *

Silas broke the seal at moonset.

I want to record this properly, since recording things is the only prayer I ever learned. They unfolded the page with the care of a person handling something alive. The memory-ink moved under their fingers—my words swimming, rearranging, the old rite waking. And then Silas pressed their palm flat against the writing, and closed their eyes, and reached into the paper the way they reach into everything, and lifted.

Fifty years fell on me like weather.

Not gradually. That's the lie everyone tells about returned memories, that they come back gently, like tide. They don't. They come like a wall of water, all at once, and you are simply standing where the coast used to be.

I was nineteen. I was in my grandmother's kitchen in a village whose name I have not spoken or heard in half a century, and she was singing, and the song was not a lullaby—it was a map, verse by verse, the ridgeline and the salt road and the door that opens only to what you love enough to lose—and outside the window the wardens were coming up the lane with their list, and my mother pushed the founding deed of our house into my hands and said *run, and whatever they offer you, whatever they take, keep one thing, keep one true thing of this country inside you, because a country lives as long as someone remembers it*—

And I ran. Of course I ran. And the wardens caught me at the ferry, and I was young and clever and terrified, and I made the trade they offered: my memory of home, surrendered forever, in exchange for my name struck from their list. I walked out of that office free and hollow, and I swore I would build something so large around the hole that no one would ever see it.

I built the guild. I built it out of the shape of my own theft. Every memory I brokered, every widow's grief and soldier's courage I priced and shelved, was me telling the world: *see, it's fine, see how well it keeps, see how little it matters.* And underneath, the swallowed coin, unreadable, weighing the same every single year.

I wept. There is no dignified way to report this, so I will report it plainly: the guildmaster of the Glass Steppe Guild lay on a salt flat in a borrowed blanket and sobbed like the boy at the ferry, and Silas Kavanagh knelt beside him and held his shoulder and did not look away, which was the only kindness left that could have reached me.

"My grandmother," I managed. "Her name was Sefa. Sefa Okoro-Kavanagh. She sang the road to the Door." I pressed my hands against my face. "I traded her. I traded all of them. For a name on a list that said I was nobody."

"No," Silas said. Firm. Appraiser-firm, correcting a mispriced lot. "You traded the *memory*. The country isn't in the memory. Listen to me—you carried it anyway. Fifty years, and you couldn't read it, and you still built your whole life around protecting it. That's not nothing, Master Corvane. That's the key."

And I stopped. Because the boy at the ferry had heard the wardens say what the Door required—he'd heard it through the office wall, the clerk explaining it to another condemned family, bored, procedural—and in the flood of returning years I had forgotten I knew it, the way I'd forgotten everything, but now it surfaced, clean and terrible:

The Door does not open for a memory *carried*. Carried memories can be stolen; carried memories can be lied about; a carrier can turn back. The Door of Salt opens for a memory *surrendered*—given wholly, given forever, poured out at the threshold until the giver no longer possesses it. You do not bring your love of the country to the Door. You leave it there. Whoever opens it walks through knowing they will never know why the place mattered.

"The key was never a thing," I whispered. "It's a giving. Silas—the Door doesn't want a memory brought to it. It wants one given *up*. Forever. Whoever pays it loses the Steppe out of their mind—grandmother, lullaby, name, all of it, permanently, irretrievably—" I grabbed their wrist with the arm that still half-worked. "No one in that camp can pay that. Do you understand? Not one of them was born remembering. Except—"

I stopped. Looked at them. Watched them understand.

"Except the one person here who has touched every page of the founding records," Silas said slowly. "Who knows the songs are maps. Who has carried fragments of this country in their skin since they were a child lifting memories off clients' hands." Their face did something complicated. "Except me."

"I'm not asking you to," I said. Which was true, and also the smallest sentence I have ever spoken, because I had crawled three miles dying specifically so that someone would be able to ask them to, and we both knew it, and I watched them know it, and I hated myself with the clean efficiency of long practice.

Then I spent what I had left on honesty, since I'd finally learned where I kept it.

"Listen to me," I said. "I am not worth mourning and I am not worth imitating. I took the coward's trade at nineteen and called it survival, and then I made ten thousand people take versions of it so mine wouldn't look so small. If you give the Door what I refused to give—if you walk through not-knowing—then everything I wasted gets spent after all. Do you see? A man's life is an entry in a ledger, Silas. Mine is written crooked and can't be corrected. But the balance can carry forward. Let my waste be your—" I coughed, waved a hand, lost the word, found it. "*Your opening.*"

* * *

Near dawn I asked for the ledger.

Not the founding volume—Silas had that safe—but a plain notebook, and a stub of pencil, because a man should make his last entry himself. My hand shook. The letters came out like a drunkard's fence. I didn't care. I wrote what mattered:

*Corvane, guildmaster. Sold the hunted to save the house, and the house was already lost, and so was I, decades earlier, by my own signature. Founding memory recovered through the agency of Silas Kavanagh, who owes me nothing. Key to the Door of Salt: surrender, not possession. Tell them I remembered, at the end. Tell them the country was real. Grandmother Sefa's kitchen faced east. The song goes: ridge, then river, then the door that opens to what you love enough to lose.*

I signed it. I looked at the signature a long time. It was the first honest line I had drawn in fifty years, and it looked exactly like my handwriting, which I found unbearably funny, and I laughed until the brand lit up and the laughing stopped mattering.

Silas took the notebook from my hands. "I'll enter it properly."

"In your new ledger," I said. "The one without debts." My vision was going soft at the edges, the moon fading, the fires fading, everything going the gentle grey of an unfinished page. "Put me in it anyway. Mark me *paid in full, by another's hand.*"

The last thing I saw clearly was the Door of Salt, far off on the ridge, holding its shimmer against the paling sky. The last thing I felt was Silas's hand resting over the brand on my chest—not lifting, just resting, the way you rest your palm on a closed book you intend to open later—and I thought, with the last coherent corner of my mind: *so that's what it weighs. So that's what it feels like, on the other side of the touch. No wonder they never stopped.*

I died the way I lived: leaving instructions.

But I died knowing the combination.

* * *

They tell me the camp went quiet when Silas stood up with the founding page in one hand and my notebook in the other, and said, in a voice pitched to carry across every bedroll and cookfire:

"He named the key. And he named who can pay it."

Vespera was at Silas's side before the sentence ended, map-cases and all, her eyes moving between the page and Silas's face and finding there the thing Neme, they say, had already seen alone in the dark for three nights running: that the Door would open tomorrow, and the price was Silas, entire.

The thirteenth moon set. The column began to wake.
