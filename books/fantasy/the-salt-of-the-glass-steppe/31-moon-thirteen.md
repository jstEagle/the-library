## 31. Moon Thirteen

I have drawn coastlines that did not exist and erased rivers that did, so believe me when I tell you I know what a lie looks like at scale. A false map is a beautiful thing: confident lines, tidy labels, the whole world agreeing to behave. What I was looking at now refused to behave.

The thirteenth moon rose over the ridge in the last hour of dusk, and it rose doubled—two discs, one silver-white and one faintly rose-gold, overlapping like a coin seen through water. Every astronomer in the capital had sworn there were twelve moons. I had redrawn their charts myself, twice, with my own hand, because a cartographer who admits the sky has thirteen moons is a cartographer who gets asked uncomfortable questions about what else she's been smoothing over.

The double moon threw two shadows from every person on the steppe. Hundreds of us, and hundreds more shadows, all leaning east toward the ridge where something was beginning to shimmer.

"There," Neme said.

The child had said little since the weigh station. They stood beside me on a shelf of glass-salt that creaked faintly under our boots like lake ice in early spring, and they pointed with the terrible economy of someone who had already seen this moment once and hadn't enjoyed it.

The Door of Salt was not a door. That was the first thing. It was an absence—a tall seam in the ridge where the air went wrong, catching light the way oil catches light, vertical, maybe forty feet of nothing shaped like a doorway if you squinted and wanted very badly to be home. As the twin moons climbed, the seam brightened, its edges rippling, and a sound came off it that I felt in my teeth before I heard it in my ears: a low chord, like wind across the mouth of a bottle, or a choir humming behind a wall.

Behind me, the refugee column stopped murmuring.

* * *

You should understand what that column was, because I helped make it and I still barely believe it. Three hundred souls, give or take—salt-smugglers who'd spent their lives singing navigation disguised as drinking songs; families from the Steppe settlements with their goods strapped to their backs; Odile with her tavern keys hanging off her belt like a trophy she'd forgotten to put down; eleven freed captives from the weigh station walking with the careful steps of people relearning that ground doesn't have to hurt; and Corvane's ledger of the hunted, copied out in Silas's small precise hand, distributed among six carriers so no single death could erase it again.

And at the front, slightly apart, Silas Kavanagh, guild clerk, memory-thief, the person who had burned down the only life they'd ever built and looked calm about it in a way I found both admirable and infuriating.

They were staring at the Door the way I stare at a blank sheet before a commission. Not afraid. Calculating.

"Vespera." Odile's voice at my shoulder, pitched low. "It's open."

"It's opening," I corrected, because I am constitutionally incapable of letting imprecision stand even during miracles. "Look at the edges. It's still sealing itself shut between pulses. We go when it holds."

"When will it hold?"

"When the key turns." I didn't say what the key was. Half the column knew by then—Corvane had died naming it, and secrets travel through a refugee column faster than plague—but knowing and understanding are different countries, and most of us were still standing at the border.

Neme tugged my sleeve. The child's foresight made them eerie company; they kept glancing at things before they happened, reacting to a stumble two seconds before the stumbler took it. Right now they were looking at Silas, and their face was doing something I'd learned to read as grief arriving early.

"What do you see?" I asked quietly.

"A long line," Neme said. "People going through. And—" The child stopped. Whatever else lived in that tomorrow, they swallowed it. Children shouldn't know how to swallow grief like that. Nobody should. "And you drawing," they finished, which was either the truth or a kindness, and with Neme it was often both.

* * *

By full dark the Door held.

Not steadily—it breathed, widening and narrowing like something alive, its hum rising and falling—but it held open long enough, each time, that you could see through it. And what you could see through it broke the column's heart in the best possible way.

Grass. Green grass, moving in a real wind, under a sun-warm sky that had no business existing at midnight. A river's glint. Hills. A country that appeared on no chart I had ever touched, because I had personally helped erase every chart it appeared on, and here it was anyway, patient as arithmetic, waiting a century for someone to admit it existed.

The murmur that ran through three hundred people sounded, to my ear, exactly like the sound a crowd makes when it remembers.

"That's the Salt Steppe," an old smuggler said behind me—not our Steppe, the glass flats we'd been crossing, but the other one, the true one, the grandmother-country. "That's—I know that river bend. My mother sang about that river bend." His voice cracked on *sang*, and half a dozen people near him made the small involuntary sound that crowds make when a memory lands.

Then the hum of the Door changed pitch, deepened, and a pressure rolled across us like weather, and the crowd's forward motion simply—stopped.

Because everyone had understood, in that same rolling instant, what Corvane had died explaining. The Door opened to a freely surrendered memory of the country, given up forever. Not carried. Surrendered. Whoever paid became the key, walked through, and never knew why any of this had mattered. No grandmother. No lullaby. No river bend in a mother's song. The Steppe would be gone from them the way it had been erased from the maps—cleanly, completely, by their own hand.

Nobody moved.

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened next, because history—real history, the kind I swore my new maps would hold—deserves honesty more than it deserves dignity.

For a long time, nobody moved, and it wasn't cowardice. It was arithmetic. Everyone was doing the same sum I was: *what would I lose?*

A woman near the front—I'd marked her days ago as a widow from the eastern settlements, practical face, hands like tree roots—took one step toward the Door and stopped, and I watched her mouth shape the word *no* without sound. She had lost a husband to the Hunt already. Her memories were all she had left of him. Asking her to also lose the country they'd shared—

An old man began arguing with his son in a low fierce whisper. "You're young. You'd forget less. You never even—"

"I remember everything you told me, Da. That counts."

"It doesn't count for the Door. It has to be *true*. Your own. Firsthand."

"How do you know?"

"Because Corvane tried to fake it, and look at him." Which was grimly fair. Corvane had spent decades selling other people's pasts and had ended up dying in the salt with a brand on his chest, begging for the return of the self he'd pawned. If the Door accepted counterfeits, Corvane would have walked through it years ago.

Silas stood at the front through all of it, saying nothing.

That silence had a shape. I knew Silas well enough by then—well enough to love them, though we had been circling that word like two cartographers mapping the same coastline from opposite shores and pretending not to notice the overlap—and I knew that their silence was not hesitation. It was a person standing at a podium waiting for the room to finish its arguments, because they already knew how theirs would end.

My stomach dropped a full fathom ahead of the rest of me.

"No," I said, too loudly, into a lull.

Heads turned. Silas turned. In the doubled moonlight their face was calm in the specific way that I had learned meant the opposite of calm.

"You've thought of something," Silas said. Not a question.

"I've thought of several somethings, and none of them involve you volunteering."

"Vespera."

"Don't *Vespera* me. I renounced a crown for you. I can renounce whatever this is too."

"It's not mine to renounce." Their voice stayed level, which was worse than shouting. Around us the crowd had gone quiet in the particular way of people who realize they're watching something private and cannot look away. "Think about it. Who here actually carries the country? Truly, firsthand?"

I ran the roll in my head and hated every entry. The old smuggler with the mother's song—he'd been born after the erasure; his knowledge was secondhand, inherited, lovely and insufficient. The widow—the same. Nearly everyone in the column had been born inside the erasure, raised on fragments, songs, rumors. The Door wanted a true memory, freely given, and true memories of the Salt Steppe were a currency so rare that our entire nation of exiles was, collectively, nearly bankrupt.

Nearly.

There were exactly two people in that crowd who had been born before the erasure closed, who remembered the green hills and the river bend and the taste of the salt bread, firsthand, in their own skulls. One was a smuggler matriarch named Yeva who had crossed with us from the flats and who was, at this moment, eighty-one years old and visibly wrestling with the arithmetic herself—I watched her hand rise halfway toward the Door and fall back, watched her shame at it, watched her granddaughter grip her arm and whisper *don't you dare, Gran, don't you dare leave yourself behind.*

And the other was Silas Kavanagh. Grandmother's lullaby that was secretly a map. Grandmother's kitchen. Grandmother's homeland, carried thirty years in a clerk's careful head.

One candidate with a full purse, and four hundred people watching.

"You could split it," I said desperately, quietly. "Give part. Surely a partial memory—"

"The key isn't a toll, Vespera. It's a surrender. Corvane's memory was clear." Silas almost smiled. "All or nothing. The Door doesn't haggle. Believe me, I checked the ledgers."

"This is not funny."

"I'm aware." They finally looked away from the Door, at me, and their composure flickered—one heartbeat of naked terror, quickly filed back under competence. "But it's the only exit, and it seals for another century when the moon sets. There are children in this column who've never seen grass. There's a whole country on the other side that needs a cartographer." A breath. "It needs one more than it needs a clerk who can't even remember why the salt matters."

"I'll remember for you," I said. It came out cracked, undignified, true. "I'll write it all down. Everything you tell me tonight. Every detail. I'll build you your country back page by page, and when we cross, I'll—"

"And I won't know why you're crying while you do it." Silas reached out and took my hand, palm against palm, and I felt the ghost-touch of their gift—careful, restrained, offering rather than taking. For a moment I felt what they felt, or a sliver of it: not resolve. Grief, enormous and pre-paid, being carried the way you carry a wound you've decided not to scream about. "That's the price. Not the forgetting. Watching the people who love you grieve something you can't even name."

The crowd had gone utterly silent. Four hundred people holding their breath in two kinds of shadow.

Yeva, the old smuggler, stepped forward. "Boy," she said—she called everyone boy; she'd called Odile boy, she'd called Warden Ilsette's second-in-command boy through the bars of his own cage—"boy, if it must be one of us two, it should be me. I'm eighty-one. I've had my country longer. Take mine."

"It doesn't work by worthiness, Yeva." Silas's voice gentled. "And it isn't a lottery. It's a door. Someone walks to it and gives what they carry. I carry the most. So."

"So nothing," Odile growled, pushing forward, tavern keys jangling like an argument. "We find another way. We wait a century. We—"

"The moon sets in three hours, Odile."

"Then we spend three hours finding—"

"*No.*"

Silas said it quietly, and it landed louder than any shout. The hum of the Door filled the pause, that choir-behind-a-wall sound, patient, indifferent, eternal.

And then Neme spoke.

The child had been silent so long I'd half forgotten they were there, small and grave at the edge of the firelight, two shadows making a strange hourglass shape behind them. Now they walked forward—all the way forward, past Odile, past me, until they stood directly in front of Silas and looked up with those eyes that had seen tomorrow and come back to report.

"You die," Neme said.

Every sound in the world stopped. Even the Door's hum seemed to lean in.

"Not tonight," the child went on, in the flat careful voice of someone reciting coordinates. "Not there. But the tomorrow I saw, the first one—you walk through alone, and later there's a crossing, a river crossing, and you die not knowing anyone's name, and Vespera draws your grave marker because nobody else knows what to write on it." Neme's chin trembled, and they steadied it with visible effort. "That was before. Tomatoes change."

"Tomatoes?" Odile whispered.

"Futures bend," Neme said, with the weary authority of a child correcting adults, which is the most exhausting thing a child can do. "Around choices. This morning I saw a different line. If—" and here the child faltered, genuinely faltered, and looked at me, and what passed between us was not words. It was the question every seer dreads: *may I spend the future to buy the present?*

I nodded, because I saw no other honest answer.

"If Silas goes to the Door with someone beside them," Neme said, "holding their hand, the line changes. I can't see past the change yet. It's foggy. But it's not the river. It's not the grave marker." The child turned to the crowd, to four hundred faces, and delivered the sentence that unsealed us:

"The Door takes a memory. Nobody said it takes you alone."

Silence. Then the widow with tree-root hands laughed—one short, wet, astonished bark—and said, "Well. Obviously. Obviously you hold someone's hand. What kind of country do we think we're founding?"

The murmur that went through the column then was nothing like the earlier one. It was warmer, rougher, a sound like a fire catching. People began shifting, pressing gently forward, and I understood that something had changed in the arithmetic—not the Door's terms, but ours. We had spent the whole night calculating loss, and a child had just reminded us that some things are carried better in pairs.

Silas looked at Neme for a long moment. Then at me.

"Well, cartographer," they said, and their voice finally shook, and I loved them completely for it. "How's your memory?"

"Excellent," I said. "It's the best thing about me. Ask anyone I've ruined."

"I'm about to ask you to hold onto all of mine."

"Silas—"

"Every detail. The lullaby. The kitchen. The way the salt bread tasted. Write it all down, and when we cross—" their grip tightened on my hand, "—read it to me until I believe it. That's the bargain. That's my price for being the key. You become my country, Vespera. Page by page."

Above the ridge, the thirteenth moon climbed to its zenith, doubled and burning, and the Door of Salt widened until its hum filled the whole sky, and four hundred people stood in the terrible stillness before sacrifice, and Silas Kavanagh took one step toward the shimmering absence with my hand in theirs—

—and stopped, because far behind us, down the dark slope of the glass flats, lanterns were coming. Many lanterns, moving fast, in formation.

Warden's lanterns.

"We have minutes, not hours," Odile said, and drew her keys like a weapon.
