## 32. What Silas Gives Away

I have spent my whole life learning what things cost.

That is the trade, after all. That is the whole of it. Everything in the Glass Steppe has a price written somewhere—in a ledger, on a tag, in the careful arithmetic of a client's face when they set a memory on the scale and watch it weighed against bread or passage or the settling of a debt. I have recorded ten thousand transactions. I know to the copper piece what a mother's voice goes for these days, what a first love fetches at midsummer versus midwinter. I could quote you the going rate for courage, and tell you how it drops after a bad harvest.

So you would think that when the moment came—when I stood before the Door of Salt with four hundred refugees behind me and one moon-turn left and every eye in the crowd fixed on my back—I would have known exactly what I was about to spend.

I didn't. Not even close.

* * *

Let me back up an hour, because the hour matters, and because I am stalling, and because if this is the last time I get to be the narrator of my own life I intend to narrate it properly, digressions and all.

The Door had come open at dusk like a held breath finally released—a seam of pale light along the ridge where there had only been salt-crusted stone. Corvane's dying words had told us the shape of it: *the key was never a thing.* No map fragment, no token, no bloodline. The Door opened for a memory of the country itself, freely surrendered, given up forever. Whoever paid would walk through knowing nothing of why they had come.

Vespera worked it out loud, pacing the salt flats with her arms crossed against the cold. "It's a toll," she said. "The oldest kind. The country doesn't want to be carried out by people who own it. It wants—" she stopped, jaw tight, "—it wants to be *given*."

"Given by whom?" Odile asked. "Half these people were born after the erasure. They've got nothing to give."

"That's not true." Vespera's voice went quiet in that way it does when she's about to say something that costs her. "They remember their grandmothers' songs. Their fathers' stories about a village that isn't on any map. Fragments. Secondhand country. It counts—it would count—but no one here will give up the last piece of it they have. And I don't blame them."

She looked at me when she said it. She'd been looking at me since Neme came back from her visions gray-faced and silent, since Corvane died with his restored memories weeping down his cheeks and the Door's true key named in his last breath. She'd been looking at me the way you look at someone standing on the edge of a thing.

Because she knew. Of course she knew. She had watched me carry strangers' memories in my hands for months. She knew better than anyone alive that I was full to bursting with the Salt Steppe—not just my own remembering, but my grandmother's lullaby learned at her knee, the smugglers' liturgy, the name-songs, the way the glass flats sang under moonlight like wine glasses struck soft. I carried more of this country than anyone living.

Which meant I was the richest person on this ridge.

And rich people, as any ledger-keeper knows, are always being asked to pay.

* * *

The crowd parted for me without being asked to. Four hundred people, silent, and the silence made a corridor straight to the light.

Neme found me halfway there. She took my hand—small, chapped, steady—and didn't say anything about tomorrow. Whatever she'd seen, whatever version of me fell at the threshold of this thing, she kept it behind her teeth. She just walked beside me for thirty steps, which was its own kind of testimony, and then let go and stepped back into the crowd.

Odile pressed something into my other hand as I passed. Her brand-token, the old tavern-keeper's mark, worn smooth from her thumb worrying it for forty years. "For after," she said gruffly. "In case you forget yourself entirely. Someone ought to know your name even if you don't."

"You'll remember it for me?"

"I'll shout it across any square you're standing in, boy. Don't you worry about that." Her voice cracked on *boy*, and she shoved me forward before either of us could make a scene of it.

And Vespera walked with me the last hundred yards. Neither of us spoke. What was there? We'd said everything worth saying in the flooded cellar, in the tent after the riot, trading memories deliberately for the first time like two children exchanging treasures—here, hold this grief, it's heavy; here, take this joy, I want you to have a copy. There were no words left that weren't already carved somewhere in both of us.

At the threshold she stopped. I turned.

"Don't do this because it's arithmetic," she said fiercely. "Don't stand there tallying lives against memories and decide four hundred beats one. You're not a ledger anymore, Silas. If you give this, give it because you choose to."

"I am always a ledger," I said. "But—" and here is the strange part, the part I keep turning over even now, even without knowing why it mattered—"but this isn't subtraction. I keep reaching for the feeling of it, the weight of what I'm about to lose, and it won't sit still on the scale. It keeps getting *lighter*. Like the country already knows. Like it's saying *finally, someone understands the terms*."

Her hand found mine. Her skin against mine, and I felt her give me something unasked—one bright sliver of herself, freely offered: the two of us, years from now maybe, older, laughing at something I couldn't yet imagine, in a place with green in it. Green. Actual green. My grandmother's country had none, and I hadn't known until that moment how badly I wanted to see some.

"That's cheating," I whispered.

"I'm a disgraced royal cartographer. Cheating is my native language." But her eyes were wet. "Walk through angry if you have to. Walk through grieving. Just walk through."

* * *

The Door was taller up close than it had looked from the valley floor. A seam of white-gold light hanging in the air above a sill of ancient salt, humming the note I'd heard all my life in the glass flats but never identified—the lullaby, the actual lullaby, sung by geology. My grandmother hadn't made it up. She'd translated it. All those years rocking me on her hip in a garret in the capital, singing about a door and a doubled moon, and she was giving me navigation instructions the entire time, and I thought it was just a song.

I laughed. I couldn't help it. Four hundred people watching, Warden Ilsette holding the pass with her wardens disarmed and her second-in-command in irons, the thirteenth moon rising fat and doubled over the ridge—and I laughed, because the joke was on me. I'd been carrying the key since I was four years old. I'd sung it myself, off-key, a thousand nights.

The light warmed as I approached. Not welcoming, exactly. *Recognizing.*

I put my palm flat against the light.

Here is what surrendering a memory actually feels like, since no one has ever managed to write it down and I appear to be the last authority: it doesn't hurt. That's the horror of it. When clients gave things up in our guild hall, they always described relief, lightness, the lifting of a weight they hadn't known they carried. The grief comes later, arriving like a bill collector, when you reach for the missing thing and find smooth wall.

For me it happened all at once, and it happened like tide going out.

First went the small things, the sediment: the smell of salt on hot stone, the particular squeak of glass sand underfoot, the taste of steppe water—mineral, faintly sweet. Then the geography, peeling away like wallpaper: the ridge roads, the weigh stations, the market square where Odile's tavern leaned its comfortable shoulder against the world. Then the people. Odile shouting my name—I felt that go, and I grabbed at it, too late, the way you grab at a dream. The child Neme's hand in mine. Ilsette's face, and whatever complicated thing lived between us now.

Then my grandmother.

That one fought me. Or I fought for it. I hung onto her the way a drowning man hangs onto driftwood—her hands, flour-dusted, kneading bread in a garret kitchen; her voice climbing through the lullaby's turns; her laugh, low and cracked, when I asked her why we never went home. *We are home, little ledger. Home is a thing you carry.* I held that sentence with both hands and the tide took it anyway, gently, the way you'd take a toy from a sleeping child. And with her went the lullaby, the notes dissolving even as I reached for the melody, and with the lullaby went the reason I ever knew this place at all—

—and with that, the last of it: the origin of my own name. *Silas.* Grandmother's choosing. Something about salt, something about keeping. Gone. I stood at the Door of Salt unable to say why my name was mine.

The tide finished going out. I stood in the empty room of myself and listened to the silence where a country used to be.

* * *

The Door widened.

Not dramatically—no thunder, no earthquake. It simply opened the way a hand opens, the way a fist relaxes, the way suspicion becomes trust in a single long exhale. The seam became a gate became a doorway wide enough for ox-carts, for families walking abreast, for four hundred people and their bundles and their terrified hope.

Behind me, the crowd made a sound I will hear for the rest of my life—which, granted, may not be long, given Neme's earlier forecasts, but still. It wasn't cheering. It was quieter than that and bigger: the sound of four hundred people realizing the way was open, and that it had been bought, and guessing correctly who had paid.

Then Odile's voice, cracking across the flats like a whip: "*SILAS KAVANAGH!*"

I didn't know who she meant. That's the truth of it. I turned toward the sound with everyone else, a stranger among strangers, and saw a broad woman with a tavern-keeper's forearms pointing at me with fierce, wet-eyed certainty, and I understood dimly that I had done something enormous for these people, though the shape of it slid away whenever I tried to look at it directly, the way a word repeated too many times loses meaning.

Vespera took my arm. "Come on," she said softly. "Time to go through."

"What's through there?" I asked. It seemed a fair question.

"Green," she said. "I promise you green."

* * *

We crossed in the last light of the doubled moon. I walked beside people whose names I didn't know, past faces I'd apparently bled for, and every one of them looked at me the way you look at a returning soldier—with gratitude and grief tangled together, and something else underneath, something almost unbearable: the careful tenderness you show someone you love who has lost themselves. They kept almost speaking to me. They kept stopping themselves.

One child—small, dark-eyed, familiar in a way that made my chest ache with no attached explanation—took my free hand and swung it as we walked, chattering about nothing, and I let her, because her hand fit mine like it had been designed to.

The far side of the Door smelled different. Richer. Softer. When I crested the rise beyond, I saw what Vespera had promised: green, spreading valley-dark in the twilight, rivers threading it silver, land that had never been taxed or mapped or sold. Behind us the Door shimmered, already narrowing, sealing slowly behind the last of the column like a wound closing over a splinter.

Ahead, Vespera stood on the rise with a charcoal stick already in her hand and a scrap of hide flattened on a rock, drawing the first line of a map of a country that had never appeared on any map. She caught me looking and smiled—tired, wrecked, incandescent.

"So," she said. "Who are you, then? Now that you've paid for the privilege of asking."

I considered the question honestly. I was a man with calloused hands who knew ledgers, apparently. A stranger with a name he couldn't explain, standing in a country he'd bought with the only self he had. A blank page.

"I don't know," I said. And then, because it felt true in a way I couldn't account for: "Ask me again tomorrow. I have a feeling I'm going to be written."

Below us, in the gathering dark, the first fires of the settlement were being lit—and every face around those fires, I noticed, kept turning toward me. Watching. Waiting. As if they'd all agreed on something I hadn't been told.

As if they were planning to give me something back.
