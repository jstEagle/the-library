## 35. Ilsette's Report

The ink was the hardest part.

Not the composition—I have composed a hundred reports in my career, each one a small architecture of omissions, each one load-bearing for someone's career or someone's neck. Composition I could do in my sleep, and on several memorable occasions nearly did. No, what made the ink hard was that this time every omission had to point *away* from the truth so precisely that the truth would be safe inside its own absence. A lie told carelessly gets people caught. A lie told well gets them free.

I sat at my desk in the Hall of Wardens, third window from the corner, the one that catches the morning light and makes everything on the page look more innocent than it is, and I wrote:

*Report of Warden Ilsette Vay, concerning the expedition to the Salt Steppe and the alleged phenomenon known as the Door.*

And then I did what any honest officer of the crown does at the start of an honest report.

I lied.

---

Let me be clear about something, because history—should anyone ever read this, should it survive me, should it somehow drift across whatever border the exiles crossed—will want to call me a traitor, and I would like the record to show that the traitor position was already filled. By me. Years ago, when I stood in a magistrate's office with my hands folded like a good daughter of the law and gave them the name of my own brother.

Everything since has merely been interest accruing on that principal debt.

So no. This is not treason. Treason implies a turning. I turned long ago; I simply kept walking in uniform, because it turns out that a uniform is the best disguise ever devised for a woman carrying contraband grief. The crown never once searched itself.

* * *

The report took four days.

Day one, I established the terrain. This was the easiest part, because the terrain genuinely is hostile, and the best lies are built on load-bearing truths. *The glass flats extend further than any prior survey suggested,* I wrote. *Temperatures at night fall below the tolerance of horse and man alike. Three wardens suffered frost-cracking of the skin; one lost two fingers to the glass.* All true. I had watched young Brannoc weep over his bandaged hand and pretend he wasn't weeping, which is a skill the crown teaches you along with swordwork.

Day two, the phenomenon. Here I slowed down, because this was the sentence that mattered, the sentence that would outlive me if anything did:

*No door exists.*

Three words. Well, three plus the formal padding around them, but the substance was three words: *no door exists.* I described the ridge where the refugees had gathered—I described it accurately, because why not, accuracy is free—and reported that our party found only salt formations, wind-carved, unremarkable. I noted that the "Door" as described in smuggler folklore bore striking resemblance to certain optical effects produced by moonlight on crystalline sodium deposits. I even cited a naturalist's treatise. I invented the naturalist. He had a very plausible name—Maester Corvel—and a very plausible book, and by the time anyone thought to check, either the book would exist or it wouldn't, and I would be retired or dead, and honestly the afterlife holds few terrors for a woman who has already imagined her brother's face at every tribunal she's ever attended.

Day three, the people. This was the part where my hand actually shook, and I will admit it here because I am writing this account for no one and therefore may afford honesty the way rich men afford charity: cheaply, and only in private.

*The fugitives who fled the Steppe jurisdiction number fewer than forty individuals,* I wrote, *of whom perhaps half are children or elderly. It is the assessment of this warden that they cannot have survived the crossing.* 

Forty. There were over three hundred. I had counted them from the ridge in the moonlight, their column snaking down toward a shimmer in the air that I had ordered my wardens not to look at too directly—not by command, nothing so incriminating, just a casual remark about conserving lamp oil, about how staring at mirages breeds superstition, and my second-in-command, dull loyal Tarrow, had nodded and adjusted his goggles and looked away like a good soldier.

They crossed. All of them. I held the pass open for the better part of an hour by inventing a supply inventory that required recounting. Tarrow recounted it twice. He was so thorough about it. I could have kissed him, and the fact that I occasionally think this should tell you everything about the state of my soul.

But the report said forty, and said they likely died, and recommended no further expenditure on search parties, because *the steppe offers nothing of value to the crown and much danger to its servants.*

Day four, the recommendations. Every report must have recommendations; it is how the bureaucracy digests failure without tasting it. I recommended that the Salt Steppe be formally reclassified from "contested territory harboring fugitives" to "geological curiosity, low priority." I recommended that the memory market raids be suspended pending review—a review I would chair, a review that would take years, a review whose findings would be that there was nothing left to find. And I recommended, in language of exquisite blandness, that the guild's surviving records be sealed rather than distributed, since their evidentiary value was "degraded by the guildmaster's documented falsifications."

Corvane's falsifications. Yes. Let the dead man carry some of the weight. He sold enough names to the crown to furnish a dozen pyres; he can hold up a filing cabinet in the archives of my conscience alongside my brother.

I signed it. I blotted it. I carried it upstairs to the Chancery myself, because I wanted to watch the clerk's face when he read the summary aloud to the undersecretary, and the face did not disappoint. Boredom. Pure, bureaucratic boredom. The Door was a myth, the Steppe was empty, the hunt was concluded, the file was closed.

The most dangerous thing I have ever done, and it was filed under G for Geography.

* * *

That evening I went back to my office and sat with the brand-token.

It sits on my desk, third object from the lamp, positioned—though I would deny this under oath—with its stamped face turned toward the chair, so that whoever sits there faces it. A small disc of blackened tin, the size of a coin, punched through at the top where a cord once ran. On its face, the brand-mark itself: a circle broken by a single line, the sigil they sear into gifted flesh above the collarbone. Tam's mark. They gave me the token afterward, as a courtesy, as a receipt. *Your report has been processed,* the token said, in the language of metal. *Your brother has been processed. Thank you for your service to the crown.*

He was nineteen. His crime, as far as anyone could determine before the branding, was that he could hear what people meant instead of what they said—which sounds like a poet's affliction until you learn it was literal, that he heard the true sentences underneath, and that a boy who hears underneath things cannot help telling you what's there. He told a customs officer what was underneath *his* sentences. The officer reported him. The officer got a commendation. I got a receipt.

For eleven years I kept that token in a drawer, because a drawer is where you put things you refuse to look at but refuse to lose. Then Silas Kavanagh—ledger-keeper, thief of memories, the most reluctant criminal I have ever pursued—put their hand on my wrist in an interrogation room and gave me back the thing I had paid the crown to bury: the memory of Tam's branding, whole, unedited, mine again. The smell of the iron. The sound he made, which was not screaming, which was worse—it was my name, spoken through his teeth, asking me why.

I let them escape. Or I believed I did, which amounted to the same thing on the paperwork, and paperwork is the only country I've ever governed.

Since then the token lives on the desk. In the open. Where I can see it while I write reports declaring doors mythical and steppes empty. People notice it sometimes—young wardens, clerks—and ask, and I tell them it's a case memento, an early assignment, nothing current. They nod. They assume sentimentality in an old woman the way they assume furniture in a room. Nobody asks the question that matters, which is: *Warden, why does the brand of a convicted gift-bearer sit on the desk of the crown's chief hunter like a saint's relic?*

Because it is one, is why. The first relic of the church I actually attend.

* * *

A week after the filing, Tarrow came to my office with his cap in his hands, which is what he does when he has bad news and no category for it.

"Warden," he said. "There's a... situation. With the southern traders."

"Tarrow, the southern traders are always a situation. It's practically their coat of arms."

"Yes, but—" He set a paper on my desk, next to the token, and I watched his eyes flick to it and away, the way eyes do. "This came in from a salt-factor at Weigh Station Nine. He says—well, he says there's a market forming. Out past the flats. Past where the survey says nothing can live."

"A market of what?"

"He wouldn't say by letter. Said it had to be said quiet. But the caravans are coming back heavier than they went out, and they're paying in—" he hesitated, hating the word even as he reached for it, "—in memories. Freely traded, he says. Not stolen. Traded. Like goods. Like *honest* goods."

I looked at the letter. I looked past it, out the window, south, toward a horizon that officially contained nothing at all.

"What else did your factor say?"

Tarrow shifted. "He said the traders are calling it something. A name." He swallowed. "He said they're calling it a *country*, Warden. And that they keep ledgers there."

Ledgers.

I felt the word land in my chest like a coin dropped into a dry well—long fall, small splash, echo going down and down. Somewhere beyond the reach of my report, beyond the reach of Maester Corvel and his fictional treatise, beyond the reach of every seal I had pressed into wax, Silas Kavanagh was keeping books again. Of course they were. You don't stop being a keeper of accounts because the account changes. I knew that better than anyone; I had spent thirty years keeping the crown's books, and when the entries finally shamed me, I hadn't stopped writing—I'd only changed which side of the ledger I stood on.

"They keep ledgers," I repeated.

"That's what he said." Tarrow wrung his cap. "Do you want me to open an inquiry?"

I considered him. Good, dull, loyal Tarrow, who recounts supplies twice and looks where he's told and has never once heard what people mean underneath what they say. I considered the inquiry forms, the warrants, the machinery of pursuit all polished and waiting. I considered my brother's brand shining dully under the lamp between us.

"No," I said. "File it under rumor. Unverified. Low priority."

"And if it's not rumor?"

"Then it's geography, Tarrow." I picked up my pen. "And geography is not my department."

He nodded, relieved to be given a smaller world, and left me alone with the lamp and the token and the long shadow my signature had cast across an entire nation of the disappeared.

Officially, they are dead. Officially, the door was never there. Officially, the steppe lies empty under a moon the astronomers insist has only twelve sisters.

Officially.

But I know what lives underneath what a report says. I learned it from a boy who heard underneath things, and from a thief who returned them, and from my own hand, which lied beautifully for four days straight and has not once trembled since.

Underneath, there is a country. Underneath, there are ledgers.

And underneath, on my desk, in plain sight where no one thinks to look—the truth, branded and blackened and still warm.

I dipped my pen and began, for the first time in my career, to write a private record: not a report, not a lie, just an account, entry one, of everything the crown believes never happened.

Someone, someday, should know what was underneath.
