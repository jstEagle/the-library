## 36. The New Ledger

The trouble with founding an archive, I discovered, is that everyone assumes you already know what you're doing.

I had kept ledgers for the Glass Steppe Guild for eleven years. I knew how to rule a line, how to date an entry, how to record the weight of a memory in drams and the price of it in coin and the name of the person who'd sold it in a hand so neat it looked printed. I could appraise a grief at forty paces. I knew exactly what a memory of a mother's voice was worth on a Tuesday versus a market day.

What I did not know was how to build a ledger where nothing was for sale.

"You're overcomplicating it," Vespera said. She was stretched across the bench of our new counting house—which was to say, a converted salt shed with a roof that leaked in two places she had personally mapped and named, the way cartographers do, as *Leak One* and *Leak Two*—sketching the settlement's streets by lamplight because apparently the woman could not stop drawing even now that no crown was paying her to. "A ledger is just a promise with good posture. You've made thousands of them."

"I've made thousands of *debt instruments*," I said. "That's different."

"Is it? Write down what was given. Write down who gave it. Write down that they can have it back." She didn't look up from her sketch. "The rest is ink and vanity."

The rest, as it turned out, was three months of arguments, two ruined drafts, one small fire—I maintain to this day that the lamp was faulty—and a great deal of standing in the doorway of our shed-house at odd hours watching people walk past and wondering what they would want remembered and whether anyone had ever asked them before.

Because that was the thing. That was the whole rotten heart of the old trade, if I was honest with myself, which I was trying to be more often lately, since honesty had turned out to be the only currency the new country recognized. In eleven years at the guild, not once—not once—had anyone asked a seller *do you want this back someday?* The guild bought. The guild held. The guild sold to someone else who wanted your daughter's face or your first love's laugh or your courage on a bad morning, and you walked out into the street lighter and poorer and you did not get to ask questions, because asking questions implied you were still a person with interests in the transaction, and the entire architecture of the trade was built on you not being that anymore.

Corvane hadn't invented that architecture. He'd inherited it, polished it, and eventually traded his own past away inside it until there wasn't enough man left to feel the floor give way beneath him. I'd watched it happen from behind my desk with a quill in my hand and called it bookkeeping.

So: no theft, no debt. Those were the only rules I started with. Everything else we built out from under them like a house on two stones.

* * *

The first entry was Vespera's, naturally, because she refused to let me put her anywhere but first.

"Write it plainly," she said. She sat across the desk from me—the same desk I'd hauled across the flats on a smuggler sledge, because some habits are load-bearing—and folded her hands like a woman giving testimony. "No flourish. No poetry. If this thing is going to work, it has to be boring."

"*Boring,*" I repeated.

"Boring as a boundary stone. Boring as rain." Her mouth twitched. "You can't cheat something that bores you. Corvane's ledgers were beautiful. That was half the problem. Every page was a little cathedral and nobody thought to ask what was buried under the floor."

So I wrote it plain:

*Entry the First. Given freely, this ninth day of the settling, by Vespera Anselin, sometime royal cartographer of a country she no longer names: the memory of drawing her first map, age seven, on the back of her sister's hand, in jam.*

"And the return condition?" I asked. It felt strange in my mouth, *return condition*, like a lock looking for a door.

"There isn't one. That's the point." She leaned forward. "Write down that she may have it back whenever she asks, for any reason or none, forever. And write down—" and here her voice did something complicated, the way it always did when her sister came near the surface of things, "—write down that if she never asks, that's allowed too."

I wrote it. My hand was steadier than I expected it to be. Eleven years of recording other people's losses will teach you a certain economy of stroke, and I suppose some part of that discipline survives translation.

Then, because the rules of the old world died hard in both of us, I said, "You understand I could take it instead of copying it. Right now. With a touch. You'd never know."

"I know," Vespera said. "That's why I'm going first." She held my eye, and there was no fear in it, which frightened me more than fear would have. "Someone has to show them what trust looks like written down, Silas. You can't lead with a blank page."

* * *

Word got around, the way word does in a settlement of four hundred people who all arrived with nothing and are therefore exquisitely attuned to the movement of anything valuable.

They came at dusk, mostly. There's something about dusk—it loosens the tongue, or maybe it just makes people honest about what they've been carrying all day. Odile came first after Vespera, hauling her considerable presence through our door with the expression of a woman inspecting a tavern's cellar for rats.

"So this is it," she said, turning in a slow circle. "The famous archive. Where's the vault?"

"No vault."

"The guards?"

"No guards."

"Silas." She planted herself in front of my desk and lowered her voice to the register she used when the child Neme was within earshot and the subject was delicate. "You spent eleven years guarding the most valuable hoard on the Steppe. You're telling me you're keeping people's memories in a leaky shed with a curtain for a door?"

"I'm telling you I'm keeping them in a leaky shed with a curtain for a door, and that anyone may come read the ledger, including the people whose memories are in it, including their neighbors, including you." I tapped the page. "It turns out theft requires darkness, Odile. Take that away and there's remarkably little left to guard."

She stared at me for a long moment. Then she laughed—that great rolling laugh of hers that had scared off more than one hunter's informer in the old days—and said, "You've gone mad. Good. The sane ones built the old country and look how that went." She sat down and gave me a memory of her mother teaching her to pour beer without foam, and the return clause she dictated herself, verbatim, with the gravity of a treaty: *returnable every year on her mother's birthday, whether asked for or offered.*

"That last bit's not standard," I said.

"Make it standard," said Odile. "Some people won't ask. Write it so the ledger does the asking."

I've been thinking about that ever since. About all the machinery of return we might build that doesn't wait for courage.

* * *

Not everyone came willingly, and I want to be honest about that too, because a ledger founded on honesty that flatters itself has already failed.

There was a man—Marek, a salt-cutter with forearms like mooring rope—who stood in the doorway for six evenings running before he came in. When he finally sat, he said, "I know what you are. What you were. You took memories for thieves and priced them like turnips." He laid his hands flat on the desk. "My brother's laugh. I sold it. Back in the old country, when the debt collectors had me. I don't even remember selling it, that's the hell of it. I just remember the hole."

"I remember the hole," I said quietly. "I've recorded several thousand of them."

"Can you give it back?"

"No." And I watched him start to close, the way people close, the whole body becoming a door swinging shut. "But I can write down that the hole exists. I can write down its shape and its edges and everything around it. And I can write down that if his laugh ever surfaces—in the riot-memories, in someone else's head, in whatever strange tide brings these things back—then it comes here first, and it comes to you free, and nobody in this room or any room takes a dram of payment for it."

He was quiet for a while. Outside, Leak Two performed its evening aria onto the packed earth.

"That's not an archive," he said finally. "That's a prayer."

"Maybe those stopped being different things somewhere on the road here," I said. "I missed the moment. I was busy carrying strangers' griefs."

He gave me the hole itself, in the end. Described it to me in detail—where it sat in him, what it ached against—and I entered it, hole and all, returnable the instant its opposite appeared. He shook my hand on the way out, hard enough to rearrange bones, and I understood that for men like Marek the handshake was the signature.

* * *

Neme came at moonrise, of course. The child always did prefer the hours when the thirteenth moon threw its double shadow, as if ordinary singular shadows were insufficiently interesting.

"You're building a thing that remembers on purpose," Neme said, standing in the doorway with the particular stillness children have when they've rehearsed a speech and abandoned it halfway.

"We're trying."

"It won't work forever." Neme said it without cruelty, the way the child reported weather. "Things that remember get found. Things that get found get wanted."

"I know." I set down my pen. "And yet."

"And yet." Neme nodded slowly, satisfied, as though *and yet* were the correct answer to some question the child had been asking all along. Then: "When I'm older, I'll have things worth putting in it. Things that haven't happened yet." A pause, and something flickered across the small face—there and gone, quick as a fish turning—before the usual composure resettled. "Some of them I'll probably need to warn you about first."

"Neme—"

"Goodnight, Silas." And the child was gone into the double-shadowed dark, leaving me with a cold finger drawn lightly up my spine and absolutely no idea why.

Children, in my experience, are either entirely transparent or entirely opaque, and Neme had long ago chosen opacity as a lifestyle. I filed the unease where I filed everything I couldn't solve, which is to say in the drawer marked *later*, next to the question of what exactly the freed memories of the riot had been doing all winter, rooting themselves in four hundred heads like seed grain, and the question of why the Door of Salt sometimes sang on windless nights.

Later. Everything was later. We had a country to build and it was mostly made of later.

* * *

By spring the shed had a second shelf, then a third, and Odile bullied the settlement council into funding a proper roof, on the grounds that "the man keeps the town's soul in there, least we can do is keep the rain off it," which was generous of her and also wildly overstated, and also, I noticed, not contested by a single soul in the room.

Vespera came every evening. Officially she came to draw—she was making a map of the settlement, the first map of the first unmapped country, and she treated each street and well and stubborn goat with the reverence of a woman annotating scripture. Unofficially she came because she came, and we had long since stopped pretending otherwise, though neither of us had yet found words plain enough for the thing itself. Cartographers and bookkeepers, we were both trained to precision, and it is a peculiar cowardice that afflicts precise people: we can measure anything except the obvious.

One evening she stood over my shoulder while I ruled a fresh page, and read the header aloud: "*Ledger of Memories Freely Given and Freely Returnable.*"

"It needs a shorter name," she said. "People will call it something anyway. Better you choose it than the tavern."

"What are they calling it now?"

"'The Giving House.' Odile started it. It's spreading."

"The Giving House," I repeated. It sounded like a charity and a brothel had had a very polite child together. But names aren't chosen, they're survived into, so I shrugged and wrote it on the doorframe myself, in paint, crooked, because I have many skills and sign-making is not among them.

Then Vespera said, "Put me down as witness."

"Witness?"

"On the ledger. First page. Under the founding entries." She pulled the stool around and sat facing me, and her hands were not quite steady, which for Vespera was equivalent to another person weeping openly. "Every archive needs someone outside it who saw it begin. Corvane's ledgers had no witnesses. That's how he erased himself inside them—no one could say *no, that's not how it happened, I was there.*" She met my eyes. "I was there, Silas. For all of it. Put my name where it can't be quietly removed."

So I did. Beneath the founding entries, in my best hand, the hand that had once served thieves and hunters and a man who sold his own past:

*Witnessed: Vespera Anselin, who saw the old ledgers burn and the new one begin, and who swears the difference between them is real.*

She signed it. Then she did something I didn't expect: she touched the wet ink with one fingertip and pressed the fingerprint beside her name.

"There," she said. "Now it's a map too. Of me. At this exact place and time." A pause, and the corner of her mouth lifted. "You should add yours."

"I'm the keeper. Keepers don't sign their own ledgers."

"Keepers who don't sign end up like Corvane," she said gently. "Present in every line and nowhere in the book."

I pressed my thumb beside hers. Two prints, side by side, drying brownish in the lamplight. It occurred to me that this was the closest thing to a vow either of us had managed in all these months, and that we had conducted it entirely in the language of paperwork, and that I had never in my life been happier about a bureaucratic act.

* * *

The ledger grew. I won't catalogue it—a keeper learns restraint—but some entries I'll carry whether or not they're written down:

A grandmother's recipe for flatbread, given so it could be copied out and *left open on the counter for anyone*—the first memory in history to be donated to the general public.

A soldier's terror, returned to him voluntarily by the woman who'd bought it years ago in the old country and carried it across the Door rather than leave it in Corvane's vault; she handed it back at our desk and said she'd been waiting her whole life to give something back to somebody.

Neme's memory of the crossing, which the child insisted on entering early—"so it's official before I change my mind"—with a return clause reading, in Neme's own careful letters: *returnable when I am brave enough, which will be soon.*

And once, at closing time, an entry in a hand I didn't recognize, slipped through the curtain while my back was turned. No name. Just a single line:

*Given anonymously: the smell of bread baking in a country none of us can quite picture, to everyone, forever, no return needed—we'd all like to keep it.*

I sat with that one a long while after the lamps were out. Somewhere out there was a person who had crossed into a new land and immediately tried to give it a shared past. Or perhaps it was many persons. Perhaps the riot-memories were learning to write. I've stopped ruling things out; it saves time.

* * *

Here is what I know now, keeper of the Giving House, late of the Glass Steppe Guild, formerly a man who priced grief by the dram:

The old ledger was a wound dressed as a book. This one is a scar forming as one. Scars are better. Scars mean the body decided to go on.

We are imperfect at it. People forget their return clauses. People quarrel over whose version of a shared event is accurate—Vespera referees those disputes with the patience of a saint and the ruthlessness of a surveyor. Some mornings I wake reaching for the old reflexes, the appraiser's cold arithmetic, *what is this worth, what will it fetch*, and I have to lie there a moment and remind myself: nothing, everything, not for sale, that was the whole point.

And some evenings, when the light comes in low through the curtain and falls across the shelves in gold bars, I stand in the doorway and watch neighbors come and go—reading, adding, taking back what was theirs, leaving what wasn't needed anymore—and I think: this is what the guild pretended to be. This is the lie it told about itself, except here it's true, and truth, it turns out, is mostly a matter of who's allowed to touch the book.

I was writing up the day's final entries when the knock came—not the settled knock of neighbors, but the sharp double-rap of someone arriving fast, with news.

It was one of the salt-cutters' boys, breathless, cheeks bright with cold. "Keeper," he gasped, using the title like it had always existed. "Rider at the gate. Come up the east road an hour past. Says—" he caught his breath, "—says she's from the old capital. Says she has papers. Says the kingdom's fallen, and there are people coming to the Door."

I set down my pen.

"People," I said carefully. "What kind of people?"

The boy shifted. "She wouldn't say to me. But keeper—she's wearing grey. Hunter's grey. And she says she knew you, back when. And she's asking—" he swallowed— "she's asking whether the Door still opens."

Outside, the thirteenth moon was rising, throwing its two shadows long across the salt, and far off toward the ridge, faint as a struck bell, the Door had begun, very softly, to sing.
