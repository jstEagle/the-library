## 37. Neme's Tomorrow, Revised

Here is the thing nobody tells you about seeing tomorrow: it is mostly boring.

I saw Odile burn the porridge forty-one times before she finally learned to take the pot off the fire when I coughed. Forty-one. And every time, she said the same thing—"Neme, love, why didn't you *say* something?"—and every time I had to explain, again, that saying things changes them, and then the porridge doesn't burn, and then I have to see a different tomorrow, and honestly it's exhausting being a weather vane for breakfast.

But that was Before. Before the Door. Before the crossing.

Now, when I look ahead, the tomorrows go on for so long that they make my eyes ache, like looking down a road that keeps growing while you watch it.

* * *

The settlement has no name yet, which drives Vespera quietly mad. She has drawn four maps of it—four!—and each one has a little blank space at the top where the name goes, like a mouth waiting to be fed words. She asked me once, very casually, the way adults ask children things when they've already decided the answer, "What do you think we should call it, Neme?"

And I looked ahead, the way I do, and all I could see was a hundred thousand mornings of people arguing about it, and I said, "You're going to fight about this for years," and she laughed until she had to sit down on a salt crate, which was the correct response, in my opinion.

That was a good day. I collect good days now. Before, my tomorrows were mostly bad ones with good ones hiding inside them like beans in a husk. Now it's the other way around. Mostly.

I say mostly because of the door.

* * *

It started three days after the crossing, when Silas still walked around the settlement like a man wearing someone else's coat. They'd given away everything—the grandmother, the lullaby, the whole country that used to live behind their eyes—and what was left was a person who kept reaching for shelves that weren't there anymore. I watched them stand in front of Odile's hearth one evening with an expression I recognized from the mirror of my own head: the look of someone trying to remember a dream that everyone else insists actually happened.

I touched their sleeve. I shouldn't have. Touching is how I see best, and touching someone who has just emptied themselves out is like looking into a well after they've dropped the bucket—all you get back is the echo of your own question.

But in the echo, something moved.

A tomorrow. Not mine. Not theirs either, exactly. A tomorrow that belonged to the space where Silas used to keep the Salt Steppe, and in it I saw—

Light. A door-shaped light, standing alone in flat country under a sky with too many stars. Far off. Years off. Decades, maybe. Someone walking toward it carrying something small and bright, the way you carry a coal from one fire to start another.

Then Silas pulled their sleeve back—not unkindly, but firmly, the way you close a window against a draft—and the tomorrow folded itself up and went wherever tomorrows go when nobody's watching.

I didn't tell anyone. Not even Odile. Especially not Odile, who would have held my face in her flour-dusted hands and said, "Neme, love," in the voice that means *you will do no such thing*, which is the voice that guarantees I will absolutely do the thing.

* * *

Here is what changed, though. Here is the strange arithmetic of it.

Before the crossing, when I looked ahead at our future, I saw endings everywhere. The hunters finding us. The salt flats cracking open under wardens' boots. Odile old and alone. Vespera's maps burned. Silas—sometimes I saw Silas dead on the glass, and I never told anyone about that one either, and I carried it for months like a stone in my shoe, a stone shaped exactly like a person I loved.

That tomorrow is gone now. I check sometimes, the way you check a scar. It's gone. In its place: the settlement, growing. Roofs going up along the river that runs sweet instead of salty. Fields where the freed memories took root—because that's what happened here, did you know? When the riot scattered them across the Steppe, they didn't die. They planted. People here remember things that never happened to them, and those things are true anyway, and the barley grows taller where the remembering is thickest. Vespera says that's not how agriculture works. Vespera has been wrong about several things lately and is taking it relatively well.

I look ahead and I see Odile's tavern—it will be a tavern again, of course it will, some things are laws older than crowns—I see it full of grandchildren who aren't born yet, arguing about whether the Door story is true. I see Silas's ledger on a shelf behind the bar, fat with entries in a hand that gets steadier every year. I see Vespera's map hanging above the hearth, the blank space at the top finally filled in, and I can't read what it says because names are slippery in foresight—they haven't been decided yet, so they won't hold still—but I can see that whatever it is, Odile will claim she suggested it first, and she'll be lying, and everyone will let her.

Long lives. All of them. Long and loud and stubborn.

When I understood that—the sheer *length* of what I was seeing—I had to go sit by the river for most of an afternoon. Because before, my gift was a knife. Every tomorrow it showed me was something to survive or something to dread. And now it's showing me... Tuesdays. Hundreds of thousands of Tuesdays. Ordinary time, stretching out like a road through good country.

Nobody tells you that hope can be as heavy as fear. It just weighs less per unit, so you can carry more of it.

* * *

The door came back last night.

I dream in foresight sometimes—that's the worst version of the gift, dreams don't ask permission—and in the dream I was standing on the ridge above the settlement, the ridge where the Door of Salt opened and closed, except there was no Door there anymore, just wind and moonlight and the memory of a shape. And far off—so far I only knew it was there the way you know a mountain is there, by the change in the air—another light. Door-shaped. Standing in country I have never seen, under stars arranged wrong, in a pattern that hurt my teeth to look at.

And I understood two things in the dream, the way you understand things in dreams, completely and without evidence:

One: it was a real door. Another one. There are doors the way there are rivers—one here doesn't mean there aren't others, it means water finds ways.

Two: someday it would need opening. Not today. Not soon. But someday, somebody would stand in front of that far-off light the way Silas stood in front of ours, holding a memory they were about to give away forever, and the choice would be theirs, and it would be terrible, and it would be necessary, and—

And the dream ended, because dreams are cowards.

I woke up with my heart going like a drum in a parade and lay there listening to Odile snore in the next room, snoring the way only truly safe people snore, and I thought about waking her. I got as far as sitting up.

But here's what stopped me: in the dream, when I looked at the far-off door, I wasn't afraid. I want to be honest about this, because everyone always assumes the child who sees the future must be frightened by it, and mostly they're right, but this time—this time what I felt was something I didn't have a word for until I heard Silas use it a week later, talking about the ledger.

They called it *inventory*. Looking at what you have and naming it plainly. And what I had, in the inventory of my seeing, was this: a home that lasts. People who stay. And one door, very far away, patient as salt, waiting for a century or five.

That's not a nightmare. That's a horizon.

* * *

This morning I did the thing I almost never do. I went looking for the future on purpose, wide awake, no touching, just me and the long road of it.

I found Silas at the new ledger house—they built it out of salt-wood and ship-timber the smugglers brought over, and it smells like resin and ink, and Silas has hung a sign over the door that says MEMORIES GIVEN FREELY MAY BE RETURNED, which took Vespera three weeks to letter properly because she kept redoing the F—and I sat on the step outside and looked down the years.

I saw Silas old. That's the part I want to write down properly, so it stays written, so if I ever doubt it I can come back to this page: I saw Silas Kavanagh old, with white in their hair and reading-glasses they refuse to admit they need, still writing in the ledger, still asking newcomers to tell them about the old country they can't remember, still listening the way they listen, like your story is a debt they intend to honor. Their hands shake a little. Their laugh doesn't.

I saw myself, grown, coming up the path toward the ledger house with something cupped in my hands—something bright, coal-bright, door-key bright—and I couldn't see my own face, because that's the rule, the oldest rule of my gift: I am the one blind spot in all my seeing. I can see everyone's tomorrow but my own, which I used to think was a curse and now think might be the kindest feature of the whole arrangement. Whatever I carry toward that far door, I'll find out when I get there, same as everybody.

I saw the settlement with smoke from fifty chimneys, then five hundred, then so many the valley looked like it was breathing. I saw children racing down to the river who have great-grandparents not yet born. I saw the Door of Salt's ridge, empty and quiet, visited once a year by people who leave flowers on it the way you'd leave flowers on a grave—or a birthplace; I genuinely could not tell which, and I suspect the answer is both.

And underneath all of it, far beneath, like a fish deep under ice: the other light. Waiting. Patient.

Not my problem today. That's the revelation, I think. That's what the revised tomorrow taught me. For my whole life Before, every future was *now*, every danger immediate, every vision a summons. But a horizon isn't a summons. A horizon is just the world telling you how big it is.

* * *

At midday, Silas found me on the step. They do that—find me—with an accuracy that would worry me if I didn't already know they can't see the future at all. They just pay attention. It turns out paying attention is nearly as good, and available to anybody, which seems unfair, frankly, but there it is.

"You've got your looking-face on," Silas said, sitting down beside me. Their coat still smells like the old world, cedar and ink, the last ghost of a country they gave away. "Anything I should know?"

I thought about the far door. I thought about the stone-in-my-shoe tomorrows that aren't there anymore. I thought about the old Silas with the glasses, and the me with the bright thing in my hands, decades downstream of this exact step, this exact conversation.

"Some tomorrows," I said, "go on for a really long time."

Silas nodded slowly, the way they nod when they understand more than the sentence contained. "That sounds new."

"It is." I picked at the step's edge where the salt-wood was starting to silver. "It used to be that when I looked ahead, I was looking for the ending. Now I have to look *past* all the living to find anything that matters."

"And?"

"And there's a door," I said. "Far off. Nobody needs to worry about it yet. But somebody might, someday. Somebody who hasn't been born."

Silas was quiet for a while. Down the street, Odile was yelling at two boys who'd stolen honeycomb, and Vespera was arguing with her map, and the settlement made its ordinary noise, the sound of a place that intends to continue.

"Then we'd better build them a world worth opening doors into," Silas said finally. "That's the whole job, isn't it? Leave the next key-carrier something worth trading a memory for."

And that's when I knew I'd tell them eventually—maybe not today, maybe not for years, but sometime before the far door mattered, I'd sit Silas down and give them the whole inventory, horizons and all. Because that's the difference between the old ledgers and the new one. In the old country, knowing was theft. Here, knowing is a gift you get to decide when to give.

I have seen when. I'm not telling.

Some tomorrows should get to arrive as surprises—even mine. Especially mine.

I went in to help with the ledger, and behind us the day came on like a promise kept, and far away, past the edge of every map Vespera will ever draw, the other door stood open a crack in the dark, keeping its light warm for whoever comes next.

It has time.

We all do, now.
