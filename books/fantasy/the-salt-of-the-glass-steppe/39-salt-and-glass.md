## 39. Salt and Glass

The ridge above the settlement has no name yet. I have decided that this is a feature, not a flaw.

Vespera would disagree. She names things the way other people breathe—automatically, compulsively, with occasional sighs of dissatisfaction. In the year since we crossed, she has christened three rivers, two hills, a rock formation she insists looks like a sleeping whale, and one unfortunate goat that now answers to Chancellor Bristlewick whether it likes it or not. The ridge remains unnamed because every time she raises the subject, I tell her I'm still thinking about it, which is true in the sense that all lies are technically composed of true words.

Tonight I climbed up here alone, which is also becoming a habit. Not to get away from her—I want to be clear about that, because Vespera reads my absences the way she used to read coastlines, and she always assumes the worst, by which I mean she assumes I'm brooding, and she's usually right, but tonight I am not brooding. Tonight I am doing something rarer.

I am waiting for moonrise without dread.

There was a time—not so long ago, though it feels like another country, possibly because it literally was—when the sight of the thirteenth moon clearing the horizon made my chest tighten like a fist closing around a ledger page. Moonrise meant counting. Moonrise meant arithmetic of the cruelest kind: days remaining, names remaining, turns of the sky remaining before the Door sealed for another century and everyone I had failed stayed failed on the wrong side of history.

Now the moon comes up over the glass flats and it's just... a moon. Doubled, still, in that strange way this sky has learned to do—the pale body and its fainter twin rising half a breath behind it, like an echo that never quite caught up. Neme says the doubling is getting softer. Neme says a lot of things, most of them about next Tuesday, and most of those turn out to be right, which is deeply unfair in a child who also refuses to eat anything green.

I sat down on the salt-crusted stone and did what I came to do. I took off my gloves.

Yes, gloves. Old habits. A lifetime of keeping my hands covered, of touching nothing bare-skinned, of shaking hands through wool and pretending to be fastidious rather than terrified. Out here nobody needs the pretense anymore. But some nights, when I climb, I wear them anyway, just so that taking them off means something. A small ceremony. Odile would approve; she's been teaching us all her smugglers' rites, and the first rule of every one of them is that you mark the threshold or the crossing doesn't count.

Bare-handed, I reached out and touched the air.

This sounds like madness. It sounded like madness when I first did it, six months ago, standing on this same ridge after a day of hauling foundation stones, too tired to be sensible. But the wind off the salt flats carries more than mineral sting. It carries memory. Loose memory, unowned memory, the freed recollections from the market riot and the burned ledgers and the Door itself, drifting through this country like pollen, looking for somewhere to root.

And lately—this is the part I haven't told anyone, not even Vespera—some of it drifts toward me.

I felt it tonight, faint as a fingerprint on fog. A memory arriving on the wind, settling against my skin where the salt air touched it, gentle as a moth landing. Not mine. Never mine, not anymore; I gave away everything that was mine, and the giving took. What came to me instead was something older and stranger: a woman's hands kneading bread on a table I have never seen, in a kitchen lit by a window I have never looked through, while someone sang a lullaby I only half remember learning.

Half-remembering is the whole point. That's the bargain I made at the Door, the price I paid for four hundred souls to cross: every memory of the Salt Steppe, surrendered freely, forever. My grandmother's face. The lullaby's melody. The origin of my own name—which, I've been told, means "keeper of accounts," which is either cosmic irony or cosmic bookkeeping, and with my luck probably both.

I don't miss these things, exactly. You can't miss what you can't hold. What I feel instead is a kind of shaped absence, a silhouette cut out of me in the exact outline of a homeland, and into that silhouette, slowly, the wind has begun to blow.

Not returning what I lost. Returning something new. The country's own memories, offered back—not as restitution but as introduction. As if the land itself had noticed the hollow in me and said, well, since you're here anyway.

The bread-kneading memory faded, as they do, dissolving back into the salt wind like sugar into warm water. I sat with the residue of it: flour-dusted knuckles, the smell of yeast and woodsmoke, a feeling of ordinary, unremarkable safety. Someone's Tuesday evening, a hundred years dead, handed to me like a gift left on a doorstep.

*Thank you,* I thought, not sure to whom.

And then—and I swear this on whatever ledger the dead keep—the wind shifted, and I felt something answer. Not words. Memory doesn't work in words when it moves like this. But a sensation, unmistakable: the way a room feels when someone who was angry at you decides, quietly, without announcement, to stop being angry. A loosening. An exhale across a hundred miles of glass.

If I believed the old guildmasters, I'd say I imagined it. If I believed Corvane—and I don't, not anymore, not since we buried him under the white poplars with his restored memories and his ruined name and, at Odile's stubborn insistence, a decent marker—I'd say forgiveness was a currency and I hadn't paid enough. But out here I'm learning to believe in simpler arithmetic. Something like: you opened the Door, and the country walked through it, and now you live inside each other. Debts dissolve in shared habitation. Or maybe they were never debts at all. Maybe they were just weight, and weight can be set down.

The doubled moon cleared the last of the haze. Below me, the settlement lit itself window by window—lamplight in Odile's tavern, which is less a tavern than a town hall that serves ale to disguise its ambitions; lamplight in the schoolroom where Neme teaches the little ones letters and, unofficially, tomorrow; lamplight in the long low building Vespera and I share, where the New Ledger sits open on its stand, waiting for morning's entries.

I could see figures moving down there. Small, indistinct, human. Someone was arguing cheerfully outside the smithy—Odile's voice, carrying the way it always does, like she's addressing a crowd even when the crowd is one apprentice. Somewhere a fiddle had started up, badly. The sound drifted up the ridge and broke apart in the salt wind, and I found, to my considerable surprise, that my eyes were wet, and that this didn't feel like grief.

It felt like interest accruing. The good kind. The kind you never have to repay.

I put my gloves back on—no. I didn't. I stuffed them in my coat pocket, where they'll stay, gathering salt dust, until they disintegrate into the general inventory of useless relics. Let the record show: Silas Kavanagh, keeper of ledgers, lifter of memories, former ghost of the Glass Steppe Guild, walking home bare-handed in full view of anyone who cares to look.

Let the record further show that it took me forty chapters' worth of ruin to manage it.

* * *

The path down is better than it used to be. Vespera surveyed it herself, naturally, and complained the entire time about the grade, the drainage, and the fact that I kept calling it "the path" when clearly it wanted to be called something dignified. I told her paths earn their names the same way people do. She threw a pebble at me. We have a very mature relationship.

Halfway down, I passed the marker tree—the gnarled salt-cedar where the refugees hang their tokens. When someone arrives in the new country, they tie a scrap of cloth to its branches, dyed with whatever they had: beet juice, ash-water, crushed brick. Four hundred scraps now, maybe more. They flutter in the evening wind like a flock of small, patient birds. Each one says the same thing in a different color: *I was erased once. I decline to repeat the experience.*

Tonight there was a new scrap, bright madder-red, tied high where children couldn't reach it. Fresh. Someone had arrived today that I didn't know about, which meant the smuggler routes were running again, which meant word was spreading beyond our borders despite Ilsette's official report declaring this entire region a myth. Bless that terrible, magnificent woman. She filed her lie with the crown eighteen months ago—*the Door of Salt does not exist; the Steppe is empty; recommend closing the file*—and her lie has held like mortar ever since. Hunters turned historians come sometimes to the far side of the flats, poking at salt formations and writing disappointed papers. We wave. They wave back, confused. It's become a sort of national pastime.

I stood under the cedar a while, watching the red scrap dance. I don't ask newcomers their stories anymore. They give them freely, in their own time, usually over Odile's stew, usually crying, always forgiven in advance. That's the house rule. Odile posted it above the hearth in letters she burned in herself with a poker, because paint is for people with patience:

**ALL DEBTS REMEMBERED. NONE COLLECTED.**

She stole it from my ledger, obviously. First entry in the New Ledger, the night we founded it. I've made peace with being quoted. It's the only kind of theft practiced here, and we tax it heavily in wine.

* * *

The lights grew as I descended, and the smells came next—woodsmoke, bread, the iron tang of the smithy, and underneath it all the salt-and-glass scent of the steppe itself, which I am told is distinctive and which I will take entirely on faith, since my own nose has no baseline to compare it to. This is the texture of my life now: everything verified secondhand. Vespera tells me what the lullaby sounds like when she hums it. Odile tells me what my grandmother's recipes taste like, having known her longer than I can remember knowing her. Neme tells me, occasionally and with unbearable gentleness, things I did before the crossing—"You laughed at your own joke before you finished telling it, even then"—building me back up out of borrowed testimony, one anecdote at a time.

I used to think this would torment me. Losing the archive of myself seemed like the crueler half of the sacrifice; opening the Door was duty, but losing Gran, losing the Steppe of my childhood, that felt like the price. And for a while it did ache, the way a missing tooth aches, tongue returning again and again to the gap.

But here is what nobody warns you about surrendering yourself: other people pick up the pieces, and they assemble them with love, and the result is a self that fits better than the original. Because the original was built in secret, in a ledger room, alone, gloved, invisible. The reconstruction is built in company. Patchwork, yes. Imperfect, certainly. Mine?

Well. That's the interesting question, isn't it. And the answer, I've decided, is: increasingly.

The fiddle player had improved slightly by the time I reached the tavern square—or possibly I had lowered my standards, which is the more economical explanation. A knot of children scattered from the well as I passed, shrieking something about a game whose rules I will never understand and whose existence I thoroughly endorse. One of them, a small girl with flour on her face—bread-memory girl, I thought absurdly, though the timing was coincidence, probably—ran up and grabbed my bare hand without hesitation, without flinching, without any of the fear that would once have made such contact a catastrophe.

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened. Taking is a choice; I choose not to take. Giving is a choice; I give when invited. The child just wanted to show me a beetle. It was an excellent beetle. I said so with complete sincerity, and she ran off satisfied, and I stood in the square holding a hand that had touched and taken nothing, and felt the strangest thing of all this strange year:

Ordinary. Gloriously, unremarkably ordinary.

"Silas." Vespera's voice, from the tavern steps, where she stood with two cups of Odile's terrible mullet wine and the particular expression she reserves for catching me being sentimental in public. Her hair was down, which it only is after long days at the drafting table, and there was ink on her jaw, and she was, as ever, as always, as permanently, the most maplike person I have ever loved—that is to say, precise at every scale, and full of territories I'll spend a lifetime charting. "You were on the ridge."

"I was."

"At moonrise."

"I was."

"Alone."

"Vespera. Are you interrogating me or joining me?"

She handed me a cup. "Both. Interrogation first: what did the wind say?"

I looked at her for a long moment. She knows about the wind-memories—she knows everything, that's the arrangement, that's the whole architecture of us, no more secrets held behind guildmaster doors. But she doesn't know they've changed. She thinks they still arrive the way they did at first: fragments, echoes, the country talking to itself through me.

"They said," I began, and stopped, because some entries deserve care. "They said the bread is ready."

Her eyebrows performed a small, skeptical cartography. "The wind speaks in baking metaphors now."

"The wind," I said, "spoke in *baking*. Kneading. A kitchen. Someone's ordinary evening, given freely." I turned the cup in my bare hands. "And then something answered. Ves, I felt it forgive me. Or thank me. Or—both. The distinction stopped mattering somewhere on the ridge."

She was quiet. Then she set down her cup, took my bare hand in hers—deliberately, ceremonially, the way we trade memories now, gift for gift, never theft—and pressed her palm flat against mine. And I let her in, and she let me see what she chose to give: the ridge from below, seen tonight, the doubled moon and a small figure sitting on the crest with its hands open to the dark, and beneath the image, her feeling about it, which was not worry.

It was pride. Fierce, unhidden, cartographer's pride, the kind she used to waste on crowns.

"You looked like the country," she said aloud, when the touch ended. "Sitting up there. Like you belonged to it and it to you. I've drawn a thousand borders, Silas. I know what belonging looks like. It looks like that."

"The country and I have an arrangement," I said. "It lends me Tuesdays; I lend it my Tuesdays. Very equitable. You'd approve of the terms."

"Terrible terms. No witnesses." She picked up her cup and nodded toward the tavern door, where light and fiddle music and the smell of burning cedar spilled out onto the stones. "Come inside, keeper of accounts. There's stew, there's wine, there's a smuggler from the western routes who swears he saw lights on the far horizon last week—green ones, moving—and Odile wants you to write it down before he drinks himself into unreliability."

"Green lights," I repeated. "Moving."

"Mm. Probably marsh gas."

"On the salt flats."

"Extremely haunted marsh gas." Her mouth curved. "Or something else. Something with a door in it, maybe. Farther off. Waiting."

I looked toward the west then, past the tavern roofs, past the token-tree, to where the flats ran out into darkness and the doubled moon was climbing. Somewhere out there, the horizon held its secret. Somewhere out there, Neme's long-view foresight pointed at a shape none of the rest of us could see yet—one more door, the child says, far off, that may someday need opening. And whoever opens it, I suspect, will have to pay in the same coin I paid. Will have to walk through lighter than they arrived.

But that is a ledger for another day. Tomorrow's entry, perhaps, or next year's. Tonight the wind smelled of salt and bread, the lights below were warm, my hands were bare, and the woman beside me had ink on her jaw and futures in her eyes.

"All right," I said. "One entry. Then the stew."

"One entry," she agreed. "Then everything else."

We went in together, out of the salt wind, into the light.
