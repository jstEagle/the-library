## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The broth was the color of a storm about to decide.

Xanthe Fairweather lifted the silver spoon — tasters' spoons were always silver, though no one in the kitchens could tell her why, only that the king insisted and the king's insistence was older than anyone could remember — and held it to the lamplight. Beneath the surface of the soup, threads of saffron curled like something drowning. The smell was good. Leek, marrow, a whisper of thyme from the last of the autumn bundles. Somewhere under all of it, she knew, was hemlock extract, steeped for six hours by a man who had been paid more for one vial than Bram earned in a season.

She sipped.

Warmth. Salt. The faint sweetness of leek cooked past its shyness. And nothing else. No bitterness climbing the back of her throat, no numbness spreading across her tongue like frost on a window, no sudden intimacy with her own heartbeat. Just soup. She swallowed, set down the spoon, and waited the full hundred count, because waiting was the ritual and rituals were what kept the valley safe.

Bram counted aloud from the hearth, where he was turning a joint of lamb with one hand and holding a pipe he wasn't allowed to smoke indoors with the other. "Sixty. Sixty-one. You know, most people would use that hundred heartbeats to think profound thoughts. I'd wager you're thinking about whether we've got enough barley for Thursday."

"I'm thinking we don't."

"See? Profound." He grinned at her around the unlit pipe. Bram had a face like a dropped loaf — broad, misshapen, warm where it landed — and forearms that could crack walnuts by reputation alone. Twenty years he'd run the royal kitchens of Salt Valley, and twenty years he'd been the closest thing Xanthe had to family. "Well? Is His Majesty going to die horribly?"

"No."

"Pity. I'd have to invent a new gravy." He came over, took the spoon from her, and tasted the broth himself — a scandalous habit he performed daily with the defiance of a man daring the universe to notice. He made a face. "Too much thyme?"

"There's no such thing."

"That's why you're the taster and I'm merely a genius." He ladled out a bowl for himself, then paused, spoon hovering, and looked at her the way he sometimes did when the kitchens were quiet — that long, sideways look, as if checking whether she were still there. "You didn't flinch again. The hemlock batch. Cook's apprentice swears this one's strong enough to drop a horse."

"It's fine."

"You're fine," he corrected gently. "The broth is fine. There's a difference, girl, and one day I'd like you to know which one you are."

Xanthe said nothing. She took up the tray instead, checked the bowl for chips, straightened the napkin twice. This was the shape of her days: taste, wait, carry, return. Taste, wait, carry, return. The kitchens of Salt Valley Keep ran on routine the way the mills ran on the river, and she was one more wheel in the works, turning smoothly, going nowhere.

* * *

Salt Valley earned its name honestly. The cliffs on either side wept white mineral into the river until everything downstream tasted faintly of tears, and the terraced fields grew barley and sorrel and nothing else, because nothing else would tolerate the soil. Travelers who came through — and few did; the valley sat in a fold of hills that maps tended to forget — remarked on two things. First, that the air felt strange, clean in a way that left a ringing silence behind it, like a room after music stops. Second, that the people were remarkably healthy and remarkably dull, and never seemed to notice the contradiction.

Xanthe had noticed the strangeness exactly once, when she was nine, and had never managed to notice it again since. It was like trying to look at the space between two stars.

What everyone noticed, eventually, was Xanthe herself. The king's household went through poison-tasters the way other households went through candles. Three had died in Xanthe's memory, carried out feet-first with their lips gray and their families paid off in land. Then came Xanthe, a scrawny orphan girl taken in at seven from — no one ever quite established where; the records said foundling, the matron said trouble, Bram said *mine now, and that's the end of it* — and she had not so much as hiccupped in thirteen years. Nightshade, arsenic, distilled crowfoot, a truly ambitious attempt involving powdered glass and elderberry wine: she drank them all down and reported on flavor like a woman reviewing jam.

The king called her his Salt Miracle and liked to show her off at feasts. The court physicians called her an anomaly and wanted to open her up to find out why. Bram called her lucky and refused, loudly and often, to let any physician within arm's reach of her. Xanthe called herself nothing at all. Names required a self to hang them on, and some mornings, rinsing bowls before dawn, she felt about as substantial as steam.

She carried the broth up through the keep's back stairs, past guards who nodded to her without seeing her, into the antechamber where the king took his supper alone when the queen's melancholy kept her abed. Aldric III was not a grand figure close-up. He was a tired man in his fifties with ink stains on his fingers and a habit of talking to his food.

"Fairweather." He looked up as she set the bowl before him. "Still alive, then."

"The broth agrees with me, Your Majesty."

"The broth disagrees with everyone else." He broke bread and didn't eat it. "Do you know there are kingdoms where they've done away with tasters entirely? Some clever charm, I'm told. A spell woven into the plate itself, turns poison to water." He said the word *spell* the way other men said *plague*. "Not here, of course. Nothing works here. That's rather the point of us."

"Yes, sire."

He waved her off, already bent over a dispatch, and she descended the stairs feeling the familiar small ache she had no better name for. *Nothing works here.* Everyone said it proudly, the way you'd praise a fortress wall. Magic couldn't cross into the valley. Enchantments died at the salt line like moths against a lamp. The whole country was a room with the doors bolted, and everyone inside agreed it was cozy.

Xanthe had begun, lately, to wonder about the doors.

* * *

The evening kitchen was Bram's kingdom at its best: copper pots catching firelight, apprentices chopping in rhythm, the great spits turning with a groan like old men getting out of chairs. Xanthe worked through the tasting queue without thinking — the pheasant pie (arsenic, subtle, in the glaze), the honeyed figs (clean tonight, but she checked twice), the spiced wine (crowfoot again; whoever the poisoner was, they lacked imagination). Each verdict delivered in the same flat voice. Safe. Safe. Unsafe, remake it.

"You do that thing," Bram said, appearing beside her with a paring knife and half a sack of apples. "When you taste. Did you know?"

"What thing?"

"Nothing. Your face does nothing. Most folk wince even at pepper. You sit there like a well." He peeled a long unbroken spiral of skin, a trick he'd shown her a dozen times and she'd never mastered. "I knew a sailor once — well, I say *knew*, he drank most of my profits — who'd sailed past the valley's edge. Said once you're over the hills, the world gets *loud*. Colored lights growing out of the ground. Rivers that sing. Folk who could light fires with a word." He snorted. "Liar, obviously. But a good story."

"Was it a good story, though?" Xanthe heard her own voice come out too quick, too hungry, and covered it by reaching for the next spoon.

Bram noticed. Bram always noticed. "It was a story," he said mildly, "about a lot of places you'll never see if you keep standing in front of that wine."

"I'm the king's taster. I can't leave."

"You can't leave," Bram agreed, "or you won't leave. One of those. Hard to tell the difference from inside, I expect." He handed her the peeled apple like a peace offering. "Thirteen years, girl. Thirteen years tasting other people's supper and swallowing your own life along with the poison. When's the last time you walked past the market well? Past the mill bridge? When's the last time you saw anything you hadn't already seen a thousand times?"

"I see you every day and haven't gotten bored."

"Flatterer." But he laughed, and the laugh loosened something in her chest, the way it always did, and for a moment the kitchen was enough — the warmth, the noise, the apple sweet and sharp on her tongue, the one flavor in Salt Valley she never tired of because Bram bought them from a cart and always picked the bruised ones first so she'd get the good ones.

Then the door banged open and a page stumbled in, out of breath, shouting for the guard captain, saying there was a man collapsed at the market well, bleeding, strangers coming down the hill road behind him, armed men in dark livery asking questions — and the kitchen's warmth broke apart like a dropped plate.

Bram was already moving, barking orders. Xanthe stood with the apple still in her hand, listening to the words *strangers* and *armed* and *questions*, feeling something wake in her that she hadn't known was asleep.

Later — much later, when everything had changed — she would understand that the restlessness had never been idle. It had been waiting. Something on the far side of the salt line had finally sent for her, and it had chosen, of all messengers, a dying man at a well.

But that evening she only set down the apple, untied her apron, and followed the crowd toward the market square, telling herself she was merely curious, merely going to look, merely doing what any neighbor would do.

The well stood in the last light like a black ring on pale stone, and beside it, in a pool that was very red and spreading, lay a stranger in a travel-stained coat — and even across the square, even before she reached him, Xanthe felt his eyes find hers with terrible precision, as if he had crossed a hundred miles of country that did not exist for the sole purpose of looking at her face.
