## 2. A Stranger at the Salt Line

Market day smelled of onions, wet wool, and the particular mineral tang that rose off the salt flats when the wind turned east — a smell Xanthe had breathed every day of her twenty-two years without ever once tasting it. She carried two baskets of Bram's kitchen leavings to the midden by the well, which was the excuse she always used, and the truth of it was that market day was the one hour in seven when the palace let her stand among people who didn't need anything from her but her tongue.

The well stood at the center of the square like an old blind prophet, its rope grooved pale by a hundred years of hands. Farmers argued over the price of barley. A fishwife slapped a trout down on the scales. Children chased each other around the trough with sticks for swords. Ordinary morning noise, ordinary morning light.

Then the crowd went quiet the way water goes still before something rises.

He came out of the eastern lane at a stumbling run, and even from across the square Xanthe could see he was wrong for Salt Valley. His cloak had been fine once — deep green, silver-threaded at the hem — and now hung in scorched ribbons. He left prints on the packed earth: boot, boot, smear. Boot, boot, smear. The smear widening as his legs forgot their argument with gravity.

"Someone help him," the fishwife said, half-rising.

Nobody moved. In the valley you learned early that strangers were weather — they passed through or they didn't — and that reaching toward them could get your hand struck. But Xanthe was already moving, baskets forgotten, because she had spent her whole life tasting other people's dangers and here, at last, was a danger she could see with her own eyes.

She caught him three steps from the well.

He was lighter than he should have been, the way men get when the body has started spending itself faster than it can be fed. Blood had soaked through his side in a wide dark bloom, and his breath came in short torn pieces, like cloth being ripped on purpose. Up close she saw that the scorch marks on his cloak weren't fire at all. They were *absences* — places where the fabric simply stopped existing, edges smooth as cut glass.

"Water," she said, and hauled him to the well's lip, and got a cupful into him. Half of it came back up pink.

His hand shot out and closed on her wrist with a strength that startled a cry out of her. His eyes were the color of tea gone cold, and they were not confused, and they were not afraid. They were *searching*. Moving over her face the way a man reads a letter he has crossed a country to receive.

"You," he said. "You're—"

"I'm nobody," she said, the valley's oldest prayer. "Hold still, I'll fetch—"

"The door." His grip tightened. Blood ran from the corner of his mouth and he did not trouble to wipe it. "*The door remembers you.*"

"I don't know what you mean."

"No." Something like grief crossed his face, or maybe relief. "No, you wouldn't. That was the whole point of you."

He pressed something into her hands then — folded, small, heavy paper that crackled oddly under her fingers, like leaf-skeleton or old wing. She tried to give it back. Her hands would not obey. They folded the thing small and slid it into the pocket of her apron with a speed and certainty that belonged to someone else entirely.

"What is this? Who are you?"

"Tell them nothing," he said, and now he was looking past her shoulder, at the mouths of the lanes, and his voice dropped to a thread. "They can't make me talk. But you — you they can hurt. Remember that. Whatever they ask, whatever they promise — *you taste everything and feel nothing*, girl. Don't spend that gift on their questions."

"How do you know—" 

But the light had already gone out of his face, not all at once but the way a lamp gutters, twice, three times, and then the well rope creaked in the wind and the market noise came back all around her like water closing over a stone, and the man in the ruined green cloak was dead against the well stones with his cold-tea eyes fixed on the middle distance where, presumably, doors remembered people.

Xanthe knelt there holding a dead stranger's wrist and understood, with terrible clarity, that she had exactly as long as it took a rider to cross the salt causeway.

* * *

The justiciars came in eleven minutes.

She counted. She couldn't help counting; it was the taster in her, the part that measured drops and grains. Eleven minutes from the moment the first runner boy bolted for the garrison, and then the square filled with grey cloaks and the particular silence that follows the king's law the way shadow follows a cart.

There were six of them, and then a seventh, and the seventh was Corvane.

She knew him only by reputation and by one glimpse across a courtyard two winters past — chief justiciar, the king's own hound, a man said to have closed more cases than he'd opened graves, though the difference was disputed. He was tall and dry-looking, with a grey-streaked beard trimmed close and eyes that did the arithmetic of a room before his feet finished crossing it. He looked at the dead courier for a long moment. Then he looked at the crowd, and the crowd looked at its shoes.

"You found him?" he asked, and it took Xanthe a heartbeat to realize the question was aimed at her, because everyone else had taken a simultaneous step backward, offering her up like a tithe.

"Yes, sir. I tried to give him water."

"Did he say anything?"

"He said—" Her heart performed a strange maneuver, dropping and hammering at once. "He said 'water.' Then he died."

Corvane studied her. Not her face — her *hands*, first, as if hands told truer stories. Then her face. And something moved behind his eyes that she would spend the rest of the day trying to name. Recognition was too strong a word. It was smaller than that, colder: the look of a man who finds, in a drawer he thought he knew completely, a key he doesn't remember owning.

"Your name?"

"Xanthe Fairweather. I'm the king's taster."

"Taster." He weighed the word. "And how does a taster come to be kneeling at a well on market day?"

"Bram's baskets needed carrying, sir. Kitchen scraps to the midden."

"Mm." He crouched beside the body with a care that surprised her — no disgust, no impatience, the gentleness of a man who had done this many times and refused to let it become nothing. He turned the dead man's hands over. Looked at the scorched-absent places on the cloak. Ran one finger along an edge so smooth it must have shaved the whorl of his fingerprint clean.

"Search the lanes," he said quietly, without rising. "House to house. Anyone seen him come in. Anyone he spoke to. Anyone who left the square since the bell." A pause. "Anyone carrying paper."

*Paper.* The word landed in Xanthe's chest like a swallowed coal.

They searched. She watched them do it — watched two justiciars work the crowd with practiced courtesy, turning out a grain merchant's satchel, unrolling a seamstress's ribbon-bag, making a farmer empty his boots onto the cobbles while his neighbors pretended not to watch. No one protested. This, too, was valley weather; you stood in it until it passed.

And all the while the map lay against her stomach, folded small, heavier than paper had any right to be. She could feel its edges through the apron linen. She became acutely aware of her own breathing, of the necessity of appearing bored — bored, above all things, because boredom was the one emotion the justiciars never arrested. She picked up her baskets. She carried them to the midden. She dumped Bram's onion skins with hands that did not shake, because her hands never shook, because her hands were famous for it, and for the first time in her life the numbness felt less like armor than like a debt coming due.

When she came back, they were loading the body onto a cart. Corvane stood apart, speaking low to a lieutenant, and though she caught none of the words she caught the shape of them — *border, causeway, seal the passes* — and then his gaze lifted and snagged on hers again across the square, and held, one second, two, three.

He knows something, she thought. He doesn't know *what* he knows. But he'll keep pulling the thread until it gives.

"Fairweather," he called, and her whole body went to ice and somehow her feet walked her over anyway. "One question more." He came closer, lowered his voice beneath the crowd's hearing. "In the kitchens. Have you ever tasted anything that didn't frighten you?"

"Sir?"

"A poison you couldn't name. A flavor with no origin. Something that shouldn't have been in the valley at all." His dry eyes didn't blink. "Think carefully. Your answer matters more than you know."

Every drop of blood in her body said: *tell him nothing.* But lying to a justiciar was a skill she didn't have, and he would hear the lie the way she heard bitterness in broth — instantly, totally, in the root of the tongue. So she gave him the truth bent sideways, the way Bram bent pastry.

"No, sir. Everything in the valley tastes of exactly what it is. That's rather the point of us."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. Agreement, maybe. Or mourning.

"Yes," he said. "That's rather the point of you." He stepped back, dismissed her with a nod, and turned to his men — but as she walked away she heard him murmur to the lieutenant, almost too soft to catch:

"...ran the border posts myself, before the maps were corrected. I know a crossing-wound when I see one. Someone opened a road today that has been shut for twenty years. Find me the key."

* * *

That night she did not sleep.

She sat on her narrow bed above the washhouse with a single candle lit and the map open on her knees at last, and it was worse and better than anything she had imagined. Not a map of anywhere she knew. Nine lines like rivers — or roads, or roots — converging from nine directions on a single point, drawn in ink that shifted color when she tilted it: brown, then blue, then a black so deep it seemed to drink the candlelight. Around the convergence point, someone had written words in no alphabet she recognized, except that when she looked at them sideways, sleepless, tears standing in her eyes without any reason she could defend, they almost resolved into meaning. Almost. Like a word on the tip of the tongue.

*The door remembers you.*

She should burn it. Every sensible voice she owned said so in chorus. The safe thing. The valley thing. Feed it to the kitchen fire tomorrow between the bread and the roasts, and go back to sipping poisons and feeling nothing, and live to sixty the way everyone in Salt Valley lived to sixty: comfortably, completely, and never once having been anywhere at all.

She thought about the justiciars going house to house. About Corvane's finger tracing that impossible smooth edge. *Find me the key.*

She thought about the dead man's eyes, searching her face like a letter finally delivered.

And underneath the fear — beneath it, feeding it, indistinguishable from it — was a feeling she had no name for yet, because she had never needed one before. It was the feeling of a tongue pressed to a tooth with a hole in it. You know you shouldn't. You cannot stop.

Twenty-two years of tasting everything and feeling nothing, and tonight, alone, with a dead man's country unfolded on her knees, Xanthe Fairweather felt more than she had ever felt in her life, and nearly all of it was hunger.

Outside, far off toward the garrison, a bell began to ring — not the hour bell, not the alarm. The third bell. The one Bram called the *knock-and-search*, and which had never once been rung in her lifetime.

Boots in the lane below. Torchlight climbing her wall.

She folded the map along its old creases, small, smaller, palm-sized, and looked around the little room that was the whole world she'd ever owned — and understood that in about thirty seconds, one of those two things would have to end.
