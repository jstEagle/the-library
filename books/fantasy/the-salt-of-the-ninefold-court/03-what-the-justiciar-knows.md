## 3. What the Justiciar Knows

The broth had been laced with nightlock, Corvane learned in the first quarter hour. The taster girl had sipped it and lived.

He sat in the kitchen's cold corner while the staff of the Salt Valley royal house filed past him one by one — scullions, spit-boys, a pastry woman with flour still ghosting her knuckles — and he asked each the same questions in the same flat voice, and received the same frightened nothing in return. No stranger seen. No stranger remembered. A courier might have passed through the market that morning; couriers always did. No, none had lingered. No, no one had spoken to him. No, no one knew anything about anything, and wasn't it terrible about the poor man at the well.

The poor man at the well. Dead before the king's own physicians reached him, dead of wounds that told Corvane more than any witness could: three cuts, delivered by someone who wanted him to talk first and bleed out second, and a fourth across the throat to make certain the talking was finished. His boots were good leather worn through at the heel from long roads. His cloak had been brushed clean of road dust, which meant he'd expected scrutiny. Which meant he'd known who would come for him.

And his hands, when they'd turned the body over, were empty. Whatever he'd carried had already found new pockets.

Corvane let the pastry woman go and studied the kitchen.

It was not like other royal kitchens. He had stood in the steam and clamor of the palace kitchens in Aurelmark, where forty cooks shouted over forty fires and the air was thick enough to carve. Here there were six fires, nine cooks, and a silence that sat over everything like snow. The valley did its work quietly. Even the knives seemed muffled.

That was the enchantment, of course. Everyone said so. The Salt Valley kept magic out the way a dyke keeps out the sea, and inside the dyke the world ran on nothing but muscle and fire and salt. Corvane had read the histories. There had been a country once — the name changed depending on which banned chronicle you read, Ninefold Court, Court of Rivers, the Country Under the Stars — and the valley had been drawn around it like a noose, or perhaps like a bandage. The maps in the royal library showed only white space where it had been, with a single word stamped across the emptiness in the old king's hand: *ERASED.*

Corvane had served at a border once that looked very much like that white space.

He did not think of it often. He had trained himself not to, the way a man trains himself not to touch a burn. But kitchens smelled of woodsmoke and rendered fat, and the border post had smelled of those things too, and so for a moment, sitting in the cold corner of the Salt Valley kitchen, he was twenty-eight years old again and stationed at a checkpoint that appeared on no chart, watching a line of refugees walk out of a fog that should not have existed, and every one of them was weeping, and every one of them had forgotten why.

They had come through carrying nothing. That was the order's genius — the erasure took their luggage, their letters, their names if the fog was feeling thorough. Corvane's commanding officer had called them *the unburdened* and had them processed like livestock. Corvane had signed the ledgers. He had been very good at signing the ledgers. It had taken him eleven years and a dead friend to understand what the ledgers actually recorded, and by then the border itself had been wiped from the maps, and the men who'd served there had been scattered across the kingdom like seed corn, promoted just enough to keep them loyal and far enough apart to keep them silent.

He had never spoken of it. He suspected he was one of maybe thirty men alive who could.

"Ser?" The under-steward hovered at his elbow. "The kitchen staff is accounted for. All present, all local-born."

"And the taster?"

"Faireweather. Xanthe Fairweather." The steward consulted his list unnecessarily; Corvane could hear the pride underneath the formality. "Twenty years in service. Never lost the king so much as an afternoon to illness. She tasted this morning's broth herself, as she does every morning."

"Send her to me."

* * *

She came when called without hurry and without fear, which was itself unusual. Most people who entered the room where Corvane sat arranged themselves into smaller versions of themselves — shoulders curled, eyes down, voices thinned to paper. Xanthe Fairweather folded her hands and waited, upright as a fence post, her face offering him the pleasant blankness of a well-trained servant and nothing else.

"Tell me your morning," he said. "From waking."

"I wake before the bells. I draw water. I taste the king's breakfast — bread, butter, honey, small beer — then the broth at midmorning. Then I help Bram with the noon prep until it's time to taste again." She recited it the way she must recite it every day, and probably every day of her life, and Corvane felt something twist behind his ribs that he refused to examine.

"You tasted the poisoned broth."

"Yes, ser."

"How much?"

"A spoonful. Two."

"The dose would have killed a grown man in an hour. You've known nightlock before?"

"It comes up. Maybe twice a year." A pause. "I don't feel it anymore. I don't feel most of them anymore."

"No pain? Numbness? A taste of copper?"

"Nothing." And here, for the first time, something moved behind her eyes — not fear, not guilt. Something quieter and sadder. "Nothing at all. That's rather the point of me, ser."

"That's rather the point of you," Corvane repeated softly.

He rose and crossed to the window, mostly because he did not trust his face. Below, the market was recovering its rhythm around the well; they'd scrubbed the stones by now, but he could see the crowd's shape had a hole in it, the way crowds do after death, people giving the spot a wider berth without knowing why.

A dying man runs to a specific person in a crowded market. Out of everyone, he chooses her. *The door remembers you.* Those were the words the witnesses finally produced, coaxed out hours later, half-guessed, contradictory — but consistent on those four words. Corvane had spent twenty years learning to weigh testimony, and he believed them.

Which raised a question he had no business asking and could not stop asking: how does a door remember?

There were doors that remembered. He had stood in front of one, once, at a border that no longer existed. It had not looked like a door. It had looked like weather, or light, or the place where your eye refuses to focus. His commanding officer had put his hand against it and recited the sealing words, and afterward the officer had aged ten years in a season and never slept a whole night again. *It remembers us,* the man had muttered once, drunk, in a voice Corvane still heard sometimes at the edge of sleep. *That's the horror of it. We erase the country, and the door remembers us instead.*

"Look at me," Corvane said.

Xanthe Fairweather looked at him. Grey eyes, level, patient. And beneath the patience, beneath the trained blankness, something that snagged at him like a nail in cloth — a familiarity he could not place and did not want to place. He had seen faces like hers before. At the border. In the fog. People walking out of erasure with that exact quality to their gaze, as though they'd left something on the far side and their eyes were still turned toward it.

No. Not quite that.

As though they were waiting for something on the near side to finish happening.

"Where were you born?" he asked.

"Here, ser. The valley."

"Your parents?"

"My mother died bearing me. My father—" the smallest hesitation, smooth as poured cream, "—I never knew him. The temple raised me until the kitchens took me on."

Smooth. Too smooth. Twenty years of tasting poison teaches a person to deliver truth and lie with the same tongue, and Corvane was suddenly, unpleasantly aware that he was being tasted in return — weighed, measured, assessed by the girl in the apron as thoroughly as she assessed the king's supper.

He should press her. He was chief justiciar of the realm; pressing suspects was the work of his life, and everything in him — the dead courier, the missing map, the words *the door remembers you* — screamed that this woman stood at the center of whatever was moving toward the throne. One more hour of questions. One warrant. One cell below the keep, and by evening he would know what she carried and whom she served.

Instead he heard himself say: "You'll be needed for further questioning. Routine. Report to my sergeant tomorrow at the eighth bell."

"Yes, ser."

"And Mistress Fairweather." He held her gaze. "You are not accused of anything. I want that understood. Answer honestly when asked, stay within the keep walls, and there will be no trouble for you."

Something flickered across her face — relief, quickly mastered, and under the relief a current of resolve so steady it chilled him. She thanked him, curtsied exactly the right depth, and went.

Corvane stood alone in the cold kitchen a long time.

* * *

That night he did not sleep. He climbed to the top of the watchtower instead, where the wind came off the salt flats carrying the smell of the valley's edge, and he took from his breast pocket the thing he had carried for nineteen years and shown to no living soul: a scrap of ledger paper, soft as cloth from handling, bearing a single entry in his own younger handwriting.

*Item: one infant, female. Removed across the line by unknown party prior to sealing. Unregistered. Pursuit declined.*

Pursuit declined. Three words that had cost him nothing at the time and everything since. A midwife's cart, he remembered now — he had let himself forget, and forgetting was a mercy he had charged against his own soul — a midwife's cart rattling south through the fog ahead of the sealing, and a bundle in it that cried once, thin and furious, and Corvane, twenty-eight years old and holding the ledger open, had looked at the fog, and looked at the pen, and written *pursuit declined* in a steady hand while his heart hammered like a traitor's.

He had told himself it was mercy. He had told himself it was exhaustion. Nineteen years of telling himself both, and tonight, standing over a sleeping valley with a dead courier cooling in the mortuary and a taster's grey eyes fixed in his memory, he admitted what it actually was.

It had been the last honest act of his career.

Below him, the keep lay dark. Tomorrow his sergeant would question Xanthe Fairweather, gently, per his orders — and report back, per his orders, to him alone. He had set a watch on her without calling it a watch. He had warned her off without warning her. He had done everything except the thing his office demanded, and he understood, standing in the wind with his scrap of paper gone soft as skin in his hand, that he was no longer hunting the courier's killer, or even the map.

He was hunting the moment when he would have to choose which side of the ledger to stand on. And somewhere below him, in a kitchen that smelled of woodsmoke and salt, a woman with nothing in her veins was already packing to run.

He felt it the way you feel weather change — not knowledge, just pressure, the valley's whole enchanted stillness drawing one long breath before the storm came through it.

By morning, he knew, she would be gone.
