## 4. The Map That Shouldn't Exist

The map would not open in the kitchen.

Xanthe learned this the hard way, at three in the morning, kneeling on the cold flagstones behind Bram's flour barrels with a dead man's paper trembling in her hands. She had tried to unfold it twice. Both times, the creases sealed themselves like a mouth pressed shut, and the parchment went warm against her fingers — warm, then hot, then sharp enough that she'd snatched her hand back expecting blisters.

There were none. There were never any.

"It knows what I am," she whispered. "That's why it won't—"

"That's why you're doing this in my kitchen instead of your room," Bram said from the doorway, arms crossed, flour to his elbows like a man interrupted mid-baptism. "Because your room has a window, and windows have eyes, and you've gone soft in the head." He didn't sound angry. He sounded frightened, which was worse. In eleven years of tasting the king's poison together, Xanthe had never once seen Bram frightened.

"Bram—"

"Give it here."

She hesitated. The map sat in her lap like a sleeping animal she'd promised not to wake. "It burned me."

"You're immune to burning."

"Not to *that*."

He crouched beside her, took the folded square with the confidence of a man who had kneaded dough through two famines and a fire, and pulled it open.

The creases parted for him without resistance. No heat. No whisper of magic. It lay flat across his palms, blank as an unwritten page, and Bram stared at it with his jaw working.

"Well," he said. "Either it's shy or it's stupid."

"It opened for me. Just now. For half a breath." She reached out, laid one fingertip against the corner — and the parchment shuddered, ink rising beneath its skin like bruises surfacing, rivers bleeding into view before her very eyes. Nine silver threads, converging. Then the whole thing snapped shut again, stinging her finger like a struck match.

Bram looked at her for a long moment.

"Kitchen's closed," he said quietly. "Come upstairs."

* * *

His rooms above the kitchens smelled of rosemary and pipe smoke and thirty years of bread. He cleared the table with one sweep of his forearm — plates, a ledger, a small carved horse Xanthe had made him when she was nine — and spread the map in the center of the wood. It stayed stubbornly blank under the lamplight until she touched it, and then it opened like a wound.

Nine rivers. They came from nine directions, thin as hairs at the edges of the page, thickening as they ran inward, each labeled in a script that hurt to read — letters that seemed to rearrange themselves whenever she looked away, so that she could only ever catch them peripherally, the way you catch a star.

And where they met, in the exact center of the map —

"There," Bram breathed. "Right there. That's us."

It was. Xanthe knew the valley's shape the way she knew her own hands, and the shape beneath the ninefold confluence was unmistakable: the long bowl of the Salt Valley, the king's keep at the northern lip, the market town cupped in its floor like water in a palm. Every road she had ever walked was drawn there, fine and true, down to the goat track behind the tannery that only she and Bram used.

But no valley appeared on any chart she had ever seen. The royal cartographers' maps showed forest there, and a notation: *barrens, unproductive, avoid.* The valley was the most fertile ground in nine provinces. It fed three armies. And it was not on the map because—

"Because the map isn't ours," Xanthe said slowly. "Look at the scale. Look at how much is around us." Beyond the valley's rim, the parchment bloomed with country: ranges she'd never heard named, forests with trees drawn individually, a coastline stitched with harbors. A whole world, dense and deliberate, pressing in on the little hollow where she had lived her entire life.

At the center of the confluence, where the nine rivers kissed, someone had drawn a door.

Not painted. Not inked. *Drawn* — the way a child draws, in heavy strokes that had been traced over and over until they grooved the parchment, until the door stood up from the page in relief, a ridge of fiber she could feel under her fingertip. It was set into a hillside that did not exist, in the heart of a valley that officially did not exist, and around its frame someone had written a single line in that shifting, unreadable script.

"What does it say?" Bram asked.

"I can almost—" She tilted her head, let the letters blur. "Something about... remembering. *The door remembers.*" Her voice failed on the last word, because that was what the dying courier had said, blood bubbling between his teeth, his fist closing hers around this impossible thing. *The door remembers you.*

Bram sat down heavily on his stool. He poured two cups of wine without asking and pushed one across.

"I'm going to say a thing," he said, "and you're going to let me finish it." He drank. "Twenty-two years ago, before your time and near enough mine, there was a woman who worked these kitchens. Foreigner. Quiet. Sang songs nobody knew the words to. She left one night in winter, quick as a thief, and the justiciars tore the valley apart looking for her, and never found her, and after some years everyone agreed she'd never existed at all." He turned the cup in his hands. "You have her chin, girl. You have her way of standing in doorways."

Xanthe could not speak. The lamplight seemed very far away.

"The stranger today," Bram said. "He wasn't running *to* something. He was running home. And they cut him down at our well for it." His knuckles whitened on the cup. "Which means whatever door this is, the crown wants it shut, and wants shut anyone who might know how to open it. Which means by tomorrow noon, every justiciar in the province will be asking after the woman who spoke to him last."

"They saw me."

"They saw a taster. Tasters are furniture." He smiled, and it broke his face. "Furniture doesn't hide maps in flour barrels."

Outside, far off toward the keep, a bell began to ring. Not the supper bell. Not the alarm for fire or flood. The third bell — the one rung, in all of Xanthe's twenty-two years, exactly never.

Bram was already moving. "Search warrants," he said. "They'll start with the kitchens. They always start where the poison is."

* * *

They came at dawn, twelve justiciars in grey, their boots loud on flagstones that had known nothing louder than dripping water for a generation. Xanthe watched them come through the market from Bram's window and understood, with terrible clarity, that the valley had never been safe. It had only ever been asleep.

"They'll take you first," Bram said. He was packing a satchel with hands that shook and did not stop working: bread, cheese, the good knife, a flask of the broth base she'd survived on since childhood. "Old cook, loyal servant, nothing to find. I'll give them a name and a direction and waste their morning. By the time they work out I've lied, you'll be past the north pasture."

"Past the north pasture is the salt line." Her mouth had gone dry. "Nobody crosses the salt line, Bram. Nobody even *wants* to."

"Somebody has to carry that country somewhere it can breathe." He closed the satchel and pushed it into her arms, and for a moment neither of them could look at the other. Then he hugged her, hard, flour and all, and she felt his heartbeat going too fast against her cheek. "Eleven years you've tasted everything and felt nothing. Go feel something. That's an order from your cook."

The pounding on the kitchen door downstairs shook dust from the beams.

Bram stepped back, wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand, and became, instantly and completely, a tired old man annoyed at being woken. "Back stair," he murmured. "Go."

She went — but at the top of the steps she stopped, one hand on the wall, because below her she could hear the door give way, hear boots, hear a voice she knew from the interrogation yard: Corvane, the chief justiciar, dry as slate.

"Where is the taster?"

"Taster? Asleep in her bunk, likely. Lazy girl sleeps like the dead—"

"Do not lie to me, old man. We found blood at the well. We found witnesses. Where—is—the—taster."

A pause. Then Bram, calm as Sunday: "Son, I've fed kings and buried queens of the larder both. Ask me proper and I might answer."

Something struck flesh. Xanthe's whole body clenched — but Bram's voice came again, wheezing, laughing, infuriating: "Is that all you have? I've been hit harder by dough."

She made herself climb down. She made herself go, because staying meant making his beating worthless, and because the map was warm against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and because the door remembered her and she was beginning, horribly, wonderfully, to remember it too.

The back stair let out behind the smokehouse. Dawn was coming up grey over the north pasture, and beyond it, faint as a scar, the pale shimmer of the salt line — the border of her whole world, and the edge of everything else.

Xanthe Fairweather ran.

Behind her, the kitchen burned bright in the early light, and the justiciars' voices rose, and above them all, clear and cracked and utterly unafraid, Bram was singing a song nobody knew the words to.
