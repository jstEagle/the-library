## 5. Roads Not on Any Chart

The valley ended at a line of salt so old it had stopped looking like salt. It lay along the ground in a pale seam, like the scar of an old wound, and Xanthe crossed it at dusk with her boots wet from the river and Bram's goodbye still lodged in her throat like a fishbone.

*Go,* he'd said, shoving her toward the gate with flour-white hands. *You were never made for this place, girl. You were only ever waiting in it.*

She had not looked back. Looking back was how people got caught.

Now she stood on the far side of the scar and waited for something to happen — a sound, a snap, some great exhalation of the world changing its mind. Nothing happened. The hills went on being hills. The sky went on being the color of pewter. But when she breathed in, the air tasted different: layered, complicated, alive in a way the valley's clean flat air had never been. It tasted like a kitchen where someone else had been cooking.

She walked until full dark, following the map's inked thread north by west, and it was somewhere past the first ridge that the world began, quietly, to misbehave.

The first sign was small. A fox watched her pass from atop a fence post — sat there like a gentleman, tail curled around its feet, and inclined its head as she went by. Not a trick of light. She turned to stare at it and it stared back, patient as a toll-keeper, before dropping off the post and trotting away into the dark.

The second sign was less polite. The road forked ahead of her, and she was almost certain — *almost*, which in the valley would have been certainty — that it had not forked when she'd checked an hour ago. She stood at the junction with the map open under the moon, and the map disagreed with the ground, and then, when she looked again, the map disagreed with itself. The inked road shifted like a snake settling into new coils.

"All right," she said aloud, to no one, because saying things aloud kept fear at a manageable size. "All right."

"That's what I said," remarked a voice above her, "when they moved it last Tuesday."

A crow sat in the dead tree overhanging the fork. It was a large crow, scruffy at the breast, with the air of someone who had seen better decades. It looked at her with one glossy eye and then, deliberately, the other.

"You talked," Xanthe said.

"I did," the crow agreed. "You're taking it very well. Most people scream, or weep, or try to give me bread in exchange for prophecy. The bread is acceptable, for the record."

"I'm not from here." She said it the way you'd confess to a crime. "Where I'm from, birds are just birds."

"Mm." The crow hopped down a branch, closer. Its eye had a lamplight shine to it, though there was no lamp. "And where exactly is that? Because I've been watching you walk since sundown, and I'll tell you what I see. I see the roads ignoring you. I see the mist parting around your boots instead of through them. There's a snare-willow two miles back that's been singing travelers off the path all season, and you walked past it humming, and it *shut up*. So either you're carrying something very old, or—" it cocked its head— "you're something very empty. No offense meant. Emptiness is rarer than you'd think."

Xanthe's hand went to her coat, where the map lay folded against her ribs. "I'm going north. To the ninth river."

"Everyone who goes anywhere interesting is going to the ninth river," the crow said. "Or running from it. Which are you?"

"Both."

"Ah." The crow nodded, deeply satisfied, as if she'd confirmed its entire worldview. "Then you want the left fork. The right one goes to the sea eventually, but it takes eleven years and costs you your reflection. Left. And keep walking when the lights start. Don't count them. Counting them is how they get a grip on you."

"What lights?"

The crow looked at her for a long moment, and something in its look was almost pity. "You'll know. Walk fast, empty thing."

It flew off into the dark, and Xanthe stood alone at the crossroads with the wind moving through the grass in long slow waves, like something breathing under a blanket, and understood with total clarity that she had left the world she knew behind, and the world she had come to did not want her dead. It wanted something stranger than that.

She took the left fork.

* * *

The lights started at moonrise.

They rose out of the fields on either side of the road, first one, then dozens, pale gold and drifting, like seeds from some enormous dandelion burning slowly. They were beautiful. That was the trap, obviously — the crow had told her, and besides, Xanthe had spent twenty years learning that beautiful things in kitchens were usually poison. She walked through them the way she walked through a banquet she wasn't allowed to enjoy: eyes forward, hands steady.

They gathered anyway. They pressed close, curious, brushing her shoulders like moths. One hovered before her face, and inside its glow she saw something — a suggestion of a doorway, a warm room, her mother's voice calling her in to supper. Except Xanthe had no memory of any mother's voice, and the light was offering her one anyway, tailor-made, the exact shape of her loneliness.

"No," she said quietly. "I don't buy it. I've tasted every lie ever brewed, and you're a cheap one."

The light flickered — offended, perhaps — and drifted off to bother some other traveler. Behind her, far down the road, she heard a man laughing softly at nothing, walking into the field, walking away from the road, still laughing. She did not look. The crow hadn't said anything about looking, but she felt the instruction in her bones anyway.

Near dawn she came over a rise and saw fires below — a dozen wagons drawn in a circle, mules cropping the dark grass, figures moving between the flames. A caravan. Her stomach, which had opinions about everything these days, announced loudly that she hadn't eaten since the valley.

She was still debating whether strangers were safer than hunger when a voice at her elbow said, "You look like a girl who's outrun something."

Xanthe spun. A woman stood there — tall, gray-streaked hair bound in a red scarf, sleeves rolled over forearms mapped with old burn scars. She held two tin cups and offered one without ceremony.

"Isolde," the woman said. "This is my road, technically, though I try not to be possessive about it. Roads hate that. You've noticed."

"They move," Xanthe said.

"When they're in the mood." Isolde sipped her own cup. Steam curled from it; it smelled of chicory and something sharper underneath. "My picket didn't see you coming. Neither did my wards. I have very good wards. I paid a witch in Ostermere three goats and a grudge for those wards. So either my witch robbed me, or—" her eyes were quick and gray and missed nothing— "you slid past them the way rain slides past oiled canvas. Which is it?"

"The second one, I think."

"Hm." Isolde considered her over the rim of the cup. "Well. I'm not in the business of turning away mysteries before breakfast. Come down, eat, sleep if you can. In the morning you can tell me what's in your coat that makes the lights ignore you, or you can lie to me beautifully, and either way I'll pretend to believe you. That's the smuggler's covenant. We deal in trust the way other people deal in silk — carefully, and at a markup."

"Why would you take me in?" Xanthe asked. "You don't know me."

"No," Isolde agreed. "But I knew the look on your face. I wore it myself, twenty years ago, walking the wrong direction out of a country that had stopped existing." She turned back toward the fires, then paused. "The name's optional, by the way. Plenty of my people travel under no name at all. But if you've got one you're not using anymore, the fire takes those too."

Xanthe followed her down into the ring of wagons, into the smell of woodsmoke and frying onions and mule, into the warmth of strangers' fires. Someone handed her bread without asking who she was. A child stared at her openly until his mother smacked his shoulder and he grinned and stared some more. Nobody asked her business. Nobody flinched from her. It was the first company she'd kept since Bram, and the strangeness of it — of being *included* rather than merely *endured* — caught in her chest like a swallowed ember.

She slept in the lee of a wagon wheel with the map against her ribs and dreamed, for the first time in her life, in colors the valley had never grown.

* * *

Three days she traveled with them, north by west, and each day the world got wilder and Xanthe got more conspicuous by being invisible to it.

On the fourth day a bridge argued with Isolde about its toll and won, and they forded the river cold to the waist while the bridge sulked. On the fifth day the road behind them simply stopped existing — Isolde sent a boy back to check, and he returned pale, reporting forty miles of unbroken moor where their own tracks should have been. "It does that when it's nervous," Isolde said, spitting into the moor for luck. "Something's frightened it. Something coming up our back trail."

And on the sixth day, the crows came.

Not one crow. Dozens, then hundreds, wheeling over the caravan in a vast slow spiral, silent, which was worse than talking. The caravan stopped. Mules screamed. Isolde stood in the wagon bed with her scarf whipping and shouted up at the wheeling dark: "We've paid! Every crossing, every ford, every whisper-post between here and the sea — we've PAID!"

One crow dropped out of the spiral and landed on the wagon rail. It was the same scruffy-breasted crow from the crossroads; Xanthe knew it the way you know a face from a bad dream.

"It's not you it wants," the crow said, ignoring Isolde entirely. Its lamp-bright eye found Xanthe among the wagons. "There's riders behind you. Gray cloaks. They move without dust and they ask after a girl by description, not name — which means whoever sent them knows her name wouldn't help them." The eye glittered. "Justiciars. Salt Valley iron, that badge they wear. They're two days back and gaining, because the roads are letting them. The roads don't do favors, little empty thing. Somebody has *bought* the roads."

The silence in the wagon circle was absolute. Then Isolde turned, slowly, and looked at Xanthe with those quick gray eyes, and Xanthe braced herself for the covenant to end — for the fire to take back its warmth, for the bread-givers to become strangers again.

Instead Isolde said, "Gray cloaks. How many?"

"Nine," the crow said. "Maybe ten. One of them walks like a man who used to be kind."

Something flickered across Isolde's face — recognition, calculation, an old debt surfacing. "Corvane," she murmured, half to herself. Then, louder, to the caravan: "Break camp. We run the moor road tonight, all of it, no stops. Anyone who objects can stay and discuss jurisdiction with the justiciars."

Wagons groaned into motion. Isolde swung down beside Xanthe and lowered her voice, and for the first time the wry kindness in it had steel underneath.

"You're going to the ninth river," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Yes."

"And the men behind us would rather you drowned in it than reached it." Isolde studied her a moment longer, then jerked her chin toward the lead wagon. "Ride with me, mystery girl. And tonight, on the moor, you'll tell me what's folded in that coat — because whatever it is, it's worth nine sets of gray cloaks, and I don't bleed my people for cargo I haven't inventoried."

Xanthe put her hand flat against her ribs, over the map, over the country in her pocket, and understood that the road had narrowed to a single thread now, and that every mile of it would be bought.

Ahead, the moor waited under a sky going the color of old bruises, and somewhere on it, two days behind and closing, rode the law.
