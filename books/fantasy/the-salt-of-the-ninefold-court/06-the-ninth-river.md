## 6. The Ninth River

The river had no name, because names were the first thing it took.

Isolde told them that at dusk on the eighth day out of the valley, when the ground began to hum beneath their boots like a struck tuning fork. The smugglers had gone quiet in the way people go quiet in a sickroom. Even the crows — Xanthe's three gray shadows, who had followed her from the valley border and argued cheerfully about everything since — folded their wings and said nothing at all.

"That's how you know you're close," Isolde said. She stood at the crest of a low hill, her silver-streaked braid snapping in a wind that smelled of ozone and old rain. "The quiet. The river doesn't roar, girl. It listens. And everything it hears, it keeps."

Below them, in the last light, the water ran backward. Xanthe watched it flow uphill toward some source that wasn't there, and felt the fine hairs on her arms rise. She had crossed eight rivers to get here. This one was different. This one looked less like water than like a held breath.

"What happens if we cross?" she asked.

"You cross, or you don't." Isolde shrugged, but her knuckles were white around the strap of her pack. "Most folk cross and come out the other side missing things. A face they loved. Their mother's voice. The taste of bread. The storm takes what it takes, and there's no bargaining. My second-in-command, Ferro — he went through eleven years ago. Came out not knowing his own daughter. Walked with us another six years before he figured out enough of himself to go home." Her jaw tightened. "He found the girl remembered him perfectly. Said that was worse."

The caravan made camp without fires. Nobody suggested one. Xanthe sat sharpening a knife she didn't need to use and listened to the humming grow louder as night came down, until it wasn't a sound so much as a pressure behind her eyes.

She should have been afraid. She was, a little. But underneath the fear sat that same flat numbness she'd carried all her life, the taster's tongue that knew every poison by name and flinched at none of them. Standing at the edge of an erasure-storm, she mostly felt *curious*.

*What would it even take from me?* she thought. *I have nothing it wants.*

The crows disagreed. All evening they had been circling her head in tight, agitated loops, croaking single words: *"Remember. Remember. Remember."*

"Shut up," she muttered at them.

"They're right, though," Isolde said, dropping down beside her. In the dark her face was half shadow, half starlight. "You want to tell me why the wraith-reeds bent away from you at the sixth crossing? Or why the road unknotted itself for our wagons after you walked it? Roads don't do favors, girl. Not for me, not for anyone I've ever run with." She turned, and her eyes were very steady. "You're carrying something. Or you *are* something."

"I'm a kitchen girl."

"You're a kitchen girl who drank hemlock tea for breakfast for twenty years and never once blinked." Isolde smiled, but it didn't reach her eyes. "I've hauled contraband past every ward and watchfire between here and the coast. I know impossible when I see it. I just usually charge extra."

Xanthe looked away, toward the river. The stars were coming out overhead, nine constellations where the maps showed five, wheeling slowly in patterns no astronomer in Salt Valley had ever charted. Somewhere beyond this water lay a door drawn in ink on a dead man's map. Somewhere beyond it lay whatever the courier had died whispering about.

"The door remembers you," he'd said. She still didn't know what it meant. But her hands had known, these last eight days — her hands had known which fork in the road to take before her eyes did, had pulled the map from her coat at exactly the moments she needed it, as though the paper were reading *her*.

"I'll go first," she heard herself say.

Isolde laughed, sharp and disbelieving. "Absolutely not."

"If it takes my memories, I've lost nothing worth keeping. If it kills me—" She shrugged. "You'll have saved yourself a fare."

"That's the bleakest thing anyone's ever offered me, and I once traveled with a man who sold his own funeral twice." Isolde studied her for a long moment. Then, quietly: "Why?"

And Xanthe, who had spent her whole life tasting other people's food and feeling nothing, heard herself answer honestly for the first time since the stranger fell bleeding against the well. "Because something out there knows me. And I am so tired of being nobody."

The humming swelled, as if the river had heard and approved.

* * *

They crossed at dawn, when the storm was thinnest — or so Isolde said, though Xanthe suspected dawn simply meant the moment when courage and exhaustion balanced out.

The caravan strung themselves together with rope, twelve smugglers and one kitchen girl, and waded into water that wasn't cold. That was the first wrongness: the river had no temperature at all. It had no wetness either. Walking into it was like walking into the space between two thoughts.

Then the light changed, and the storm rose.

It came off the water like heat-shimmer given teeth — a standing wave of silver static, taller than any of them, singing a note that Xanthe felt in her fillings. Where it touched, things *unhappened*. Ferro's replacement, a scarred woman named Dagny, walked into it and staggered out the far side with her mouth moving soundlessly, hands pressed to her temples, hunting for a name that was no longer attached to anything. The drover Tam lost the memory of his own left hand and had to be shown, gently, over and over, that it belonged to him. One by one the caravan passed through the shimmering wall and came out diminished, blinking, holding onto each other like men in fog.

Xanthe walked into the heart of it.

The storm sang around her, probed her, poured over her skin like a tide testing a seawall —

— and broke, and parted, and flowed around her on both sides, leaving a corridor of perfect stillness where she stood.

For one suspended moment she could see the entire storm from inside: a vast slow whirlpool of stolen years, faces and lullabies and first loves spinning in silver suspension, ten thousand memories circling like fish. It looked down at her — she felt it look, the way you feel eyes between your shoulder blades — and it *hesitated*.

Then it recoiled. Gently. Almost courteously. The way one steps back from a sleeping child.

Xanthe walked out the far side untouched, and turned to see Isolde frozen mid-river, staring at her with an expression Xanthe had never seen on the smuggler's weathered face. Not fear. Something older than fear.

"Awe," Xanthe realized. "That's awe."

"You—" Isolde's voice cracked; she swallowed and tried again. "Nothing crosses that clean. Nothing living. The storm took my grandmother's face from me in '09, and I never even got to say goodbye to the memory." She waded the last few yards, grabbed Xanthe's shoulders with both strong hands, and searched her face like a woman reading scripture. "What *are* you?"

"I don't know," Xanthe said. And then, because the map was warm against her ribs and the ninth river had washed the last of her lies away: "But I think I'm going to find out."

Behind them, Dagny was quietly crying because she couldn't remember why the river frightened her. Ahead, across the grey flats, the land began to change — grass the color of verdigris, trees with leaves like hammered tin, and far off, a shape on the horizon that might have been hills or might have been walls.

Isolde made her choice quickly, the way she made all her choices. "Right," she said, slinging her pack. "I've spent my life selling what kings say doesn't exist. Seems fitting I walk the last mile with the girl who shouldn't exist either." She jabbed a finger at Xanthe. "But when this is done, you owe me the whole story. Every word."

"It might be a long story."

"I bill by the hour anyway."

* * *

Corvane caught them at midday, and he did not come alone.

Xanthe saw the riders first: six figures in slate-grey, moving fast across the flats, and her stomach dropped through the verdigris grass because she knew that silhouette, had known it since childhood — the long coats, the blank helms, the justiciars of the king's third circle. And at their head, riding a black mare that seemed too intelligent for its own comfort, the chief justiciar himself, helmless, iron-haired, his face like a cliff that had decided to be disappointed in the sea.

"Run," Isolde breathed, hand going to her blade.

"No," Corvane called, reining in thirty feet away. His voice carried flat across the flats. "Don't. Please." He dismounted, slowly, showing empty hands, and the gesture was so unlike everything Xanthe knew of him that it stopped her better than any command. "If I wanted you taken, girl, I'd have taken you at the seventh river, while you slept. I've had six days to think about whether I would."

"Then take me now," Xanthe said. Her voice didn't shake. Twenty years of swallowing poison had seen to that. "You know what I carry."

"I know what you carry." He came closer, boots crunching on the strange grey soil. Up close he looked older than he had in the kitchens — or perhaps just more tired, the way a man looks when he has finally set down something heavy and discovered his arms remember nothing else. "I've known since the market well. You have her face, you understand. The queen's face. I stood honor guard at the Ninefold Court for three years, before the Erasure, before they burned it from every chart and made men like me swear it had never been. I carried the infant princess out myself." His eyes, pale as river ice, held hers. "I gave you to the fairweather family myself. I chose the valley. Poison-proof, magic-proof, memory-proof. The safest hiding place in the world, because officially the world had no door in it."

The flats went very quiet. Behind her, Xanthe heard Isolde exhale a curse in three languages.

"Then arrest me," Xanthe said. "Or let me pass."

"I mean to let you pass." Corvane glanced back at his riders — five of them, Xanthe noted, watching the sixth hang back at the ridge, and wondered briefly what had happened to the sixth. "That is why I rode four days without sleep. Listen to me, and listen well, because I will only say this once." His voice dropped, and for the first time she heard something underneath the iron: fury, banked and terrible. "The king has stopped sending justiciars to find the door. He's sending sappers. Dragon-oil, salt-fire, the works. They're three days behind me, maybe less." He let that land. "He doesn't mean to seal the door anymore, girl. He means to burn it. With everyone still inside."

The words went through her like a blade through broth.

"Everyone," she repeated. "There are *people* in there."

"There are nine thousand people in there, starving behind a spell their own king — your grandfather's king — threw up to bury a war everyone lost. And now ours means to finish it with fire rather than risk them stepping through." Corvane's jaw worked. "I have spent thirty years enforcing the law of a crown that taught me the Court was a fairy story. Then a dying courier bled out at a well and a kitchen girl looked at me with the queen's eyes, and I understood that I have spent thirty years helping to erase living people." He straightened, and drew off one grey glove, and extended his bare hand — an oath, the oldest kind, sworn on skin. "I can't give you the law. The law is going to burn them. So I'm giving you something older instead."

Xanthe looked at the hand. At the man who had carried her out of a dying country and now, twenty years later, stood ready to betray his king to carry something back in.

"And if I open the door," she said quietly, "and nine thousand starving people step through into a kingdom that remembers them only as invaders — what then?"

"Then," said Corvane, "we had better make certain the first thing they meet is someone who feeds them." A ghost of something crossed his face — not quite a smile. "You were a taster. You'll know how that's done."

Behind him, the horizon shuddered — a distant orange bloom, thin as a coin on edge, and beneath it a sound like the world clearing its throat. Dragon-oil, already burning the outer passes.

Three days. Maybe less.

Xanthe took his hand. "Then we'd better hurry," she said, "because I have a country to save and a supper to cook — and I don't yet know the recipe."

Far ahead, where the verdigris grass met walls that weren't hills, a door was waiting. And it remembered her.
