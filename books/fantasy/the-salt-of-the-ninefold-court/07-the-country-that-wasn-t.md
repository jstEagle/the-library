## 7. The Country That Wasn't

The storm let her through the way a mother lets go of a child's hand — reluctantly, and only at the very last.

Xanthe came out of it on hands and knees in wet grass, her hair full of river-mist, her mind intact and therefore alone. Behind her, Isolde still stood at the water's edge, staring at her own empty hands. The erasure-storm had passed over the smuggler like a scythe passing over wheat, taking with it the name of her first horse, her mother's face, the reason she never slept on her left side. Xanthe had watched it happen from inside the eye of it, untouched, and had understood for the first time what she was: not lucky. Not blessed. *Foreign.* The storm had spared her the way a border guard spares a citizen of another country — with indifference, because she was not its business.

Isolde turned. Her face was composed, which was worse than weeping.

"You crossed that," she said, "and you are still whole."

"I don't know why."

"Yes," Isolde said quietly. "You do." She looked past Xanthe then, to what lay beyond the ninth river, and her composure cracked along old seams. "Gods above. It's real. We always said it wasn't, and it's real."

Xanthe followed her gaze, and forgot how to breathe.

The valley she had grown up in was grey and honest — stone, salt, smoke, the brown of winter turnips. What lay before her now was none of those things. Nine rivers ran down out of nine ranges of mountains, braiding together across a plain of impossible green, and where they met they made not a delta but a *city* — towers of white stone veined with silver, bridges that curved like drawn bows, terraced gardens climbing the hillsides in steps of violet and gold. Above it all hung nine moons, pale as fingernail parings, though the sun still stood high in the afternoon sky. Stars among them, ninefold, arranged in a wheel no astronomer of Salt Valley had ever charted.

It should have been loud. A city that size should have roared. Instead the silence reached her across miles, thin and wrong, the silence of an empty house.

"It's starving," Isolde said. She had been this way before, then — or near enough. "Look at the gardens. Half of them are fallow. Look at the light." She shook her head slowly. "Magic is food here, girl. And there's been nothing coming in or going out for twenty years. Nothing but whatever leaks under the door."

Xanthe's hand went to her breast pocket, where the map rode against her heart. The courier's blood had stiffened into the folds of it. *The door remembers you.*

"Come on," Isolde said, and started downhill toward the nearest bridge. "If anyone's alive in there, they'll want to know the storm didn't take us."

* *

They found the first people at the outer gate, and the first thing Xanthe noticed was that they were beautiful the way autumn leaves are beautiful — vivid, intricate, and dying.

There were six of them guarding the gate, if *guarding* was still the word. They wore armor of lacquered reed-bark, once brilliant, now dulled to the color of ash. Their faces were fine-boned and ageless and hollow-cheeked, skin stretched over beauty like silk over a chair frame. One of them carried a spear; the others leaned on each other. The youngest-looking one, a woman with hair like spun copper gone grey at the temples, saw them coming and did not raise the spear. She just stared, and her eyes filled, and she said something in a language Xanthe did not know — and yet some syllable of it snagged on something deep in her chest, a hook set long ago.

"What did she say?" Xanthe whispered.

"She said," the copper-haired woman answered, switching to the trade-tongue with an accent like water over stones, "that she dreamed someone would come over the storm-water. She said it every year on the same night, and every year she was called a fool." Her gaze moved over Xanthe's travel-stained coat, the salt-white scar on her forearm from the kitchen cauldron years ago, and stopped. Stopped entirely. The woman went very still, the way animals go still when they scent something they cannot name.

"Turn your head," she said softly. "To the light. Please."

Xanthe turned. She felt foolish doing it. Behind her she heard the other guards stir, heard Isolde murmur a warning.

The copper-haired woman reached up with two fingers and touched the skin below Xanthe's left ear — a place Xanthe had never thought about in her life, a small pale patch she'd had since birth, smooth as a drop of wax, that the other children in the valley had called her *kiss-mark*. The woman's fingers trembled against it.

"The starfall mark," she breathed. "Left of the heart-line of the jaw. Ninth position." Her voice cracked clean through. "Only one person alive wears it in the ninth position."

"I don't understand—"

"There is a song," the woman said, and her tears fell freely now, without shame. "A lullaby. Every child of the Court knows it. It goes—" and she hummed four notes, low and rising, low and rising, a little turning figure like a bird circling before landing.

The world tilted.

Xanthe knew the song. She had known it all her life and never known she knew it — it lived in her the way her heartbeat lived in her, surfacing only when she stirred a pot or walked home in the dark, four notes she hummed without ever hearing herself do it. Bram had laughed at her for it once: *"You've got a tune stuck in your head your whole life, girl, and it hasn't any words."*

It had words. The words were in the copper-haired woman's mouth, in the language Xanthe shouldn't know and somehow did, the way you know the shape of a room you were born in.

"Who sang it to me?" Xanthe asked. Her voice came out very small. "When I was a baby. Who sang it?"

The woman knelt. All six guards knelt, in the mud of the road, in their dead grey armor, and the sight of it frightened Xanthe more than the storm had.

"Come inside," the woman said. "There is one who can answer better than I. But oh — oh, child. Twenty years we have kept a cradle-cloak folded in a cedar chest because we could not bear to burn it, and it will be too small for you now, and we will show it to you anyway." She rose, took Xanthe's hand with a grip surprisingly strong, and said the rest like a woman setting down a weight she had carried so long it had grown into her shoulders:

"The queen is dying. And she has been singing that song to an empty cradle every night for twenty years."

* *

They took her through streets that broke her heart by degrees.

Everything in the Ninefold Court was beautiful and everything was failing. Fountains ran dry, their carved dolphins gaping at a sky that gave no rain. Lanterns burned in windows with flames the color of memory — blue, violet, green — but the flames guttered low, and behind many of the windows there was no one to see them. The people who came out to watch her pass were few and silent, and they looked at her the way starving people look at bread they cannot afford: hunger and dignity fighting openly on their faces. An old man pressed his hand flat against a wall as she passed, and the wall bloomed faintly with lilies where he touched it, and the effort of it bent him nearly double.

"That's the last of him," Isolde murmured beside her. "He'll sleep for a week after that. Magic costs, girl. Out here, everything costs, and the pantry's been bare for twenty years."

"Why doesn't the queen leave?" Xanthe asked. "Why does she stay behind a sealed door, starving, instead of—"

"Because the door only opens from one side," the copper-haired woman said. "And the key isn't a key." She glanced at Xanthen sidelong. "We hoped, once. We sent a baby out through it, wrapped in salt-soaked wool, so that she would grow beyond magic's reach and become the one thing that could walk back through untouched. A taster-child. Poison-proof, spell-proof, blank as fresh snow. We told ourselves the world outside would keep her safe until she was ready." Her voice dropped. "Then the border sealed itself behind her, and we learned the door does not open for the one who waits. Only for the one who returns."

They stopped before the palace — no fortress, this, but a long white hall built around a courtyard garden gone to seed, its nine towers leaning together like tired sisters. Inside, the air smelled of dried herbs and cold candlewax and, beneath it, faintly, of salt. Xanthe's salt. Home-smell, here at the end of the world. She had to stop walking.

"Say it," she said. "Whatever you're all thinking. Say it plainly. I'm nobody. I taste soup in a kitchen. My friend Bram makes the best barley stew in Salt Valley and he teases me about leaving and I have a *bed* there, I have a—"

"You have a name," the copper-haired woman said gently. "You've simply been carrying it folded up, the way you carry the map. Shall I unfold it for you? Or would you rather hear it from her?"

* *

The queen's chamber was warm, which surprised her. After all that fading, after all that grey — the room was lit with a hundred candles and hung with tapestries of nine rivers and nine moons, and in the great bed beneath the tallest window lay a woman so thin that the quilt seemed to be covering driftwood.

She was awake. Of course she was awake. She had been awake for twenty years, Xanthe thought, waiting for footsteps on the storm-road.

The queen's eyes found her across the room — grey eyes, the exact grey of the sky over Salt Valley in November — and widened, and filled, and the sound she made was not a word in any language. It was the sound a person makes when something long broken sets back into place.

"My lady," the copper-haired woman began, "she came over the storm-water, she bears the mark, she knows the—"

"*Leave us,*" the queen said, and the room emptied like water through a grate, even Isolde, even the guards, even the shadows seeming to withdraw to the walls.

Then it was just the two of them, the dying queen and the kitchen girl, and neither of them moved for a long moment. Outside the window, one of the nine moons drifted slowly across the sun.

"I used to imagine this," the queen said at last. Her voice was dry paper, but steady. "Twenty years of imagining, and in none of my imaginings were you carrying a satchel and looking at me as though I might bite you."

"You might," Xanthe said. "Everyone keeps kneeling at me. I don't know why. I taste broth. That's the whole of what I am. I sip poison for a living and feel nothing, and everyone in the valley says I'm the luckiest girl alive, and yesterday a man died at a well telling me a door remembered me, and today six strangers knelt in the mud, and I am *so tired* of not knowing things."

"Then know this one." The queen lifted a hand from the quilt — a hand like a bird's foot, translucent at the edges, glowing faintly at the fingertips with borrowed, rationed light — and beckoned. Xanthe crossed the floor as if wading upstream and sat on the edge of the bed, and the queen touched her face, both hands, framing her jaw, tracing the small pale patch below her left ear with a thumb that shook.

"You were born on the night of the ninefold conjunction," the queen said. "In this bed. You weighed almost nothing and screamed almost never, and you had your father's temper and my stubbornness, and when the king's justiciars burned the border-towns and the erasure-storms came, I gave you to Maren—" her voice caught on the name, the copper-haired guard's name, Xanthe realized, "—and Maren wrapped you in salt-wool and carried you through a door that has not opened since. Because magic could not follow you. Because *nothing* could touch you. Because I needed my daughter to live even if living meant never knowing me." Her thumb traced the birthmark again, back and forth, back and forth, like a woman smoothing a wrinkle from cloth. "I chose the cradle-song myself. So that if you ever came back, some part of you would know the way home before your head did."

Xanthe sat very still. Something enormous was happening inside her — a coastline rearranging, a river changing course — and she held herself motionless the way she'd been taught to hold still while poison worked its way through her body, feeling everything and letting none of it show.

"I have a mother," she said finally, stupidly. "Her name was Marta Fairweather and she died when I was nine and she made plum jam and she never sang."

"No," the queen agreed softly. "But someone did. Somewhere in you, all these years. Four notes, low and rising. Tell me I'm wrong."

Xanthe couldn't tell her she was wrong. The four notes were rising in her throat right now.

"They call you Xanthe Fairweather out there?" the queen asked.

"Yes."

"It's a good name. Keep it, if you want it. But you were born—" and here the queen said a word in the tongue of the Court, a word like a bell rung underwater, and it settled into Xanthe's chest and fit a hollow she had never known was there, the way the lullaby had, the way the salt-smell had.

Outside, somewhere in the starving city, a bell began to toll — slow, cracked, joyful, the sound of a country remembering hope and not quite trusting it.

"They'll want to celebrate," the queen murmured. Her eyes were closing, her strength spent, her hand sliding from Xanthe's face. "Don't let them. There's no time. Listen to me, daughter, while I still have hours in me." Her eyes opened again, burning. "The door opens from this side for you alone. But opening it will bring the wrath of your king down on us, and he will come with fire — Corvane himself may already be on the road. Before you turn that key, you must know exactly what you're choosing, and whom you're choosing between."

She drew a breath that rattled like wind through a dry reed-bed.

"And first," the queen said, "before anything — before duty, before war, before crowns — feed me. I am told you are a taster. I have not tasted anything without pain in twenty years. Let me see what my daughter can do."

Xanthe looked down at her mother — at the hollow cheeks and the burning eyes and the twenty years of an empty cradle — and felt the two halves of her life grind together like millstones.

Somewhere south, an army was gathering. Here, a kettle waited in the hearth.

She went to find it.
