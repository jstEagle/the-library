## 8. The Queen's Last Supper

They gave Xanthe a chamber in the high tower, and she did not sleep in it.

The bed was soft as a held breath, heaped with furs that had never known frost, and the walls were hung with banners of a country that existed only behind her eyelids now — nine rivers silver on black, nine stars above them. Through the window, the Court glimmered under its own failing sky, towers lit by lamps that burned without oil, gardens where the flowers opened only when someone remembered them. It was beautiful the way a dying man is beautiful: every feature sharpened by what was leaving it.

She sat instead at the foot of the queen's bed, in a chair some servant had padded for her hours ago, and watched her mother breathe.

*Her mother.* The words still fit strangely, like a shoe bought for a foot that had grown. Twenty years of being no one's daughter, and then a lullaby hummed in a fading voice — the same lullaby Bram sang when the ovens were hot and the kitchen boys needed soothing — and the whole architecture of her life came down like sugar spun too thin.

The Queen of the Ninefold Court lay beneath quilts stitched with river-maps, her hair spread white as salt across the pillow. Once, Isolde said, she had been tall enough to shame the pines. Now she was a rind of herself, hands like folded paper, eyes that still burned — that was the terrible thing, how brightly they still burned — in a face going out around them.

"You're staring," the queen said, without opening her eyes. "You have your father's stare."

"I don't know my father's stare."

"No." A ghost of a smile. "You know his stubbornness, though. You crossed an erasure-storm to bring me a map you couldn't even read." Her eyes opened then, grey-green as sea glass, and fixed on Xanthe with an attention that felt like being weighed on very fine scales. "Tell me about the broth."

Xanthe blinked. "The broth?"

"The one you tasted this morning. In the kitchens below. I'm told you went down before dawn and drank from every pot." The queen's voice was dry as autumn leaves, but there was amusement in it, real and sharp. "My cooks nearly fainted. They've never seen anyone *test* food that way. We lost the art generations ago, when we lost our poisons with everything else."

"It's habit." Xanthe looked at her own hands, taster's hands, unscarred, unblistered. "I sip first. Always. Even water. Even bread. My tongue finds the bitterness under things — foxglove in honey, hemlock in wine. It always has." She hesitated. "In the valley they called it a gift."

"And what do you call it?"

Xanthe thought about it honestly, because lying to this woman seemed suddenly impossible — not forbidden, just *pointless*, the way you cannot lie to a mirror. "A wall," she said at last. "They built a wall into my mouth so nothing could get past it. And then they raised me inside the wall and called it safety."

Something moved through the queen's face — grief, or rage, or both wearing the same mask. "Yes," she said softly. "That is exactly what they did. That is exactly what *we* did." She turned her head against the pillow, and for a moment the burning dimmed, and she looked only tired, only old, only mortal in a way queens are not supposed to be. "Come here. Closer. Death has taken my hearing first and I refuse to miss what I say next."

Xanthe moved to the edge of the bed. Up close, her mother smelled of dried lavender and something underneath it, faint and wrong — the smell Xanthe had learned in the royal pantry, the smell of a body quietly poisoning itself from within. Not any poison she could name. Age, maybe. Or exile. Or a kingdom running out.

"Twenty-two years ago," the queen said, "I held you for three days. Three days, and then I gave you to a smuggler woman with salt-white braids and told her to take you somewhere magic could never find you. Somewhere the king's justiciars could never follow. Because there was a prophecy — there is *always* a prophecy — that a child born beyond all enchantment would one day stand at our door, and the door would know her, and open." Her hand crept across the quilt, paper over paper, until it found Xanthe's wrist. Her grip was nothing. Her grip was everything. "I sent you into a cage to save a country. I have regretted it every day since, and I would do it again tomorrow, and I think that is the truest definition of a queen there is."

"Isolde says you're dying."

"Isolde is honest to the point of rudeness. Yes. I am dying." She said it the way other people said *it's raining*. "Which brings me to what I say next, and why I needed you closer than arm's reach to say it."

* * *

The queen talked for a long time.

She talked about the door beneath the ninth river — how it was not stone but agreement, an accord older than either kingdom, sealed when the Ninefold Court chose exile over war. How it could be opened from either side but only by one who stood outside all magic: a person the enchantments could not see, could not touch, could not turn. How the valley's founders had built their poison-proofing, their numbness, their blessed blankness into a child on purpose — because a door needs a key, and keys must be made, not found.

"You were made," the queen said simply. "By us. Smuggled out. Raised by accident in the one place in the world that finished the work." A pause. "Bram finished it, I think. Whatever the alchemists began, that cook completed. Love does that. It finishes what power starts."

Xanthe's throat was tight. "And if I open it? What happens?"

"Then the Court comes home." The queen's eyes closed. "All nine thousand of us. The starving, the fading, the ones who remember flowers by humming to them. We pour back into the world through a door your king has spent twenty years pretending isn't there." She opened her eyes again, and now there was iron under the sea-glass. "He will call it an invasion, Xanthe. He will be right to call it an invasion. Nine thousand exiles do not come home gently, no matter how gently they are invited. There will be panic. There may be war. There will certainly be a justiciar named Corvane standing at the front of whatever comes, and I am told he is an honorable man, which makes him more dangerous than ten dishonorable ones."

"Then don't ask me." Xanthe heard her own voice crack on it. "Don't put this on me. You're asking me to choose between the only home I ever had and — and a mother I met four days ago. Between a king who fed me and a country that *made* me and then threw me away—"

"Yes," the queen said. "That is exactly what I'm asking."

The silence stretched between them like a drawn wire.

"I won't soften it," her mother went on, softer now. "I could lie to you. Tell you the Court only wants peace, that the king will welcome us with open arms, that no one will burn. But you are a taster, my darling. You were built to find the bitterness under things, and I will not insult you by sweetening this." Her hand tightened on Xanthe's wrist, that nothing-grip, that everything-grip. "I ask because you are the only one who can. And I ask knowing you may refuse. Refusal is also a choice, and I will love you under either one. That is the one thing I can give you that no crown ever could: I will love you *either way*."

Xanthe pulled her wrist free and stood up, because if she didn't move she was going to come apart right there at the foot of a dying woman's bed, and princesses — apparently — did not come apart. She walked to the window. Below, the Court glimmered. Children in the garden were singing to roses that bloomed in answer, and the song was thinning, year by year, note by note, like a fire running out of wood.

"How long do you have?" she asked the glass.

"Weeks. Perhaps days. The physicians disagree, which means neither of them knows."

"And if I say no? If I seal the door forever and go back to the valley and taste broth until I die of old age, numb and safe?"

"Then I die behind a locked door," her mother said, "and my people fade with me, and you live a long quiet life never quite knowing why your tongue tastes of ash." A breath. "And I will still have loved you either way."

* * *

She went down to the kitchens before dawn, because her hands needed work and her heart needed silence and the kitchens offered both.

It was a poor kitchen, by Salt Valley standards — no marble, no spice-rooms, no pantry guarded like a treasury. Just copper pots gone cloudy with age, a hearth that burned memory-light instead of wood, and shelves of food that looked abundant and wasn't: grain that had been regrown from the same seed for two decades, honey traded through the storm at ruinous cost, vegetables grown in gardens that bloomed only when remembered. The Court was starving the way the queen was starving. Slowly. Politely. To death.

Xanthe tied on an apron and started cooking.

Not court food. Not feast food. She cooked the way Bram had taught her — the way you feed one specific person on one specific bad night. She made a broth first, because broth is what you make when someone can no longer chew the world, and she made it slowly, skimming, tasting, adjusting.

Here was the thing about the taster's art that no one in the valley had ever understood: it was never really about poison. Poison was just bitterness with ambition. The art was in finding *everything* hidden in a thing — the sweetness buried under the salt, the warmth under the fat, the hundred small flavors that a careless cook let drown. Bram called it listening with your tongue. And Xanthe had spent twenty years listening to food the way other people listened to music, and she knew, without knowing how she knew, exactly what a body craved when it was shutting down.

Ginger, for the nausea. A whisper of honey, for the fear — sweetness goes straight to the frightened animal brain, Bram said, bypasses all the thinking parts. Bone stock cooked till it shook, for strength she couldn't build anymore but could borrow. And underneath it all, barely there, a thread of something bitter — wormwood, the Court's last vial of it, hoarded against pain — because the queen was hurting, had been hurting for months, and pride had kept her from saying so.

Xanthe balanced it the way she'd once balanced a poisoned broth meant for a king: precisely, tenderly, tasting and re-tasting until the bitterness was not erased but *held*, carried inside the sweetness like a stone carried inside a bird.

She carried it up on a tray with bread she'd baked herself — the Court's grain, the Court's honey, rising anyway, because dough forgives almost anything — and the queen was awake, propped on her pillows, watching the door.

"You cook," her mother said.

"Bram always said you can tell everything about a person by what they make for someone who's suffering."

"And what does this tell you about me?" Xanthe set the tray down.

"That you're in pain and haven't told anyone." She spooned broth carefully, blew across it, held it to her mother's lips. "Wormwood under the honey. Don't argue. Taster."

For a long moment the queen said nothing. Then she drank, one swallow, two, and Xanthe watched the pain leave her mother's face the way fog leaves a harbor — not all at once, but steadily, tide by tide. When the bowl was empty, the queen lay back, and her eyes were clear for the first time since Xanthe arrived.

"Twenty years," her mother murmured, "and the first thing my daughter gives me is ease." Her hand found Xanthe's again. "Whatever you decide — door open, door sealed, war, peace, salt, starlight — remember this morning. Remember that you already chose who you are. Everything else is just geography."

Xanthe bent her head over the joined hands, and breathed in lavender and wormwood and the end of things.

Outside the tower window, far off across the faded country, a light appeared on the horizon. Then another. Then a line of them, patient and orange, marching through the dark like a sentence being written.

Torches. An army's worth of torches, coming from the direction of the valley — and at their head, she would have sworn it even from here, a banner she knew from nightmares and market-square proclamations alike.

The king had found the door.
