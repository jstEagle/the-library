## 10. Salt and Starlight

The door did not open so much as remember itself.

One moment it was a seam of salt-white light in the cliff face above the ninth river, trembling under the king's fire-oil and Corvane's raised hand; the next it was simply *there*, tall as a cathedral gate, carved with nine rivers that flowed in slow silver relief across its surface. Xanthe stood before it with her palms bleeding where she had pressed them against stone that drank her immunity like wine.

She felt it leave her.

Not pain — nothing so simple. It was as if a coat she had worn since birth were being unpicked thread by thread, and every thread had been a wall between her and the world. The numbness went first, that blessed blankness that had let her sip poison like spring water. Then the flatness beneath it, the gray quiet where feeling should have been. What rushed in to fill the space was everything at once: the bite of smoke from the army's torches, the green smell of the river, terror, grief, hope so sharp it was nearly indistinguishable from fear.

She tasted the air, and for the first time in her life, it had a flavor.

Behind her, Corvane held the pass alone. She heard the justiciars' boots, their shouted orders, the scrape of his sword clearing its sheath — one man against thirty, buying her ninety seconds with his honor and probably his life.

"Go, girl," he called, not turning. "And tell your mother—" His voice caught on the word, twenty years of buried conscience surfacing all at once. "Tell her Veyran Corvane kept faith with someone, in the end."

She ran through the door.

* * *

The Ninefold Court received her the way the sea receives a river: without ceremony, without resistance, as though she had always belonged to it and had merely been away.

The land beyond was dying and beautiful at once — nine rivers braiding toward a white palace whose towers leaned like old men, orchards heavy with fruit no one had strength to harvest, streets swept clean by people who refused to stop sweeping even as they faded. The starving country. The erased one. Its people came out of doorways as she passed, drawn by some tide in the air, and they looked at her the way she had once looked at the map: at something that should not exist and yet did.

The queen was waiting in a kitchen.

Of course she was. Not the throne room with its ninefold stars wheeling on a ceiling of lapis, but the long low kitchen at the palace's heart, where a fire still burned because fires are the last thing a dying household surrenders. Queen Ysolde — and Xanthe would learn later, laughing, that Isolde of the caravan carried a smuggled echo of that name — lay propped on cushions by the hearth, small and silver-haired, her face so much like the face Xanthe saw in mirrors that her breath stopped in her throat.

"You have her hands," the queen said, when Xanthe could speak. "My taster's hands. I used to press my thumb into broths the way you do. I watched you do it in the vision the river gave me, years ago, and I wept into the stock." She smiled. "The cooks thought it was the onions."

"I don't understand any of this."

"No. But you will sit down, and you will eat, and you will understand it by degrees, which is the only way anything worth understanding arrives." The queen's voice frayed at the edges but the iron underneath was unmistakable — the same iron that had smuggled an infant out through a storm of erasure rather than watch her daughter fade. "You came. Twenty years I told myself you wouldn't. It made it easier."

Xanthe sat. Her legs simply gave up pretending.

"The king's army is at the door," she said. "Corvane is holding it, but he can't hold it long. They mean to burn it. They'll burn everyone inside."

"Then we had better be quick about living." The queen reached out and took her daughter's hands — the taster's hands, the poison-proof hands, or what remained of them. "The valley gave you its gift, child. Did you spend it?"

"At the door. It wanted it back. It wanted—"

"It wanted you *openable*," the queen said softly. "Yes. A door sealed by magic can only be opened by one who carries none. That was always the shape of it. That was why we sent you there, my love. Not to save you from the Court." Her thumb traced the birthmark at Xanthe's wrist, the nine-pointed star she had borne all her life without knowing its name. "To save the Court with you."

Outside, faintly, the first horn of the king's army sounded off the cliffs.

* * *

They signed the treaty in the kitchen, because the throne room's ceiling was crumbling and because Queen Ysolde refused, on principle, to negotiate her people's return from anywhere but the place where food was made. "Kings make war in halls," she said, waving off her chamberlain's protests. "Peace gets made where someone is feeding people. Always has been."

So it happened around a scarred oak table dusted with flour: a dying exiled queen wrapped in three shawls; a princess who until yesterday had been a kitchen girl; and across from them, arriving under a banner of parley with ash on his cloak and blood on his knuckles, Justiciar First Rank Veyran Corvane, who had held the pass long enough for the parley horns to sound, and who now stood in the doorway of a foreign kitchen looking like a man who had walked into the middle of his own conscience.

"Your Majesty," he said, and bowed — lower than protocol required, lower than he had ever bowed to his own king.

"Justiciar." The queen studied him a long moment. "You served at the border once. Before they burned it from the maps. You were a young man, and you saw what lived beyond the salt line, and when they ordered you to forget it, you obeyed." She tilted her head. "But forgetting isn't the same as believing. You never quite believed."

"No, Your Majesty." His voice was rough. "I've spent twenty years not quite believing. It's an uncomfortable way to live. I recommend against it."

"And yet here you stand, having turned your sword against your own king's order."

"He wasn't going to burn a door, Your Majesty. He was going to burn a *country*. There were children in those streets." Corvane's jaw worked. "I've done hard things in the king's name. I couldn't do that one. Call it treason if you like. I find I prefer 'correction.'"

Xanthe watched the two of them — the hunter and the hunted queen, the man who had ordered her watched and the woman who had ordered her smuggled — and understood that history does not turn on battles. It turns in kitchens, over tables, among tired people deciding what they will and will not carry anymore.

The treaty itself was short. The Ninefold Court would return to the world openly, its borders restored, its name written back into every chart and chronicle. In exchange, the Court would open its granaries to the drought-stricken southern provinces — for the valley's enchantment, stretched thin by the door's opening, had finally failed, and Salt Valley's famous poison-proofing would fail with it, and the kingdom would need friends who knew how to make bread from starlight and river-water. The queen dictated; Xanthe wrote, in a careful taster's script; Corvane witnessed, signing with the hand that had held the pass.

"There," the queen said, when the wax had cooled. "The first treaty of the new age, drafted beside the soup pot. Let the chroniclers choke on it."

Then her eyes closed, and she did not wake for two days.

* * *

The poisoning, when it came, nearly killed Xanthe.

She learned afterward that it had come in a gift-basket from a border lord who preferred the old arrangement, in which the Ninefold Court stayed a rumor and its queen stayed dead — a basket of candied plums laced with something the south called widow's-kiss, subtle and patient and cruel. In her old life she would have eaten one absently and felt nothing at all.

Now she ate one and felt everything.

The pain was astonishing. It climbed her throat like a creature with intent, and she sat down hard on the kitchen floor, gasping, tasting death in layers — bitter almond underneath, hemlock's cold iron above it, and beneath both something older, a flavor like the inside of a sealed tomb. She thought, absurdly, of Bram. Of how many times she had swallowed such things and felt nothing, and called that safety.

Bram. She hadn't even been able to say goodbye. He had shoved her into the root cellar while justiciars tore apart the kitchen above, and pressed a sack of bread into her hands, and said, "Go taste the world, then, you impossible girl," and that was the last she'd seen of him. If the valley's enchantment had truly failed—

Later. Grieve later. Survive first.

Her hands knew what to do. Twenty years of training lived below thought. Mustard seed — the pantry of the Court kept it, old habit of an old paranoid court — chewed, swallowed, fighting the toxin for ground. Milk, salt, charcoal from the hearth. She crawled, vomited, crawled again, and somewhere in the worst of it the queen was there — awake now, furious, terrified, kneeling on a kitchen floor in her nightgown holding her daughter's head the way mothers have held their poisoned children since before either country existed.

"You're mortal now," the queen said afterward, when Xanthe lay pale and alive on a cot by the fire. "Do you regret it? The trade?"

Xanthe considered. Her body ached everywhere. The widow's-kiss had left a scorch down her throat that would take weeks to heal. And when she breathed, she could smell the woodsmoke and the lavender and the rain starting outside, all at once, distinct as voices in a choir. When she moved, she felt the ache and the warmth of the blanket and, underneath everything, a low bright hum that she slowly recognized as happiness.

"I regret the plum," she said. "Nothing else."

The queen laughed, and then cried, and then laughed again, and Xanthe held her mother's hands and tasted salt — real salt, her own tears, the valley's oldest gift returned at last in the only form worth keeping.

* * *

They asked her, in the spring, what name she would take.

It was custom in the Ninefold Court: names were chosen, not given, taken up like offices when a person became who they meant to be. The heralds wanted something grand. *Princess Xanthe of the Nine Rivers*, they suggested. *The Returned*. *Starborn*. She declined them all in the queen's kitchen, where she had, by quiet acclamation, become the mistress of the household — not a servant's role in this country, but an honored one; the person who fed the queen, who tasted for the table now not out of necessity but devotion, who had turned the taster's art from a shield into a language of care.

"What then?" the queen asked, mending a napkin by the window. Spring light came through it the color of honey.

Xanthe looked around the kitchen — at the copper pots, at Corvane teaching the scullery boys knife-work in the yard because the man could not stop being a justiciar even in retirement, at the door where Isolde had arrived that morning with her caravan, grinning, saying she'd always known there was a country at the end of her route and demanding back-pay for her intuition. At the table where treaties were signed and soup was salted and her mother sat wrapped in shawls, alive, alive, alive.

"Fairweather," she said.

The queen's needle stopped. "That's the name the valley gave you."

"That's the name the *people* gave me. Bram gave it. He found me nameless in a kitchen and he called me Fairweather because I was good luck in bad skies." She smiled, and felt the smile all the way through her, every muscle of it, a luxury she would never tire of. "I was born twice, Mother. Once in this palace, once in that kitchen. I won't pretend the first birth cancels the second. Xanthe Fairweather. Taster to the Ninefold Court. Let the heralds write it however fits on the page."

Outside, the nine stars were fading into morning, giving way one by one to the sun — the same sun that rose over Salt Valley, over the road Isolde would ride home, over a cook named Bram who was owed a visit, a letter, an apology, a job offer, whatever a woman could give the man who taught her that a kitchen was a kind of country all its own.

The kettle began to sing. Xanthe rose to meet it, tasting the morning, feeding her family.
