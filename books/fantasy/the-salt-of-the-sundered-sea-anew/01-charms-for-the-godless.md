## 1. Charms for the Godless

The night market of Vessamare smelled of tar, fried eel, and the particular desperation of people who had learned to want small things. Xanthe Oyelaran had set up his stall between a woman who sold candles made from rendered gull-fat and a man whose entire inventory was three barrels of pickled whelks and a smile that promised nothing good. It was a good spot. Close enough to the harbor that the dockworkers passed on their way home with coin still warm in their pockets, far enough from the Salt Row shrines that no one with official eyes would bother to look twice at what he sold.

He never sold magic. That was the first law of his survival, carved into him so deep it had become less a rule than a bone. He sold *charms*—knots and braids and folded paper, little objects that any grandmother in any village might make, carrying meanings that were old enough to be folklore rather than heresy.

A luck-knot was just a knot. Everyone knew that.

"Three coppers," he said, holding it out across the plank counter.

Brannick the stevedore—Xanthe knew his name because Brannick told everyone his name, loudly, whether they wanted it or not—took the knot and turned it over in hands like cured hams. The knot itself was simple work: three strands of cord, one dyed the gray-green of harbor water, braided into a pattern that had no beginning and no end if you looked at it right. Which was the point. If you looked at it wrong—if you looked at it the way Xanthe looked at it—it was a map. A very small map, of a very specific place: the safe channel through the shoals below the Gull's Teeth, where the tide ran strange and boats went missing every winter.

It was also, incidentally and deniably, lucky.

"You sure this'll hold?" Brannick asked, as he asked every time, working the cord between finger and thumb.

"It held last month," Xanthe said. "And the month before."

"That was the blue one."

"The blue one was the same knot in a different color." Xanthe kept his voice flat, bored, the voice of a tradesman discussing rope. "You came back, didn't you?"

Brannick grinned, gap-toothed under a beard full of yesterday's supper, and looped the cord around his thick neck. "Aye. Came back." He dropped three coppers onto the plank, hesitated, added a fourth. "For the wife. She's been sleeping bad since the fever took the Ondrell boy down the lane."

"Fever's not something knots fix," Xanthe said, and heard how quickly the words came out of him, how much care was packed inside the denial. Too quick. Too careful. A man selling mere trinkets wouldn't have an opinion about what fevers did or didn't do. He softened it with a shrug. "But she can hang it by the window. Something to look at besides the wall."

Brannick grunted agreement and lumbered off into the smoke and lantern-light, and Xanthe exhaled slowly and rearranged his stock of nothing-much: knotted cords, folded paper stars, river-stones painted with spirals that were decorative, obviously, purely decorative. The fourth copper sat on the plank where Brannick had left it. Xanthe left it there a moment longer than he needed to, because taking money for a blessing—even a fake one—was the kind of line you didn't cross until you absolutely had to.

Around him the market breathed. Lamplighters worked the oil-lamps strung between poles, their flames shivering in the salt wind off the Sundered Sea. Somewhere a fiddler played a tune with more notes missing than present, the gaps filled by laughter. Children chased each other between stalls, and a fishwife argued with a customer about the freshness of mackerel with the kind of theological intensity usually reserved for scripture.

Vessamare had been built on faith once. Now it was built on fish and freight and fear, and only two of those were legal.

Xanthe had been in the city three years. Long enough to know which streets flooded when the tide lied about its schedule. Long enough to have a face the market guards skimmed past without seeing. Long enough to be nobody, thoroughly, expertly, in a city where being somebody with his particular talents meant a cell beneath the Citadel and a witch-hunter asking questions in a room with drains in the floor.

He was twenty-six years old, and he could not have told you what he'd been doing at twenty-two. Not truly. There was a shape where those years should have been, smooth and featureless as sea-ice, and he had learned—not easily, not all at once—to stop pressing his thumb against it the way you press a bruise. The memory-broker in the Undercity had called the procedure *lightening the cargo*. Xanthe had walked out of that dim shop with fewer years behind him and a forged history in his head that fit like borrowed boots: close enough to walk in, never quite his own stride.

What remained was the gift, because the gift lived in his hands and his eyes, not his memory. He could look at a stretch of empty air or open water or the space between one breath and the next, and see the seams. The veil that hung over the world after the Sundering—the great sundering of the sea, when the old gods' war had torn the ocean floor and drowned half the coastline and given the city its name and its laws—was not invisible to him. It was a texture. A weave. And where the weave thinned, whole realms pressed through, places that existed at angles to Vessamare, unmapped, unnameable, hungry.

He drew them. That was the compulsion underneath everything else, the thing the memory-broker couldn't take without taking Xanthe himself. Charts of places no ship could sail and no pilgrim could walk, inked in secret on paper he burned or buried or sewed into his coat lining. Three years of hidden masterwork, drawn by lamplight with the shutters closed, maps of nowhere that were more real to him than the city above them.

The luck-knots paid the rent. The maps kept him sane. Neither, ever the twain.

* * *

She came to the stall near midnight, when the crowd had thinned to drunks and insomniacs and the candle-seller had begun packing her unsold fat-lights back into their basket with the tenderness other women reserved for infants.

Xanthe saw her coming before she reached him—a woman moving with the terrible straight-backed purpose of someone who has run out of other options—and his stomach dropped before she said a word. He knew that walk. He had seen it perhaps a dozen times in three years, and each time he had watched it approach with the same cold dread, the way a sailor watches a waterspout form on the horizon.

She stopped at his stall. Fortyish, maybe younger; grief ages people unpredictably. Her dress was clean but worn shiny at the cuffs, a laundress or a mender's dress, hands reddened from lye. Behind her eyes was the look he dreaded most—not hope, exactly. Hope could be deflected. This was the stage past hope, where hope curdled into demand.

"You're the charm-man," she said. "They say your charms work."

"They say wrong," Xanthe said. His voice did its job: pleasant, apologetic, already closing the door. "Knots and paper, mistress. Nothing more. For luck against the tide and safe roads."

"My son is dying."

The words landed in the space between them and neither of them moved, the way neither moves when lightning strikes close.

"He's eleven," she went on, and now the words came faster, tumbling, rehearsed and unrehearsed at once. "Mara. The physicians bled him twice and the fever-priest—" she caught herself; even desperate mothers used the careful word, *physician*, because *priest* was a word that got houses searched— "the physician says it's in his lungs now, and there's nothing—" Her hand came down on the plank counter and gripped it, knuckles white. "They say your knots work. Old Jorey swears his boat hasn't shipped water since he bought your cord. They *say*—"

"They say," Xanthe agreed quietly. "People say many things."

Inside his chest, his heart was doing something complicated and painful. Because here was the truth he could never say aloud: the knots weren't nothing. The knots were the smallest, safest expression of a craft that could reshape reality's geometry, and sometimes—rarely, deniably—a knot tied by his hands did nudge the world, the way a pebble nudges a landslide. And here was the second truth, the one that mattered: he could not afford to be the man who healed a child in public. One miracle witnessed was a rumor. Two was an investigation. The Sundering purges had begun exactly this way, with grateful mothers telling neighbors about a charm-man whose luck-cords kept sailors alive, and had ended with pyres on the seawall that burned for a week.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I sell trinkets. If I could save him, mistress, don't you think I'd have saved my own—" He stopped. He didn't know what he'd almost said. *My own family* rose in him like a bubble through dark water and burst before reaching air, leaving nothing behind, no name, no face, just the ache of a shape that didn't match anything he remembered. He blinked. The woman was watching him strangely.

"There are others," she whispered. "On Salt Row. They say the old shrines... they say someone answered a prayer at the Tide-Mother's altar last week. Bread for a starving family. Real bread. And the fever breaking in the Foundling House, all at once, forty children, and no physician could explain—"

"Superstition," Xanthe said, too fast again. "Old stories. The gods are gone. Everyone knows the gods are gone."

Everyone knew it. It was the first fact of the age, taught to children alongside their letters: the Sundering broke the world and killed its gods, and what survived of divinity was outlawed superstition, and the Crown's witch-hunters kept it that way. Ninety years of silence from heaven. Ninety years of the sea lying flat and dead where miracles used to walk.

So why, then, was his skin crawling? Why did the candle-flames along the market row all lean, just slightly, toward the woman, as if listening?

"Please." Her voice cracked. She leaned across the counter and grabbed his wrist, her grip shockingly strong, lye-reddened fingers closing over the pulse point. "I'm begging you. I have nothing left to lose. Take everything I have. Just—try. Whatever you do, whatever they say you are. *Try.*"

Her touch burned. Not metaphorically—or rather, yes, metaphorically, but metaphors had weight in Xanthe's hands, always had. Through her skin he felt it faintly, the way he felt the veil everywhere in this city: a thinning. Around this woman, around her grief, the weave of the world had gone soft as wet paper. As if her need were wearing a hole in it.

And through the hole, faint as a smell of smoke from a distant fire, came something looking back.

Xanthe pulled his arm free. Harder than he meant to; she stumbled.

"No," he said. "Get out. Get away from me, and don't come back, and don't tell anyone you asked me this. Do you hear me? If the wrong person hears you saying these things—bread multiplying, prayers answered—they'll take you in for questioning, and the questions they ask in the Citadel don't stop when you answer." He was leaning forward now, gripping the edge of the counter, and he hated the fear in his own voice because it wasn't performance. "Your son needs a physician. Go find one. Sell your shoes, sell your hair, sell anything, but don't stand in a night market talking about answered prayers. That talk gets people hollowed out and hung on the seawall. Go *home*."

For a moment she stared at him, and he thought she would cry, or curse him, or both. Instead something worse happened: understanding. Her eyes went over his face, his stall, his carefully deniable wares, and whatever she found there made her step back.

"You're frightened," she said wonderingly. "You're more frightened than I am."

"Go home, mistress."

She went. He watched her go, small in the lantern-light, swallowed by the crowd, and then he stood with both hands flat on his counter, breathing, until his heart remembered its rhythm.

The candle-seller glanced over. "Rough customer?"

"Rough night," Xanthe said, and began packing up early, because his hands had started to shake, and because the flames were still leaning, all of them, gently, steadily, westward—toward the sea, toward Salt Row, toward the drowned altars—as though the whole market were a congregation turning toward a pulpit.

* * *

He told himself he would not follow her. He told himself this while he closed the stall, while he counted the night's coppers into a pouch, while he walked the long way home along the canal to avoid passing the mouth of her street. He told himself he had survived three years precisely by knowing which doors not to knock on, and that a dying boy in a rented room was a door with the Citadel standing behind it.

Then he took the turn toward Salt Row anyway, and hated himself with a fluency that suggested long practice.

He told himself it was reconnaissance. If prayers were being answered in Vessamare—if the impossible word *answered* had crept back into the city's vocabulary—then it concerned him whether he liked it or not, because he was possibly the only man in Vessamare who knew what answers prayers in this drowned corner of the world. He had charted it. Years ago, in another life, with hands that remembered things his mind had sold away: a survey of the Sundered Sea, the deepwater regions where the veil hung in rags, where the old god-things that had survived their own war lay coiled in the dark under the drowned coast. He knew what lived out there. He knew that it did not love. He knew, in the part of himself that was pure cartographer and therefore incapable of comfort, that anything answering prayers in Vessamare was not doing it out of kindness.

Kindness was not a currency the old things understood.

Salt Row climbed the seaward bluff in crooked steps, tenements stacked like driftwood, laundry lines sagging between buildings like rigging on some doomed ship. The woman's building was easy to find: a knot of neighbors on the stoop, murmuring, faces lit from within by the sickroom's candle-glow. Xanthe kept to the shadow of a rain barrel across the lane, hands in his coat pockets, fingers touching the folded charts hidden in the lining without quite meaning to, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth.

Through a window on the third floor, a rectangle of amber light. A silhouette bent over a bed. Small. Still.

He waited. He was good at waiting. The tide muttered against the seawall below, and somewhere a dog was barking at nothing, and the wind off the Sundered Sea carried the smell of deep water and old salt and, underneath, so faint he might have invented it, something like incense. Like a temple that hadn't burned down yet.

The window went dark. Then, minutes later, light again—candlelight spilling out as a door opened somewhere above, voices, footsteps on the stairs. The mother emerged into the street, and even across the lane Xanthe saw that she was swaying, held up by two neighbors, and for one black instant he thought: *dead, the boy is dead, she came to me and I sent her away and the boy is dead.*

Then he saw her face.

She was laughing. Laughing and weeping in the same breath, pressing her hands to her mouth, and the neighbors were crowding in, and someone shouted up at the building, and a window opened, and a voice—a child's voice, thin, wrecked, but *alive*—called down into the street:

"Mama? Why's everyone shouting?"

The lane erupted. Neighbors fell on each other. Someone began ringing a pot with a spoon. And the mother turned, in the middle of it, and looked directly across the lane at the shadow where Xanthe stood—looked at him with wet, blazing eyes, and raised one hand, not in thanks but in *accusation*, or benediction, or both, and Xanthe stepped back into the dark with his heart slamming against his ribs because he had done nothing, he had refused her, he had sent her away untouched.

He had done nothing. So whatever had pulled that boy back from the edge of the grave had not needed his permission, or his craft, or his cowardice. It had simply taken the prayer she'd carried all night, the one she'd finally spoken aloud to a stranger in a night market, and it had answered.

The boy's voice drifted down the stairwell, thin and wondering, carried on the salt wind to the shadow where Xanthe stood frozen:

*"Who's Ossuar? I dreamed about him. He said he knows my name."*

The pot-ringing stopped. The wind died all at once, the whole lane going still as held breath. And out over the black water of the Sundered Sea, far beyond the seawall, beyond the shoals, beyond the edge of any chart Xanthe had ever dared finish, something vast turned over in its sleep—and, for the first time in ninety years, opened its eye.

Xanthe walked home fast, not running, because running attracted attention, but wanting to. In his rooms he barred the door, lit no lamp, and sat in the dark with his hands flat on his knees, feeling them tremble.

Ossuar.

He knew that name. He had written it himself, once, in ink he'd mixed from drowned-earth and squid-black, at the center of a chart of a place where no city had ever stood—before he'd sold the year in which he'd drawn it to a broker with soft hands, before the map became a hole in his life shaped exactly like a map. He couldn't see the chart anymore. But his hands remembered its weight, and his hands were shaking now with a recognition his mind could not share.

The gods were gone. Everyone knew the gods were gone.

Someone, out there in the dark beneath the sundered sea, had evidently not received the news.
