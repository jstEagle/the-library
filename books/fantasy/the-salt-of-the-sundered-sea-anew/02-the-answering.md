## 2. The Answering

The rumors came to Vessamare the way fog came off the harbor—thin at first, then everywhere at once, until you could not remember the city without it.

Xanthe heard the first of them on a Tuesday, from a fishwife who had come to his stall not for charms but for shelter from the rain. She stood under the patched awning of his cart in the Salt Row market, wringing brine out of her skirts and talking because silence was worse.

"They say Mother Ilset's bread did it again," she said. "Third loaf turned into seven. In front of witnesses. Her own priest swears he only prayed for it."

"Prayers don't do anything," Xanthe said, keeping his voice flat as slate. "That's the whole point of living here."

The fishwife gave him a look that said *you sell luck-knots for two coppers, boy, don't preach atheism at me*, but she let it go. Rain drummed the awning. Down the row, a lamplighter made his rounds early against the gloom, and his flame guttered twice before it caught, which everyone knew was an omen, though no one agreed anymore about of what.

Xanthe sold her a knot anyway—one woven with river-reed and a sliver of green glass—and told her it would keep her nets full. It wouldn't. It couldn't. The knots were theater, misdirection, a conjurer's flourish built over nothing at all, because nothing was all the magic anyone in Vessamare was permitted to have. The Sundering had seen to that. Three generations of law written in ash: no working, no warding, no naming. The witch-hunters still walked their rounds with iron scales and longer memories.

But when the fishwife left, he sat with his hands flat on his cart's scarred wood and thought about what she'd said. Bread multiplying. In front of witnesses.

There were tricks for that. Cheap ones. He'd sold a dozen illusions less impressive.

He told himself it was one of those, and almost believed it.

* * *

By Friday, the rumors had stopped being rumors and started being lists.

Xanthe kept a ledger like any honest tradesman—sales, debts, the price of reed and glass and dyed cord—and by week's end the margins of it had filled with a different kind of accounting. He wrote them down the way he wrote everything, in his small precise hand, because writing a thing down made it data instead of dread:

*Fever broke at the Foundling House on Cordwainer Lane. Eleven children. The matron had prayed all night.*

*A drowned net-mender walked out of the harbor after two days. His wife had gone to the old tide-shrine—the ruined one, the illegal one—and wept on the stones.*

*Mother Ilset's loaves again. Now they speak of it at the Cathedral, though the priests there call it devilry and post watchers at her door.*

*Old Tam Wicke, who hadn't spoken since his daughter drowned, sang a hymn at dawn in perfect voice. Then sat down in the square and hasn't moved since.*

It was the last one that made him stop chewing.

Xanthe read it back three times. Around him the night market roared on—fried squid and cheap wine, a knife-grinder's wheel singing, someone's argument about dice turning physical two stalls down—but he heard none of it. He was looking at the words *hasn't moved since* and feeling something cold walk up the back of his neck on familiar feet.

He closed the ledger. Opened it. Read it again.

Then he went home through streets slick with rain, past the shuttered shrine on Tide Street with its desecrated carvings, past the witch-hunter post where two men in grey played cards by lantern light, and climbed the four flights to his room above the chandler's shop, and locked the door, and did something he had not done in three years.

He opened the floor.

* * *

The boards came up along a seam he'd cut himself, hidden under the rug, invisible unless you knew to look for the sawdust ghost of it. Beneath lay a shallow cavity lined with oilcloth, and in the oilcloth lay his masterwork: a chart of the Sundered Sea as it had been, drawn in inks mixed with ground shell and his own blood, because some things wanted to be drawn true or not at all.

He had not unrolled it since the night he'd bought his way out of the purges. He'd sworn an oath over its folded weight—an oath to himself, worth exactly as much as he was, which had been very little at the time—that he would never look at it again. Looking was half the crime. A chart was a weapon; every hunter who'd ever burned a mapmaker had said so with a straight face and a lit torch.

But Old Tam Wicke hadn't moved since.

Xanthe knelt on his cold floor and unrolled the chart across the room, weighting the corners with candlesticks and his boots and the chamber pot, until the pre-Sundering sea spread beneath him in all its drowned glory. Harbors inked in gold that no longer existed. Shipping lanes drawn in a hand steadier than his present one. And along the deep-water margins, in the cipher-script he'd invented at nineteen and never taught another living soul, the annotations of a young man who had believed knowledge was safety.

His eyes went where they had always gone. To the place he had marked with three concentric rings and a word.

The word was not Ossuar. He had never learned the god's name; names like that were not given, they were *found*, and finding one was the work of a lifetime he no longer had. But he had marked the location itself—a trench beyond the drowned harbor, deeper than soundings, where the old charts showed currents moving wrong. Currents that circled. Currents that, if you read them right, formed a pattern.

A pattern that answered.

Because that was the thing he had understood at nineteen, kneeling over this same vellum with ink on his fingers and arrogance in his heart, and had spent three years trying to forget: the sea here had never been empty. The Sundering hadn't killed what lived below the sundered water. It had only buried the doors. And things that are buried behind doors do not die. They listen. Through stone, through salt, through the thin places where shrines used to stand—they listen for the one sound that carries through everything.

They listen for prayer.

And they answer.

The chart's margin note, in his younger self's looping cipher, read: *"Where the current circles, the listener waits. Do not pray within forty leagues. It does not give. It trades."*

Xanthe sat back on his heels, and the room seemed to tilt around the chart like a ship coming about.

Bread multiplied. Fevers broke. A dead man walked.

And Old Tam Wicke sang, and then stopped moving, because the trade had been collected.

He thought of the hollow-eyed beggars he'd seen lately near the ruins of the tide-shrine—there were more of them every week, sitting in doorways, breathing, blinking, present the way furniture is present. He'd taken them for the usual wreckage of a port city. Grief, gin, the gray sickness of the poor. Everyone had. That was the genius of it, he realized with a nausea that rose slow and total from his stomach to his scalp. No one looked twice at the emptied, because Vessamare was *full* of the emptied. The god—or whatever it was—had chosen a hunting ground where its leavings would never be missed.

It wasn't answering prayers out of mercy. It wasn't even answering them out of hunger, not exactly. He stared at the circling currents, at the rings around the trench, and understood it the way he understood coastlines: structurally, completely, horribly. Prayer was a knock on a door. Every knock weakened the wood. Every answered prayer was a hinge swinging wider, and each worshipper who walked through—gave themselves over in gratitude, poured devotion into the dark—was a brick carried out of the wall, one soul at a time, by the faithful themselves.

Three years of hiding, and the wall was coming down anyway. Not because anyone had broken the law. Because people were *praying*. Quietly, desperately, in kitchens and sickrooms, in the way human beings prayed when the law offered them nothing—which was to say constantly, which was to say always.

The witch-hunters couldn't stop it. You couldn't arrest a whisper to a ceiling.

Xanthe got up on numb legs and lit every candle he owned, because the room had gone dim without his noticing, and then he sat cross-legged beside the forbidden chart and did the most dangerous thing he had done since the purges.

He began to plot.

Not crimes—coordinates. He took the ledger with its list of miracles, and beside each one he wrote the location: Cordwainer Lane, the harbor, Tide Street, the square. Eleven points of answered prayer scattered across the city like pins in cloth. And when he drew lines between them—not on the masterwork, never on the masterwork, but on a scrap of wrapping paper with charcoal—he felt his gift wake in him like a muscle long bound, stretching, remembering itself.

The lines converged.

Of course they converged. Everything in Vessamare drained downhill toward the sea, and the sea drained into the trench, and in the trench something old had put its ear against the world's skin and was listening with its whole vast patience, and the city above it had begun, unknowingly, to sing to it.

The convergence point fell just off the quay. Just past the drowned harbor's edge. Forty feet of black water, and below that the sunken gates of a harbor that had been the pride of the world before the Sundering swallowed it, and below *that*—

Below that, the rings on the chart. The listener. The thing his younger self had named in cipher with a boy's confidence and an old man's terror: *it does not give, it trades.*

Xanthe blew out the candles one by one and rolled the chart away and pressed the boards back into the floor, and then he sat in the dark at his little table with his head in his hands, listening to the rain, and admitted it to himself at last, plainly, the way you admit a diagnosis:

He knew what answered prayers in this city. He had known since Friday. Possibly since Tuesday. And knowing was no longer a thing he could set down and walk away from, because he had seen the list, and the list had a shape, and shapes were his trade, and a man who could see the shape of a thing and did nothing was not neutral. He was furniture.

Somewhere across the rooftops, a bell began to ring—not the alarm bell, not yet, just the midnight toll—and under it he heard, or imagined he heard, the far-off groan of the sea against the seawall, patient as breath.

Old Tam Wicke hadn't moved since.

Tomorrow, Xanthe decided, he would go and look at him. Tomorrow he would count the hollow-eyed in the doorways, properly count them, and compare the number against last month's. Tomorrow he would start a new page in the ledger.

Tonight he only sat in the dark and listened to the rain, and hated how clearly, across three years of careful forgetting, he could still hear his own young voice saying the margin-note aloud, ink-wet and certain, like a prophecy he'd written for exactly this night:

*It does not give. It trades.*

And somewhere out past the drowned harbor, in the trench where the current circled, something heard him think it—and, he would have sworn ever after, something *answered*.
