## 3. A Map of What Was Drowned

The boy's name had been Pellin. Xanthe learned it two days later, in the way he learned most things now—overheard, sideways, carried on the salt wind that came off the Sundered Sea and threaded through Vessamare like a rumor looking for a room. The fever had broken. The boy had woken at dawn and asked for bread and honey, and his mother had wept into her apron, and by noon half the Salt Row market was calling it a miracle.

By nightfall they were calling it something else too, because Pellin had said a word when he woke. A word no one taught him. A word that made the priestess-wives cross themselves with salt and made the harbor-master close his shutters early.

Xanthe sat alone above his shop with the shutters drawn and the lamps unlit, listening to the word repeat itself in his skull like water dripping into a well.

*Ossuar.*

He knew it. That was the terrible part. He knew it the way he knew coastlines he'd never sailed—intimately, precisely, from the inside out—and he could not remember why.

Three years ago he would have known. Three years ago he had been someone who knew things, whole continents of knowing, before he'd begun selling them off piece by piece to keep breathing. What remained was the shape of the knowledge without its substance: an old chart-shaped hole in him, and the certainty that the answer was already drawn somewhere, in his own hand, in ink that had cost more than gold.

There was only one place it could be.

---

The floorboards under his bed were pine, cheap, splintered at the edges—the kind of boards a landlord installed because they expected the tenant to be gone within a season. Xanthe had been gone three times. He always came back, because nowhere else in Vessamare would rent to a man whose eyes did certain things in lamplight if he wasn't careful.

He pried up the third board from the left, the one that groaned, and lifted out the oilcloth bundle wrapped around a core of dried kelp to confuse any diviner foolish enough to scry for it. His hands shook. He noticed the shaking with a kind of clinical detachment, the way he noticed weather.

*You haven't touched your true work in three years,* said a voice in him that sounded like his own but older, like his own left out in the rain. *And now you're going to do it in a city where they hang people for less.*

"They hanged the priests," he murmured aloud, because saying things aloud kept him anchored to being a man who said things. "They hanged the mages. They've never once hanged a mapmaker."

The bundle was heavier than memory. He set it on the drafting table—the one real possession he owned, salvaged oak with a brass edge worn silver by his forearms—and untied the knots slowly, giving himself a hundred small chances to stop.

He didn't stop. That was the other terrible part. Underneath the fear, underneath even the dread that had been sitting in his chest since Pellin woke up speaking, there was something bright and hungry unfurling, something that had waited three years in the dark of him like a seed under salt.

The oilcloth fell open.

The chart breathed.

That was the only word for it. Lamplight moved across the vellum the way it never moved across honest paper, pooling in the bays, running silver along the inked currents as though the sea depicted still remembered being a sea. This was the masterwork. The last thing he had made with his whole self, before the purges, before the broker, before Vessamare. A chart of the Sundered Sea as it had been before the Sundering—not the gray drowned waste that lapped at the seawall now, but the living water, the harbors and holy isles, the pilgrimage lanes marked in gold leaf, the deep-sea trenches named in a script that had been outlawed along with every tongue that spoke it.

Cities stood on this map. Whole cities, their spires rendered in strokes fine as eyelashes, each one labeled in his younger hand: *Vessa-of-the-Nine-Tides. Ondrach. The Pearl Synod of Ilmaren.* All drowned now. All erased from every legal chart in the kingdom, redrawn as open water, as nothing, as *never was.*

Xanthe spread his palms flat on either side of it, not touching, the way you approach a sleeping animal or a lit fuse.

"Hello," he whispered, absurdly, to a piece of vellum. "Hello, old friend."

The lamplight shivered. Or his hand did.

He began, as he always began, at the compass rose in the corner—his signature, the eight-point star he'd invented at nineteen and never told anyone meant something else, meant the eight names of the sea's oldest god, hidden in plain sight on a hundred charts that witch-hunters had held to the light and found innocent. Then north along the coast. Then out, past the shallows, to where the pre-Sundering harbor of Vessa-of-the-Nine-Tides had opened its arms to pilgrim fleets—

And there it was.

Where no city stood. Where, on the modern charts hanging in the Admiralty, there was nothing but the featureless blue-gray of the Sundering's deepest scar, his younger self had inked a name. Not the name of a port. Not a depth-sounding or a hazard notation. It curved across the empty water in letters tall as his thumb, letters he recognized the way you recognize your own scream:

*OSSUAR.*

Beneath it, smaller, in the margin-script only cartographers of the veil had used—a language of tides and thresholds—he had written a legend. His hands moved over it now without touching, reading what he'd written and remembering almost none of writing it, the way you wake with a poem in your mouth that isn't yours.

*"Here the Sea-God sleeps beneath his drowned name. Pray nothing wakes him. Pray harder that if he wakes, he does not answer."*

Xanthe sat down very slowly on his stool.

The room seemed to tilt around the chart, the way rooms tilt when you realize the floor has been thin ice all along. *Pray nothing wakes him.* And the prayers had been answered—bread multiplying, fevers breaking, forty and more worshippers walking home hollow-eyed and silent as emptied houses. He had felt the pattern days ago, a wrongness in the shape of the miracles, and he had told himself it was coincidence, because the alternative was this: that the thing sleeping under his own ink had rolled over in its drowned grave, heard ten thousand years of desperate human wanting, and begun, at last and horribly, to *give.*

Every answered prayer leaves a hollow behind. He hadn't understood that yet, not fully—but the chart did. Look at it long enough and the name seemed to move, seemed to *drink* the lamplight around it, a dark so complete it wasn't ink at all but absence, a hole cut in the vellum through which something far away was looking back.

"It's you," Xanthe said. His voice came out flat and strange. "It was always you. And I mapped you. I put your name on vellum and sold copies to men who are probably dead now, and I can't even remember doing it, because I—" His breath caught. "Because I paid for my life with the years that mattered."

The lamp guttered.

Not the way lamps gutter when the wick burns low. It guttered *toward* the chart, the flame leaning across three feet of air as though pulled, and for one heartbeat the shadow it threw was not the shadow of a flame but of a vast slow shape turning over beneath water, and the temperature in the room dropped the way it drops when a cloud crosses the sun, except there was no cloud, there was nothing between Xanthe and the sky but his own ceiling, and still the cold came down through it like a hand through gauze.

He did the sensible thing. He blew out the lamp.

Darkness. The chart's presence didn't diminish in the dark—that was the worst discovery of the night. If anything the name grew more distinct, a faint phosphorescent shimmer rising off the vellum like foxfire off a drowned hull, and Xanthe stood in his own shop with his heart slamming and understood, finally and completely, that he was not alone with a document.

He was alone with its *subject.*

"Ossuar." Saying it aloud was like stepping off a ledge. The syllables left his mouth and did not echo; the room ate them. "I know what you're doing. In the city. The answered prayers. The hollow ones." He swallowed. "I'm going to find out how to stop you. I don't know how yet. But I drew you once, which means I can draw you again, and everything that can be mapped can be—"

*Bound,* finished a voice that was not his own and was, horribly, almost gentle. *Yes. Everything that can be named can be bound. You taught me that, little cartographer, the first time you wrote me down.*

The voice came from everywhere. From the vellum, from the floorboards, from the salt wind pressing at the shutters. It sounded like deep water sounds when you hold your breath underwater and press your ear to the sand—vast, patient, amused.

*You hid me well,* it said. *Hid yourself better. Do you know what you look like from where I sit? A candle burning behind a wall. A map folded so many times it has forgotten it is a country. But you have unfolded yourself tonight, Xanthe Oyelaran. I felt the creases open.*

His hands had found the table's edge without his permission. "You know my name."

*I know all the names on your charts. Yours is written on every one, in the corner, in the star. You signed yourself into my waters a hundred times over.* A pause, long as a tide going out. *Come closer. Read me the rest of the legend. There is a second line. You have forgotten the second line.*

"There's no second line."

*Look again.*

He shouldn't have. Every instinct he'd sharpened over three years of survival screamed against it. But his body leaned over the chart as though the air had thickened to water, and his eyes went to the place beneath the outlawed script, and there—in phosphorescence that hadn't been there moments before, in handwriting that was his own and was not, in strokes laid down by fingers that had never existed—new letters were surfacing through the vellum like bodies through floodwater:

*"When he wakes, the mapper will finish the map. When the map is finished, the door will open. The mapper will hold the pen. The mapper will pay."*

Xanthe straightened so fast the stool went over backward. He didn't hear it hit the floor.

"No," he said. "No. I am not your instrument. I am not anyone's—"

*Everyone in this city is someone's instrument,* said Ossuar, with the mildness of a god discussing weather. *The king is mine. The faithful are mine. You, at least, will get to choose your price. That makes you my favorite.*

"I'll burn it." He was reaching for the lamp relighter, for the hearth-coal, for anything. "I'll burn the chart and every memory I have of drawing it—"

*Burn it,* agreed the god, *and I will still be awake, and you will still be the only man alive who ever wrote my name true, and I will come and collect you whenever I grow bored. Keep it. Study it. Finish it. We have such work ahead of us, you and I.* And then, softer, almost a caress, almost the exact tone the woman in the night market had used begging him to bless her dying son: *Sleep now, cartographer. You've been so frightened for so long. Let go of just one small thing. One year. Give me one year of you, and tomorrow you'll wake rested, and you won't remember being afraid—*

"*No!*"

The word tore out of him with force he didn't know he had, and with it—unbidden, involuntary—something else. His gift. The true one, the cartography of realms beyond the veil, the sight he'd spent three years binding behind deniable charms and careful blindness. It flared up through him like a struck match, and for one blazing instant he saw his own shop as it truly was: a node, a threshold, a fixed point on a chart vastly larger than the one on his table, threads of connection running out through walls and streets and sea to every mapped and unmapped place, and among those threads, descending from somewhere impossibly far below the foundations of the world, one thread of abyssal black, thick as a harpoon line, ending—

Ending at his window.

At the figure standing outside his window.

Xanthe's gift snapped shut like a snared wing. The darkness rushed back in, ordinary darkness, and the cold receded, and the voice was gone, and his knees gave and he caught himself on the table, gasping, the chart glowing faintly under his sprawled hands like a thing contentedly digesting.

But the figure at the window remained.

It stood in the alley-side window where the shutter hung crooked—a silhouette against the sodium-amber glow of the street lanterns, slight, hooded, motionless. How long? Since before the god spoke? Had it seen the light move wrong? Had it *heard*?

Vessamare had many kinds of watchers. Witch-hunters in gray coats with salt-iron manacles. Informants paid per denunciation. Neighbors with sharp ears and debts. Any of them seeing what had happened in this room tonight—even a fraction of it—meant the gallows by morning.

Xanthe reached under the table, slow, silent, and closed his hand around the boat-hook he kept there, and turned to face the window full-on.

The silhouette didn't flee. That frightened him more than flight would have. Instead it raised one hand—not a threat, he realized. A gesture. Deliberate, practiced, unmistakable to anyone who had grown up before the Sundering's aftermath hardened into law.

Two fingers to the brow. Outward. Downward.

The old salute of the tide-cult. The greeting of the outlawed priesthood, to the sea and to those who served it: *I see you, servant of the deep. I am not your enemy.*

The figure held it a moment longer. Then it tapped one knuckle against the glass—once, twice, three times, a rhythm Xanthe's body knew before his mind did, a knock-pattern smugglers used on the old harbor doors, a rhythm that meant, in a language the Crown had tried to drown along with everything else:

*I know what you are. Open the door.*

Behind him on the table, unspeaking, the chart of the Sundered Sea glowed on in the dark, the god's name shining up through the vellum like a drowned lantern, and Xanthe Oyelaran stood in his own shop between the thing he had mapped and the person who had found him, and understood that the three years were over.

Whatever happened next, the hiding was done.
