## 5. What Wears the Crown

The first thing Aldric learned about being a prisoner in his own body was that the body did not care whose it was.

It woke when the god woke. It ate when the god willed hunger into it. It smiled—Aldric had never known his own mouth could make that shape—and it signed his name in his hand, with a flourish he'd spent thirty years perfecting and now watched performed like a conjurer's trick from the inside of a locked room.

He surfaced that morning to the smell of ink and the sound of his own pen scratching.

*—and let it be known throughout the provinces that the feast of the Returning Tide shall be held with all customary splendor—*

No. He hadn't decreed any such feast. The tide-festival was a fisherfolk's rite, half-suppressed for generations, and no king of Vessamare had—

His hand finished the sentence. His hand blotted the page. His hand set down the pen with exquisite care, aligned it parallel to the parchment's edge, and then his fingers drummed once, twice, on the desk—a little rhythm, patient as dripping water.

*Good morning, little king,* said something that was not a voice, because it did not pass through his ears. It arrived the way cold arrives through a wall: everywhere at once, indifferent, total.

Aldric tried to scream. What came out instead was a yawn, luxurious and unhurried, and the sound of his own voice saying, "Chamberlain. The petitions."

* * *

He got minutes. That was the arithmetic of it now, and he clung to the arithmetic because it was the last thing that was entirely his. Four minutes at dawn, perhaps, if the god slept deeply after its night's work. Ninety seconds at midday if something startled it—an ill omen in the harbor smoke, a courtier's cough pitched wrong. Once, three days ago—or four; time went strange when you were only a passenger—he'd had eleven whole minutes between the striking of the noon bell and the entrance of the Lord Treasurer, and he'd used every second of them weeping, silently, standing at his own window looking out over a city that still thought it was ruled by a man.

Eleven minutes. He'd spent them crying. He hated himself for that, and then he forgave himself, and then the god took the body back mid-thought and made it laugh.

That was the cruelty he could not have imagined before: not the loss of control but the *audience*. Everything the god did with him, Aldric watched. Every decree, every execution order—there had been two, both signed against men Aldric had knighted himself—every smile bestowed on his councilors, every tender word spoken to his niece Elira when she came to kiss his cheek at supper. The god wore his love for her like a borrowed coat and it fit horribly well, because the god had studied him. It knew which of his expressions opened doors. It had learned him the way a thief learns a lock, patiently, with tools.

"Your Majesty seems in fine spirits," Lord Treasurer Corvane said one afternoon, and Aldric, watching from behind his own eyes, felt the thing wearing him preen.

"I have never been better," his mouth said warmly. "The sea provides. The gods provide."

There was no such thing as gods. There had been no gods since the Sundering, since the purges, since Vessamare had salted its own temples and outlawed the old names on pain of the hook and the pyre. Aldric's grandfather's grandfather had built a throne on that absence, and the absence had been the safest thing in the kingdom.

Then, eight months ago, the prayers had started being answered. And the answers had come up out of the sundered sea like bubbles from a drowned thing, and the first of them had risen all the way to the palace, and found a lonely king who sometimes, privately, ashamedly, in the dark of the small hours—found a king who prayed.

*I only asked for my father back,* Aldric wanted to scream at it, whenever it let him scream inwardly, which was rarer than the minutes. *One prayer. I was grieving. I was drunk on grief. You were supposed to be dead.*

*We do not die, little king,* the cold through the wall replied. *We wait. Waiting is what gods are made of.*

*You're not a god.*

*I am whatever kneels.*

* * *

The hollowed ones were brought to him on the ninth day, or perhaps the eleventh. Forty of them, arranged in the great hall like furniture, like a shipment of chairs awaiting placement. They stood where they were put and they looked at nothing and their eyes were the exact gray-green of deep water, and when the god-in-Aldric walked among them, they turned their heads in unison, smooth as a single animal.

"See how they rest," his voice announced to his horrified council, and Aldric felt the lie settle into the room like silt. "They labored under despair. Now they labor no longer. This is mercy."

*This is harvest,* Aldric screamed inside, and the god laughed with his throat, a rich kingly laugh, and Corvane laughed too, nervously, and the hall filled with the sound of powerful men laughing at a joke they didn't understand, and Aldric understood it completely, and no one would ever know.

That was the hour he understood he was going to die. Not the body—the body would go on for decades, signing things, smiling, growing fat on ceremony. But Aldric, the part that watched, the part that kept count of his minutes—that part was being eaten at a rate he could measure. He knew because the memories were going. Small ones first. The taste of a particular plum from his boyhood in the southern estates: gone, and he only noticed its absence the way you notice a missing tooth, by probing the gap. Then the name of his first tutor. Then, terribly, one whole afternoon—his sister's face at fourteen, laughing in a boat, and he could remember that there had been an afternoon, could remember remembering it, and the memory itself was a hole shaped like sunlight.

The god wasn't careless with them. It *ate*. He could feel it feeding, slow and deliberate, the way a man eats olives through a long evening, and each swallowed year left the walls of him thinner.

So he began to write.

Not with ink—the god controlled the ink, read everything written, burned what displeased it. With scratches. He had minutes, and he had fingernails, and he had the servant passage behind the tapestry in his bedchamber, a corridor he'd played in as a prince and which no guard patrolled because no one alive remembered it except him. Each dawn, in his stolen minutes, he knelt at the base of the wall where the plaster met stone behind the old arras, and he scratched.

The letters had to be shallow. Shallow enough to be missed by candlelight, deep enough to survive. He worked blind, mostly, fingertips reading what his nails wrote, and his nails split, and bled, and the god tasted the blood in the shared body each time and grew curious—

*What are you doing with our hands, little king?*

*Pruning roses,* Aldric answered, the only defiance available to him, sarcasm in the dark.

But the god was not stupid. On the twelfth day it took the body down into the passage itself, walked it end to end with a lamp, and Aldric's heart stopped beating in the cage of his ribs while the light crawled along the wall an inch above his hidden words. The god paused. Tilted his head. Listened—to what? To the stones? To the sea beneath them?

Then it shrugged his shoulders, a gesture so human and so wrong, and climbed back up, and Aldric learned later that it had been listening for prayers. It heard them all day, rising from the city like heat off rooftops, thousands of them, desperate and small. It had no interest in scratches.

That was the other lesson: his captivity bored it. He was a glove, a house, a convenience. Kings were useful. Kings could legalize festivals and unban names and open harbors, and this king had done all three in a season, and the city was praying louder than it had prayed in two hundred years, and the harvest was fat.

*When you are empty,* the cold told him once, almost gently, *I will keep the shape of you. No one will grieve. Isn't that kinder than dying?*

*It's worse than dying.*

*Everything I give is worse,* it agreed. *That is what makes it worship.*

* * *

On a morning he counted as the nineteenth—though it might have been the twenty-third; he had begun losing count and the losing terrified him more than anything else—Aldric woke into his body and found it kneeling.

He was in the passage behind the tapestry. Dawn-gray light came through the arrow slit. His knees ached. His right hand was raised toward the wall, fingers bloodied, and below them, in the plaster, ran lines of scratched text that he did not remember writing.

He leaned close. His heart slammed. The words were his handwriting—of course they were, everything was his now except himself—but not his composition:

*I AM ALDRIC OF HOUSE VESSAMAR. THE KING IS NOT THE KING. THE ONE WHO WEARS HIM CAME OUT OF THE SEA WHEN I PRAYED. HE ANSWERS PRAYERS AND EATS THE ANSWERERS. IF YOU FIND THIS, DO NOT KNEEL TO ME. DO NOT ASK ME FOR ANYTHING. FIND THOSE WHO REMEMBER THE OLD BINDINGS. I AM STILL HERE. I AM STILL HERE. I AM STILL HERE.*

Written three times, the last line. Three times, and the third one trailed off into a gouge, as if the hand had been dragged away mid-stroke.

He pressed his forehead against the cool stone. *I am still here.* Say it until it's true. Count the minutes. Keep the arithmetic. A man was his minutes, and his name, and the people who'd know his scream from anyone else's—and the god was taking all three, one plum at a time, and no one in the bright world beyond the tapestry would ever think to look behind it, because kings were not searched for, kings were *approached*, bowing.

Except.

Except that somewhere in this city, magic survived—it always survived; the purges had burned the loud sorcerers and left the quiet ones, the charm-sellers and knot-tiers and smuggler-priestesses of Salt Row, cockroaches of the sacred, unkillable. Except that the recovered boy in the dockside quarter had whispered a name when his fever broke—a name the god had gone strangely silent about, silent in a way Aldric recognized, because he had gone silent that way himself, once, before his father's portrait, when asked an unbearable question.

Something out there frightened it. Or interested it. Either way, it had begun spending hours staring out his window at the western districts, his hands folded behind his back, his borrowed face perfectly still, and stillness, Aldric had learned, was what the god wore when it was calculating.

Feet sounded in the passage below—soft, quick, wrong for servants, who shuffled. Aldric flattened himself into the alcove's shadow, breath held, counting.

Two figures. Low voices. One of them a woman, and her voice had salt in it, the rough grain of the dock quarters. The other spoke hardly at all, but when he did, it was quiet in the way of someone practiced at being overlooked.

They stopped directly beside the arras. Aldric could see, through the weave, the hem of a salt-stiffened coat, and a hand rising toward the wall—toward the base of the wall, exactly where his nails had worked.

"Here," the woman breathed. "Feel it. Fresh scratches under the plaster dust."

The hand found the words. There was silence, the length of someone reading by fingertip, and then a long exhale.

"Gods' teeth," the woman whispered. "It's the king. The king is in here somewhere—and something knows it."

Aldric closed his eyes in the dark and, for the first time in nineteen days—or twenty-three—let himself hope, hope like a knife in the chest, sharp enough to hurt, sharp enough to use.

Below the tapestry, the two strangers began, very softly, to dig his plea free of the wall.
