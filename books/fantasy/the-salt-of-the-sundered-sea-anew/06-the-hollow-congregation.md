## 6. The Hollow Congregation

Sefa had smuggled contraband gods for eleven years, and she knew the smell of a shrine that had been used wrong.

It was not incense. It was not the brine-and-tallow stink of tide-cult candles, or the copper tang of blood offerings. It was sweeter than any of those and worse—like honey poured over something already rotting, like a feast left too long in the sun. The smell drifted up out of the drowned chapel beneath the Salt Row tannery and settled on her tongue, and her body, trained by years of hiding, told her what her mind refused: *someone has been feeding here, and they have fed too much.*

"You're sure about this?" Xanthe stood at her shoulder at the top of the cellar stairs, hood drawn low over his eyes. In the lamplight his hands were very still against his sides—the stillness of a man who had taught himself to be furniture.

"No," Sefa said. "But I'm sure we're out of time."

She took the stairs first. That was the arrangement between them, unspoken but binding: she went first into every room where a god might be waiting, because she had spent her life learning how to kneel without breaking, and he had spent his learning nothing of the kind.

The chapel had been a tannery's curing cellar once, before the Sundering made even the old shrines contraband. Salt-crusted pillars held up a ceiling low enough to touch. Someone had hung driftwood lanterns from hooks meant for hides, and their light fell unevenly across forty bodies kneeling on the cold stone.

Forty. She counted them twice, because the first count felt like a lie.

They knelt in rough rows facing an empty alcove where a statue should have been—where *had* been, once; you could see the chisel-scars on the wall where the tide-mother's likeness had been pried away during the purges. They faced it anyway. Forty people on their knees, backs straight, hands open on their thighs, faces turned upward toward a wall with nothing on it but scars and salt.

Not one of them was breathing hard. Not one of them had shifted in the minutes since Sefa reached the bottom step.

"Saints' teeth," Xanthe whispered behind her.

"Don't invoke anything," she said quietly. "Not down here."

* * *

She moved along the first row slowly, lamp held high, the way she'd move through a hold full of cargo she suspected of being alive. The worshippers did not track her. That was the worst part—not the stillness itself, which she had seen before, in fever-wards and opium dens and once in a plague pit outside Harrowgate—but the *quality* of it. These were not sleeping bodies or drugged ones. Sleeping people still reached for warmth. These faces were turned up and outward, attentive, like listeners waiting for the next word of a song—and there was nothing behind the listening at all.

Their eyes were open. Their eyes were dry. Their eyes were the color of watered milk, and when she passed her hand before one woman's face, the pupils didn't so much as flicker.

"Are they dead?" Xanthe asked. His voice came from farther back than she expected. He had stopped three rows deep and would go no closer, and she found she couldn't blame him.

"No." She pressed two fingers to the nearest throat. A pulse answered, slow as a tide pool draining. "No, they're—" She searched for the word and hated every word she found. "Empty. This is what happens when something answers."

"When something answers," Xanthe repeated. He said it flatly, testing it, the way he tested everything—as if language were a chart he was checking for errors. "You've seen this before."

"I've heard of it." She kept walking. "Old stories. From before the Sundering, when the sea-gods still spoke plainly. My grandmother's grandmother was a priestess, back when priestess wasn't a word you got flayed for. She wrote that devotion is a door, and doors swing both ways, and if something on the other side is hungry enough—" She gestured at the rows of kneeling flesh. "This. The prayer goes out. The miracle comes back. And the price comes due all at once, collected from the one who prayed."

"But forty of them. At once."

"That's why I brought you." Sefa turned, letting the lamplight find her face deliberately, watching him watch her. "Because forty isn't a congregation anymore, cartographer. Forty is a harvest."

* * *

She found Tam Voss in the third row.

She knew him instantly, though she'd only seen him once, at night, by lantern-light, buying a luck-knot off Xanthe in the market—broad shoulders bent from twenty years hauling rope, a rope-burn scar circling his wrist like a second cuff. She remembered him because he'd haggled. Because he'd laughed. Because when Xanthe's charm had done its small deniable work and the man's bad knee had loosened enough to let him climb rigging again, Tam had pressed an extra copper into the cartographer's palm and said, loud enough for half the market to hear, *best coin I ever spent.*

Now he knelt with the rest, hands open, face upturned, and the laugh was gone somewhere no one would ever find it.

Sefa crouched beside him. Her knees cracked on the stone and she barely noticed. Up close she could see what the lamplight had hidden: faint lines around his mouth, deeper than age, as though he had spent his last days smiling harder than any man should smile—as though joy had been drawn out of him the way marrow is drawn from bone, and his face hadn't yet learned its new shape.

"Tam." She touched his shoulder. Nothing. "Tam Voss. Your daughter Maren sells mussels off the east quay. You told me. You wouldn't stop telling me."

Nothing. The pulse under her fingers ticked on, patient and vacant, like a clock in an abandoned house.

"He came to me about his knee," Xanthe said. His voice had gone thin. He had come close enough now to see the face, and whatever he saw there had undone some of his careful distance. "I sold him a knot. A luck-knot. It wasn't—I didn't pray for anything. I don't pray. There was no prayer to answer."

"There's always a prayer," Sefa said. She stayed crouched, unwilling to rise, unwilling to leave Tam's side just yet. "That's the first thing they teach you before they make teaching it a hanging crime. Every charm is a prayer wearing work clothes. Every blessing. Every whispered *please*. The old powers don't care what shape the asking comes in. They care only that someone asks." She looked up at him. "You gave him a charm. Something answered it. Do you understand me? Whatever woke up in this city—it's using your little knots the way a fisherman uses chum."

Xanthe had gone the color of the salt on the walls. But to his credit—he did not run. She watched the fear move through him like weather and watched him stand inside it, and she thought: *there. That's the one I need.* Everyone in this city was afraid. Most people let the fear steer. This one gripped the tiller anyway, white-knuckled, and stayed in the boat.

"What happened to them?" he asked. "Exactly. If you know."

"I know the stories." She rose at last, joints aching, and swept the lamp across the congregation. "Devotion isn't just a feeling. It's substance. It has weight, the way water has weight. A person prays, and the praying fills them up—hope, faith, the whole salt-water of belief. And when something on the other side answers, it doesn't create the miracle out of nothing. Nothing is created out of nothing. Not even by gods. Especially not by gods." Her voice hardened. "It takes the devotion. All of it. Drains the vessel that offered it and pours it out as bread, as healing, as whatever the prayer asked for. And a person emptied of devotion—" she nodded at Tam, at all of them— "doesn't grieve. Doesn't want. Doesn't hope. They just... wait. For the next thing to pour into them. And there's never a next thing, because the thing that drained them doesn't refill what it takes. It only takes."

"Then they'll die here."

"Some will. Cold, thirst, their own bodies forgetting to bother." She swallowed. "Some will live years like this. My grandmother's book called them the Hollowed. Said the temples used to keep them. Feed them. Pray over them hoping to spark something back." She shook her head. "It never sparked back. Once it's taken, it's taken."

* * *

They worked in silence after that, because there was nothing useful left to say and much useful left to do.

Sefa sent word through two of her trusted runners—a tannery boy, a mussel-seller who owed her—and by the third hour the hollowed were being carried out in fish-carts under sacking, four at a time, to a warehouse on Gull Street that her crew kept for cargo that could not be declared. It was slow, dangerous, absurd work. Forty living people who could not walk unassisted, moved through the night streets of a city where magic was a hanging offense and miracles had started happening in daylight. Any watchman who looked inside a cart would see exactly what the witch-hunters had been swearing for months was real.

"They'll ask questions," Xanthe said, steadying Tam's weight across his shoulders with more strength than his frame advertised. "Your people. About what's in the sacks."

"My people don't ask questions. That's why they're my people." Sefa adjusted her own burden—a young woman, maybe nineteen, light as driftwood, terrifyingly light. "Besides. Half my crew prays to something or other, no matter the law. Sailors always have. When they hear what emptied these folks, they'll carry them double-fast and sleep with salt at their doors after."

"And the families?"

"The families get told their people are sick. Fever-sick. Contagious." She hated it even as she said it, and said it anyway. "It's kinder than the truth, and safer. Truth travels fast in this city, and lately truth gets people followed."

By the time the last cart rolled out, dawn was graying the slits above the curing cellar, and Sefa stood alone in the empty chapel except for Xanthe, who had not left—who had instead walked the rows one final time with a scrap of charcoal and a strip of paper, and was now studying what he'd written with an expression she recognized from the market night: the look of a man reading something written in a language only he knew.

She came to stand at his elbow. On the strip he'd sketched the cellar, quick and crude—the pillars, the alcove, the positions of the kneeling figures as they'd found them.

"Why map it?" she asked. "It's a cellar. It's not going anywhere."

"It's not the cellar." He tapped the sketch. "The positions. They weren't random. Look." He drew lines connecting the figures, one to another, and Sefa felt the hair lift on her arms, because the lines resolved—she could see it even in the crude charcoal—into a curve. A sweeping, deliberate arc, like the path of something enormous turning in its sleep.

"They're arranged like points on a chart," she breathed.

"Like soundings." His voice had changed, dropped into something older and surer, and for a moment she was looking at a different man entirely—someone who belonged to deep water and old paper, not to market stalls and luck-knots. "Forty points. Forty prayers. Whoever—or whatever—is doing this isn't harvesting at random. Each answer marks a location. Each hollowed worshipper is a pin stuck in the map." He looked up at her, and his eyes were bright with a horror that was also, unmistakably, recognition. "They're drawing something, Sefa. Across the city. Across the *sea*. And I think I've seen this pattern before."

"Where?"

He hesitated. She watched him weigh it—the old reflex, the smuggler's reflex she knew intimately because it lived in her own chest too: *hold back, hold secret, survive.* She watched him set it down.

"I have a chart," he said. "At my rooms. A chart of the sea as it was before the Sundering. I drew it three years ago. There's a name inked where no city stands, and I've spent three years pretending I misread my own hand." He folded the sketch into his coat. "Come with me. Tonight, before the light. If I'm right about what's waking up, then the king's voice, the answered prayers, all of it—it's not a haunting and it's not a revival. It's a *return*. And there's only one thing in the old rites that returns wearing borrowed mouths."

Sefa looked back once at the empty alcove, at the chisel-scars where the tide-mother's face used to watch over her grandmother's grandmothers, at the salt weeping down the wall like the building itself grieving.

"Say it plain," she said.

"Ossuar," Xanthe said. "The Drowned King. The one they sank the sea to bury." He pulled his hood up. "And I think he's almost finished drawing himself home."
