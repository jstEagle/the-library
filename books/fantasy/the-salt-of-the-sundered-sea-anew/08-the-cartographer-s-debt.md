## 8. The Cartographer's Debt

The dream-chart was still fading from behind his eyes when Xanthe woke with his own name missing from his mouth.

He lay in the dark above his shop and tried to say it—*Xanthe Oyelaran*—and found the syllables slippery as wet ink, running off the page of his tongue. Not gone. Nothing the god showed him was ever simply *gone*. It was more like a word in a language he had once spoken fluently: he knew the shape of it, knew that it had meant something, could not for his life produce the sound.

*You sold this,* he thought, before he remembered whether that was true.

Then the rest of the night came back to him in pieces—the broker's shop, the ledger, the little brass scales—and he understood what Ossuar had done. The god had not shown him a memory. It had shown him the receipt.

He sat up. His hands were shaking. Outside, Vessamare breathed its salt breath against the shutters, indifferent, and somewhere across the rooftops the palace glowed with festival lanterns being tested for tomorrow's tide-night, gold light leaking up like something drowning and rising.

*Three days,* Sefa had said. *Tide-festival, the servants' gate runs thin, and we go in or we don't.*

But first he had to remember what he had spent. Because you could not pay out coin you didn't know you'd carried, and you could not walk into a god's house without knowing exactly how much of yourself was already in its pocket.

* * *

The memory-broker's shop had been on Candlemakers' Row, in the year the purges burned hottest—a narrow slot of a place between a chandler and a man who sold caged wrens, with a door painted the gray of old oyster shell. There was no sign. There never was. You found it because someone dying of the same thing you were dying of told you where it was, and then they were gone, and then it was your turn.

Xanthe had been twenty-two, maybe twenty-three—he would never be sure again—and he had come through the door with a rolled chart under his arm and ash from the Temple Quarter still in his hair.

The broker had been a small woman with hands like a laundress's and eyes like a woman who had read every letter ever addressed to someone else. She'd sat behind a table on which stood a pair of brass scales, and beside the scales, a ledger bound in gray leather, and beside the ledger, a single cup of tea she never drank.

"You're the mapmaker," she said. Not a question. "The one from the Salt College. They're saying the College burned with everyone in it."

"The College didn't burn." His voice had cracked on it. "They made us burn it ourselves. Chart by chart. And then they made us say the words while it burned, so the fire would take the *making* too, not just the paper."

"And yours survived." Her gaze went to the tube under his arm the way a hungry person looks at bread.

"Because I never signed it. A map without a maker's mark is just pretty lines. The witch-hunters can't prove a thing." He set it on her table anyway. "I need passage north. A berth on a smuggler's boat, papers, three weeks of silence. I'm told you arrange such things."

"I arrange everything." She unrolled the chart a hand's width and stopped, and her breath did something small and involuntary, and Xanthe felt—for the last time in years—the pride of craft, hot and clean as a struck flint. Even a stranger saw it. What he had drawn was the Sundered Sea as it had been before the Sundering: the drowned coastlines, the pre-tidal harbor of Vessamare, the deep channels, and along the margin in a cartographer's cipher only three living people had ever been taught—the names of things that lived beneath.

She looked up at him with those letter-reading eyes. "Do you know what they'd give me for this?"

"I know what they'd give *me*," Xanthe said. "A rope and a short drop off the Lantern Bridge. Which is why I need to be gone by morning."

"Mm." She closed the chart. "Passage costs money. Papers cost money. Silence costs more than both." She turned the ledger toward him. "But I deal in other currencies. Sit down, mapmaker."

* * *

In the present, Xanthe lit no lamp. He sat on the floor of his own back room among the blank paper and the hidden inks and pressed his palms flat against the boards, as if he could steady himself against the floor of the past.

The scales had weighed memories. That was the part his mind kept sliding away from, even now, even knowing it. She had explained it in the flat voice of a woman reciting tide-tables: a memory, once freely given, could be drawn out whole—root, branch, and bloom—and what remained behind was a smooth place, a healed-over absence, which the owner would fill in afterward with guesswork and call it remembering. The trade ran both ways. She could also *place* a memory, one harvested from another seller, and for the right price she could make you believe a childhood you never had.

"Why would anyone sell?" he had asked her, young, appalled.

"Why does anyone sell anything?" She had sipped nothing from her untouched cup. "Because they need the money more than they need the thing. Now." She dipped a pen. "Let's begin with something small. Tell me about your mother."

And here was the shape of it, the terrible arithmetic, laid out plain at last:

The passage north cost coin. The papers cost coin. But the smuggler who would carry a fugitive mapmaker—the most hunted kind of fugitive in Vessamare that year—wanted insurance. He wanted certainty that Xanthe would never talk, never lead the witch-hunters back to his route, never even be able to *describe* it. And the broker had explained, gently, that there was one form of insurance better than fear, better than gold, better than any oath sworn on any dead god.

"Give me the years," she said. "Not all of them. Just the ones that make you *you*. Your mother's face. The name of your home village. The reason you learned to draw in the first place. Give me those, and the witch-hunters can pull your fingernails out one by one and get nothing but a man who honestly doesn't know. You'll survive them the way stone survives rain. Isn't that what you want? To survive?"

It was. God help him, it had been.

* * *

The first thing he sold was small, deliberately small, because the broker was canny and started every client on something small. A summer afternoon: his mother shelling peas on a step, the smell of the pods, a song she hummed with no words in it. He gave it and watched her draw it out—there had been an instrument, a shallow bowl of dark glass, and his memory had risen out of him like heat-shimmer off a road, visible for one impossible moment as a thread of pale gold—and then it was in the bowl and he was looking at the bowl and he could feel that something had been there and could not have said what.

"That wasn't so bad," the broker said, and wrote in the ledger: *One summer afternoon. Paid.*

The second was larger. His mother's face—not the afternoon, but the face itself, the whole archive of it, every angle the sun had ever caught. He kept the fact of her. He knew he had a mother, knew she had loved him, knew she had died in the Sundering's first winter. He simply could not have picked her out of a room of one.

By the fourth transaction he had stopped crying. By the seventh he had stopped being able to remember why the seventh had been hard.

*Village:* gone. The name of the village where he'd been born, the smell of its one street, the specific gray of its sky. *First teacher:* gone—an old college master named... named... there had been a beard, he thought, or the idea of a beard. *The reason he drew:* gone, and this one frightened him worst when he noticed it, months later on a smuggler's deck in the northern cold, because he was drawing to keep his hands sane and he realized he no longer knew why he had ever started. Talent without origin. A river with the spring cut out of it. He drew anyway. Whatever had made him a mapmaker lived deeper than the memories that explained it, some bedrock the broker's glass bowl couldn't reach—or hadn't been paid enough to reach.

There had been one final item. He remembered the negotiation if not the content. At the end, broke of everything else worth taking, he had offered her the chart itself, and she had refused it.

"No," she'd said, closing the ledger. "Keep the chart. Sell me instead—" and here his memory of the memory went white, smooth as the healed places, "—and we'll call our account settled. You'll find, mapmaker, that I always settle fairly. One day you may want to buy something back. My door will know your knock."

* * *

Present-day Xanthe got up, lit the lamp after all, and pulled the masterwork from its hiding place beneath the floor.

The pre-Sundering chart unrolled across the boards like a held breath released. Three years he had owned it. Three years since he'd bought it back—one purchase, the only one—from the broker's successor on Candlemakers' Row, paying in coin at last, real coin, three years of charm-craft hoarded kopeck by kopeck. He had told himself he bought it to finish the work of finding the god's name. He had told himself it was strategy.

Standing over it now, lamp trembling in his hand, he admitted the truth: he had bought it because it was the only object in the world that had known him *before*. Every line on it had been drawn by hands that still had all their years attached. It was the closest thing to a portrait of himself that existed.

And Ossuar had offered to return everything. Whole.

He knelt and spread his fingers across the drowned coastline, and let himself do the accounting properly, for the first time. Not the broker's arithmetic—hers had counted only what was taken. His own. What the sales had actually purchased.

They had purchased survival, yes. But trace it further. Survival had brought him back south in calmer years, back to Vessamare, because a man without a past drifts toward the last place he remembers having one. Survival had taught him to hide his gift inside trinkets—luck-knots, salt-charms, deniable little mercies—because a gift worn openly was a gift confiscated. Every charm he'd ever sold had been priced in forgotten time: an hour of craft bought with a year of childhood, a customer's gratitude bought with a mother's face. He had built a life on the exchange rate of himself, and called it prudence.

And now the god stood at the counter offering to reverse every sale, and Xanthe understood—with the clarity of a man reading a chart in perfect light—that acceptance would not restore him. It would replace him.

That was the trap's teeth, and he'd felt them close last night in the dream and only now understood the wound. If Ossuar poured those years back—the village, the mother, the teacher, the *reason*—the man who received them would be a different man. A man with a home to grieve and a face to mourn and a whole self to protect. A man who had never needed to hollow himself out would never choose to spend what remained of himself mapping a god's true name onto living water, stroke by stroke, memory by memory, until nothing was left to hold the pen. Wholeness would make him sane, and sanity would make him refuse, and refusal would leave Aldric wearing the crown until the crown wore through to bone.

The god was not tempting him with his past. It was trying to buy his future with it.

"No," Xanthe said aloud, to the empty room, to the chart, to whatever listened through the salt-dark. His voice sounded strange to him—half a stranger's voice, which was accurate. "I know what I am. I'm what's left after the bill came due. And what's left says no."

The lamp guttered though there was no draft. On the chart, in the margin where the ciphered names ran along the drowned coast, one line of ink seemed darker than it had been a moment ago, fresher, as if the sea beneath the paper had exhaled on it.

He leaned closer. The name of the binding-place—the stretch of water Sefa's ritual texts called the Hollow Harbor—had acquired a faint ring around it, drawn in no ink he owned, precise as compass-work.

Something was annotating his map.

* * *

Dawn came gray and smelling of festival bread. Xanthe rolled the chart away, hid it, and went down to open the shutters, because a charm-seller's stall must open regardless of gods, and because Sefa would come at midmorning with her crew and her half-stolen ritual text and her eyes like a woman who had already decided how she would die and found it acceptable.

He was sorting luck-knots when he heard the footsteps—three sets, light, unhurried, stopping across the lane. Through the shutter-slats he saw them: Sefa in her fishwife grays, and two of her people he half-knew, and none of them smiling.

Sefa crossed the lane alone, came under the awning, and set something on his counter without a word.

A page. Old, vellum, edges scorched—the missing half of the ritual text, the half that should have burned with her when the traitor sold her out. Except she hadn't burned. Except it was here.

"Found it this morning," she said quietly, "nailed to the door of my shrine. With this written underneath."

She turned the page over. On the reverse, in a flowing hand that belonged to no living scribe—in ink that gleamed faintly, wetly, like the sea at dusk—ran a single line of the old cipher. Xanthe read it twice, and his blood went cold and then colder, because it was his cipher. The College's cipher. The one only three living people had ever been taught, and he had sold two of those teachings away himself.

*Bring the mapmaker,* it said. *Or I will come collect him myself, and I collect everything.*
