## 9. Breaking Into the Palace

The tide-festival came once a year, on the longest night, when Vessamare pretended—for a single sanctioned evening—that it still loved the sea.

Sefa watched from a rooftop on the Salt Row side of the palace district as the procession wound up the ceremonial stair: lantern-boats carried on poles, priests of the sanctioned cults in salt-white vestments, and above them all, on a litter draped in grey silk, the King of Vessamare. Aldric's face was painted for the festival—kohl at the eyes, ash along the jaw, the old mourning-marks that predated the Sundering—and he smiled down at his people with a smile Sefa had seen before, on things that wore faces badly.

"That's not him," she murmured. "Look how he holds his hands."

Beside her, Xanthe crouched with his elbows on his knees, studying the procession through a cracked spyglass he'd bought off a dead man's stall three years ago. "Holds them like what?"

"Like someone taught him the shape of holding hands and never told him why." She took the glass from him, found the king again. The litter paused at the top of the stair; the crowd roared; Aldric raised one hand, fingers splayed just slightly too wide, like a puppet remembering strings. "The real Aldric used to grip the rail. White-knuckled, every festival. He hated heights. This thing doesn't hate anything."

Xanthe was quiet a moment. Below them, the city glowed—a thousand lanterns set adrift in the canals, bobbing out toward the sundered sea where they'd gutter and drown, little fires given to water that no longer wanted them. "You've seen the real king?"

"Once. From farther away than this." She handed back the glass. "He was younger then. So was I. He threw bread to the gulls and got mobbed by children and laughed like it embarrassed him." Her chest tightened around something old. "That's who we're digging out."

"And if there's nothing left to dig?"

"Then we'll know," Sefa said, "and we'll burn the thing wearing him instead. Either way, tonight ends with an answer."

Below, the last of the procession vanished into the palace's sea-gate, and the great doors began to close—and stopped, held open by protocol, because tradition demanded the doors stay unbarred until the last lantern-boat crossed the harbor bar. Old law. Tide-law. Law written before the Sundering by people who trusted the ocean more than any invader.

Sefa smiled without warmth. "There's our door."

* * *

Her crew gathered in the flooded cellar of a chandler's warehouse two streets from the palace wall: Bren, broad and quiet, who could carry a grown man over one shoulder and had done it twice; Ivetta, small and quick-fingered, whose lockpicks were filed from fishhooks; and Odo, who hadn't spoken since his daughter Mara started going hollow-eyed after the god answered a prayer she'd whispered over a broken doll. Odo knew the palace servant tunnels better than anyone alive—he'd been a kitchen boy under the old steward, before the purges—and he'd insisted on coming the moment Sefa said the word *palace*, with the flat desperation of a man who had nothing left to lose and knew exactly what that felt like.

"The tide-gate first," Odo said, laying out a grease-pencil map on a fish-crate, drawn from memory older than the war. "Festival night, they flush the canal culverts to clear the muck before the morning processions. The gate opens four times between midnight and dawn. Thirty heartbeats each time. We go on the second."

"Guards?" Bren asked.

"Two at the inner grate. Festival pay means festival drink. They'll be slow."

"And past the grate?" Sefa traced the line herself. "The servant passages. You drew them from memory, Odo, but memory lies. Walls get bricked up. Doors get moved. If we hit a dead end in the wrong corridor with the whole watch between us and the way out—"

"I know my tunnels," Odo said, too sharp, and immediately looked ashamed of it. His hands wouldn't stop moving, worrying the edge of the crate.

"You do," Sefa said gently. "But we brought something better than memory tonight."

She turned to Xanthe.

He stood apart from the others, as he always did, near the ladder, half in shadow—the posture of a man perpetually about to leave a room. In the weeks since Salt Row, since the shrine of forty hollowed worshippers and the ritual text torn in half when the witch-hunters took her, since she'd clawed her way out of the Crown's cells during the riots with Mara's guilt-ridden father sobbing directions behind her—in all that time, Xanthe had kept his gift leashed so tightly she could forget he had one. He sold luck-knots. He drew maps of nowhere. That was the story, and he wore it like armor.

Tonight the armor was coming off, and he knew it.

"When we're inside," Sefa said, "I need you to do what you did in the shrine. See the passages. All of them. Not Odo's memory of them—the truth of them, right now, under our feet."

Xanthe's jaw worked. "You understand what you're asking."

"I'm asking you to stop hiding for one night."

"It's not hiding." His voice stayed level, but she heard the strain beneath it, rope under load. "It's survival. The moment I open the sight fully—unmasked, in a warded building, during a festival when every sanctioned priest in the city is sensing for stray workings—I'm not a smuggler anymore. I'm evidence. Every hunter, every ward-sensor, every god-damned scrying pool in that palace will feel me light up like a signal fire."

"Yes," Sefa said simply. "They will."

The cellar went quiet. Bren shifted his weight. Ivetta examined her picks with sudden fascination.

"So why ask?" Xanthe said finally.

"Because Ossuar is sitting on the throne of Vessamare wearing its king, and the ritual to unbind him needs a cartographer who can find the binding-place through the veil, and the veil does not open politely." She stepped closer. "You've spent three years paying memory-brokers to forget yourself one year at a time so you'd be safe. I watched you trade away your mother's face for a luck-charm. I'm not asking you to throw that sacrifice away, Xanthe. I'm asking you to make it worth something. Just for tonight."

Something moved behind his eyes—not anger, not fear. Recognition. The look of a man seeing his own reflection in someone else's confession.

"The risk," he said slowly, "invites everything."

"The risk *is* everything. It always was." She held out her hand, palm up, the way the tide-cult offered trust. "We can walk away. Find another way. Take another month, another season, while Aldric erodes hour by hour and Odo's daughter forgets her father's name."

Odo flinched. Xanthe looked at him—at the hollow spaces where Mara used to live—and Sefa saw the decision land before he spoke it.

"No," Xanthe said. "No more months." He took her hand. His palm was cold and ink-stained and shaking only slightly. "Show me where the sight opens easiest. Under the waterline, if we can manage it. Water remembers routes."

* * *

The tide-gate opened on the second flushing exactly as Odo promised, and they went through in the dark and the stink and the roar of displaced water, Bren carrying Odo, Ivetta first with her picks already out, Xanthe pressed against Sefa's back with his breath shallow and fast. The inner grate's guards were exactly as slow as predicted; Ivetta dropped them into festival-drink sleep with powder in a wine skin passed through the bars an hour earlier, and then they were inside the bones of the palace, standing in a service tunnel that smelled of tallow and old salt, and Sefa gave the word.

"Now, cartographer. Open it."

Xanthe closed his eyes.

Sefa had seen workings before—tide-priestesses pulling the swell, hedge-witches reading weather in smoke—but she had never seen anything like this. It wasn't spectacle. It was the opposite of spectacle: the air around Xanthe went *precise*. Dust hung differently. The darkness of the tunnel acquired depth, structure, layers, as though the world had been a smudge and he had wiped it clean. And then—

"Oh," Ivetta breathed, because the walls of the tunnel had become visible in a new way. Not lit. *Mapped.* Faint lines of pale gold traced themselves through the stone, branching, splitting, rejoining—a living diagram of every passage, every stair, every sealed door within some expanding radius, drawn in light only they could see. Sefa could see the kitchens to their left as a constellation of warm rooms. Above, the great hall blazed with festival noise rendered as pressure. And threading everywhere, thin as spider silk, lines of cold blue: wards. Dozens of them. Hundreds.

"There," Xanthe whispered, and his voice had changed—steadier now, almost drunk with it, the voice of a man returning after long exile to the country that made him. "The servant passages run deeper than Odo remembered. They extended them after the purges—new corridors, warded ones, built to move prisoners without crossing the public halls." His hand rose, tracing air. "There's a stair behind this wall. It goes up four levels and comes out behind the king's private chapel. There's a guard post at the third landing—two men, awake, bored. We can wait for the changing of the watch; there's a gap of ninety seconds when both posts are looking at each other instead of the stairs."

"How in the deep can you know that?" Ivetta whispered.

"Because the passage knows it. I'm not imagining this. I'm *reading* it." He turned, and his eyes had gone strange—faintly luminous, chart-colored, the grey-green of ink wash drying on vellum. "This is what I was. Before Vessamare. Before I sold—" He stopped himself. Swallowed. "This is what I was."

For one heartbeat Sefa saw it all plain on his face: the exhilaration, vast and terrified, of a man using his whole self for the first time in three years. It was beautiful. It was also a beacon, and she could almost see the cost arriving.

Far above, faint as a bell rung underwater, something in the palace *noticed*.

"What was that?" Bren's hand found his knife.

"A ward-sensor," Xanthe said, and the drunk joy drained out of his voice. "Somewhere above us. It brushed the edge of my radius and pulled back. It's... reporting. We have minutes. Maybe less."

"Then we move." Sefa clapped his shoulder. "Lead us, cartographer. Fast and quiet, and don't you dare dim that light until we're at the king's door."

* * *

They climbed through the palace's secret anatomy like blood through a vein. Xanthe led, reading the golden passages in real time, adjusting course as the living map revealed what memory could not: a bricked-up arch here, rerouted around; a collapsed stair there, bypassed through a wine cellar where Ivetta lifted a trapdoor on hinges oiled that very season; a stretch of corridor where the cold blue ward-lines ran so thick that Xanthe walked them through single-file along a seam thinner than a coin's width, sweating, whispering *don't touch the blue, don't touch the blue*, until they emerged into a cleaner passage beyond and Bren let out a breath he'd been holding for a hundred steps.

Twice, the sensor came back.

The second time, Xanthe staggered, caught himself against the wall. "It's hunting now. Casting outward in sweeps. It's..." His brow furrowed, and then went white. "It's not a sensor. Sensors don't sweep like this. This is *him*. Ossuar. He's felt me before—through the dream-chart. He knows my signature."

"Can he pinpoint you?"

"He's trying. But the palace is old, the stone remembers a lot of prayers, and I'm standing inside a maze of tide-law architecture that predates him." A ghost of a grin, feral and brief. "He built his nest in a house I could draw blindfolded. Come on. Chapel stair. Ninety-second gap starts in four minutes."

They made the landing with two minutes to spare and waited in the dark behind a tapestry that smelled of incense and dust, listening to two guards argue about festival rations, listening to the bells mark the hour, listening to the gap arrive—both men turning toward each other, boots receding, ninety seconds of silence opening like a door.

They crossed the landing. Up the final stair. Through a door that Ivetta opened in eleven seconds flat.

And there it was: the king's private chapel, small and old and smelling of cold ash, moonlight falling through a salt-crusted window in a single pale blade across the floor. Across that floor, scratched into the flagstones in a shaky hand—scratched deep, scratched with fingernails by the look of it, scratched over and over until the letters bled shadow—ran words that stopped Sefa's heart:

*I AM STILL HERE*

*HE SIGNS WITH MY HAND*

*IF YOU CANNOT FREE ME — KILL ME — DO NOT LET IT WEAR ME TO THE PEOPLE —*

*KILL ME*

Below the plea, half-obscured by a fallen kneeler, a fresh line, newer, the letters weaker:

*IT KNOWS YOU ARE COMING*

Sefa read it twice. Beside her, Xanthe's luminous sight swept the room—and fixed on the far wall, where the golden lines of his living map converged into a knot of dense, tangled light behind the altar. A hidden door. A hidden *something*.

And threaded through that knot, coiling around it like ink dropped in water, ran lines of cold blue that pulsed.

Not wards.

A heartbeat.

"It knows we're coming," Sefa repeated softly. She drew her knife. "Good. Saves us the trouble of announcing ourselves."

Behind the altar, the knot of light shuddered—and began, slowly, to unfold.
