## 10. The King in the Wall

The minutes came back to Aldric like a man surfacing from deep water—gasping, disoriented, and certain that something below was still holding his ankles.

He had learned to count them. That was the first thing he'd taught himself, back when the lucidity lasted whole hours and he'd still believed hours meant rescue. *Count the breaths. Count the heartbeats. Count everything, because counting is yours.* The god did not count. Ossuar moved through time the way tide moves through a wrecked ship, without noticing the compartments, and so the counting belonged to Aldric alone: four hundred and eleven days of possession, and in all that time perhaps nine full hours of himself.

Tonight's allotment was short. He could feel it already fraying at the edges, the way candlelight frays when the tallow runs low.

But tonight, for the first time in over a year, he was not alone in the dark.

Voices. Below him, beyond the wall of the old salt-vault where Ossuar slept him when it slept—a narrow cell the palace records called an archive and the king had come to call the wall, because it was the wall he pressed his palms against while the god wore his hands elsewhere, signing decrees, blessing executions, smiling at banquets with his mouth. The voices were low, careful. A woman's, roughened by salt and smoke. A man's, quieter, with the cadence of someone who measured things even when speaking.

Aldric's whole body went rigid with hope, which was itself a kind of pain, because hope had been beaten out of him so many times that its return felt like a wound reopening.

*The scratch,* he thought. *They found the scratch.*

* * *

He had carved the plea over three stolen minutes in the spring, using a fishhook he'd palmed off a servant's tray—the same trick he'd used as a boy to steal figs from the kitchens, though the boy who did that had never imagined the skill would one day be the last honest act of his reign. His fingers had been clumsy. They were always clumsy now; Ossuar kept his body like a tool kept in a sheath, oiled but unused, and fine motor control was the first thing to rust.

He'd written:

*I AM ALDRIC. I AM STILL HERE. IF YOU READ THIS AND CANNOT FREE ME, THEN KILL ME. I BEG IT. THE THING WEARS ME TO DO CRUELTY WITH A KIND FACE. KILL ME BEFORE I FORGET I EVER WAS ANYONE. —A.*

Then he'd covered the words with a hanging tapestry of the Sundering—some courtier's idea of tasteful—and gone back under, and for months he hadn't known if anyone would ever pull the cloth aside.

Now there were lantern-glow lines under the tapestry's hem. Now the voices were close enough to catch words.

"—not rat-marks, Sefa. Look at the spacing. Someone took care with this."

"I'm looking." The woman's voice again, and Aldric realized with a jolt that he knew her voice—not from before, not as king, but from *inside*. He had been present, surfacing briefly, on the night six weeks past when the god had ordered the arrest of a smuggler-priestess from Salt Row and had watched her face across the interrogation table with an interest that had made Aldric's stomach turn even through the fog. She hadn't broken. She'd smiled at the god wearing his face, and said, *You're afraid of something down there in the water, and you've been afraid a long time.*

Ossuar had ended the session early that night. Aldric had counted that as a victory, small and bitter as a green plum.

"Read it aloud," said the second voice—the quiet one. "All of it."

A pause. Then Sefa read it, and her voice changed as she went, losing its smuggler's flatness, taking on something rawer. When she reached the signature—the single letter, *A*—she stopped, and the silence stretched long enough that Aldric could hear the drip of brine somewhere in the vault's foundations, and his own heartbeat, fast and human and entirely his own for these few minutes, doing what it liked.

"He's asking us to kill him," the quiet man said finally.

"He's asking us to free him first," Sefa answered. "*If* we cannot. That's not the same prayer."

"It's worse. It means he doesn't believe anyone can."

Aldric closed his eyes. Pressed his forehead to the cold stone. *I believe it less every day,* he thought, willing the words through the wall as if thought could carry. *And I believe it more every day that someone must try, or this city will learn what its mercy has bought.*

* * *

The minutes were going. He could feel Ossuar stirring beneath his thoughts like sediment rising in shaken water. There was no pattern to the god's returns—it slept after great exertions, and tonight it had exerted greatly: a decree dissolving the harbor courts, another ordering the temple-burnings investigated by witch-hunters who answered only to the crown, a third, quietly horrifying, granting stipends to the families of the hollowed worshippers, which the city would read as compassion and which Aldric knew, from the inside, was husbandry. You fed what you intended to harvest.

Three hundred seconds, maybe. Perhaps fewer.

He began to move. Not loudly—that would bring guards, and guards brought the god's attention, because the palace watch had been seeded with men whose prayers had been answered years ago and who were hollow in ways that let Ossuar see out of their eyes too—but with the desperate economy of a drowning man. He dragged the tapestry fully aside. The fishhook was still wedged in the mortar crack where he'd left it. He took it up.

And he did the thing he had promised himself he would never do, because doing it meant admitting how little time he had ever really had.

He knocked.

Twice, softly, on the inner wall—the wall that backed onto the old servants' passage behind the archive, the passage the conspirators must have used, because no loyal path led here anymore.

The voices below stopped dead.

Then, very quietly, the quiet man's voice, right against the stone on the other side, so close Aldric could feel the words as vibration: "Who knocks?"

"Aldric Vessamar," the king said, and heard his own voice crack on his own name like ice on a pond. It had been four hundred days since he'd spoken as himself. Ossuar spoke constantly, fluently, beautifully, but it never said *I* and meant him. "Third of that name. Or what's left of him. You have my message in your hands and my voice at your ear and I have perhaps two hundred breaths before the thing that wears me wakes up and wears me back."

Silence. Then Sefa: "Prove it. Tell us something only the king would know."

Aldric almost laughed. Almost. What did he know that wasn't public? What hadn't Ossuar rifled through, catalogued, worn smooth with use?

Then he had it, and shame and relief arrived together, twins.

"The winter I was nineteen," he said quickly, "before my father died, I used to slip out of the palace through the cistern gate and go down to the fish-market in a borrowed coat, and there was a girl there—Maren, Maren Salt-Thumb, they called her, for the way she licked her thumb to test the nets—who taught me to gut a cod in four strokes, and I paid her in court gossip, and the last night I ever went she told me—" His voice caught. Two hundred breaths, and he was spending them on this, but this was exactly the coin, wasn't it? Not statecraft. Not seals and decrees. This. The small ungilded truth. "She told me I'd make a better fisherman than a king, and I laughed, and she was right, and I never told a living soul because a prince doesn't gut cod in a market and a king especially doesn't remember why it mattered."

Silence again. Longer.

"He's shaking," the quiet man said—not to Aldric, to Sefa, but loud enough to carry. "I can hear it in his voice. A liar performs. This man is just—"

"Terrified," Sefa finished. "Yes." A pause, and then, closer, her palm flat against the stone—he could hear the difference between a voice at a wall and a hand at a wall, after a year of listening to walls. "Your Majesty. We can't take you out through this passage tonight. The vault is watched by hollow men, and if the god feels you moving it will slam you under and know our faces, and everyone in this room dies including you. Do you understand me?"

"I understand." And he did, and understanding it was like swallowing stones. "That's why the message says what it says."

"No." Sharp now. "No, listen to me. We are not here to kill a king. We're here because a cartographer found the name of the thing that's wearing you, and there is a rite—old, older than the Sundering, older than the temples it burned—to unbind a soul from a god. It has never been done. It may not be possible. But it is not *nothing*, and I will not stand at this wall and tell a living man we've chosen the knife instead."

Aldric slid down until he sat on the cold floor, back against the stone, the fishhook loose in his fingers. Beneath him, faint but rising, he felt the first tremor of the god's return—the sensation he had come to know too well, like a tide coming in over his own footprints.

"You don't know what it's like," he said. He hadn't meant to say it aloud. He said it anyway, spending breaths like a spendthrift, because if these were among the last he'd have as himself then damn the economy. "It doesn't hurt. That's the horror of it. It's *comfortable*. Like sinking into warm water. And every time I surface I'm smaller. I've forgotten my mother's voice. I've forgotten the taste of the wine from our own vineyards—I signed away half the harvest last month and I couldn't have said if it was a good vintage. It takes the edges first. It's taking the middle now." He turned the fishhook over. "When there's nothing left but the smile it puts on me, whatever is standing in this room begging strangers for death won't be me anymore. It'll just be the shell it likes best. So I ask you again, and I ask it as your king and I beg it as a man: if the rite fails—if you get one chance and only one—spend it on the knife and not on me."

"No," Sefa said.

"Sefa—" the quiet man began.

"I said no." He heard her breathe, steadying herself against the stone. "Your Majesty, my whole life I've survived by knowing which fights to walk away from. Salt Row teaches you that or the Row buries you. But I walked away from forty people in a shrine on Tanner's Close because walking away was the only way to live and fight later, and I have regretted it every hour since, and I am *done* walking away. If the rite fails, we fail, and we die trying something worthy instead of dying safe having tried nothing. That is my vow, made at your wall, and I don't make vows lightly."

"And mine," the quiet man said. Closer now—Aldric pictured him with his palm beside hers. "My name is Xanthe. I'm the cartographer. I drew the chart that holds the god's name, and I will draw it true on living water if it costs me everything I have, because—" a hesitation, brief, private, like a man deciding how much of himself to show, "—because I know what it is to trade pieces of yourself away to survive, and I know the bill comes due. Yours has come due. We mean to pay it."

Aldric pressed his brow to the stone. For a moment—for one suspended, impossible moment—he was not a king in a wall or a puppet in a skin. He was a man in the dark with two strangers' hands against the other side of it, and the dark was still dark, but it was *shared*.

"Then do it fast," he whispered. "Please. Whatever it is. Do it fast."

* * *

The tide rose.

He felt it the way he always felt it—a soft pressure behind the eyes, warmth spreading down the throat, the world beginning to dim at the edges like paper held near flame. Ninety seconds, maybe less. Enough for goodbyes, if goodbye was what this was.

"One more thing," Aldric managed, fast, slurring slightly as the god's gravity took hold of his tongue. "The guards rotate past the vault at the third bell. The one called Doss—red beard, chews cloves—his prayers were answered two years ago. He's hollow. The god sees through him. Never speak of this rite where Doss can hear. Never—" The warmth reached his chest. His hands were no longer his; they lay open on the floor, slack, waiting for their owner to come home. "Never trust the answered ones. Trust the ones whose prayers were never—"

The sentence drowned.

From inside, Aldric watched, as he always watched, as his hands flexed and settled, as his spine straightened, as his breathing slowed into the calm, unhurried rhythm of something ancient putting on a familiar coat. The god did not rush. The god never rushed. It blinked his eyes once, twice, tasting the vault air, tasting the fading warmth of voices at a wall.

On the far side of the stone, footsteps retreated—swift, silent, professionals. The tapestry swung gently back over the carved plea. Somewhere above, a bell tolled the third hour, and boots rang on marble, and a red-bearded guard paused outside the archive door, sniffed the air once like a hound, chewed his clove, and moved on.

*A rite,* Aldric thought, from his shrinking room beneath the flood. *Older than the Sundering. A cartographer who pays in memory.*

He held the thought the only way he still could—small, wordless, a coal cupped in bare hands—as the warm water closed over his head, and the thing that wore King Aldric Vessamar smiled with his mouth in an empty room, and began, patiently, to plan for guests.

Beyond the palace walls, down on Salt Row, in a shuttered loft that smelled of ink and brine, a lamp burned late over a half-stolen ritual text—and one of the crew who had sworn a vow at a king's wall sat apart from the others, turning a purse of crown silver in his hands, thinking of a daughter whose fever had broken three nights ago, and whose eyes had begun, very quietly, to empty.
