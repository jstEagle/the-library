## 11. Betrayal on Salt Row

The safehouse on Salt Row had been a smokehouse once, and it still smelled of brine and old herring no matter how much incense Sefa burned. She'd chosen it for that reason. Nobody lingered where the air made them hungry.

She stood at the long table with the ritual text spread open before her, weighted at the corners with candlesticks, a cleaver, and a fist-sized chunk of sea-glass that had belonged to her grandmother. The parchment was older than Vessamare's walls, copied and recopied so many times that three different hands argued across its margins in faded ink. *The Unbinding of a Devoured King.* Half of it she trusted. Half of it was probably a lie some desperate priest had stitched in to save his own skin.

"You're reading it upside down again," said Davor from the doorway.

Sefa didn't look up. "I'm reading the marginalia. It argues with itself."

"Everything holy argues with itself." He came in and set down two cups of chicory brew, sliding one toward her. His knuckles were split from the nets, same as always. Twenty years hauling lines off the *Kindly Tern* had worn grooves in him the way water wears stone. "Xanthe still at his charts?"

"He doesn't sleep."

"Doesn't seem to need it." Davor lowered himself onto a crate, cup cradled in both hands. He watched her the way he'd been watching her for three days now — too carefully, too often. Sefa had survived Salt Row by learning what people looked like when they were carrying something heavy behind their eyes. She knew the look on smugglers about to skim cargo. On lovers about to lie. On priests who'd stopped believing but kept the vestments because they were warm.

She'd told herself it was fear. Everyone was afraid now. The hollowed congregation had grown past forty — past eighty, if the whispers from the Temple Quarter were true. Whole families walking home from prayer with their eyes like unswept hearths.

"Davor," she said quietly. "How is Liss?"

The cup stopped halfway to his mouth.

That was answer enough. Sefa closed her eyes for one breath. Liss, twelve years old, who used to braid rope-tassels and leave them on the *Tern*'s rail like offerings to a tide-cult her father pretended not to remember. Liss, whose mother had been taken up by the answering prayers last month and come back smiling and empty, a woman-shaped vessel with nothing left inside to pour.

"She prays now," Davor said. His voice was level the way a deck is level before the wave hits. "Every night. Kneels at the window and prays to whatever's listening. And yesterday—" He swallowed. "Yesterday she came back from the shrine on Candle Lane and she couldn't remember my name. For an hour. She called me 'the fisherman.' Then it came back, and she laughed about it, and I laughed, and then I went out and I—"

He set the cup down because his hands were shaking.

"Davor." Sefa rounded the table slowly. "Whatever you've done—"

"I haven't done anything yet." But his eyes went to the window. To the alley beyond it. To the fog coming off the harbor in gray ropes.

And Sefa understood, all at once and completely, the way you understand a hull has breached — not by seeing the water, but by feeling the list.

"You've already done it," she whispered.

Footsteps in the alley. Many. Boot-heels in cadence, not the scattered rhythm of drunks or dockhands. The Crown's witch-hunters marched like that — iron-shod, uniform, certain.

"They have healers," Davor said, standing, and his face was terrible because it was honest. "The witch-hunters keep a physician who treats the hollowing. He's seen it before, he says, in the old country. He can hold Liss steady until this is over. All he asked—"

"—was us," Sefa finished.

"All he asked was proof I wasn't lying. Names. Places." Davor's jaw worked. "You'd have done the same. Don't tell me you wouldn't. You preach survival like scripture, Sefa. Well. This is me surviving."

"For your daughter." She could hate him later. Right now there were perhaps forty heartbeats between the alley door and iron in the door, and every one of them counted. "For her, yes. I'd burn the whole row for her. So I can't even curse you properly, you miserable bastard, and that's the worst part."

His face cracked. "Sefa—"

"Back door. Now. Where's Xanthe?"

"The chart room—"

"Get him. Get him and go through the fish cellar and don't stop running until you're under the aqueduct, do you understand me?" She was already sweeping the ritual text off the table, rolling it, tearing it — because it would not fit through the cellar grate whole, and half a map was worth more than none. "Go!"

Davor went. That was the thing she would carry longest, she thought later: he actually went. He ran to fetch Xanthe as she'd ordered, because twenty years of following her orders lived deeper in him than any bargain with witch-hunters.

Sefa tore the parchment along its old fold-lines. The first half — the invocation, the naming-structure, the chart of binding-places — went into the oilskin sleeve against her chest. The second half, the severance rite, she thrust through the cellar grate into Xanthe's waiting hands just as he appeared below, wild-eyed, ink still wet on his fingers.

"What's happening—"

"Witch-hunters. Davor sold us." She heard it land on him like a blow; she had no time to be gentle. "Take the severance rite and run. Don't argue. Don't wait. If they take both halves, Aldric stays a puppet forever, and everything we've bled for drowns here tonight."

"Sefa—"

"*Run*, cartographer. That's an order from a priestess of a dead god, and those still count for something." She slammed the grate shut over his protest and turned the shelf of salt barrels across it, buying seconds, only seconds.

Then she took up her grandmother's sea-glass, faced the front door, and waited.

* * *

They came in with lanterns and a warrant and the particular politeness of men licensed to be cruel. Six witch-hunters, gray-cloaked, their badges stamped with the Sundering sigil — the broken wave inside a circle. Their captain was a woman with a scar bisecting one eyebrow and the patience of a fisherman reading weather.

"Sefa Vane," the captain read from the warrant. "Smuggling of contraband relics. Trafficking in outlawed rites. Conspiracy against the Crown's person." Her gaze flicked to the overturned table, the scattered candles, the incense still curling. "Consorting with unlicensed practitioners."

"My priestesshood predates your licensing office by nine hundred years," Sefa said. "But do go on. I do enjoy fiction."

The captain almost smiled. "Where's the rest of your text?"

"Burned. What you'll find in my ashes won't help you."

"We'll see." The captain nodded, and two hunters took Sefa's arms. They were gentle about it, which frightened her more than roughness would have. Gentle meant they'd been briefed. Gentle meant someone above them understood exactly what she was worth alive.

As they turned her toward the door, Sefa saw the alley full of lantern-light, and beyond it, on the far side of the street, a small figure watching from beneath the eaves of the net-mender's shop.

Liss. In her nightdress, barefoot on cold cobbles, praying with her hands pressed together — praying, Sefa realized with a lurch, *for her*. The girl had followed her father's guilt through the fog like a scent.

Their eyes met. Liss's lips moved around words Sefa couldn't hear, and something in the child's face was still whole, still hers, still warm.

Sefa lifted her chin and gave the girl the old sign of the tide-cult, quick and small: two fingers drawn across the heart, then flung outward like scattering seed. *What the sea takes, the sea remembers.*

Then they put her in the wagon, and Salt Row slid away behind the bars like a coastline going under.

* * *

The cells beneath the Palace of Tides were dry, which Sefa found obscene. A city built on drowned ground, and they'd carved dungeons into the rock where no drop of seawater ever reached. It felt deliberate. It felt like the Crown saying: *here, even the sea obeys us.*

They'd taken the sea-glass, the oilskin sleeve, the charms braided into her hair — everything except the invocation hidden in the only place no hunter searches thoroughly, which is a prisoner's own body, worked into the stitching of a wound she'd given herself years ago and kept reopened on purpose ever since. Old habits. Old faiths.

Hours passed, or what she assumed were hours. No windows. A slot in the door for bread and water. Once, boots passed and paused, and a voice outside murmured to another voice, and one word drifted through the stone like a fish slipping past a porthole:

"—the king will question her himself."

Which was wrong. Kings did not descend into cells. Kings sent ministers, inquisitors, men whose names were curses in taverns. Kings did not come down into the dark for a smuggler-priestess.

Unless, of course, the king was not entirely a king anymore.

Sefa sat forward on the cot, chains whispering, and began, very softly, to work the invocation loose from the stitches in her arm. Not to use it. To *know* it was there. To feel the shape of it against her fingertips the way a drowning woman feels the surface overhead.

Because here was the arithmetic Davor had taught her without meaning to: everyone sells something. The witch-hunters sold their conscience for a wage. Davor sold his crew for his daughter. The hollowed worshippers upstairs in the city had sold their prayers for miracles, never reading the fine print written in themselves.

And Ossuar — Ossuar wore a crown and signed decrees and smiled with a stolen mouth, and what had *it* paid? Nothing. That was the difference. That was the line Sefa had spent her whole life walking: everyone spends, but gods only take.

Well. She had half a rite sewn under her skin, a friend running somewhere in the dark with the other half, and a traitor's daughter praying for her on the cold stones of Salt Row. The ledger was not balanced. But it was not empty.

Boots in the corridor. Two sets, then four, then the scrape of a bolt drawn back with ceremonial slowness, as though the door itself were being announced.

Light spilled in, gold and wrong, carrying a shadow that walked ahead of the man who cast it.

Sefa rose to her feet, chains clinking, and looked up into the face of King Aldric — and into the eyes of something ancient that had been wearing him like a coat, and that now regarded her with the mild, hungry curiosity of a collector examining a piece that had finally come to market.

"Priestess," it said with the king's mouth, and the voice underneath the voice was like water finding cracks in a hull. "We should talk about what you're willing to trade."

Behind its smile, deep down in the drowning dark of the king's stolen face, Sefa thought she saw — for half a heartbeat, no more — a man screaming.
