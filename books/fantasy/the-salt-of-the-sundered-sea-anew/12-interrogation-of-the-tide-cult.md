## 12. Interrogation of the Tide-Cult

Aldric woke in his own hands.

That was how he had learned to measure it now—not by the light through the windows, not by the bells of the palace chapel, but by the small treacheries of his body. When the thing that wore him was at the wheel, his hands were steady as a hangman's. When he surfaced, they shook. He looked down at them now, trembling over a breakfast he did not remember ordering, and understood he had perhaps minutes. Perhaps less.

The tray held salt fish and dark bread, Vessamare's plain fare, though it was set on gold. He had eaten nothing for three days—*he* had eaten nothing; the mouth ate, chewed, swallowed with perfect courtesy before ministers and generals, but Aldric went hungry inside his own skull like a beggar locked in a granary. He tore the bread and shoved it into his mouth, choking, weeping without sound because sound drew attention, and *it* noticed everything Aldric did with the body's attention, the way a rider notices a horse favoring a leg.

*Eat*, he told himself. *Strength. You will need strength for whatever comes.*

The last clear thing he remembered—the last whole hour that belonged to him—was the night of the tide-festival, months ago now, or days; time ran like water through cupped hands. He had stood on the seawall and prayed. That was all. He had knelt where his father had knelt, where nine generations of Oyelaran kings had never knelt because the old gods were outlawed and the Sundering had drowned their temples, and he had said, alone, in the wind: *I cannot carry this city alone anymore. If anything is listening—*

Something had listened.

He could not describe what came next except as drowning in reverse: being pulled up out of himself instead of down under the sea. And since then he had watched from the cold interior of his own life while something ancient signed decrees in his hand, smiled with his mouth, kissed his queen's forehead with lips that tasted to him of brine and iron, and called itself, in the privacy of the king's own bedchamber, by no name at all—but Aldric had heard it think one, once, when it believed itself unobserved even by its host.

*Ossuar.*

The name sat in him like a fishhook. He dared not think it often. He suspected, without knowing how, that the god felt every thought that touched its own name, the way a spider feels the outer rings of her web.

There was a knock at the door—two soft strikes, a page's knock—and Aldric's heart lurched with hope so violent it hurt, because for one absurd moment he thought: *rescue*. Then he remembered who he was. Kings were not rescued. Kings were managed.

"Your Majesty," said Lord Chancellor Verel from the doorway, an old man with a face like folded parchment. "The prisoner is ready."

*Prisoner.* The word arrived without context, and Aldric hated that—hated most of all the gaps, the hours torn out of him like pages cut from a ledger. He made his voice work. "Send her in."

*Her.* So it was a woman. He knew nothing else, and the not-knowing was its own kind of terror: what had his body done, what orders had it given in his voice, while Aldric slept beneath?

* * *

They brought her through the morning room rather than the dungeons, which told him something. Chains of lead-grey metal at her wrists—witch-iron, forged during the purges, the kind that deadened gift-craft—and between two witch-hunters in their bone-white half-masks walked a woman built like weather: broad-shouldered, sun-darkened, with rope scars across her knuckles and eyes the flat grey-green of the open sea. She looked around the morning room—at the tapestries, the tall windows full of grey light, the table set with documents she was not invited to touch—with the frank appraisal of someone pricing cargo.

Sefa. The name surfaced in him from the torn-out pages, unbidden: *Sefa, of Salt Row, smuggler-priestess of the tide-cult.* He must have read it off the arrest warrant his own hand had signed. His own hand. He wanted to tear it off at the wrist.

"Sit," said the mouth that was his mouth, and Aldric felt the words leave him like objects handled by gloves.

She sat. She did not slouch and did not grovel, which he found—he was ashamed of finding it—magnificent. Everyone groveled in this room eventually. Ministers, ambassadors, his brother-in-law. The chairs were designed for it, high-backed and thronelike, angled to make the visitor crane upward.

"You are Sefa Vane," said Ossuar-through-Aldric, consulting a paper it did not need to consult. "Proprietor of a chandlery on Salt Row. Licensed importer of rope, pitch, lamp-oil." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat against skin. "And unlicensed importer of certain other goods."

"I sell what the harbor needs, Majesty." Her voice was low and roughened, dockside vowels. "If the harbor's needs have changed, nobody sent me word."

"The harbor's needs." Something behind Aldric's eyes smiled, and he felt it the way you feel a draft under a door. "You consecrate altars in the hulls of ships. You sing the old invocations over keels. You baptized forty worshippers into the tide-cult in the parish of Saint Maren alone. Shall I go on?"

"Only if you enjoy hearing yourself talk, Majesty. Most kings do."

One of the witch-hunters shifted. The other struck her across the mouth with the back of his gauntlet—a short, professional blow—and blood touched her lip, and she did not make a sound, only looked at the man the way you look at a wave that has soaked you: noted, expected, already behind her.

*Stop him,* Aldric screamed inside his own throat. *Stop him, you are the king, this happens under your seal, in your name—*

But of course it happened *by* his seal. That was the genius of it. Every cruelty wore Aldric's face and bore Aldric's ring, and the city learned daily what its king was, and the learning was the point.

"Tell me about the cartographer," said Ossuar.

Aldric felt the question land. Felt, more strangely, the woman feel it—not flinch exactly, but go still in the way of deep water before a squall.

"What cartographer?"

"The one whose charms your boy carried. The recovered child, the miracle on Tanner's Lane. Luck-knots, fever-strings, little deniable mercies." The voice was gentle now, and gentleness in that voice was worse than rage. "Charms do not come from nowhere in Vessamare. Someone made them. Someone with the gift and too much sense to use it properly. You traced the prayer that healed him—we know you did; your people talked in front of the wrong ears. So tell me who sold him his luck, priestess, and you may yet die of old age in a cell instead of on a pyre."

Inside, Aldric hung on the answer with both hands. *A cartographer.* The word opened a crack in the torn pages—a smell of ink and salt, a memory not quite his own anymore of charts pinned in some hidden room, coastlines drawn where maps said only water—

"There are a hundred charm-sellers in the night market," Sefa said.

"A hundred sellers of knots and buttons. One whose knots *work*."

"Then your hunters should buy more luck-knots, Majesty. They seem to need them."

The gauntlet rose again, and this time Aldric's arm rose with it—his own arm, obeying—and he caught the witch-hunter's wrist mid-swing, and the room went very quiet.

"No," said the king's mouth, mildly. "We are having a conversation, not a beating. Leave us. Both of you."

"Majesty—" began the elder hunter.

"*Leave us.*"

They left. The doors closed. And Aldric understood, with a lurch, why the thing wearing him wanted privacy, and the understanding was ice water: it was not going to torture her with fists. It was going to do something far worse. It was going to be *kind*.

* * *

Ossuar rose—or Aldric rose, the grammar failed him here, had always failed him—and walked slowly around the seated woman, and the god spoke through the king's mouth in the king's voice, and it said:

"Do you know what I am?"

"A thing that ought to still be drowned," Sefa said.

"A thing your cult prayed back." The voice was almost tender. "Every whispered invocation over every smuggled altar. Every sailor's superstition, every grandmother's salt-charm, every desperate little *please* addressed to the old water. Prayer is weight, priestess. Enough weight, and even the Sundered Sea rises. I did not break my bindings. Your faithful unpicked them stitch by stitch, out of love. And now the love is coming due."

"Then collect it from them. Leave the boy alone. Leave the—"

"The hollow ones?" Ossuar laughed, and the laugh in Aldric's throat was the worst sound he had ever made. "Priestess. Answered prayer is not free. It has never been free. Your cult sang that truth for six hundred years and then forgot it the moment the singing got lonely. Devotion poured into an empty vessel returns filled with something else. They gave, and they were given to, and the ledger balances. I keep excellent accounts."

"They're *people*."

"People are accounts," said the god, and turned away, bored, and looked out the window at the grey harbor, and for one moment—one strange, suspended moment—Aldric felt the attention behind his eyes shift outward entirely, toward the sea, toward whatever vast arithmetic it was always running, and the grip on him loosened the way a fist loosens in sleep.

He had learned to recognize these moments. They came maybe twice a day, lasted seconds, sometimes a full minute if the god was deep in its dreaming of tides and names. He had wasted the first dozen in panic, sobbing, screaming into the inside of his skull. He had learned better. He had learned to hoard them like a miser.

He had one minute. Maybe less.

His mouth moved. His voice—his true voice, rusty, cracked, a stranger's and utterly his own—came out low and fast, pitched under the room's silence:

"Hear me. Hear me quick. I'm in here. I'm still in here."

Sefa's head came up sharply. Her chained hands went still. Her eyes—flat sea-color a moment ago—went wide and then narrow, fast, calculating, and he saw her understand in the space of a breath, the way dockside people understood things, whole ledgers at a glance: the change in the voice, the shaking hands, the sweat standing out on the brow of a king who a moment ago had been carved from marble.

"Your Majesty," she said carefully.

"Don't waste the time on me." The words fell out of him like coins from a torn purse. "It's listening through me when it wants—it'll feel this, later, maybe—I don't know how much I can hide even now. Listen. The binding. It told you it didn't break its bindings. It lied. Half-lied. Its bindings broke, yes, but the *anchor*—the place it was tied to when the Sundering took the rest—was never destroyed. It can't return to the deep while the anchor holds. That's why it wears a crown. That's why it needs the throne, the city, the worship—it's *feeding the anchor*, keeping itself moored here until it's strong enough to snap the last line and take the whole coast down with it."

"Where?" she breathed. "Where is it anchored?"

"The drowned harbor." The words were costing him; he could feel the god's attention beginning to turn back like a weather-vane swinging. "Under the old city—the pre-Sundering quay, where your maps say only silt. There's a stone there. Not an idol—a *keel-stone*, first stone laid when Vessamare was founded, they bound it into the foundation oath, king by king, generation by generation—my line, my blood, that's why it needed *me*—" His vision was graying at the edges; the pressure behind his eyes was enormous, patient, returning. "The ritual to unbind it exists. Tide-cult liturgy, third hidden book, the one your order calls *the Ledger of Return*. Find the cartographer. Whatever it offered you for his name—don't. He's the only one alive who can chart the way down."

"How do you know about—"

"It dreams," Aldric gasped. "It dreams its memories through me. I've seen your face in its dreams. I've seen *his*." The gray was closing. He forced the last words out through teeth that were no longer entirely his. "Tell him—tell the cartographer—it will offer him everything he ever sold away. It keeps what men trade to brokers. Don't let him—"

The world slammed shut.

* * *

He came back to himself standing at the window with his hands clasped behind his back, watching gulls over the gray water, and the god's attention settled over him again like a wet sail.

*You spoke*, it said. Not in sound. In pressure, in depth, in the voice of the sea heard through a ship's hull.

*I don't know what you mean*, Aldric thought, arranging his mind into blankness, into the dull loyal fog he had learned to fake.

*A minute*, the god mused. *Perhaps ninety seconds. You spend them so recklessly, little king. On a smuggler. On secrets.* A pause, long as a tide turning. *Do you imagine she will thank you? Do you imagine any of them will know?*

*No*, Aldric answered honestly, because lying to it about large things had never worked and small truths made good camouflage. *No. Probably not.*

*Then why?*

And Aldric, watching the gulls, found that he had an answer, had carried it since the night on the seawall, since the prayer that had damned him—that had, he still believed, in the stubborn chamber of himself that the god could not reach, *saved* him:

*Because a king who does nothing while his people are eaten is not a king. Because somebody has to be in the room with the monster, and I'm the only one who gets to stay.*

The god considered this the way the sea considers a swimmer.

*Sentiment*, it said at last, almost fondly. *The anchor-drug of your entire species. Very well. Keep your ninety seconds. Spend them where you like. It changes nothing. By the new moon the tide comes in over everything you love, and I will wear your face while it does, and you will watch.*

*Maybe*, Aldric said.

And then, because he was still a king, still Aldric Oyelaran of the founding line, still the man whose blood was mixed into a keel-stone under a drowned harbor—he added, in the deepest vault of himself, where even gods grew tired of digging:

*But you should know something, Ossuar. You sleep sometimes. And I have started counting the nights.*

The god did not answer. But somewhere below the palace, below the silt, below the drowned quay where the first stone of Vessamare lay bound in oaths and salt, something old and vast turned over in its anchorage—and for the first time in three hundred years, it dreamed uneasily.

In her cell beneath the morning room, Sefa Vane pressed her bleeding lip closed with two fingers and began, silently, to recite the opening lines of the Ledger of Return, committing herself to memory the way her order committed everything: against the day the books burned.

*Third hidden book. Drowned harbor. Keel-stone. Find the cartographer.*

She had a name to trade for it after all—not his name, never his name, but a description, a debt, a direction. All she had to do now was survive long enough to spend it.
