## 13. The Unmapped Realm

The veil did not open so much as admit him.

Xanthe had imagined a door all his life—had drawn doors on his charts, little hatched thresholds at the edges of the drowned sea where his ink always seemed to pool and darken as if the paper itself remembered something he didn't. He had assumed crossing would feel like falling.

It felt like being read.

He stood in a place that was not Vessamare's night market or Salt Row or any street he had ever walked, and it was made of everything he had ever drawn. Coastlines hung in the air like ribbons of wet silk. A mountain range drifted past at knee height, its peaks still glistening with the graphite of his earliest stylus. The light came from nowhere and was the color of vellum, warm and patient, and beneath his feet—or through them, for there was no ground exactly—the sea moved. Not water. The idea of water. The Sundered Sea before it sundered, rendered in the confident strokes of a cartographer who had once believed maps could hold the truth of things.

"Careful," Sefa had told him at the harbor's edge, her salt-crusted fingers gripping his sleeve. "The tide-cult texts say the realm beyond takes its shape from what you bring. Go in carrying fear and you'll drown in it. Go in carrying want—" she had stopped herself, jaw tightening, "—and you'll never come out."

He had gone in carrying both, because he was Xanthe Oyelaran, and that was the only way he knew how to travel.

* * *

He walked because walking was the one language every realm understood.

The coastlines followed him. He noticed it after the first hundred steps: whenever he turned, the ribbons of ink rearranged themselves, shy as fish, presenting him always with new horizons and never the same one twice. His own handiwork, curving around him like a school of nervous eels.

"You're not real," he said aloud, and his voice fell flat here, absorbed rather than echoed. "None of this is."

But even as he said it, the realm rippled with something like offense, and the nearest coastline—a stretch of the old southern shore he had charted at nineteen, back when his hands still shook with ambition—curled inward to show him the detail work. Every cove. Every sound. The little notation he'd invented for tidal caves, three dots and a dash, which no other mapmaker had ever used.

This place wasn't showing off. It was introducing itself. *I am what you know,* it seemed to say. *Nothing more. Nothing less.*

That was worse than a lie. That was a mirror.

The ritual text—Sefa's half of it, smuggled out of the witch-hunters' archive in a hollowed prayer-book—was clear about the next step, and merciless about what it required. *To write the god's name upon the living sea, the writer must first stand upon the sea as it was.* And the sea as it was existed now only beyond the veil, only here, in the memory-shaped country where Xanthe had spent three years peering like a boy at a locked garden gate.

He had mapped this realm from afar. He had never dreamed he'd have to walk it.

Ahead, the vellum light thickened, and shapes began to resolve in it—not geography now but figures, standing in loose clusters like people waiting at a ferry landing. At first he thought they were worshippers, the hollowed ones Ossuar had harvested, and his stomach clenched with guilt for every charm he'd ever sold.

Then the nearest figure turned, and Xanthe stopped walking.

It had his face.

Not approximately. Not the vague resemblance of kinship. It was his face as it had been at twenty-two, before the lines carved by sleeplessness, before he'd taught himself to flinch at the word *mage*. The young man wore the traveling coat Xanthe had owned in another city, in another life, and he looked at Xanthe with an expression of mild, terrible curiosity—as if Xanthe were the stranger here.

"You're late," the young man said. His voice was Xanthe's voice, younger, unbroken by caution. "We've been waiting years. You don't remember how many."

* * *

They gathered as he approached, the way a shoreline gathers driftwood. There were nine of them. Xanthe counted twice, because counting was the oldest magic he knew, and because he needed something to do with his hands besides shake.

Nine versions of himself, arranged along a shore that was not a shore, each wearing a year of his life like a uniform.

A boy of perhaps twelve, barefoot, clutching a stub of charcoal, the age when he'd first discovered he could see the seams between realms. A youth of sixteen with ink-stained cuffs and a bruise fading on his cheekbone—Xanthe touched his own cheek without meaning to; he had no memory of that bruise. A man of twenty-five in the gray coat of a guild apprentice, the one who had walked into the memory-broker's shop on Wren Street and asked, *how much for a year?*

That one wouldn't meet his eyes.

"You sold us," the boy said. Not accusing. Simply stating it, the way children state weather. "One by one. To keep the rest alive." He turned the charcoal over in his small fingers. "Do you know what it's like, being a thing someone chose not to be?"

"I didn't choose," Xanthe heard himself say. "The purges—"

"You chose," said the twenty-five-year-old, finally looking up, and his eyes were the worst thing in the realm, because they were tired the way Xanthe's eyes were tired, tired with a weariness Xanthe had paid to forget. "You sat across from the broker and she laid out the years like coins and you picked. You kept your craft. You gave up—" he gestured at himself, at all of them, "—us. Mother. The house on the headland. Your name before you shortened it. Every year you spent learning that magic was a thing you loved instead of a thing that got people burned."

Xanthe's mouth was dry. Here, in a realm shaped by memory, dryness was a choice too, he realized—the realm giving him the sensation because some part of him expected it. Everything here was expectation. Everything here was him.

"The ritual needs me whole," he managed. "That's what the text says. Whole enough to write."

"No," said the boy softly. "The text says *whole*. You brought half a man across the veil and hoped we wouldn't mention it."

The shore shifted underfoot—not violently, just a slow settling, like sand after a wave—and Xanthe understood that the realm was listening. That his terror and his grief were becoming landscape even as he felt them. Somewhere behind the nine figures, a cliff rose that hadn't been there before, tall and sheer, and he knew without asking that it was the wall of his own denial, freshly built.

"I need to get to the deep water," he said. "The name of Ossuar is written where the old harbor stood. If I can reach it—"

"You can't reach anything here by walking," said the young version of him, almost kindly. "You should know that. You drew this realm. Distance in here isn't measured in miles." He held up his hand, and ink welled in his palm like blood. "It's measured in what you'll take back."

* * *

They led him inland, or what passed for it, and the country unspooled around them in scenes.

A kitchen with copper pots and steam on the windows. His mother humming a song whose melody Xanthe's heart recognized and his mind did not—he stopped dead in front of it, and the scene let him stop, let him stand at its edge like a beggar at a lit window, close enough to feel the warmth and not one degree warmer.

"That's yours," the boy said quietly, watching him. "Year four. She sang that when you were sick, and you forgot being sick, and you forgot her voice with it." A pause. "The broker keeps it well, wherever she is. Or kept. Or—" the boy shrugged, a gesture Xanthe knew intimately, having used it his whole life to mean *I will not cry about this now.*

"We're not here to torture you," said the twenty-five-year-old, taking his elbow. His grip was solid, warm, unbearably human. "We're here because you can't finish this alone, and we are the only company you brought."

"Then help me," Xanthe said. "Show me the way to the deep water. Show me where the name goes."

The nine exchanged glances—nine variations of the same face deciding—and then the eldest among them, a man Xanthe's own age but grayer, calmer, stepped forward. This one he somehow hadn't noticed before, though he must have been there all along. The eldest wore plain clothes and carried nothing, and his eyes held the particular stillness of someone who has finished grieving.

"I'm the one you'd be," the eldest said, "if you'd never sold anything. I'm not real yet. I'm the sum the arithmetic keeps promising." He smiled, and it hurt. "You want the deep water? Then answer honestly. Why are you doing this?"

"For Aldric. For the hollowed. For—"

"Why?"

And the realm went silent around the question, every drifting coastline stilled, every scene frozen mid-flicker, waiting. Because the realm was memory, and memory does not accept performance.

Xanthe stood in the middle of himself and tried to find the true answer, and found, to his horror, that he couldn't locate it cleanly—that somewhere in the years he'd traded away lay the reason he'd become a mapmaker at all, the original wound or wonder that set his hand moving, and he had sold it so thoroughly he no longer knew whether he freed kings out of mercy or out of some borrowed, secondhand longing for the person he used to be.

"I don't know," he admitted.

The realm exhaled. The cliffs softened. The kitchen scene dimmed to embers, mercifully.

"Good," said the eldest. "That's the first honest thing you've said since you crossed. The unmapped realm doesn't punish ignorance, Xanthe. It punishes pretense. Now—" he pointed, and the horizon split, and far away, down a long corridor of darkening water, something vast and patient waited: the drowned harbor, the living sea, the place where a god's name slept in the silt like a knife in a wound. "There's your deep water. But understand the price before you walk toward it."

"Everything costs," Xanthe said. "I know the currency."

"You think you do." The eldest shook his head slowly. "Out there, you pay in years you chose to forget. In here, writing on the living water, you'll pay in memories you *keep*. Chosen ones. The ones that make you *you*." He leaned close, and his breath smelled of ink and salt. "Ossuar knows this. The god built the bargain that way. Every stroke of the name will take something you love knowing, and you won't get to pick which. So ask yourself, cartographer: how much of yourself is the map worth?"

* * *

They walked him to the edge of the corridor and no farther.

At the boundary—where the vellum light failed and the deep water began, black and breathing—the nine stood in a line behind him, and Xanthe felt their gazes on his back like sunlight through glass.

"Wait," he said, turning. "When this is done—if I survive—do I get you back? All of you?"

The boy looked at the eldest, and the eldest looked at the water, and for a long moment nobody answered, and the silence was the answer.

"Some prices aren't refunds," the boy said finally. "But—" and he smiled, quick and bright, twelve years old forever, "—your hands will remember us. Hands are loyal that way. When you draw the new world, we'll be in the lines. Look closely."

Xanthe wanted to say something worthy of the moment. What he said was, "I'm sorry," and the realm took the words and made of them a small rain that fell upward, back toward the light, back toward the self he might have been.

Then he turned and stepped off the edge of his own memory into the black water, and it closed over him like a page turning, and far above—or far behind, distance meant nothing now—he heard the eldest call out, one last thing, urgent and thin across the darkening miles:

"It knows you're here! The god has felt every step—it's already sending something up from the harbor to meet you, and it wears a face you sold!"

The water swallowed the warning. Around him, in the depths, something was rising—and even before he saw it, even before the shape resolved in the abyssal dark, Xanthe knew whose face it would be, because there was only one face he had ever sold that Ossuar could use against him, only one year worth more than all the others combined—

His mother opened her eyes beneath the sea, and smiled at him with a god's patience, and reached out her hand.
