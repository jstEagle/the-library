## 14. Negotiating with a God

The dream began with ink.

Xanthe knew it was a dream because the sea was wrong—too calm, too black, stretched flat as a sheet of vellum from one horizon to the other, and because he was standing on it. His boots made no sound. When he looked down, he saw that the water was not water at all but paper, and across its surface ran lines of fine cartographic script in a hand he recognized as his own: depth-soundings, current-marks, the little careful annotations he had spent three years teaching himself to stop making.

*Here be the Sundered Sea,* said a voice that came from everywhere and nowhere, from beneath the paper and above it. *Here be nothing. Here be what I drowned.*

"Ossuar." He said the name aloud to anchor himself. In dreams, names were rope.

The horizon moved. That was the only way he could describe it—the whole black line of it tilted, swung toward him like a door opening, and out of the dark between the stars came something vast and patient wearing the shape of a man. It was almost Aldric's shape, but not quite. The proportions were a half-breath off, the way a portrait is off when painted by someone who has only ever heard the subject described. Its eyes were the eyes of a drowned thing: white, lidless, lit from behind by no light at all.

"You are difficult to find," Ossuar said. "You hide well for a man who sells luck-knots."

"I hide well," Xanthe said, "for a man who wants to keep living."

"And yet here you are. In my sea. On my page." The god looked down at the black vellum under their feet with something like affection. "You drew this once. Did you know? Not this copy. The original. Before the Sundering, when your craft was not a crime. You were very good. You are still very good—you simply don't remember how good."

Xanthe's mouth went dry. "I've never drawn anything before the Sundering. I was a child."

"Were you?" The god smiled with Aldric's mouth, and it was terrible, because the smile was warm. Kings smiled like that when they wanted something from you. "Let me show you."

It reached out—not touching him, never quite touching him—and turned over a corner of the sea-paper as if lifting the edge of a tablecloth. Beneath was not more darkness. Beneath was a memory.

*

He stood in a harbor that did not exist anymore. He knew it instantly, the way you know your own handwriting: the great curved mole of the pre-Sundering quay, the lighthouse burning oil that smelled of myrrh, the ships riding at anchor with their figureheads carved as leaping fish. And there, at the foot of the seawall, a woman was teaching a boy of perhaps ten years to draw.

She held his small hand in hers, guiding the charcoal across a slate. "No," she was saying, laughing, "you're drawing the *idea* of the ship. Draw the *ship*. Look at where the water bites her hull. Everything true is in the meeting of two things—the water and the wood, the light and the stone. A map is just a record of meetings."

The boy looked up, and his face—

His face was Xanthe's face. Younger. Whole. Eyes bright with the particular hunger of a child who has found the thing he will do forever.

"That was you," Ossuar said softly, beside him now, watching the memory with the tenderness of a collector viewing a prized piece. "That was the first year I took. Do you see? You didn't sell me that one. You sold it long before I ever came to collect. But it was always mine to return."

The harbor flickered. The woman turned, as if she had heard them, and Xanthe saw her face clearly for the first time, and something in his chest tore along a seam he had not known was there. He did not know her name. He should have known her name. He knew the way she held chalk, the callus on her middle finger, the exact angle of her laugh—but her name was a hole shaped like a word, and around the hole, everything else remained, sharp and agonizing in its detail.

"Mama," he said. The word came out cracked. He hadn't meant to say it.

"She called herself the Mapmaker of the Mole," Ossuar said. "She drowned with the harbor, in the first wave of the Sundering. You survived because you were quick and because you were lucky, and later, because you paid. Shall I tell you what else is mine? Your sister's voice. Your teacher's name—it begins with an L, does that help? The smell of your childhood kitchen. Seven years of your life, sold in pieces to brokers so that the witch-hunters could never follow the thread of who you were back to what you could do. And the original chart of this sea, which you burned yourself rather than let them find it—which I kept, because I keep everything that sinks."

The god gestured, and the black sea-paper bloomed. Charts rose out of it like fish surfacing—hundreds of them, thousands, every map Xanthe had ever drawn or dreamed of drawing, hanging in the dark like leaves in still water. His life's work, complete. The masterwork he'd been too frightened to attempt since he was twenty-two years old, already finished, waiting.

"All of it," Ossuar said. "Every year you sold. Every name you traded away. Your mother's name. Your own name, the true one, the one you buried so deep even the brokers couldn't price it—I have that too. Leave Aldric bound. Walk away from the priestess, from the ritual, from the drowned harbor. Go home to your little room above the chandler's shop, and tomorrow morning you will wake up *whole*. Every memory restored, seamless, painless. You will be the person you were always meant to be. The greatest cartographer this coast has ever produced. No hiding. No fear. No more selling pieces of yourself to stay alive."

The god leaned close, and its breath smelled of low tide and old iron.

"I am not cruel, little mapmaker. I am *fair*. This is the fairest offer any god has ever made a mortal. All I ask is that you stop trying to take from me one king."

*

The dream held its breath.

Xanthe stood in the middle of his own recovered life and felt the pull of it the way a drowning man feels the surface—total, simple, gravitational. His mother was right there. Her name was one syllable away. He could feel it pressing against the inside of the hole where it had been, patient as water.

And that was how he knew it was a trap.

Not because the offer was false—he believed, absolutely, that Ossuar would honor it. Gods of Ossuar's vintage did not lie; they didn't need to. They only needed to be generous at the correct moment.

He knew it was a trap because of what the wholeness would *do* to him.

He thought about the boy in the night market, three weeks ago, whispering a god's name with fever-bright eyes while his mother wept into her hands. He thought about the dockworker, Bren, who had bought a luck-knot and prayed too hard and now sat in a shrine with eyes like unlit lamps. He thought about Sefa's face when she'd spoken of her congregation—forty people, forty hollowed faithful, devotion harvested like wheat. He thought about Aldric, scratching pleas into a palace wall with fingernails worn to the quick, minutes of selfhood bought at some cost the god had never explained.

If he woke up whole, he would be the man who had drawn the original chart of this sea. The man with a mother's name and seven unbroken years and no holes anywhere. And that man—that seamless, gifted, grieving man—would have done what?

Would he have hidden? Yes. Of course he would have. A man who remembered losing everything to the Sundering would clutch what he rebuilt with both hands. A man who remembered his mother's face clearly would not risk himself for a stranger in a crown; he would go inland, far inland, past the salt line, where the gods' voices couldn't reach. A whole man protects his wholeness. That was the oldest arithmetic there was.

But the man with holes—the patched man, the deniable man, the man who had already paid and paid and paid until payment itself had become his native language—that man had walked into Salt Row with a smuggler-priestess. That man had mapped servant passages in real time with witch-hunters at his heels. That man was standing, at this moment, in a dead god's sea, refusing to drown.

The gaps had made him. The missing years were not scars over the purpose; they *were* the purpose, the way a river's shape is made by what has been removed from its path.

"You want to make me someone who would never have come this far," Xanthe said.

Ossuar's expression did not change, but the sea darkened by a shade, the way a room darkens when a cloud crosses the sun. "I want to make you *happy*."

"No." He shook his head slowly. "You want to make me *finished*. There's a difference. Happiness isn't the bait, it's the hook. You dangle my mother's name in front of me because you know that whoever takes it stops being the kind of person who stands in front of a god and says no. Wholeness isn't a gift, it's a cage with all the walls put back. You'd give me everything I lost, and the price is that I'd become someone who never would have walked into the palace, never met Sefa, never seen the king's plea scratched into the stone. You wouldn't be buying Aldric's freedom from me. You'd be buying it from someone I'll never meet."

The charts hanging in the dark trembled, all together, like a forest in wind.

"Clever," Ossuar said. The warmth drained out of Aldric's borrowed voice, and underneath it was the sound the sea makes against a hull at night, vast and indifferent and older than speech. "The brokers taught you this, didn't they. To audit your own grief. To hold your longing at arm's length and weigh it. What a wretched thing we have made of you, we gods and our laws and our purges. What a *wretched*, useful thing."

"I'm not clever," Xanthe said. "I'm just expensive. You should know. You're holding most of the bill."

Something that was almost laughter moved through the black water. "Then hear my last fairness. I will not offer again. When next we speak, I will take what remains of you, and I will wear your hands the way I wear the king's, and I will draw maps for a thousand years with your clever fingers, and you will watch from inside and learn what Aldric has learned about the hospitality of gods."

"Then take it now," Xanthe said, "and save us both the conversation."

For a long moment the god only looked at him. And in that look, Xanthe understood something that made his skin crawl: Ossuar *couldn't*. Not here, not yet. The dream-chart was a parley ground, old law binding even things as old as it. It could tempt him. It could terrify him. It could not touch him until he set foot in its domain in the flesh—at the drowned harbor, where the ritual waited, where the god's strength pooled like bilgewater.

Which meant the god needed him to come willingly. Or needed him broken enough to come anyway.

"You will come," Ossuar said at last, gently, horribly. "Because you are already paying, and men who pay always believe the next installment will be the last. Because the priestess believes in you, and her faith is a rope around your neck. And because—" the god's smile returned, and now it was not Aldric's smile at all, it was something wearing Aldric's smile the way a hand wears a glove, "—because I have begun, little mapmaker. While you sleep and bargain, I have begun. Ask your city what it dreamed tonight. Ask it why the temples are full at this hour. Ask it why the bells are ringing."

Far away—or not far away at all; in the dream there was no far—the sound arrived. Bells. Hundreds of them, discordant, frantic, the alarm-pattern Vessamare used only for fire or invasion.

"What have you done?" Xanthe whispered.

"What I promised the faithful," Ossuar said. "They prayed for deliverance. I am delivering them." It stepped backward into the dark, and the sea-paper began to roll up around Xanthe's feet like a scroll closing. "Come to the harbor, cartographer. Bring your ink. Bring your courage, whatever's left of it after all these sales. And bring your grief—it is the finest instrument you own. I will be waiting with everything you sold me. We will settle accounts, you and I. One way or another, we will settle accounts."

The black page curled shut over his head, and the last thing he saw was his mother's face, turning toward him through the closing fold, mouthing a name he would not let himself read.

*

He woke on the floor of Sefa's back room with salt crusted on his lips and dawn the color of a bruise coming through the shutters, and the bells were real.

He could hear screaming now too, distant but spreading, and beneath it a sound he had no name for—a vast rhythmic sighing, like a congregation breathing as one, coming from the direction of the Temple Quarter. Sefa burst through the door already dressed, a knife in her belt and a look on her face that stopped his heart: not fear. Recognition.

"The hollowed," she said. "They're standing up. All of them, all across the city—they're standing up and walking toward the water." She grabbed his arm, her grip bruising. "Xanthe. Whatever happened in your dream—we don't have days anymore. We have hours. Maybe less. Tell me you know the rest of the ritual."

He looked at his hands. Half a text. Half a name. Half a man.

"We go to the harbor now," he said, "and I finish it, or we drown with everyone else."

Outside, the bells went on ringing, and somewhere beneath them, patient as tide, the sea had begun to rise.
