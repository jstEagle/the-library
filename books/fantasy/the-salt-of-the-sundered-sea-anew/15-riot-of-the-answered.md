## 15. Riot of the Answered

The cell smelled of brine and old blood, and Sefa had learned to tell time by the drips.

One drip from the ceiling seam every forty heartbeats. She counted them the way she'd once counted knots in a net, the way her mother had taught her to count waves before a crossing—because counting was a kind of prayer, and prayer was the only thing the witch-hunters hadn't thought to take from her.

Her wrists were raw where the salt-iron manacles bit. Salt-iron: the Crown's one honest inheritance from the Sundering. It didn't hurt, exactly. It *muted*. Somewhere beneath her skin, the tide she had served since she was nine years old went on pulling, patient as it had always been, and the iron sat over it like a hand pressed flat on water. She could feel her god—*her* god, the small wet dark one of Salt Row, not the thing that wore the king—and she could feel how far away he'd become, muffled under all that metal, like a voice calling up through fathoms.

Three days in this cell. Or four. The drips said three; her bruises said four.

Through the slit window—thumb-wide, angled so she could see nothing but a stripe of sky and the crown of a tower—she heard the city change.

It began with bells. Vessamare rang bells for everything: coronations, market days, executions, the tide-festival that had come and gone while she rotted here. But these bells were wrong. They were rung fast and ragged, no pattern, no order, alarm-bells dragged by hands that didn't know which rope was theirs, and underneath them came a sound Sefa knew from her childhood in the flooded districts—the sound of a crowd that has stopped being a crowd and started being water.

Boots in the corridor. Keys. Then a voice she recognized and hated, warm as spilled tallow.

"Up, priestess." Sergeant Halloway, witch-hunter, a man who smelled of incense he stole from the shrines he emptied. "You'll want to see this. Or maybe you won't. Maybe you'll pray about it."

"Which god are you afraid of tonight, sergeant?" she asked, because her mouth had never learned obedience. "The outlawed ones, or the one wearing your king?"

His fist taught her ribs a lesson about questions. She laughed through it, because that too was liturgy, the oldest rite her cult kept: *they can have your body, never your mockery.* Then he hauled her up by the chain between her wrists and dragged her to the slit window anyway, and made her look, and Sefa stopped laughing.

The Answered were walking.

She had seen them before, of course—the hollow-eyed faithful carried out of shrines by their weeping families, set in chairs in doorways like dolls, fed with spoons, spoken to in the bright false voices people use around the dying. Forty of them across the city that anyone would admit to. Probably more. Empty vessels, the priests whispered. Devotion harvested clean.

But empty vessels still hold a shape. And something had come along and poured itself into every one of them at once.

They moved down the Processional Way in perfect unison—not marching, nothing so crude—*gliding*, hundreds of feet lifting and setting down in the same rhythm, as though the street itself were breathing them forward. Their eyes were open. That was the worst of it. The hollowed had always stared at nothing, but now they stared at everything, and there was something behind their gaze like lamplight behind horn. In the lead, arms swinging in the shared rhythm, Sefa saw a broad back and a missing left ear.

Tam. Her dockworker. Her first customer in Vessamare, the man who'd bought a luck-knot against his own better judgment and gone home to a son who lived. He'd been found in the Shrine of the Drowned Compass two days after her arrest, sitting upright among the pews, smiling.

He was still smiling. They were all still smiling.

"Holy Mother of Tides," she breathed, and felt the salt-iron bite as she pressed her forehead to the wall. Not a curse. A diagnosis. "He's gathered them. He's *wearing* them."

"The Answered," Halloway said. His voice had lost its warmth. "They started rising an hour past midnight. All at once. From their beds, their chairs, the hospices. Walking toward the temples."

"Toward?"

The sergeant's jaw worked. "Away from, actually. Away from the old ones. Toward the Grand Basilica. And when the basilica priests barred the doors—" He stopped. Swallowed. "You've seen what the sea does to a breakwater when it decides the breakwater is a suggestion. It was like that. With people. Doors don't matter. Bones don't matter. They just keep coming, smiling, and what's left of whatever stands in the way—" He looked at her then, and she saw that the witch-hunter was afraid in the specific way of a man whose profession had just been revealed to be a superstition. "Your lot spent thirty years telling us the gods were dead or harmless. Which lie was it, priestess?"

"Neither," Sefa said. "We told you they were *sleeping*. We told you not to wake them. You put a god in a crown and called it a miracle."

"I put nothing anywhere. I arrest smugglers." But his hand shook on his baton, and both of them turned back to the window, because there was nothing else in the world worth looking at.

*

From her thumb-width of sky, Sefa watched Vessamare discover that salvation has a body count.

It happened the way fires happen—one spark of panic finding dry tinder everywhere. First the temple district: someone, some terrified apprentice or some zealous captain, decided that if the gods were answering prayers again, the answer was to burn every altar in the city before more prayers got answered. By midmorning smoke stood up from six points of the skyline in black columns, and the bells had given up entirely, replaced by a lower sound, a continuous roar like surf, which was the sound of ten thousand voices with nothing left to believe except each other's fear.

She saw the mob come down Salt Row itself—her street, her people—and she gripped the bars until her knuckles bled because she could do nothing, nothing, chained in the Crown's cellar while the tide-cult's last safehouses went up like festival lanterns. Old Merren's chandlery, where the ritual texts slept in oilskin under the floor. The bathhouse with its false-bottomed cistern. The shrine behind the fishmonger's that wasn't even hers, wasn't even real, just a room where lonely people came to say things aloud.

They burned it all. They burned it singing hymns, half of them, hymns to gods they were killing, because terror makes strange liturgists.

And through the smoke, unhurried, untouched, walked the Answered. The fire parted around them or perhaps they simply did not burn—Sefa couldn't tell at that distance, and told herself she couldn't tell, because the alternative was worse. They walked out of the burning district and turned, all together, all at once, toward the palace on its hill. Toward the crown. Home to the hand that moved them.

"They're going to him," she whispered. "All of them. He's calling his congregation home."

Halloway had gone gray. "To the king."

"To what's *in* the king." She turned on him, chains rattling, and put every year of her hidden priesthood into her voice. "Listen to me, sergeant, because you're going to have exactly one moment of usefulness in your life and I'm telling you this is it. Ossuar doesn't want the palace. Palaces are furniture. He wants the *city*. Every answered prayer was a hook, and every hook has a line, and the lines all run to the throne now. Your witch-hunters, your wards, your salt-iron—" she rattled her cuffs at him "—none of it was built for a god that learned patience. You've been guarding the henhouse against foxes while something old grew up inside your king."

"Then why haven't we all died already?" Halloway shot back, and it was a fair question, and she hated him for having it. "If this Ossuar is so mighty, why is he still sending puppets around on errands? Why hasn't he just—"

"Because a god bound into a body is a god on a leash," Sefa said. "Even a leash he wove himself. He needs the congregation because the congregation is strength. He needs worship the way a drowning man needs air. And there's something he's afraid of, sergeant, something that can write his name out of the world, and he's spending an army to stop it." She thought of Xanthe—of the cartographer's careful, deniable hands, of the chart she'd glimpsed rolled in his coat, inked with a coastline that predated the Sundering—and prayed, for the first time in years, to a god she couldn't feel through the iron: *be faster than the tide, mapmaker.*

Halloway stared at her. Down the corridor, the whole prison was screaming—keys turning, cell doors banging, the garrison abandoning their posts in orderly, cowardly streams.

"You're enjoying this," he said weakly.

"I'm grieving," Sefa said. "It sounds similar from outside."

*

The keys arrived at dusk, and they arrived in the hands of a girl.

Sefa knew her instantly, though they'd never spoken: Liora Vance, seventeen, freckled, perpetually worried, daughter of Tam Vance the dockworker—no. No, that was wrong, and Sefa caught the error the way you catch a stair missing in the dark. Tam's daughter was named Maren, and Maren was twelve and had been moved to her aunt's house in the hill country months ago, thank every merciful depth. This girl was Wren Corvane, daughter of Joss Corvane, who had rowed with Sefa's crew for six years, run cargo and contraband and the occasional fugitive priest, and who eleven days ago had sold them all to the Crown's witch-hunters for one reason only: the hunters had shown him a list of names, and his daughter's was on it, and the price of striking a name off was striking five others on.

Betrayal on Salt Row. Sefa had spat at his feet when they dragged her past him. She'd meant every drop of it.

Wren Corvane stood at her cell door with a ring of stolen keys and eyes red-rimmed from crying, wearing her father's guilt like a hand-me-down coat two sizes too large.

"He's hollowed too," the girl said, before Sefa could speak. "My da. Last night. Mam found him standing in the kitchen at moonset, facing the sea, smiling. He didn't—he couldn't—" Her breath hitched. "The guards all ran. Nobody's watching the cells. I've been down three rows already. Everyone's gone. So I came for you."

"Why?" Sefa asked. It wasn't cruelty. It was triage; trust was a resource and she was down to her last coins. "Because you love justice? Because you pity a smuggler?"

"Because my father sold five people to save me," Wren said, "and the god took him anyway, and I have been sitting in our rooms for two days understanding that there is no trade that works. There is no arithmetic that comes out even. He did the math everyone does, and the answer was still *them taking everything*." She lifted her chin, and for a second she looked so much like her father on a bad crossing that Sefa's chest hurt. "So I'm done with math. I'm letting everyone out of these cells that I can reach, starting with you, because the woman in the next row says you're the only person in this city who knows what that thing in the palace actually wants."

"And what does the next row say I should do about it?"

"She says find the cartographer." Wren's hands were shaking on the keys, but they found the right one. The lock gave. "She says the cartographer's gone to the drowned harbor with half a ritual and no time."

Sefa stepped out of the cell, and the absence of the salt-iron hit her like surfacing—her whole body ringing, the tide rushing back into channels long dammed, her small wet dark god suddenly *close*, close enough to taste, filling her like cold clean water. She swayed. Wren caught her elbow.

"Careful," the girl said. "You look like you're drowning in reverse."

"That's exactly what this is." Sefa flexed her fingers, feeling power pool in her palms for the first time in eleven days, and looked up through the grate at the burning skyline, at the column of the Answered flowing up the palace road like a river running uphill. Somewhere in that flow, maybe, walked Joss Corvane, smiling. Somewhere on a flooded quay, a man who paid for his survival in memories was drawing a name in living water, spending himself stroke by stroke, and he had half a ritual text and none of the words that opened the final seal—words that lived in Sefa's memory alone, because she had eaten the page years ago rather than let it be found.

Words the traitor's daughter had just handed her the freedom to carry.

"The drowned harbor," Sefa said. "North quay, below the old customs house, where the sea took the streets. Can you get us out of the city?"

Wren almost smiled—a small, cracked, defiant thing. "There's a smugglers' gate under the fish market. My da showed me when I was little. He said, *if anything ever goes truly wrong, girl, go to the water.*" Her voice broke on it, and steadied. "I think it finally counts."

Above them, the last bell in Vessamare fell silent, cut off mid-peal, as though a hand had closed gently over the city's mouth.

Sefa ran, and the girl ran with her, and behind them the prison stood open and empty, and ahead the sea waited—dark, sundered, listening—for whatever name was being written upon it.

"Keep up, Wren Corvane," Sefa called over the roar of the burning city. "Tonight we learn what the water remembers."
