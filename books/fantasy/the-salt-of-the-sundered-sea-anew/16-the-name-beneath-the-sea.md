## 16. The Name Beneath the Sea

The drowned harbor still remembered its shape.

Xanthe felt it before he saw it, standing on the black shelf of rock above the tideline where Vessamare's older self lay sunk beneath the Sundered Sea. The water here was not like the water of the inner harbor—flat, salt-locked, obedient to the tides as a hound is obedient to its master's whistle. This water *moved*. It moved with intention, slow and vast, the way a sleeping man moves in his sleep, turning toward a sound. It had been waiting three hundred years for someone to come down to it and speak.

Sefa stood beside him at the edge of the rocks, her tide-cult vestments stripped away, dressed now as she had been the first night he'd met her: plain wool, salt-stiff boots, a smuggler's knife at her hip because old habits were the last things anyone surrendered. Behind them both, half-hidden among the sea-stacks, waited what remained of her crew—two faces he trusted, one he did not, and the half-burned ritual text bound against his ribs beneath his coat, warm as a living thing.

"You don't have to do this the hard way," Sefa said. It was the fourth time she'd said it since they'd left Salt Row, and each time she said it a little more quietly, as if the words themselves were being eroded by repetition, worn smooth like sea glass. "We could go back for the other half of the text. We could find another way."

"There is no other way." He said it without heat, without argument. That was the strange thing about decisions that cost everything—they arrived not as thunder but as arithmetic, quiet and final. Aldric was running out of minutes. The hollow congregation was growing. And somewhere beneath the flooded streets of the burning city behind them, Ossuar was learning, hour by hour, how to be a king. "The name has to be written on living water. The text is explicit. Water that moves, water that remembers."

"And every stroke takes something from you." She wasn't asking. Her eyes stayed on the sea, but he could feel her attention on him like a hand on the back of his neck. "You told me the god took your memories when you sold them. Now you're going to give them away yourself."

"That's different."

"How?"

He turned the question over honestly, because she deserved honesty tonight, of all nights. "When the broker took them, I was selling pieces of myself to buy my survival. It was a transaction. I was goods changing hands." He looked out at the dark water, at the shapes below it—the sunken arches of the old customs house just visible in the moonlight, a drowned bell tower leaning like a drunkard, streets that had been streets when gods walked openly and magic was a craft and not a crime. "This is different because I choose which memories go. I choose the order. I choose what I keep until the end. That's the only thing left that's mine, Sefa. Not what I remember. *How* I let it go."

She said nothing for a long moment. Then she reached over and gripped his forearm, smuggler-fashion, wrist to wrist, the way partners sealed bargains in the night market. "Then let's spend well," she said.

* * *

They went down to the water across a causeway that only existed at this tide—a spine of ancient stone the cult had kept secret through three centuries of outlawry, one of the last roads to the pre-Sundering harbor that anyone alive knew. Xanthe walked it with his gift unshuttered for the first time in the open air, and the world bloomed around him.

It was always like this when he stopped hiding. The veil between realms was not a wall; it was a membrane, thin as oil on water, and his gift let him see the currents running through it. Here at the drowned harbor the currents were enormous. He could see the scar the Sundering had left in the fabric of things—a wound in the world shaped like a coastline, still raw after three hundred years, still bleeding faint light into the deep places. And threaded through the wound, moving with terrible patience, he could see *him*.

Ossuar. Not the puppet-crown in the palace. The god itself, vast and coiled through the sundered sea like ink through water, wearing the drowned city the way a hermit crab wears a stolen shell.

It felt him seeing it.

The sea went still. Every wave between him and the horizon stopped mid-motion, held like a breath, and in that perfect silence Xanthe heard—not a voice, nothing so crude—a pressure against his mind, vast and amused, the way a mountain might be amused by an ant rearranging grains of sand at its foot.

*Little cartographer,* the pressure said. *Come to measure me.*

"I've come to name you," Xanthe said aloud, because his voice worked better than his thoughts did, because speaking aloud kept the terror in his throat instead of his skull. "Names are measurements too. The truest ones."

The amusement sharpened. Somewhere far off, in the city behind them, a temple bell began to ring—alarm or festival, he couldn't tell, and didn't have time to care. The waves resumed their motion, unhurried, contemptuous. *I offered you everything once. I offer again. Your mother's face. Your father's voice. The year you were nine. The road home. Walk away, and wake whole.*

"Whole isn't the same as mine," Xanthe said.

And he knelt at the waterline, and opened the ritual text, and began.

* * *

The first stroke was easy. That was the trap of it—he understood that immediately, the way you understand a current's pull only once you're in it. The ritual required him to draw Ossuar's name onto the surface of the living sea, stroke by stroke, using light pulled from his own gift, and the name was long. Older than language. Each character was a sentence; each sentence was a binding; the whole of it, complete, would be a cage no god could wear.

He drew the first character, and it hung above the water in lines of pale gold, beautiful beyond anything his hidden charms had ever managed, and the sea beneath it shivered with recognition—and then the price came due.

He felt the memory leave the way you feel a tooth come loose: a bright, clean pain, and then a gap where something solid used to be.

*His grandmother's kitchen.* He knew it was gone even as he reached for it—the smell of cardamom, the chipped blue bowl she'd kept olives in, the way she'd sung when she thought no one listened. He could remember *that there had been* a kitchen. He could remember that he had loved her. But the texture of it, the warm particular weight of it—that had poured out of him, down his arm, through his fingers, into the second stroke of gold now burning on the water.

The sea drank it. Somewhere in the deep, Ossuar made a sound that was almost a laugh.

*That one was sweet,* the god said. *Give me another.*

"No," Xanthe said, through gritted teeth. "You get what the ritual takes. Nothing more. You don't get to shop."

But he had learned from the broker's thefts, all those years ago. Learned the hard way, one vanished year at a time. And so before he began he had done what no memory-broker had ever allowed him: he had *ordered* himself. He had sat alone in the shrine on Salt Row with a candle and a scrap of charcoal, and written a list, and memorized the list, and ranked every memory he owned by what he could bear to lose.

The small ones first. The taste of a wine he hadn't loved. A rainy afternoon in a foreign port, pleasant and formless. The name of a man who'd cheated him at dice and the satisfaction of never playing him again. He spent them deliberately, one per stroke, watching each dissolve into gold light on the water, and it hurt less than he expected and more than he could say, because pain wasn't really the shape of it. The shape of it was *choice*. For three years he had been robbed in the dark. Now he stood in the open and paid his debts facing forward, and there was a fierce, terrible dignity in it that he suspected was the closest thing to freedom he would ever hold.

Sefa crouched beside him, reading the text aloud when his concentration wavered, her voice steady as a keel. When the fifth stroke took his memory of the first map he'd ever drawn—his father's study, the vellum, the wonder—she caught his arm as he swayed.

"Stay with me. Say your name."

"Xanthe Oyelaran."

"Say it again."

"Xanthe Oyelaran." His own voice sounded strange, thinner. How many strokes until that, too, was currency? He pushed the thought down and drew the sixth.

* * *

By the twentieth stroke the moon had crossed a third of the sky, and Xanthe had spent the ordinary furniture of a life.

He had given up his tenth summer entire—a season of gulls and rope-burns on some coast he could no longer picture. He had given up the face of the first person he'd ever kissed, keeping only the knowledge that it had happened, a door painted on a wall with no room behind it. He had given up grief, and discovered that grief was load-bearing: without the memory of who he had mourned, the mourning itself became a phantom ache, a cramp in a limb that wasn't there. He wept without knowing why, and Sefa held his shoulders, and he drew the next stroke anyway.

The god grew quieter as the name grew longer. That was the thing Sefa's texts had promised and Xanthe had barely dared believe: the binding worked both ways. Each character fixed Ossuar more firmly to the drowned harbor, and each character cost the god something too—the reach of it, the freedom of it, the easy wear of a borrowed crown. He could feel it straining against the half-finished name the way a wolf strains against a snare it does not yet understand. Its offers stopped. Its threats began. The sea around the causeway turned restless, waves crossing at wrong angles, the deep water glowing a sick phosphorescent green.

*Xanthe Oyelaran.* It used his full name now, and hearing it in that vast pressure-voice felt like being handled by something cold. *Do you know what you are spending? You are spending the man your mother loved. The boy who walked out of the burning quarter. In an hour you will kneel here and not know why. I will still be here. The sea forgets nothing.*

"The sea doesn't need to forget," Xanthe panted. Sweat ran cold down his spine. His hands shook, and he steadied them against his knees. "I do. That's what makes it mine."

He drew the twenty-first stroke, and lost the color of his childhood bedroom.

He drew the twenty-second, and lost his reason for leaving home.

Somewhere in there, past the thirtieth, he stopped cataloguing what went. There wasn't enough of him left to be the clerk of his own demolition. Sefa read the text; he drew; the name spread across the living water like a bridge being built to nowhere, forty feet of golden script lying on the black sea, and the drowned city below lit up along its sunken streets as if the old harbor had mistaken the light for dawn.

* * *

At the fifty-second stroke, the sea attacked.

There was no other word for it. The water rose in a wall off the causeway's flank—not a wave, a *hand*, vast and deliberate—and came down where Xanthe knelt. Sefa screamed his name. He threw his gift outward the only way he knew how, mapping the strike as it fell, reading its weight and vector in the veil-currents, and flung himself sideways along a line no ungifted man could have found. The water smashed the stones where he'd been and the spray alone lifted him off his feet and slammed him down ten yards along the causeway, ribs cracking against rock, the ritual text skidding toward the sea.

Sefa dove on it. One of her crew—Daro, the one he trusted—hauled Xanthe up by the collar.

"Can you finish?" Sefa shouted over the roaring water. "How many are left?"

Xanthe looked at the name lying on the sea. Golden, incomplete, beautiful. Sixty-three characters in the full binding. Fifty-two drawn. Eleven left.

Eleven memories. He took inventory of himself the way he'd take inventory of a satchel before a long road, fast and merciless, and found what remained: the feel of Sefa's grip on his forearm. The sound of the boy in the night market whispering a god's name. The plan of Vessamare's palace, mapped in real time while guards closed in. Sefa's face. The list itself. And at the very bottom of the satchel, wrapped in oilcloth, saved for last because saving it for last was the whole point: his own name. The knowledge of who Xanthe Oyelaran was, gathered up from everything else, the single thread left holding the garment together.

"Sixty-three," he said, and got to his feet, and tasted blood. "Eleven left. I can finish."

The god rose in the water off the end of the causeway—not a body, not a face, but a presence the size of a storm front, wearing the shapes of drowning men and hollow-eyed worshippers and, briefly, horribly, King Aldric's smiling mouth. The sky above it churned though there were no clouds.

*Then come and pay,* Ossuar said, and the whole Sundered Sea leaned toward the shore.

Behind them, far across the black water, the city of Vessamare lit with fires—from the palace district first, then spreading, and above the roar of the rising sea Xanthe heard a sound he had never heard in his lifetime: temple bells rung not in warning, but in answer, dozens of them, pealing out over the drowning streets as the god called everything it had ever harvested home to defend its name.

Sefa pulled her knife. Daro raised the lantern. Xanthe wiped the blood from his chin, knelt back down at the waterline, and picked up stroke fifty-three—the memory of his mother's voice singing, chosen last of the small things because he had wanted, just once, to carry something lovely all the way to the edge.

He pressed it into the gold.

Far away, beneath the burning city, King Aldric felt the god's grip falter—and inside the drowned harbor, Ossuar turned its storm-front attention from the cartographer to the crown it wore, and shrieked through a thousand stolen mouths at once.
