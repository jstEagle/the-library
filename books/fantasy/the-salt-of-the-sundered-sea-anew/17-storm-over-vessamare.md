## 17. Storm Over Vessamare

The god felt it first as an itch beneath the crown.

Ossuar had worn this body for one hundred and forty days now—had counted them the way a miser counts coins, the way a prisoner counts cracks in a wall—and it knew every corner of King Aldric's mind as well as it knew the drowned streets of its own name. It knew the king's childhood fear of deep water. It knew the taste of his grief for his dead queen. It knew the small locked room at the center of him where Aldric hid himself each night, whispering his own name like a prayer against the dark.

And now it felt something scratching at the foundations of that room.

Far away, at the drowned harbor where the old sea still pooled black and saltless, a man was drawing on the water. Ossuar could not see him. Could not reach him with offers or threats or dreams. But it could feel the strokes of the pen the way a man feels a surgeon's knife through the wall of his own chest—each line of the true name cutting into what held it here.

*No,* the god thought, and for the first time in centuries, the thought was not patient.

It rose from the throne. Around it, the council chamber's candles guttered and bent toward it as if bowing. The king's mouth opened, and what came out was not a word but a pressure, a sound below sound, and every window in the palace of Vessamare fogged white with salt.

The sea heard.

The Sundered Sea, sundered no longer by anything but habit, turned its gray face toward the city and began to rise.

Not like a tide. Tides have rhythm, have courtesy. This was a hand lifting from a table. The water stood up along the whole broken coast—a wall of green-black glass taller than the lighthouse, taller than the temple spires, holding itself there for one impossible heartbeat while the gulls fled inland screaming—and then it came down and *in*, pouring through the breach in the seawall, through the storm-drains, up through the wells, filling Vessamare from beneath the way blood fills a bruise.

In the shrine district, forty hollow-eyed worshippers stood up from their kneeling all at once, in perfect unison, and began to walk toward the harbor. They did not wade. The water parted around their ankles like a servant making way.

Ossuar sat back down upon the throne, wearing Aldric's face, and smiled with Aldric's mouth, and waited for the drowning to do its work.

* * *

Sefa was on Salt Row when the wells started singing.

She had half a ritual text, a stolen skiff, a crew of four—two, now, since Tam had sold them out—and a bruise on her cheekbone shaped exactly like a witch-hunter's gauntlet. She had escaped her cell in the chaos of the burning temples with a girl named Mira clinging to her back and a guard's keys still warm in her fist, and she had been running ever since, because running was the one prayer she'd never stopped believing in.

Then the well outside the chandlery began to hum.

She stopped. Mira stopped behind her, small hands tight on Sefa's belt. The humming came up out of the ground, low and resonant, the note a wet finger makes on a glass rim, and then the water in the well rose—not flowed, *rose*—climbing the stone throat of it in a smooth column until it stood at the lip and looked at them.

Sefa had been a priestess of the tide-cult since she was nine years old. She had been raised on the litany of the deep: *the sea gives, the sea takes, the sea remembers.* She had believed, even after the outlawing, even after the purges, that somewhere under all that gray water there was still something that loved the shore.

What looked up at her out of the well did not love anything.

"Mira." Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "When I run, you run."

"Where?"

"The harbor. Xanthe's down there." She said it like a fact, though she hadn't seen him since the betrayal, since the hunters took her and he vanished into the smoke with half the ritual text pressed to his chest. She didn't know if he lived. She chose to build her next hour on the assumption that he did. "He's doing something the god doesn't want done. Which means the god is about to do everything it can to stop him. Which means—" the water in the well trembled, gathering itself, "—*run.*"

The column of water broke. It didn't splash; it lunged, a whip of gray-green striking across the cobbles where they'd stood, smashing a cart to kindling. Sefa was already moving, hauling Mira down the alley between the rope-walks, and behind them the flood came up Salt Row like a living thing—waist-deep in seconds, carrying barrels, boats torn from their moorings, a chapel door ripped off its hinges with the painted tide-cult sigil still bright upon it. The god flooding the city with its own element, drowning the worshippers it had already hollowed, spending the faithful like coin because it was out of time and it knew it.

Sefa ran. Her lungs burned. The water dragged at her knees, then her thighs, and she lost sight of the cobbles entirely and swam, one arm around Mira, kicking toward the high ground of Harbor Rise where the drowned quarter's rooftops made a broken road toward the sea.

A man floated past her face-down. She caught his collar on instinct and hauled him over: old Josse, the chandler, eyes wide and white and empty, and yet swimming—swimming smoothly, efficiently, toward the harbor, unhollowed men and hollowed alike being pulled inward like flotsam toward a drain. The god wasn't just flooding the city. It was harvesting it. Everything the water touched, the water could carry to stand between Xanthe and the name.

"Sefa!" Mira screamed, and pointed.

Ahead, atop the customs house, silhouetted against the boiling sky, stood a figure in the king's own raiment—no. Not the king. Something wearing the king's shape at fifty times its scale, vast and translucent, made of spray and shadow and crowned with a circlet of wrecked ships' masts, its arms spread wide over the drowning city like a father over a cradle. Ossuar, risen at last out of the flesh it wore, too large now to be contained, too threatened to hide.

Its head turned. Across half a mile of roaring water, its gaze found her—the smuggler-priestess who had spent her whole life learning rituals it wanted forgotten—and it *recognized* her, and its recognition felt like a hand closing around her heart.

Sefa did the only thing she had left. She laughed at it.

"You're afraid," she shouted into the storm, treading water with a child on her back, "of a *cartographer!*" And she dove under the waves and swam for the harbor as fast as her arms would take her, laughing and weeping in the same breath, because it was true—it was terrified, she could feel it, terror rolling off the god like cold off ice—and a thing that terrified that easily was a thing that could die.

* * *

Inside, Aldric fought.

There were no words for the fighting. It was not wrestling, not war, nothing with shapes or fronts. It was two wills occupying one skull, pressing against each other like hands flattened against opposite sides of a pane of glass, each trying to push the other out of existence. For one hundred and forty days Aldric had been losing that fight by inches—his memories going dark room by room, his name wearing thin from the inside, until some nights he could not remember his queen's face and could only remember that forgetting it had once hurt.

But tonight, tonight the god was *distracted*. Tonight its attention poured outward, into the sea, into the flood, into the far-off itch of the unbinding, and the walls of Aldric's locked room went thin as parchment.

He put his shoulder against them and pushed.

For ninety seconds—he counted them; counting was all he had—he was awake in his own body. Ninety seconds of staggering through the flooded great hall while the god's will gripped his limbs like a puppeteer testing strings it couldn't quite feel. Ninety seconds of watching the water rise past the windows, past the tapestries of his grandfather's reign, black and hungry and full of drifting faces.

*Ninety seconds isn't enough to escape,* he thought. *It has to be enough to help.*

He saw the guards at the doors—his guards, men he'd knighted, men whose names he still knew, Rennick and Odo and young Pell—standing rigid, eyes glazed, the water rising around their chests without touching them, held in stasis like flies in amber. He could not command them. His voice was not his voice.

But the wall was stone, and his fingernails were his own, and pain, he had learned, was the one currency the god could not counterfeit or confiscate.

He went to the eastern wall—the one facing the harbor, the one he had scratched at a hundred nights before, always in stolen minutes, always terrified of being caught mid-stroke—and he tore at the mortar with bleeding fingers, and beneath the scratches of his earlier pleading he carved three new words, fast and deep, in letters a child could read:

**THE HARBOR. NOW.**

Because he had felt what the god felt. He had felt the itch, the knife-strokes, the fear. Whatever was happening down at the drowned harbor, it was working, and it needed to work faster than the flood.

His ninety seconds ended. The pressure closed over his mind like a lid. But as the god poured back in—as Ossuar seized his limbs and turned him, dripping, to face the shattered windows and the distant figure of the man drawing on the water—Aldric felt something he had not felt in one hundred and forty days.

He felt the god hesitate.

It stood there, wearing his legs, wearing his crown, looking out at the harbor where a lone cartographer knelt on a flooded quay with a pen of bone in his hand, and it hesitated, because it could not decide which of them to destroy first: the man unraveling its name, or the king who had just, for ninety seconds, helped him.

*You should have taken my hands first,* Aldric thought, from the darkness where it kept him. *I was always better with my hands than you'll ever be.*

And far out over the black rising water, the first stroke of the final line of the name blazed silver against the sea.

* * *

Sefa reached the harbor as the sky tore open.

Rain fell upward there—or so it seemed, the spray of the breaking wave-wall sucked into the air and hanging frozen, ten thousand tons of suspended ocean glittering in the lightning like a ceiling of glass above the quay. Beneath it, on the last dry arc of stones, she saw him: Xanthe, soaked to the bone, kneeling with the ritual text spread open on his knee, drawing on the living water with a stylus that glowed the color of deep-sea fish.

Around him, the harvest converged. Hundreds of them—hollowed worshippers, drowned sailors, the floating dead and the walking empty—all closing along the quays from every direction, slow and silent and inevitable, the god's last army made of everything it had ever eaten.

"Hold the line!" someone screamed, absurdly, uselessly, and Sefa realized it was herself, standing waist-deep beside Mira, waving her arms at the burning skyline. "Xanthe! The rooftops! Come down the roof-road, it's clear to the customs house!"

He didn't look up. His hand moved. Another stroke of the name sank into the water and the whole harbor shuddered, groaned, rang like a struck bell, and the nearest rank of hollowed ones stopped as one, tilted their heads—

—and began, all together, to scream.

Not with their mouths. With everything. The scream came out of the water, out of the rain, out of Sefa's own bones, and she understood: the name hurt it. Every stroke hurt it everywhere it had ever reached, and it reached everywhere. It was dying in a thousand places at once and it had decided, with the simple arithmetic of the drowning, that it would rather pull the whole city under with it than let one more line be drawn.

The suspended ocean-ceiling began to fall.

Sefa grabbed Mira and ran for the quay, running through water that fought her every step, running toward a man she'd known three weeks and would spend the rest of her life for, screaming his name over the roar of the collapsing sky—and ahead of them, Xanthe dipped his stylus one last time, and did not look up, and began the final stroke of the god's true name.
