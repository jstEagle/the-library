## 18. The Unbinding

The sea had come into the city to collect its god, and Xanthe stood waist-deep in the water that had been Salt Row, drawing.

The quay beneath his feet was drowned. The night market where he had sold luck-knots for three years was a rumor of rooftops and floating lanterns. Somewhere above the storm, bells were ringing in every tower at once, and somewhere below it, in the flooded dark, Sefa was still fighting her way toward him — he could not know that, he could only believe it, and belief was all the ritual asked of him now.

He knelt on the stone with the chart-skin spread across his thighs, and he drew Ossuar's name in living ink: seawater thickened with his own blood, traced by a stylus of drowned-harbor bone. Each stroke went down through the stone. Each stroke went up through him.

The first stroke took the smell of his mother's kitchen. He felt it leave like a tooth drawn whole — no pain, only a new hollowness where something warm had been, and the knowledge, already fading, that there had been warmth there once.

*Good,* he told himself, and made the second stroke.

The name was vast. That was what no one had understood — not the exiled priests with their fragmentary texts, not Sefa with her smuggled rituals, not even Aldric, who had fought the god from inside his own skull for a hundred nights. A true name is not a word. It is a *map*. It has coasts and depths, currents and wrecks, places where the thing named is shallow and places where it goes down past all sounding. To speak such a name you would need a throat the size of the sky. But to *draw* it —

To draw it, you needed only a cartographer willing to pay in the only currency the unmapped realms honored.

Memory.

Ossuar knew it too. The storm above Vessamare bent toward him as he worked, a funnel of rain and screaming wind, and out of its heart came a voice that was not a voice — pressure, tide, the groan of hulls breaking.

***CARTOGRAPHER.***

Xanthe did not look up. He made the third stroke, and lost the sound of his father laughing.

"You can shout," he said. His own voice sounded strange to him now, thinner, as if some of it had gone with the memories. "I'm working."

***YOU KNOW WHAT I OFFER. I HAVE SHOWN YOU. YOUR MOTHER'S FACE. THE ROAD YOU CAME BY. THE BOY YOU WERE BEFORE THIS DROWNING CITY. ALL OF IT RESTORED, WHOLE, THE MOMENT YOU LIFT YOUR STYLUS.***

"I know."

***AND YET YOU DIG.***

"Because I finally understand what you're selling." He dipped the stylus again. His hand was steady. Three years of hiding his gift had taught him this much: a shaking hand makes a lying line, and a lying line on a chart kills sailors. "You're not offering me my past. You're offering to make me someone else. Someone who never sold anything, never hid, never learned what things cost. And that man—" the fourth stroke, and the color of his childhood home went gray and featureless behind his eyes— "that man would put down the stylus. That man would let you keep your king. So no. I'll stay hollow if hollow is what the work needs."

The storm *screamed*. Water leapt off the flooded streets in standing waves. Down the drowned avenue, shapes came wading toward him — the hollowed ones, forty strong, their eyes like unpainted paper, moving with the terrible patience of things that do not tire. They would reach him before the last strokes. He had known they would. Sefa's ritual text had been explicit: *the name must be finished under the god's full attention, or the binding will not hold.* The god had to watch. The god had to come close enough to be caught.

That was the trap he was building, and the bait was himself.

* * *

The fifth stroke took his sister's name.

He hadn't remembered he had a sister until the memory began leaving — a small girl on a dock, waving, calling a name across bright water, and then the wave receding and taking her with it, so that afterward he knew only *someone waved*, and then not even that. He wept without deciding to. The tears fell into the ink and the old priests' text said this was right; grief was a solvent; the name drank it.

He thought about Sefa. He held onto the thought deliberately, the way a drowning sailor holds a spar. *Sefa of Salt Row. Smuggler-priestess. She laughs like she's daring you to join her. She kept one chart back from the god and won't tell me why.* He rehearsed her name like a charm against losing larger things.

The sixth stroke took his first map — the one he'd drawn at seven years old, of the lane behind his house, with the crooked chimney rendered in loving detail. He felt it go and laughed, actually laughed, because of course. Of course the god would take that first. Of course it wanted the root of the craft.

"You're afraid of the seventh stroke," he said aloud. "I can tell. You keep talking."

The storm did not answer. That was answer enough.

* * *

The hollowed ones reached the edge of his circle and stopped.

They stood in the flood around him, chest-deep, forty empty people arranged in a perfect ring, and Xanthe understood with cold clarity that they were not here to stop him. They were here to *watch*. Congregation. Witnesses. Ossuar wanted its faithful present for the death of its enemy, or its own — and looking at their hollow faces, Xanthe saw the dockworker from the night market, the man who'd bought a luck-knot for his bad knee, whose last free act had been to whisper thanks to a stranger. His knee had healed within the hour. His self had emptied within the day.

"I'm sorry," Xanthe told him. The man's blank eyes gave nothing back. "I'm going to give you back to yourselves. All of you. That's what this is. Not just the king."

And because it might be the last true thing he ever said, he added: "Your name started with a B. I remember that. Somebody should."

Then he made the seventh stroke.

It took everything from before Vessamare. It did not take it gently. It came into him like the sea itself, prying up floorboards, and Xanthe gripped the stylus and *kept drawing through it* — the eighth stroke blind, the ninth blind, his hand guided now purely by craft, by the geometry of the name which lived deeper than memory, in the part of him that was lines and tides and the mathematics of coastlines. Let it have the boy. Let it have the road, the family, the reasons, the face in the mirror of ten years ago. The name did not need those things.

The name needed only a hand that did not stop.

* * *

Above him, the funnel of storm tore open.

What came down through it was not a shape so much as a *presence* — a pressure like the whole Sundered Sea remembering itself, an antiquity pouring toward the quay, wearing lightning the way kings wear ermine. In its descent it passed through the hollowed ring and they all turned their faces up like sunflowers, adoring, and Xanthe felt the water around him go taut as a sail.

***LAST OFFER, CARTOGRAPHER.***

The voice was inside his skull now, gentle, which was worse than the thunder.

***YOUR NAME. THE ONE YOUR MOTHER GAVE YOU. LET ME KEEP IT AND I WILL RELEASE THE KING UNBOUND AND UNBROKEN. HE WILL REIGN FORTY YEARS. NO ONE NEED EVER KNOW WHAT ALMOST HAPPENED. YOU CAN BE A STRANGER IN A SAFE CITY.***

Xanthe looked up at the descending god, and found — strangely — that he was smiling.

"That's not mercy," he said. "That's you trying to survive with your dignity intact. You don't want my name. You want *no witnesses*." He set the stylus to the stone for the final stroke. "But here's what you've never understood about names, Ossuar. You wear Aldric's crown, you speak with Aldric's mouth, and you think that means you've *taken* him. But a name isn't a possession. It's a definition. And mine—"

He thought of Sefa. He spent the thought like a coin, gladly.

"—mine is whatever I choose to pay."

He drew the last stroke.

* * *

The name closed.

There was no sound. There was a silence so total that the storm, the bells, the burning city, the sea itself seemed to lean in to hear it — and in that silence, Ossuar's true name lay complete upon the flooded quay, every current and depth of the god rendered in blood and salt and spent memory, and the name *knew* its bearer, and the bearer was bound by knowing.

The god screamed with a thousand drowned voices.

It tore. That was the only word. It tore out of King Aldric the way a hook tears out of a deep-caught fish — and Xanthe, watching through the rain, saw far above him, on the palace terrace, a man's body arch and convulse and *shrink*, the vast presence collapsing out of it, draining away down through the storm toward the name that had caught it. For one heartbeat Xanthe saw Aldric's face clearly — hollowed, aged ten years, but *his own*, his eyes wide and wet and human — and then the king collapsed behind the terrace balustrade, alive, free, and the god hit the name like a wave hitting a seawall.

The flood around the quay shuddered.

The hollowed ones gasped — all forty at once, a single indrawn breath — and staggered, and one by one they sat down hard in the water, blinking, filling, *remembering*. The dockworker looked at his own hands as though meeting them. Then he looked at Xanthe, and opened his mouth, and Xanthe would never learn what he meant to say, because that was when the god, dying, made its last trade.

***THEN TAKE MY GOING-GIFT, CARTOGRAPHER,*** the voice said, no longer gentle, no longer anything, just vast and ending. ***EVERYTHING REMAINING. FAIR IS FAIR. YOU DREW THE NAME WHOLE; I LEAVE NOTHING OF YOU WHOLE.***

And it took.

Xanthe felt it move through him like fire through a library. His three years in Vessamare — the market, the luck-knots, the woman begging for her son, the boy whispering a god's name — gone. Sefa's laugh — gone. Her name — *gone*, and worse than gone, the shape of the hole where it had been smoothed over, so that he would not even grieve it properly. The rituals, the plan, the king, the reason he knelt bleeding on a drowned quay in a ruined city — stripped away stroke by stroke as the god drained itself out through the last of him, spending Xanthe's self to buy its own exit from the world.

He did not fight it. That was the choice, and he made it with his eyes open, the way he had made every stroke. Because the alternative was written plainly in the god's departing touch: *fight me, and I tear the name apart as I go, and Aldric dies with it, and the forty wake to find their savior failed.* The name needed a completed cartographer. It did not need him *afterward*.

So Xanthe Oyelaran knelt in the flooding ruins of the life he had built, and let the god spend him like ink, and the last thing he chose — actively, deliberately, the final entry in the atlas of himself — was to hold on long enough to watch it finish.

*Let it take the name too,* he thought, as the darkness rose like a tide. *I paid for everything else. Why not—*

The god tore free of the world.

The storm broke. Rain fell straight down, ordinary rain, exhausted rain. Far out over the Sundered Sea, something immense slid beneath the water and did not rise, and the sea closed over it with a sound almost like relief.

On the quay, in the shallows, among forty waking strangers and one sleeping king a terrace above, a man sat down in the floodwater and looked at his own hands — clever hands, ink-stained, holding a stylus of bone — and did not know whose they were.

Around his neck, on a cord he did not recognize, hung a single folded chart, unsigned.

He stared at it for a long time.

"Hello," he said to the hands, to the chart, to the rain. His voice was a stranger's. "Does anyone know... what am I supposed to do next?"

Far up the drowned street, a figure was wading toward him through the wreckage, shouting a name — his name, though it meant nothing to him — and stumbling, and refusing to stop.
