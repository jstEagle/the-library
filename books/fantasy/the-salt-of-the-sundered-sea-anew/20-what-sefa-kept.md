## 20. What Sefa Kept

The house on Candlewick Lane had three rooms, a leaking roof, and a view of nothing but the neighbor's laundry line. Sefa had chosen it precisely for that: no one looked twice at a woman who lived where there was nothing worth seeing. Now she was glad of it for another reason entirely. The man sitting at her table by the window needed a place without windows in his eyes, and this room, dim and salt-stained and smelling faintly of the tallow candles she rendered herself, was as close to nowhere as Vessamare allowed.

He sat very still, the way he had sat for six days now. His hands rested flat on the table as if he'd been told they belonged to someone else and he wasn't sure he believed it.

"Tea," she said, and set the cup before him.

"Thank you." A pause. Then, carefully, like a man testing ice: "I don't know your name."

"Sefa."

Something moved behind his face—not recognition, not yet, but the shadow a recognition casts when it hasn't arrived. "Sefa," he repeated, and his voice cracked on the second syllable, and he looked down at his cup as though it had scalded him.

She had seen hollowed worshippers by the dozen. She had held her own brother's head in her lap while the tide-cult's rites failed to call anything back into him, back when the gods were only silent instead of hungry. She knew what emptiness looked like. This was not that. The god had taken everything—his name for himself, most of all—but it had taken cleanly, surgically, the way a good surgeon takes a limb. What remained was whole. It simply didn't know what it was for.

His hands knew, though. That was the thing she kept coming back to, the thing that made her throat tighten when she watched him across the table. On the third morning she had found him at her washbasin, tracing patterns in the spilled water with one fingertip—coastlines, she realized, standing frozen in the doorway. Coastlines that didn't exist. Channels and shoals and the long drowned shelf of the pre-Sundering harbor, drawn from a memory that was supposed to be gone, living somewhere below memory, in the marrow, in the tendons.

"You were a cartographer," she had said.

He had looked up at her with those unanchored eyes and said, "Was I?" and gone back to drawing the sea in her dishwater.

* * *

She taught him himself the way you teach anyone anything: in small pieces, at odd hours, disguised as something else. That had always been her craft. Smuggling wasn't about moving goods; it was about moving goods inside other goods, salt inside fish barrels, ritual texts inside hymnals, truth inside jokes. You never carried the contraband openly. You let it ride along in the ordinary cargo until the inspectors' eyes slid past.

So she didn't say *your name is Xanthe Oyelaran*. Names were contraband. Instead—

"Pass me the knife," she said, sharpening it against the whetstone by the stove. "No—the other way. Handle out. You're left-handed."

"I am?" He turned the knife over. "I don't know."

"Your hands do." And they did. He took the whetstone from her with his left hand, angled it wrong, corrected without thinking, and the sound it made—*shhhk, shhhk*, even strokes, a worker's rhythm—was the sound of a boy who had grown up doing chores with tools too big for him. She'd guessed that much from watching him draw. Left-handed draftsmen pull their lines toward themselves. Everything about his hatching ran backward to everyone else's.

"Where did I learn that?" he asked.

"A city that doesn't exist anymore," she said, and gave him tea.

Another day: "Sing," she said, absurdly, while they mended nets on her floor because mending nets gave his hands work and idle hands in Vessamare drew attention. He looked at her like she'd lost her mind. "You heard me. Anything. A shanty, a hymn, a nursery song. Your throat is full of things your head isn't."

"I don't—" But he started anyway, some low working song about hauling lines, words half-gone, melody intact, and halfway through the second verse he stopped, frowning, because he had slipped without noticing into another song entirely—one with a different cadence, older, in a language that wasn't Low Vessamarean. His mouth kept going for two more lines before his mind caught up and strangled the sound.

"What was that?"

Sefa set down her netting needle slowly. Her heart was doing something complicated. "That was Old Tide-Tongue. Liturgical. Nobody speaks it anymore except cultists and corpses." She had learned it kneeling in smuggled shrines; she had assumed she was one of perhaps two hundred people alive who knew it. "Where did you hear it?"

"I don't know." His voice was thin. "It came up like water."

"It did that," she said quietly. "Things do that. Water finds its level. So does a person, given time." She reached over and took his hand—deliberately, an act she'd rehearsed in her head for two days and still botched, too abrupt, too warm—and pressed his palm flat against the floorboards. "Feel that? Oak. Salt-swollen. This room has stood sixty years. You have stood longer than that, wherever you stood. The floor remembers being a tree. You'll remember being you. Not all of it. Enough."

He didn't pull his hand away. After a moment his fingers curled slightly, around nothing, around the shape of a pen she hadn't given him yet.

* * *

On the ninth day he drew her.

Not her face—he hadn't attempted faces, and she wondered privately whether that was mercy or absence—but her hands, wrapped around a mug, resting on the table, rendered in quick confident strokes on the back of a scrap of packing manifest. The perspective was slightly wrong. The line weight was extraordinary. She had seen guild masters' work in the houses of wealthy clients during her smuggling years, charts that sold for more than dockworkers earned in a decade, and none of them had lines like this: lines that seemed to know where they were going before the pen arrived.

"That's me," she said.

"Is it?" He looked troubled. "I just—it wanted to be drawn. The light was on your knuckles."

"The light was on my knuckles," she agreed, and folded the scrap with exaggerated care, and put it in the tin box under the loose floorboard where she kept the things she could not afford to lose and could not afford to explain. He pretended not to see where she put it. He was getting better at pretending, which worried her. Pretending was a survival skill, and she knew exactly what kind of life taught it, and she hated that his body remembered that lesson even if he didn't.

At night he slept in her second room, which had been her storeroom, which meant sleeping among crates of false-bottomed hymnals and jars of consecrated brine, and she lay awake in the next room listening to him breathe and conducting arguments with herself that never ended well.

One voice said: *he is a duty. The king owes him. The city owes him. Someone has to pay.*

Another voice said: *you are lying to yourself, Sefa Eriksen's ghost, you old fool, you sat outside the healers' ward for four days while they scraped the god's residue off him and you held his hand through the worst of the shaking and you have not held anyone's hand since Tam died.*

A third voice, the oldest, the salt-encrusted one, said nothing at all, just turned its face toward the sea the way it always did, waiting.

* * *

On the twelfth day she opened the floorboard and took out the chart.

She had stolen it back from the god itself, or near enough—pried from the flooded reliquary beneath the drowned quay while the waters receded and the dead god's residue still smoked off the stones, while Aldric's men shouted her name across the wreckage and Xanthe stood blank-faced in the rain asking strangers who he was. She had run with it inside her coat, against her heart, through streets running ankle-deep with seawater that tasted of grief, and she had not shown it to anyone since. Not to Aldric, though he'd asked. Not to the remnant of the tide-cult, though they'd begged. It wasn't theirs. She had known whose it was from the moment she touched it, the way you know a child's cry in a crowd.

Now she laid it on the table between the teacups and turned it to face him.

He went very still.

It was a chart of the Sundered Sea—not the sundered sea as it was, but as it had been before the Sundering, before the gods' war tore the harbors down and drowned the old world and taught Vessamare to fear its own reflection. Every coastline on it was a coastline that no longer existed. Cities sat on shores that were now forty fathoms down. And across the open water, in a fine hand that Sefa had come to know better than her own handwriting lately, ran annotations in three scripts and a fourth that was no script at all—current-lines, depth-soundings, the breathing-patterns of tides, and here and there small marginal notes in a cramped everyday hand: *ask the ferryman about the north channel*, *Mother knew this coast*, *wrong, redo*, and once, in letters pressed so hard the pen had nearly torn the vellum, a single word: *home*.

"This is mine," he said. Not a question. His fingers hovered above it without touching, the way people hover over relics, over sleeping animals, over wounds.

"Yes."

"I made this."

"You made this."

"When?"

"Before," Sefa said. "Before you traded yourself away piece by piece to keep the witch-hunters from finding you. Before the god ate what was left. Before any of us." She pulled out the chair opposite him and sat, and made herself keep her voice level, because the next part mattered and she could not afford to shake. "I stole it back. From the reliquary, after the unbinding. It was lying in the floodwater with the rest of what he couldn't keep."

Xanthe looked at the chart for a long time. The candle gutted. Somewhere down the lane a cart rattled past, and neither of them moved until the sound faded.

"There's a word on here," he said finally. "*Home.* I wrote *home* about a place that's underwater."

"People do," Sefa said. "Homes drown. Doesn't stop them being homes."

"But I don't remember it." His voice was careful, controlled, and underneath the control something was tearing. "I look at this and I can see every mistake in the projections and I can tell you the sounding at the harbor mouth is off by two fathoms and I have no idea why I care. I know *how* to read this. I don't know *me* reading it. There's a stranger's skill in my hands and a stranger's grief in—" he stopped. Pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "Why did you keep it? Why not give it to the king, or the guild, or burn it? It's evidence. Magic. Forbidden. You could hang for possessing it."

"Because it's yours," Sefa said.

"That's not a reason."

"It's the only reason there is."

He lowered his hand and looked at her then, really looked, and she saw the tearing thing win briefly, surface, show itself: not confusion, not gratitude—something rawer and more frightened than either. "You've been teaching me myself," he said. "Left-handedness. Songs. Which way to hold the knife. Little pieces, day by day, like feeding someone who can't swallow much at once. Why? You don't owe me that. Whoever I was—I don't even know what I am to you."

"No," Sefa agreed. "You don't."

"Tell me."

"And if I tell you, you'll believe me, and you'll build yourself out of what I say, and you will never again get to build yourself out of what you find." She leaned forward. "I've watched men come back from the hollowing. The ones who recover fastest are the ones who let other people author them—*you loved this, you hated that, you were brave here*—and they come back fast and they come back *small*, fitted to the shape other people remember. I won't do that to you. I'll give you the materials. The building's yours."

"That's cruel," he whispered.

"It's love," Sefa said. "The two look alike from inside."

The candle guttered again and steadied. Xanthe looked down at the chart—at the drowned cities, at the marginal notes, at the word *home* pressed nearly through the vellum—and slowly, deliberately, he placed his left hand flat upon it, fingers spread, the way a sailor touches a mast in a storm. Not reading. Claiming. Or asking permission to claim.

"Then give me a pen," he said.

* * *

He worked through the night. She dozed in the chair and woke to find him adding to the margins—new soundings, corrections, and once, in fresh ink, a small precise sketch of her hands around a mug, tucked into a corner of the drowned sea like a compass rose. When the gray light of dawn came through the window he sat back and looked at what he'd done and laughed—a short, startled sound, the first laugh she'd heard from him, rusty as a seized hinge.

"There's a harbor here," he said, tapping a bay on the far side of the chart, in the deep water beyond the Sundering's scar. "Not an old one. A new one. It doesn't exist. It *could* exist—the depths are right, the currents would carry—" He stopped, staring at his own lines as if they'd spoken. "Sefa. I'm not drawing what was lost. I'm drawing what comes next."

Her chest did the painful thing again, the thing it had been doing all twelve days. "Yes," she said. "That's the trick of it. That was always your trick."

He looked up at her. "How do you know these things about me, when I don't?"

"Because I kept watch," she said, "while you were busy surviving. Someone had to remember you. It might as well be someone who—" She stopped. Twelve days of discipline, and there it was, rising like a tide she couldn't turn.

"Someone who what?"

Outside, boots on cobbles. More than one pair, moving fast, and a knock—not a polite knock, a fist, the kind that preceded warrants or news, and in Sefa's experience the two traveled together.

Xanthe was already on his feet, the chart swept behind his back, his body remembering danger before his mind could name it. She crossed to the door and put her eye to the seam and saw, standing in the gray morning light of Candlewick Lane, a palace page in royal livery, breathless, holding a letter sealed with the king's own signet.

"Sefa of Salt Row?" the page called. "By order of King Aldric. He says— he says to tell you the office is ready. The one for the unmapped realms. And that there's a position open." The page swallowed. "He says to say: *for the cartographer, if he'll take it.*"

Behind her, she heard Xanthe go quiet. When she turned, he was looking at the chart hidden behind his back, at his own new-drawn harbor in the middle of the drowned sea, and then at her, and his expression was one she recognized from the mirror, from years ago, from the day the tide-cult first asked her to risk everything:

The face of a man deciding who he is about to become.

"Well?" she said.

He held out his hand for the letter.
