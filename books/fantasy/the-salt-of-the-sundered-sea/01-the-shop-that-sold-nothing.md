## 1. The Shop That Sold Nothing

The trick to selling nothing, Wendell Eastwick had learned in her first year behind the counter, was to arrange it beautifully.

She rose before the gulls did and set out her stock with the care of a woman displaying treasure: sea-glass polished smooth as a coin, driftwood carved into knots that meant nothing, shells strung on waxed cord, little pewter fish that caught the morning light off the harbor. Every piece of it honest. Every piece of it dead. That was the whole secret—dead things carried no hum, no whisper, no weight of the old world clinging to them like brine.

Greyharbor's charm market opened at dawn along Saltcandle Row, forty stalls beneath awnings bleached the color of bone, and by the time the first customers shuffled down from the terraces smelling of coal-smoke and porridge, Wendell's table looked exactly as harmless as it was.

"Morning, Missus Thackeray." Old Piet from the chandlery next door, already three cups into his rum. "What's today's miracle?"

"Sea-glass," she said. "Green, if you're lucky."

"Liar. It's all green."

"Then you're very lucky today."

He laughed, bought two pieces for his granddaughters, and moved on. Wendell smiled until he turned away, then let the smile drop like something heavy set down on a shelf.

Twelve years. Twelve years of *Missus Thackeray* and harmless trinkets and laughing at Piet's jokes, and not once—not on the coldest night, not when the loneliness got into her bones like damp—had she let herself be Wendell Eastwick out loud.

Because Greyharbor had drowned its mages. Because the Ministry of Unmaking kept ledgers of bloodlines the way other cities kept ledgers of debts. And because beneath the cellar of her shop on Tidewatch Lane, sealed under flagstones her mother had laid with her own hands, there was a gate—and gates, even sleeping ones, even starving ones, *hummed*.

She could feel it now, faintly, the way you feel a tooth you've stopped trusting. A low note under the floorboards. A note only she could hear, because she was the last person alive who was supposed to hear it.

Wendell adjusted a string of shells so it hung exactly one finger's width lower than its neighbors, and went to make tea she wouldn't drink while it was warm.

* * *

The trouble started, as it always did, with white gloves.

Ministry inspectors wore grey coats and white gloves, and they came to Saltcandle Row twice a year on a schedule everyone knew and nobody said aloud—the way you don't say aloud that the tide comes in. They checked licenses. They checked scales. They ran their gloved fingers over every trinket on every stall, feeling for the warmth that magic left behind even when it was trying to be quiet.

Wendell had a system. Everything on her table passed through the cold-box first—a lead-lined chest her mother had warded with geometry instead of spellwork, which was legal, because geometry was just shapes, and shapes couldn't hang you. Three days in the cold-box and any lingering resonance bled away into the lead. Her stock came out of it duller than it went in, but dull was safe, and safe was the only currency Greyharbor paid reliably.

At midmorning she saw them coming up the Row: two inspectors, gloves immaculate, boots too clean for the fish-guts cobblestones. The taller one she recognized—Inspector Brann, who'd been doing this since before the Drowning and enjoyed his work the way some men enjoyed fishing. The younger one was new. New was bad. New inspectors hadn't yet learned what was worth noticing and what wasn't.

"Thackeray." Brann didn't look at her; he looked at her table, moving his gaze across it slowly, the way a man reads a confession. "Quiet winter?"

"Dead quiet," Wendell said. "Like my sales."

That earned her half a smile. Brann liked her, or liked whatever he thought she was—a tired widow with a failing stall, no husband, no children, no history worth ink. She had spent twelve years building that woman plank by plank. *No family.* The truest lie she owned.

The new inspector drifted along the table's edge. He wasn't touching anything yet. His eyes were doing arithmetic.

"This is a lot of inventory," he said, "to have sold nothing."

"It's a lot of inventory *because* I sell nothing."

He picked up a pewter fish. Turned it over. Set it down with a small frown, as though it had disappointed him, and Wendell felt the cold-box's work hold steady—nothing in it but dead metal and her own heartbeat, which had picked up an unprofessional rhythm.

"You're Eastwick-licensed?" Brann asked, producing his ledger.

"Widow Thackeray. Licensed eleven years running. You've stamped it yourself, Inspector."

"I know what I've stamped." He flipped pages. "I'm asking where you were before eleven years ago."

"Saltmouth. My husband's people. They died in the fever year." She recited it flat, bored, the story worn smooth as her sea-glass. "There's nothing before Saltmouth worth stamping."

Brann grunted and made a mark. But the new one was still circling, still reading her table like scripture, and Wendell realized with a slow, sinking clarity what was bothering him: the arrangement. Nobody arranged dead shells this carefully. Careful arrangement was what you did with living stock—with charms that needed their hum tucked in neat, hidden in plain sight among the junk.

He was looking at her display the way you'd look at a locked door in a house that claimed to be empty.

"May I see your cellar?" the new inspector said. "Storage. Unlicensed goods turn up in cellars."

"Cellar floods," Wendell said. "Twice a month, high spring tides. Nothing keeps down there but mold and my regrets."

"I'd still like to look."

Behind him, Brann sighed the sigh of a man whose morning was being lengthened by ambition. "Kettering. We've done this row four times. Thackeray here sells glass to grandmothers. Move along."

Kettering didn't move along. He was looking at the floorboards behind her counter—at the trapdoor ring, iron, salt-rusted, exactly where a cellar hatch should be—and Wendell watched him watch it, and underneath her feet, beneath the flagstones and the mud and the drowned dark, the gate *hummed*. Not louder. Never louder; it had been silent for twelve years. But she felt it the way you feel someone saying your name in another room.

Her mother's voice, in memory: *If they ever come below, Wendell, you let them find water. Water explains everything.*

"Suit yourself," she heard herself say. "Mind the third step. It's rotten."

* * *

The cellar smelled of brine and old wood, lit by one lamp Kettering insisted on carrying himself. Water stood ankle-deep on the far side, black and still, seeping through the harbor wall the way it always did. The gate lay beneath the flagstones at the center—under six feet of stone and clay and her mother's careful geometry—and Wendell stood on top of it with her heart going like a gull in a sack, smiling her shopkeeper's smile, and waited to be unmade.

Kettering knelt. Pulled off one glove—he shouldn't have; regulations said glove, always glove—and pressed his bare palm flat against the wet flagstones.

Nothing. Of course nothing. Twelve years of silence, purchased with her mother's life-work and Wendell's entire self, folded up small and buried deep.

But as he knelt there, Wendell saw it: a tremor run through the standing water. Just a shiver, fine as a hairline crack, radiating out from the stones under his hand. Kettering frowned. Lifted his palm. Looked at his fingers as if they'd told him something.

"Water table's strange down here," he said.

"Told you. Floods twice a month."

He stood slowly, and for one long moment he looked at her—really looked, past the apron and the tired eyes and the widow's plainness, hunting for whatever it was he almost had—and Wendell held his gaze with the flat patience of a woman who has nothing to hide and would very much like to go back up and sell him a shell.

"Huh," said Kettering.

Upstairs, Brann shouted down that the Row wasn't going to inspect itself, and Kettering climbed the ladder without another word. At the top he paused, one hand on the rotten step, and said, without turning around:

"The tide's supposed to come in tomorrow, isn't it? High one."

"Spring tide," Wendell agreed. "Highest of the season."

He nodded slowly, like a man filing something away in a drawer he'd open later, and followed Brann out into the noise of the market.

Wendell stood alone in her cellar with the lamp guttering, and beneath her feet, soft as breath, the gate hummed—and this time, she could swear it was answering the tide. This time, it sounded almost like it was waking up.

She climbed the ladder, closed the trapdoor, and told herself it was nothing. She was very good at telling herself things.

By evening, the wind had swung round to the east, and the harbormaster's boys were nailing storm-warnings to the posts along Saltcandle Row: highest spring tide in nine years, due at dawn.

Wendell stood in her doorway watching the water rise in the harbor basin, black and patient, coming for her door.
