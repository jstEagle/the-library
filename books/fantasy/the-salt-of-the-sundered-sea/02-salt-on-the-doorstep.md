## 2. Salt on the Doorstep

The tide went out at half past nine, and Wendell Eastwick watched it go from behind her counter like a woman watching a debt collector leave.

Low tide was the dangerous hour. At high water, the gate beneath her cellar hummed so faintly she could almost believe she'd imagined twelve years of listening for it—a lullaby through the floorboards, a shiver in the wine bottles. But when the sea pulled back and the drowned foundations of old Greyharbor stood dripping in the moonlight, the hum changed. It thinned. It *reached*. And on nights like this one, with no cloud to muffle the moon, Wendell could feel it pressing up through the cellar stones like a voice calling through a locked door.

She did what she always did. She lit a candle, carried it down the stairs she'd sworn never to descend again, and pressed her palm flat against the seventh stone.

"Wendell," she said quietly. "Wendell Thackeray. Shopkeeper. Nothing else."

The names worked like salt in a wound—the lie that kept the wound closed. The humming settled, sulky and slow, and she climbed back up into the shop of harmless things: luck-knots braided from harbor grass, glass beads that caught the light pretty enough to pass as charms, dried kelp bundled for tea. Everything in Thackeray's Sundries was exactly what it appeared to be, because everything in Wendell's life had to be.

She was counting the till when the knocking started.

Not knocking, precisely. A dragging. Something heavy hauling itself across her doorstep in the dead hour between tides, and then a fist against the door—once, twice, weak as a fish flopping in a net.

Greyharbor taught you not to open your door after midnight. The Ministry's curfew bells had rung hours ago; anyone abroad now was either drunk, desperate, or dead already. Wendell stood very still behind her counter and told herself all three of those things.

Then she heard him say, through the wood, in a voice like water draining through gravel:

"Eastwick."

Nobody had called her that in twelve years. Her mother had been dead eleven. She crossed the shop before she decided to, which was how she knew the decision had been made for her long ago, and lifted the bar.

He fell across the threshold like something the tide had given up on. He was grey-skinned and soaked through despite the dry night, and where his coat was torn open at the shoulder, he wasn't bleeding blood at all. What welled out of him was brine—clear, glittering, smelling of deep water and lightning. It pooled on her floorboards and the boards *drank* it, darkening like they were thirsty, and Wendell felt the cellar-gate hum rise through her feet in answer, a low keen she hadn't heard since her mother died.

"No," she whispered. "No, no—not here—"

"Thackeray." His mouth bent around the false name with contempt, or pity; both, maybe. "Clever. Twelve years clever." He fumbled inside his coat. "But debts don't forget. The sea doesn't forget."

"The Eastwicks are dead," she said. "Everyone who used that name is dead or gone."

"Not everyone." His hand came out of the coat, and in it was a roll of skin—pale, translucent, stretched over something rigid, like a drumhead cured over bone. Fish-skin, but etched. Even by candlelight she could see lines scored into it, fine as spiderwork, a map of waters she didn't know and channels she feared she did. "Take it."

"I don't want it."

"You'll want it." Bloodless lips peeled back. "The Sundered Sea is tearing. The covenant is failing, girl, and there's only one blood left that can—" A cough took him, and what came up with it was seawater, actual seawater, pouring out of a man on a dry shop floor a mile from any shore. "They're coming for you now. They've always been coming. The map knows the way. Your mother's way."

"My mother's way led to the bottom of the harbor," Wendell said, and hated how her voice shook.

"Yes." He smiled, and there was something terrible and tender in it. "And out the other side. Take it, keeper. The Eastwick debt comes due."

She took it. That was the unforgivable part, later—that some traitor instinct in her hands reached out and closed around the fish-skin before her mind could stop them, and the moment her fingers touched it the etched lines *warmed*, faintly gold, like embers under ash, and the hum beneath the floor rose to a note that was nearly a word.

The stranger sagged back against her counter, spent. "Bar the door," he managed. "When I'm done. Bar it and burn nothing and touch nothing. They read ash. They read salt." His eyes found hers, and they were the wrong color for a human face, the grey-green of water over sand. "You were hidden well, little keeper. Better than your mother hoped. Don't undo it now by being kind."

"Who are you?" she asked. "What are you?"

"What's left of the ferryman," he said. "Your mother's ferryman. What's left."

He died an hour before dawn, sitting propped against her counter with his hand still resting near hers, as if they were two friends sharing a quiet drink. There was no struggle in it. One breath, then none, and the strange pressure in the air went out of the room like a snuffed lamp—and the hum under the floor sank back to its old sullen murmur, cheated.

Wendell sat on the floor beside him until the windows went grey. She thought about running. She inventoried what running would mean: leaving the shop, leaving the cellar, leaving the gate unwatched with the Ministry already circling the city like gulls over offal. She thought about her mother, who had chosen hiding, and hiding had bought eleven quiet years and one drowned woman, and the quiet had ended anyway, tonight, at her own doorstep. The past didn't stay buried. It waited for low tide.

So instead of running, she cleaned. She washed the brine from the boards with vinegar until the wood stopped drinking, wrapped the body in sailcloth from the storeroom, and told herself that a shopkeeper had found a dying sailor on her step and done her Christian duty, and if she repeated it often enough the story might hold. Then she barred the door as instructed, put the fish-skin map in the flour bin—because no inspector on earth searched a flour bin twice—and sat down to wait for morning, which she knew, with the certainty of a woman who has lived by vigilance, would come with boots.

It came at half past seven.

Three pairs of boots, in fact, ringing on the cobbles outside, followed by the flat official knock that Greyharbor shopkeepers learned to fear above all other sounds. Wendell smoothed her apron, arranged her face into mild sleepy irritation, and opened up.

"Ministry of Unmaking," said the man in front. He was tall and dry as driftwood, with a face like a folded map and eyes the color of a winter sky over slate—Inspector Corvane; she knew him by sight the way every charm-seller in the city knew him, the way sheep know the particular dog. Behind him stood two wardens in grey coats, and between them, looking small and terrified, her neighbor's boy, dragged along as witness.

"Madam Thackeray." Corvane's voice was pleasant the way ice is smooth. "A body was seen brought toward this street in the night. Carried by the water, some say. Unnatural, some say further." He peered past her into the shop with nostrils flaring faintly, like a man catching a scent no one else could. "You'll have noticed nothing, of course."

"I sleep like the dead," Wendell said. "Ask my neighbors. Ask anybody."

"Oh, I intend to ask everybody." He stepped forward without being invited, and she had to let him—it was that or become interesting. He moved through her shop slowly, trailing one dry finger along her counter, her shelves, the bundles of kelp. The wardens fanned out with practiced boredom. In the flour bin, six feet from where Corvane stood, lay a map etched on skin that warmed at her blood's touch, and Wendell discovered that a heart can hammer hard enough to hurt.

"You sell trinkets," Corvane observed, lifting a glass bead to the light. "Beads. Knots. Kelp. Twelve years, madam, you have sold nothing but nothing, and yet—" He set the bead down with a small precise click. "—you are still here. Shops fail in this quarter every season. Yours endures. Curious, that. A woman selling nothing, keeping a shop nobody needs, in a house the tide seems fond of." He turned those winter eyes on her at last. "Tell me about last night."

"A sailor knocked. Drunk, I thought. I gave him water and sent him on."

"Sent him on." Corvane smiled without any warmth reaching his face. "Where did you send him, madam?"

"Home. To the harborside. I didn't ask directions of a drunk."

"No," he agreed softly. "People rarely do. And yet the harbormaster reports no missing sailors. And the watch reports no drunkard seen leaving this street. And this morning, at low water, the tide left something on the flats below your cellar wall." He paused, letting it land. "Salt, madam. A perfect circle of it, burned white into the mud. Circles of salt are a registered phenomenon under the Statutes of Severance. Do you know what we call such circles, when we find them?"

Wendell kept her face still. Inside, cold water was rising. The stranger's death had marked the ground; the sea itself had signed its name.

"No idea," she said.

"Neither do I, officially," Corvane said. "Unofficially, we call them *doorways*." He straightened his coat, brisk again, a man satisfied with a day's work begun. "I'll want the name of your supplier. Your grandfather's name. Your mother's maiden name, while you're at it—routine, purely routine." His eyes flicked, just once, toward the cellar door. Just once. "We'll speak again, Madam Thackeray. Soon. The tide has been very talkative lately, and I find I'm a good listener."

They left. She barred the door behind them and stood with her forehead against the wood, breathing, until her legs remembered how to hold her. Through the floor, faint as a heartbeat, the gate hummed—and for the first time in twelve years, Wendell understood that it wasn't humming to itself.

It was humming to her.

By noon she had made her decision, though her hands shook doing it: the map, the coin-charm her mother had left sewn into her old coat lining, the small bag of a life packed in panic. Because Corvane would come back, and next time he'd bring instruments instead of questions, and the flour bin wouldn't save her.

There was one person in Greyharbor who owed her mother a favor and owned a ship that sailed without asking permission of anyone. Which meant, Wendell thought grimly, pulling her cloak on in the empty shop, that she was about to do the single most foolish thing of a carefully invisible life.

She was going to buy passage from smugglers.
