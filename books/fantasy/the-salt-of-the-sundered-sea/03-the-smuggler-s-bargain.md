## 3. The Smuggler's Bargain

The fish-skin map did not leave Wendell's hands for three days.

She slept with it under her cheek, woke with its edges printed faintly against her skin like a bruise made of letters. She tried fire. She tried ink, lamp-oil, vinegar, the acid she kept for cleaning tarnish off brass. The lines stayed stubborn as scars—harbor channels she half-recognized, then water she didn't, and past all of it, at the far edge where the parchment should have ended, a circle drawn in what looked like a child's careful hand: *the gate.*

And below the circle, in script that shifted when she wasn't looking directly at it, two words that never changed no matter how hard they blurred: *Eastwick debt.*

On the third morning she wrapped the map in oilcloth, buried it in her flour barrel, put on her least remarkable shawl, and went to find smugglers.

Everyone in Greyharbor knew the Kestrel's Tooth was real. Everyone also knew it was a story sailors told to frighten each other—a ship that came up the channel on moonless tides, took on cargo nobody asked about, and vanished before the harbormaster's clerks finished their breakfast. Wendell had sold charms to three men over twelve years who smelled of bilge and gunpowder and secrets, men who paid in foreign coin and never haggled, and every one of them had the same salt-scoured voice. She'd spent years pretending not to know what they were.

Now she stood at the end of Ropewalk Stair at low tide, where the harbor's bones showed themselves—black pilings furred with weed, mud flats stinking of sulfur and old fish—and waited for the story to find her.

It took until dark.

* * *

The boat came in without lights, without sound, sliding through the shallows like something the sea had coughed up rather than something men had built. Four figures rowed. In the stern sat a woman whose outline seemed cut from the darkness itself—tall, still, with hair bound back so tightly it might have been a helmet.

Wendell stepped out of the shadow of the piling before her nerve could fail.

"I need passage," she said. "Out of the harbor. Past the shelf."

The oars stopped. The silence that followed had weight, texture, the particular quality of four people deciding whether a stranger was worth killing.

"You're lost, shopkeeper." The woman in the stern had a voice like gravel poured slow. "This stair is for rats and eels."

"Then you'll want to hear what a rat has to sell." Wendell held up the oilcloth bundle. Inside it, wrapped in a second layer of silk, lay the last charm of her mother's she still owned—a compass needle that pointed not north but toward whatever the holder needed most. It hummed against her palm even now, faint as a sleeping wasp. Twelve years she'd kept it hidden, telling herself she was saving it for an emergency. Apparently this was the emergency.

The woman climbed out of the boat without hurry, boots finding purchase on slime-slick stone, and crossed the distance between them like a blade being drawn. Up close she was younger than her voice—younger than Wendell, maybe thirty—with a scar running through one eyebrow and eyes the flat grey of deep water. She did not ask to see the charm. She looked at Wendell instead, long enough to be an insult.

"You're the one off Candlemaker's Row," she said. "Sells love tokens and luck-knots to girls who ought to know better."

"That's me."

"The Ministry walked through your shop last week. Turned your cellar out." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "Found nothing."

"I'm very good at nothing."

Something moved at the corner of the woman's mouth—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would live if she'd ever grown one. "Isolde Vane," she said, though Wendell hadn't asked. "Captain of the Kestrel's Tooth. And this is my crew, who are currently wondering why I haven't thrown you in the mud yet."

Behind her, the rowers had come ashore. They were a strange congregation: a huge man with a beard braided into three tails and rings of yellow metal worked through them; a wiry youth picking his teeth with a fishhook, watching Wendell the way a gull watches a dropped clam; and a small, grey-faced woman whose hands never stopped moving—knotting, unknotting a length of cord, over and over, like a heart that couldn't sit still.

"Bray," Isolde said, indicating the bearded giant. "Finch. Sable. They bite, mostly figuratively."

"And the price?" Wendell asked. "For passage?"

Isolde held out her hand. Not for the charm—for the map.

Every instinct Wendell owned screamed at once. The map was the only proof the courier's death had meant anything, the only thread connecting her to whatever debt her family had left rotting under the sea. Handing it over felt like handing over a lung.

But she couldn't read it alone. That was the truth she'd been choking on for three days. The lines moved; the words dissolved; whatever the map wanted, it wanted something from her that she didn't understand—and she could not walk into open water carrying a mystery she couldn't open.

"Not the map," Wendell said. Her voice surprised her by holding steady. "Passage and provisions, and you can look at the map whenever I look at it. But it stays in my hand."

The knotting woman stopped. Finch pulled the fishhook from his teeth. Even the tide seemed to hold its breath.

"Bold," Isolde said. "Stupid, but bold." She turned her palm up anyway, waiting. "Then show me what you were going to pay with, since the map isn't on the table."

Wendell unwrapped the silk.

The compass needle woke the moment air touched it, trembling, straining toward Isolde Vane with an eagerness that made the captain go very still. Toward what Isolde Vane needed most. Wendell watched her watch it, watched something flicker behind those deep-water eyes—grief, old and calcified, the kind that stops being a wound and becomes a wall.

"What does it point at?" Isolde asked quietly.

"Whatever you need most. My mother made it. Before—" Wendell closed her hand around the needle, cutting off its song. "Before things went wrong."

Silence again. Bray shifted his weight; the pilings creaked. Somewhere out in the black water, a buoy bell rang once and quit.

"One condition," Isolde said at last. "Whatever's on that map. Whatever debt it's talking about. If it touches my crew—if it puts so much as a scratch on them—I put you off at the nearest rock and I don't look back."

"That's fair."

"It's not fair. It's generous. Don't confuse the two." She jerked her chin toward the boat. "We sail on the midnight tide. Be here or don't."

* * *

Wendell went home and burned her ledger.

Not the accounts—the other ledger, the list she'd kept for twelve years of every charm sold, every customer, every coin traced and re-traced to make sure nothing ever led back to the gate beneath her floor. The pages curled and went orange and then black in the kitchen stove, and she watched them with a strange lightness spreading through her chest, like a fever breaking.

Twelve years of being careful, and it had bought her nothing but a dead man bleeding brine on her doorstep and a debt she hadn't known she owed. Carefulness was just fear with good posture. She was done with good posture.

She packed in twenty minutes: bread, cheese, the knife under the floorboard, a spare dress, the map. At the door she stopped, went down to the cellar, and stood a moment with her palm flat against the stones above the sealed gate. It hummed beneath her hand, patient and vast, and for the first time she spoke to it aloud.

"I'm coming back," she told it. "And I'm bringing answers."

It didn't answer. But the humming changed pitch—so slightly she might have imagined it—and she chose to believe she hadn't.

* * *

Midnight found Ropewalk Stair empty of everything except fog and one small boat. The crew loaded her in silence: Bray took her bag as if it weighed nothing, which for him it nearly didn't; Sable pressed a strip of dried fish into her hand without meeting her eyes; Finch grinned around his fishhook and said, "Shopkeeper smells like flour," which Wendell chose to interpret as a welcome.

Isolde stood at the tiller of the Kestrel's Tooth proper—larger than Wendell had guessed, lean and black-hulled, her sails already unfurling like wings unfolding from sleep. As Wendell came aboard, the whole ship gave a shudder, a tremor running keel to masthead, and every member of the crew froze.

"She's never done that," Finch said.

"She does it when she likes someone," Bray rumbled. "Or when she smells trouble. Usually both."

Wendell kept her face neutral while her pulse hammered. Ships didn't shudder. Ships especially didn't shudder at gatekeeper's blood walking up their gangplank—but then, ships built by smugglers probably knew more about magic than the Ministry would ever admit.

Isolde watched her with that flat grey gaze, reading everything, conceding nothing. "Below decks," she said. "There's a bunk by the galley. You get one night's sleep, and at dawn you show me exactly where we're going and exactly what we're sailing into. If I don't like either answer—"

"You put me off at the nearest rock. I remember."

"No," Isolde said. She stepped close, close enough that Wendell could smell tar and salt and something underneath both, something older, like lightning on stone. "If I don't like the answers, I put myself off. This ship goes where the map goes, shopkeeper. I've been looking for that drowned gate for nine years." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "My brother is there. Or he was. So we sail together, or not at all."

The fog swallowed the harbor lights astern. Ahead, the open sea breathed in the dark, vast and waiting, and somewhere out in it, a circle drawn in a child's careful hand.

Wendell gripped the rail and let the wind take her hair loose for the first time in twelve years.

At dawn, she thought, the map would finally open. At dawn, she would learn what her family had done.

The Kestrel's Tooth ran east, into the rising dark, and the sea began to sing.
