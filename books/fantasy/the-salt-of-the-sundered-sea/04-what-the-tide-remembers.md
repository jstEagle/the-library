## 4. What the Tide Remembers

The Kestrel's Tooth smelled of tar, old blood, and onions that had been fried within living memory but not since. Isolde Vane stood at the helm with her boot hooked over the tiller and counted her crew the way she always did at dusk—by sound. Brannick's cough from the fo'c'sle. The twins arguing over dice in the hold. Old Sefa humming something tuneless as she coiled line. All present. All hers.

And the shopkeeper, sitting on a crate by the mainmast like a woman waiting for a dentist who was also going to rob her.

Three days out of Greyharbor and Wendell Eastwick had touched nothing. Not the rigging, not the rails, not the galley pot unless invited twice. She apologized to things when she bumped them. She had paid for passage in a charm so beautiful it made Sefa cry, and then sat down as if she'd handed over a sack of turnips instead of a small miracle.

Isolde had smuggled fugitives, relics, one archbishop, and a live wyrm-egg. She knew what cargo looked like when it was lying about itself. This woman was a locked box wearing an apron.

"Captain." Brannick's shadow fell across the deck boards. "She's been staring at the map again."

"I know."

"She's been staring at it for three hours."

"I said I know, Brannick."

He grunted and withdrew, which for Brannick constituted diplomacy. Isolde turned the tiller a hand's width into the swell and let herself look at the shopkeeper properly.

The map lay across Wendell's knees where it had lain since they cleared the harbor lights: fish-skin, salt-stiff, marked with lines that looked like nothing at all. Coastline smudge. A few scratches. Isolde had held it up to sun, moon, lantern flame, and once, in desperation, to Brannick's opinion. Nothing. It was the most expensive piece of nothing she'd ever sailed after, and she'd sailed after plenty.

But the shopkeeper kept touching it. That was the thing. Every few minutes her fingers would drift to the fish-skin and rest there, light as a confession, and every time, Isolde would have sworn—would have bet the Tooth itself—that the lines *shifted*. Just at the edge of seeing. Like writing underwater.

There was one explanation for that, and Isolde didn't like any of its relatives.

She waited until full dark, until the twins lost their dice game and went to sulk, until the sea settled into that long black breathing that made even hardened sailors speak quietly. Then she crossed the deck, took the tiller out of its crutch, and said, "Eastwick. Helm's yours. Hold her steady."

Wendell looked up, startled. "I don't know how to sail."

"You don't have to sail. You have to hold a stick still." Isolde wedged the tiller into her hands. "Feel that? When she pulls left, you push. That's the whole religion of it."

Wendell gripped the wood like it might bolt. "Why me?"

"Because I want to talk to your hands."

Isolde crouched by the crate, pulled the map off the shopkeeper's knees, and spread it flat between them. In the lantern light it was the same grey nothing. She laid her own palm on it. Salt-stiff skin, faintly warm, faintly wrong, like touching something that was pretending to be dead.

"Three days," Isolde said. "You've looked at this map more than you've looked at my crew, and my crew includes the twins. So here's what I think. I think this isn't a map for eyes. I think it's a map for blood."

Wendell's hands went very still on the tiller. The ship held her course, obedient, and something in Isolde noted that too—the woman was a natural, or the sea liked her, and both possibilities were interesting.

"That's superstition," Wendell said.

"Everything worth anything is superstition until it works." Isolde nodded at the map. "Touch it."

"I've been touching it."

"With your fingers. Through the fear." She kept her voice level, almost bored. "I mean really touch it. The way you'd touch it if nobody were watching. The way you touch whatever it is you keep under the floorboards of that shop, because don't tell me there's nothing—I've seen a hundred people hide things, and you hide yours like it breathes."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, Sefa's humming stopped, which meant Sefa was listening, which meant everyone was listening. Fine. Isolde had never run a discreet ship; she ran an honest one.

"The Ministry burned my cousin's shop with my cousin in it," she said, conversational. "Not for doing magic. For *having* some. There's a difference, and Corvane's people don't care about it, and I need to know which kind of trouble I'm carrying before it climbs aboard and names itself."

"You think I'm carrying magic."

"I think you're carrying something, and it's heavier than you are."

Wendell stared at her. And Isolde watched—the particular pleasure of her trade—watched the arithmetic happen behind the woman's eyes: the odds, the distances, the water all around, the fact that Isolde had been decent to her and could stop being decent at any moment. Watched her arrive, reluctantly, at the truth: that there was no version of this voyage where she stayed a shopkeeper.

"If I show you," Wendell said quietly, "you can't unsee it. Neither can your crew. And if the Ministry learns what I am—" Her jaw tightened. "They won't burn the shop this time. They'll take the person in it apart slowly enough to make it educational."

"They'll try," Isolde said. "I've a fast ship and slow morals."

Something flickered across Wendell's face—not quite a smile. The ghost of one, visiting.

Then she let go of the tiller with one hand, knelt, and pressed her palm flat against the fish-skin.

Isolde felt it before she saw it. A hum, low, felt in the teeth and the soles of the feet, the same note she'd heard once as a child through the floor of a drowned chapel. Light crawled out from under Wendell's fingers like ink dropped in water—and the grey nothing bloomed.

Lines everywhere. Channels and depths and currents, drawn in silver that moved, that *breathed*, the whole Sundered Sea rendered alive across the fish-skin. And at the center, pulsing faintly, a mark Isolde recognized from stories her grandmother had told to frighten children straight: a gate. Drowned. Waiting.

"Well," Isolde said, after a while. "That's not harmless trinkets."

"No," Wendell said. "It isn't."

"And you're not a shopkeeper."

"I am a shopkeeper. It's just not all I am." She lifted her hand and the light sank back into the skin, sulking. "My family kept a gate. Between this sea and... the other one. The one the Ministry says doesn't exist. My mother sealed it and hid us in plain sight and died pretending to be ordinary, and I've spent twelve years doing the same, and I would like to go back to doing it now, please."

"The gate that's failing."

Wendell's head came up sharply. "How do you know that?"

"Because the courier who brought you that map bled brine instead of blood on my dock, same as he did on yours, and because I've been sailing these waters twenty years and the tides have gone wrong the way they only go wrong when something big is holding its breath." Isolde stood. "And because I asked around before I agreed to carry you. You're not the only one hunting answers, Eastwick. You're just the only one the map will talk to."

* * *

Below decks, in the cramped cabin that smelled of lamp oil and Brannick's pipe, Isolde poured two fingers of plum brandy into tin cups and pushed one across the table. Wendell wrapped both hands around hers without drinking, which told Isolde more than the confession had.

"My brother's name was Tam," Isolde said.

Wendell blinked. "You have a brother?"

"Had. Have. Depends which side of the water you ask." Isolde drank. The brandy bit. Good. She wanted the bite. "Nineteen years ago I was running cargo out of Greyharbor, young and stupid and convinced the sea owed me a living. A storm took us near the Shallows. Boat broke. Tam went under, and I went under after him, and I saw them."

"Them."

"The sea-folk. The ones your covenant bound." She set the cup down. "Everyone in Greyharbor thinks they're a story we invented to scare children away from deep water. They're not a story. They pulled Tam out of the wreck. One of them looked at me—looked right at me, through me—and they took him down. Alive. Deliberate. And I've spent nineteen years telling myself they drowned him, because drowned is a thing you can bury."

Wendell had gone very pale. Or perhaps very still; with her it was hard to separate the two. "And now?"

"Now the tides are breaking and the gate is failing and everything your family hid is coming up for air." Isolde met her eyes. "If your gate opens—if the other shore comes back into reach—then somewhere down there is a man who's been on the wrong side of the water for nineteen years. Maybe dead. Maybe worse. Maybe sitting at somebody's table learning their songs." Her voice did something she hated, a small crack, quickly patched. "I'm not sailing you to save the world, Eastwick. I'm sailing you because it's the first time in nineteen years anyone's offered me a door to knock on."

The lantern swayed. Above them, boots crossed the deck—Brannick walking his rounds, unhurried, faithful.

Wendell set down her untouched brandy. When she spoke, her voice was rough. "My mother used to say the sea-folk sang our name at night. That the covenant wasn't a wall, it was a promise, and promises need two voices." She looked at her hands, the hands that had lit the map. "I thought she was being poetic. She was being literal. Everything in that house was literal and I thought I was just a strange child who heard humming under the floor."

"What did the humming sound like?"

"Like someone calling from very far away and very politely not insisting."

Isolde laughed—a short, surprised bark—and found that something in her chest had come loose by half a degree. Nineteen years of grief, and here was a woman describing exactly the sound she imagined Tam making at the bottom of the sea: patient, distant, refusing to give up.

"Your mother hid you," Isolde said. "Mine taught me to read charts and lie to harbormasters. Different methods, same instinct." She refilled both cups. "So here's where we are. You're the last of a line of gatekeepers, sailing toward a drowned gate with a failing covenant and a Ministry inspector who smells blood in the water. I'm a smuggler chasing a brother who may be a memory or may be a man. Neither of us trusts the other further than we can throw the anchor."

"That about sums it."

"But we're both going anyway." Isolde raised her cup. "To bad options."

"To bad options," Wendell echoed, and this time she drank.

* * *

Near dawn, Isolde came up for the watch change and found Wendell at the rail, the map open against her knees, glowing faintly silver in the pre-light. Not hiding it anymore. That was new.

"It moved again," Wendell said, without turning. "The mark. The gate. It's... pulling. Like the lines lean toward it."

Isolde followed her gaze east, where the sky was going the color of a bruise healing. The sea out there lay flat and shining and utterly wrong—too calm, too bright, holding its breath exactly the way she'd described.

And rising out of that brightness, thin and high and unmistakable, carried across the water on a wind that hadn't reached them yet:

Singing.

Sefa froze mid-coil. Brannick's pipe went out. Even the twins came up silent, hanging on the shrouds, listening to a sound none of them had words for.

Wendell's hand found the rail. Her knuckles were white.

"They know we're coming," she whispered.

The song rose, and beneath it—only Isolde seemed to hear it, only Isolde had spent nineteen years listening—a second voice, deeper, threading through the choir like a signature.

She knew that voice.

"*Tam,*" she breathed, and the Kestrel's Tooth ran on into the light.
