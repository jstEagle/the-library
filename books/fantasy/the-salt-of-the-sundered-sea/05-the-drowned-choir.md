## 5. The Drowned Choir

The map stopped being a map on the third night out.

Wendell knew it first in her teeth—a low hum, like biting foil, like the cellar-gate's whisper but thinner, stretched across open water. She'd spent twelve years learning every pitch of that hum. She could tune it out over ledgers and customers. Out here, with nothing between her and the horizon but black water, it was all she could hear.

She climbed to the deck, where Isolde stood at the wheel with her coat collar up against the spray.

"It's louder," Wendell said.

"I know." Isolde didn't look at her. "Crew's been hearing it since sundown. Tam swears it's whales. Pip swears it's his dead mother. Both of them are wrong."

"Then what is it?"

Isolde finally turned. In the lamplight her face was unreadable, which Wendell had learned was its own kind of answer.

"Your blood will tell you before I can."

* * *

The change came at dawn, and it came all at once.

One moment they were sailing grey water under a grey sky, the Kestrel's Tooth creaking through a long swell. The next, the sea ahead of them *lit*—not reflected light, not sun breaking cloud, but something rising from beneath, a shimmer spreading across the surface like oil made of moonlight. The water turned translucent in patches, and far down, where no depth should have permitted sight, Wendell saw color: green and violet veins threading through the deep like the stained glass of a drowned cathedral.

The singing resolved into words she couldn't translate but somehow understood anyway—the way you understand a lullaby from before memory.

Every soul on deck had come up. Tam gripped the rail with white knuckles. Old Sefa had both hands pressed flat over her heart. Even Pip had gone quiet, his usual patter dead in his throat.

"That's the Sundered Sea," Isolde said quietly, at Wendell's shoulder. "My brother's charts called it the Bleed. Sailors who cross it don't always come back the same. Some don't come back at all."

"The map," Wendell said. Her hands were shaking. She pulled the fish-skin from its oilcloth wrap, and the moment her bare fingers touched it, the etched lines began to move—ink swimming like schools of silver fish, redrawing itself, pointing not forward but *down*. "It wants us to stop here."

"Here?" Tam's voice cracked. "Captain, there's nothing here but—"

"There's everything here," Wendell said, and didn't recognize her own voice. It came out steady, certain, in a tone she hadn't used since she was a girl pressing her ear to the cellar floor. "Directly below us. Twelve fathoms, maybe less. The gate isn't only under my shop. There are... anchors. Roots. This is one of them."

She looked down into the glowing deep, and something in her chest that had been clenched for twelve years loosened half an inch.

"It's beautiful," she said, before she could stop herself. "Gods help me, it's beautiful."

* * *

They anchored rather than fight the current, because the current had opinions and the Kestrel's Tooth was losing the argument. The shimmer thickened around the hull until it seemed they floated on light instead of water.

Wendell knelt at the rail with her sleeves pushed back, one hand trailing toward the surface—not touching, never quite touching—and the voices rose to meet her. Not drowned souls, she realized. Not ghosts. *Singers.* Voices layered in harmony so old it predated grief, and threaded through them, faint as a signature at the bottom of a letter, a melody she knew.

Her mother's melody. Hummed over a cradle, over a chopping board, over the sealed trapdoor, once, when she thought her daughter was asleep.

"You knew this," Isolde said. She had come to stand beside her, arms crossed, watching Wendell's face more closely than the sea. "Before today. You've heard it."

"My mother sang it. I thought it was just—" Wendell laughed, and it came out wet. "I thought it was a song. A silly thing she made up. I spent twelve years telling myself the humming under my shop was pipes. Old plumbing. Anything." She shook her head. "I buried it so deep I almost buried the memory of burying it."

"And now?"

"Now I feel like someone handed me back a limb I cut off myself and told me to be grateful."

The crew kept their distance, but she felt them listening. Sefa murmured something in a tongue from the southern isles; Tam crossed himself with a salt-caked thumb.

Then the water broke.

It rose without splash or sound—a small shape, pale as sea-foam, crowned with hair that moved like kelp in a slow current. A child. A girl, no older than ten, her skin patterned with faint lines of silver that pulsed in time with the singing, her eyes large and dark and utterly unafraid. She hung in the air above the rail as though the water were a stair she'd simply paused halfway up.

No one breathed.

The child looked at the crew, at Isolde, at the ship—cataloguing, curious. Then her gaze found Wendell and fixed there, and her whole face changed. Lit. The silver lines along her arms flared bright.

"*Lirienne,*" the child said.

Wendell's knees nearly went.

"What did you call me?"

But the girl wasn't finished. She drifted closer, close enough that Wendell could smell the deep cold off her skin, salt and stone and something green growing. "*Lirienne. Lirienne of the Shore Gate. You have her voice. You have her hum.*" The child's Common was accented, liquid, each word rounded like a river stone. "We waited for your song. We heard it under the ground for many winters, small and sad. But now you bring it to the water yourself."

"My name," Wendell said, and her voice cracked down the middle, "is Wendell Eastwick."

The child tilted her head, uncomprehending, or pretending to be. "That is your shore-name. This is your water-name. Your mother gave it to us before you were born, so we would know you when you came." She reached out one small webbed hand, hovering an inch from Wendell's cheek, asking permission. "May I? You are cold. Up here everyone is so cold."

Wendell nodded before she decided to.

The touch was warm. Warmer than human skin, warm as a bath, warm as the kitchen of her childhood, and Wendell felt something in her crack wide open—twelve years of aprons and ledgers and *don't hum, don't listen, don't be*, all of it flooding up her throat at once.

"They told us you would come angry," the child said softly. "Or frightened. Or not at all. But you came *listening*. That is the best way to come."

"What do I call you?" Wendell managed.

"Neris." The girl smiled, and her teeth were small and pearly and entirely too numerous. "I was born in the Bleed the winter your gate began to fail. The elders say that is why I can hear shore-songs better than the others. Why I dreamed of your shop before I ever saw it." Her expression flickered—grief moving behind the wonder like a shadow under clear water. "It is failing badly now, keeper. The choir is thinning. Every year fewer voices. My grandmother's grandmother sang at the sealing, and even she cannot remember all the verses anymore."

Behind Wendell, Isolde spoke, very quietly. "Ask her about taken sailors."

Neris's dark eyes went to the captain, and something ancient passed across the child's face—recognition, perhaps, or regret.

"I know why you ask," Neris said. "The tall sorrow-woman with the brother-shaped hole in her. Yes. We know of him. He is not dead." She looked back at Wendell. "But that bargain is not mine to tell, and yours to hear, and neither of them is free. Come down when the choir calls you properly, keeper. All of you. Bring your questions and your debts."

She sank then, unhurried, the water closing over her like a door drawn gently shut—and the last thing visible was her hand, raised in a wave, silver-lined and small against all that glowing deep.

* * *

For a long moment nobody moved.

Then Pip said, faintly, "Right. So. Do we row down, or...?"

"We wait for the choir to call," Wendell said. Her cheeks were still wet; she wiped them briskly, businesslike, as if drying dishes. "She'll tell us how."

"And you're going to trust a child made of glow-water about what comes next," Tam said, "because she knew your mum."

"I'm going to trust her," Wendell said, "because when she touched me, the humming under my skin—the thing I've been drowning out for twelve years—went quiet for the first time. Not gone. *Quiet.* Like it was finally being heard by someone who understood the words." She turned to Isolde. "Your brother. She knows him. That's why you really signed on for this run, isn't it? The map was just the tide carrying you somewhere you already needed to go."

Isolde held her gaze a long moment. Whatever moved behind her eyes, she didn't spend it cheaply.

"Get some sleep, Eastwick," she said at last. "All of you. If we're going down to meet whatever lives at the bottom of the world's oldest wound, I want this crew sharp." She paused at the companionway, her back to Wendell. "And Eastwick? Next time something calls you by a name you don't know—you tell me *before* the glow-child does."

Then she was gone below, and Wendell stood alone at the rail as the light beneath the sea brightened, gathering itself like a breath before a note.

The choir began to call.
