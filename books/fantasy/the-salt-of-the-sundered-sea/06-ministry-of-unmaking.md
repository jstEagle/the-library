## 6. Ministry of Unmaking

Inspector Corvane did not believe in the sea. He believed in ledgers.

The sea was a thing that moved things—cargo, corpses, contraband—and a moving thing could be tracked, weighed, entered into a column, and closed out at the end of a quarter. The Sundered Sea itself was an accounting problem: one rift, one gate, one covenant, all of it sitting on Greyharbor's books like a debt no auditor had been permitted to read for two hundred years.

Until now. Until a shopkeeper named Wendell Eastwick took ship on a smuggler's hull and made his quarter very complicated indeed.

He stood at the rail of the Ministry cutter *Verity*, watching the Kestrel's Tooth's wake fade in the gray distance, and turned over in his mind the report from Greyharbor that had arrived by pigeon at dawn. His junior inspector, Pell, had written it in the careful hand of a man who wished he were writing something else.

*Subject's apprentice detained. Subject cooperative under standard measures. Apprentice claims ignorance of mistress's destination.*

Corvane did not believe in ignorance either. It was simply knowledge that hadn't been invoiced yet.

"Inspector." Pell came up beside him, pale from three days of bad weather and worse work. "The Tooth has altered course. Bearing east-northeast."

"Into the Shallows," Corvane said.

"You don't think they'd actually attempt the crossing. The charts say—"

"The charts are wrong on purpose." He said it without heat, the way he might note that a merchant's scale ran heavy. "Every chart issued since the Drowning is wrong by exactly as much as the Ministry needs it to be. The Sundered Sea isn't unmapped, Pell. It's *redacted*. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point of our office."

Pell looked at him with the expression junior inspectors wore when they suspected their superior had gone soft or strange. Corvane allowed it. Softness and strangeness were excellent camouflage for an auditor.

Below decks, the *Verity*'s hold carried forty barrels marked *salted herring* that contained no herring whatsoever. They carried the Ministry's answer to the covenant—a device assembled in the cellars beneath the Hall of Records by men who had never touched salt water, from plans torn out of Eastwick family papers confiscated sixty years ago. The papers called it a *keyhole saw*. The men who built it called it a breaker, because that was what it broke: not gates, but promises.

Corvane had read the orders twice, then a third time, because the third reading was where the truth usually showed its seams.

*Secure the drowned gate. Deploy the instrument. Ensure the covenant cannot be renewed by any party. Report harvest yield estimates to the Director within the season.*

Harvest yield. As if the other realm were a herring fishery.

He had spent eleven years in the Ministry of Unmaking and had told himself, all that time, that unmaking meant tidying—that the world accumulated magic the way harbors accumulated silt, and someone had to dredge it. It was a comfortable story. Comfortable stories, like comfortable shoes, eventually revealed themselves to have been pinching the whole while.

Now he stood on a cutter full of disguised ordnance, hunting a woman whose only crime appeared to be inheritance, and understood that the dredging had always been for something. You did not drain a swamp because you loved clean water. You drained it to build on the land.

"Signal from the lookout," Pell said. "They've reefed sail. They're... slowing down, sir. In open water."

Corvane raised his glass. Far off, the Kestrel's Tooth sat nearly becalmed, and around her hull the sea had begun to do something the ledgers had no column for. It glowed. Faintly, green-gold, like light through the bottom of a bottle, spreading outward from the smuggler's keel in slow rings.

The rift waters. The Tooth had found them first.

"Increase sail," Corvane said quietly. "And Pell—when we take that ship, I want the shopkeeper alive. The others are inventory. She's the asset."

He watched the glowing rings widen and thought of the apprentice in the cells below the Hall of Records, and wondered—not for the first time, but for the first time usefully—what exactly he was auditing, and for whom.

* * *

They had taken Tam's boots first. That was how the interrogation began every morning: a clerk entering the cell and removing his boots with great formality, as though the boots were evidence, as though Tam Olliver might escape barefoot through four locked doors and a courtyard full of crossbowmen. Then they would leave him barefoot on cold stone until his feet went numb, and bring him up to the white room, and ask him questions until his feet hurt worse than anything they actually did to him.

The white room had a table, two chairs, a lamp, and Inspector Pell, who was back in Greyharbor now, running the interview end of things while Corvane sailed. Pell was not cruel. That was somehow the worst part. He asked questions in the tone of a man helping you find your keys.

"Tell me again about the night the courier died."

"I've told you nine times."

"Tell me the tenth. Memory improves with rehearsal."

Tam looked at his bare feet. Twelve days in custody. Twelve days of the same six questions rotated like crops. Where did she go. What did she take. Who helped her. What did the map show. What did she keep in the cellar. What did she tell you about her mother.

That last one was new, twelve days ago. That last one had changed the temperature of the room.

"She didn't talk about her mother," Tam said. "Same as I don't talk about mine. Some things aren't stock."

"Your mistress kept a sealed door in her cellar. Sealed doors are stock, Master Olliver. Inventory. Someone pays for them, someone guards them." Pell leaned forward. "You ground pigments for her for six years. You slept above that cellar. You never once heard what was behind the door?"

"I heard humming. I told you. Low hum, like a wet finger on glass. I thought it was the pipes."

"And you thought that was *normal.*"

"I thought everything in Greyharbor was normal until this year." Tam raised his head. There was something new in his voice, something that had been hardening day by day in the white room, and he only now let it out. "Here's what I know, Inspector. She sold nothing in that shop. Nothing real. Little tin charms and dried kelp and lies for tourists. Six years I worked there and I never once caught her doing actual magic—not a cantrip, not a spark. Do you understand what that costs? To have whatever she has and sit on it, every day, for twelve years? I used to think she was a coward." He swallowed. "I know better now. Sitting on it was the bravest thing I ever watched anyone do. And you people are going to drag her back here in chains for the crime of having been born."

Pell was quiet a moment. Then, gently, in the key-finding tone: "Who said anything about chains?"

And that was when Tam saw it—the seam in the man's careful face. Not cruelty. Something worse. Pity.

"What happens to her," Tam said slowly, "if your inspector catches her?"

"That's above my station."

"Pell. You've sat across from me for twelve days. You've brought me tea twice when it wasn't authorized. What. Happens. To her."

Pell looked at the door. Then at the lamp. Then, finally, at Tam, and his voice dropped to something almost human.

"The orders say the Eastwick woman is to be secured intact because the covenant requires an Eastwick to renew it. But the Director's standing directive—" he stopped. Started again. "There's a machine on that cutter, boy. Built from her family's own papers. It doesn't seal the gate and it doesn't open it. It *unmakes the promise*. And once the covenant's broken, the realm behind the gate stops being a neighbor and starts being a resource. Fisheries. Salt mines. Things that live there, rendered down. There are men in the Hall of Records who have already drafted the tariffs."

The lamp hummed. Somewhere below, a door slammed, and Tam's numb feet felt suddenly very far away.

"They're not trying to stop her," he said. "They're trying to stop the gate from ever being *kept* again."

"Now you've done your sums," Pell said. He rose, gathered his papers, and paused at the door with his hand on the latch—just long enough. "The cells on the harbor level flood at high tide. Old design flaw. Never fixed, because no one important is ever held there." He met Tam's eyes. "High tide is in four hours. I'd hate for a clerk to have left a file cabinet unlocked on his way out. Clerks make so many mistakes."

The door clicked shut. Tam sat alone in the white room, barefoot, doing arithmetic.

Twelve days of questions, and the thirteenth had finally given him an answer worth escaping for.

* * *

Aboard the Kestrel's Tooth, the glow had reached the rigging.

Wendell stood at the bow with Isolde, watching green-gold light climb the shrouds like ivy, and tried not to think about how beautiful it was, because beauty had been the bait her whole life. Every time the magic showed itself, something followed. An inspection. A death. A war.

"It's singing," Brack said behind them. The big man had his cap in his hands, which he did perhaps once a year. "You hear it? Whole crew hears it. Like choir practice underwater."

"Drowned Choir," Wendell said. "My mother wrote about it. Waters where the rift bleeds through. The voices are—" She stopped.

"Are what?" Isolde asked.

"Mine. Ours. The sea-folk sing the names of everyone the covenant ever bound." Wendell's throat tightened. "It keeps a ledger too. A different kind than yours, Captain."

Isolde didn't answer right away. When she did, her voice was flat in the way Wendell had learned meant danger. "Then it'll have my brother's name in it."

Before Wendell could speak, the lookout shouted—not alarm, but confusion. Sail on the horizon, flying no colors, then colors after all: grey and silver, official, unmistakable.

The *Verity*. Making straight for them, and making no effort to hide.

Isolde swore with the fluency of a woman who had been swearing professionally for thirty years. "We're becalmed in their blessed choir-water. Can't run. Can't fight a cutter."

"We don't need to run," Wendell said slowly. Because she was looking at the glow, and the glow was answering her—rising toward her hands, warm, insistent, like a dog that knows its name. "The rift's bleeding through here. Which means the gate's close. Which means—" She turned to Isolde. "How fast can your crew get below and batten everything?"

"Why?"

"Because the Ministry didn't send a cutter across the Sundered Sea for me." Wendell watched the Verity's grey silhouette resolve, watched the crane arm swinging out over its deck, lowering something long and dark and wrong toward the waterline. Her stomach dropped through the hull. "They sent it for the gate. Whatever that is they're carrying—it's not a weapon aimed at us."

Brack squinted through the glass and went grey. "Captain. That barrel-work. That's not cargo. That's got sigils burned into it. Deep ones."

Isolde took the glass herself. A long silence. When she lowered it, her knuckles were white around the brass.

"It's pointed at the water," she said. "At the gate itself. They mean to break it open from this side—or break the covenant holding it shut, I don't know the difference, and I'm guessing neither does anyone on that boat." She wheeled on the crew. "All hands! Get below, seal the hatches, lash everything that matters!"

Wendell stayed at the rail, staring at the approaching cutter, at the machine gleaming dully in its cradle. Twelve years of hiding, of smallness, of selling nothing—and the whole time, her own city had been building this. Building it from her family's stolen papers, with her family's own craft turned inside out.

Greyharbor hadn't outlawed magic. It had nationalized the ambition.

"They're not chasing me," she said quietly, to Isolde, to the choir singing in the water, to whoever was listening. "They never were. I'm just the loose thread. The gate's the garment."

Isolde gripped her shoulder, hard. "Then we cut their loom. Get below, Eastwick. Whatever that thing does when it fires, I'd rather be deaf below decks than blind at the rail."

But Wendell lingered one heartbeat longer, watching the Verity close the distance—and saw, in the green-gold light rising from the deep, the shapes of the drowned choir gathering beneath the hull. Not fleeing the machine.

Turning to face it.

And beneath the singing, faint but unmistakable, a new voice joined the chorus—one that sang a name that was hers, and her mother's, and older than both:

*Eastwick. Eastwick. Come and keep.*
