## 7. The Price Named Twice

Isolde Vane had haggled with slavers, out-drunk a kraken-cult priest, and once talked her way past three Ministry cutters with nothing but a forged manifest and a smile. None of it prepared her for the sea-folk's idea of hospitality.

They came at dusk, when the Kestrel's Tooth lay anchored in the drowned choir's shimmer, hull groaning against currents that moved like breathing. They rose without splashing—faces like wet moonstone, eyes the color of deep water over black sand—and they did not board. They simply appeared on deck, saltwater pooling around their feet in rings that never spread.

The crew backed against the rail. Even Brannick, who swore he'd wrestled a reef-wyrm and lost only his pride, went pale.

But Wendell stepped forward. Shopkeeper's posture. Chin up. Terrified and refusing to show it, which Isolde was beginning to understand was simply how Wendell Eastwick did everything.

"You called me by my mother's name," Wendell said to the tallest of them. "Say what you came to say."

The one who spoke had a voice like water finding cracks in stone. "The gate weakens, daughter of Miriel. The covenant frays. It must be reforged."

"Then tell me how, and I'll do it." Wendell's hands were shaking. Isolde saw it. So did the sea-folk, probably. "I've spent twelve years hiding what I am. I'm done hiding."

*There she is,* Isolde thought, and hated how much it mattered to her.

"The terms are old," the sea-voice said. "Older than your city's drowning. A gate between realms cannot be sealed by force nor opened by will. It must be *kept*. And a keeper must be bound by name and blood—and by price."

"Name it," Wendell said.

"A life. Freely given. The old covenant took your mother's sister. The one before that, a fisherman who loved the sea more than his shore. The gate drinks what is offered willingly, and nothing else will feed it."

The deck went very quiet. Somewhere below, the drowned choir sang on, sweet and terrible.

Wendell nodded slowly, like a woman doing arithmetic. "Fine. Mine, then. I'll give mine."

"No," Isolde said.

Everyone turned. She hadn't meant to speak. She spoke anyway.

"Wendell—" She crossed the deck, boots ringing on wet planks. "You don't get to volunteer. You're the only gatekeeper left. If you go into that gate, the covenant dies with you the next time it frays. You're not offering a life. You're offering an ending."

"I'm offering what's owed," Wendell snapped. "My family built this gate. My family let it rot while they hid in a city that drowned mages. That debt is mine, Captain, and I don't recall asking you to co-sign."

"It's not a debt you can pay and walk away from. There's no walking away. That's the whole point."

"And what would you know about it?"

And there it was—the question Isolde had been swallowing since the map first bloomed red under Wendell's blood. She felt every eye on her: Brannick, Sefa at the wheel, little Pip pressed against the mast. Twelve years of carrying it alone, and here was a woman who'd done the same, and it made lying feel obscene.

"My brother," Isolde said. "Tammas Vane. Taken by the sea-folk eleven years ago, off the Teeth. I've run these waters ever since, half-looking, half-dreading. Because if he's alive down there—" her voice cracked and she let it, damn it, let it in front of the crew, in front of the sea-folk, in front of God and the drowned choir— "then someone paid for him. Someone gave a life so he could be kept."

The tall one inclined its head, slow as a tide turning. "The boy Tammas lives, daughter of the shore. He was given passage when a drowned mother offered herself for her child. He serves in the deep courts now. He remembers his name."

Isolde's knees nearly went. Brannick caught her elbow and she shook him off, because if she leaned on anyone right now she'd come apart entirely.

"So it can be traded," she said hoarsely. "A life for a life. Then hear me, gatekeeper: I offer mine. Freely. For Tammas's freedom and for your gate and for the simple fact that I am tired, Wendell, I am so tired of everyone I love being on the wrong side of the water."

"No," Wendell said again, quieter now, which was worse.

"You don't get to refuse for me."

"You don't get to die for me! You've known me three weeks!"

"I've known you long enough," Isolde said, and couldn't stop, words spilling out like a breached hull, "to know you'd have done it for any of us without blinking. You patched Pip's fever-burns at three in the morning. You stood between Sefa and a Ministry harpoon gun. You think nobody sees you, shopkeeper, but we see you, and if you think I'll stand on this deck and watch you walk into a gate that eats willing hearts while I have a heart to spare—you are gravely mistaken about who I am."

Wendell stared at her. The sea-light made her eyes look like shallow water over gold.

"That's not love, Isolde. That's arithmetic. My life against yours, and you've decided yours weighs less."

"Don't you dare—"

"Because of Tammas? Because you feel guilty for surviving when he didn't?" Wendell's voice rose. "I buried my mother alone in a cellar I wasn't allowed to cry in. I know exactly what guilt does. It doesn't get to steer this ship."

For a moment Isolde genuinely considered throwing her overboard. The urge passed. Mostly.

"Then what's your plan, gatekeeper? Refuse the price and watch the Sundered Sea eat Greyharbor?"

"My plan," Wendell said slowly, "is that nobody has explained the terms properly. Terms are contracts. Contracts have loopholes. I've read every scrap my mother left behind, and she wrote one line over and over in her journals: *the gate keeps what keeps it.*"

She turned to the sea-folk. "You said a keeper must be bound by name, blood, and price. You assumed price means death because that's what my ancestors offered. But my mother also wrote that the first covenant was sealed by a woman who gave up her *shore*—her home, her world—to live between. Not died. Lived differently. Which was it? Death, or severance?"

Silence. Even the choir seemed to lean in.

The tall one's expression flickered—something like surprise, or maybe respect. "The gate accepts a life freely given. What constitutes *given*... has narrowed with each sealing. Each keeper chose the smaller reading. Death is certain. Anything else is risk."

"Risk," Wendell repeated. "So it's not fixed. It's tradition wearing the mask of law." She laughed, short and disbelieving. "Twelve years of hiding, and the answer was that the gate is just as scared as we are. It takes what's offered completely. Nobody's ever offered anything *complete* except their life."

"What would complete mean, then?" Isolde asked, despite herself.

"Not dying," Wendell said. "Everything else. Every secret, every hiding place, every wall I built. I write my name into the covenant as steward—not sacrifice—and I hold both shores until something stronger than me takes it up. I stop being invisible. I stop being safe. Forever." Her voice wobbled on the last word. "That's the price I can pay that neither of you can. My anonymity. My ordinary life. The thing I've guarded harder than my blood."

The sea-folk exchanged looks like light through water.

"It may hold," the tall one said at last. "It has never been tried."

"Of course it hasn't," Isolde muttered. But she was watching Wendell—this woman who'd spent twelve years making herself small, volunteering to become enormous—and something in her chest was cracking open along fault lines she'd thought were scar tissue.

"We argue like sisters," Isolde said abruptly.

"We've known each other three weeks."

"Long enough." She stuck out her hand, rough palm up, the gesture absurdly mundane above all that singing water. "Truce, then. No more volunteering each other for death. We argue about living things instead. Rations. Heading. Whether Brannick cheats at cards."

"I don't cheat," Brannick said, wounded. "I *win.*"

Wendell took her hand. Her grip was warm and salt-sticky and it did not let go as quickly as a truce required.

"For the record," Isolde said, "if the loophole fails tomorrow—if the gate wants blood after all—I'm still standing between you and it. Truce covers arguments, not stupidity."

"Noted, Captain. Same terms apply in reverse."

Above them, the stars were coming out through the shimmer, doubled and trembling where the other realm bled through. Below, the choir sang a note that might have been warning or welcome.

Then the water at the ship's flank lit from beneath—a spreading green-gold glow, rising fast—and a shape broke the surface: a young man with moonstone-pale skin and eyes like deep water, hauling himself toward the rail with arms that remembered rope work and a face Isolde had last seen aged nineteen.

"Sol?" he said. "Sol, is that you?"

Isolde Vane, who had not wept in eleven years, grabbed the rail with both hands.

* * *

Behind her, Wendell watched brother and sister collide across the rail, and the tall sea-figure drifted close to her shoulder.

"Keeper-to-be," it said softly. "Understand what you bargain. The Ministry fleet sails behind you. They do not wish the gate reforged—they wish it *broken*, that they may harvest the far realm. They will reach this water by dawn."

Wendell looked at Isolde laughing and crying into her brother's hair, and set her jaw.

"Then we'd better make the gate ready before sunrise," she said. "How do I begin?"
