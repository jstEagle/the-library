## 8. Storm Over the Gate

The gate found them before they found it.

Wendell felt it first, in her teeth—a low hum rising through the hull like a struck bell that never finished ringing. Twelve years she'd lived above that sound, sleeping over its muffled pulse in her cellar, and now here it was again, vast and unhidden, thrumming up through a hundred fathoms of black water. The Sundered Sea's heart. The drowned gate.

"Captain," she called. "We're close."

Isolde Vane stood at the wheel with salt crusted white along her jaw, three days without proper sleep, and she didn't need telling. She'd gone still in that way of hers, the way Wendell had learned to read as attention rather than fear. "I feel it in the wood," Isolde said. "Ship knows it too."

The Kestrel's Tooth ran before a wind that shouldn't have existed this far out—warm, tasting of green things, of rivers, of somewhere that was not the grey world Greyharbor had made. Ahead, the sea itself was wrong. The water glowed from beneath with a pale gold light, and where the light broke the surface it rose as mist, and in the mist—

"There." Pip's voice cracked. The boy was at the bow rail, pointing with a hand that shook. "There's a *door* in the water."

Not a door. An arch, vast as a cathedral's mouth, standing upright beneath the surface, its stone veined with the same gold light. It had been built by hands, or by something that had once had hands, and it was *cracked*—a seam running through it like a wound, leaking brightness into the dark water around it.

And behind them, come up over the horizon like a bruise, sails.

"Ministry," said Brack, from the rigging. He'd seen them first; Brack always saw trouble first. "Six ships. Maybe seven. Gun decks."

Seven. Wendell's stomach turned to stone. The Kestrel's Tooth mounted two swivel guns and a great deal of nerve.

"They've been herding us," Isolde said quietly. She turned the wheel a fraction, eyes never leaving the arch. "Three days of fair wind handed to us on a plate. Corvane wanted us to lead him straight to it." Her knuckles were white on the spokes. "Clever bastard."

"He can't be allowed to touch it," Wendell said. "You heard what the choir told us. If the Ministry breaks the covenant—"

"I heard." Isolde looked at her then, and there was something in her face Wendell hadn't seen before—not the captain's mask, not the smuggler's arithmetic. Something rawer. "Wendell. Whatever happens in the next hour. You go to that gate. Do you understand me? The rest of us are furniture."

"That's not—"

"It's not a request." And then, because she was Isolde, she smiled: thin and sharp as a gutting knife. "Besides. I've got a brother to collect. Can't do that if I'm dead."

* * *

They came out of the rain-squall at full sail with the gold light painting their hull, and the Ministry fleet spread across the water before them like a hand closing.

Corvane's flagship led—a black-hulled frigate flying the grey-and-white of the Unmaking, its figurehead a woman whose face had been chiseled smooth. Erased. Wendell looked at it and felt something cold settle behind her ribs. That was what the Ministry did. That was what they'd tried to make *her*: a woman with no face, no name worth keeping, no inheritance worth killing for.

The first cannonball took the mainmast's foot and splintered it sideways.

The ship screamed—there was no other word for it—and the crew screamed with it, and then nobody screamed anymore because there was work. Brack and the twins cut the tangled rigging free while it flailed like living rope. Pip hauled powder. Old Maren, who'd claimed for six days she was only aboard to cook, loaded the stern chaser herself and put a ball through a cutter's bowsprit with a cook's casual contempt.

"We can't win a fight," Isolde shouted over the din, wrestling the wheel through the swell. "So we don't fight. We run the gap!" She pointed—at the space between two Ministry brigs, narrow as an alley, opening onto the glowing water above the arch. "Wendell!"

Wendell was already at the rail. Below, through water gone clear as glass near the gate, she could see them—the sea-folk, dozens of them, risen from the deep to watch. Pale shapes with lantern-eyes, faces old and young, and among them one small form that pressed its palms flat against some invisible boundary between waters, looking up.

The sea-child. The one who'd called her by her mother's name.

*I'm coming*, Wendell thought, not knowing if the thought could carry, not knowing if it mattered. *Hold on.*

The Tooth hit the gap at nine knots with both brigs firing point-blank. Grapeshot raked the deck and Wendell felt it pass—felt the air tear where men stood a heartbeat before. When she could see again, one of the twins was down, and Brack was dragging him by the collar toward cover, roaring words Wendell would not repeat even under torture.

Then the flagship came about, and Wendell saw Inspector Corvane himself at the rail.

He was smaller than his reputation. A neat man in a neat grey coat, holding something that caught the gold light and threw it back wrong—a rod of pale metal, humming with the same note as the gate, but *inverted*. Sour. A thing designed to unmake other things.

He raised it, and even across the water Wendell felt the pressure of it, a silence pushing against her skull like a thumb.

"No you don't," Isolde breathed.

What happened next, Wendell saw only in pieces, and would spend the rest of her life reassembling.

She saw Isolde throw the wheel hard over, putting the burning Tooth directly between Corvane's rod and the woman running for the rail. She saw Brack hurl a grappling hook that wasn't meant to catch anything but attention. She saw Pip, thirteen years old and furious, fire the last swivel gun into the frigate's stern gallery just to watch the glass fall.

She saw Corvane aim anyway.

And she saw Isolde Vane step off her own wheel, cross the deck in four strides, and put herself between Wendell Eastwick and the rod's discharge as it came.

It came as light. It came as absence. Wendell never found words for it, because the world went white and silent and when it came back the Kestrel's Tooth was burning from stem to stern, and Isolde was on her knees in the wreckage of the quarterdeck with her coat smoking, holding her own left arm as though it belonged to someone else, blood—ordinary red blood, thank all the drowned gods, ordinary red—seeping between her fingers.

"*Go*," Isolde snarled, before Wendell could say anything at all. Her face was grey as ash. "You go *now*. Don't you dare look back at me, Wendell Eastwick. I didn't get shot so you could stand there making that face."

"You're hurt—"

"I'm *annoyed*. Go."

The gate was ten feet below the rail. The sea-child waited there, arms raised. Behind Wendell the ship groaned and settled, and Brack was shouting for the boats, and Maren was binding Isolde's arm with strips of sailcloth and curses, and the Ministry fleet was coming about for one more pass.

Wendell climbed the rail.

She looked back once—she couldn't not—at her crew, her impossible crew: Brack hauling wounded men, Pip loading a gun he couldn't possibly reload again, Maren holding pressure on Isolde's arm while Isolde herself glared up with fury and terror and love so naked it looked like pain. The cost of being loved, made visible in fire and blood on a burning deck. This was what it bought. This was the price of twelve years of pretending she needed no one.

She paid it gladly.

"Wendell!" Isolde's voice, cracking across the smoke. "Whatever's down there—whatever your mother left you—you tell it *Vane sent you!*"

Wendell laughed—or sobbed, it was hard to say which—and let go of the rail.

The sea took her gently, the way it takes its own. The gold light closed over her head, and the cannon-fire became a distant drumming, and the sea-child's small hands found hers in the bright water, and pulled her down through the shimmer toward the cracked and ancient door, toward whatever waited on the other side of her mother's silence.

Above her, the Kestrel's Tooth burned like a falling star.

Below her, the gate began, very slowly, to open.
