## 10. The Keeper of Two Shores

**Wendell**

The sign went up on a grey Tuesday, when the fog off the Sundered Sea sat so thick you could barely see across the lane. WENDELL EASTWICK — CHARMWRIGHT & KEEPER, the letters said, painted in gold by Tam's unsteady hand while I held the ladder and pretended not to cry about it.

For twelve years my shop had sold nothing. Now the door stood open from dawn until the lamps were lit, and people came.

Not many, at first. Greyharbor had spent three generations drowning its mages and teaching its children to flinch at the word *magic*. But laws crack the way seawalls do—slowly, then all at once—and the Ministry's edicts were crumbling faster than anyone had guessed. The Unmaking writs had been burned in the harbor square by a mob that included two aldermen and, if rumor was true, the harbor-master's own grandmother. What replaced them was nothing so grand as tolerance. It was curiosity. It was need. The sea gave better fish than it had in living memory, and everyone knew who to thank for it.

So they came to my shop. Fishermen wanting charms against squalls. Mothers wanting salt-blessed ribbons for their daughters' wrists. An old woman who pressed my hand and said, "Your mother mended my father's net once. I never forgot." That one I carried around for days like a coal in my chest.

And beneath the shop floor, under the cellar where the gate hummed its low patient note, Isolde Vane slept in the bed I'd dragged down for her, her wound knitting at whatever pace the sea decided, which was slower than either of us liked.

"Shopkeeper," she'd say when I brought her broth, using the old name like an insult she'd grown fond of. "You've gone respectable."

"Don't tell anyone," I'd say. "I have a reputation as a smuggler's accomplice to maintain."

She'd laugh, and the laugh would cost her something, and I'd pretend not to see it. That was our arrangement now: we saw everything and pretended to see nothing, because some kindnesses are easier given sideways.

* * *

**Isolde**

I hated the bed. I hated the ceiling with its water-stain shaped like a drowned man's profile. I hated most of all being still, because stillness gave a woman time to think, and thinking led inevitably to Bram.

He came up from the gate on the third day after we'd made harbor—not walking through it, exactly, but arriving the way tide arrives, suddenly everywhere at once. My brother. Twenty-two years old in the face and forty-odd in the eyes, gills long since faded, a scar across his forearm I remembered giving him with a fishhook when he was nine.

"You got old," was the first thing he said to me.

"You got wet," I said, and then we were holding onto each other in Wendell's cellar like the storm was still going, like the Ministry fleet was still firing on us, like the Kestrel's Tooth wasn't a charred hulk at the bottom of the drowned gate with half her ribs burned away.

He stayed a week before the pull took him back. "It doesn't hurt," he told me at the threshold, the light behind him green-gold and moving. "It's just that you can hear them singing all day, and eventually you want to sing along." He squeezed my hand. "Come when you're mended. Both shores, Iss. That's what the new covenant means. Nobody has to choose."

Both shores. Wendell had written those words into the covenant with her own blood and her own name, and the whole shape of the world had quietly rearranged itself around them.

I meant to go back to the sea. I did. But the wound the Ministry ball had torn in my side healed crooked, and the crookedness kept me abed, and the bed kept me in Greyharbor, and somewhere in the fourth month I realized I'd stopped counting the days until I could leave.

That was the thing nobody warned you about found family: it didn't ask your permission. It just set a bowl of soup beside you and waited.

* * *

**Wendell**

The sea-child's name was Nerrin, though she answered to nearly anything sung rather than spoken. She had been the first voice in the Drowned Choir to call me by my mother's name, back when I still flinched at it, and when the covenant settled and the rift steadied, she swam up the harbor channel one morning, hauled herself onto my doorstep trailing kelp and wonder, and announced she was staying.

"I'm apprenticed to you," she said. "It's already been decided. I decided."

"You're what, eight?"

"Nine tides. Old enough."

Old enough. She learned the shop the way she learned everything—by touching, tasting, and occasionally flooding it. She could hold a charm up to the light and tell you whose grief had gone into the making of it, which unnerved the customers until they discovered she was always gentle about it. She wore boots with the toes cut out and refused, absolutely refused, to learn arithmetic.

"The ledger is a tyranny," she informed me.

"The ledger is how we know if we're starving."

"Then let us starve beautifully."

But here was the thing about Nerrin: every evening she'd sit on the cellar stairs above the gate, legs dangling into the hum, and sing down into it—not spells, just songs, fish-market songs and lullabies and once, memorably, a bawdy ballad Isolde taught her that made the gate flicker like a candle in wind. And the voices below would sing back. And the shop above would fill, without either of us planning it, with people who'd come to listen.

An invisible woman finally seen. A drowned choir finally heard. I kept waiting for the Ministry to kick the door in. They never did. Corvane himself came once, alone, out of uniform, and stood in the shop for a long moment among the hanging charms.

"They'll have your head for this someday," he said, without heat.

"Probably," I said.

"Hmph." He bought a luck-charm for his granddaughter, paid full price, and left.

* * *

**Isolde**

Supper happened the way harbors happen—gradually, then all at once. First it was Wendell bringing my tray down to the cellar. Then Nerrin insisting on eating with me because "the stairs count as the same room." Then Tam, Wendell's apprentice from the bad years, showing up with bread he'd stolen from his own family's bakery because "Miss Eastwick deserves proper crust." Then the crew.

They arrived the first Friday of autumn, all seven of them, filling the shop with salt-smell and argument. Brack and Ossory hauled in a cask of wine "liberated from a wreck, technically." Maren the navigator brought a chart of the new waters—waters no map had ever held, where the Sundered Sea bled through in slow silver veins, safe now, passable, alive. Little Fitch, who'd lost two fingers to the Ministry fire at the gate, had taken to carving wooden birds, and he set one on my table without a word: a kestrel, wings spread, flying.

Nobody said *for the ship*. Nobody had to.

"We're getting her back, you know," Brack said, wine sloshing. "The Tooth. There's timber upcoast, and a shipwright in Sallow Bay who owes me—"

"A shipwright who tried to hang you," Ossory corrected.

"—who owes me a favor and an apology, in whichever order he prefers."

They argued shipwrights and rigging and whether kestrels could actually swim until the candles burned low, and Wendell moved through the middle of it refilling bowls, and I watched her from my chair by the cellar door and thought: there. That's what they drowned the mages to prevent. Not power. This. A woman at the center of a room, lit by ordinary firelight, belonging to it completely.

Bram would come up tomorrow. He'd said he would, and lately the sea had been generous about letting its folk walk the shore. He'd bring pearls for Nerrin and complaints about my convalescence, and he'd sit at this table, and the circle would be whole.

I caught Wendell looking at me across the noise. She raised her spoon in something between a toast and a dare. *Well, Captain?*

I raised mine back. *Aye.*

* * *

**Wendell**

Later, when the crew had staggered home or curled up wherever they fell, and Isolde had finally surrendered to sleep upstairs—the upstairs, she'd declared in month five, because the view was wasted on a cellar—I went down to the gate alone, as I did most nights.

The hum had changed since I'd written my name into the covenant. It used to sound like warning. Now it sounded like a house settling, like breathing in sleep. I put my palm against the cool stone and felt the choir beneath it, vast and patient, and somewhere in that vastness my mother's memory turned toward me the way she always did—not accusing anymore, not pleading. Just present. Just glad.

"I kept the shop," I told her. "Turns out that was the magic all along."

Nerrin's voice drifted down the stairwell, half-asleep, singing a fragment of the bawdy ballad. Above her, Isolde's uneven footsteps crossed the floorboards. Above her, the sign with my name on it creaked in the night wind off a sea that was no longer sundered.

Twelve years I had been a woman pretending to be ordinary. I was done pretending. Whatever came next—Corvane's successors, the slow politics of two realms learning to share a shoreline, the rebuilding of a burned ship by a crew who wouldn't quit—whatever came, it would find me standing in an open doorway, seen, named, keeper of two shores.

The gate sang. I sang back.

And far out past the harbor bar, where the silver veins of the other realm shimmered under the moon, something vast and curious turned toward the city on the coast—and began, very slowly, to approach.
