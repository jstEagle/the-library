## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The mule went lame at the third mile-marker past Widow's Rest, and Paloma knew before anyone said a word that the night would go badly.

He crouched beside the animal in the dark, his fingers reading the heat of the hoof like a blind man reading scripture. The near-fore was swollen, tender as an overripe plum. Behind him the caravan waited in a hush of held breath — nine wagons, thirty-one souls, and enough untaxed salt, tin, and dreamleaf to hang every one of them twice over if the wardens caught them on the wrong side of Thornfell. Which they were, and would be for another six hours of climbing, if the mountain permitted it.

"She'll carry to the high col," Paloma said. "After that we rest her."

Doss grunted from somewhere above him on the trail, a sound like a stone rolling downhill. "She'd better. Or she'll feed the ravens and you'll walk."

Paloma didn't answer. He had stopped answering Doss three years ago, back when answering still felt like something a person could afford. He rose, brushed the grit from his knees, and took up his position at the head of the line with the lantern hooded to a coin-slit of light. Ahead of them the path threaded upward between walls of black pine, and above the pines — though no one in the caravan could see it — ran the thing that made this route possible at all: the old border wards of Thornfell, laid down centuries ago by kings who had never seen the mountains they were claiming, humming their slow silver song along the ridgeline like a plucked string that never quite finished sounding.

Paloma could hear them. That was the gift nobody had wanted, the reason Maren had taken him in and then, eventually, the reason he had left her: he heard magic the way other men heard music or lies. The wards sounded, to his ear, like a harp being tuned in a distant room — patient, endless, faintly sweet. For eleven years of running the smuggler roads he had navigated by that sound, steering the caravans through the gaps where the song thinned, avoiding the places where it swelled into the warden-patrolled crescendos. He knew Thornfell's music better than he knew his own face.

Which was why, a little past midnight, when the harp-string snapped, he was the only one who heard it.

* * *

It did not snap all at once. That was the horror of it, looking back. It frayed first — a thinning in the upper register, as though some vast hand were drawing one thread of the chord out and out until it went translucent — and Paloma stopped mid-stride so suddenly that the mule behind nearly walked into him.

"What?" Doss hissed, coming up fast. Doss always came up fast; he smelled weakness the way carrion birds smell blood. "What is it?"

"Quiet." Paloma raised a hand. His heart had begun to do something strange — not racing but stuttering, missing beats, as though his body understood before his mind did that the world had just lost one of its four walls.

The sound thinned further. He thought of Maren's cottage in the lowlands, the way she would test a charm's integrity by holding it to her ear and listening for the buzz of unraveling. *If you hear it fray,* she had told him once, kneeling in the garden dirt with a warded stone in her palm, *you run. You don't ask what's fraying it. You don't stay to watch. Fraying is the beginning of hunger, and hunger always eats outward.*

"Mare—" he began, and stopped himself, because he had not let himself say her name aloud in three years and was not about to start in front of thirty-one witnesses.

The thread snapped.

There was no thunder. There was no light. What there was instead was a silence of a very particular shape — the silence of a room after a clock has been removed, where the ear keeps reaching for the tick and finding nothing. The ward-song along the whole eastern ridgeline of Thornfell, the silver patience of it, the centuries-old sweetness — gone. Not dimmed. Gone, the way a tooth is gone from the jaw.

And then the stars went out.

Not clouded. Not veiled. Paloma looked up through the black lattice of the pines and saw the sky simply *empty* — a smooth, starless dark from ridge to ridge, as if the heavens had been wiped clean with a wet cloth. No moon either, though there should have been a fat half-moon riding high over the eastern peaks. Nothing. The darkness overhead was total in a way darkness had never been before; it had texture, weight, a faint grey sheen like the inside of a shell.

Behind him, someone in the caravan gasped. Then another voice. Then the low murmur of thirty-one people discovering, in the same breathless instant, that the oldest constant in their lives had been quietly stolen.

"The stars," whispered Tally, the drover's girl, who was fourteen and had never once been quiet in her life. "Where are the stars?"

Nobody answered her. Nobody could.

Paloma stood at the head of the line with the hooded lantern trembling very slightly in his hand, and felt the cold comfort of his own competence curdle in his chest like milk left out in a lightning storm. Because here was the truth he could not speak: the wards hadn't failed. Wards failed the way bridges fail — locally, expensively, with warning. This was different. This was a door being opened deliberately from the far side. And whatever had opened it was now pouring through a gap the size of a kingdom's entire eastern border.

*Run,* said Maren's voice in his memory, calm as ever. *You don't ask what's fraying it.*

* * *

"We turn back." Doss's voice cut through the murmur, flat and hard as a shovel striking root. "Back to the lowland camp. We wait out whatever this is."

"And lose the crossing season?" That was Vetch, the quartermaster, whose voice always sounded slightly offended. "The salt alone—"

"The *stars*, Vetch."

"I can see what happened to the stars. What I also see is nine wagons of goods worth more than everyone here will earn in ten years, sitting on the wrong side of a pass we spent a season bribing our way toward—"

"They're not bribing their way anywhere," Doss said. "Look at him." And Paloma felt every eye in the caravan swing to him, thirty-one pairs of eyes catching even that sliver of hooded lantern-light, and understood that his silence had been read — correctly — as knowledge. "Path-finder. You've got that look. The look you get when you already know how bad it is and you're deciding how much of it to tell us."

There it was. The old accusation, wearing its newest coat. Three years ago he had stood in a tavern yard in Harrowmere with two of their crew already condemned, and he had said nothing — had slipped away in the night rather than spend his own neck vouching for theirs — and the crew had named him for it ever since: *the ghost*, the one who kept himself safe while others burned. He had told himself then, as he told himself now, that survival was not betrayal. That a man who moved unseen through the world owed the world nothing except the courtesy of remaining unseen.

But the sky was empty, and the wards were dead, and the courtesy of invisibility suddenly felt less like wisdom than like a kind of rot.

"The wards are gone," Paloma said. "All of them, along the eastern reach. Whatever broke them didn't break them by accident. And the stars going out isn't weather, and it isn't omen. It's a symptom." He paused, hearing how it sounded — how much it revealed. "I don't know what of."

"A symptom," Doss repeated, savoring it. "And how does a smuggler's boy come to know what a ward sounds like when it dies?"

"Doss—"

"No, I'm asking. Genuinely. Because I've carried you three years on the strength of your ears and your maps, and tonight your ears are telling you something that makes you shake, and I want to know what else those ears have been keeping from us."

The mule shifted and groaned. Somewhere behind them, Tally started crying, quietly, the way children cry when they understand that the adults have stopped pretending.

And Paloma — standing in the road with an empty heaven over his head — made the choice that he would spend the rest of this story paying for. He chose the old reflex. He chose the ghost's arithmetic: *one set of tracks is harder to follow than thirty-two.* He chose self-preservation, which had kept him alive through famine and fever and the crown's gallows-lists, and which had never once, in eleven years, asked him whether living was the same as deserving to.

"You're right to be afraid," he said. "So am I. I'm going ahead to scout the col. Stay with the wagons. Keep the lights hooded. If I'm not back by dawn—"

"You're leaving." Doss said it without inflection, which was worse than shouting. "Of course you are. Of course. The ghost walks."

Paloma unslung his pack and took from it nothing, because everything he owned was already on his back — that was the whole art of his life, owning nothing that couldn't be carried at a dead run. He looked down the line of faces one last time: Vetch's offended scowl, Tally's wet cheeks, the drover with his arm around his shaking wife. Thirty-one people who had trusted his ears with their lives because they had nothing else to trust.

He turned north, toward the col, toward the lowlands beyond, toward the cottage he had sworn never to see again — and he ran.

* * *

He ran the way he had taught himself to run over eleven years of practice: silently, economically, with the map of Thornfell unfolding behind his eyes like a second pair of eyelids. Left fork at the split boulder. Down through the fern-hollow where the stream ran cold enough to kill scent. Up the goat-track that no crown surveyor had ever charted because the goats had shown it only to him. The smuggler's paths, the ones he knew the way a priest knows psalms — every hidden switchback, every hollow under every fallen tree, the whole secret anatomy of a mountain that the law believed it owned.

The empty sky came with him. Between the pine crowns, wherever the canopy broke, that grey sheen stared down, and each time he saw it his legs pumped faster, as though speed itself were a prayer.

He told himself he was running to warn someone. That was the story he assembled as he climbed, the lie he built plank by plank in the workshop of his own head: *I'm running to warn Maren.* It was even partly true. Maren's cottage lay two days' hard travel south and east of here, down in the foothills where the wards ran thinner, and if the blight was eating outward from the broken border, hers would be among the first doors it reached.

But underneath that story, in the cellar of himself where he kept the things he didn't look at directly, another truth sat patiently waiting for him, as it always waited: *you are running because running is what you are for.* If he warned the caravan, he became tethered to it — responsible for it, accountable to it, visible within it. If he stayed, he would have to be a person among persons, spending himself on people who had every reason to hate him. But if he ran alone, he remained the ghost. Unclaimed. Unowed. Free.

The cold comfort of it wrapped around him even now, even with the sky wiped blank. He hated himself for the comfort and took it anyway. That was the trick of self-preservation, he was learning, at the top of a mountain under a murdered heaven: it always paid out. It paid out warm and immediate, like a stolen coin, and it never sent a bill. The bill arrived years later, in other currencies, in other chapters of a man's life.

By the fourth hour he had crossed the high col and begun the long descent into the southern foothills, and it was here — lungs burning, boots slick with frost — that he felt the second thing break.

Far off to the south-east, at the very edge of his hearing's range, another note of the great ward-song died. Then another, closer. Then a third. Each snap was smaller than the first — lesser wards, farmstead wards, the humble household charms that hedge-witches like Maren wove around wells and doorsteps and sleeping children — and each death sent a little shudder through the dark air, like the last tremor of a snapped lute-string.

They were falling inward. The hunger was eating exactly as Maren had said it would: outward from the breach, then inward toward the hearths. Toward cottages. Toward gardens where old women knelt in the dirt testing stones against their ears.

Paloma stopped on the goat-track, chest heaving, and looked south toward the lowlands, and for the first time that night he felt not fear for himself but dread — dread proper, dread in its full liturgical dress, the kind that has a body count attached.

Maren had taken him in when he was eleven and nameless, a feral thing pulled half-drowned from the mill race, and had fed him and taught him and never once asked him for the one thing he refused to give: his true name. She had let him be a ghost under her own roof. And now her wards were snapping one by one in the dark, and the last person who had ever sheltered him without conditions was alone behind a dying fence, and he was running — yes — but he had run first. He had chosen the empty road over the full caravan. He had spent his courage on altitude and distance instead of on people, and no amount of speed now could purchase that choice back.

The grey sky gave him nothing back. No star, no moon, no forgiveness.

Somewhere below and behind him, faint with distance, he thought he heard the caravan — a mule's complaint, a voice calling a name into the dark, maybe Tally's, maybe no one's. He did not stop. That was the final shape of the night's arithmetic, and he carried it down the mountain like a stone sewn into his coat: *thirty-one souls behind me, and I did not warn them. Whatever comes for them comes for people who never heard the harp-string break.*

He ran on into the starless south, a ghost on a ghost-road, telling himself the only story left that still held together: *I'm going to save her.* And beneath it, in the cellar, quieter and colder, the truth waited its turn:

*Then why are you counting the miles to the border instead of the miles to her door?*

The last thing Paloma heard before the pines closed over the track and the world went wholly dark was the third farmhouse ward, miles east, snapping like a cut harp string — and then, drifting up from the direction of the Weeping Wood, a sound he had never heard before and would spend the rest of this story trying to forget.

It sounded almost like weeping.

It sounded almost like his name.
