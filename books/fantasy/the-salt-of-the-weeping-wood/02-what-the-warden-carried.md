## 2. What the Warden Carried

The wards died at dusk, and Ilsa Vane felt it the way a woman feels a tooth go quiet — not relief, but the certain knowledge that something had rotted through.

She had been riding the border road since morning, following the spine of hills where the crown's oldest charms were knotted into standing stones, and she had felt each of them in her chest like a second heartbeat. Wardens were sworn to the wards before they were sworn to anything else. Before the king, even. The old instructors at the Vigil House said it plainly enough to make the new recruits flinch: *A warden serves the realm, but the realm is only as real as its borders. Kill the border and you kill everything inside it.* So when the last stone went dark — a sensation like a harp string snapping somewhere behind her sternum — Ilsa reined her mare to a halt on the frozen road and sat very still, listening to the silence where nine hundred years of humming had been.

Then she looked up, and saw that the stars were gone.

Not clouded. Not veiled by weather or woodsmoke. *Gone*, as if someone had reached into the sky with a wet cloth and wiped it clean, leaving only a black so total it seemed less like night than like a hole cut in the world. Her mare shuddered beneath her and blew out a breath that steamed white and then, strangely, faintly gray, as though the cold itself had been diluted.

Ilsa did what wardens are trained to do when the impossible presents itself for inspection: she dismounted, checked her kit, and wrote down the time.

*Eleventh hour, first frost-month. All Thornfell wards silent. Sky empty. Proceeding.*

It was the proceeding that mattered. Anyone could write down the end of the world. It took a warden to keep walking through it.

She crossed the border line at the old ford, where the river ran shallow over stones that had once been carved with binding-script, and the moment her boot touched the far bank she understood that she had left the kingdom behind without ever leaving the ground. The air tasted different here — thinner, mineral, faintly of the sea, which was absurd, because the sea was four hundred miles east. It tasted the way the inside of a shell smells. It tasted of salt.

Her writ was folded against her heart, triple-sealed in green wax. *By order of the Crown: seize one Paloma Okonkwo, apprentice hedge-witch, wanted for desertion of bond, theft of charm-craft, and complicity in the death of two crown informants.* She had read it so many times she could recite it in the dark, and lately she had been doing exactly that, reciting it in her bunk like a prayer, because there was a name missing from it, a name that lived underneath all the others like a root under a floorstone, and that name was Tam.

Her brother Tam had written letters from the garrison at Harrowmere, cheerful, badly spelled letters full of gossip about the other soldiers and complaints about the food. The last one had mentioned a smuggler's boy the locals trusted more than they trusted the wardens — a boy who could mend a failing charm faster than a guild-witch, who knew every goat path through the Thornfells, who went by no name anyone could hold onto. *They say he's got no true name at all,* Tam had written. *Like a ghost that decided to be useful.*

Three weeks after that letter, Tam's patrol had gone into the mountains chasing the same smuggler ring, and Tam had come back alone, fevered, raving about white trees and water that wept, and he had died in the garrison infirmary with his hands crusted in something that looked like frost but wasn't. In his effects they'd found an unsent letter, still folded, addressed to Ilsa. She carried it now in the inner pocket of her coat, over her heart beside the writ, and she had not opened it since the night the surgeon handed it to her, because the one line she'd read before her eyes failed her — *Ilsa, if you get this, don't trust the quiet ones* — was enough. More than enough. Some letters you carry sealed, the way you carry a wound closed, knowing that opening it will bleed you.

So: duty above, grief beneath, and between them a boy with no name, running loose through mountains whose defenses had just died. The arithmetic was simple. The arithmetic was always simple. It was only ever the carrying that was hard.

* * *

She found the caravan at moonrise — or rather, where the moon should have risen; the sky stayed blank as slate — in a fold of the hills below the Widow's Tooth, camped in a hollow that any smuggler would have chosen and any warden would have missed. She almost missed it herself. What caught her eye was the fire: still burning, unattended, burning low and blue and giving off no heat she could feel until she was nearly upon it.

The caravan was six wagons, drawn up in a defensive circle, laden with contraband she didn't bother to catalog — brandy, bolt-cloth, unlicensed charms still wrapped in their wax paper. The draft animals stood in their traces, motionless, heads drooping, and when Ilsa approached the nearest one and laid her hand on its neck, the hide came away dusted white, and her palm stung as if she'd gripped a nettle.

The people were worse.

There were eleven of them, men and women, a girl of perhaps twelve curled against a woman Ilsa took to be her mother. They were not dead. That was the thing she kept returning to, turning over like a stone with something alive under it: they were *not dead*. They breathed, slow as tide. Their hearts beat, if you pressed two fingers to the throat — and Ilsa did press, on all of them, one after another, because some part of her refused to believe what her eyes were reporting. But their skin had gone stiff and faintly crystalline, glittering at the edges like sugar-glaze, and when she held her lantern close she saw fine white tears of salt dried on every face, tracked from the corners of the eyes down over the cheeks, as if each of them had wept themselves partway to mineral.

Their eyes were open. All of them. Open and fixed on nothing, or rather fixed on the space just in front of their own faces, as if each person were reading something written an inch from their nose, something invisible, something endlessly long.

Ilsa walked the circle twice. She spoke aloud — her own name, rank, and warrant, because procedure was a plank you clung to when the flood came — and none of them turned toward her voice. She touched the girl's shoulder and the shoulder was cold and hard as cured cheese. She tried to lift one man's hand and it resisted, gently, the way deep water resists, and when she finally raised it, grains of salt sifted from between his fingers and hissed faintly on the coals of the blue fire.

At the center of the circle, someone had built a small cairn of stones, and on top of it, weighted down with a horseshoe against the wind, lay a page torn from a ledger. Ilsa picked it up. The handwriting changed halfway down the page — began as a tally of goods, then slurred, then stopped being words at all, becoming instead a single phrase repeated over and over in a hand that grew shakier with each line:

*she is kind she is kind she is kind she is kind*

Beneath the last repetition, in fresher ink, someone else had written: *gone to look. if we're still here when you find us, we were never anywhere at all.*

Ilsa stood holding the page for a long time, hearing her own breath, feeling the wrongness of the air press against her like deep water. The stars were gone. The wards were dead. Eleven people stood sleeping upright in salt, dreaming whatever the salt dreamed, and somewhere in these mountains was a boy who mended charms and owed debts, and somewhere beyond him was the thing that had done this.

Here was the moment, she would think later, where the fault line opened — the moment when duty, which had always fit her like her own coat, shifted a half-inch out of true and became something sharper and less comfortable. Because procedure said: secure the site, mark the position, ride for the garrison at Harrowmere, report, wait for orders, return with a full company and a licensed witch of the Second Circle. Procedure said the eleven sleepers were evidence. Procedure said her heart was not relevant to the mission.

But procedure assumed the garrison at Harrowmere was still a garrison. Procedure assumed there was still a Second Circle to license anybody. Procedure assumed the sky would have stars in it tomorrow.

Ilsa folded the ledger page into her pocket beside the writ and the letter, three papers layered over her heart like armor made of ink, and made her choice in the space of one breath, the way all real choices are made.

She would follow the trail herself.

* * *

The trail was easy, which frightened her more than the sleepers had. Smugglers survived by not leaving trails; this crew had simply stopped trying, their wheel-ruts wandering off north-northwest in a line too straight for comfort, as if the drivers had steered by something other than roads or stars. And interleaved with those ruts, half a mile on, she found footprints. One set. Light, quick, the stride of someone young and used to broken country, cutting away from the caravan's path at a dead run and then settling, after a mile or so, into something steadier — southwest, toward the high passes, toward the old hedge-witch's cottage in the Weeping Wood's shadow.

Toward home, if the intelligence files were honest, which they mostly weren't. *Paloma Okonkwo, apprenticed to one Maren of the Thornfell edge, hedge-craft, unlicensed. Absconded [date illegible]. Known associate of the Doss smuggling outfit. No known true name. Do not rely on standard charm-detention protocols; subject has demonstrated ability to unravel them.*

She remembered him. That was the trouble. Two years ago — no, closer to three — a patrol charm had frayed in a storm on the high pass, the kind of fraying that leaves six wardens blind and mute to everything within a hundred yards, and a thin youth in a patched coat had appeared out of the rain as if he'd been waiting for the opportunity, and had knelt in the mud and re-tied the knot-work with fingers quick as a pickpocket's. He'd asked for nothing. He'd taken a heel of bread and a cup of the garrison's terrible tea and vanished before the sergeant finished deciding whether to arrest him. Ilsa had argued for letting him go. She remembered arguing. She remembered thinking: *the wards hold because people mend them, and here is someone who mends them for free.* She remembered his eyes, gray as the underside of slate, watching her while his hands worked — not wary, exactly. Measuring. Deciding something about her that she never learned.

Now his file sat in her saddlebag and his warrant sat over her heart and his footprints led her up into mountains where the sky had been wiped clean, and Ilsa Vane, who had never in eleven years of service bent a regulation past its breaking point, found herself bending them all at once and telling herself it was necessity.

Necessity. Or the other thing. The thing with Tam's handwriting on it.

She camped that night in the lee of a dead waystone, one of the ward-stones, its script dark as burned-out embers. She built no fire. She ate cold sausage and harder bread and took out the three papers one by one, arranging them across her knee in the starless dark: the writ, the ledger page with its litany of kindness, the letter she had never finished reading.

*Ilsa, if you get this, don't trust the quiet ones.*

Don't trust the quiet ones. The mountains were very quiet. The eleven sleepers had been very quiet. Even the wind had gone quiet, as if the whole world were holding its breath around her, listening for her answer.

"I'm coming," she said aloud, to the letter, to the mountain, to whoever was listening. "Whoever you are. Whatever did this. I'm coming, and I am bringing the law with me."

It sounded, in the dead air, less like a threat and more like a plea. She put the papers away and slept in snatches, her hand on her sword hilt, dreaming of white trees and a boy with slate-gray eyes tying knots in the rain.

* * *

Two days later, on the third morning, she smelled the cottage before she saw it — salt again, thick enough to taste, rolling down the hillside like fog off a winter sea. The Maren place sat in a fold of land below the tree line, stone walls, turf roof, herb beds gone to frost. Smoke should have risen from it; no smoke rose. The door stood open a hand's width, and from the gap seeped that mineral sweetness, and something else beneath it, something that made the hair rise along Ilsa's arms: a sound. Faint. Rhythmic. Almost like whispering.

She drew her sword — useless, she suspected, against whatever waited, but the weight of it was a comfort older than sense — and crossed the yard, boots crunching on a crust of white that rimmed the doorstep like frost, like lace, like the dried tears of something enormous.

Inside the doorway, piled against the threshold like snow drifted against a fence, lay a crust of salt two fingers deep. And as the dawn wind stirred it, lifting fine grains into the grey light, Ilsa heard what the whispering was.

Names. Hundreds of names, murmured on no breath at all, rising from the salt itself — names of villagers, names of soldiers, names she half-knew from muster rolls and gravestones — each one spoken once, softly, lovingly, and then let go, the way you let go of a rope.

And threaded among them, recurring, returning again and again like a refrain, one name spoken with particular tenderness, particular hunger, over and over:

*Paloma. Paloma. Paloma.*

Ilsa Vane stood on the salted threshold of a dead woman's house, with a warrant for that name over her heart, and understood, with the cold certainty of a woman stepping off a ledge, that she was no longer the hunter in this story.

She was the next name on the list.
