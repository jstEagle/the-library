## 3. Salt on the Doorstep

Paloma had told himself he wasn't going back.

He had said it aloud, in fact, somewhere on the second night of running — a hoarse whisper to no one, to the dark, to the hole in the sky where the stars should have been. *Not going back. Not to Maren's. They'll watch the places you love.* It was the first rule of invisibility, older than any smuggler's code: never return to what you've abandoned, because abandonment leaves a shape in the world, and shapes can be found.

But his feet had other opinions. Feet were honest that way. Three days of descending through Thornfell's grey shoulders, past the cairns and the hanging pines and the streams that ran too quiet, and they had carried him down out of the mountains along paths so worn into him he could have walked them blindfolded, drugged, or dead — the switchback above Miller's Drop where the rock held boot-holds like cupped hands, the sheep-track that threaded between two leaning stones the shepherds called the Old Couple, the ford at Coldbrook where you crossed on the upstream side of the willow because the current lied downstream. His feet knew these things the way a tongue knows its own teeth.

And now here he stood, at the top of the last rise, looking down into the little fold of valley where the cottage sat, and all his fine resolutions lay around him like dropped coins.

The smokehouse chimney was cold. That was the first wrongness, and it went through him like a fishhook. Maren's chimney never slept. In six years of apprenticeship — before he'd run, before the night he'd climbed out the loft window with everything he owned in one satchel and his true name folded up inside himself like a knife wrapped in cloth — he could not remember once waking to a cold chimney. She banked her fire the way other women banked grudges: carefully, and for use later. Smoke meant Maren was alive, thinking, working. No smoke meant the world had come off its hinges.

The second wrongness took longer to name, because it was made of light. The morning sun came over the eastern ridge and struck the cottage door, and instead of the warm brown of old oak it flashed white. Blindingly, wetly white, as though the door had been lacquered with frost in high summer.

He went down the hill slowly. There was no point hurrying toward a thing that might kill you; hurrying was how you met it unprepared. He kept to the hedgerow, an old habit, hand brushing the blackthorn, and the blackthorn felt wrong under his fingers too — dry, brittle, crusted. When he lifted his hand he saw a fine powder on his palm, glittering faintly, tasting of nothing when he touched it to his tongue except of *absence*, which is a taste I hope you never learn.

Salt.

Not sea salt. Not the coarse grey crystals Maren bought from the salters' carts every autumn and kept in stoneware jars for warding circles and curing meat. This was finer than flour, brighter than snow, and it clung to everything the way grief clings — not sitting on surfaces but grown into them, as if the hedge itself had wept it from some wound deep in the wood.

*The stars going out,* he thought. *And this.* Two wounds in one night, three days apart by road. He was no hedge-witch, whatever else he was, but even a smuggler knew that when two strange things happen close together, they are usually the same thing wearing different clothes.

The garden gate hung open. He stopped before it, remembering — absurdly, unbearably — that he had left through this same gate in the dark three years ago, and had promised himself he would never see it again, and had also, in some cellar of his heart he refused to visit, imagined that when he did see it again it would be because Maren stood in the doorway scolding him for coming home with frostbite. He had rehearsed her anger more times than he would ever admit. Her anger was a home he could have lived in.

The gate swung at his touch without a sound. The hinge didn't creak. Nothing creaked anymore; the whole valley seemed oiled with silence.

Up close, the door was worse. The salt had crusted thick as candle-drippings down its face, building up in runs and rivulets, layer over layer, as if someone had wept there for years instead of days. And it was still moving. As he watched, a new bead swelled at the lintel's edge, gathered itself, and slid slowly down the grain of the wood, leaving a bright trail. The cottage was crying. Slowly, steadily, without pause, the way old people cry — the kind of crying that has given up on being comforted.

He put his ear near the door, because he was a fool and always had been.

It whispered.

Not wind in the eaves. Not settling timber. The salt itself whispered, a dry sifting sound that resolved, if you let your listening soften, into voices. Many voices, thin as paper, overlapping like pages turning. And every voice was saying names.

*"Aldous. Aldous. Aldous."* That one came from low on the door, a child's rhythm, sing-song, the way children say names to make them magic.

*"Brida of Harrowmere, Brida, Brida—"*

*"Maren Okonkwo—"* and Paloma flinched as if struck, because that was not right, that was backwards, that was *her* surname given to *him* — unless the salt did not care about such distinctions, unless it only wanted names, any names, gathering them the way a magpie gathers anything that shines.

He stepped back. His heel found the doorstep, the worn stone he had scrubbed a hundred times as a boy-apprentice while Maren stood over him explaining that a threshold was a promise, that a house's word lived in its step. The stone was furred white with salt now, and where his boot pressed it, the crystals crackled softly, and from directly beneath his foot a voice said, very clearly, very patiently, as if it had been saying it to the stone for years:

*"Paloma."*

He was off the step and three paces into the yard before he knew he'd moved, heart slamming, hand on the knife he'd bought from a fence in Thornmouth and never once used for anything but cutting rope. He stood in the middle of the dooryard breathing like a landed trout, and the cottage went on weeping, and the salt went on whispering its harvest of names, and none of them, after that first one, was his.

*Maren,* he thought. *Where is Maren.*

The thought arrived without permission and immediately began to hurt.

* * *

The door was not locked. Of course it wasn't locked. Maren had never locked her door in her life — "Locks keep out the poor and the honest," she used to say, "and invite in everyone worth meeting" — and Paloma pushed it open with two fingertips and a rising dread, and the smell hit him before the sight did.

Brine and ash. Old smoke and the sea, though the sea was forty leagues west. Underneath both, faint as memory: dried yarrow, tansy, the camphor chest where she kept the winter herbs. Home-smells. His chest did something complicated and painful about them, and he made himself breathe through it, because a man who cannot breathe cannot think, and a man who cannot think dies in interesting times.

Inside, the cottage had been searched.

Not ransacked — ransacking was violence, and this had been thoroughness. Every drawer opened and closed properly. Every jar re-set on its shelf, though several were empty, their contents gone. Her bundles of dried herbs hung in their usual rows from the crossbeam, but half of them had been taken, snipped neatly at the strings. The great grimoire lay open on the worktable — open, which Maren would as soon do as leave her teeth in a bowl overnight — its pages weighted with river stones, and someone had turned through it and left it at a page concerning wards of concealment.

Someone had come here looking for knowledge of hiding. And Maren had either given it or been made to give it.

Or had left it herself, said a small voice, and left before they arrived.

On the hearth, the ashes were days old and cold as charity. Beside the hearth, in her chair — the willow chair with the rush seat she'd woven herself the summer he turned fourteen, complaining the whole time that he wove rushes like a drunk spider — sat a figure, and Paloma's heart stopped clean between beats before his eyes sorted shadow from substance and told him: not a person. A person-shaped absence. A mound of her shawls and cloaks arranged just so, sleeves draped over the arms, as if she'd meant to be sitting there and simply hadn't arrived yet. Waiting. The way the whole valley seemed to be waiting.

He checked the sleeping loft anyway. Empty bed, blanket folded — folded, not thrown, deliberate, a message in cloth. Maren had not been dragged anywhere. Maren had *gone*, and gone in order, and had left her cottage behind her like a letter addressed to whoever came next.

To him. Because who else? Who else in the world knew that the third floorboard from the hearth was loose?

His hands were shaking when he knelt and pried it up. Underneath, in the shallow cavity that smelled of earth and old lavender, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp: papers. A slim bundle of them, tied with the green thread she used for binding charms. He took them to the worktable, into the grey light from the window, and untied the knot with fingers that refused to steady.

Most of it was hedge-craft he already knew, or nearly: notes on the salt-wards along the southern marches, sketches of sigils, a recipe for a tanning salve he'd once ruined a good pair of boots testing. And then, fourth from the bottom, a single page in her sharp, slanted hand — but written fast, fast for Maren, the letters leaning like drunks, ink blotted in her hurry:

*I write this knowing who will read it, because only one person living knows where the third board sleeps, and I am sorry, child, for what that means you'll feel when you find it. Do not grieve long. Grief is what IT eats.*

*The blight is not weather. The blight is not war. The stars did not fall out; they were TAKEN, and the taking has a source. I traced the leylines through the autumn and every line bends, every blessed line bends toward the Weeping Wood like iron to a lodestone. Something old in the Wood has learned a new hunger. It feeds on sorrow — that much the oldest stories got right — but it has grown past feeding on what mourners bring it freely. Now it takes the mourning itself. Takes the grief OUT of the grieving, and leaves them hollow, salt-white, smiling. It ate Harrowmere's memories in a night. It has sent runners into the courts of men. Child, I believe it wears a crown already.*

*I am going in. Me, alone, because I am old and my griefs are used up and it will find little in me to drink — an old woman's regrets are mostly vinegar by now. But the counter-working needs things I do not have and cannot make. It needs a name freely surrendered, and I have none to give that matters. Mine is spent, common, worn smooth like a river pebble. It needs a HIDDEN name, a name kept secret so long it has gone sharp again.*

*I know what I am asking. I am asking you to follow me into the Weeping Wood, and I am asking you to forgive me for asking. Both are too much. Forgive me anyway. Or don't, and follow me regardless — you always did walk toward trouble like it owed you money.*

*Burn this after reading. Trust no warden. Trust no crown. And child — the salt whispers names. Never tell it yours.*

Paloma read it twice. Then a third time, because his mind kept sliding off the middle of it, refusing the weight, the way a horse refuses a fence.

Then he became aware that he was weeping, and that the tears fell on the page, and that where they struck, the ink did not blur but *bloomed* — each drop spreading into a small white ring, like frost, like salt, like the door outside. He wiped his face furiously with his sleeve and looked at the page and understood, with a lurch, that the cottage had heard him. That it had been listening the whole time. That grief was what IT ate, and he had just set a meal on the table.

The whispering beneath the floorboards changed. It had been a tide of strangers' names; now it gathered, focused, drew itself together like a crowd turning to look at one man, and from everywhere at once — the boards, the walls, the salt-crusted window glass, the cold chimney — it said, soft and pleased and patient:

*"Paaa-lomaaaa."*

He ran. No shame in it; he'd have despised anyone who didn't. He snatched the page and the bundle, kicked the third floorboard loosely back into place out of some superstitious tidiness, and went out through the crying doorway into the yard with his lungs full of knives. Behind him the whisper followed to the threshold and stopped there, precisely there, at the edge of the worn stone — because thresholds are promises, Maren had taught him that, and whatever wore the cottage now was bound to keep the house's word even while it broke every other law of heaven.

He did not stop running until the valley was behind him and the land rose into pine and shadow, and even then he walked fast, faster than sense allowed, until the light failed.

He camped badly that night, in a hollow under a granite shelf, no fire because fire draws eyes and his name apparently traveled without one. He sat with his knees drawn up and the stolen page against his knee and tried to think and could not stop feeling.

Because here was the arithmetic he couldn't escape: Maren had known. She had known what was coming, had traced it, named it, gone to fight it — and where was her apprentice? Three years gone. Three years of smuggler roads and false names and telling himself the lie he had polished smooth as sea-glass: *I left to become someone. I left because she wanted to bind me to the village, to the hedge-craft, to a small life.* And the truth, which the drowned dead of his own conscience now dragged up dripping: he had left because she had begun to speak of teaching him the deep craft, the name-craft, the workings that require a true name spoken aloud — and he had a name he could not bear to give. Had fled rather than surrender it. Had chosen the ghost-life, the smuggler-life, the life of aliases and averted eyes, over being *known*.

And she had stayed. She had stayed and fought with her spent, common, worn-smooth name, alone, and now she was in the Weeping Wood, or dead, or dissolved into the salt that whispered on her own front door — and the last words she'd ever aimed at him were an apology for asking too much.

"I'm coming," he said to the dark, and his voice cracked in the middle like a boy's. "Do you hear me? I'm coming, you stubborn old woman. Just — stay alive. Stay *you*. Whatever it costs."

The dark did not answer. But far off, down in the valley, he thought he heard the cottage weeping on, patient as rain, whispering its hoard of names into the empty air —

— and among the pines behind him, closer than kindness, something answered. Not in words. In a sound he knew from his childhood, from the herb-loft, from better years: the particular dry click of Maren's knitting needles, keeping time, keeping count.

Click. Click. Click. Coming down the mountain after him through the trees, unhurried as fate.

* * *

He did not sleep again. When the sky went the color of dirty pewter he packed, checked the page was safe against his chest, and stood a moment at the mouth of the hollow, listening.

No needles. Only wind, and the enormous silence where stars should be.

He took out Maren's page once more, and read the lines he had read a dozen times in the night, the ones his eye kept snagging on like a nail in cloth: *every blessed line bends toward the Weeping Wood. It needs a hidden name, freely surrendered.*

Below those words, in the margin, cramped sideways in the same hurried hand, almost an afterthought — or not an afterthought at all, but the sentence she had saved for last, the hook set deepest:

*If the salt has already tasted your name, child, then it knows the road you must take better than you do. Follow the maps. And trust the warden.*

Paloma read it three times, and laughed — one short, ugly note with no humor in it, swallowed by the starless dark.

Trust the warden. Trust the woman whose writ had his description on it, whose hounds had chased him through two counties, whose duty it was to clap him in irons and march him to a hanging judge. Maren might as well have written *trust the wolf to shepherd the lamb.*

But she had never been wrong about anything that mattered. And she had gone into the Wood believing someone would need to follow her — believing *he* would — and she had left this page like a lamp in a window, and lamps in windows are instructions whether you like them or not.

He folded the page away and turned east, toward the salt road, toward the lowlands, toward the Weeping Wood lying somewhere beyond the horizon like a bruise on the world's skin.

Behind him, faint and fading, the needles clicked once more — and stopped.
