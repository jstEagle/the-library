## 4. The Price of Old Debts

The ravine had no name on any map Paloma trusted, which was why he had chosen it, three years ago, as the place where he would stop running and start hiding. It was a seam of grey stone split down the middle of Thornfell's shoulder, narrow enough at the top that the sky became a ribbon, deep enough at the bottom that snow never fully melted even in high summer. The meltwater ran cold and mineral-bright over slick shale, and it made a sound like someone whispering through their teeth — a sound he had once found comforting, because it covered footsteps.

He did not find it comforting now.

Now it sounded like an audience waiting for him to speak.

He heard them before he saw them, the way he always did — not boots but breathing, the particular hush of people trying not to breathe loudly, which is its own kind of noise if you've spent your life listening for it. Six of them, maybe seven. He counted the echoes off the ravine walls and came up with six and a half, which was a joke his old master Doss would have appreciated, and the thought of Doss appreciating anything tonight turned Paloma's stomach over like a boat in weather.

There was no way out. He had walked into the ravine because it was the fastest route south toward Maren's cottage, and because three years of safety had made him stupid with comfort. The walls rose sheer on either side for forty feet, then in shelves, then in scree you could climb only slowly and loudly. Ahead, the ravine bent east around a spur of rock. Behind him, the light was failing, and the stars were still gone.

That was the thing he could not stop thinking about, even now, even with death walking toward him in the dark. Two nights since the sky had emptied itself, and no one else seemed to be screaming about it. The smugglers in the caravan had grumbled about bad omens and then argued about card debts. The wardens' beacon-fires still burned on their peaks, patient as ever. As if the whole world had agreed to pretend the heavens were merely closed for repairs.

Paloma knew better. He had felt the wards snap. He had felt them *die* — felt the border's long harp-song cut short mid-note, felt something come pouring through the wound in the world like brine through a cracked hull. And every instinct he owned, every instinct Maren had sharpened in him before he'd thrown her kindness back in her face and run, told him the same thing: go home. Go to the cottage. Find out what she knew.

But first he had to live through the next quarter hour.

They let him see them all at once, which was courtesy or cruelty depending on how you took it. Shapes detaching from the shadows of the ravine walls — from ledges he would have sworn were empty when he passed beneath them. Lanterns unshuttered one by one, shuttered tin throwing hard yellow coins of light onto wet stone. Six men. He knew five of their faces. That was worse than strangers.

"Evening, ghost," said Doss.

* * *

Doss had aged wrong. Not old — Doss was perhaps fifty, and fifty sat on some men like a good coat — but *wrong*, the way a house ages after a fire, everything structurally sound and nothing untouched by smoke. His beard had gone salt-white at the chin in patches, as though grief had bleached it unevenly. He stood with the lantern held out from his body like a man who had learned to keep his hands free, and his eyes went over Paloma the way a butcher goes over a carcass: measuring, pricing, finding the cuts.

The other five spread along the ravine without being told. Paloma noted it professionally, the way he noted everything, because noting things was the skill that had kept him alive this long. Ferro on the left, big through the shoulders, a climbing axe slung across his back. The Vetch twins on the right, moving with the eerie synchrony of brothers who had shared every crime since they were nine. A young one he didn't know at the rear, holding the rope coil, face still soft enough to show unease. And leaning against the ravine wall with her arms crossed, half in shadow —

"Sef's sister," Paloma said quietly.

"Marta," said the woman, and her voice was flat as slate. "You remember my name. That's a mercy. You didn't remember it when the wardens were asking theirs."

Paloma's mouth had gone dry. "Marta."

"Don't." She pushed off the wall. "Don't say my name back like we're friends at market. You want to use names, use Brack's. Use Sef's. They can't hear you say them anymore, thanks to you."

"They hanged them," Doss said. He said it simply, without heat, which somehow made it worse — heat could be answered, argued with, survived. This was just fact, laid on the wet stone between them like a corpse. "Spring before last. Gallows at Cael Ford. Brack went quiet, like he always did. Sef sang. I expect you'd have found that funny."

"I didn't—" Paloma stopped. Every sentence available to him was a lie, and lying to Doss was like lying to a dog about the hand that beat it; the animal didn't parse the words, only the flinch behind them. He tried again. "I gave them nothing. I never gave the wardens anything. I ran, Doss. That's all I did. I ran, and they took two of you to make the ledger balance, because someone had to hang for the Salt Road job whether I was caught or not."

"Someone had to hang," Marta repeated. "Hear how easy that comes off you? *Someone.* Like they were weather. Like they were toll."

"The ledger balances," Paloma said. His own voice sounded far away to him, thin against the whispering water. "I know how it works. I lived inside it for eleven years. But I didn't tip it."

"No," Doss agreed. "You *ran* from it. And there's the difference, lad, and there's the crime. Brack had a wife in Lowmarsh. Sef had a mother. You had nobody, so you got to be the one who vanished, and the rest of us got to stand in a courtroom and watch ropes do arithmetic." He set the lantern down on a shelf of rock, deliberately, the way a man sets down a cup before a negotiation. "I'm not here in anger. I want you to understand that. Anger burns off. What I've got is colder than that. I've got accounting."

The water whispered. Somewhere above, along the ribbon of sky, cloud moved where stars should have been and wasn't.

"You've had three years to find me," Paloma said. "Why now?"

Doss smiled, and the smile had salt-bleached teeth in it. "Because until two nights ago, you weren't worth finding. A runaway apprentice who mends charms and knows goat-paths — the crown wants you for desertion and theft, small coin, small rope. But two nights ago the wards died, boy. All of them. Every stone from Thornfell to the sea, silent as snuffed candles, and do you know what the crown is paying now? For anyone who can read the borders?" He shook his head slowly. "Wardens are offering pardons. Smugglers are offering fortunes. And every crew between here and Cael Ford has remembered that *you* were the one who knew the Thornfell paths best of anyone alive — including the ones that don't appear on any map. Including ours."

Understanding arrived in Paloma's chest like cold water finding a crack.

"You want my routes."

"I want what's owed," Doss said. "You cost me two men and the best year of work my crew ever had. You'll pay in the only coin you've got. Every path. Every pass. Every hollow and hideout you've been sitting on since you learned them at Maren Okonkwo's knee —" he said the name gently, mockingly, "— every secret, spoken aloud, mapped for my crew, before you walk out of this ravine. Or before you're carried out. Either way, the knowledge leaves you. Only the manner's negotiable."

* * *

They put him against the ravine wall with his hands visible and let him think, which was the cruelest part, because thinking was supposed to be the thing he was good at.

The lanterns made the wet stone gold and the shadows black, and in between, in the lamplight's trembling margin, Paloma looked at the faces of men he had eaten beside, frozen beside, bled beside, and searched himself for the thing they wanted him to feel. Guilt was there — he was honest enough with himself to know that, honest enough to know it was the truest thing about him. He had run. On the worst night of the crew's life, with the warden nets closing over the Salt Road cache, he had done the arithmetic of one life against six and slipped away alone into the fog, telling himself that a dead hero saved nobody, that survival was its own kind of loyalty, that he would come back for them someday when the heat died down.

The heat had never died down. And Brack and Sef had hung for the silence he left behind him.

But guilt was not the same as agreement, and here was the problem, the problem he turned over while the water whispered and the Vetch twins watched him with identical flat eyes:

His knowledge was all he was.

He had built himself out of secrets the way other men built houses. His name — his real name, the name his mother had breathed over him, the name that Maren had warned him never to speak aloud where anything might be listening — he had buried so deep even he barely dared look at it. In its place he had constructed Paloma-of-the-paths, Paloma-who-knew-the-way-through, a man made entirely of hidden roads. Take the roads away and there was nothing left standing. Just the buried name, and the shame, and the salt-smell of a night two years gone when the stars had gone out and he had run again, because running was the only prayer he knew.

And yet.

And yet Doss was right about the wards. Something had come through. Something that crusted doorways in weeping salt and emptied the sky. If the realm was going to need mapmakers of the hidden places in the days coming — and every hair on Paloma's arms said it was — then hoarding his knowledge was no longer survival. It was just another kind of running.

Besides. There were ways to pay a debt and still keep the purse strings.

"All right," Paloma said.

The word dropped into the ravine like a stone into a well. Even the water seemed to listen.

Doss tilted his head. "All right?"

"You want my paths. Fine. I'll give you one. The best one. The one worth more than all the others together, and worth more than my life, which is why I've kept it shut in my chest for eleven years like a swallowed coal." He pushed off the wall, slowly, hands still open and visible, and met the old smuggler's eyes. "The Shepherd's Cut. The pass through the spine of Thornfell that lets a laden mule-train cross the border in four hours instead of nine, under the wardens' noses, any season, any weather. No map has it. No warden believes it exists. Half the crews who've hunted for it have died looking."

Silence. The young one with the rope shifted. One of the Vetch twins licked his lips.

"That pass is a story," Doss said. "Old men tell it to keep apprentices hungry."

"It's a story the way I'm a ghost," Paloma said. "It's real, and it's standing in front of you." He held up one finger. "One condition. Not my freedom — take me to the edge of the lowlands and turn me loose, I don't care, I'm done with your road anyway. My condition is this: you hear it once. I speak it, I draw it in the dirt, your crew memorizes it, and then the drawing gets scuffed and the words stay in your heads. Because that's how I keep it — in my head — and a thing like the Cut written down on paper is a thing the wardens eventually find on a corpse. You want it safe, you keep it oral. Sworn. Which means each of you owes me silence on it till the day you die, and I know how your crew treats oaths, Doss. I learned oath-keeping from you."

Something moved behind the old man's salt-flecked eyes. Calculation, yes — but underneath it, faintly, almost unwelcome, something older. The ghost of the man who had taught a skinny apprentice to read starlight off wet granite and told him, drunk once on plum brandy, *knowledge is the only wealth that grows by giving it away, lad — provided you choose who you give it to.*

"And why," Doss said softly, "would you spend it? Your one great coin. Your life-price, spent buying your life, which means you end where you started — alive and broke. Why not fight? Why not run? The Vetches swear you can't outrun them in a ravine, but ravines have two ends, and you're the one who knows them."

"Because two nights ago the stars went out," Paloma said, "and I felt the wards die, and whatever killed them isn't going to care about passes or patrols or crews. Because Brack and Sef are dead either way, and this is the only payment they'll ever see from me. And because—" his voice caught, and he let it, because Doss could smell a performed emotion at fifty paces and might believe a real one, "—because I'm tired, Doss. Eleven years of being nothing but what I know. Maybe if I spend some of it, there'll be room in me for something else."

The water whispered. Marta stared at him with an expression he couldn't read and didn't dare try.

Doss crouched, slow, joints cracking, and drew a circle in the wet shale with one finger. Then he looked up. "Speak, then. And mind — if the Cut turns out to be a story after all, we'll come find you. We found you once."

"You found me because I let myself be found," Paloma said, and knelt opposite him. "Now listen. And swear first. All of you. On the crossing-coin and the open road."

They swore. Even Marta, last and grudging, her oath bitten off like gristle. And then Paloma opened his chest and paid.

He spoke the Shepherd's Cut aloud for the first time in his life — the entrance behind the split-face cliff above Harrowmere's old quarry, the chimney climb where you sent the mules up blindfolded and singing so the echo confused the wardens' listening-charms, the gallery where the mountain itself had been hollowed by water into a road wide enough for carts, the exit in the birch fold below Greywatch where a man could stand with one foot in Thornfell and one in the king's country and no ward on either side would whisper. He drew it in the dirt with a stick and made each of them recite it back until they had it perfect, and then he dragged his boot through the drawing and watched eleven years of his own hoarded self smear into mud and wash toward the drain of the ravine.

It hurt more than he expected. That surprised him. It hurt like pulling a tooth that had grown into the jawbone — relief and loss in the same motion, and a strange new whistling emptiness where the ache used to live.

When it was done, Doss stood, joints cracking again, and studied him for a long moment.

"You'll do," the old man said at last, which from Doss was nearly tenderness. "Ferro — give him bread and the heel of cheese. He walks south at first light." He paused, then added, quieter, so the others wouldn't carry it: "Where is it you're walking to, boy? South is the lowlands, and the lowlands are wardens and gallows and a queen's writ with your description on it."

"A cottage," Paloma said. "An old woman's cottage on the west slopes. She taught me the paths."

Doss's face changed. Not much. But the fire-light caught it, and Paloma saw the change arrive and understood it before the old man said a word.

"We passed that way two nights ago," Doss said slowly. "Following a rumor of you, as it happens. There's a cottage on the west slopes with its door crusted white, and the salt on it still wet, still *weeping*, and no tracks leaving. My lads wouldn't go near it. Neither would I, and I've robbed graves." He picked up his lantern. "If that's your old woman, ghost, she's either fled into the mountains barefoot in a snowstorm, or something in the Weeping Wood has started reaching out past its borders. And either way—"

He didn't finish. He didn't need to.

Paloma was already turning south, toward the cottage, toward the salt, toward the home he had fled and the woman whose forgiveness he had never asked for — walking faster now, breaking into a run, while behind him six sworn men stood silent in the dark of the ravine and the nameless water whispered on, like something counting the names of the forgotten.
