## 6. The Map That Lies

The cottage had a smell to it that grief has no name for but every abandoned house knows: cold ash, dried herbs gone bitter in their bundles, dust with the sweetness of long-sleeping beeswax underneath. Paloma stood on the threshold for a long time after the salt had stopped whispering his old master's name, and he made himself look at the room the way Maren had taught him to look at everything — *first what is there, then what is missing, then what was never yours to see.*

What was there: the hearth swept clean. The kettle hung from its iron hook as though someone meant to come back and fill it. The bed was made with hospital corners, which Maren had never once managed in her life; she'd believed a rumpled blanket kept bad dreams from settling.

What was missing: Maren.

And — he felt it before he understood it, the way you feel a tooth is loose before your tongue finds it — her maps.

He crossed the room in six strides. The floorboard under the third hearthstone lifted the way it always had, with a sigh of old wax, and the hollow beneath was empty except for a single folded square of oilcloth, placed dead center, as deliberately as a coin on a dead man's eyes.

His hands shook opening it.

Inside lay one map. One, out of the dozens Maren had drawn across forty years of hedge-craft and quiet treason — maps of the Thornfell passes, of the warden patrols' seasonal rhythms, of the herb-roads and the salt-roads and the roads that had no name because naming them would be the fastest way to get people hanged. He'd grown up tracing these lines by candlelight while she pretended not to notice, learning the mountains the way other children learned psalms. They were gone. Every one of them, gone, and this single sheet left behind like the last page of a letter.

It was a map of the southern reaches, the country below Thornfell's spine where the forest thickened toward the border of the Weeping Wood. And it was wrong.

Paloma knelt in the middle of the cottage floor and held the map flat between his palms, and told himself, very calmly, that his eyes were tired, that the light through the shuttered window was poor, that grief did strange things to the mind.

The road to Harrowmere bent the wrong way. He knew that road — had walked it at fourteen with a basket of Maren's poultices and a head full of songs — and it ran straight along the river until it reached the ford. Here it curved north, swung wide around hills that were drawn slightly too tall, and rejoined itself miles east of where any honest cartographer would have put it. The Salt Road, the great artery of smuggler trade that he himself knew blindfolded, had been redrawn too, nudged south by degrees so subtle that only someone who had carried cargo down it in the dark would ever feel the wrongness, the way you notice a picture hanging crooked without knowing why.

Every route. Every route led, eventually, with a drunkard's stubborn drift, toward the same place. Toward the western edge of the parchment where the ink grew sparse and the trees were marked in little clustered strokes like scars, and beneath them, in Maren's cramped hand, two words:

*the Wood.*

* * *

He sat with his back against the cold hearth until the light went amber and then grey, and the anger came up in him slow and scalding, like water forced through limestone.

She had known. That was the thing that made his jaw ache with clenching. She had known he would come back here — someday, someday, when the running wore thin or the wardens got close or the loneliness finally outweighed the fear — and she had prepared for it. She had taken down every map he might have used to survive, every path home through the mountains he'd spent four years memorizing and three more pretending to forget, and she had burned them or buried them or carried them away with her, and left him this.

This map that lied.

*You could have just asked me,* he said aloud to the empty room, and his voice cracked on it like green wood in fire. *You could have written me a letter. You knew where I'd go first, you knew I'd lift that stone, you planned for me like I was — like I was one of your patients. Like I was a fever you were drawing out.*

Because that was what this was. He saw it now, and seeing it made it worse. It wasn't a trail. Trails were for people you trusted to walk them. This was a treatment. A course of medicine laid out for a patient who couldn't be reasoned with, who had to be *led*, who would resist being helped if you told him you were helping. Redraw the roads. Let the liar follow a lying map to the truth, because the liar would never follow an honest one.

The whispering salt at the doorway had gone quiet, as though even it was listening.

He stood up too fast and the blood roared in his ears. He wanted to throw the map into the hearth. His hands actually closed on the parchment to do it — he felt the fibers creak under his grip, felt the old oilcloth smell of it, tallow and juniper and the faint mineral tang that had gotten into everything in the last weeks, into the whole kingdom apparently, salt rising like damp through the world's foundations —

— and under his thumbs he felt the ridges.

Ink laid over ink. Palimpsest work. He'd seen Maren do it a hundred times: write a charm, scrape it back, write another over the ghost of the first, because good vellum was worth more than pride. But this wasn't scraped. This was fresh ink over old, and the old lines still showed through when he tilted the sheet toward the window's last light.

There had been another map underneath. Honest. Correct. Harrowmere's road straight as a plumb line, the Salt Road true, the passes marked in their proper places. And over it, in a different hand entirely — thinner, hurried, the letters leaning like wind-bent grass — someone had drawn the false routes on top.

Not Maren's hand.

Paloma lowered the map very slowly, the way you lower a weapon.

* * *

He spent the evening going through the cottage properly, and the cottage gave up its secrets grudgingly, one by one, like a prisoner who has decided to cooperate but resents it.

Maren's writing desk stood against the eastern wall under the shelf of stoppered jars, and its lock had been broken — not picked, broken, splintered inward, by someone in a hurry or someone strong or both. Inside, drawers pulled out and emptied except for the bottommost, where a false back had been pried loose and left hanging by one nail. Behind it, wedged into the gap where the mechanism should have been, a scrap of paper no bigger than his palm.

He recognized the handwriting immediately, because he'd been staring at it all afternoon. The thin, wind-bent letters. The stranger's hand.

*If you are reading this, she trusted you with the stone, and I trust her judgment, though I never met you. She went west on the new moon. She made me copy the maps first — she said the boy will check the roads, he always checks the roads, he won't be able to help himself. She said you'd know the difference between a map that lies and a map that leads. I hope she was right. I have her notes. I could not carry her.*

No signature. No date. The paper smelled faintly of pine resin and something else, something cold, the way the air over deep water smells.

Paloma read it eleven times.

*She made me copy the maps.* So the false routes weren't Maren's work at all. Someone else had redrawn them — at her instruction, or with her consent, or — no. He turned the scrap over. Blank. Turned it back. *I trust her judgment.* The stranger had copied the maps faithfully underneath, and drawn the false roads above, and the false roads all bent west, and Maren had gone west on the new moon, and the new moon had been — he counted on his fingers, and stopped counting, because the answer was seventeen days ago, and the wards had died nine days ago, and the stars had gone out eight nights since, and the arithmetic of it sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

She had gone into the Wood before the blight broke open. Before the wards snapped. Before the salt came weeping down out of the high country and crusted doorways with the names of the lost.

She hadn't fled it. She had walked toward it while it was still only a rumor on the wind, while the crown still called it a drought and the wardens still called it smuggling, while everyone with sense was looking anywhere else. She had known, or half-known, or feared enough to act on the fear, and she had rearranged the entire geography of his childhood to make sure that when he finally crawled home — bleeding, hunted, too proud to send word ahead — he would find exactly this: a locked desk, a lying map, a stranger's note, and a direction.

West.

*I hope she was right. I have her notes. I could not carry her.*

Could not carry her. The words snagged on him like a fishhook. Not *would not*. *Could not.* As if carrying her had been attempted. As if she had been heavy in some way that had nothing to do with flesh.

He looked at the hearth, at the kettle waiting on its hook, at the bed made tight by hands that weren't hers, and he understood that the stranger had stayed here after Maren left. Had waited. For how long? Days? Weeks? Long enough to sweep the hearth and break the desk searching for — what? Whatever they hadn't found. Whatever Maren had kept hidden even from her accomplice. Long enough to leave one note in one hiding place, addressed to a person they'd never met, on the faith of an old woman's certainty.

*The boy will check the roads. He always checks the roads. He won't be able to help himself.*

Paloma laughed — one short, ugly sound with nothing in it — and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes.

She had known him so well it was humiliating. Four years gone, and she had predicted him like weather. He had spent those four years building himself into someone unguessable — new names, new routes, new habits layered over the old ones like bark over sapwood — and she had simply reached through all of it and found the boy who checked the roads. Who couldn't not. Who had learned every pass and ford and goat-track not for profit, whatever Doss and the others believed, but because Maren had taught him that knowledge of paths was knowledge of people, and he had never once stopped wanting to know where everyone was, so that no one he loved could ever again disappear without a trace.

The way she had disappeared now.

The anger was still there — he could feel it banked in him, coals under ash, and it would flare again, he knew, probably at the worst possible moment. But walking through it, underneath it, was the other thing, the harder thing. She had manipulated him. She had also trusted him absolutely, and the two facts were the same fact. You only lied to someone's map if you were certain they would read it. You only built a maze if you were certain the mouse would run it. She had bet his whole stubborn, suspicious, runaway nature on the idea that he would come home — not to her, maybe not ever to her, but to the stone under the hearth, to the shape of the life he'd abandoned — and she had staked the fate of whatever she'd gone west to fight on that bet paying off.

Nobody had ever trusted him like that. Not the crew, whose trust had been conditional on usefulness and ended at the gallows. Not the wardens, whose trust was a leash. Not himself, if he was honest, which he tried not to be.

He folded the map along its original creases, careful as handling a bird, and tucked it inside his coat against his chest, where it lay warm and faintly crackling like a second heartbeat. The scrap of the stranger's note went with it.

Then he did the things that needed doing, because Maren had raised him to do them and dead houses deserved better than neglect. He filled the kettle and set it near the dead hearth anyway — habit, superstition, he didn't examine which. He took down the bundle of dried yarrow above the mantel and crushed a leaf between his fingers; the scent came up faint and faithful, summer preserved against winter, and his eyes stung and he let them, briefly, standing in the darkening kitchen of the woman who had taught him that grief unlived becomes poison, and that the antidote was always the same: keep moving, keep mending, keep the kettle on.

At the door he paused, one hand on the frame, and spoke to the salt crusts softly, the way you speak to a guard dog you mean to pacify rather than fight.

"I'm going after her."

The salt whispered. Not his name this time — or not only his name. He leaned close, and among the murmuring syllables he caught others, older ones, worn smooth as river stones: *Maren*, yes, and under it something longer, something that sounded like a title or a sentence, *she-wept-and-was-not-drowned*, repeated over and over like a bell struck underwater.

He straightened. West, then. Down the lying map's true intention, past Harrowmere, along the redrawn roads toward the treeline where the stars had gone underground.

He stepped off Maren's doorstep onto the salt road, and the night received him, starless and vast, and somewhere far to the west, faint as a memory of light, he thought he could see — or imagined he could see, which amounted to the same thing on a night like this — a smudge of deeper darkness where the Weeping Wood began.

Two days to Harrowmere, if the lying map told the truth about the distance even while it lied about the way. Three, if the roads were watched. He touched the folded parchment over his heart once, like a man confirming a wound still bleeding, and started walking.

Behind him, in the cottage that smelled of cold ash and patience, the salt at the doorway whispered on — his name, her name, and the third name, the long one, the one that was not a name at all but a warning, wearing the dark smooth as water wears stone.
