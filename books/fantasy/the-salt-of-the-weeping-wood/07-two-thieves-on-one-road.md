## 7. Two Thieves on One Road

The salt road ran white as a scar through the brown throat of the valley, and Paloma hated it for exactly that reason.

Every other road in Thornfell had a shape he could trust — a switchback that lied about its ending, a ford that deepened after rain, a goat track that looked like nothing and went everywhere. Roads were arguments between the land and the people crossing it, and Paloma had spent six years learning every lie the mountains told. But the salt road was honest, and honesty on a road meant exposure: three miles of crushed chalk and old cart-ruts running straight as a plumb line from Maren's empty cottage toward the low country, with no cover taller than gorse and no bend deeper than a wagon's length.

He crouched at the treeline where the pines gave out and studied it the way he'd once studied locks — not for beauty but for weakness. The morning fog was burning off in rags. Somewhere below, a cart creaked. A dog barked twice and stopped, which was worse than continuous barking; dogs that stopped had been told to stop.

*You're being followed,* said the part of his mind that had kept him alive since he was fourteen, the part that never slept so much as listened. *You've been followed since the ravine.*

He'd tried to lose them twice in the night — doubling back through a burn, wading upstream over stones, walking backward in his own footprints like Maren had taught him when he was small enough to ride her shoulders through market crowds. It hadn't mattered. Whoever it was didn't blunder. They simply arrived wherever he arrived, a half-mile behind, patient as weather.

So he did the only thing left to a man with no secrets remaining: he walked out onto the salt road in the open, hands loose at his sides, and waited for the mountain to show him its teeth.

* * *

She came up the road at a warden's walk — not hurrying, not stalking, just covering ground with the flat economy of someone who has marched ten thousand miles and expects to march ten thousand more. Grey cloak, grey eyes, the crown's silver birch-leaf pin dulled with road dust. Her crossbow was slung across her back rather than leveled, which was either courtesy or confidence, and Paloma suspected confidence.

Warden Ilsa Vane. He knew her face before she was close enough to have one — knew it from wanted-notices nailed to smugglers' posts, from Doss's spit-and-curse descriptions, from two years of mending patrol-charms for wardens who never learned the name of the quiet apprentice who did the work. She was the one who wrote reports that got people hanged. She was also, if half the stories were true, the only warden in three counties who'd ever carried a dead poacher's body nine miles through snow so his mother could bury him whole.

Paloma had spent years deciding what he believed about Ilsa Vane. Now she was forty feet away and all his beliefs felt like coins from a country that had fallen into the sea.

"Apprentice," she said, when she was close enough not to shout. Her voice was dry as the road. "Paloma Okonkwo. Runaway. Wanted for theft of guild property, breach of indenture, and the death by negligence of Warden Tullis Corve." She recited it like a shopping list. "In the king's name, I'm placing you under arrest."

"You're a long way from anyone who cares whose name you invoke," Paloma said.

"The name works fine at any distance."

"Does it?" He tilted his head at the sky — at the blank, starless bruise of it, pale even now in full morning, the way it had been since the night the wards snapped and every light above the world went out. "I stood on the border ridge four nights ago, Warden, and watched your king's wards die like cut strings. I've seen a caravan of living people crusted in salt. I've read my dead mistress's notes about a Wood that eats grief and a court that smells wrong. You want to arrest me in the king's name?" He spread his hands. "I'd want to be very sure whose king we're talking about first."

Something moved behind her eyes — quick, controlled, gone. But he'd spent his life reading faces across campfires and card games and customs checkpoints, and he caught it: not surprise. Confirmation.

"You know," he said slowly. "You already know something's wrong."

"I know you're under arrest," she said, but her hand had drifted to her belt, not to irons — to a folded paper there, worn soft at the creases. She looked at it. Looked at him. And then, in a voice stripped of everything official, she said: "Do you know how to unbind a charm without breaking it?"

The question fell between them like a dropped knife.

"Anyone can break a binding," she went on. "Cut the thread, smash the vessel, done. My whole career I've been breaking things. But my brother—" Her jaw worked. "There's a letter I've been carrying for three years because I can't send it and can't burn it. There are charms on my patrol route going sour one by one, and the men who made them are dead or scattered, and last week I watched a village gate-ward eat itself trying to hold against whatever came down out of Thornfell, and I stood there thinking: *the apprentice could fix this. The apprentice who re-strung our wards in an afternoon and never took payment and never gave a name.*"

"That wasn't charity," Paloma said. "It was hiding. Wardens who like you don't look closely at you."

"It was both," Vane said. "Most things are." She pulled the paper from her belt but didn't unfold it. "Here's what happens next, apprentice. I put these irons on you, I drag you three hundred miles to the capital, and we arrive to find a court that — " she stopped. Started again, quieter. "A court that isn't asking questions it should be asking. And somewhere between here and there, whatever is coming out of that Wood arrives too, and neither of us is standing where we need to stand."

"And the alternative?"

"The alternative is that you're not my prisoner. You're my guide."

* * *

They walked while they talked, because standing still on the salt road felt like standing in an open grave. Vane set the pace — warden's pace, relentless — and Paloma matched it, angling them off the chalk and onto a drover's path only shepherds used, partly to make the point that he was worth keeping alive, partly because his skin crawled whenever he was more than a stone's throw from cover now.

"So let me hear the offer straight," he said. "My knowledge of the Weeping Wood in exchange for — what, exactly?"

"My silence."

"On all of it?"

"Tullis Corve died on a slope above the Kestrel Pass with your crew's rope-harness in his fist. Two of your people swung for it. You ran." She said it flatly, factually, a woman laying cards face-up. "Officially, that makes you a killer as well as a thief. Unofficially — " her mouth twisted, "— I've read the incident record eleven times, and harnesses don't climb uphill to grab falling men, and Tullis was carrying a writ to raid the Okonkwo cottage the same week your mistress reported him for taking bribes from the very crew he was hunting. Somebody wanted you all tangled together, apprentice, and it wasn't the mountain that did the tangling."

Paloma stopped walking.

He hadn't known that. About the bribes. About the report. Three years he had carried Corve's death like a stone sewn into his coat, telling himself the same story every night: *you mended their charms, you fed their information, you ran, and a man died because of the life you chose.* And underneath the story, the colder whisper: *and you ran anyway, and that makes it yours.*

"You didn't know," Vane said, watching him. It wasn't a question.

"No one tells runaway apprentices anything," Paloma said. His voice came out rough. "That's rather the point of us."

They walked again. The drover's path climbed along a stream gone strangely clear — no silt, no weed, water bright as glass and tasting, when he wet his lips, faintly of the sea though they were fifty miles inland. Salt. It was in the water now. It was in everything.

"Understand me," Vane said. "This isn't mercy. If you run, I'll hunt you, and I'll catch you, and the offer dies. If you lead me into a trap, I'll put a bolt through your knee and drag you the rest of the way. What I'm offering is a ledger, not a pardon. Your roads and your craft against my silence until this thing is dealt with. After that—" she shrugged, "—after that we see what the world looks like when it stops ending."

"And why should I believe a warden keeps ledgers?"

"Because I carried a dead man nine miles through snow so his mother wouldn't have to bury a stranger," Vane said, "and you already know that story, which means you've been weighing me for years, same as I've been weighing you. So weigh this instead: I could have shot you on the road. I didn't. That cost me something, and wardens don't spend what costs them on lies."

It was, Paloma admitted privately, the most honest sales pitch he'd ever heard. It was also, he noted, missing several pieces.

"You're leaving things out," he said.

"So are you."

"Name mine."

"You flinched," she said, "when I mentioned your mistress. Not grief — guilt. You know where she went, or where her notes say she went, and you haven't told me yet, which means you're planning to manage that information like a smuggler manages cargo. Sell it when the price is right." She glanced sideways at him. "And I'm leaving out what happened at court last month. Fair's fair."

"What happened at court?"

"When you tell me about Maren."

They glared at each other across four feet of mountain path, and Paloma felt the strange vertigo of recognition — the unpleasant, exhilarating sense of meeting someone built to the same specifications as yourself. She lied the way he lied. She trusted the way he trusted: provisionally, arithmetically, always with one hand on the exit. Dealing with her was going to be like dealing with a mirror that occasionally drew a knife.

"Terms," he said at last. "One: I choose the routes. You don't override me on the road, ever. Two: you tell me what you know about the court, all of it, before we're within sight of the capital. No hoarding. Three—" he hesitated, then made himself say it, "—when this is done, whatever's left of the ledger, we settle it in front of a magistrate, not in some ditch with irons and a crossbow. If I've earned hanging, I'll hang clean. But I'll have it earned, not ambushed."

Vane considered him for a long moment. Above them a crow crossed the blank sky and seemed, briefly, embarrassed by the emptiness.

"Accepted," she said. "With one addition. Four: no more hidden names. I know you've been 'the quiet apprentice' and 'the boy who mends charms' and half a dozen other shapes. Whatever name you're actually carrying — whoever you were before you ran — I'll need it before this ends. Not today. But before this ends."

The words landed harder than she could know. Paloma kept his face still through long practice and a lifetime of needing to, but inside, something turned over like a boat shipping water. *No more hidden names.* She might as well have asked him to hand over his heartbeat.

"Before this ends," he said. It was, technically, agreement. Smugglers survived on technically.

They shook on it — her grip hard and calloused, his careful — and then there was nothing to do but walk, two liars bound into one road, each listening to the other's breathing for the sound of betrayal.

They made camp that night in a hollow beneath a fallen rowan, and Vane took first watch, and Paloma lay staring up at the starless dark thinking about Maren's maps and the redrew annotations in the hand that wasn't hers, and about how the trail she'd left him pointed not away from the Weeping Wood but straight into its black heart.

*She wanted me to go there,* he thought. *She went first. And she left the map for me.*

Which meant that somewhere ahead of them, past Harrowmere, past the drowned borders of the forest that wept salt, his mistress was waiting — or what remained of her was — and the bargain he'd just struck with the woman watching the dark ten feet away was going to have to hold long enough to get them both there.

He was nearly asleep when Vane spoke, quietly, not turning from her watch.

"Apprentice. The village ahead — Harrowmere. When we reach it tomorrow, stay close, and don't answer anyone who calls you by a name." A pause. "Any name."

Paloma sat up in the dark, cold walking up his spine like a finger.

"Why?"

"Because," said Warden Ilsa Vane, "when I passed through three days ago, they were still calling the children home."
