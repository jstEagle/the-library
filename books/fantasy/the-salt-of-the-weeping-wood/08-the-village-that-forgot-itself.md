## 8. The Village That Forgot Itself

*Paloma*

The road to Harrowmere smelled wrong before they saw it.

Paloma had walked the salt road a dozen times in his years of running, and it had always smelled of pine resin and woodsmoke, of wet stone and the green rot of fallen leaves. Now there was something else beneath it — a thin mineral tang, like the air over a dried lakebed, like the inside of an old jar kept shut too long.

"Do you smell that?" he asked.

Ilsa Vane rode half a length ahead, her warden's grey cloak beaded with morning damp. She did not answer at once. She had not answered much in the two days since the chapel, since she'd watched him speak the unbinding rite with his mouth shaped around a silence where his name should have been. But she drew rein now, lifted her chin the way a hound lifts its nose, and said quietly: "Salt."

"Sea's four hundred miles east," Paloma said.

"Then something brought the sea to us." Her hand moved to the writ-case at her belt — an old habit, he'd noticed. When she was afraid, which was rare, she touched the law as if it were an amulet. "How far to Harrowmere?"

"Just past that ridge." He pointed to the shoulder of moorland ahead, where a crooked line of chimney smoke should have been rising. There was no smoke. There should have been smoke even if every hearth in the village had gone cold — Harrowmere burned peat, and peat smolders for days. "There ought to be smoke."

"There ought to be a bell," Ilsa said. "It's near noon."

She was right. He could hear nothing but wind moving through the gorse, and beneath the wind that faint mineral whisper, so quiet he might have been imagining it, might have been hearing his own blood in his own ears.

They came over the ridge together, horse and foot, and looked down on Harrowmere, and Paloma felt something in his chest go still and cold as a stopped clock.

The village sat in its shallow bowl exactly as he remembered it — the millpond, the turf roofs, the long turf wall against the winter wind, the shrine to some minor harvest saint whose name nobody had ever quite agreed upon. Everything was in its place. And everything was white.

Not snow-white. Salt-white. A crust of pale crystal furred every surface: the gateposts, the well-chain, the washing line where shirts hung stiff as grave-clothes, their colors bleached to ghosts of ghosts. The millpond had gone opaque, a sheet of milk glass. Even the grass at the village edges glittered faintly, each blade silvered, as though the whole place had been dipped in brine and left to dry.

And the people —

The people were working. That was the worst of it. A man was mending a fence post, driving pegs with slow, perfect strokes. A woman stood at a doorway sweeping a step that needed no sweeping. Two children — no, not children; young men, though they moved like old men — carried water from the well, one bucket apiece, unhurried, unerring, their faces smooth and empty as the crust on the pond.

None of them spoke. None of them looked up. Their eyes, when Paloma caught them, were the color of skimmed milk.

"They're alive," Ilsa breathed beside him. "Gods below, they're alive."

"I know." His voice came out flat. He made himself keep looking. Running away was the thing he was best at, and this — this was the moment every instinct in him screamed *go around, take the high track, let it be someone else's problem*. He planted his feet instead. "Ilsa. Look at the children."

"There are no children."

"That's what I mean." He dismounted slowly, keeping his hands loose and visible, though no one in Harrowmere seemed capable of noticing him. "This is a farming village. Where are the little ones? The babies? I've passed through here before — there were always children underfoot, always. Either the salt took them somewhere, or—"

"Or the village forgot them," Ilsa finished.

They looked at each other across the white silence of the street.

"The Wood feeds on grief," Paloma said. "That's what Maren's page said. Grief is the root of it. And grief—" He swallowed. "Grief is just love with nowhere to go. If you wanted to feed, you wouldn't take love away. You'd take away what it's attached to. Leave the ache, cut out the reason."

"Then these people are grieving."

"For something they can't remember losing." Paloma started down into the village, and after a moment he heard Ilsa's boots follow. "Look at how they move. Like mourners. Like the whole town is sitting shiva for a funeral no one can name."

The man mending the fence did not see them pass three feet from him. The woman sweeping looked straight through Ilsa's warden grey, through the royal writ, through all the authority of a crown that, as far as either of them knew, had been hollowed out and worn like a coat. Paloma reached out as he passed and touched the fence-post — solid, real, crusted cool under his fingertips — and the man went on hammering, each stroke soft, as though he dared not make a loud sound in a house where someone slept.

At the well, the two young men lowered their buckets. Paloma leaned over the lip. Far down, the water reflected nothing — not the sky, not his face. Just a pale shimmering, like moonlight trapped in milk.

He stepped back. "We find whoever's most themselves and ask questions. The priest, the reeve, the oldest person—"

"Paloma." Ilsa had gone very still. She was staring at the nearest cottage, at the door, and her hand had gone to her sword hilt without her seeming to notice. "Come and look at this."

Above the lintel, written in salt crust in a careful, childish hand, were words:

*WE HAD THREE. WE ARE SURE WE HAD THREE.*

* * *

*Ilsa*

Ilsa Vane had served the crown for nineteen years, and in that time she had learned to measure horror the way other people measured distance — in small, practical units. A murdered traveler: one day's hard riding, one report filed, one family notified. A burned village: three days, two reports, a requisition for aid. Horror, properly administered, fit inside paperwork. That was the mercy of the law. It chopped the unbearable into bearable pieces.

Harrowmere would not chop.

She walked the street beside Okonkwo — she refused, on principle, to think of him by any warmer name — and counted what she saw, because counting was her form of prayer. Fourteen adults visible. No elders except one, an ancient woman seated in a chair before the last cottage, wrapped in blankets, facing the road. No children. No dogs — she noticed that only now, and it raised the hair on her neck. Every village had dogs. Harrowmere had none, not even bones. Whatever had come here had taken the animals first, or the animals had fled, or—

No. Not taken. *Forgotten.* The distinction mattered, and she hated that it mattered, and she held onto it anyway because holding distinctions was the last discipline she had left.

The salt wrote on things here. That was the second discovery of the afternoon. On the shrine, in crust: *SOMEONE IS OUR SAINT. THE NAME IS LOST. WE KEEP IT ANYWAY.* On the mill door: *THERE WAS A MILLER. HE WAS KIND.* On the turf wall, repeated over and over in lines of fading script, as if written by many hands: *REMEMBER REMEMBER REMEMBER REMEMBER* — and then, further along, in a different, defeated hand: *remember what.*

"It's writing its own epitaphs," Okonkwo said. He crouched by the wall, reading, and she watched his face do something complicated. "Or its own prayers. Maren used to say hedge-witchery was mostly memory work. Names, births, debts, grudges — a village's whole ledger lives in its hedges and its grandmothers. Take the memory out and you don't kill a place. You hollow it." He glanced up at her. "You get a vessel."

Like the king, he didn't say. Like the man on the throne who speaks in borrowed phrases. Neither of them said it. They didn't need to anymore; the unsaid things between them had developed their own grammar.

"The grandmother," Ilsa said. "The one elder. If anyone remembers anything—"

"If anyone suffers anything, you mean."

"Yes. That's what I said."

They approached her together. The old woman sat facing the road as though she had been set there — placed, deliberately, like a figure on a mantel — and at first Ilsa thought she too was empty, another milk-eyed statue of flesh. Then the woman's head turned, slowly, joints cracking like green wood, and her clouded gaze found Paloma.

And her face broke open.

"Oh," she said. Her voice was rust and honey. "*Oh.* There you are. There you are, love. I knew you'd come back. I told them. I told everyone — my boy comes home when the stars go dark, that's what I said, and they laughed, but look — look at you."

She reached out both hands, trembling, and Paloma froze.

Ilsa had spent two days learning to read the smuggler's face: the quick calculations, the flicker of contempt he thought he hid, the surprising steadiness underneath. Now every calculation fell away and what remained was raw, unguarded, terrible — a young man watching a dying woman reach for him with love meant for someone else.

"Mistress—" he began carefully. "Mistress, I'm not—"

"You're so tall." She caught his hands before he could retreat. Hers were cold as the fence posts, crusted faintly at the knuckles. "You were so small when you went. Such a scrap of a thing. You used to hide in the barley and I'd pretend I couldn't find you and you'd laugh — you had a laugh like water over stones, my Tam, do you still laugh like that?"

Paloma's mouth worked. Nothing came out.

"Sit down, sit, your legs must be tired, walking all that way—" She tugged at him with shocking strength, and he sank to his knees before her chair, eye-level with her, and Ilsa saw that he was shaking. "Your father said you wouldn't come back. He said the roads would eat you. But mothers know. Mothers keep a door open in their hearts and the draft never stops, does it, the draft never stops—"

"Ma'am." Ilsa stepped closer, gently. "What's your name?"

The old woman blinked at her without recognition, then dismissed her entirely, the way you dismiss weather. Her attention returned to Paloma with the fierce focus of the starving. "Tam. My Tam. Tell me about the stars, love. Tell me about the night they went out. Were you frightened? I was frightened. I woke in the dark and it was so quiet, and then I couldn't remember why I'd woken, and I couldn't remember your face, and I couldn't—" Her breath hitched, wet and rattling. "I can't remember your face, Tam. I know I loved you. I have all this love and nowhere to put it. They took the picture out of the frame and left me holding the frame."

"*Who took it?*" Ilsa asked. "Think. Who came? What did you see?"

But the woman wasn't listening. She had raised one hand to Paloma's cheek, and Paloma — gods help him, the fool, the kind fool — leaned into it.

"I'm sorry," he whispered. "I'm sorry, mistress. I'm not your son. Your son isn't coming back. I think—" His voice cracked clean through. "I think your son went wherever the children went. And I think he'd want you to know that being loved by you wasn't wasted. Even forgotten. It wasn't wasted."

For a long moment the old woman simply looked at him. And then something moved behind the milk of her eyes — a fish under ice, a shadow of a shadow — and she smiled, and the smile was more herself than anything else in Harrowmere.

"No," she agreed softly. "You're not my Tam. My Tam is dead, isn't he. I've known since the stars went out. I keep forgetting I know it." Her hand fell away from Paloma's cheek. "But you stayed a minute anyway. Nobody stays anymore. Everybody here just... goes on, like clocks with no one wound. You stayed." She patted his knee, twice, like a benediction. "Whatever name you're hiding, love — and you're hiding one, I can taste it, hiding names is salt-work and you smell of salt — whatever it is, it's a good name. Don't let them take it. Do you hear me? Don't ever let them take it. A name is the last coin a poor soul has."

Then her head tipped back, her breath went long and slow, and she slept — or became still in the way the others were still — and would not wake for anything they tried.

* * *

*Paloma*

They buried nothing, because there was nothing to bury; she was breathing, faintly, evenly, like the tide. They settled her deeper in her blankets instead, and Ilsa stood over her for a long moment with her hat in her hands, and Paloma walked away because if he watched that for one more second he was going to come apart in front of the warden, and he would rather be hanged.

*A name is the last coin a poor soul has.*

He stood at the well and stared down into the blind white water and pressed his palm flat against his own chest, against the place where his name lived, folded small and secret, the thing he'd buried so deep even Maren had only ever guessed at it. Six years ago he had done the rite — the same rite he'd taught Ilsa in the chapel, inverted, turned inward, a hedge-witch's trick for becoming unfindable. He'd paid for it with the only currency he had. And it had worked. For six years he had been a ghost with a heartbeat, invisible to wardens, untraceable by creditors, forgettable by design.

He had told himself it was survival. Survival was true. It was also not the whole truth, and the drowned dead under the Weeping Pool would someday, he suspected, ask him for the rest.

"A penny for the sermon you're preaching yourself."

Ilsa had come up behind him without sound. Warden training. He didn't startle anymore — that was the trouble with spending years around her; she wore grooves in his nerves.

"She was wrong," he said. "About it being a good name."

"Was she?" Ilsa leaned on the well beside him. Up close her face was tired in a way he'd never seen on her before — not road-tired. Older than that. "I've been thinking about what she said. About the frame with the picture gone." She nodded back toward the sleeping village, the salted roofs, the words crusted over every door. *Remember remember remember remember. Remember what.* "These people aren't empty. That's what I thought at first — that the Wood drained them. But it didn't drain them. It *redirected* them. All their love, all their grief, still burning, with nothing to burn on. That's not theft. That's..." She searched for the word and didn't like the one she found. "That's husbandry. Someone is farming them."

"Farming grief," Paloma said. "A crop that regrows. You don't slaughter the herd. You shear it."

"And the king?" Her voice dropped low. "Is he a field too?"

"He's a granary." The word came out of Paloma before he'd decided to say it, arriving whole from some part of his mind that had been working the problem in the dark. He turned to look at her. "Think about it. One village yields grief for a season and then it's spent — these people will die soon, or fade, there's nothing left to take. But a kingdom's king? Everyone loves the king a little. Even the people who hate him — hate is just love wearing armor, Maren used to say. Feed off one man positioned at the center of a whole realm's feeling, and you never need to salt another village again. Except—" he gestured at Harrowmere, at the white silence, "—except he's nearly ripe, isn't he. The solstice. Something happens at the solstice. This is the taste of what's coming for everyone, if we're slow."

Ilsa was quiet for a moment. Wind moved the stiff shirts on the washing line; they clinked like chimes made of bone.

"My brother's letter," she said at last. "The one I carry. Unsent. I wrote it eleven years ago and I've never been able to deliver it because there's no one left to deliver it to, and I've never been able to burn it because—" She stopped. Started again, flatter. "If the Wood showed itself to me right now, on this road, and offered me Tam back — really offered, not the way it tempted me before — I don't know what I'd say. I'd like to believe I'd say no. I said no once. But saying no is a muscle, Okonkwo, and it fatigues."

Paloma looked at her. Really looked — at the warden who had every legal right to clap irons on him and every practical reason not to, standing in a dead-alive village admitting weakness to a criminal because the criminal was all she had.

"Then we get strong enough fast," he said. "Because here's what the old woman gave me, whether she meant to or not." He touched his chest again, over the hidden name, and felt the secret pulse there like a second heart. "The Wood takes names. That's its whole economy — Maren's notes said it, the salt says it, this village proves it. It eats what we call ourselves. Which means every name we keep is a wall, and every name we surrender is a door." He grimaced. "And I've built myself a fortress with no doors at all. So when we reach the Wood, Ilsa — when it shows us our worst memories and offers us our dead — you'll have a wall. And I'll have..."

He didn't finish. He didn't need to. He watched her understand it: that the man walking beside her had already amputated the very thing the enemy fed on, and that a man with no name has nothing left to lose except everything else — or possibly, depending on how you counted, nothing at all.

Down the street, the man mending the fence drove one last peg, set down his hammer, and turned his milk-white face toward the ridge road. Toward the east. Toward the distant, unseen darkness of the Weeping Wood.

One by one, without a word, every soul in Harrowmere turned to face the same direction. Standing. Waiting. Like a congregation hearing a bell only they could hear.

Ilsa's hand closed on Paloma's arm. "Tell me that's normal."

"Nothing about this is normal." But Paloma's scholar's mind was already moving, pulling threads — because the villagers weren't shambling toward the Wood, weren't marching, weren't called anywhere. They were simply *oriented*, the way compass needles are oriented, and that meant the pull wasn't a summons at all.

It meant something in the Wood had just begun to draw. Strongly. Recently. As of today.

As of, perhaps, the moment two travelers had crossed the border wards with a hidden name and a dead man's letter between them.

"We need to move," he said. "Now. Before whatever felt us decides to reach back."
