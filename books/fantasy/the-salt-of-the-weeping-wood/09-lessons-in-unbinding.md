## 9. Lessons in Unbinding

The chapel had been dead longer than either of them had been alive.

Paloma found it the way he found most things — by feel, by the way the land leaned. The road above the ravine bent around a shoulder of granite, and where roads bend, old things hide. He pushed through a curtain of blackthorn and there it was: a roof like a broken ribcage, walls of grey stone furred with moss, and one bell tower standing stubborn against the dark with no bell in it at all. The doorway gaped open, its wooden door long since burned or stolen, and inside, the floor was a carpet of pine needles gone soft as wool.

"No consecrated ground," he said. "No wards. Nothing left to keep anything out."

Ilsa Vane stood behind him with her hand on her sword hilt, as if the ruin might object to their presence. "Then why sleep here?"

"Because nothing keeps anything *out*." He stepped over the threshold into air that smelled of cold stone and old resin. "And nothing keeps us in."

She didn't answer that. She never answered when he said things that were true about himself.

They made camp the way travelers do when they are pretending not to be afraid: efficiently, without speaking. Ilsa cleared the needles from the base of the altar — the chapel's god was long gone, but the stone remained, worn smooth by centuries of hands — and built a small fire there, which was probably blasphemy of some kind. Paloma hung the kettle over it and told himself the trembling in his fingers was only the cold.

The salt road was two days behind them now. Harrowmere was three days behind him and would be behind him forever, if he could help it. He still heard the grandmother's voice sometimes, in the space between waking and sleep — *Tomas, is that you, love? Come closer, the light's bad* — and each time, some animal part of him wanted to say *yes*. Yes, I'm Tomas. Yes, I'm anyone you've lost. Give me your grief and I will wear it like a coat, because grief is warm and my own name is not.

He fed the fire instead of feeding that hunger.

"Sit," he said. "Before we reach the Wood, you need to learn what Maren taught me. All of it."

Ilsa lowered herself onto the altar step across from him, knees drawn up, hands loose around them. In the firelight her face lost the hardness the road gave it, and he saw for the hundredth time how young she was underneath the warden-grey. Younger than him, maybe. It was easy to forget. She carried herself like a woman who had been old since the day she took her oath.

"I know ward-craft," she said. "Crown craft. Sigils cut to pattern, oaths sworn to office. What your hedge-witch taught you is folklore."

"Folklore kept me alive in Thornfell for six years while crown craft got four of your patrols buried under snowdrifts." He let that sit. "Maren used to say the crown builds walls and calls them safety. She built doors and called them the same thing. You need doors, Warden. The Wood eats walls."

He reached into his pack and drew out the things he'd carried since he was eleven years old, when Maren first put them into his hands: a spool of red thread gone pink with age, a knife of black river-iron no longer than a finger, and a pouch of ash sifted from hearths where someone had died well — peacefully, surrounded, remembered. Grief-ash, Maren called it, though it wasn't the grief that mattered. It was the *well*.

Isla watched him lay them out on the flat of his palm like a fortune-teller. "That's it? That's the arsenal?"

"That's everything." He smiled, and it hurt his face. "Hedge-craft isn't power, Warden. It's permission. You're asking the world to remember what it already knows. The thread is the asking. The iron is the honesty — river-iron has been drowned and reborn, so it doesn't lie. And the ash—" He hesitated. "The ash is the memory. Of everyone who ever sat where you're sitting and did this right."

"Did what right? What exactly am I learning?"

"Unbinding." He turned the word over carefully, the way you turn a blade. "Everything the salt-thing does is binding. It binds grief into flesh. It binds memories out of people and silence into them. It bound itself into the king. Every knot it ties can be untied — that's not hope, that's physics, if physics were older than physics. But unbinding is the opposite of everything a warden does. Wardens bind. Oaths, cells, chains, laws. All of it knots."

"And yours unknots."

"Maren's does. Mine—" He stopped. Fed the fire again, unnecessarily. "Watch first. Then you'll do it."

* * *

He taught her the way Maren had taught him: slowly, with his hands over hers, correcting before mistakes became habits.

First the thread. "Red because blood remembers being red. You don't tie a knot in it — you make a *loop*, an open loop, and you hold it in your off-hand like this, thumb here, so the opening faces your heart." He watched her frown at the loop, at the way it seemed to want to close itself, the way the thread pulled faintly against her fingers as if it had somewhere to be. "It wants to finish. Everything wants to finish. That's why binding is easy and unbinding is hard. You have to hold something open that every part of you wants closed."

Her loop held. Of course it did; she was Ilsa Vane, and discipline was the one language she spoke fluently. But he saw the effort of it in the tendons of her wrist, and something in his chest went quiet and strange, watching her struggle at something he'd once struggled at, in this same posture, in front of a woman who smelled of rue and woodsmoke.

"Now the iron. Cut the air in front of you — not a slash, a *statement*. One line, slow, like drawing a sentence you mean." He guided her arm. Her skin was cold even after days of walking; wardens ran hot in stories and ice in life. "What are you saying?"

"That whatever binds this thing can be severed."

"No." He shook his head. "That's a threat. Threats don't work on the Weeping Wood — it's heard every threat ever shouted at it, and it ate them all. Say it as truth instead. Not *I will cut you free*. Say *you can be cut free*. Offer, not attack."

She tried again. This time, when the black knife moved through the air, the fire guttered — just once, just slightly, like a held breath — and both of them felt the chapel pay attention. The shadows leaned in. Somewhere up in the empty bell tower, wind found a gap and made a low tone, almost a note, almost approval.

Isla exhaled slowly. "It heard."

"It always hears. Most people just never say anything worth hearing." He gathered the ash pouch next, and here he slowed, because this was where Maren had slowed, and some lessons you inherit whole. "Last part. The hardest. You speak the name of what's bound. Not a description. Not a title. The *name*. Names aren't labels, Warden — they're handles. They're the place where a thing grips itself. To unbind the king-thing, we'll need the true king's buried name. To unbind any working, any knot, any curse—" he met her eyes across the fire, and made himself keep meeting them— "the caster speaks a name too. Their own."

The wind-tone in the tower faded. The fire cracked. Ilsa didn't move.

"Their own," she repeated.

"The rite runs through you like current through wire. It needs to know what it's passing through. So you give it yourself — spoken aloud, whole, unhedged. Your true name, the one your mother used when you were small, the one that means *you and no one else*." His voice stayed level. He had practiced staying level about this for years, and the practice held, mostly. "You can't fake it. The rite knows. A borrowed name is a dry well; it rings hollow and the working dies. That's why hedge-witches guard their names like kings guard crowns. Because a name given truly is a self spent — once spoken in a rite, it's known, and what's known can be found, and what's found can be taken."

The silence stretched. Ilsa looked at the fire, and then at him, and he watched her arrive at it — watched the thought move through her face like weather, the way it must have moved through her mind a dozen times on the road already and finally, tonight, found its shape.

"You've never told me your name."

"Paloma Okonkwo," he said lightly. "You've known since the writ."

"That's the name you run under." Her voice didn't rise. It did something worse; it went quiet and precise, the voice she used for evidence. "Six years of smugglers' roads and you've never once said a word about your mother, your house, your birth-day, any of it. I thought it was just — running. Runners shed their pasts." She turned the loop of red thread between her fingers, not looking at it. "But you don't shed it. You *guard* it. Like a hedge-witch guards a name."

The fire popped. He made himself breathe.

"Every runner sheds something," he said. "Most shed other people."

"But not you." She set the thread down with exaggerated care, the way she'd set down something that might go off. "You shed everything except the one thing that matters. You've kept your name so hidden that even the rite—" She stopped. Started again, slower. "You can't perform the unbinding yourself. Can you."

It wasn't a question, but he answered it anyway, because lying to her tonight felt suddenly like drowning. "Not alone. When the time comes, I'll speak it. I'll have to." He looked into the fire. "But every day I carry it unspoken is another day I stay invisible. No working can grip me. No scrying finds me. No salt-thing tastes me on the wind. It's the only reason I'm still alive, Warden. The thing hunting this kingdom cannot see me, because there is almost nothing to see. Just a name in a box, carried by a boy who won't open it."

"And the box opens at the solstice. Whether you want it to or not."

"Whether I want it to or not."

She was silent a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she picked the thread back up and began practicing the loop again, thumb placed, opening facing her heart. "Then teach me to do it right," she said. "So that when you open the box, it costs as little as possible."

Something in his chest cracked — not painfully. More the way ice cracks in spring: a sound announcing that something frozen might, eventually, move.

* * *

They practiced until the moon crossed the broken tower and started down the other side.

She learned the loop until it held without effort. She learned the iron-line until the fire bowed toward her instead of away. She learned the breath Maren had called *the long inhale* — four counts in through the nose, hold seven, out eight, until the body forgot it was afraid — and he learned, watching her count silently with lips barely moving, that teaching someone is a kind of intimacy he had no defenses against. You cannot show a person how to hold a thread open without showing them how you hold your own hands. You cannot correct their breathing without noticing the exact rhythm of their chest. By the third hour they sat close enough that their shoulders nearly touched when they leaned over the same knot, and neither of them moved away, and neither of them named it.

"Tell me about her," Ilsa said, during the rest between repetitions. "Your hedge-witch."

"Maren." The name came out soft. "She smelled of rue and tallow. She talked to her garden more than to me, and the garden talked back, which should have frightened me and mostly just annoyed me. She took me in when I was eleven and a liar already. Never asked what I'd run from. Said the past was a pack everyone carried and she wasn't going to add weight to mine." He turned the black knife over in his fingers. "She taught me everything in one year and then spent nine more making sure it stuck. She used to say I had a talent for doors. Openings. That I could find the seam in anything." A pause. "Including her patience."

"She sounds like she loved you."

"She had a strange way of it. Mostly she fed me and corrected me and pretended I was a chore." He smiled at the fire. "But when the fever took me in my third winter — the sweating sickness — she sat up with me four nights straight, doing the loop, holding the opening, refusing to let the sickness bind itself into my lungs. Four nights without sleeping. I woke on the fifth morning and she was asleep sitting up, thread still in her hand, and she'd worn a groove in the floorboard where she rocked." He hadn't meant to say that last part. It came out the way truth does when you're tired: unbidden, complete.

Ilsa was quiet. "My brother wrote letters," she said at last. "Never sent them. Just wrote them, hundreds, to no address. After he died I kept reading them. Same letter, mostly, over and over — *I'm fine, the work is good, come visit when the roads clear.*" She fed the fire. "I understand wearing a groove in the floorboard."

For a moment the ruin held them both gently, the way ruins do — indifferent to rank and crime and oath, offering the same pine-needle floor and star-cracked ceiling to warden and smuggler alike. Above them, through the missing roof, the sky was wrong. He kept catching it: stars too few, constellations with gaps like pulled teeth, the darkness between them seeming to press closer every night, as if the sky were a page being erased from the edges inward.

"One more thing," he said, because the night was deep enough for it. "When we do the rite — at the end, before the court, wherever it happens — the rite takes truths. All the truths in the circle get spoken, or it fails. Whatever's hidden comes out." He looked at her. "Whatever you're carrying, Warden. The letter. Your brother. Whatever you haven't said aloud since he died. It'll come out. Into whatever room we stand in."

"I know what truth costs," she said. "I arrest people for a living."

"It's different when it's your own."

"I know that too." She said it simply, and did not elaborate, and he understood that this was the most she had given anyone in years, and that she knew he understood, and that this mutual knowing was the closest thing to trust either of them had traded yet.

She rolled out her bedroll near the fire. He took the watch first, as always — runners slept shallow and wardens slept prepared, and some habits needed no discussion.

* * *

Near dawn, the horses screamed.

Paloma came up out of a half-dream with the black knife already in his fist, heart slamming, and found Ilsa standing at the tree line with her sword drawn and her eyes fixed on the road. The horses were stamping at their picket, whites of their eyes showing, but nothing moved on the road. Nothing moved anywhere. The dawn had come up the color of skimmed milk, and the frost on the blackthorn glittered wrong — too white, too uniform, crusted thick as candle-drip on every branch facing east.

Salt-frost. Miles from any sea, miles from the Wood.

"There," Ilsa said quietly.

On the chapel's doorstep — the empty threshold where they had walked in last night without a second glance — the stones were crusted white with a thin weeping crust, and in the crust, written by no hand, letters had formed. Old letters, deep-cut, the kind carved into boundary stones.

Two words.

*COME ALONE.*

Paloma read them twice. Then he read the third line, smaller, beneath, in a cramped and familiar hand — a hand he knew better than his own, a hand that had corrected his grip on a black iron knife a thousand times:

*I bought you time. Spend it wisely. — M.*

"She's alive," he heard himself say. His voice sounded very far away. "Ilsa — she's *alive*."
