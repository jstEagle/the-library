## 10. The Queen's Cipher

The courier found them on the third morning out of Harrowmere, or perhaps they found him — it was difficult afterward for Ilsa to say which was true.

They had made camp in the lee of a fallen watchtower, one of the old border stations that predated the wards themselves, its stones furred with moss the color of old bruises. Ilsa had taken first watch because she did not trust Paloma to take it alone. This was not an accusation she voiced; it was simply the arithmetic of their arrangement. He knew the hidden roads. She knew how to hold a blade steady when something came out of the dark. Neither of them trusted the other with more than half of any task, and so they split everything down the middle like two children dividing bread — carefully, suspiciously, each watching to see if the other took the larger piece.

She heard the horse before she saw it. Not the drum of a gallop but the ragged, stumbling rhythm of an animal ridden past its limit, and beneath that a sound that made her hand go to her sword hilt before her mind caught up: weeping. A man's voice, cracked and thin, sobbing without any apparent awareness that he was doing it.

"Someone's coming," she said, and Paloma rose from his bedroll without argument, which told her he'd been awake too. He slept like a cat in a strange house — lightly, always, one ear cocked toward the door.

The horse broke from the tree line as the sky went grey with false dawn. It was a crown courier's mount, Ilsa saw at once — the branded sigil of the royal post on its haunch, half-hidden under a smear of mud — but no crown courier rode like this. The rider slumped forward over the animal's neck, his reins looped twice around his wrists as though he had feared even unconsciousness would take them from him. His cloak was stiff with dried salt. It had crystallized along every fold and seam until the garment looked less like cloth than like something grown, a chrysalis that had failed halfway through and left the man inside unfinished.

"Stop there," Ilsa called, sword drawn now, level. "Name yourself."

He stopped. He tried, at least — the horse obeyed, and the man simply continued leaning forward, swaying, as though the command had taken a long time to travel down into him. When he lifted his head Ilsa saw his face and felt the bottom drop out of her stomach.

She knew him. Tam Weller, junior dispatch-rider, second class. She had shared a fire with him at Coldford garrison not four months ago. He had laughed at her joke about the warden-kitchen's stew. He had been twenty-three years old and pink-cheeked and full of stories about a girl in the river districts he meant to marry when his posting ended.

His eyes were the eyes of someone who had seen the inside of his own skull and could not stop looking.

"Warden," he croaked. His voice sounded scoured, as if he had screamed himself hoarse somewhere between here and the capital. "Warden Vane. They said — the relay said you'd gone into Thornfell. They said to find you. *She* said to find you." His hands came up off the reins and pressed against his temples, and Ilsa saw that salt had crusted in the creases of his knuckles, white as frost. "It hurts to carry it. She said it would hurt to carry it, and that the hurting was the proof it hadn't been read. Please. Take it out of me."

"Take what out of you?" But she was already moving toward him, sword lowering despite every instinct that said a salt-touched man might be a trap walking on two legs. Behind her she heard Paloma shift, and then his low voice:

"Ilsa. Don't touch him barehanded."

She paused. The use of her given name still startled her each time — they had never agreed to it, it had simply begun happening somewhere on the road past Harrowmere, the way a crack begins in ice before either side of the river admits it. "Then how?"

Paloma crouched by the dead campfire and pulled a strip of oiled leather from his pack, wrapping it around his right hand with quick, practiced motions. Hedge-craft, she realized. Maren's lessons, worn smooth into habit. "Salt rides on skin," he said. "Skin remembers what touches it. Leather forgets." He approached the courier slowly, palms open, the way one approaches a spooked animal or a wounded stranger — and Ilsa noted, with a strange tight feeling in her chest, that those were the same gestures. That Paloma Okonkwo, smuggler, betrayer, runaway, moved through the world as though every person he met were both.

"In your satchel," Tam gasped. "Left side. Under the grain ration. She sealed it — she sealed it with—" His face contorted. "I wasn't supposed to look. I looked anyway. One corner. Just to know what I was dying for." He began to shake. "Is it true? Is the king—"

"Don't," Ilsa said sharply.

But it was too late to unask, and maybe too late to answer, because Tam Weller's eyes rolled back and he slid sideways off the horse, and only Paloma's quick lunge kept his skull from cracking on the tower stones. They lowered him down together, the three of them reduced by exhaustion and terror to something almost like cooperation. His breathing was shallow but steady. Salt glittered at the corners of his mouth, fine as sugar.

"He'll live," Paloma murmured. "Barely. Whatever carried him here used him up doing it." He glanced up at Ilsa, and in the grey light his face looked older than his years, carved rather than grown. "Whoever sent him spent a great deal to reach us. We should know who before we touch anything else."

* * *

The satchel yielded the packet on the left side, exactly where Tam had said, tucked beneath a wax-wrapped ration brick that Ilsa suspected was not grain at all — Paloma turned it over twice, sniffed it, and pronounced it "a very good disguise for a very illegal tincture," which told her the queen's couriers were better supplied than the queen's wardens.

The packet itself was small. Oilcloth, triple-wrapped, and sealed with wax that made both of them go still.

It was black wax, shot through with threads of silver — and stamped into it was a mark Ilsa recognized from a hundred childhood summers, though she had never expected to see it outside a hedge-witch's cottage door. A circle broken by a vertical line, the sign of the hearth-keeper. The sign of Maren Okonkwo's craft, the seal she pressed into charm-loaves and healing salves and the little paper packets of fever-tea she'd traded to farmwives for eggs and gossip.

"That's hers," Paloma said quietly. He was not touching the packet. He stood a pace back from it as though it were a snake sunning itself. "That's her seal. That's—" He stopped. Swallowed. "No one else uses that mark. No one alive."

"The queen does, apparently," Ilsa said. "Or someone who wants us to think she does." She worked her thumbnail under the edge of the wax, hesitating. "You taught me hedge-craft in a ruined chapel, Okonkwo. Teach me this too: what happens if the seal is trapped?"

"If Maren sealed it, nothing. She didn't trap things meant for friends." His jaw tightened. "If someone else sealed it wearing her mark, we're already dead, and the question is only whether we know it yet."

Comforting. She broke the seal.

Inside were two things. The first was a letter, written in a small, precise, elegant hand that Ilsa did not recognize — court-trained, certainly, every letter disciplined within an invisible margin. The second was a single loose page covered edge to edge in dense notation: columns of symbols, circles within circles, lines connecting them like the roots of some inverted tree.

She read the letter aloud, slowly, while Paloma held the notation page at arm's length and frowned at it.

*"To the warden Vane, if she lives, and to the one she carries with her, whoever he has decided to be this season. You will forgive the indirection. The palace listens through walls that do not exist and servants who do not know they serve. I write to you because you are the only two people in this realm positioned to act, and because neither of you can be reached by ordinary treason.*

*Understand what follows as truth, whatever else you doubt: the man on the throne is not the man I married. I have known since the night the stars went out. I have played the devoted wife for forty-one days because playing anything else would have killed the last chance we have.*

*The thing wears him well. Too well. It speaks in his phrases, signs with his hand, weeps at the funerals it caused. But it is hollow, and it is growing less patient, and on the solstice it intends what its kind call renewal — the final consumption of what remains of my husband. After the solstice there will be nothing left to restore. Nothing at all. Not memory, not name, not bone.*

*I cannot stop it from inside. The court is blind or bought or afraid, and most days I cannot tell which is worst. So I am spending the only coin I have left: secrets.*

*Enclosed is a cipher page. If you are reading this, then you already know its notation — it comes from the same school as the charms the boy mended for your patrols. I will say no more plainly. Read the page. Trust the hand that wrote it. And move quickly, wardens. The solstice is eleven days away.*

*Burn this. Burn Tam's memory of carrying it if you can. He volunteered knowing the cost, which shames me more than I can say.*

*— E."*

Silence settled over the ruined tower like snowfall.

Paloma sat down. Not gracefully — his knees simply quit, and he landed on a mossy stone with the notation page still clutched in his wrapped hand, staring at it as though it had begun to bleed.

"She knows," he said. His voice had gone strange and flat. "She knows about me. 'The one she carries with her, whoever he has decided to be this season.'" A laugh escaped him with no humor in it whatsoever. "The Queen of the realm knows I exist. I spent six years making sure nobody knew I existed, and the queen sends me letters."

"Focus on the part that matters," Ilsa said, though her own heart was hammering against the cage of her ribs. Eleven days. Forty-one days of playing the loving wife beside a thing that ate kings. She thought of the throne room, of the king's shadow lagging half a beat behind his body like a child failing to follow the music. She had told herself she had imagined it. She had wanted desperately to have imagined it. "Eleven days, Okonkwo. Can you read that page?"

"I can read the notation." He turned it toward her, and his finger traced one symbol after another. "This is Maren's hand. Her real hand, not the market-hand she used for receipts. These are binding-formulae — no, wait." He leaned closer. His breath had gone shallow. "These are *unbinding* structures. Deep ones. Older than anything she taught me. And these marginal marks—" He stopped.

"What?"

"These aren't hers." His finger hovered over a series of tiny annotations crowded into the margins, written in a different ink, a different pressure, a hand that slanted hard to the left. "Someone corrected her work. Or — no. Someone *collaborated*. Look at the sequence. She drafted, they revised, she redrafted. Back and forth, dozens of times. This page is the result of two people working together over months, passing notes, arguing in symbols." He looked up at Ilsa, and she saw the exact moment understanding arrived in him, arriving like cold water. "Maren wasn't just hiding from the blight. She was fighting it. From her cottage. For the crown."

*The hedge-witch served the crown secretly.* The thought arrived in Ilsa's mind fully formed, and with it came a cascade of reordering — every small thing sliding into new alignment. Maren Okonkwo, who lived at the crossroads of three smuggling routes and heard everything. Who had trained her apprentice in ward-mending precise enough to impress a royal patrol. Who had vanished into the Weeping Wood leaving a trail annotated in a hand that wasn't her own.

Two people. Working together. Arguing in symbols.

"The second hand," Ilsa said carefully. "In the margins. Could it belong to someone at court?"

"It could belong to anyone who studied under the same tradition. It's—" Paloma bent over the page again, and Ilsa watched him work, watched the smuggler fall away and the scholar emerge, the boy Maren had raised becoming, for a moment, entirely hers. "It's formalized. Court-style. They teach you to standardize notation so anyone in the college can read your work. Maren hated that — she said standardization was how the crown stole craft from the countryside." He tapped a marginal note. "But this writer standardized anyway. Habit. Training." He went quiet.

"And?"

"And there's a signature convention. Craftworkers sign their formulae with a personal glyph. Maren's was the broken circle." His finger moved to the bottom corner of the page, where two glyphs sat side by side like witnesses. "Hers. And this one." He drew it in the air: a vertical stroke crossed by three horizontal bars, descending in length.

"Do you know it?"

"No," Paloma said. "But I've *seen* it. On the writ you carried into Thornfell. Stamped in the lower-left corner, next to the crown seal. Every warden writ carries it." He looked up. "It's the counter-signature of the Office of the Royal Wardens. Whoever Maren was working with sits at the top of your own order, Vane. Your master, or their master. Someone with authority to issue writs."

The world tilted, gently, horribly. Ilsa thought of her superiors in the capital — of Warden-Commander Haleth, who had signed her commission, who had shaken her hand, whose office had dispatched her into Thornfell with a writ for a runaway apprentice. Had that dispatch been genuine? Or had she herself been a piece on a board, moved by hands she couldn't see — sent to retrieve the one person in the realm who might matter, or sent to bury him where no one would ever look?

"You didn't know," Paloma said. It wasn't a question. He was watching her face the way he watched trails — reading sign.

"No. I didn't know." She heard how flat her voice had become and could not soften it. "I've spent my whole career believing the writ above me was clean. Now the queen tells me the throne is rotten, and you tell me the warden's office may be compromised, and the only honest person left in this kingdom appears to be a hedge-witch who traded her memories for time." She laughed, short and bitter. "Do you understand what that means? It means the law itself might be hollow. It means when I arrest you — when I finally do my duty — I might be serving *it*."

"Yes," Paloma said simply. "I worked that out around the time you put irons on me in the ravine."

She glared at him. He almost smiled. It was, absurdly, the closest thing to comfort available, and she took it.

Tam stirred at the edge of the camp and moaned, and the sound recalled her to practicalities. "We can't carry him and move fast. We can't leave him — the salt will finish what it started if no one tends him." She thought. "There's a shrine two miles south, the Shepherd's Rest, still consecrated as far as I know. Old road-stone. Salt doesn't like old road-stone." It was a guess dressed as knowledge, but it was all she had.

"There's a drover's track that reaches it without touching the high road," Paloma said immediately. "Half a day lost, maybe less. We take him there, bar the door, and move on." He was already packing, already moving, converting grief into motion the way he converted everything into motion, and Ilsa understood suddenly that this was the deepest scar the Wood had left in him: Paloma Okonkwo could not sit still inside a feeling. He had to outrun it.

"Okonkwo." He paused, a bedroll strap in his teeth. "Your master. The woman who taught you. Do you believe she's still alive?"

He worked the strap through the buckle. Took longer than it needed to.

"Maren bargains," he said at last. "That's what she does. What she *did*. She bargained with weather, with spirits, with tax collectors, with death itself — she used to say everything alive will negotiate if you find the right currency." His hands went still. "Memories are currency to the Wood. She'd know that better than anyone. If she gave them memories, it's because she got something back." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious and hopeful all at once, and it cost him visibly to let her see it. "She's alive, Vane. Half-eaten, probably. Maybe worse than half. But alive. Because Maren never spends more than she has to, and she'd spend almost anything to finish a rite worth finishing."

Hope and fear, twined together like the silver threads in the black wax. Ilsa felt them both and could not separate them: hope that they had allies in the capital, in the margins of a cipher page, in the queen signing herself only *E.*; fear that every ally they found would turn out to be another debt the Wood had already collected.

They loaded Tam Weller onto his exhausted horse, and Paloma led them down a drover's track that did not exist on any map Isla had ever seen, winding south through birch scrub toward the Shepherd's Rest. Overhead the sky remained starless, a bowl of featureless grey that made noon feel like dusk and dusk feel like drowning. Eleven days. Somewhere east, a court rehearsed its solstice ceremonies for a king who was no longer in it. Somewhere west, a forest wept brine into its roots. And somewhere between them, a woman who had once mended charms and argued in symbols had left her life's last work in the hands of a fugitive and the warden sworn to take him.

At the crest of the hill, Paloma stopped. He was looking back the way they had come, north, toward the distant smudge of the Weeping Wood on the horizon.

"Vane," he said. "The margins. The second hand. There's one more thing I didn't say."

She waited.

"The corrections weren't just revisions. The last annotation — the newest one, the ink's freshest — isn't formulae at all." He turned to her, and the grey light made his face unreadable. "It's a warning. Written to Maren, in that court-trained hand, and it says: *"They know where you are. Come inside the walls before the solstice. I cannot protect you beyond them."*

A pause.

"And beneath it, in Maren's hand, one word in answer." His voice dropped. "*Staying.*"

The wind moved through the birches, carrying the faint mineral smell of brine from a forest none of them could see, and Ilsa understood that the trail they followed ran not away from the Weeping Wood but straight into its drowned heart — and that the woman who had marked the way had known, when she wrote that single word, exactly what staying would cost.

"We ride faster," Ilsa said.

They rode faster.
