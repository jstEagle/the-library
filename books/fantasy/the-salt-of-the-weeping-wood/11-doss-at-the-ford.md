## 11. Doss at the Ford

The river came on them the way bad news always did — first a sound, then a smell, then the thing itself too close to avoid.

We had walked since before dawn, Ilsa and I, down out of the high passes where the Thornfell stone still held some warmth from summer and the air tasted of pine instead of salt. For two days I had let myself believe the worst was behind us: the dead caravan, Maren's empty cottage, the whispering crust of brine that had sealed her doorway like a wound healing wrong. The river would mean low country, means, fish maybe, a road with living people on it.

Then the smell reached us — mineral and old, like a cellar flooded generations ago — and I knew the river was already sick.

"The Greywater," I said, though Ilsa could see it as well as I. It lay below us in its valley, wide and shallow where it braided over the ford, and every channel of it ran white. Not foam-white. Salt-white. The water moved, but it moved thickly, dragging itself over the stones the way honey drags off a spoon, and where it splashed against the boulders it left crusts that caught the grey light and threw it back dull as bone.

Ilsa crouched at the lip of the slope and studied it the way she studied everything, as if the landscape were a suspect she intended to break. "Is there another crossing?"

"Not for twenty miles, and twenty miles north is deeper into what's following us." I nodded upstream, where the white thickened toward some source we didn't want to name. "The ford is the way. It's always been the way."

"Always been," she repeated softly. She had taken up my habit of turning my own phrases back on me, holding them to the light to see what crawled underneath. "You trusted this road once."

"I trusted it because it was *mine*. Every smuggler's path in Thornfell is mine. That was the whole point of me." I heard the bitterness in it and didn't try to sand it off. Some bitterness you earn; spending it felt almost honest.

Ilsa stood, brushed grit from her warden's coat, and said nothing about my tone, which was its own kind of mercy, though I wouldn't have recognized it then.

We went down.

* * *

The ford was worse up close. The stones beneath the shallows had gone grey and furred with crystal, and the reeds along the banks stood stiff as candle-snuffers, each one glazed. Where I crouched to test the water with two fingers, the cold that came up through the salt was not river-cold. It was the cold of a room where someone has just died.

And under everything — faint, rhythmic, patient — the sound I had first heard at Maren's door. Whispering. A great many voices saying a great many names, all of them at the edge of hearing, none of them mine.

Yet.

"We cross single file," I said. "Middle channels are deepest but fastest; the edges will crust your boots and slow you. Keep your hands out of the water. If it touches skin past the wrist—" I stopped. I didn't actually know what happened then. Everything I knew about the blight I had learned from a page in Maren's handwriting and two days of watching it eat the world.

"If it touches skin," Ilsa finished for me, "we have a problem. Understood." She looked at me across the white water, and for a moment neither of us moved, because moving meant trusting the other one's plan, and trust between us was still a currency neither of us carried much of. Then she stepped in first. Warden's privilege, or warden's stubbornness. With Ilsa Vane the two had never been distinguishable.

The water took her to mid-thigh and held her like a hand deciding whether to squeeze. She crossed in slow, deliberate strides, face set, breath hissing through her teeth at the cold. I followed three paces back, hands high, feeling the pull at my legs like fingers testing the seams of me.

We were halfway across when the rocks started falling.

* * *

They came off the eastern scree without war-cries, which was how I knew them before I saw their faces. Crown wardens shout. Soldiers shout. Smugglers come down silent, because silence is the first thing the trade teaches you and the last thing it lets you keep.

There were eight of them, then ten, spilling down the slope in patched leathers, faces wrapped against the river-smell, crossbows and long knives and one old boarding net weighted with hooks. And at their head, big as a door and moving faster than anything that size should move, Doss Merrick.

My stomach did not drop. It was already dropped; it simply confirmed the arrangement.

"Doss!" I shouted across the water, and my voice cracked like a boy's, which I hated. "Doss, listen to me—"

He listened the way an avalanche listens.

The first bolt went into the water a hand's width from my hip and sank with a sound like a swallowed word. The second clipped my sleeve. Ilsa turned and put herself between me and the scree, shield up — she'd bought the battered thing in Harrowmere from a man who no longer remembered owning it — and bolts began to ring off it like hail on a coffin lid.

"They're shooting at *you*, not me!" she called back.

"They think I'm worth more than you! Don't take it personally!"

Downstream, two of them were wading into the shallows with the boarding net slung between them, and I understood the shape of the plan the way you understand a knife coming at you — not with thought but with the body's older arithmetic. They didn't want me dead on the bank. They wanted me dragged out, dried off, delivered somewhere. Delivered to whom? I thought of the salt-crusted caravan, the villagers of Harrowmere standing in their doorways with their children's names gone out of them like candles snuffed, and I thought: *the Wood doesn't send hunters. It sends neighbors.*

Because that was the worst of it. I knew these men. Knew the way Tam Osk shifted his weight before he loosed a crossbow; knew Ferren's limp from the winter the ice took him; knew that the big one with the net was called Brack and hummed when he worked and that Doss — Doss had carried me two miles through snow when I broke my ankle at seventeen, and never once mentioned it after, because mentioning kindness in the trade was a kind of weakness too.

Two of their own had hanged for the job I abandoned. I had run rather than swing beside them, and I had told myself a hundred versions of why, and every version had a different shape but the same weight.

Now here they were, come to collect.

"Paloma!" Doss's voice rolled across the water like a keg going downstairs. "You see me? You *see* me, little ghost?"

Little ghost. My name among them, the name I'd chosen, the name that meant *no one, nothing, nowhere to find.* I had built that name stone by stone so that no one could hold it over me, and now a man I'd betrayed was throwing it back across a poisoned river like a stone with my teeth in it.

"You owe me two ropes!" Doss roared. "Two ropes around two necks, and I've come to balance the books!"

Ilsa grabbed my arm. "Talk later. Move." We drove forward through the last of the channels while the net-crew angled to cut us off downstream, and I remember thinking, absurdly, of Maren's doorway, the salt whispering names — and wondering whether Doss's name was in there too, whether the Wood had already begun to drink him, whether the men on the scree were even entirely men anymore or just grief wearing familiar leather.

Then the deep channel opened under Ilsa like a mouth.

* * *

She went down without a sound, which frightened me more than any scream could have. One moment her shoulder was beside mine; the next the white water had folded over the place she'd been, and only her shield floated free, spinning slowly, absurdly, like a coin deciding which way to land.

The salt water closed over my own head a heartbeat later because I dove after her, and the world became pressure and murk and cold that bit through cloth to the marrow. Down there the whispering was everywhere, inside my ears, inside my skull — *names, names, names* — and through the churned whiteness I saw her: Ilsa, coat billowing, boots dragging, her eyes open and furious even underwater, one hand clawing toward a surface that receded above her like a promise being taken back.

Her fingers found my collar. Her grip said everything her pride would never allow her mouth to say.

I kicked for the light. The river pulled the other way, patient and strong, and the whispering rose around us until I could pick words out of it — *Tam Osk, Ferren, Brack, Doss Merrick* — the crew's names, the crew's names being *said*, being counted, being kept, the way a lender keeps accounts.

No. Not like a lender.

Like a ledger that eats its entries.

We broke surface downstream of the ford, in the slack water below the falls of the braided channels, and I got an arm around Ilsa's chest and hauled her toward a gravel spit while she coughed half the Greywater out of herself and swore the other half. Behind us, on the bank, the crew was coming along the waterline at a trot, Doss in front, and I had perhaps forty breaths to decide something I would carry the rest of my life.

Here is what the decision looked like from the inside.

It looked like Doss wading out too far. It looked like the deep channel — my deep channel, the one I'd mapped at fifteen and named the Swallower because I was fourteen once too and thought cruelty was cleverness — taking him exactly as it had taken Ilsa, except that Doss could not swim. I had known that for nine years. Big man, river-born village, drowned brother, wouldn't go near deep water, wouldn't speak of why. The crew had steered every route around his fear for nine years because Doss was the load-bearing wall of us, and you don't test load-bearing walls.

Now he was in the water because of me, chasing me, hating me, and the Swallower had him by the coat, and his crew — Tam, Ferren, Brack — were standing on the bank doing nothing, and I understood in one cold flash why they were doing nothing.

They had forgotten how.

Not forgotten Doss. Forgotten *fear for* Doss. The salt had gotten into them somewhere on the road down from the passes, into the soft places where worry lives, and it had sanded the worry smooth. They watched their leader drown with the mild attention of men watching weather.

The old Paloma — ghost-Paloma, the boy built out of exits — said: *swim, you fool. This is the river settling your account. Two ropes for one drowning, and the books balance, and you walk away lighter.*

The new Paloma, the one who had spent two weeks watching a kingdom forget itself one grandmother at a time, said nothing at all. He dove.

* * *

Underwater, the world was white noise and white light, and Doss Merrick was a great dark shape sinking with terrible calm, arms spread, face tipped up toward a surface he'd feared his whole life as if finally meeting it had emptied him of struggle. His eyes were open. When I reached him and hooked my arms under his, they focused on me with a slow, salt-blurred surprise.

His mouth moved. No sound came out, but I knew the shape of it. *You?*

*Me,* I would have said, if water allowed words. *Who else owes you this much?*

Kicking for the surface with a drowning man in your arms is a lesson in the difference between strength and stubbornness. He was twice my weight and the river wanted him the way the Wood wanted everything lately — thoroughly, patiently, forever. Twice the current tore at us and spun us, and twice I felt that vast gentle tug begin again, downward, homeward, and the whispering swelled with something that was almost welcome, almost sweet, almost the sound of being *known* —

— and I did the one thing I had sworn never to do with anyone. I spoke my true name into the water.

Not the ghost-name. Not *little ghost, no one, nothing.* The name Maren gave me at my mother's grave, the name I had buried so deep even I visited it rarely, the name that was the single thread the Wood could follow straight to the loom of me. I spent it like a man throwing gold to a wolf. Three syllables, lost instantly to the roar of the river, heard by no one — I made sure of that, mouth full of flood, voice smaller than a bubble — but *meant* at the top of my soul, and meaning, Maren always said, is the whole engine of hedge-craft. A rite is a truth performed. And the truth was:

*I am someone. Someone is pulling him up. Someone refuses.*

Whether it was craft or fury or just the blind arithmetic of muscle, we rose. The white ceiling broke above us. Air hit my lungs like a slap, and then gravel under my knees, and then Doss on his side coughing river out in great retching heaves while I knelt over him shaking, my secret name ringing in my own ears like a cracked bell.

On the bank, ten paces away, the crew stood in a line and stared. Tam's crossbow was up but drifting, aimless as a weathervane. Brack blinked at Doss the way Harrowmere's grandmother had blinked at me — recognition arriving from very far away, knocking politely at a door no one had answered in weeks.

And something in the water behind them — I saw it only for a heartbeat, a pale seam in the current, a long attention passing beneath the ford — something noted my name, or tried to. Felt along the river for it and found, I think, mostly static. Mostly the ghost. But not entirely. Not anymore. A thread had shown, silver-brief, and been paid out again, and whatever held the ledger downriver knew now that the thread existed.

Costs. There were always costs.

* * *

Doss sat up as the sun came through, weak and watery, and looked at me for a long time.

Nine years I had rehearsed this moment — usually at night, usually unable to sleep — and in every rehearsal he killed me or I ran. Instead he spat Greywater, wiped his mouth with the back of his hand, and said, hoarse as gravel:

"Why?"

One word. It held the hanged men, and the snow, and the broken ankle, and every route I'd sold out from under them, and it held, underneath all of that, the question a drowning man asks the whole world.

"I'm tired of the books balancing," I said. My voice came out wrecked. "Somebody has to leave a page blank sometime. Might as well be me."

Doss stared. Then he laughed — a short, broken bark, half cough, the laugh of a man whose humor had been buried with better years — and it was such a human sound in that salted, whispering place that two of the crew flinched, and Tam lowered his crossbow all the way, and Brack sat down abruptly on a rock as if his strings had been cut.

"You always were a strange one, little ghost," Doss said. He got to his feet, swaying, and I saw now what I hadn't seen on the scree: the grey at his temples crusted faintly white, the salt-frost creeping in at the edges of him, the whispering having already gotten its fingernails under his skin. His eyes were still his own. They were the only things that were entirely his own. "Strange enough to save a man who came to kill him. Strange enough to be worth listening to." He jerked his chin at the river, at the white seam still moving beneath the ford, patient as debt. "So talk fast, and tell me why my crew can't remember why they're angry, and why I dreamed of a green forest all night with my eyes open."

Ilsa came limping up the gravel, soaked, shieldless, alive, and stood at my shoulder — my prisoner-guard, my ally, my witness — and gave Doss the flat look she gave kings and liars alike.

"Sit down," she said. "Both of you. It's a long story, and it ends at a place called the Weeping Wood."

At the word *Wood*, every man on that bank shivered at once, like a single beast, and far downstream, beneath the singing water, something turned over in its sleep and began, very quietly, to count us.
