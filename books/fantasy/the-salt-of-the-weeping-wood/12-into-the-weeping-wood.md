## 12. Into the Weeping Wood

The trees began to bleed a mile before the Wood proper started.

Paloma saw it first as a dark line on the trunks of the birches along the salt road — seams of moisture that caught the grey light like knife edges. He touched one and brought his fingers away red-brown, then watched in the wind as the color paled to white. Brine. The trees were weeping salt water, slow as sap, and the smell of it hung in the air: old tide, dead fish, the inside of a shell left too long in the sun.

Ilsa rode half a length behind him, her warden's cloak drawn tight against the mist that came off the ground in slow coils. She had not spoken since dawn. Neither had he. There was a particular silence that grew between people who had agreed not to trust each other and then spent three days discovering they worked well together anyway, and it was heavier than any quarrel.

"That's not rain," she said at last.

"No."

"Say what it is, smuggler. I've crossed this border my whole life and I have never seen trees bleed."

"You crossed it with wards standing." Paloma wiped his hand on his knee. "The Wood was always hungry. Maren said so once — she said the wards weren't built to keep the Wood out. They were built to keep its attention off us. A blindfold over one eye of something enormous." He looked up at the sky, which had been starless for eleven days now, a flat pewter lid pressed down over the world. "We took the blindfold off. Now it sees."

"And it's crying," Ilsa said.

"It's *feeding*," Paloma corrected quietly. "Crying is just how it digests."

They left the horses tethered at the last shepherd's stone — the animals would go no closer, stamping and rolling their eyes, and Paloma did not blame them. From there the road simply stopped, the way roads do when no one has needed them in a hundred years, and the Weeping Wood stood up out of the mist ahead of them like a wall built by grief.

It did not look wrong, exactly. That was the worst of it. The trees were tall and grey-barked and ordinary in shape, beech and black alder and the pale ghost-birches of the high country. But they stood too close together, and they stood *still* — no wind moved in that canopy, though the branches overhead were full of leaves trembling without cause, shivering like wet dogs. And everywhere, from every seam in every trunk, the brine seeped and ran and gathered in pools at the roots, so that the forest floor glimmered faintly, as if the ground itself were sweating.

Ilsa dismounted first. She checked her sword, her knives, the royal writ wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, and then — a small gesture she probably thought he hadn't seen — the unsent letter in its inner pocket, soft-edged from years of carrying.

"Your brother," Paloma said.

"Don't." Her voice was flat as the sky. "Don't use what you know about me to make this easier for you."

"I wasn't going to." He slung his pack. "I was going to say you should decide now whether you want it with you in there. Because the Wood will find it. It finds everything you're carrying. That's how it hunts."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she buttoned the pocket closed again and set her jaw. "With me," she said. "Everything I carry stays with me."

He nodded. He understood. Some things you did not surrender even to save your life — especially not those.

They stepped across the threshold together.

* * *

The silence inside was total.

Not quiet — Paloma knew quiet; he'd grown up in the hush of hedge-craft and mountain snows. This was different. This was the silence of a room after someone has stopped speaking mid-sentence, forever. Their boots made no sound on the brine-slick leaves. When Ilsa drew her sword an inch to check the blade, the scrape of steel was swallowed before it finished. Even their breathing seemed to happen somewhere outside themselves.

And above them — through gaps in the motionless canopy where no stars should have been visible at all — there were stars.

"They're underground," Ilsa whispered, and even whispering felt like shouting. "Look. Under the roots."

She was right. Between the pale root-systems, where the ground showed through in hollows and washouts, points of light burned far below — constellations reflected in deep water, or perhaps *growing* there, cold and patient. The Salt Stars, Paloma thought, remembering Maren's page. *The stars went down into the water and the water learned their names.* Above, the sky stayed empty. Here, buried beneath the forest, the heavens persisted — dim, inverted, drowned.

"The Wood didn't put them out," Ilsa said slowly. "It took them down. It's keeping them."

"Maren wrote that it feeds on grief. On loss." Paloma crouched beside a root-hollow, careful not to touch the light. "What's the greatest loss anyone can imagine? Everything missing from the night sky. It didn't destroy them. It hoarded them. Like a magpie that takes only the things that shine." He straightened. "Which means they can be taken back. Which means Maren's rite might actually—"

He stopped.

Ahead, maybe thirty paces into the gloom between the trunks, someone was standing. A figure in a warden's oilskin, broad-shouldered, sandy-haired, with the easy slouch of a man who has never once doubted that the law is on his side.

Ilsa made a sound Paloma had never heard from her — small, cracked, involuntary.

"Tomas," she said.

* * *

The figure turned. It had Tomas Vane's face — Paloma had never seen it, but he knew it instantly from the way Ilsa's whole body leaned toward it, the way a compass needle leans north. Younger than her by two years, if the letters she'd mentioned in sleepless camps were true. Drowned six winters ago in the Greywater crossing, chasing a smuggler's raft in flood season, and Ilsa had been the one who ordered the pursuit. He smiled at her now, and river-water ran steadily out of his hair, out of his sleeves, pooling around his boots.

"You shouldn't have come back here, Ils," the thing said, in a voice like water over stones. "You always blamed yourself. You didn't need to. But you did, and I heard you, all these years, and I thought — why waste it? Why let all that grief go to nothing?"

"Stop," Ilsa said.

"It wasn't your fault, Ils. Come and tell me it wasn't your fault. Say it out loud, here, where it matters, and I'll believe you, and then you'll finally be able to sleep—"

"*Stop.*"

Her voice cracked across the still air like a whip, and for one heartbeat the figure flickered — its outline stuttering, brine-light showing through its chest — before it steadied and kept smiling, patient, terrible, waiting.

Paloma stepped forward and put himself bodily between his companion and her dead brother.

"It's not him," he said, low and fast. "It doesn't know anything Tomas knew. It knows what *you* know. It's a mirror wearing a memory. Every word it offers you is a word you've already said to yourself at three in the morning."

"I know that." Her hand on his shoulder was shaking. Her voice was not. "I know that, Paloma. Move anyway. Let me look at him."

"You said everything you carry stays with you. Don't give it this."

For a long moment she didn't move. The thing wearing her brother's face waited with an awful gentleness, hands open, water dripping. Then Ilsa Vane did the hardest thing Paloma had ever seen anyone do: she looked at her dead brother, memorized him one more time — he could see her doing it, taking inventory of the face, the slouch, the smile — and turned her back on him.

"Warden," the thing said behind them, and its voice changed, flattened, became something older and colder, "we will speak again. You are not done grieving. No one who enters is done grieving."

The forest floor swallowed the sound of their footsteps as they walked away. Ilsa did not look back. Tears cut clean lines through the salt dust on her face, and she let them fall without wiping them, as if acknowledging them privately were the only concession she would ever make.

"He used my name," she said finally. "My milk-name. Nobody living knows it."

"The Wood does. The Wood knows everything we know." Paloma glanced sideways at her. "That's what I tried to tell you. It isn't attacking us. It's *reading* us."

"Then it will read you next." Her eyes, red-rimmed, found his and held them. "You've got more locked up than anyone I've ever met. What happens when it opens you?"

He didn't answer. Ahead, the trees thickened, and the drowned stars beneath the roots burned brighter, and somewhere far off — or very near, it was impossible to say — something began to hum a lullaby in Maren's voice.

* * *

It came for him at dusk, if dusk meant anything in a place with no sky.

They had made camp in the hollow of a lightning-split alder, back to back, a hedge-fire burning low and green because Paloma fed it salt-marsh weed and it gave almost no light, which was the point. They ate hard bread and dried plums and did not talk about Tomas Vane. Around them the trees bled their slow bright tears, and beneath the roots the stolen stars turned in their drowned courses, and the silence listened.

"Tell me about Maren," Ilsa said, when the quiet had gone on too long. "The woman who taught you. You talk about her like she's scripture."

"She was—" He stopped. Started again. "She took me in when I was eleven. Found me stealing eggs from her henhouse and instead of setting a charm on me to blister my hands, she sat me down and taught me to mend the charm on her fence instead. Said punishment teaches you fear, but craft teaches you worth." His throat tightened. "I repaid her by running. When the wardens came asking questions about the caravan routes, I ran rather than stand and face it, and I told myself it was strategy, self-preservation, keeping her out of it — but it was cowardice dressed up clever, and she paid for it, whatever happened to her, because I wasn't there."

"You don't know she paid for it."

"I know the Wood took her memory piece by piece to buy time for a counter-rite, according to her own notes. I know she's in there right now not knowing my name." He stared into the green fire. "So yes. I know."

Ilsa was silent a moment. "When we find her," she said — not *if*, and Paloma noticed, and was grateful, and hated that he was grateful — "she won't remember you. Are you prepared for that? Truly?"

"No," he said honestly. "But I'm prepared to do it anyway. There's a difference. Most people never learn it."

The fire guttered.

And the forest, which had been waiting with the patience of water wearing down stone, opened him.

* * *

There was no transition. One moment he sat by the green fire with Ilsa's shoulder warm against his; the next he was nine years old, standing in the doorway of his mother's house in the fen-country, watching men in crown colors carry out the loom, the pots, the cradle he was too old for but had never let her burn. Debt collectors. His father's debts, his father's flight, his mother's shame — and Paloma had hidden in the rafters because she'd told him to hide, *don't let them take a bond-child, Paloma, don't let them register you,* and so he'd watched her kneel on the bare floorboards and sign herself into service rather than give them her son's name.

His mother's voice, saying the words he had built his whole life on top of:

*"Never tell them your name. A boy with no name belongs to no ledger. Be a ghost, my love. Ghosts survive."*

He had obeyed her for twenty years. He had mended charms under false names, walked smuggler roads as nobody, signed nothing, claimed nothing, loved no one openly, and called it freedom, and called it survival, and never once admitted — not in the deepest cellar of himself — that what he was really doing was making sure no one could ever again watch him be taken away and do nothing.

The memory shifted. The Wood was not content with the past; it wanted the wound, not the scar. He saw himself at Maren's gate, three years ago, pack on his shoulder, Maren standing in the doorway with her grey braid and her sharp kind eyes, and he heard himself say *it's safer if I go alone* — and he saw Maren's face as she understood that her apprentice, her almost-son, was choosing invisibility over staying, choosing the ghost-life over being known, and he saw that she let him go because she loved him and not because she agreed —

"Paloma."

Ilsa's voice. Real. Rough. Her hand gripping his wrist hard enough to bruise, her face inches from his, the green fire blazing unnaturally high and the trees around them streaming brine like open wounds.

"You were gone," she said. "Four minutes. Your eyes were open and you were somewhere else and the fire was reaching for you — the flames bent toward you like they were listening. I've been shouting your—" She stopped. Frowned. "I don't actually know your name. Do I?"

The question landed in the silence like a dropped coin.

"No," he said. His voice came out sanded flat. "No one does."

"What did it show you?"

"Nothing I'll thank it for." He pulled his wrist free, gently, and fed the fire another handful of marsh weed until the green flame steadied. "It showed you your brother. Fair's fair — ask me tomorrow, on the road, in daylight. Not here. Nothing confessed in this place stays private. I can feel it drinking every word."

Isla studied him for a long moment — the warden measuring the criminal, the survivor measuring the survivor — and whatever she saw, she accepted the deferral. She resettled against the alder bark, sword across her knees.

"For what it's worth," she said to the fire, "whoever taught you to hide taught you well enough that it nearly killed you tonight. So whoever you really are — the name underneath all of it — I hope he comes out of this Wood alive. I think I'd like to meet him."

Paloma said nothing. But beneath the fear and the exhaustion and the salt-stink of the bleeding trees, something in his chest shifted — a hairline crack in a wall twenty years in the building, letting through a thin draft of something that was not quite pain.

* * *

They slept in turns, and the Wood let them.

In the deepest hour, when Ilsa kept the watch and Paloma drifted in the shallow gray sleep the forest permitted, he spoke without knowing he spoke — one word, barely a breath, shaped by whatever dream the drowned stars were spinning behind his eyes.

Ilsa Vane heard it.

A name. Soft, unguarded, worn smooth by twenty years of hiding — a name that fit him the way a key fits a lock it was cut for. She sat very still in the green firelight, watching his face slacken into youth, into vulnerability, into the boy his mother had ordered to be a ghost, and she understood suddenly, completely, the size of what he carried and what it would cost him to speak that word aloud in front of the assembled court, in front of the crown whose ledgers his mother had feared.

She could arrest him on it. A true name, freely given in evidence — no writ, no trial needed. The thought passed through her like a fish through clear water and was gone.

Instead she banked the fire, drew her cloak around them both against the brine-damp cold, and said nothing.

Above the trees, the empty sky pressed down. Below the roots, the stolen stars turned. And somewhere ahead, deeper than either of them had yet gone, a woman with no memories hummed a lullaby over a rite half-finished, waiting — without knowing it — for the apprentice she could no longer name.

At first light, they found the trees ahead stripped bare of bark for a hundred yards, and carved into the naked white wood, in a hand both of them recognized, a single sentence:

*THE POOL REMEMBERS WHAT THE SKY FORGOT. COME DOWN BEFORE SOLSTICE. — M.*

"She's alive," Paloma breathed.

"She's bait," Ilsa said — but she was already checking her blades, already turning toward the dark mouth of the descending path the message marked, where steps of black stone went down beneath the roots, toward the drowned stars, toward the Weeping Pool, toward the heart of everything that grieved.
