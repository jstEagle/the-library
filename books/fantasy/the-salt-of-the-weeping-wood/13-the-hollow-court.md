## 13. The Hollow Court

They took her at the treeline, where the Weeping Wood began its long dark argument with the sky.

Ilsa had seen it coming — had felt the wrongness of the ground for half a mile, the way a warden feels a snare before her boot finds it — but there had been nowhere else to go. Behind her, the salt road crawled with figures that had once been travelers. Ahead, only the Wood, and somewhere inside it, answers. She had chosen the Wood because the alternative was standing still, and standing still was how people became those white, patient shapes she'd left kneeling in Harrowmere's lanes.

The salt-wights came out of the trees wearing warden armor.

That was the detail that undid her. Not their faces — faces she could have managed, even faces like wet chalk with brine weeping from the eye sockets. But the armor. Patrol-leather and half-plate of crown make, the kind issued from the armories at Corvenholt, each piece stamped with the sunburst of the royal warrant. Some of the suits were new. One of them still bore a name-plate scratched into the pauldron: *Brannoc*. She had trained Brannoc. She had drunk with Brannoc two seasons past, in a wayhouse outside Thornfell, while he complained about his boots and wrote letters home by candlelight.

"Brannoc," she said, because saying names was a hedge-craft too, Maren's craft, the craft Paloma had been teaching her around dead fires in ruined chapels. "Corporal Brannoc. Fourth patrol, eastern reach."

The thing wearing him tilted its head. Salt crusted in the seams of his gorget like frost on a window. When it spoke, the voice was Brannoc's voice worn down to its grain, a voice used as a tool by something that had never needed one.

"Warden Vane," it said. "You are expected."

* * *

They did not bind her hands. That frightened her more than irons would have. They simply walked her into the trees, and the trees closed behind them like water over a stone, and she understood that running had already been decided against — not by her, but by the forest itself, which rearranged its paths the way a man rearranges furniture in a house he owns.

The stars were down here. That was the other wrongness she could not stop cataloguing, because cataloguing was all she had left of herself. Above ground, since the night the sky went black, there had been nothing overhead but an unbroken lid of dark. But here beneath the canopy, between the bleeding trunks, stars burned — hundreds of them, thousands, hanging in the air among the branches like lanterns in a drowned ship. They gave no heat. Their light fell without weight, silver and cold, and where it touched the brine-slick roots, the salt glittered like crushed glass.

"They're ours," Paloma had said, three nights ago, hunched over Maren's stolen maps. "The stars. The Wood took them. That's why the sky went out — it didn't lose anything. It was *robbed*."

She thought of that now, walking through a forest lit by stolen light, and made herself look at the stars directly. Made herself remember they were evidence. Evidence could be carried out. Evidence could be sworn to.

Her brother's letter pressed against her ribs where she'd hidden it inside her coat, wrapped in oilcloth, against her heart. Tam's last letter, never sent, found in his quarters after the drowning — no, not the drowning; after the *disappearance*; the crown had never given her a body to bury, only a drawer full of effects and a form to sign. She had read it so many times the ink had begun to fade along the fold-lines, and she knew whole passages by heart the way other people knew prayers.

*Ilsa — if you're reading this, I didn't come back from the Wood, and you should know the last thing I thought about was—*

The letter always ended the same place: unfinished. Whatever he'd thought about last, the sentence held it like a fist holds smoke. For six years she had imagined endings for it. For six years she had carried it across every mile of border the wardens patrolled, and told herself it was sentiment, and known it was something closer to a wound she kept open on purpose because closing it would mean admitting nothing could heal it.

*You are expected,* the wight had said.

She wondered, with a coldness that surprised her, whether the thing in the capital knew about the letter. Whether it had read Tam's heart the way she'd watched it, from across a throne room, read the court's.

* * *

The hollow court sat in a clearing that was not a clearing.

It was a hall, or had been — roof beams of black timber arched overhead, though no walls stood between them and the trees, and the floor was root-laid stone furred with moss that wept when stepped on. Great candles burned along the aisles, except they weren't candles; they were tapers of grey salt, burning with a low blue flame that gave off the smell of low tide, of harbors after fish-failures, of the specific grief of things that dry out.

And at the end of the hall, on a throne of woven roots, sat the king.

Not the king-thing of the capital — or rather, yes, the same, but here it did not bother with borrowed flesh. Here it wore what it actually was: a figure of translucent salt, tall as two men, seated in the shape of majesty, its surface crawling with slow rivulets of meltwater that ran downward and fed the moss. Where a face should be there was a suggestion of one, smoothed and featureless as sea-worn glass, and yet Ilsa felt — horribly, unmistakably — that it was looking at her with attention, with interest, with something that in any other creature she would have called *kindness*.

Around the throne knelt more wights. Dozens. Wardens, soldiers, couriers, a bishop whose vestments had gone stiff with crust. Her country's missing, arranged in worship.

Warden training said: assess the threat, count the exits, control your breathing. Hedge-craft — Paloma's lessons, Maren's inheritance — said: salt feeds on grief; do not give it yours; name what you feel before it names you.

So she stood in the ruined hall under stolen stars, in front of a god of salt, and named what she felt.

*Afraid,* she thought. *Afraid, and grieving, and so tired.*

The salt throne gleamed.

*Ah,* she thought. *That was a mistake.*

"You carry a letter," the king-thing said. Its voice did not come from its face. It came from everywhere — from the candles, from the weeping moss, from inside her own skull, spoken in a tone like tide going out. "A letter from Thomas Vane. Warden. Lost in this Wood six years ago. You have carried it against your heart through every winter since."

"Don't," Ilsa said. Her voice came out steady, which astonished her. "Don't say his name. You don't get his name."

"I have his name." The rivulets on the salt body quickened, silver threads running down like tears running down a face. "I have everything of Thomas Vane. His last morning. His fear of deep water, which he conquered every day he wore the badge. The song he hummed when he mended charms, badly. The letter — oh, warden, the letter. He finished it, you know. Before the end. He spoke it aloud, here, hoping the trees would carry it to you. The trees are mine. I heard every word."

The floor seemed to tilt. She locked her knees and stayed standing through sheer, stubborn warden discipline, and hated that the creature could see the effort, could taste it, the way it tasted everything.

"He finished it," she repeated. Flat. A warden taking testimony.

"He did." The salt face inclined, courteous, monstrous. "Would you hear it?"

* * *

Later — much later, walking beside Paloma on roads that smelled of brine — she would try to explain to him what those four words did to her, and find that language failed her, that there was no honest way to describe being offered the one thing you wanted most in the world by the one thing you trusted least. He would nod anyway. He understood hunger disguised as duty. He had lived on it for years.

But that was later. Now there was only the hall, and the blue candlelight, and the silence stretching out like deep water.

Here is what she knew, and she made herself hold it up and look at it, hard, the way you inspect a blade for flaws: the thing on the throne lied the way rivers run downhill. It had hollowed a king and worn him. It had emptied Harrowmere of its own children's faces. Every gift it gave, it gave as a hook gives purchase to a fish.

Here is what she also knew, and could not put down: Tam had gone into this Wood and never come out. Six years of not knowing had carved a Tam-shaped hole in her, and the hole had its own gravity, and she was falling toward it even as she stood still.

"Hear it," she said. "Then tell me the price."

The salt body laughed — a sound like a wave collapsing on shingle, soft and vast and empty.

"No price," it said. "A trade. Fairer than any your crown has offered you. You have hunted the runaway apprentice — Okonkwo, the smuggler, the ghost of the Thornfell paths. He travels with you now. I have seen you together, two thieves on one road, each pretending not to notice the rope between you. Bring him to me. Bring me the boy who carries Maren's maps and Maren's craft, and I will give you back your brother."

The hall went very quiet. Even the moss stopped weeping.

"Not his corpse," the king-thing continued, gentle as a mother settling a child. "Not a memory, not an echo. The Wood keeps what it takes, whole and warm and waiting. Thomas Vane kneels in my court as you see these others kneel — asleep, preserved, the salt holding him at his last living moment like amber holds a fly. Speak the word of release, and he walks out of these trees with you. He will finish his letter in your presence. He will grow old. He will complain about his boots. All of it, warden. Everything the drowning took from you, returned. In exchange: one boy. A runaway. A traitor to his crew, a thief of roads. Tell me honestly — is he worth more to the world than your brother?"

And there it was. The single breath. The beat the chapter of her life would turn on, though she couldn't know it yet.

She thought: *Tam.* She thought of his handwriting slanting uphill when he was tired. She thought of the way he'd stood in the wayhouse doorway the last time she saw him alive, backlit, raising two fingers off his brow — not quite a salute, just *there, sister, present*, the way he always signed himself in letters. *Present.* Six years of absence, and she could buy the end of it. One word. One betrayal. One boy who mended her patrol's charms with clever fingers and never once charged her, who looked at her sometimes across a campfire with the expression of a man reading a map he was afraid to follow.

She thought: *he is twenty-three years old and he ran away because staying was killing him, and I am the law, and the law does not sell people.*

She thought: *the law. Whose law? The law of a hollowed king? The law written by the thing that sits in front of me wearing majesty like a coat?*

And that — the vertigo of it, the floor dropping out of the one certainty she had left — that almost did it. Not temptation. Despair. If the law was hollow, if the crown was salt, then what was she loyal *to*? Why refuse? Refusal required something solid to stand on, and everything solid had turned out to be a costume.

The salt face waited. Patient. It had waited six years for Tam; it could wait out one warden's heartbeat.

Ilsa reached inside her coat and drew out the letter.

She held it up between two fingers, oilcloth glinting, and let the blue candlelight fall across it, and watched the salt throne lean forward — actually lean, the rivulets running faster, the whole vast body straining like a dog at a leash — and she understood suddenly, completely, in one cold flash of clarity, why it wanted Paloma and why it played this game at all.

It couldn't take the letter. It could take kingdoms, kings, memories, stars — but it could not take what was freely held and refused to open. Grief was its food, and grief freely surrendered was a feast, and grief defended was ash in its mouth. Maren's notes had said it in her crabbed hedge-hand: *what it cannot make you give, it cannot have.*

"You want him," Ilsa said slowly, "because he knows the unbinding. Because Maren taught him, and Maren is in your Wood right now doing something you can't stop. And you want *me* because I'm the fastest knife you've ever seen aimed at him."

The salt body went very still.

"So here is my counter-offer," Ilsa said, and her voice did not shake, and she would remember that with pride for the rest of her life. "You keep your court. You keep your candles. And I keep the letter — unopened, unread, unfinished — because my brother's last words belong to *me*, not to you, and I will hear them from his living mouth or I will die carrying them. And Paloma Okonkwo walks out of this Wood with me, and we go and wake the man you're wearing in the capital, and then, salt-thing, I am going to come back here with the full law of a restored crown behind me, and I am going to take my brother out of your amber myself."

She lowered the letter. Tucked it back against her heart, where it belonged.

"And if you can't accept that," she said, "then take me instead. Right now. Add me to your collection and lose the only person who can deliver the boy — because he'll never trust another soul enough to walk into the Wood, and you know it, and that knowledge must burn worse than any candle you've got."

The silence stretched. One breath. Two. Somewhere in the dark beyond the hall, the trees creaked like ships.

Then the king-thing smiled — or performed the shape of a smile, cracks spreading across the salt face in a curve — and said, softly:

"Barely, warden. That was barely a refusal. I tasted it. You were mine for half a heartbeat, and half a heartbeat is a long time in the deep places. Keep your letter. Keep your boy, for now. But know this—" and the voice dropped until it was inside her bones, tidal, certain, "—when you stand at the last choice, and you will, you will choose again. Everyone does. The second time is harder."

The candles guttered. The stars dimmed. And the clearing tilted sideways, roots shifting beneath her feet, and Ilsa felt the Wood begin, gently and irresistibly, to move her — not toward the exit, but deeper.

Toward whatever the forest wanted her to find next.

* * *

The trees released her at dawn — or what the Wood offered as dawn, a grey-green thinning of the stolen starlight — at the edge of a pool she did not recognize, in a part of the forest the maps showed as blank. Her legs ached as though she'd walked all night, and perhaps she had; the wights had gone somewhere in the small hours, dissolving back into the brine-mist, leaving her alone with her heartbeat and her fury.

She was still standing there, getting her bearings, deciding which lie of a path to try first, when she heard voices.

Two of them. Low. Arguing quietly, the way people argue who have learned that loudness costs.

One voice was Paloma's.

The other was older, cracked, worn smooth as river stone — a woman's voice, and beneath the wear something achingly familiar, the cadence of someone who had taught half the hedge-craft on the border, whose crabbed handwriting covered the maps in Ilsa's saddlebag.

"—told you not to come," the old voice was saying. "I left the trail so you wouldn't have to. So you could live your invisible little life and stay out of the deep water."

"Maren." Paloma's voice broke on the name, and Ilsa froze behind a trunk of weeping bark, unseen, unwilling to intrude on something that sounded like a man finding his way home to a house that no longer remembered him. "Maren, it's me."

A long pause. Then, gently, terribly kindly:

"I'm sorry, child. Who's Maren?"
