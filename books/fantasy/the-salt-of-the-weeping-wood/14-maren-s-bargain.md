## 14. Maren's Bargain

The Wood changed its mind about him three times before he found her.

That was how it worked here — Paloma had learned this much in the first day of walking beneath trees that bled brine down their bark in slow, glistening threads. The Weeping Wood did not simply hide things. It *reconsidered* them. A path he had walked an hour past would be gone when Ilsa turned to look back at it; a clearing where they'd slept would be a thicket of black saplings, dense and wrong, as though the forest had grown a denial overnight. The trees did not move. That was the worst of it. They stood perfectly still and simply were not what they had been.

"Stop doing that," Ilsa had said to the forest on the second morning, in exactly the tone she used on smugglers who lied to her about tolls. It hadn't helped.

But on the third day, the paths stopped lying to him. He couldn't have explained how he knew. It was like walking into a house where someone had rearranged the furniture for your benefit — the corridors opened ahead of his boots with a courtesy that made his skin crawl. The Wood was guiding him. Or testing whether he would follow.

He followed. What else was there? Behind them lay the salt roads and Doss's crew, the warden's writ with his description inked on it, the whole kingdom going quiet and white at its edges like paper left out in frost. Ahead lay whatever Maren had wanted him to find, if the altered maps meant anything at all, if the annotations in that stranger's-yet-familiar hand weren't just one more cruelty the Wood invented to feed itself.

The trees wept harder the deeper they went. Brine ran down the trunks in channels worn smooth as old scars, pooling between the roots in shallow basins that reflected no sky — only stars, hundreds of them, burning cold and clear underground while the real heavens above stayed blind. Paloma kept catching himself looking up at the root-cellar dark and then down at the pools instead, drinking starlight from the ground like some inverted creature. His mother had told him stories about places like this. She had also told him stories about honest kings, so perhaps her authority was limited.

"You keep stopping," Ilsa said behind him. Her hand never strayed far from her sword now. In the Wood, salt-wights wore warden armor; he'd seen her face when they passed the first set of tracks, bootprints pressed in crusted white, each print bearing the crowned-sword stamp of the crown's patrol. "You hear something."

"I don't hear anything," Paloma said. "That's the problem. There are no birds. No insects. But there's—" He stopped, listening again, because it came again: a sound too small for the silence around it, rhythmic, patient. Scratching. Like a quill on parchment, or a fingernail tracing letters over and over into stone. "There. You hear it?"

Ilsa tilted her head. Her breath fogged even though the air wasn't cold, exactly — it was *empty*, air with nothing living in it to warm. "Writing?"

"Someone writing." His mouth had gone dry. Three days he had let himself hope without naming the hope, because hope in Thornfell was a debt collectors always found you eventually. Now the debt was coming due, and he was afraid of the interest. "Maren wrote everything. Recipes, charms, complaints about my handwriting, the price of tallow. If anyone survived out here, she'd be—"

"Don't finish sentences you can't stand to finish," Ilsa said. It was almost gentle, which from her was more alarming than a drawn blade. "Walk."

* * *

They found the cottage at the bottom of a hollow that shouldn't have existed, ringed by willows whose branches trailed in water that fell upward, droplets rising from the pool to vanish among the stars below the roots. The cottage was built of the same black wood as the trees, and it wept too — brine streaking its walls in long pale runnels, crusting the windowsill, furring the doorframe with crystals that caught the drowned starlight and scattered it back broken.

But there was light inside. A lamp. Real firelight, guttering amber through salt-frosted glass.

Paloma didn't remember crossing the hollow. One moment he was standing at the rim with Ilsa's warning half-spoken — *it could be bait, boy, think* — and the next his hands were on the door, pushing through a crust of salt that gave way with a sound like breaking ice, and then he was inside, in warmth, in the smell of beeswax and dried yarrow and lamp-oil, and every year since he ran away collapsed at once into nothing.

She sat at a table by the window, writing.

"Maren."

She looked up.

For a heartbeat — one full, merciful heartbeat — she was entirely herself. The same sharp gray eyes under brows like hedge-thorns. The same ink stain on her right middle finger, permanent as a birthmark. The same expression she'd worn every time he came through her door muddy and bleeding and lying about why: a look that saw straight through him and decided, every time, to love him anyway.

Then the look moved on. Passed over him. Settled somewhere near his shoulder, polite and unfocused, the way one regards a stranger who has entered a room too quickly.

"Mind the threshold," she said. Her voice was hers, but slower, each word placed down carefully like a cup that might spill. "The salt takes what crosses without invitation. Sit, if you're sitting. Don't drip on my pages."

"Maren, it's me." He took a step and stopped, because up close he could see what the lamplight had hidden, and seeing it was like swallowing something too large. Her left arm was salt. Not salted, not crusted — *was*. From the elbow down it was a limb of translucent crystal, faintly luminous, and through it he could see the shape of the bones, or what had been bones, rendered in white mineral like a specimen in a glass case. Her hair had gone half-white, not with age but with mineral, strands of it glittering where they touched her shoulders. And her eyes — when they blinked, the blink came a fraction late, as if the lids had to consult something first.

"It's me," he said again, uselessly. "Paloma."

Something flickered across her face. Recognition's ghost, knocking at a door that had been sealed from the other side. "That's a nice name," she said. "Was it your mother's choosing?"

The words went through him like a fishhook. He heard Ilsa stop dead in the doorway behind him, heard her breath catch — she knew, by now, enough to know what that question cost.

"You don't remember me." It wasn't a question. Questions required the possibility of a different answer.

"I remember everything," Maren said, with a small, precise irritation that was so completely her that he nearly laughed and wept in the same breath. "That's rather the point. I remember every charm I ever laid, every rite I ever drafted, every notation system I ever invented, including the one I invented for people who needed to send secrets through hostile country. I remember all of it perfectly." She lifted her salt arm and turned it slowly in the lamplight, watching the starlight swim in the crystal. "It's everything else I've been spending. Faces. Years. My sister's laugh — I know I had a sister; there's a hole shaped like a sister where she used to be. The taste of plums." Her voice didn't waver. That was somehow worse. "The boy who ran away. I know there was one. I've written myself notes about him." She nodded at the table, at the pages stacked there in drifts, covered edge to edge in that familiar spiky hand. "But notes aren't remembering. Notes are just grief with footnotes."

Paloma sat down across from her because his legs made the decision without consulting him. The chair was the same chair. There was a burn mark on its arm from the night he'd knocked a candle reaching for the honey jar, eleven years old, terrified of the scolding, and she'd laughed instead and said *now the chair has a name too.*

"What bargain," he said. "Who did you bargain with?"

"The keeper." She said it the way farmers said *the flood* or *the fever* — a word for something too large to argue with. "The old thing under the pool. It holds the Wood together, such as it is, keeps the hunger of it pointed outward instead of in. But it's bound to the deep roots, can't leave them, and the thing wearing the king has been picking at the bindings for years. So I offered a trade. Time to finish the counter-rite, paid for in—" she gestured vaguely at her own head, at the holes in it— "this. It eats memories the way the Wood eats grief. Same appetite, different dish. I give it a memory a day and it gives me a sunrise. We're both quite satisfied with the arrangement, I'm sure."

"A memory a day." Paloma's voice cracked on the arithmetic. "How long have you been here?"

"I don't know," Maren said simply. "I stopped keeping count of days after I realized I was counting days I wouldn't remember having counted." She pulled a page toward her, dipped her quill — her left hand was still flesh, barely — and made a note in the margin of whatever rite she'd been drafting. Then, without looking up: "Sit still, boy."

His heart slammed once, hard. "You said you don't—"

"I said notes aren't remembering." Now she did look up, and those gray eyes fixed on him with an intensity that had nothing absent in it at all. "But I've watched you since you came through that door. You put your hand on the chair arm, on the burn, before you sat. You knew it was there. Nobody knows it's there. And you flinched when I mentioned the boy who ran away, the way people only flinch at their own wounds." She set the quill down. "So either you're him, come back after all these years, or you're something wearing his guilt, and I confess I don't know which possibility frightens me more."

Behind him, Ilsa shifted. "Madam," she began, in her warden voice, "we've come about the king—"

"He's dying," Maren said flatly, "or being eaten, which is the same thing with better ceremony. Yes. I know. Why do you think I'm here?" She pushed back from the table, and Paloma saw her sway slightly, see her salt fingers brace against the wood. "The counter-rite needs three things. A caster willing to be unmade — I've provided that; I'm mostly margins now, the text of me went to the keeper months ago. It needs the unbinding spoken in a voice that the kingdom owes truth — I'll get to that. And above all it needs the true king's buried name, the one the salt-thing swallowed whole when it crawled into him, because a binding unravels from the knot, and the knot is the name."

"Where's the name?" Ilsa asked.

"In the drowned heart," Maren said. "Under the Weeping Pool. The keeper holds it — has always held it; that's most of what a keeper *is*, a lock that walks. It won't give the name up freely. Nothing down here does. Everything costs, and the currency is always the same." Her gaze slid to Paloma, and there was something unbearably tender and unbearably distant in it, a mother's love arriving secondhand, through notes, through inference, through the shape of a flinch. "Whatever it asks of you — and it will ask, it asked me, it will ask *you* — understand that it doesn't lie about the price. That's the horror of the place. Only the honest bargains survive here."

Paloma looked at her salt arm. At the pages, hundreds of them, a life rewritten in margins. At the woman who had taught him every charm he knew, who had mended his broken wrist when he was nine and his spirit when he was thirteen, and who now looked at him the way one looks at a kind stranger in a difficult season.

"Do you want to know?" he asked quietly. "Your boy. Do you want me to tell you about him?"

The quill stilled in her fingers.

For a long moment the only sound was the brine ticking against the windows, drop by drop, patient as a clock made of water. Then Maren shook her head, and smiled, and the smile was the realest thing in the room.

"No," she said. "If I fill the holes with borrowed stories, I'll spend the rest of what I am trying to believe them. Better I should work with what's true: that he existed, that he was loved, and that he came back — because whoever you are, you crossed the Weeping Wood to sit in a dying woman's kitchen, and that's answer enough to any question worth asking." She turned back to her pages, and her voice hardened into the brisk command he remembered from a hundred botched lessons. "Now. Both of you. Sit. The counter-rite has a speaking-part for a warden — yes, I know what you are, child, you wear your office like armor and it chafes exactly where armor chafes — and I've only got one sunrise left to teach it in."

She drew a fresh sheet toward her, and as she wrote, her quill slowed, and slowed, and stopped.

She frowned at the page. At the last line, written in her own hand, in ink already dry: *the keeper's price is a keeper.* Beneath it, smaller, added later, the letters shakier: *someone must stay. find out who before you go down. do NOT let him volunteer blind.*

Maren stared at the warning as if she'd never seen it before — because, Paloma understood with a cold turning in his chest, she hadn't. She had written herself instructions she could no longer read as anything but another stranger's hand.

"Well," she said at last, briskly, terribly. "That's ominous. Someone want to tell me who 'him' is?"

Paloma looked at the page. At the word *him*. At the keeper's price, waiting at the bottom of the Weeping Pool like a coin at the bottom of a well.

"That part," he heard himself say, "I think I already know."
