## 15. The True Name of the King

The fragments did not want to be read.

Maren's notes had always been difficult — her hand cramped small, her shorthand a thicket of hedge-signs and alchemical abbreviations that only an apprentice who had ground her inks and copied her ledgers for six years could hope to untangle. Paloma had untangled them for two days now, kneeling in the hollow beneath the Weeping Pool's outflow where the guardian's roots made a room of the dark, reading by the light of a candle stub he guarded like a heartbeat. The pages were salt-stiff. They crackled when he turned them, like frost on a morning puddle, and several had gone translucent at the edges, as though the salt were slowly drinking even the memory of the ink.

But the last intact page — the one Maren must have written in her final lucid hour, because the letters were enormous, gouged into the paper rather than written upon it — was simple enough that a child could have read it.

*THE RITE NEEDS A NAME THAT WAS BURIED. NOT YOURS. HIS.*

Paloma read it eleven times. Each time, the words rearranged themselves into a worse shape.

Below it, in the smaller, fading hand — the hand of a woman already trading herself away piece by piece — came the rest: *The king was unmade at his own hearth. They took him apart the way you take apart a charm, thread by thread, until nothing remained but the shape of him. But a true name cannot be destroyed, child. It can only be hidden. The Wood hides things in its drowned heart. Beneath the pool where all grief drains. The keeper keeps it. The keeper has always kept it.*

*Ask the old thing under the roots what it costs to be given a kept thing. It will tell you true. It has never learned to lie. That is why it is so lonely.*

Paloma set the page down with hands that would not quite obey him. The candle guttered in air that tasted of brine and old sorrow. Somewhere above him, muffled through fathoms of root and stone and weeping water, he could hear Ilsa's boots on the shore — pacing, always pacing, a woman who trusted silence the way other people trusted locks.

He should go up. He should tell her now, immediately, before the dread could calcify into secrecy. That was the lesson of this whole cursed Wood, wasn't it? Every secret kept here became salt. Every truth withheld fed the thing that wore the crown.

And yet his mouth would not open around it. Because the page said *not yours*, and Paloma Okonkwo — no, not that name either; that name was a coat he'd stolen off a dead man in Corvane's Reach, and he had worn it so long he sometimes dreamed in it — knew exactly whose buried name the rite required, and he knew what it would cost to fetch it, and he was already, in the wordless way of the heart, bargaining for a way out.

*Not your life,* the page seemed to say, in Maren's voice, in the voice she used when she caught him cheating at cards. *Worse. Your choice.*

He climbed the root-ladder anyway.

* * *

Ilsa heard him before she saw him — the particular rhythm of his climb, quick and then hesitant at the top, as if the light up here offended him. She had catalogued his rhythms over these weeks on the road the way she'd once catalogued patrol routes: dawn habits, fire habits, the tells of his lying (he touched his left cuff), the rarer tells of his honesty (he went very still, like a bird deciding whether to flush).

He was still now. Standing at the edge of the shore where the Weeping Pool lay flat and grey as hammered tin, holding a scrap of salt-stiffened paper as though it might go for his throat.

"The keeper talked," she said. Not a question. She had watched him descend two days ago and had spent the intervening hours doing what she did instead of praying, which was making lists. Lists of what they knew. Lists of what they didn't. Lists, increasingly, of the things he wasn't telling her.

"The keeper talked." He came to stand beside her, close enough that she could see the salt dust in his hair, silvering him prematurely at the temples. "The rite needs a name. The true king's name. The one they buried when they hollowed him."

"Where?"

"You know where." His mouth twisted. "Beneath this. The drowned heart. The pool."

Ilsa looked out across the water. It did not reflect the sky, because there was no sky here worth reflecting — the canopy of the Weeping Wood closed overhead like a lid, and yet through some trick or wound of the place, stars showed beneath the surface, dim and many, a sky drowned upside-down. She had stopped trying to explain it. In Thornfell she had learned that explanation was often just fear wearing a scholar's hat.

"Drowned," she repeated. "As in—"

"As in someone goes down after it." He said it lightly. Too lightly. She heard the scaffolding under the lightness, the beams of a lie being assembled in real time.

"And you were going to tell me when?" Ilsa asked. "After you'd slipped away in the night? You've done it before. You're good at it. It's practically a trade skill."

"I wasn't—" He stopped. Pressed his lips together. And then, to her genuine surprise, he laughed — a short, bitter, honest sound. "Yes. All right. I was going to tell you from the bottom of the ford at Vell's Crossing, with a note pinned to a tree saying *gone to do the brave thing, don't follow.*"

"That's the note I'd expect," Ilsa said. "It's the note a coward writes who thinks he's a hero."

His head came around sharply. For a heartbeat she thought she'd finally gone too far, that the prickly, slippery, infuriating man would bolt into the trees and she'd lose him to the Wood and the realm to the salt-thing both. But he didn't run. He stood there taking the blow, and she saw something move behind his eyes that she recognized from the mirror: the look of a person who has been called by their worst name and cannot entirely dispute the charge.

"You're right," he said quietly. "That's exactly the note I'd write. Which is why—" He looked down at the fragment in his hand. "Which is why I'm standing here instead. Telling you first."

Progress, she thought. From him, it felt like a parade.

"There's more," he said. "There's something else, and before I say it, I need—" He exhaled. "I need to ask you something, and I need you to answer honestly, because I think we've reached the part of this road where dishonesty kills people. Harrowmere killed people. The caravan on the pass killed people. Everyone in this story who kept a secret has fed that thing on the throne, whether they meant to or not."

Ilsa's hand went, without her willing it, to the inner pocket of her coat — the pocket where her brother's unsent letter lived, soft as cloth now from handling. Tam's letter. *Dear Ils, I've taken the warden's oath too, isn't that a laugh, we'll be on opposite borders and never meet except at midwinter—* She had carried it through three years and two hundred miles and had never once told Paloma what it was. Never told anyone. Duty sharpening into obsession, obsession curdling into something she refused to name, and all of it wrapped around a piece of paper she couldn't deliver and couldn't burn.

"Ask," she said.

"Do you know my name?"

The question landed strangely, out of true, like a stair that isn't where your foot expects. "Paloma Okonkwo. You told me on the salt road."

"I told you a name," he said. "A name is a coat. I asked if you know *mine*. The true one. The one Maren gave me at my binding, the one that keeps me invisible to charms and wards and scrying and every warden net ever cast between here and the sea. The one I traded everything to keep. Do you know it?"

And here was the moment. Here was the fork in the road she had been walking toward since the ruined chapel, since the night he taught her the unbinding rite by firelight and she had seen — not guessed, not deduced, but *seen*, laid bare in the memory-shapes the chapel's old stones still held — the truth of him. The stones remembered everyone who had ever prayed there, and when she'd slept against them she had dreamed his dreams: a boy of nine in a burning village, a boy running, a witch catching him up and whispering a name into his ear like a key turning in a lock, the boy folding that name away into the deepest pocket of himself and never, never taking it out again.

She had known for weeks. She had said nothing. She had told herself it was strategy — leverage, in case the bargain soured. She had told herself a great many things, and every one of them had tasted of salt.

"Yes," Ilsa said.

The word came out steady, which surprised her, because inside she felt the way the border wards must have felt the night they snapped.

"How?" His voice was very quiet. Very controlled. She had arrested men with that exact control in their voices, men standing at the edge of the gallows-step.

"When you taught me the rite in the chapel. The stones there hold memories. I slept against them. I dreamed—" She stopped, made herself finish, made herself give him the whole ugly truth rather than the tidy version. "I dreamed your binding. I saw Maren speak it. I saw where you put it. I've known since the chapel, Paloma. Weeks. I told myself it was leverage. I told myself I was waiting for the right moment. Those were lies, and I'm telling you they were lies, because you asked for honesty and you're going to get it even if it costs us everything."

The pool lapped at the shore. Underwater, the drowned stars burned on, indifferent.

"You read it," he said. "You read my name out of my own sleeping head, and you sat across fires from me for weeks and let me keep thinking I was safe."

"Yes."

"One word, Ilsa. One word from you and every ward in the kingdom opens to me like a door. One word spoken aloud anywhere near a charm-caster and I'm a naked man in a snowfield. You held my life in your mouth for weeks."

"I know."

"And you didn't use it." He wasn't asking anymore. He was working it through aloud, the way he worked through Maren's ciphers, testing each joint. "Why not? Leverage that good doesn't keep. Every smuggler knows that. Spend it or lose it."

"Because it wasn't mine to spend." Ilsa turned to face him fully. "Because a name isn't evidence, it's a person. Because—" Her hand found the letter in her pocket and pressed it flat, an anchor. "Because I know what it is to carry something you can't put down. I've carried my brother's last letter for three years. Unsent. Unsendable. He wrote it the day before the salt took the eastern garrison, and I have read it so many times I can recite it, and I have never once been able to do the one thing it asks, which is to be delivered. So yes. I know what a kept thing weighs. I would not add my weight to yours. Not like that. Not secretly."

For a long moment Paloma said nothing. Then he did the last thing she expected: he sat down. Right there on the salt-grey stones at the pool's edge, cross-legged, like a boy at a hedge-witch's knee, and looked up at her.

"Say it," he said.

"What?"

"My name. Say it. To me. Here, where the only witness is a pool full of drowned stars and a forest that eats secrets." His jaw worked. "If this alliance is going to stand on anything, it should stand on the truth. Both directions. So say it, and then I'll decide — properly, awake, with my eyes open — whether I forgive you. Not because you didn't use it. Because you're using it *now*. Giving it back."

Ilsa knelt, because it seemed wrong to say such a thing standing over a man. The pool's breath was cold against her face. She thought of Tam's letter, of all the undelivered truths in the world, and she leaned close and spoke the name — his true name, the nine-syllable hedge-given name that had kept him ghost-thin and uncatchable since he was nine years old — softly, into the space between them, giving it back the way you return a borrowed knife handle-first.

The air changed. She felt it happen, felt some thin membrane somewhere in the world part, felt the Wood itself lean in and take note. On the pool's surface, one of the drowned stars flickered — brightened — as though something vast below had opened one eye.

Paloma shivered once, head to foot, like a horse shedding rain.

"So that's what it feels like," he murmured. "Being seen. Sixteen years. That's what I ran from, and it turns out it's just — cold air on skin." He laughed, wet-eyed, appalled at himself. "I built a fortress around one word and the gate was this easy to open. All you had to do was knock."

"Are we forgiven, then?" Ilsa asked. "Or is that a debt you'll collect later, when it's useful?"

"Both," he said, and grinned, and it was the first grin she'd seen on him that wasn't armor. "That's what makes it honest. Forgiveness with interest. That's how smugglers do contracts, and apparently how friends do them too." He said the word *friends* carefully, like setting down something fragile, and she pretended not to notice how much the pretending cost him.

* * *

They went down together to the hollow beneath the roots, and Paloma spread Maren's fragments on the flat stone the guardian kept cleared for them — a courtesy that still unnerved him, the way the ancient thing arranged its domain around their needs without ever being asked. The candlelight moved. The roots overhead breathed, slow as a sleeper's ribs.

"It needs the king's buried name," he said, tapping the gouged letters. "Hers says it plain. And the keeper holds it — has held it since before crowns, maybe. Since the first grief the Wood ever drank." He hesitated. "There's a line here I haven't shown you. About what it costs to be *given* a kept thing."

"Show me."

He turned the page toward her. Below Maren's final instructions, in handwriting gone almost transparent, almost eaten: *Nothing kept is free. The keeper does not give — the keeper trades. Ask what the price is before you dive, child, and believe the answer, however terrible, because the old thing cannot lie and will not soften it.*

"The guardian already told me some of it," Paloma said. "When I asked about the counter-rite. It said the name could be released to a bearer." His finger traced the root-shadow on the stone. "It said the bearer must carry it out of the drowned heart in the only vessel the Wood trusts. Living memory. Someone has to *hold* the king's name — take it into themselves, all of it, everything the name means, every year and burden and grief bound up in a crowned life — and carry it up through the water and out of the Wood without letting any of it spill."

"That sounds survivable," Ilsa said warily. "What did it cost?"

"It didn't say. That's the part I have to go down and ask." He met her eyes. "It wants to tell me alone. Says the question changes depending on who hears the answer. Hedge-law. Even Maren's notes mention it — some truths are load-bearing, and you can't show them to everyone at once or the whole structure comes down."

Ilsa was quiet for a long moment. Above them, through the canopy they could not see, the starless night pressed down like a thumb on the world. Two hundred miles east, in a throne room hung with salt-white banners, a ceremony was being prepared. The queen's cipher had named the date: solstice. Four days. Perhaps fewer now — time moved strangely in the Wood, and neither of them fully trusted their count.

"Then ask," she said at last. "And whatever it tells you — however terrible — you bring it back up that ladder and you say it out loud. No fortress. No coat. No note pinned to a tree." She tapped the salt-stiff page, over the words about the old thing that could not lie. "That's the contract. Honesty with interest. Both directions. Remember?"

"Remember," Paloma said.

He stood at the pool's edge and began, with the unhurried care of a man performing his last familiar ritual, to strip off his boots and coat. The water waited, grey and patient, its inverted stars burning beneath the surface like a promise or a threat. As he waded in to his knees, Ilsa saw him pause — saw him look back at her, and mouth something she could not hear over the weeping of the trees.

Then the pool took him, and the drowned stars closed over his head like a door.

Ilsa Vane stood alone on the shore with a dead woman's fragments, an undelivered letter, and four days — and did not pray, because prayer was a kind of asking, and she had already asked for everything she needed.

She counted his heartbeats by feel, across the water, the way wardens count the steps of prisoners they have decided to trust.

One. Two. Three.

Down in the dark, something old began to speak.
