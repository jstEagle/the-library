## 16. Descent Through Grief

The Weeping Pool did not ripple.

That was the first thing wrong with it. Wind came off the Wood in long salt-laden gusts, bending the brine-bleeding trees until their branches groaned like ship's rigging, and yet the pool lay beneath it all flat as poured pewter, holding a sky full of stars that did not exist above. Paloma stood at its edge and looked down at constellations he had navigated by for six years — the Ploughman, the Salt Crown, the Weeping Sisters — burning in water where no honest star should be.

"They're underground," he said. His voice sounded small against the hush of dripping trees. "Maren wrote that. *The stars are visible underground though none shine above.* I thought it was a figure of speech."

"Most of what she wrote probably wasn't." Ilsa crouched at the pool's rim, warden's coat dark with spray that fell upward from the bleeding branches. She had tied her hair back with a strip of Maren's map — a scrap of redrawn coastline, ink still faintly wet after twenty years, which was its own kind of blasphemy — and her face in the starlight was the face of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. "How deep?"

"No one knows. The drowned dead keep it. That's what the fragments said." He turned Maren's last page over in his hands, the one with her cramped notation spiraling inward toward a single word: *heart*. "They test you. The rite needs a name freely given at the bottom, but they don't let just anyone reach the bottom. They take everything you're carrying first. Everything you've told yourself."

Ilsa was quiet a moment. Above them, deeper in the Wood, something howled — not wolf, not wind, but the sound salt makes when it remembers being sea. The wights had been tracking them since the threshold, patient as tide.

"So it strips you," she said. "And if there's nothing left when you get down—"

"Then there was nothing to begin with." He tried to make it a joke. It came out sounding like a eulogy.

She stood, and for once she didn't arm herself with law or wit or the flat grey stare that could stop a smuggler mid-stride. She put her hand on his shoulder instead, brief and hard, like setting a splint. "I'll hold them off up here. However long it takes. You come back up, apprentice, or I swear on my writ I'll arrest your corpse for obstruction."

"That's the kindest thing anyone's said to me in years."

"I know." Her mouth almost moved. "Go."

He stripped off his boots, his coat, the bundle of Maren's notes wrapped in oilcloth which he pressed into her arms along with every other thing he owned. That left him in his shirt and trousers and nothing else — no tools, no false papers, none of the thirteen names he'd worn like coats over the years. Just himself, which was the one cargo he'd spent half a lifetime trying not to carry.

Paloma Okonkwo stepped into the Weeping Pool.

The water took him without a splash, the way a secret takes a confession.

* * *

It was warm.

That was the second wrongness, and somehow the worse one. Mountain pools in Thornfell were cold enough to stop a heart; this water closed around him gentle as bathwater, thick as milk, lit from below by those impossible stars so that he sank through layers of soft blue glow. His ears filled. His hair lifted. Somewhere far above, muffled to nothing, he thought he heard steel ring against salt — Ilsa keeping her promise already — and then even that was gone, and there was only the descent, and the waiting shapes.

They came up to meet him out of the dark like questions rising to the surface.

They were people. That was the horror of it — not skeletons, not wraiths, but people, whole and sodden and serene, drifting in slow spirals with their eyes open and their mouths gently smiling. A woman in a fisherwoman's oilskins. A boy who might have been twelve. An old man whose beard floated around his head like a halo of grey weed. Their skin had gone the white-grey of salt crust, but their eyes were bright, and every one of them fixed on Paloma as he sank past, and every one of them spoke — not aloud, he realized, but directly into the water, into his bones, into the place behind his ribs where he kept the things he never said.

*Why did you run?*

"I didn't," he said, and bubbles tore the words apart. "I had to. Doss would have killed me if I'd stayed. The crew blamed me for the Amberholt job, two of them hanged because of my route, my maps—"

The fisherwoman drifted closer. Her smile didn't change. The water around her tightened like a hand.

*You left before the hanging.*

The warmth went out of the pool in an instant. Cold closed on him now, true mountain cold, the cold of the ravine above Amberholt on the morning the crown's men strung up Ferro and little Wren from the alder bridge while Paloma watched from three ridges away, hidden, safe, invisible. He had told himself for six years that he hadn't been able to help. That he'd been too far. That there had been no time.

He had been two hours' walk away. He had known the warden patrol's schedule better than the warden did. He could have cut them down in the night with the same knowledge that let him slip every toll and trap in the mountains — and he had sat in the dark instead with his arms around his knees, telling himself the oldest lie in the smuggler's catechism: *there was nothing I could do.*

"There was," he said to the water. It accepted the words without comment and pulled him deeper, past the boy, past the old man, down toward a glow below that was not starlight.

*Why did you leave Maren?*

This lie was older, softer, worn smooth as a river stone from handling. "She sent me away," he said. "She said I'd learned all she could teach. She wanted me to go."

The drowned dead gathered around him now in a slow wheel, and the water showed him things — not images exactly, more like temperatures. The night he'd packed his satchel while Maren slept. The note he hadn't left, because a note meant words, and words meant someone could follow them back to him. The way she'd stood in the doorway the next morning — he'd seen her from the ridge, he'd stopped to look back, he'd always insisted to himself that he hadn't looked back — standing in her doorway with her hand raised, not waving, just raised, held up in the grey light like a woman hailing a boat that was already too far out to hear her.

"You were angry at her," the dead said together, in voices like water over gravel. "Tell us why."

"She lied to me." His chest was burning now; the air in his lungs had gone stale hours ago, or minutes, or years. "My whole childhood, she — the hedge-craft, the charms, the unbinding rites — she taught me everything except who I was. Except why my name had to be hidden. Except what we were hiding *from*. She made me a ghost and called it protection."

*Yes,* said the dead. *And you thanked her by becoming very good at disappearing.*

The truth of it hit him harder than the cold. He hadn't run from Doss, not really. He hadn't run from the crown. He had run because Maren's silence about his own name had become unbearable — because living beside the locked door of yourself is worse than any prison — and because hiding was the one craft at which he'd ever excelled, and pride is just grief wearing a working man's clothes.

"I hated her for it," he admitted, and the wheel of dead turned faster, gentler. "I hated her and I loved her and I left without saying either, because saying it would have meant staying, and staying would have meant asking the question, and she'd never answer it, and I couldn't bear—" 

His lungs seized. The water pressed in close and kind and absolute.

*One more,* the dead said. They had stopped moving. All of them hung around him in the blue glow, faces tilted, patient as the sea. *The last one. The one you tell yourself every morning when you wake. The one that lets you get up and be no one again.*

And Paloma, sinking, drowning, emptying, found it waiting where he knew it would be, at the very bottom of himself: *No one is looking for you. No one wants you found. Being nobody keeps you safe.*

"That's not a lie," he whispered into the water. "That's survival."

*Is it?* said the drowned woman, and her smile finally changed — softened, saddened, became the smile of a mother watching a child touch a hot stove for the tenth time. *Safe. Six years of safe. And the night the wards broke, when every soul on that caravan needed warning — you ran alone. Because no one is looking for you. Because wanting to be found would mean risking being caught.*

The stars below burned brighter. He was nearly at the bottom now, and he understood suddenly that the drowned dead did not hate him, did not punish him. They only held up mirrors, and waited, and grieved — because they had all, each of them, once been someone who preferred the comfortable lie to the costly truth, and the Wood had collected them for it, or they had collected themselves here, he could no longer tell which, and perhaps there was no difference.

"If I let go of it," he said — of the lie, of the namelessness, of the whole architecture of invisibility he'd built plank by plank across six winters — "if I say it, truly say it, down here where it counts — I won't be able to hide anymore. Not ever. Not from wardens, not from smugglers, not from myself. Everything I am will be visible to anyone who looks."

*Yes,* said the dead, with terrible tenderness. *That is what the rite requires. That is what it has always required. Truth given freely costs everything except itself.*

Above him, faint as memory, steel rang on salt again. Ilsa. Still holding. Still spending herself on a promise to a criminal she should have clapped in irons in chapter one of their acquaintance. If she could stand in the open under an empty sky and fight for a liar, then he could stop lying in the dark.

"My name," Paloma Okonkwo said to the drowned dead, to the water, to the stars that shone only underground, to Maren asleep in a doorway twenty years gone, "is Paloma. Dove. My mother named me for the bird that finds its way home over any distance, through any storm, because she believed I always would. And I have spent my whole life proving her wrong, and I am done. I am Paloma Okonkwo. Find me. Here I am."

The word left him like an arrow leaving a bowstring, and the recoil nearly broke him open.

The pool convulsed. Every drowned face turned toward him at once — and then, slowly, like frost melting off a window, they smiled wider, wider, wider, and dissolved. Not violently. They simply came apart into brightness, into salt, into light going home, and the water around him cleared from milky blue to something ancient and transparent, and Paloma saw the bottom.

There was no bottom.

There was a garden. Roots thicker than castle towers descended through the flooded dark, braided and vast, and among them, in a hollow of living wood, something moved — something old, older than crowns, older than the kingdom it kept, coiled around a single point of light that pulsed like a held breath. The king's buried name, kept in the dark by a keeper older than kings.

And as the last of the drowned dead faded around him, as his burning lungs passed the point where burning stops and something quieter begins, Paloma felt the great shape in the roots turn its attention upon him — vast, weary, curious, hungry in the way of things that guard rather than devour — and he heard it speak, in a voice like the first rain after drought:

*A diver. At last. Come down, little dove. Come and see what I keep — and tell me what you would give to carry it away.*

The light in the roots pulsed once. Twice. Like a heart deciding whether to go on beating.

Paloma swam toward it, and his air ran out, and the dark took him kindly by the hand.
