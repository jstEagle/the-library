## 17. The King Beneath the Roots

The pool did not want him to breathe.

That was the first thing Paloma understood as he sank past the light, past the last gray shimmer of the surface where Ilsa's face hung above him like a moon he had once betrayed — the water was not water in any way that mattered. It was memory with a weight to it. It pressed against his chest not like drowning but like being *remembered*, thoroughly and without mercy, by something that had all the time in the world.

He kicked downward anyway.

Maren's rite still burned on his tongue, the syllables she had traded her whole self to leave him, and they glowed faintly in his mouth even now, a warmth of hedge-craft against the cold. *Breathe when it lets you,* she had written, in the fragment Ilsa had read aloud by torchlight above. *The Wood gives breath to those who carry truth. It takes it from those who lie.*

So he did not lie. Not aloud. But the pool heard everything, and every fathoms-length of descent peeled back another layer of the story Paloma Okonkwo told himself about himself.

*I ran because Maren would have made me stay.* The thought rose unbidden, silvering in the dark like a fish turning, and the water tightened around his ribs until he answered it honestly: *No. I ran because staying meant being known, and being known meant being held, and being held meant I could be hurt by someone who knew exactly where to press.* The pressure eased. He sank faster.

*The smugglers deserved what came to them.* Tightness, crushing, black spots blooming at the edge of his vision. *No. Two men hanged because I left the ledger open where the wardens' spies could find it. I told myself it was carelessness. It was rage. Doss laughed at my mother's name once and I never forgot, and I let them pay for his laughter.* The water loosened its grip, almost gently, like a hand that had expected worse.

He was passing through a forest now, though he had left the true Weeping Wood far above. Roots descended around him like the pillars of a drowned cathedral, pale and salt-encrusted, trailing filaments that stirred at his passage. Small lights moved among them — not fish, not quite. They were shaped like memories he recognized: a lantern swinging in Maren's kitchen window; a boy's bare feet on a mountain path; the particular quality of starlight over Thornfell before the night everything went dark. Each light flickered as he passed, leaning toward him like faces in a crowd, and each one dimmed again when it saw what he carried — nothing. No name. A hollow where a self should be.

*Almost there,* said the water, or something speaking through the water, in a voice like tide withdrawing over shingle.

His lungs were beginning to burn in earnest now. Below him, impossibly deep, there was a glow: not the cold white of salt but a warm gold, buried like an ember under ash, like a hearth someone had tended faithfully for years and then been dragged away from.

Paloma swam toward it.

* * *

The floor of the Weeping Pool was not mud. It was bone-white stone, veined with brine, and in its center stood a tree.

It had no business growing here, in the lightless cold, and yet it grew — vast beyond reason, its trunk wider than Maren's cottage, its roots gripping the stone like fingers holding fast to the edge of a cliff. Its bark wept, as all the Wood's trees wept, but this tree wept gold: slow beads of amber light that rolled down its flanks and pooled at its base, and it was from these pools that the glow came. And within the largest pool, cradled among two roots like an egg in cupped hands, lay a single word.

Paloma felt it before he saw it. The word pulled at him the way the pull of home pulls — not toward a place but toward a person. It was warm and heavy and old, older than the salt, older than the grief, and he understood without being told whose it was. The king. The true king, the man beneath the crown, the name that had been buried here the night the stars went out, hidden so deep that the thing wearing his body above could not find it, could not finish becoming him.

All Paloma had to do was take it.

He reached out —

And the roots moved.

Not violently. Nothing in this place was violent; violence required urgency, and the guardian had none. One vast root simply lifted from the stone with the patience of a glacier and settled between Paloma and the word, and another, and another, until he stood before a wall of pale wood weeping golden tears, and the word was gone behind them.

"You came down carrying no name," said a voice.

It did not come from the tree. Or rather — Paloma looked up, blinking through the stinging brine, and corrected himself — it came from everywhere the tree was. From the bark. From the roots. From the slow gold dripping into the pools. The voice was neither male nor female, neither young nor old, though it contained echoes of both, the way a mountain contains echoes of every weather it has ever endured.

"I'm looking for the king's name," Paloma said. His own voice sounded small and bubbling in the water, absurd, a child shouting into a cathedral.

"Yes." The word rippled through the roots. "You are the third to come for it. The witch came first. She traded well, but she did not come to take. She came only to learn how it might be taken, and to leave the learning behind her, like bread crumbs for a bird." A pause, long as a season. "You are her bird."

"Maren." His throat closed around the name. "Is she alive?"

"She is half-alive, which for humans is often the more painful condition. That is not why you have come, though you think it is. You have come because the word behind my roots is the key to your kingdom, and you wish to carry it up into the light." The golden tears fell faster, softly, softly. "I will give it to you."

Hope flared in Paloma's chest so suddenly it hurt worse than the drowning.

"Freely?" he asked, because Maren had taught him that nothing in the old places was given freely, and things given freely were either lies or catastrophes.

"Nothing is free," agreed the guardian, and the roots shifted, and for the first time Paloma saw what sat within their heart. Not a face. Something like a face — a suggestion of one, formed of salt-crusted wood and drifting filament, with eyes that were two pools of the same amber light the tree wept. It regarded him with those eyes, and Paloma had the vertiginous sense of being looked at by something that had watched the first kings be crowned and the first graves be dug, and had found both occasions equally unremarkable. "There is a price. There has always been a price. You may refuse it. Most who understand it do."

"Tell me."

"I am the keeper." The guardian's filament-hands spread, indicating the tree, the pools, the buried word, the whole drowned dark. "I keep what must not be lost and must not be found. Names. Vows. The true shape of things that the world above has agreed to forget. It is a small duty and an endless one. I took it willingly, long ago, when the Wood was young and the grief it fed on was only the ordinary grief of living things. Someone must sit at the bottom of sorrow, or sorrow rises and drowns everything above." The amber eyes did not blink. "I am tired, little nameless one. I have been tired for longer than your language has existed. I will release the king's name to you — freely, gladly, with blessings — if someone remains in my place."

The words went through Paloma like cold water through cloth.

"Someone," he repeated.

"You," said the guardian, without cruelty. "Or no one. And then the word stays where it is, and the thing wearing your king finishes its meal at the solstice, and the stars stay dark, and the villages go on forgetting themselves until there is no one left who minds." The root-wall creaked, settling. "I do not threaten. I do not persuade. I state the arithmetic. It has always been thus: a keeper for a keeper. The witch knew it. She began building her counter-rite the moment she understood, and she built it knowing she could not pay the price herself — she had already spent too much of herself buying time. So she sent the bird who owed her everything and owned nothing. She sent the boy with no name, because a boy with no name can become anything."

Paloma floated there, weightless, his lungs screaming, and did the terrible arithmetic.

It was simple. That was the horror of it. All the way down through the lying water he had thought the reckoning would be complicated — trials, bargains, cleverness, some hedge-witch's trick that would let him slip out of fate's ledger the way he'd slipped out of every other ledger in his life. But there was no trick. There was a word behind roots, and a price in front of it, and the price was him.

If he took the name, he saved the king, the queen, the realm, Harrowmere's dying grandmother and her forgotten son, Ilsa Vane with her unsent letter, Doss and his crew of fools and murderers. Everyone. Everything.

And he would sit here. In the cold and the gold and the endless weeping, keeping names that no one would ever come to claim, watching the surface as a distant bright ripple, forever, alone.

"No," Paloma said.

The guardian tilted its suggestion-of-a-head. "No?"

"Not like this. Not — " His voice cracked, bubbles streaming upward. "I didn't dive through every lie I've ever told myself just to make a martyr's bargain in the dark where no one will ever know. If I stay, I stay because I choose it, in front of witnesses, with my name in my mouth. That's the whole point. Don't you see? That's what the Wood eats — grief that's hidden. Grief nobody speaks. If I vanish down here silently, I'm just feeding it one more course." He pressed his palm flat against the root-wall, feeling the slow pulse beneath the bark, ancient and weary. "Give me until the solstice. Let me speak the name, wake the king, stand in the light and be seen — truly seen, name and crimes and all. And then, if the arithmetic still demands a keeper, I'll walk back into the water myself. Willingly. On my feet. Not traded. *Chosen.*"

Silence. Long enough that his vision began to spot and fray at the edges.

Then the guardian laughed.

It was not a sound Paloma could have described afterward — a vibration more than a sound, moving up through his bones, through the stone, through the drowned roots of the world — and there was something in it that might, in a younger creature, have been delight.

"The witch said you would say that," it murmured. "She wrote it, in fact. In her fragments. 'He'll refuse the trade,' she wrote. 'He'll insist on doing it loudly.' She said it was the only thing about you that gave her hope." The roots groaned, shifted — and parted. Just wide enough. Beyond them, the word lay gleaming in its cradle of amber, patient as a sleeping child. "Take it, Paloma Okonkwo."

His true name, spoken aloud for the first time in eleven years, struck him harder than any blow. It rang through the water, through the pools, through the golden light, and every drowned memory-light in the great dark flared at once — and he felt it settle back onto him like a coat he had outgrown and forgotten he loved. Heavy. Warm. *His.*

"How — " he gasped, swimming forward, reaching.

"I keep names. I do not need yours written down to know it." The amber eyes dimmed, almost kindly. "Go. Your air is nearly spent, and the woman above is holding the wights at the threshold with a broken patrol-charm and pure fury, which is a renewable resource in her family, I believe. Take the word. Speak it in the light, before witnesses, as you promised."

Paloma closed his hands around the king's name and lifted it from its cradle, and it was lighter than a coin and heavier than a mountain, and it sank into his chest like a coal sinking into snow.

"One thing more, keeper-to-be." The guardian's voice followed him as he kicked upward, toward the distant gray promise of the surface. "A warning, freely given, since you gave me honesty freely given. When you speak the king's name in that court, the unbinding will demand a balancing truth. That is the nature of rites — they weigh. The witch's counter-rite requires the caster's own name spoken beside the king's. You know this. What you may not know—" the golden light below was fading now, the vast dark closing gently around him like a book, "—is that the rite does not ask. It *takes*. It will drag your name out of you whether you offer it or not. Prepare the ones you love for the sight of you."

Paloma broke the surface gasping, choking, alive, the word burning in his ribs like a second heart.

Above him, on the shore, Ilsa Vane stood ankle-deep in salt-water with her sword drawn and her patrol-charm guttering blue, surrounded by the slow-advancing shapes of the wights — and when she saw him rise, saw the light coming off him like heat-shimmer, her face did something he had never seen it do.

It hoped.

"Paloma," she called across the water. "Tell me you've got it."

He spat brine and grinned with a mouth full of drowning. "I've got the king," he said. "Now get me out of here — we have a court to break into."
