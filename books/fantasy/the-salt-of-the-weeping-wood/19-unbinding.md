## 19. Unbinding

*Paloma*

The smuggler roads had no name for the last mile into the capital, because the capital's builders had never known they existed — a seam of packed earth running beneath the roots of an ancient hedgerow, emerging at last through a collapsed well-shaft behind the tanneries. Paloma came up out of the ground like something buried and not entirely dead.

The city was wrong. That was his first thought, before fear, before purpose: *the city is wrong.* The stars were still gone — three nights now, a sky like a lid over a pot — but the streets below were lit with a pale, sourceless glow that came from everywhere and nowhere, the color of moonlight on salt flats. People stood in their doorways watching it fall on them. Not hiding from it. *Bathing* in it. A woman held her hands up to the light as though it were rain in a drought, and her face was slack with a peace so complete it looked like drowning.

He smelled the Wood before he saw any sign of it: brine and rot-sweetness threading through the smoke of the city, the same smell that had soaked Maren's doorway, Doss's ruined ford, every mile of road since the night the stars went out. It was in the stones now. In the bread. He could taste it on his own tongue, faintly, like tears he hadn't cried yet.

In his breast pocket, wrapped in oilcloth against his heartbeat, the guardian's gift sat cold as a river stone: the true king's name. Four syllables in a dead dialect, given to him by a creature older than crowns in exchange for a promise Paloma still didn't fully understand he'd made. He touched the packet once, twice, a third time, the way you touch a wound to confirm it hurts.

The solstice bells had already begun. Somewhere ahead, past the tanners' row and the weavers' stair, the great doors of the hall stood open, and light poured out of them like water from a breached ship.

He ran.

* * *

*Ilsa*

Ilsa Vane had spent her whole life believing that the law was a wall — that if you pressed your shoulder to it long enough, honestly enough, it would hold. She had crossed dead wards carrying a royal writ, she had walked through villages where mothers had forgotten their children's faces, and she had kept pressing, because the writ said the law reached even here, even now, into the dark.

She had stopped believing it somewhere around the moment she broke into the throne room through a servant's passage, alone, without warrant, committing treason in front of two hundred witnesses.

The court was assembled. Of course it was. The renewal ceremony had drawn every lord and lady who could still walk — drawn them the way a lantern draws moths, except the moths here did not flutter. They stood in ranks, in silk and ermine, utterly still, faces turned toward the throne with expressions of such serene vacancy that Ilsa thought of the crusted caravan in Thornfell, of Harrowmere, of every silent place the salt had passed through. They weren't spectators. They were *reserves.*

And on the throne, the king-thing wore its borrowed body like a coat cut slightly too large. The consumption was nearly finished. Ilsa could see it — see the way the man's edges had gone translucent, the way the salt-light moved beneath his skin like something swimming under ice. His shadow lay across the marble steps, and it was not his shape anymore. It was branching. Rooted. A tree's shadow wearing the memory of a man.

"Renewal," the king-thing said, in the voice of a herald reading weather, "is mercy. Renewal is rest. Grief is heavy, and We will carry it."

"You're not carrying it," Ilsa said, and stepped out of the passage into the open floor of the hall.

Every head turned — all at once, in perfect unison, which was worse than anything. Two hundred faces, smooth as eggs. Only the guards near the doors showed human confusion, and only because they were new, conscripted after the blight began, their forgetting incomplete.

"Ilsa Vane," the king-thing said, and smiled with the king's mouth, which was the worst theft of all. "The loyal hound has come home. You have brought Us nothing. Where is the boy?"

"He's coming," Ilsa said. Her voice sounded small in all that marble, and she let it sound small. Let them think her mad; madness bought seconds. "But first you should hear what your court won't say. Your king is dead or nearly so. Your memories are being eaten. Every one of you has lost something — a child's laugh, a mother's voice, the reason you weep at certain songs — and you've been told this peace is a gift." She turned slowly, addressing the frozen ranks. "It's not a gift. It's a harvest."

Silence. Then, from somewhere in the ranks, a single crack: an old duchess whose lips trembled, whose hand rose halfway to her mouth and stopped, as if reaching for a memory just out of reach. Small. But real.

"Doors," said the king-thing mildly.

The guards moved. And Ilsa, warden of the crown, drew her sword in her own sovereign's hall and prepared to die badly in defense of a truth no one wanted.

That was when the doors themselves burst inward, off their hinges, and Paloma Okonkwo — runaway, smuggler, thief, the boy whose name she had read in his own worst memory and never spoken — came through the wreckage with salt water streaming off his cloak and something blazing in his fist.

"I'm late," he called. "Sorry. The roads are terrible."

* * *

*Paloma*

Later — if there was a later — he would remember the throne room as a held breath. The stillness of the court, the root-shadow crawling on the marble, Ilsa standing alone with her sword up and blood already on her sleeve, and himself, dripping, half-dead, holding the only thing in the world that mattered.

The king-thing looked at him, and the look was hunger wearing patience.

"The apprentice," it said. "The invisible boy. Do you know what you carry, little ghost? A name. Names are grief compressed. Give it to Us and be empty. Be *quiet.* You have wanted quiet your whole life."

Every word landed where it aimed. That was the horror of the thing — it didn't lie. Paloma had built his invisibility brick by brick, name by abandoned name, until no one living could call him home. The entity simply held up the mirror.

"Yeah," Paloma said. His voice shook. He let it shake. "I did want quiet. And then I walked through Harrowmere, and I saw what quiet costs." He looked out at the court, at the duchess with her trembling hand, at the guards who were only men again, confused and afraid. "You don't get to keep people by making them forget. That's not mercy. That's just theft with better manners."

"Unbind him," the thing said to the guards, "and bring Me the—"

"Warden," Paloma said, and turned to Ilsa. "Now. While they're listening. Truth first. It has to be truth first — Maren's notes were clear. Salt feeds on what's hidden. So we starve it in public."

Isla lowered her sword — not in surrender; in ceremony. She planted the point on the marble between them like a boundary stone, like the old border-stones of Thornfell, and she spoke in the clear carrying voice of a woman trained to be heard over wind and war:

"My name is Ilsa Vane. Warden of the Third March. I love my brother Tomas, who died four years ago, and I have carried his unsent letter ever since, and I never sent it because I couldn't bear for anyone else to read it, and I am sorry, Tomas. I am sorry I chose duty over you at the end. That is my truth."

The words fell into the air and *rang*, and where they rang, the salt-light flickered — guttered, like a candle in a draft. Along the ranks of the court, a ripple went through the stillness. Someone gasped. Someone began, very quietly, to cry.

The king-thing's smile curdled. "Stop them," it said, and its voice cracked down the middle, and the guards — the new ones, the human ones — looked at each other and did not move.

"It can't stop us," Paloma realized aloud. "It can't *lie*, and it can't make anyone else do it either. Truth isn't a command. It has to be given." He stepped forward, into the open floor, into the sight of two hundred eyes, and understood with terrible clarity that this was the thing he had run from his whole life: to be seen. Fully. Without cover.

His hands were shaking. He let them shake.

"My name is Paloma Okonkwo." Saying it felt like pulling a splinter from the root of his tongue. "Son of Adaeze and Kwame Okonkwo of the river country, sold north at nine years old, apprenticed to Maren the hedge-witch, who taught me charms and maps and how to mend what's broken. I left her cottage without saying goodbye because I was ashamed of what I'd become, and I have regretted it every day since, and I love her, and I hope she knows it wherever she is." Breath. "I got two men hanged — Bren and Cottar of Doss's crew — by giving testimony to save my own neck. I told myself I had no choice. That was a lie. There is always a choice, and mine was cowardice, and their deaths are mine to carry." Breath. "And my name — the name under everything, the one I hid even from myself, the one that keeps me visible whether I want it or not —"

"Don't," the king-thing whispered, and for the first time it sounded afraid, and its root-shadow writhed on the marble like a thing burning.

"— is *Okonkwo*. Child of the okonkwo-tree, the one that stands when everything else falls. My mother named me for endurance. I spent ten years trying to disappear from a name that means *standing fast*." He laughed, wet and cracked. "Well. Here I stand."

The salt-light in the hall shuddered like a struck bell.

"Now," Paloma said, turning back to Ilsa. "Him. Say it with me. All of it. The name I brought, and everything it belongs to."

Ilsa took the oilcloth packet from his hand. Her fingers brushed his, steady where his shook. And together, warden and outlaw, in front of the assembled court of a kingdom that had forgotten itself, they spoke the true king's name into the salt-thick air — four syllables in a dead dialect, the name his mother had given him before crowns and councils took it away, the name the Wood had guarded in its drowned heart because grief must be kept somewhere and the forest had volunteered.

The effect was not thunder. It was quieter than that, and worse.

The king-thing did not scream. It *drained*. The salt-light bled out of it like dye from cloth held underwater, pouring upward, outward, seeking the absent stars and finding none, dissipating into nothing at all. The borrowed body slumped forward — and caught itself — and straightened, slowly, the way a man does when waking from a fever that has lasted years. The root-shadow peeled off the marble and unraveled into ordinary darkness, an ordinary man's shadow, trembling.

And the king opened his eyes, and wept.

Not the salt-weeping. Real weeping — ugly, human, hiccuping sobs from a hollowed man feeling his own shape fill back in, memory returning to him like blood to a numb limb, and with the memory, everything he had lost: his father's funeral, his daughter's laughter, the taste of the wine at his coronation, the weight of every decision he had ever made and every mistake he had ever made worse. He wept in front of his entire court, and the court — breaking apart out of its stillness like ice going out on a spring river — began to weep with him.

The duchess was calling her son's name. A guard had dropped to his knees. Somewhere in the ranks a woman laughed and sobbed at once, saying *I remember, I remember, I remember* over and over like a prayer answering itself.

Paloma stood in the middle of it, empty-handed, emptied-out, more visible than he had ever been in his life — every eye in the hall capable of finding him now, his name known, his crimes spoken aloud, his ghosts dragged into the light — and he found, to his astonishment, that he was not afraid.

He was lighter. Lighter than the night he'd run. Lighter, maybe, than he had ever been.

Ilsa came to stand beside him, sheathed her sword, and did not take his arm — but she stood close, shoulder almost touching shoulder, which from Ilsa Vane was an embrace.

"You gave them your name," she said quietly. "In front of everyone. You know what happens next."

"I know what happens next," Paloma said.

Above the shattered doors, above the palace, above the whole grieving city, the sky — which had been a lid, a blank, a blindness for eleven nights — began, at its eastern edge, to prickle with light. One star. Then three. Then a scatter of them, like sparks off a flint, catching and holding, while below the court wept and remembered and the true king knelt weeping on his own steps, alive, named, found.

Ilsa looked up at the returning stars, then down at Paloma, and her expression was the one he was beginning to know best: duty sharpened by something warmer underneath.

"Paloma Okonkwo," she said formally, "you are under arrest."

"I know," he said. And smiled.
