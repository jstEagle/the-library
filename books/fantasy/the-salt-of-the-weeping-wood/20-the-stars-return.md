## 20. The Stars Return

They came back one at a time, like lamps being lit in a house where someone had died.

Paloma stood on the palace balcony — he had never stood on a balcony in his life, and the stone under his boots was cold and absurdly smooth — and watched the first star kindle above the eastern ridge of Thornfell. It hung there a moment, uncertain, as if it too remembered it had once been something. Then a second answered it. A third. Within an hour the whole broken ceiling of the world had stitched itself back together, and the light that fell across the capital was the old light, the true light, silver instead of salt-white.

Below him, the city was weeping.

Not with grief. That was the thing no one could explain yet, and perhaps no one ever would: when the king woke from his long hollowing, when the salt-thing came apart on the flagstones of the throne room like a wave collapsing, every person in the realm who had lost a memory to the Wood felt it come home. Paloma could see them even from this height — figures stepping out of doorways, standing in the streets with their faces turned up, mouths open, saying names. Their own names. The names of their children. The name of the road they'd grown up on. Harrowmere's grandmother would wake tomorrow knowing her son's face again, and if her son was dead, she would at least know her grief, which was its own terrible gift.

The stars had gone out because the Wood had swallowed the sky's reflection, Maren had told him once — or rather, her notes had told him, in a hand that wasn't quite hers anymore. *The Weeping Wood is a mirror that learned to drink.* And now the mirror had been tipped upright, and everything it had hoarded was pouring back out into the world.

He heard footsteps behind him and knew without turning who it was. Heels, measured, warden-cadenced. But slower than usual. Ilsa Vane walked like a woman who had spent everything she had and hadn't yet counted what remained.

"You should sleep," she said, coming to stand beside him at the balustrade. There was salt dust still in the seams of her armor. She hadn't let anyone take it off. "You've been awake for three days."

"Have I?"

"Don't be clever, Okonkwo."

"I'm not being clever. I genuinely don't remember." He looked at her sideways. In the new starlight her face was older than it had been a month ago, or maybe just more honest about how old it had always been. "Do you know what I keep doing? I keep looking up. Like a man checking whether a debt is still there. Except the debt is paid, and I don't know what to do with my hands."

Ilsa said nothing for a while. Below them, somewhere in the tangle of streets, a bell began to ring — not the alarm bell, not the solstice bell, but some small parish bell that someone, in the confusion and joy, had simply decided needed ringing. Other bells took it up. It sounded like a city remembering it had a voice.

"The court is asking questions," she said finally. "About you. About the unbinding. About what exactly you spoke aloud in front of two hundred nobles and the entire royal guard." Her mouth twitched. "You made quite an impression, shouting your own name at the throne."

"It seemed only fair. Everyone else got theirs back. Seemed rude to hold onto mine."

"That's not—" She stopped. Rubbed at her eyes with a gauntleted hand, an oddly human gesture. "There will be a trial. You understand that. Whatever you did up there, you also ran contraband through three provinces for six years, and two men are dead because of routes you sold. Doss will testify. He's already asked to."

"I know." And he did know; he had known since the moment he'd pulled Doss coughing and cursing out of the river ford, since the moment mercy had stopped being an abstraction and become a wet, furious man spitting riverwater into his face. "I'm not running, Vane. That's the strange part. I keep waiting for my feet to get ideas and they just — stay planted."

"Good." Then, quieter, almost grudgingly: "The queen asked after you. Personally. She remembers what you said about borrowed phrases. She wants to give you a fair hearing herself."

"Then I'll have one fairer than most smugglers get."

They stood together in silence as the bells worked themselves out. When she left — a touch on his shoulder, brief and hard, the closest thing to tenderness either of them permitted — Paloma stayed on the balcony until the sky was so full of stars it hurt to look at, and then went down into the palace, following directions given by servants who kept stopping to stare at him, because by now everyone in the building knew his face.

*No longer invisible,* he thought, and found the phrase fit strangely, like a coat cut for a bigger man. He had spent six years being nobody, and now he was a name shouted in a throne room, and both states felt like exile. There was a third state somewhere ahead of him. He didn't know its shape yet. But for the first time since the stars went out, he wanted to find out.

* * *

Maren was dying in a room full of rosemary.

They had installed her in the infirmary wing of the palace itself — the queen's insistence, over the objections of physicians who did not understand what they were treating. What did their medicine know of a woman who had traded sixty years of memory to buy the realm eleven days? The hedge-witch who had served the crown in secret, whose cipher ran through royal correspondence like a thread of green through grey wool, lay in a bed far finer than any she'd slept in, and she was dissolving anyway. Slower now. The bargain was broken, the counter-rite complete, the Wood's claim discharged. But memory, once poured out, does not all come home. Some of it had soaked into the ground of the Weeping Pool forever.

Paloma had been sitting with her for two days when the change came.

She had been quiet, mostly — peaceful in the way of the very old and the very tired, watching the window, occasionally murmuring fragments. *Mind the third stone past the ford. Salt before sugar in a binding. Who taught you to whistle like that?* Questions aimed at no one. Once she had looked straight at him and asked if he was the physician, and he had said yes, and she had thanked him gravely for his care, and he had walked out into the corridor and pressed his forehead against the cool stone wall until the shaking stopped.

But on the second evening, as the relit stars came up outside her window, she sat bolt upright.

"Maren?" He was at her side instantly. "What is it? Are you in pain—"

She was staring at him. Not through him, as she had done for two days — *at* him, with eyes suddenly sharp and dark and entirely her own, the eyes that had looked at him across a kitchen table ten years ago and said *you'll do, boy, but only if you stop pretending you're stupid.*

"Paloma Okonkwo," she said.

His heart stopped and started again, badly.

"You grew," she said wonderingly. Her hand came up — thin as winter twigs now, spotted, trembling — and touched his cheek, tracing the line of jaw that had been soft when she'd last truly seen it. "Look at you. Look at your face. I gave away your face, I think. Somewhere along the way. I'm sorry, love, there were so many things and I had to choose what to carry, and I chose the rite, I chose the rite over—" Her breath hitched. "Over everything. Over everyone."

"You chose the realm," Paloma managed. His voice cracked in the middle of it. "It was the right choice. It worked. Maren, it *worked*. The king is awake. The stars are back. Look—" He caught her hand and turned her gently toward the window, toward the blazing field of stars. "You did that. You and me. Us."

"Us," she repeated, tasting the word. And then she laughed — low and rusty and exactly the laugh he had followed through a hundred lessons, the laugh that meant *well done, apprentice, now do it again properly.* "Listen to him. *Us.* You always were greedy with credit."

"I learned from the best thief I knew."

"Thief." She clicked her tongue, but her eyes were shining. "Hedge-witch. Say it right at my funeral or I'll haunt you."

"There will be no funeral. You're going to recover, and you're going to complain about the food here for twenty years, and you're going to teach me the unbinding variations I still don't—"

"Paloma." Her hand tightened on his. The laughter faded, and what replaced it was gentler and worse. "Sit down, love. Let me say the things I have time to say. I can feel the edges of myself going soft again. This clarity is borrowed — the bargain's broken but the ledger isn't balanced, not all of it. I have tonight. Maybe a piece of tomorrow. So sit down and let an old woman spend her coin."

He sat. He held her hand. Outside, the stars burned steady, and neither of them looked at them again.

"I was proud of you," she said. "Before all this. Before I went into the Wood. Did I ever tell you that? Properly, I mean, not just the grudging way I praised your charm-work." Her thumb moved over his knuckles. "You ran away from me. From the cottage, from the apprenticeship, from the whole small life I could offer. And I was angry — I'll admit I was angry, I called you ungrateful to the hearth-god for a solid month — but underneath the anger I was proud. Because you survived out there. Six years, alone, hunted, and you survived on wit and nerve and paths you memorized like scripture. I taught you letters and you taught yourself mountains."

"I taught myself nothing," Paloma said roughly. "Everything I am came out of that cottage. Every trick, every rite, every—"

"Yes, yes, flattery." But her grip tightened. "Let me finish. I'm spending coin, remember, and I won't be interrupted." She drew a breath. "When I understood what was happening to the king — when the Wood showed me the truth of it, in the pool, in the roots — I knew I couldn't fight it alone and I knew the crown didn't know enough to ask me. So I went in. And I left the trail for you. Altered maps, changed notes, the whole sly apparatus of it. I manipulated you, boy. I took your guilt about leaving and I aimed it like a weapon at the Wood. I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm telling you the truth, because truth is the only currency worth anything now — we learned that together, didn't we, in a throne room full of salt?"

"You could have just written to me."

"A letter reaches a man. A trail *moves* him." The old sharpness flickered in her smile. "And I knew you'd come. That's the terrible arithmetic of teachers, Paloma. We make people who can finish our work, and then we must trust them to."

Silence. The rosemary in the room — sprigs of it everywhere, someone's idea of comfort — filled the space between words with its bitter clean smell.

"What happened to you," he said quietly, "in there. At the end. When the rite completed."

"I remembered you." Simply. "That was the payment that mattered, in the end. The Wood wanted grief, and grief needs names, and I had been holding yours safe inside the rite itself — woven into the countersign, where the salt-things couldn't taste it. When the rite broke open, your name came home to me first. Before anything else. Before my mother's face. Before my own." She smiled, small and crooked. "You were the first thing I got back. I hope you know what that means. I hope you never stop knowing what that means."

Paloma bowed his head over their joined hands. He had faced salt-wights without flinching. He had spoken his hidden name before a court and felt it cost less than this.

"I'm going to die," Maren said, in the tone she used for stating the weather. "Not tonight, probably. Soon. The body ran on borrowed time and the loan's called in. Don't argue — arguing wastes coin. Instead, listen. Three things."

He lifted his head.

"One. The Wood is not finished with us. It's older than crowns and patient as water, and a bargain broken is not a war ended. Watch the borders. Watch the wards. Teach someone the old signs — you, personally, teach someone, don't hoard knowledge like a dragon, it's the one sin I never forgave myself." 

"Two. The keeper's choice — I heard, don't look surprised, the walls of that forest talk to each other. You offered yourself. Noble, foolish, very you. And the Vane woman out-lawyered a creature older than the moon. Marry that woman or at least stay friends with her; the realm will need both of you and you're stronger as a pair than as two lonely knives."

"Ilsa is going to arrest me at trial, most likely."

"She's going to testify *for* you, and she'll hate every minute, and she'll do it because it's true, and that woman would walk into fire before she'd walk around a truth. Trust her." 

"And three?"

Maren was quiet a moment. When she spoke again her voice had gone soft, the sharpness set aside, and what remained was only an old woman in a fine bed with a boy she loved kneeling beside her.

"Three. You spent your whole life hiding your name because hiding kept you alive. I understand that better than anyone — I hid mine inside a rite. But a name is not a wound, love. It's a handle. It's how the people who love you get a grip on you. So let them hold it. Let them use it. Be somebody's Paloma, not everybody's stranger." Her thumb traced his knuckles one last time. "That's my blessing. Take it. It's the last spell I know."

He kissed her hand, because there were no words equal to the moment, and hedge-witches understood gestures better than speeches anyway.

"Now go," she said, settling back against the pillows, suddenly exhausted, the clarity already draining from her eyes like tide from a shore. "Go and sleep, and come back tomorrow and be a stranger to me again if it comes to that — I'll forgive you for being unfamiliar, if you'll forgive me for forgetting. Fair trade. We're good at those."

He reached the door before her voice caught him once more, faint and fading, already half-dreaming:

"Third stone past the ford, Paloma. Mind the third stone."

"I'll mind it," he promised the dimming room. "Every crossing. All my life."

And from the bed, almost too quiet to hear, warm with the last of her borrowed light: "*That's my boy.*"

He stood in the corridor a long time, one hand flat against the wall, letting himself cry in a palace hallway under relit stars, and finding — to his astonishment — that grief with a name attached hurt differently than grief without one. Deeper. Cleaner. Like salt that had finally remembered it used to be sea.

* * *

Dawn found him on the training ground behind the warden barracks, of all places, because Ilsa had summoned him there with a note reading only *come armed, bring chalk.*

She was waiting with two practice staves and an expression that brooked no argument. Behind her, the eastern sky was doing things Paloma had forgotten skies could do — piling up gold and rose over Thornfell's distant teeth, lighting the frost on the flags.

"You're teaching me the ward-mending charms," Ilsa said, tossing him a staff. "The ones you did for my patrol. Before you were a criminal. The queen has authorized it — provisional instruction, part of your sentence, whatever the tribunal decides. And I want the old border-signs too. Everything Maren would have taught you. Everything."

Paloma caught the staff, weighed it. Somewhere beyond the mountains, in a green-dark country that bled brine, something ancient was keeping its patience, keeping its accounts. Somewhere in the palace a dying woman was sleeping off the last of her miracle. Somewhere in a cell Doss waited to testify, and in a tower the true king was learning to weep again, and overhead the stars were burning down at all of it like witnesses who refused to look away.

"Everything takes years, Vane," he said. "You'd better plan on keeping me close."

Something that was almost a smile crossed her face — the first he'd ever seen there, and unlikely to be the last.

"That," said Warden Ilsa Vane, lifting her staff, "is between me and the tribunal. Now — show me the third stone past the ford. Start from the beginning."

So he began.
