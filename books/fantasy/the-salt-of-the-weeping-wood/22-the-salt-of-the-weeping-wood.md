## 22. The Salt of the Weeping Wood

The courtroom smelled of beeswax and old stone, and Paloma Okonkwo stood in it with his name spoken aloud like a man standing in rain for the first time in years.

*Paloma. Okonkwo.*

The herald had said it twice, once to announce him and once because a clerk had asked him to repeat himself, and both times the syllables had landed on Paloma's shoulders with a weight he was still learning to carry. For seven years his name had been a coin kept hidden in his boot, spent only in whispers, traded only when no other currency would do. Now it rang off the vaulted ceiling of the King's Bench and came back down to him changed — not smaller, as he had always feared, but larger. A name said in daylight did not fit the same way a name said in darkness did.

"Paloma Okonkwo," the Lord Justice read from the scroll, "late apprentice of Maren Ashwell, hedge-witch of Thornfell; confessed smuggler of salt, charm-iron, and unlicensed remedies across the crown roads of the Thornfell passes; deserter of a caravan on the night of the Blotting; betrayer of the border wards—" The old man paused there, quill hovering. "—and restorer of them."

The pause had been argued about, Paloma later learned. Three days the clerks had debated whether that last clause belonged in an indictment at all.

He stood in the dock in borrowed clothes — a plain grey coat that fit him wrong at the shoulders, because everything fit him wrong now, because he had grown into a shape the world had not seen before. His hands, once quick as weasels on lock and latch and warding-knot, hung quiet at his sides. He had decided, months ago in the dark beneath the Weeping Pool, that whatever happened here he would not lie. It seemed a poor repayment of the drowned dead's instruction otherwise.

"How do you answer?" the Lord Justice asked.

"Guilty," Paloma said, and his voice did not shake, though the word tasted of brine. "To all of it but the last."

A murmur went through the benches. Somewhere near the front, a woman in warden grey did not move at all, which was its own kind of eloquence. Ilsa Vane sat with her hands folded on her knee and her eyes fixed on him with the flat patience she brought to everything — blizzards, ambushes, kings returned from the dead. She had testified for two days already: the caravan, the ravine, Harrowmere, the ford where he had pulled Doss half-drowned out of the river rather than let the river have him. She told it all without softening anything, and then she had added, in the same unsoftened voice, *And every road he ever showed me through those mountains, he showed me true.*

The Crown's advocate rose. He was a thin man with a voice like a filing knife, and he had spent the morning doing his duty well.

"You fled your master's cottage," he recited. "You abandoned thirty-one travelers on the worst night this kingdom has known. You sold knowledge of hidden ways to men who used them to move contraband — and when two of those men were caught and hanged, you saved yourself." He let each item sit in the air like a stone dropped in still water. "Is this accurate?"

"It is accurate."

"And you ask the court to weigh against these things... what? That you helped a warden walk into the Wood and come back out?"

"I ask the court to weigh nothing," Paloma said. "I ask it only to hear me say plainly what I did. The weighing is yours."

That had been Maren's phrase, or near enough. *Don't argue the scale, boy. Just put everything on it.* He had run from her teaching for seven years and here it was anyway, arriving in his mouth at the moment of most consequence, the way her lessons always had.

The Lord Justice conferred with his fellows for a long time. Sunlight moved across the floor. Paloma watched it the way he had learned to watch light in the Wood — knowing it could be trusted today, unsure it always would be.

When they returned, the sentence was read in a voice that had been carefully rehearsed to sound reluctant:

"The crimes are grave. The restitution is extraordinary. The court notes that the prisoner surrendered his own concealment — the one possession that had kept him safe — as the price of the realm's restoration, and that he did so before any promise of mercy was made." A rustle of parchment. "It is therefore ordered: Paloma Okonkwo shall serve the Crown for a term of seven years, being the length of his flight, in the office of Royal Surveyor of Hidden Ways. He will map every smuggler's path, every warden's cut, every forgotten track from Thornfell to the sea, and deliver these maps to the Crown's keeping alone. He will wear no mask, use no false name, and hold no secret road from the law. In exchange, his sentence of hanging —"

Someone in the gallery gasped. Paloma hadn't known until that moment that hanging had been on the table. He looked at Ilsa. Her expression gave away nothing except, perhaps, around the eyes, the smallest tightening — the look of a woman who had fought something behind closed doors and won it by a margin she did not intend to celebrate publicly.

"— is remitted. This is a pardon contingent on service. Let the record show it was argued for by Warden-Captain Vane, and seconded by His Majesty himself."

*Himself.* The true king, three months crowned again, still thin as a winter branch, still weeping sometimes in the middle of audiences without appearing to know he did it. He had sent a letter with his own seal and, oddly, a pressed sprig of rowan, which the clerks could make nothing of but which Paloma understood at once: rowan grew at the edge of the Weeping Wood, the last tree that stayed sweet when all the others turned to salt. The king remembered what had held the line. He wanted it noted somewhere that could not be burned.

"Do you accept the terms?" the Lord Justice asked.

Paloma thought of a great many things in the space of one breath. Doss, alive and surly, hauling timber now above the ford instead of salt below it. Maren's cottage, swept clean, the doorway salt scrubbed away, a new hedge starting green along the fence. The stars, every one of them back in their places, so that the whole kingdom had spent the spring learning to sleep outdoors again just to look at them.

"I accept," he said.

And that was how Paloma Okonkwo, who had spent seven years being no one anywhere, became an officer of the Crown of Thornfell with a salary, a seal, and a room in the mapmakers' hall that smelled of ink and cedar and had his own name painted on the door in letters tall enough to read from across the street.

* * *

The first maps took him the whole summer.

He walked every road he had ever run, in daylight, with instruments instead of instincts, and found that knowing a thing truly was different from knowing it secretly. The hidden pass he had sold to Doss in the ravine — spending that secret like blood — he surveyed properly now, triangulated from three peaks, drawn fair with its scree slopes and its widow-maker cornice marked in red ink. The warden cuts Ilsa had patrolled blind for a decade he mapped for her too, and watched her read them with the expression of a woman discovering the house she'd lived in had rooms she'd never opened.

"You knew this gap in the ridge line," she said one evening, tapping the vellum. "Above Coldermere. Wardens died trying to find that gap in '41."

"Smugglers used it twice a year. We called it the Keyhole." He hesitated. "My crew called it that. Before."

"Before you were a traitor or after?"

"Before I understood what I was trading away when I traded things." He said it steadily, because it was true, and because saying true things aloud had become a discipline with him, like drill or prayer. The drowned dead under the Weeping Pool had made him rehearse every lie he'd ever told himself until the telling wore the lies smooth and he could see the shape of the truth underneath. Some habits survived even miracles.

Ilsa grunted — her highest register of praise — and rolled up the map.

They fell into a rhythm without deciding to. She had leave accrued from eleven years of service and spent none of it resting; instead she requested assignment as escort to the Royal Surveyor, citing, in her application, "the security sensitivity of the materials and the subject's history of flight." Everyone in the warden command understood this to mean she didn't trust him. Nobody but Paloma understood that it meant she trusted him more than she trusted anyone else to see him clearly.

By autumn they had walked the whole spine of Thornfell together. By the first snows, they had reached the southern marches, where the border stones ran down out of the mountains toward the low country, and beyond the stones the land fell away toward a distant smudge of trees that neither of them liked to name aloud but both of them looked at often.

The Weeping Wood was quieter now. Not healed — Paloma doubted it would ever be healed; grief was its food and grief would never want in this world — but fed differently. The counter-rite, finished at last by a king who remembered his own name and spoke it daily as penance, had taught the Wood to take sorrow offered freely instead of stolen. Pilgrims went there now. They wept among the brine-bleeding trees and left lighter, and the trees drank what was given and grew strange pale flowers that smelled, travelers said, like forgiveness.

Maren had gone into the Wood the previous spring, when her remaining time ran out, walking upright and unafraid with her memories restored to her for one final day. She had spent that day entirely on Paloma — on the boy he'd been, the lessons he'd slept through, the trouble she'd always known he'd cause and the good she'd somehow believed he'd do anyway. At the threshold she had turned and given him her blessing, which was four words long and worth more than the pardon.

*"Finish the maps, love."*

So he did.

* * *

On the eve of the anniversary — the night the stars had gone out, one year past — they camped on a high shoulder of the border range where the wardstones caught the last light and threw it back gold.

They had made a small fire, because fires were legal now on the crown side of the stones, because the kingdom was learning to be unafraid of being seen. Ilsa cooked; she cooked the way she did everything, efficiently and without discussion, and the result was edible, which after a year of trail rations counted as luxury. Above them the sky did the thing it still did, the thing Paloma did not think he would ever stop noticing: it filled up with stars.

Every star. All of them, in their ancient stations, thick as salt on a doorstep, bright enough to cast shadows. The astronomers said the sky was the same sky as before the Blotting, that nothing had been lost, only hidden. Paloma disagreed. He thought some things came back changed, having been taken apart and held in the dark. He thought that was true of skies and kingdoms and apprentices alike.

"You're staring again," Ilsa said.

"I'm allowed to stare. I paid for these."

"So did I." But she tipped her head back too, and for a while neither of them spoke, and the fire cracked, and far below a fox barked once along a hidden road that Paloma had mapped in June and that now belonged, like all the hidden roads, to the open record of the world.

"I got a letter from Doss," Paloma said at last. "He wants work. Legitimate haulage over the high passes. Says if the Crown knows all the shortcuts anyway, someone ought to be paid honestly to use them."

"Will you recommend him?"

"He hanged two of my friends. Well — the Crown hanged them. He watched. And then he tried to drown me." Paloma turned a stick in the fire. "And last month he pulled a warden's horse out of a flooded creek at risk of his own neck, because he'd heard it was mine. So. I don't know what I'll recommend. I know what I'll write: everything true."

"That's a dangerous habit."

"It's the only one I've got left." He smiled, to take the sting out, and she almost smiled back, which from Ilsa Vane was practically a toast.

She was quiet a moment longer, looking at the fire. Then, in the tone she used for terrain reports — level, factual, giving nothing away — she said: "My commission renews in the spring. Border command, southern reach." A pause exactly long enough to be deliberate. "There's a warden's station at Coldermere. Cold in winter. Good light for drafting."

Paloma looked at her. She continued watching the fire with tremendous attention, ears faintly red, and he understood that this was the longest speech she had ever made about anything that mattered, and that she had built it out of logistics the way he had once built his life out of secrets, and that underneath both constructions was the same simple material.

"There's a table at that station," he said, "big enough for full sheets of vellum. I've measured worse working conditions."

"I haven't asked you to—"

"I know. You've mentioned a table." He fed the stick to the flames. "I'm saying I've accepted the terms."

She exhaled through her nose, which was Ilsa for laughing, and threw another branch on the fire, and above them the stars burned on in their thousands, witnesses to a border that no longer needed hiding.

Later, when the fire had sunk to coals, Paloma took out his journal — the field copy, the rough one, the honest one — and wrote the day's entry by coal-light while Ilsa kept the first watch. At the bottom of the page, as he did every night now, he wrote his own name. Full and plain. *Paloma Okonkwo.* A signature, a claim, a small nightly act of courage that no one required of him and that he required of himself, because names were not things you owned, he had learned; they were things you kept choosing, the way you chose a road, every single day, in the open.

He blew out the lantern. Overhead the sky went on pouring out its light, all the way down to the horizon, all the way down to the distant dark line of the Wood, where — he was nearly sure, though he would check in the morning, because checking was his trade now — one star burned lower than the rest, steady and pale and patient, right above the place where the trees began.

It had not been there last year.

He watched it a long time before he woke Ilsa to show her, and when he finally did speak, his voice came out quiet as snowfall.

"Ilsa. Someone's lighting lamps in the Wood."
