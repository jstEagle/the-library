## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The counting house smelled of tallow, iron, and old decisions.

Petra Dravenmoor had worked in that smell for eleven years, long enough that she no longer noticed the way it clung to her sleeves and hair, long enough that she could walk the rows of shelves blindfolded and know by touch alone which ledger held borrowed years and which held bottled names. The candles were the first thing she tended each night—forty-one of them, one for every active account, their flames guttering in air thick with ink-dust. Guild law said a flame must burn for every open contract, so that nothing done in darkness went wholly unwitnessed. It was, she had always thought, the guild's one honest superstition. Everything else was arithmetic dressed as sacrament.

She sat at her desk in the corner farthest from the door, where the shadow of the great iron-bound cabinet fell across her shoulders like a familiar hand. Three ledgers lay open before her: the Shadow Accounts, the Book of Borrowed Years, and the slim, cold volume the clerks called the Namebottle, though its official title ran to two full lines of guild script. She worked left to right, as always, quill moving in small precise strokes, because precision was the only prayer she trusted.

"Shadow, one," she murmured to herself, as the ritual required. "Traded by the widow Hessel for three coppers' worth of luck against her neighbor's goats. Interest accrued: none. Shadows don't bear interest. They only *lengthen*."

She made the entry. Somewhere across Ashvale, the widow Hessel was walking home through the evening drizzle with a slightly shorter shadow than she'd had at midwinter, and Petra's neat hand was the only record in the world that anything had been taken from her at all. That was the guild's true trade—not shadows, not years, not names, but the making of loss into lines of ink, so that what had been stolen could be called *paid* instead.

Petra knew this better than anyone. Better, perhaps, than anyone knew anything, because there were things about her own account that existed only in the margins.

She turned to the Book of Borrowed Years. This was the ledger she hated least and feared most, because years were heavier than shadows. A shadow grew back, like grass after mowing. A year, once borrowed, stayed borrowed. The entries here were written in brown-black ink mixed with a drop of the borrower's blood, per regulation, and each page carried a faint smell she'd never been able to name—something like rain on hot stone, something like a room just vacated.

"Year, one," she recited. "Borrowed from Tam Ollerin, cooper, age thirty-four, toward settlement of his father's debt. Repayment schedule: none. The guild does not lend years back."

Her quill paused over the margin.

In the margin, in handwriting so small and so practiced that even Vess Corvane's sharpest-eyed auditors had never caught it, Petra kept a second set of books.

It had started years ago—a theft so small it barely deserved the name. A year here, entered as two-thirds of a year. A name there, recorded with its last letter smudged, so that when the guild came to collect, they collected a person slightly less whole than the contract promised, and the difference—the sliver, the shaving—drifted back to Petra like dust settling in a quiet room. She didn't know why she did it. That was the honest truth she kept even from herself: she had woken up eleven years ago on the guildhouse steps with no history she could prove and a talent for bookkeeping that felt less like learning than remembering, and she had built herself a life out of columns and carried-forwards. The margins were the only place she'd ever felt like more than a line item.

*Dravenmoor, P., clerk, third rank. Paid monthly. Owes nothing.*

Owes nothing. She looked at those words sometimes and laughed, alone, in the candlelight. Everyone owed something in Ashvale. Everyone except the woman who kept the accounts.

She worked until the second bell of the night-watch, balancing column against column, and the work was good, the work was *hers*, and if her own reflection ghosted faintly in the polished wood of the desk—if the face looking back at her seemed sometimes like a face worn over another face, the way a mask sweats against skin—well. Every bookkeeper saw things in the margins that weren't there.

Then the audit notice arrived.

* * *

It came up through the dumbwaiter from the vault level in a brass tube, sealed with Vess Corvane's personal sigil—a raven standing on an open book, wings spread wide enough to blot out whatever was written beneath them. Petra broke the seal without alarm. Quarterly audits were routine. The guild master ordered them whenever she wanted reminding that the counting house was hers, that Petra was merely its hands.

*Full reconciliation, all volumes, prior to dark moon. No exceptions. No omissions. —V.C.*

*No omissions.* Petra read the phrase twice. It was standard language. It appeared in every audit notice the guild had ever issued, because the guild's founders had understood that omission was the mother of all theft. But tonight the words sat differently against her eye, like a coat that had fit for years and suddenly pulled tight across the shoulders.

She began with the Shadow Accounts, because fear loves to start small.

Column by column, entry by entry, she reconciled the physical tokens in their drawers against the written records—each traded shadow pinned under glass in its little labeled cell, gray and flat as a pressed moth. Everything matched. Of course everything matched; she had written both sides of every match. Her margins held, invisible, seamless. She allowed herself one slow breath.

The Book of Borrowed Years took longer. Here the tokens were vials—small stoppered phials of something that wasn't quite liquid, isn't-quite-gold, each holding a year that some desperate soul had poured out of themselves to cover a debt. She checked forty of them against forty entries. Thirty-nine matched perfectly. The fortieth—

The fortieth was Tam Ollerin's year, and it was *fuller* than her ledger said it should be.

She stared at it. Held it up to the candlelight. The golden stuff inside shifted sluggishly, and there—she couldn't have said how she knew, but she knew, the way she knew rain before it fell—there was more year in the vial than the entry accounted for. The difference was tiny. The width of a margin. The width of a shaving.

Someone had found one of her thefts.

Not corrected it. Not reported it. *Found* it, and left it, like a hunter leaving a snare unsprung to see what came back to check it.

Petra set the vial down with hands that did not shake, because she would not permit them to shake, and reached for the third ledger. If someone was watching, the only defense was to be exactly, flawlessly what she appeared to be: a clerk performing a routine audit. She opened the Namebottle to its current page and began matching bottled names to their entries, reading each one aloud in the ritual murmur, giving the work her whole attention.

That was when she found the entry she had never written.

* * *

It sat at the bottom of the page, below the last name she'd entered, in a hand that was almost—but not quite—her own. The letters were hers. The spacing was hers. The particular way she crossed her sevens and looped her d's was hers, copied so faithfully it made her scalp prickle, and yet the whole of it moved across the page with a confidence she had never once felt, a certainty that no living clerk of her acquaintance possessed.

And the entry itself:

*Received, one soul entire. Sold by the parents, freely given, payment rendered against outstanding fees. Buyer: the Court Beneath the Mountain. Condition: sealed, pending delivery at next dark moon. Witnessed, authenticated, entered.*

Below the entry, where every contract in every guild ledger bore its authentication mark—a sigil drawn by the entering clerk, half glyph and half signature, the guild's guarantee that the transaction was real—there was a mark she had never drawn and could not look away from.

It was beautiful. That was the first obscene thing about it. Guild authentication marks were functional scrawls, ugly with haste. This was a sigil of interlocking curves, a spiral folded through a circle folded through a line that descended off the edge of the entry like a root going down, down into deeper paper than the page contained. And beneath its elegance, in the smallest characters Petra had ever seen outside of a forgery manual, ran a line of notation in no script she recognized—though her eyes followed it fluently, though some part of her read it the way she read Ashvale common, effortlessly, without translating.

The second obscene thing was this: she knew the hand that had drawn it.

Not the forger who had copied her letters onto the page. The hand *underneath* the copying, the hand that had invented the style being imitated. Her own hand, but older. Truer. Hers from before she had learned to write like Petra Dravenmoor, clerk, third rank, owes nothing.

She sat very still. The forty-one candles burned. Somewhere above her, the guildhouse settled and ticked, full of sleeping clerks who trusted her with their arithmetic.

A soul. Sold. Sealed.

Souls were not traded in Ashvale. That was the one prohibition older than the guild itself, the line even Vess Corvane did not cross publicly, because souls were the one currency that unmade the holder rather than the holder's fortunes. There was no drawer for a sold soul. There was no vial. Which meant somewhere in the guild's possession—or already spoken for, already *claimed*—was a child's entire self, entered in a dead language her own buried hand knew how to read, authenticated with a sigil that descended off the page like a root into dark ground.

*Payment rendered against outstanding fees.* Fees. Someone's debts had been tallied, found greater than their flesh could cover, and the shortfall had been made up in the oldest coin there was.

Petra became aware, gradually, that she was holding the page. That she had turned back to it four times now, and each time her fingers rested on the paper a little harder, as if testing whether it might dissolve if she refused to believe it.

The candles flickered. All forty-one of them, at once, though no door had opened, no draft had moved. For a single instant the flames bent sideways together, like grass before weather, like bowing.

Then they straightened, and the counting house was only a counting house, and Petra Dravenmoor told herself the sensible things. Told herself the entry was a test planted by Vess Corvane, baited with the word *omissions*, meant to see whether a clerk with margins full of secrets would flinch at a secret larger than her own. Told herself the sigil was a forgery too clever to matter, that the recognition she felt in her own wrist was exhaustion and nothing else.

But her eyes kept returning to the authentication mark, and the longer she looked at it the more certain she grew of a thing she had no right to know and no power to unknow:

She had drawn that sigil before.

Not copied. Not forged. Drawn—in a room she could not picture, for a purpose she could not remember, in a life that predated the night she had woken on the guildhouse steps with empty hands and a head full of quiet.

The bell above the vault stair rang once, soft and wrong, though no one stood upon the stairs.

Petra looked down at the margin where her hidden books lived, her small treasons, her private shavings of the world—and for the first time in eleven years, the margins looked back at her like a mask she had mistaken for a face.

She closed the Namebottle. She did not lock it. Instead, with movements careful as a thief's, she slipped the page bearing the impossible entry free of its binding—a trick of the craft she'd never needed before tonight—and folded it into the lining of her sleeve, against her wrist, where her pulse tapped against it like a second heartbeat asking questions.

Tomorrow, the audit would show a missing page. Tomorrow, Vess Corvane would come down the vault stairs with the raven-and-book seal gleaming on her breast, and every clever cruelty of the guild would turn its eye on the counting house.

Tonight, Petra blew out the candles—one to forty, in reverse order, as regulation demanded—and stood a long moment in the dark with a stolen page against her skin, listening to the mountain beneath the city breathe.

Because that was the other thing she now knew, in the marrow-deep way she knew the sigil: the Court Beneath the Mountain was real.

And it remembered her.
