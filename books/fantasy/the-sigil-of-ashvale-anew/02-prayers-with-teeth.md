## 2. Prayers with Teeth

The rumor reached the counting house before the sun did.

Petra knew this because rumors were a kind of currency too, and everything that moved through Ashvale's veins eventually pooled in the ledgers she kept — debts and gossip both, entered in her small, precise hand, one column for what people owed and one for what they whispered when they thought no one was counting. The whisper that morning came up the stairs on the breath of a runner boy named Tam, who delivered bread to the guild kitchen and secrets to anyone who tipped him copper.

"The idol at Saint Verren's," he said, hunched against the cold in the doorway of the counting house, his cap clutched like a bird he was afraid would fly. "It wept, mistress. Red tears down its face, all night, and old Cobb the beggar — you know Cobb, sleeps under the aqueduct — he prayed to it for his daughter's fever, and come morning the girl sat up whole. Ate a whole loaf, they say. Asked for honey."

Petra did not look up from her columns. Looking up was a habit she had trained out of herself years ago; eyes that lingered invited questions, and questions were how thieves got caught. "Idols crack," she said. "Old stone weeps groundwater. It stains red with the iron in it."

"It answered a prayer, mistress."

"Coincidence answers prayers every day. Nobody builds shrines to those."

But Tam had already gone, clattering down the stairs with his half-loaf and his copper, and Petra sat alone in the candlelit counting house with the words *it wept* hanging in the air like smoke that wouldn't disperse.

She looked at her hands. They were steady. She made them steadier.

Around her, the Ledger of Impossible Things lay open in its iron cradle, three hundred pages of Ashvale's shadow economy: shadows pawned against winter, years borrowed at ruinous interest, names bottled in green glass and sold to men who wanted to be someone else. There was a page for a widow who had traded her grief for a season of numbness and now couldn't remember why she never cried at funerals. There was a page for a duelist who had purchased his opponent's courage and found, too late, that courage was load-bearing. Petra balanced these accounts the way other clerks balanced grain shipments, with ink-stained fingers and a mind like a locked room.

And in the margins, in letters so small they might have been flaws in the paper, she hid her own thefts. That was the arrangement. That was the bargain she'd struck with herself so long ago she could no longer remember striking it: she stole what she needed to stay hollow enough to survive, and she recorded it honestly, because a thief who lied to her own ledger was a thief with no floor beneath her.

*It wept.*

Petra turned to the back of the ledger, where the oldest entries lived, written in a hand that predated hers by centuries. And there, as she had known she would find it, was the entry she'd been avoiding since Tam's boots hit the stairs:

*God-touched goods: dormant. No activity recorded in living memory. Value: incalculable. Status: sealed.*

The guild held god-touched goods the way a miser holds a coin he suspects is cursed — in a vault beneath the counting house, behind wards paid for with three generations of interest, untouched because touching them required believing they were real, and belief was the one commodity the Guild of Impossible Trades refused to stock.

Petra closed the ledger. Her candles guttered in the draft from the door, and in the dark between flames she felt something shift inside her chest — not a feeling exactly, but a pressure, like water rising behind a wall. Like animals turning over in their sleep.

She told herself it was indigestion. She was very good at telling herself things.

* * *

By midmorning, Ashvale's markets had teeth.

Petra went out because Vess Corvane summoned her, and Vess Corvane's summons had the weight of weather — you didn't argue with it, you simply dressed for it. But even if she hadn't been summoned, she would have gone out, because a bookkeeper who only knew the numbers and not the streets behind them was a bookkeeper waiting to be robbed.

The Saltmarket was seething. Not with violence — not yet — but with that particular electricity that precedes it, the crowd-noise pitched just above ordinary, every conversation bent toward the same center of gravity. Fishwives who hadn't spoken in years stood shoulder to shoulder. Children ran in packs between the stalls, chanting *the idol wept, the idol wept* in the sing-song way children chant anything, senseless or not. A man was selling splinters of wood he claimed came from Saint Verren's altar, five coppers each, and people were buying.

"Blood of the saint herself," the splinter-seller cried, holding up a sliver no bigger than a fingernail. "Blessed wood! Proof the gods wake!"

"Proof you own a knife," muttered the woman beside Petra, a cheesemonger with forearms like ship rope. But she bought two splinters anyway, and tucked them into her bodice with the furtive tenderness of a woman hiding love letters.

Petra drifted through it all with her ledger-mind running. *Demand spike: relics, votives, blessing-strings. Supply: negligible. Risk of fraud epidemic: certain.* Somewhere in the guild's inventory there were eleven crates of confiscated votive figurines from a raid twenty years ago, worthless then, worth a fortune now, and she was already composing the memo to Vess when she saw the shrine.

Saint Verren's was less a church than an alcove with ambitions, wedged between a chandler's shop and a house that leaned like a drunk. The idol stood in its niche behind cracked glass: a robed figure, face worn smooth by centuries of rain and reverence, arms open in a gesture that might have been welcome or surrender. Someone had left flowers. Someone else had left a boot, which was either devotion or drunkenness. And down the idol's smooth stone cheeks, cutting through the grime of ages, ran two dark lines.

Dried. Rust-dark. Iron-red.

Petra stopped. The crowd flowed around her like water around a stone, and she stood in the eddy of it, staring, and the pressure behind the wall inside her chest rose another inch.

Because she had seen that color before. Not on stone. On paper. In the margins of the oldest pages of the ledger, where the ink of certain entries had bled through the vellum in long rust-colored threads, and she had always assumed it was age, or accident, or the cheap tannin of old oak gall ink.

It was not age. She knew that now, in the wordless way one knows a thing has changed forever — the way a house knows an earthquake.

*They were here once,* she thought. *They bled into our books.*

A hand closed on her arm. She didn't flinch, which was itself remarkable; her body had been trained out of flinching the same way it had been trained out of looking up. She turned and found Odell beside her, the guild's fence, a broad soft-spoken man with sawdust in his hair and the patient eyes of someone who spent his life appraising things whose value depended on not asking where they came from.

"You're staring," Odell said quietly.

"I'm auditing."

"At a shrine."

"God-touched goods are guild inventory. If the inventory is waking up, someone has to count it." She heard her own voice and disliked it: too flat, too quick, the voice of a woman reciting a rulebook while something underneath her clawed at the floorboards.

Odell followed her gaze to the dark tracks on the idol's face. "Cobb's daughter really did sit up," he said. "I know the family. I fenced the mother's wedding ring last spring to pay a physiker, and the physiker gave the girl a week. This morning she asked for honey." He paused. "Honey, Petra. Fevered children don't ask for honey."

"Fever breaks."

"Not this one. Not like this." He shifted, uncomfortable, a big man trying to make himself smaller. "Vess wants you at the counting house within the hour. She's seizing everything god-touched. Every relic, every votive, every blessed rag in this city. She says—" and here Odell's voice took on the careful, flattened tone he used when quoting power, "—she says the guild does not trade in miracles, and anything that looks like one is either fraud or threat."

"And which does Vess think it is?"

"She doesn't think. That's what frightens me." He glanced at the idol again, and for a moment his patient face was openly troubled. "When I was a boy my grandmother prayed every night to saints who never answered. Never once. We all knew it. We prayed anyway, because the praying was the point." He looked at the flowers, the boot, the rust-dark tears. "Now something's answering, and the first thing the powerful want to do is put it in a vault."

Petra said nothing. But she thought: *the vault beneath the counting house. Three generations of wards. And I hold the keys.*

* * *

Vess Corvane received her in the map room, standing before the great chart of Ashvale as though the city were a board game she was winning. She was not a tall woman, but she occupied space the way a blade occupies a sheath — precisely, totally, with the promise of what she'd be if drawn. Silver at her temples, ink on her fingers, and eyes the gray of a ledger's closing line.

"Report," she said, without turning.

"The market's flooded with fake relics. Within a week someone will die over a splinter of altar wood. Confiscation will make it worse — scarcity inflates devotion faster than it inflates price."

Now Vess turned. "You're advising me to let the faithful keep their trinkets."

"I'm advising you that seized miracles become black-market miracles, and black markets are harder to audit than shrines."

Something flickered across Vess's face — not quite amusement, not quite approval. "You're the only person in this building who talks to me like a colleague instead of a storm front. Do you know that?" She crossed to the table where a single object lay on white cloth: a votive figurine, crudely cast, a saint with folded hands. "This came in an hour ago. Bought off a peddler in the Tallowmarket. The peddler swore it was warm. My man swore it wasn't. So I held it myself." She extended it toward Petra. "Hold it."

Every instinct Petra owned said *don't*. She took it anyway, because refusing would be more interesting than complying, and interesting was dangerous.

The figurine was warm. Not sun-warm. Blood-warm. Warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, heat with a pulse hidden somewhere inside it, and the moment it touched her palms the wall inside her chest shuddered — and something behind it opened one eye.

It was not a memory. It was the *shape* of a memory, the negative space where one had been cut away, and it pressed against the inside of her like a face against frosted glass. She saw — or almost saw — a vast dim hall. Stone pillars going up into darkness. A voice like deep bells saying words she couldn't hear, in a language she somehow still understood, and the understanding hurt, a bright wire pulled through the soft tissue of her mind—

She set the figurine down. Carefully. Her hands were not entirely steady now, and she let them be seen to tremble, because Vess was watching and a trembling clerk was unremarkable while a frozen one was a story.

"Well?" Vess said.

"Heavy for its size. Cast lead, probably. Warm metal holds heat a long time — your peddler handled it, your man didn't believe him, and belief runs hot." The lie assembled itself smoothly, brick by brick, and Petra hated how good she was at it. "There's nothing in it, guild master."

"No," Vess agreed softly. "There isn't. Isn't that the trouble?" She took the figurine back and looked down at its folded hands. "Three hundred years ago the gods went silent. Every prayer unanswered, every oracle dark. The guild was built in that silence, Petra. Impossible trades exist because the impossible stopped happening and men learned to counterfeit it. Shadows, years, names — all of it theater, all of it built on the certainty that nothing real was watching." She looked up. "If the silence ends, everything we are becomes either obsolete or blasphemy. I won't allow it. Nothing god-touched stays loose in this city. Seizure begins tonight. You'll draw the manifests."

"And if the goods resist seizure?"

Vess smiled, and it was the worst thing Petra had seen all day, worse than the blood on the idol's face, because it was patient. "Then we'll learn what they want," she said, "and we'll charge them for it."

* * *

Petra should have gone straight back to the counting house. She drew the manifests instead in a tavern, quickly, mechanically, and then she broke her own first rule and went looking for the answer instead of letting it come to her.

She told herself it was professional diligence. She was getting very practiced at the sound of her own lies.

Cobb the beggar was easy to find; the crowd around him made him easy to find. He sat outside Saint Verren's alcove on a borrowed stool, wrapped in a blanket some devotee had donated, and people knelt to touch his sleeve. His daughter's fever was the sermon everyone wanted to hear, and he told it badly, stumbling, bewildered, a man describing a wave that had lifted him without explaining why.

"—and I didn't even pray proper," he was saying, for perhaps the hundredth time. "I was drunk, truth be told, and angry. I said, *you stone bastard, you've watched us starve under your nose for three hundred years, if you've got one drop of blood in you give it to my girl instead of wasting it on your own face.*" He laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle. "And it did. Look at it. It gave its blood and my girl ate a whole loaf."

Petra stood at the edge of the crowd in her clerk's gray, invisible, and listened to the shape of the prayer. *Give it to my girl instead of wasting it on your own face.* An ugly prayer. A prayer with teeth. Not the polished intercessions of priests but the raw transactional snarl of a desperate man negotiating with a stone — and it had *worked*, and that fact rang through Petra like a bell struck against her spine.

Because she recognized the grammar of it. Bargain. Exchange. Payment rendered. It was the grammar of the ledger. It was the grammar of *her*.

The crowd pressed closer. Someone jostled her forward, and she caught herself against the shrine's cracked glass, palm flat, close enough to see the dried tears in detail — and close enough to feel it.

The idol was not warm. It was *awake*. She couldn't have said how she knew; the knowing arrived below language, in the animal basement of her, the place where her stolen memories slept. Something vast and slow had turned its attention toward the world after three hundred years of sleep, and it was groggy and bleeding and *listening*, drinking in every prayer in the city like a man crawling toward water, and Petra's palm against the glass was a knock on a door she had no memory of ever having knocked on.

*Come home,* said something, in a voice like deep bells, in a language she shouldn't understand. *Come home, little clerk. Your account is still open.*

The wall inside her chest did not crack. It bowed. And behind it the sleeping animals stirred, and stirred again, and one of them — the largest, the oldest, the one she had never dared name — lifted its head and *howled*, silently, a howl of such homesickness that Petra's knees nearly buckled.

She tore her hand from the glass. The crowd murmured around her; someone asked if she was ill; she shook her head and walked, fast, not running, never running, through the Saltmarket and up the hill toward the counting house with her heart slamming like a fist on a door.

At the top of the street she stopped, once, and looked back. From here she could see the whole city spread below her, rooftops and smoke and the gray sea beyond, and rising above it all, the mountain — the mountain every child in Ashvale grew up being warned about, the mountain no mine touched and no road crossed, the mountain the maps marked with a sigil no cartographer would explain.

As she watched, the light on its peak seemed to bend, very slightly, the way air bends above a fire.

Behind the wall in her chest, the animals were awake now. All of them. Pacing. Listening.

And far below the city, in a vault she alone could open, three hundred years of god-touched goods had begun, very quietly, to hum.
