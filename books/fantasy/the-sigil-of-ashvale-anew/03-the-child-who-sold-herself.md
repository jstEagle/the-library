## 3. The Child Who Sold Herself

The entry had a smell.

Petra had noticed it the night before, in the counting house, when she'd gone back to the ledger a third time and a fourth, telling herself she was only verifying figures. Old ink had its scents — iron-gall biting like a struck match, oak gall tannins gone sweet with age, the ghost of tallow where a clerk's sleeve had dragged through wet lines. She knew them all the way a gravedigger knows soils. But this entry smelled of something else: copper and cold stone and, faintly, unbearably, of candle smoke in a closed room. A smell like the inside of a reliquary. A smell like something that had been alive and was now an asset.

*One (1) soul, whole and unencumbered,* the ledger said, in Vess Corvane's own slanted hand. *Vendor: Dray and Liss Marenholt. Purchased by the Court Beneath, per standing arrangement. Consideration received: 340 marks, guild fees discharged in full, plus dispensation of ancestral debt. Delivery due: dark moon.*

There was no name for the soul itself. That was the part that had kept her awake, staring at her ceiling while the animals behind the wall in her chest paced and paced. Every other entry in three hundred years of impossible bookkeeping named its object. *The shadow of Aldous Fen, cobbler.* *Seventeen borrowed years from the widow Crake.* *A bottled name, origin unknown, contents volatile.* The guild recorded everything because the guild believed that what was written could be owned, and what could not be written could not. So somewhere in this ledger or another there should have been a line that said *the soul of* someone.

Instead there was a sigil, pressed into the margin like a wax seal but drawn in ink so black it seemed to sit above the page rather than on it. And beneath the sigil, where the name should have been, a single word:

*Maren.*

Not a surname. Not a full name. Just that — small, unguarded, the way you'd write the name of a dog or a daughter.

Petra had copied it out three times before she understood why her hand kept shaking. It wasn't fear. She was afraid of very little anymore; fear required a future, and Petra Dravenmoor had spent eleven years building a life with no more future than the next audit. No. What shook her hand was recognition. Somewhere, under all the stolen years and sealed margins, some part of her looked at that single small name and *grieved*, the way you grieve a thing you lost before you knew it was yours to lose.

She needed to see the child.

She told herself it was procedure. Auditors verified delivery conditions; it was in the bylaws; Vess would expect nothing less than thoroughness. She told herself this while dressing in her plainest coat, the one without guild buttons, and leaving through the counting house's back stair, and lying to the door-warden about a errand at the salt warehouse. She was good at lying. It was one of the few honest things about her.

* * *

Ashvale in the grey hours after dawn smelled of fish scales and coal smoke and the sour sweetness of last night's spilled ale going to vinegar in the gutters. The debt-orphan rows sat east of the tanneries, where the rent was cheap because the air ate leather and lungs alike. Petra walked the crooked lanes past doors patched with wagon-iron and windows stuffed with rags, past children already working — a boy hauling tanner's bark, two girls picking oakum with fingers blue as drowned things. Orphans were everywhere in Ashvale. The guild saw to that. Fees compounded; fees foreclosed; fees took houses and trades and, when those ran dry, took other things. The poor sold their shadows first, then their years, then their names, and the guild bought all of it at rates set by men who never went hungry.

Finding the Marenholt house took less than an hour. It wasn't a house. It was the ground floor of a collapsed dyeworks, one room walled off with planks and prayer-rags, a door that had been a table once. Petra stood across the lane in the shadow of a rain barrel and watched a girl come out.

Nine years old, maybe ten — small for either. Hair the color of wet sand, hacked short with scissors, probably her own work, probably crooked. She wore a man's shirt belted with rope and shoes that didn't match, and she came out into the grey morning carrying a chipped bowl, and she stood on the doorstep a moment with her eyes shut, face tipped up toward a sky that offered nothing but smoke-colored cloud.

Praying. The child was praying.

Petra felt it like a hook behind her ribs. After yesterday — the weeping idol, the beggar whose blindness had poured out of his eyes like dirty water, the hum rising from the vault below the counting house — every prayer in Ashvale suddenly meant something different. Prayers were no longer just breath wasted on empty air. They were letters being opened. Someone was reading them.

The girl finished, crossed herself wrong — fist to brow, fist to stern, the old way, the way nobody taught anymore — and went down the lane toward the public pump with her bowl swinging.

Petra followed.

She introduced herself at the pump as a charity assessor from the Temple Wardens, which was a real office held by real people who never actually came east of the tanneries, and the lie sat comfortably in her mouth. The girl — Maren, of course, Maren Marenholt, though everyone called her Ren — pumped water for her without being asked, because that was the training poverty gave children: be useful instantly, before anyone decides you're not worth the trouble.

"Your parents," Petra said. "Dray and Liss Marenholt."

"Gone." The girl said it the way children say weather. "Two winters. Fever took Da, and Ma went to work the river boats and the river kept her." A shrug too practiced to be casual. "I do fine."

"I'm sure. Who keeps you?"

"Herself," said a voice from the doorway of the dyeworks, and an old woman leaned out, bent as a question mark, hands wrapped in strips of rag against the dye-stains that never left the skin of people who worked the vats. "I look in on her. Widow Sef, if it matters, assessor. If you've come to move her to a ward-house, you'll want to know the ward-houses sell whatever the children still have left. At least here she owns herself."

*Owns herself.* Petra kept her face kind and neutral and felt the ledger page burning in her memory like a coal laid on paper. *Whole and unencumbered.* The legal language meant exactly the opposite of what it sounded like. Unencumbered meant no one had claimed her debts. Whole meant nothing had been carved off her yet. Both words existed in the contract for one reason: to certify that what was being delivered was undamaged goods.

"When did you last see your mother?" Petra asked the girl. Gentle. Assessor-gentle.

Maren — Ren — thought about it honestly, which hurt worse than evasion would have. "Before the ice broke. She said she'd fixed everything. She said—" The girl stopped, and something moved behind her eyes, something she'd been carrying carefully and did not want to drop in front of strangers. "She said I wouldn't have to worry about money ever again. She said the guild was satisfied and no one could take anything from me now." Her chin came up, defiant, daring Petra to laugh. "And nobody has. Nobody's taken anything. I checked."

"How do you check?"

"I count what I've got." She held up the bowl. "Bowl. Comb. Knife. Blanket. Two candles and a half. Boots." She wiggled a mismatched foot. "Every night. If something's missing I'd know."

*Nothing's missing,* the child believed. Nothing had been taken. And in a vault beneath a mountain, in a reliquary Petra had never seen but could imagine with terrible clarity, something of hers flickered in the dark like a moth in a jar, and the girl counted her boots every night and slept soundly, believing herself whole.

Widow Sef was watching Petra with old, flat eyes that missed very little. "You've gone white, assessor," she said. "Charity work does that."

"It does," Petra agreed, and thanked them, and pressed a mark into the girl's palm — too much, deliberately, so that it would look like temple generosity rather than guilt — and walked away up the lane with her heart going hard and strange against her ribs.

* * *

She should have gone back to the counting house. That was the sane move, the survival move, the move every instinct she had honed over eleven years of invisible life demanded: verify, record, report, forget. The guild had rules about attachment. Vess Corvane had rules about attachment, and Vess enforced her rules with instruments Petra had seen hanging in the discipline room and made a point of never looking at twice.

Instead Petra found herself in the records annex, pulling the fee histories for the Marenholt account, because grief, in her experience, always wanted paperwork. Grief wanted to know the numbers. Grief wanted to see the shape of the machine.

It took her until midafternoon to reconstruct it, and the reconstruction was beautiful and obscene, the way all the guild's arithmetic was beautiful and obscene. Dray Marenholt had borrowed against his dye-work license eight years ago to cover a bad season. Then against his labor. Then against his wife's labor. Interest compounded at the guild's standard rate, which was to say at a rate designed to make foreclosure inevitable, which was to say at a rate designed by Vess Corvane. By the fever winter, the debt stood at three hundred forty marks — precisely, to the coin, the consideration listed in the soul contract. Liss Marenholt had come to the counting house herself, alone, in the last week of her husband's illness, and signed.

Petra found the signature page. Liss had signed with an X, and someone — the clerk's hand was neat, patient, almost tender — had written her name beside it. Below the X, in smaller script, the clerk had noted: *Signatory informed of nature and permanence of conveyance. Signatory wept. Transaction completed.*

*Informed of the nature.* Had anyone told Liss Marenholt what a soul-sale actually meant? Not the theology of it, not the sermon version. The mechanics. That the court beneath the mountain fed on souls the way Ashvale fed on coal, slow and steady, centuries long. That the child would live every day of her life feeling a draft from a door she couldn't find, waking tired, loving things a little less than she should, dying a little easier than she ought — and never once knowing why. Had anyone explained that?

Or had they used the other form, the kind form, the one that said *ancestral debt relief* and *permanent protection from creditors*? Petra had written both forms herself. She knew their weight in the hand, how the kind one flowed smoother, how the lies were built right into the grammar.

She sat in the annex with the papers spread around her like shed skins, and behind the wall in her chest the animals had stopped pacing. They were standing still, all of them, facing the same direction, the way deer face a sound in the forest. Listening to her.

Because here was the thing she could not stop circling, the thing that had woken her at the dark hours: she knew what a sold soul felt like from the inside. Not from study. From the bone-deep, wordless knowledge that lived below memory, in the cellar of her where the stolen years were bricked away. When she'd read *whole and unencumbered*, something in her had flinched at the word *whole* the way a tongue finds the gap where a tooth was. She had a gap. She had never known where it came from. Eleven years ago she had appeared in Ashvale with forged papers in her own handwriting — she'd checked, once, drunk enough to dare it, and the authentication marks matched her living hand stroke for stroke — and no history, and a hunger in the margins of herself that no amount of careful invisible living had ever filled.

She was not ready to open that door. She might never be. But Maren's page had blown it open a crack, and through the crack she could feel cold air moving, and hear, far off, a voice pronouncing judgments in a courtroom under a mountain.

Petra put the papers away. Carefully. In order. The way she did everything.

Then she went down to the vault.

* * *

The deep archive opened to her blood and her keys and the wards that knew her step. Three hundred years of god-touched goods hummed in the dark around her as she passed — bottled names murmuring behind glass, traded shadows shifting in their lead-lined coffers like fish in barrels, borrowed years ticking faintly, each coffer a small impatient clock. The hum had grown since yesterday. She could feel it through the soles of her boots now, a vibration like a hive deciding something.

The original contracts lived in the iron cabinets at the vault's heart, organized by year, sealed with the guild's ash-sigil. Petra found the current decade, found the season, found the folio. Her lantern shook. She hated that it shook. She steadied it with the trick she'd learned as a girl — press the wrist flat, breathe low, become a person with steady hands — and drew out the folio and turned to the page.

There it was again. The smell of copper and cold stone. The sigil pressed into the margin, blacker than ink. *One (1) soul, whole and unencumbered. Delivery due: dark moon.* Nine days from now.

And beneath the sigil, where she hadn't let herself look closely the first four times, the authentication block. Every contract required one — the handling auditor's mark, the chain-of-custody attestation, proof that a guild officer had verified the goods. Standard. Routine. Thousands of them in this vault, thousands bearing her own mark, because Petra Dravenmoor audited half the god-touched traffic in Ashvale and had for a decade.

But this authentication was dated six years ago. Before her time in the counting house. Before her time anywhere she could remember.

She held her lantern close. The mark was hers. Not similar — hers. The peculiar hooked crossbar, the dot she always placed too high, the tiny hesitation-flick at the terminal stroke that no forger would think to copy because no forger had ever noticed it. Six years ago, Petra Dravenmoor had authenticated the sale of a child's soul, and then somehow arranged to forget it, and then somehow arranged to arrive in Ashvale with no past at all.

The animals behind the wall in her chest began, very quietly, to scream.

Petra stood in the humming dark for a long time — minutes, or a quarter hour, or the length of a life. The lantern burned steadily now. Her hands were perfectly still. Something had passed through her and come out the other side, and what remained on the far side of it was not dread. Dread was for things that might happen. This had already happened, six years ago and nine years ago and in a courtroom beneath a mountain she could not remember, and it was going to happen again in nine days unless—

Unless.

She looked at the page. One page, thin as anything, worth a soul. It would be treason to remove it. It would be suicide, probably — Vess Corvane did not forgive, and the guild's discipline room had instruments for thieves of documents that made the instruments for thieves of gold look merciful. Every rational faculty Petra owned, every scarred and sensible survival instinct, stood in a row inside her and said *no*. Put it back. Copy it if you must. Go home. Be invisible. Invisible people survive.

Maren was counting her boots tonight. Bowl, comb, knife, blanket, two candles and a half. Nothing missing. Nothing taken. Believing herself whole.

Petra slid the page free of the folio, folded it along its existing creases — someone else had folded it once, long ago; the creases were old and soft — and slipped it into the lining of her coat, against her ribs, where it lay like a second heartbeat.

The vault hummed. Far above, through fathoms of stone and brick, Ashvale went about its evening, lighting its lamps, saying its prayers — prayers that, these days, somebody heard.

Petra banked the lantern and climbed toward the light, carrying the theft that would unmake her life, and finding, to her astonishment, that her hands had never been steadier.
