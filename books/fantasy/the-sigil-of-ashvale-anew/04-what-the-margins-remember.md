## 4. What the Margins Remember

The counting house at night was a different animal than the counting house by day.

By day it breathed with the rustle of clerks and the clink of weighed coin, the mutter of appraisers arguing over the going rate for a grandmother's last good summer. By night it was all bone and candlelight — the great ledgers lying open on their chains like sleeping things that might wake hungry, the smell of tallow and iron gall ink and, underneath it, something older Petra had never been able to name. A smell like cold stone. Like the air in a place no one had opened for a very long time.

She had told herself she would not look at the page again tonight.

She looked at it again.

It lay before her on her own desk, separated from its ledger by a sheet of blotting paper, because some part of her — the bookkeeper part, the part that believed order was the only wall between people and the dark — could not bear to let the thing touch anything else. *Contract of Conveyance, Soul Entire,* read the header in a clerk's careful hand. *Vendor: Dravenmoor Holdings, per agent V. Corvane. Purchaser: sealed per custom of the Court Beneath.* And then the sigil.

Guild authentication marks were supposed to be simple. A stamp, a countersign, the guild master's ring pressed into wax while two witnesses watched. This one was not a stamp. It was a sigil drawn freehand in ink so black it seemed to hold depth, spirals nested inside spirals like a storm seen from above, and around its rim ran a line of script too small to read without a lens.

Petra fitted the lens to her eye.

She had expected Aurellic numerals, the dead accounting-language the old courts used for weighing grief against gold. She had expected anything but what she found.

Her own handwriting.

Not handwriting *like* hers. Hers. The particular way she hooked her sevens, the small hesitation-stroke she made at the start of every capital letter because her first writing master had beaten left-handedness out of her and her hand still flinched at beginnings. The way her *r*s trailed off at the end of a word as if reluctant to finish. No forger alive could have copied that hesitation; forgers copy what shows, not what hesitates.

She set down the lens. Picked it up. Looked again, as though the letters might have changed their minds.

They hadn't. Around the rim of the sigil, in a hand only she could have written, ran the words:

*Paid in full. Received by me, for me, from me.*

* * *

Petra did not remember writing them.

This was not, in itself, alarming. There were whole years she did not remember — she had learned to treat memory the way she treated inventory, trusting the ledger over the shelf, assuming that whatever was missing had been misplaced rather than stolen. That was the trade she'd built her life on, after all. Memory-thief. She knew better than anyone how much of a person could be carried off without the person noticing. You didn't feel the absence of a room you couldn't remember standing in.

But this was different. This wasn't an empty room. This was her own name signed to a door she had no key for.

She sat back in her chair until its joints creaked, and the candle flame leaned in the draft of her movement, and the shadows of the shelving leaned with it, long fingers across the floor. Somewhere above her, through three floors of stone and timber, Ashvale slept — the fishmarket stink settling into the gutters, the shrine bells silent since the idol had begun weeping blood, the beggar who'd prayed for his daughter's fever and woken to find her healed telling the story to anyone who'd buy him a drink. Gods waking up. Vess Corvane wanted every god-touched item in the city locked in the guild vaults by week's end, and Petra had spent two days cataloguing seized relics with hands that would not quite stay steady.

Because when the beggar had prayed, Petra had felt something move in her.

That was the only word for it. Not remembered — moved. Something deep under the floorboards of herself turning over, the way a sleeper turns when a door opens somewhere in the house. She had stood in the market crowd with tears running down her face and no idea whose they were.

Now she looked at her own handwriting circling a soul-contract like a moat, and the feeling came back. Stronger. Closer.

*Received by me, for me, from me.*

"Right," she said aloud, to the empty counting house, because saying things aloud kept them ordinary. "You wrote this. Fine. When?"

She pulled the contract's ledger toward her — Volume Nine, the oldest volume still in circulation, its spine patched with leather from at least three other books. Guild practice was to log every authentication in a marginal index: date, witnesses, fee. Her finger went down the entry for the Dravenmoor conveyance.

The margin was blank.

Not unwritten. *Blank.* The paper there had a faint gloss, the sheen of vellum that has been scraped and rewritten, and beneath it, when she tilted the page toward the candle and let the raking light do its slow work across the surface, ghosts rose up out of the skin. Palimpsest. Someone had erased the original entry and written over it. She fetched the pumice-dust and the soft brush from the restoration drawer, the tools she used for recovering damaged records, and worked the light and the dust together until the buried strokes began to surface like drowned bodies rising.

The original date was forty-one years ago.

Petra Dravenmoor was thirty-two years old.

She put down the brush very carefully, the way you put down a knife when your hands have started shaking.

Forty-one years ago, someone with her handwriting had authenticated a contract selling a child's soul to the court beneath the mountain. Forty-one years ago, that same someone had scraped the record clean and hidden the transaction inside the sigil itself, where no auditor would ever think to look, because auditors trust margins the way priests trust scripture. And now that child — Maren, nine years old, debt-orphan, who had shown Petra a gap-toothed smile and asked if the lady from the guild had come about the *money* — that child's payment was due at the dark moon, and the only person who had ever known the truth of the contract was a woman who did not exist yet.

Unless.

Unless the woman existed first.

* * *

The memory did not come the way memories come.

It did not rise gently, a scent triggering a scene, warmth spreading backward through time. It came the way floodwater comes through a cellar door — all at once, from below, with pressure behind it — and it took Petra at the desk like a wave takes a swimmer, and she was somewhere else entirely.

Stone. Black stone polished by centuries of knees, worn into shallow bowls where petitioners knelt, and Petra was kneeling in one of those bowls and it fit her body perfectly, the way a shoe fits a foot that has walked in it for years. Cold came up through the stone into her shins, into her hips, into her chest, and she understood dimly that the cold was not temperature. It was attention. The mountain itself was looking at her.

Above her — impossibly far above, or perhaps not above at all, direction meant nothing here — hung a court. Galleries of it, tiered and spiraling, carved from the living rock, and in the galleries sat figures she could not look at directly, the way you cannot look at the sun or at the exact center of a spiral. Their voices were a sound like many bells underwater.

And in front of her, filling the space the way a mountain fills a valley simply by being there, was *him*.

She never saw his face. Later — after, gasping over her desk with candle wax pooled and hardened on the wood and her own fingernails dug bloody crescents into her palms — she would try to reconstruct him and find that her mind had kept everything except the one thing that mattered. She had his voice. She had the sense of vast, patient amusement, the amusement of a creditor watching interest accrue. She had the impression of a crown that was not worn but grown, antlered and mineral, veined with ore.

And she had the voice. Oh, she had the voice. It came from everywhere the way the cold came from below.

*"Little clerk,"* it said, and the endearment was a hook dressed in silk. *"You balance beautifully. Do you know what a soul weighs? No — of course you don't. You'll learn. Weighing is the first thing I teach my clerks."*

And Petra — young, whoever-young-was, kneeling in the bowl of worn stone with her heart hammering like a fist on a door — heard herself answer. Heard her own voice, younger, steadier than she had ever been, say:

"And the second thing?"

Laughter rolled through the galleries then, bell-sound and avalanche-sound together, and something in it was genuinely delighted, and that delight was the most frightening thing in the whole flooding dark.

*"Clever,"* said the god-king. *"The second thing is theft, little clerk. Every ledger is a theft. Every number rounds away something real. The question is only ever: who is robbed?"*

He leaned close — or the darkness leaned close wearing his voice — and she felt breath that smelled of struck flint and old incense.

*"I am going to teach you both,"* he said. *"And then, when you are perfect, you are going to try to steal from me. They always do. It is the final lesson, and I confess it is my favorite."*

The galleries rang with laughter. The cold climbed into her throat—

* * *

Petra came back to herself on the floor.

She didn't remember falling. She was sitting against the leg of her own desk with her spine against oak and iron, the candle guttered to a blue nub, and outside the window the sky had gone the color of dishwater, which meant she had lost hours, not minutes. Her mouth tasted of copper. Her palms stung.

On the desk above her, the contract waited, patient as a spider.

She made herself stand. Made herself walk to the water jug and drink, made herself relight the candle from the banked coals of the office stove, made herself sit down again and pick up the lens, because she was Petra Dravenmoor, bookkeeper of the Ashvale Guild, and the one thing she had ever been able to rely on — the one thing that had survived whatever had been done to her, whatever she had done to herself — was that numbers did not lie even when people did.

So she counted what she had.

One: a contract authenticated forty-one years ago in her own hand, hidden in plain sight by its own authenticator. Two: a memory that belonged to a woman older than Petra was, kneeling in a courtroom beneath a mountain, speaking to a god-king who called her *little clerk* and promised to teach her theft. Three: Maren Tull, nine years old, due at the dark moon, eleven days hence. Four: a guild master named Vess Corvane who held the vendor's signature — *Dravenmoor Holdings, per agent V. Corvane* — on a document older than Vess's entire career, which meant either forgery or something far worse.

Five: the words around the sigil. *Received by me, for me, from me.*

She turned the contract over. She looked at the vendor line for a long time. Then she took a fresh sheet of scrap, dipped her pen, and wrote *Dravenmoor* in her ordinary hand, the hand she used for receipts and ration lists, and laid it beside the sigil's rim-script.

Identical. Of course it was identical. But that wasn't what stopped her breath.

What stopped her breath was the date arithmetic, the terrible little sum that had been waiting for her all night, patient as interest. Forty-one years ago, a woman with Petra's hand had signed a contract. Petra was thirty-two. Therefore the woman was not Petra.

Therefore the woman was someone else — someone whose handwriting lived in Petra's muscles, whose courtroom knelt in Petra's bones, whose god-king's voice echoed in the hollows of a self Petra had always vaguely suspected was furnished secondhand. Everything she called herself — the thief's caution, the bookkeeper's comfort, the taste for plain food and locked doors, the way she flinched at capitals — all of it might be a coat cut for someone else and handed down.

Her identity was a forgery. And the counterfeiter had been her.

*I stole myself,* she thought, and the thought arrived with the flat certainty of a balanced column, the way true things arrive, with no drama at all. *I stole myself and sold the original, and the sigil is the receipt.*

The candle flickered. In the counting house around her the ledgers lay chained and sleeping, thousands of pages of traded shadows and borrowed years, and for the first time in nine years of service Petra looked at them and saw not the guild's property but a graveyard — row upon row of missing pieces, and somewhere in the rows, indexed under a name she no longer answered to, the rest of her.

Eleven days.

She drew the blotting paper back over the contract, slid it into the false bottom of her document case — the one she'd built herself, the one Vess didn't know about, or claimed not to — and locked the case, and sat for a moment with her hand flat on the cool wood, feeling her own pulse hammer against her palm like something asking to be let out.

Then, from the corridor beyond the counting-house door, came footsteps. Unhurried. Familiar. The particular soft click of a cane's ferrule on flagstone that Petra had heard ten thousand times and never once, until this moment, feared.

Vess Corvane knocked once — she never waited for an answer — and the door swung open onto lamplight and the guild master's pleasant, unreadable face.

"Burning midnight oil, Dravenmoor?" said Vess, smiling. "Good. I've an audit I want done quietly, and you're the only clerk I trust with old volumes."

Her eyes went, just for half a heartbeat, to the document case.

Just half a heartbeat.
