## 6. The Thief Who Keeps Books

The counting house slept the way old animals sleep: lightly, and with its teeth showing.

Petra moved between the shelves of ledgers by candle-hooded lantern, each step placed on the boards she knew did not creak. She had spent three years learning this room's silences — which floorboards sang under weight, which hinges needed oiling, which of the night clerks dozed at the far desk after the second bell and which merely pretended to, waiting for someone foolish enough to give them a reason to be awake. The guild paid its watchers in small mercies and smaller coin, and both ran out quickly.

Maren's page sat against her ribs where it had lived for four days now, folded to the size of a prayer, warm from her skin as though it had a pulse of its own. She had not dared open the ledger it belonged to again in daylight. But she had read it forty times in the dark of her rented room, tracing the sigil with one fingernail, feeling that impossible recognition hum up through the bone like a struck tuning fork.

*A soul, sold, sealed.*

And beneath the seal, in a hand she knew better than her own because apparently it *was* her own, an authentication mark she had no memory of making.

There was only one thing a bookkeeper could do with a lie written into the truth. Copy everything.

She took down the Ledger of Impossible Things itself — the master volume, chained to its lectern by a lock she'd picked so often the mechanism had grown apologetic, opening for her with barely a complaint. The chain rattled once. She froze. Somewhere above, the rafters settled. The night clerk at the far desk snored on, dreaming whatever guild men dreamed: percentages, mostly, and the particular pleasure of catching someone else's error.

Petra opened the master ledger to its first page and began to copy.

Not with ink — ink could be found, dated, matched. She copied with memory, writing in a cipher of her own devising onto cheap unbleached paper sewn into a codex no bigger than her palm, a book that looked like nothing, worth nothing, a merchant's scratch-pad of grocery tallies. Every entry went in: the shadows traded out of the Lamplighters' Ward and who had bought them; the seventeen years borrowed from a dying cooper and never returned; the bottled names shelved in the cellar vaults, each stoppered jar labeled in a hand too fine for the ugliness of the business. Debts. Interest accrued. Collateral held.

Collateral *held.* That was the phrase the guild used for souls. Not owned. Held. As if the owners might come back for them, someday, with a receipt.

Her pen scratched. Outside, Ashvale breathed through its night noises — cart wheels on wet cobbles, a dog complaining at the moon, the distant clang of the shrine-bells that had begun ringing again since the idol in the Beggar's Square wept blood and answered a cripple's prayer. The bells rang more often now. The city pretended not to notice. The guild noticed very much indeed; Vess Corvane had tripled the seizures of god-touched goods, and every crate that came into the counting house reeked faintly of incense and fear.

Petra wrote faster.

Here was the entry for the weeping idol itself, logged two days ago: *One (1) shrine idol, marble, anomalous lachrymation. Disposition: confiscate. Note: petitioner's ailment inexplicably resolved. Recommend surveillance of petitioner.* They were going to watch a man whose legs had been healed, in case the healing spread. In case grace turned out to be contagious. Petra wrote it all down in her cipher, and her hand shook slightly, because some part of her — the buried part, the part that had surfaced in fragments these past days like drowned things rising — remembered what it was to be watched by people who feared what you might become.

*You were the first,* said the memory that wasn't a memory yet, whispering up from under the floorboards of her mind. *You were sold before any of them.*

She didn't know that. She couldn't know that. She knew only the courtroom beneath the mountain that flashed behind her eyes when she closed them too long, the gold-dark voice saying words she couldn't quite hear, and her own handwriting sealing contracts she had never signed.

Page by page. Entry by entry. The candles burned down. Her eyes ached. She copied until the master ledger lay open at Maren's entry, and there she stopped, pen hovering, because copying that page felt different from copying the others. The others were thefts. This was a sentence still being carried out. Nine years old, payment due at dark moon. Fourteen days now. Thirteen.

She wrote it anyway. A lie survives as long as someone remembers the truth. She intended to be the remembering.

* * *

She was sewing the last quire into the codex's spine when the door opened.

No creak, no warning — because of course he used the third board, the silent one, the trick she'd taught *him*. Odell stood in the doorway with his coat still dripping river-fog, a fence's satchel over one shoulder, and an expression on his face that moved through surprise, disbelief, and something very close to grief before settling on the careful blankness he wore for buyers.

"Evening, Petra," he said, very quietly.

The needle was still in her hand. The codex lay open on the table, its cipher pages fanned like the wings of a moth pinned mid-flight. Between them, on the lectern, the master ledger gaped open to the entry for a nine-year-old girl's soul.

Petra did not move. Moving was confession. "Odell." Her voice came out level, and she was privately astonished by it. "You're early."

"I'm always early. You're always gone by second bell." He closed the door behind him without looking at it, the way he did everything, without looking, a man built entirely of peripheral vision. His gaze stayed on the codex. On her hands. On the thread. "That's a cipher."

"It's a shopping list."

"In a binding I've seen you make twice, both times for things you didn't want found." He set down his satchel with exaggerated care, the way he set down goods whose value depended on their remaining unbroken. "Petra. Tell me I'm wrong about what that is."

"You're wrong about what that is."

He crossed the room. She let him. There was nowhere for either of them to go that wouldn't make it worse. He stood over the codex and looked down at it for a long moment, and then, with two fingers, touched the edge of a page — not reading, just confirming it existed, the way you touch a wound to learn how deep it goes.

"Soul-forfeit," he said. "That's the penalty. Not prison. Not the lash. They take the whole of you and sell the parts, and the parts go into jars in the cellar, and twenty years from now some broker bottles your name and sells it to a merchant's daughter who wants to feel interesting." His voice stayed low and even, but she could hear the crack running through the middle of it, hairline, spreading. "I've carried those jars, Petra. I know what they weigh."

"I know what they weigh. I keep the inventory."

"Then why—" He turned on her then, and for once his eyes were wide open, and she saw that he was not angry. Anger would have been easier. He was frightened, and worse than that, he was trying to understand, and Odell understanding a thing was a slow and dangerous process, like watching a lock decide whether to yield. "Four days ago you were the best bookkeeper this guild has. Quiet. Clean. You hid your little thefts in the margins like everyone does and nobody ever looked twice, because you made sure there was nothing worth looking twice at. And now you're sitting alone in the counting house at midnight committing high treason over—" his finger came down, gently, on the master ledger, on the line she'd been staring at, "—over *this*."

"Maren," Petra said. "Her name is Maren. Though the ledger doesn't think she needs one anymore. It lists her by lot number."

Odell went still.

"She's nine," Petra said, and having started she could not stop, the words coming up out of her like water from a cracked well. "Her parents owed guild fees on a stall they lost to the flood year, and the fees compounded, and Vess's men offered them the usual menu, and they chose the cheapest item on it. Their daughter's soul. Payment due at dark moon. She doesn't know. She thinks the paper her mother signed was a debt release. She told the woman next door she's going to be an apprentice soon, because that's what they told her, that selling her soul meant she'd get a *position*." Petra heard her own voice thinning and hated it and kept going. "I pulled her file. I traced the contract. I have the original page in my coat, and I have not been able to put it down for four days, and every hour I hold it I'm committing a crime, and I find that I don't care, Odell. I've discovered I don't care at all."

The silence afterward had texture, like felt. Somewhere above them the shrine-bells rang again, three strokes, soft, and both of them glanced up at the sound out of the same instinct, the whole city flinching toward its gods these days like a bruised animal toward a kind hand.

"That's not like you," Odell said finally. "Caring. Not about — them." He gestured vaguely upward, toward the floors where the guild's officers kept their warm offices. "You taught me the first week. *Nobody below the fourth floor gets saved, Odell. We survive, that's the whole art.*"

"I remember teaching you that."

"So what changed?"

What could she say? That a ledger had lied to her? That her own handwriting haunted a contract older than her employment, older perhaps than her employment's possibility? That she woke at night with the taste of gold in her mouth and a voice in her ears saying *clerk* in a tone that made the word sound like both an honor and a sentence?

"The accounts didn't balance," she said instead. "For the first time in three years, something in this house didn't balance. And I'm the bookkeeper. Balancing is what I'm *for*."

Odell looked at her for a long time. Then he did the most dangerous thing he could have done. He sat down across the table from her, unpinned his soaked coat, and said, "Show me the cipher."

"No."

"Show me, or report me for asking. One or the other, Petra, but don't insult us both with the middle road." He rubbed his face with both hands, and when he lowered them he looked ten years older and five years more honest. "Because here's what happens now, whether I like it or not. I walked in. I saw. Which means I'm already in the ledger of this, whatever you call it, whatever it becomes. If I walk out and say nothing and you're caught tomorrow, they'll ask me when I saw you, and what I saw, and I will have to lie to Corvane's enforcers with my heart hammering and my hands sweating, and I am a *fence*, Petra, I lie beautifully about merchandise and terribly about friends." He laughed, short and bleak. "So. Show me. Let me know exactly what I'm dying of."

She should refuse. Every instinct honed by three careful years said refuse, deflect, laugh it off, offer him a drink and a lie. But she had spent four days alone with a secret that grew heavier hourly, and loneliness, she was discovering, was its own species of treason — the traitor's first casualty is always the company they might have kept.

She turned the codex toward him and began, entry by entry, to teach him the cipher.

It took until the fourth bell. Shadows, years, names, collateral held. He learned fast; he'd always learned fast, with the quick grasping intelligence of a man who'd never been allowed a formal education and had therefore stolen one piece at a time. When she reached Maren's entry he made her stop and read it aloud twice, and the second time his jaw worked silently around the lot number, and he looked away at the wall, at nothing, at the whole rotten architecture of the world.

"There are others," he said. "Aren't there. In this book. Souls."

"Forty-one entries under collateral held. Some are marked *delivered*." Delivered. To the court beneath the mountain, though the ledger never said so, only used a sigil — that same seal, that same authentication mark in her own hand. "Some are marked pending."

"Is hers pending?"

"Dark moon."

Odell nodded slowly. He picked up her needle, weighed it absently in his palm, set it down. When he spoke again his voice had changed — flattened, decided, the voice he used when a deal was struck and there was no point pretending otherwise.

"Right," he said. "Then listen, because I've thought about this for exactly as long as it takes a coward to become something else, which is not long, we work fast. You can't hide that page forever. Corvane audits everything, and the dark-moon audit is in eleven days, and if your codex is anywhere in this building or yours or mine, we're both jars in the cellar. So it needs to be *nowhere*. Memorized and burned, both our heads, and the original page somewhere no auditor dreams of looking." He leaned forward. "And Petra — the contract itself. You said you recognized the seal."

Her stomach dropped. "How did you—"

"Because you flinched when I said *audit*, and you flinched worse when I said *contract*, and you've flinched at that ledger for four days like it owes you money." His eyes were steady on hers, and there was no accusation in them, only a terrible gentleness. "Whatever you saw in that sigil, whoever you were before the guild scraped you clean and gave you a new name — you don't have to tell me tonight. But you'll tell me before dark moon. Because I'm not walking into the mountain blind beside a woman who won't tell me why she's walking."

Beside. He had said *beside*, casually, the way he'd say *with the shipment* or *at the market*, as though it were already arranged, as though her treason were simply a venture he'd taken a share in.

"Odell," she said carefully, "if you're caught, they won't just take your memories. They'll take—"

"They'll take what's in the jars, yes, I know the inventory, I helped carry it." He stood, shouldered his satchel, and paused at the door with his hand on the latch, and for a moment the lamplight caught his face and she saw underneath the fence's blankness something older and rawer, a boy from the flood year who had lost a stall and a family and been handed a menu. "My mother prayed, you know. After the flood. Prayed for years, at the shrines, proper prayers, kneeling and everything. Nothing ever answered her. And now the idols bleed and the beggars get healed, and the guild wants to *confiscate* it." He shook his head slowly. "Maybe the gods are waking up, Petra. Maybe somebody up there finally heard her. And maybe it'd be a shame, when they ask the living who stood where, if I had to answer honestly that I stood in the doorway and did nothing."

He slipped out through the third board's silence, and the door clicked shut behind him, and Petra sat alone in the sleeping counting house with a finished codex, a stolen page against her ribs, and eleven days.

She blew out the lantern. In the dark, the shrine-bells rang once more — and stopped mid-stroke, cut off cleanly, as though a hand had closed around the bell.

She sat very still in the sudden silence, listening, and heard, far off in the direction of the fishmonger's alley, footsteps that did not sound like anyone going anywhere. Footsteps that sounded like waiting.
