## 7. A God in the Alley

The fishmonger's alley behind Salt Row smelled of brine and old blood, the way it always did at the hour when the market's noise drained out of it like water from a basin. Petra had come there to be no one. It was a skill she'd cultivated over years — walking with her shoulders rounded, her ledger-sense folded up small and quiet inside her, so that even the guild's watchers on the rooftops would mark her as a woman buying herring for supper and nothing more.

Three days since the dark moon audit was announced. Two days since she'd hidden the codex beneath the loose hearthstone in Odell's back room, where not even he knew it lay. One day since Vess Corvane had smiled at her across the counting house and said, *Petra, dear, you look tired,* in the voice of someone who could see every thread of a person's life and had already chosen which one to pull.

She stopped by the gutting table to let a cart pass, and that was when the stranger spoke.

"You dropped something."

His voice came from the shadow between two barrels, low and unhurried, like a man commenting on weather. Petra's hand went to her knife before her mind caught up. She turned slowly.

He sat on an overturned crate with his knees drawn up, a rag of a man wrapped in a coat that might once have been fine — moth-eaten velvet gone grey at the elbows, salt-stained hem. His hair was the color of wet sand, matted and unwashed, and his face was ordinary in a way that hurt to look at, the way a familiar song hurts when you can't remember where you learned it. He held out one hand, palm up.

On it lay nothing. That was the first wrong thing. The second was that the alley's ever-present gulls — the fat, shrieking thieves that mobbed Salt Row morning to night — had gone silent. Not fled. Silent. Forty birds perched along the eaves and the fish racks, heads cocked, watching him with round yellow eyes.

"I didn't drop anything," Petra said.

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You didn't. But you're missing quite a lot, all the same." He tilted his head. "I know what it is you do, little thief of other people's yesterdays. I've been watching you work. You have careful hands."

"Then you know what these hands can take from a man who talks to me in alleys." She let him see the knife now, let the candlelight — no, there was no candlelight, only the bruised grey of late afternoon — catch its edge.

Something moved across his face. Not fear. Recognition, or grief wearing recognition's clothes.

"There it is," he said softly. "That's the look. You wore it standing in my court of reeds, the first time we spoke, before your name was taken from you. Before you took it yourself." He rose from the crate, unfolding to a height that seemed too much for the space he occupied, though the barrels and walls made way for him without appearing to move. "You don't remember me. Of course you don't. But I remember you, **Serevanne**."

The name went through her like a fishhook through soft flesh.

It wasn't pain, exactly. It was the sensation of a lock turning somewhere deep inside her — a door she hadn't known existed swinging open onto a dark stairwell, cold air rising from below. Her mouth shaped the syllables without her permission. *Sere-va-nne.* Four beats. A name with water in it, river-water, mountain snowmelt.

"That's not my name," she said, and heard how thin her voice came out.

"No," the stranger said gently. "Yours is Petra Dravenmoor, bookkeeper of the Ashvale Guild, third desk from the north window, who takes her tea bitter and sleeps with a knife under her pillow because some nights she dreams of a voice reading names aloud, and wakes with salt on her lips." He spread his empty hands. "But Serevanne was yours first. And I am the one who gave it."

* * *

She should have walked away. Every guild instinct screamed it: strangers who know too much are either informants or bait, and both end the same way, in a cellar with your memories peeled out of you like fruit skin. She had spent her whole stolen life being unremarkable precisely so this moment would never come.

Instead she heard herself ask, "Who are you?"

"A minor god," he said, with the shrug of a man admitting to a small debt. "Very minor. Once. I kept the fords and the ferry-crossings of the Greywater, and the last honest prayer I heard was a miller asking me to keep his daughter's feet dry. Then the mountain god began eating prayers, and mine grew fewer, and thinner, and at last I starved down to almost nothing and slept. In the roots of a willow, if you want the whole shameful truth of it. For three hundred years." He picked at the cuff of his ruined velvet. "Your guild's ledgers woke me. All that commerce in souls and names and borrowed years, flowing under Ashvale like sewage — it stirred us. Me, and others older and smaller than me. We are waking hungry, Petra Dravenmoor, and we are finding our altars toppled and our names scratched off every stone."

"The blood idol in the Tanner's Market," she said. "The beggar whose fever broke."

"One of ours. Yes." His eyes were pale green, the exact green of water over stones, and they did not blink often enough. "You saw that prayer answered. Don't tell me you felt nothing."

She had felt something. She had stood in the crowd while a shrine idol wept thin red tears into its cupped hands, and she had felt the sleeping animals of her stolen past stir against their cage, turning over, dreaming aloud. Terror, yes. But underneath the terror, something worse. Something like homesickness for a place she'd never been.

"What do you want from me?"

"To offer a trade." He said it simply, without the oily cadence guild-fences used around the word. "You need a road into the court beneath the mountain. I have one. I was ferrying souls across the dark water long before Aurelax built his cathedral of contracts, and the old roads remember me, even if the new gods don't."

Her heart stumbled. "How do you know about—"

"I told you. I've been watching you work. The page you took. The child it belongs to." He leaned closer, and the gulls along the eaves leaned with him, forty heads tilting in perfect unison. "Maren. Nine years old. Soul due at the dark moon. You mean to steal her back. Good. That is a holy act, whatever your guild calls it. I will open the way for you."

"And the price?"

"Ah." He smiled, and it was a kind smile, which made it terrible. "A memory of your choosing. One only. Small or great, recent or distant, but freely given, and truly surrendered — none of your thieving craft, none of stealing it back later. A payment in the old style, when gods still bargained honestly with mortals."

* * *

Petra walked to the end of the alley and back. Twice. The gulls watched her the entire time, and the god watched her, sitting again upon his crate with the patience of something that had slept three centuries and could wait three more.

Every rational thing she knew told her to refuse. Gods did not bargain fairly; the guild's own archives proved it, shelf after shelf of contracts where the mortal's signature was the only honest line. This creature knew her face, her name-that-wasn't-her-name, her thefts and her intentions. He was either exactly what he claimed or something far worse wearing the shape of it, and either way, the wise move was a knife and a fast exit.

But wisdom had never gotten anyone out of Ashvale alive. Wisdom kept ledgers. Wisdom balanced accounts until the accounts balanced *you.*

And underneath the calculation, quieter than the gulls, louder than reason, was the ache.

It lived in her chest the way a splinter lives under skin — small, constant, inflaming whenever she pressed near it. The ache of the unlived past. Every night she dreamed of a voice reading names, and every morning she woke with salt on her lips and no memory of why. She had stolen hundreds of memories in her time, and she knew their texture intimately, the warmth of them, the weight. So she knew with a thief's certainty that the hollow in her chest was not emptiness. Emptiness has no edges. This had edges. Something had been cut out of her, cleanly, professionally, by hands like her own.

"Serevanne," she said, testing it again. It fit her mouth strangely, like a shoe worn by someone else, and yet — there was a rightness under the strangeness. A key sliding toward a lock it had been cut for. "If that was my name. Who took it?"

The god's smile faded. For a moment he looked very old, and very tired, and very sorry.

"You did," he said. "To save it. There are things I could tell you, little clerk, but telling isn't the same as returning, and some truths, delivered whole, would crack you like a dropped plate. Ask the mountain. Ask Aurelax, the god-king in his cathedral of debts. He remembers you better than I do. He remembers everything." He extended his hand again, palm up, empty. "So. The memory. Choose carefully. Whatever you give me, I will keep safe — that much I swear on the Greywater, on the fords and the ferry and the miller's daughter's dry feet. But choose knowing that what you surrender, you surrender forever."

Petra looked at his open hand.

What did she have? A life assembled from other people's leavings and her own careful lies. A mother's face — no, she flinched from that one without meaning to, some instinct slamming a door. The taste of the first rain after the summer drought, which she'd bought off a dying sailor for two coppers and treasured like a jewel. The smell of Odell's workshop, resin and lamp oil, the closest thing to home she possessed. Vess Corvane saying *you look tired* with kindness in her mouth and a hook behind it.

Or — the thought arrived unbidden, cold and clear — one of the dreams. The ones where the voice read names. If she gave away the dream, maybe she'd sleep whole for the first time in years. Maybe the ache would finally close over, smooth as scar tissue.

Maybe. Or maybe the dream was a thread, and pulling it free would unravel whatever fragile stitching still held her together.

"You hesitate," the god observed. "Good. Hesitation means you understand the weight of things. My brother-gods who woke before me found no one left in Ashvale who understood the weight of anything. Your whole city trades in lightness. Shadows, years, names. Nothing held, everything sold." His green eyes fixed on hers. "But you hold on, Petra Dravenmoor. Even your thefts are a kind of holding. I've watched you carry other people's memories like orphaned children. Which is why I chose you. Which is why we—" he caught himself, jaw tightening, "*I* chose you."

"*We*," Petra repeated. "You started to say *we.* Who is we?"

The gulls shifted on their perches, a rustle like a page turning. The god rose again, and this time the alley itself seemed to rise with him, shadows lengthening, the brine-smell sharpening into something older, tidal, primordial.

"We," he said quietly, "are the ones your ledgers forgot. And we are calling in our debts. Three days, Petra Dravenmoor. The dark moon comes, and with it the guild's audit and the child's delivery. If you want the road beneath the mountain, bring me your payment before the second bell tomorrow night. Come alone. Come willing." He stepped backward into the shadow between the barrels, and the shadow received him like water receiving a stone — no splash, no ripple, just absence. "And when you choose what to give me, choose the thing you're most afraid to lose. That's how you'll know it has value."

The alley emptied. The gulls exploded upward all at once, shrieking, and the ordinary noise of Ashvale flooded back — carts, hawkers, the slap of fish on wood — as if a hand had lifted from the city's throat.

Petra stood alone in the fishmonger's alley with a knife she no longer remembered drawing and a name dissolving on her tongue like sugar in rain.

*Serevanne.*

She walked home the long way, through streets she didn't see, her mind turning the offer over and over like a coin bitten for gold. By the time she reached her rooms above the chandler's shop, dusk had thickened to true dark, and the dark moon would rise tomorrow night — no moon at all, just a hole in heaven where the light should be.

She lit no lamps. She sat on the edge of her narrow bed, knife across her knees, and made herself inventory her life the way she inventoried a ledger, line by line, asset by asset, hunting for the entry she could bear to strike.

The sailor's rain. Odell's workshop. Vess's poisoned kindness. Maren's face, solemn and trusting, the day Petra had shown her a card trick and the girl had laughed —

No. Not Maren. Never Maren. She needed her guilt sharp; it was the only compass she trusted anymore.

Which left the dreams. The voice reading names. The salt on her lips each morning. The thread she was most afraid to pull, because it was tied, she now understood, to everything else — to the hollow with edges, to the name *Serevanne*, to the courtroom beneath the mountain that surfaced sometimes behind her eyes like a drowned ship.

*Choose the thing you're most afraid to lose. That's how you'll know it has value.*

Outside her window, Ashvale settled into night, and somewhere beneath the city, beneath the mountain, something vast turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to count.

Petra closed her eyes and reached, for the first time in years, deliberately, toward the dream.
