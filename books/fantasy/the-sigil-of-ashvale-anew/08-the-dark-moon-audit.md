## 8. The Dark Moon Audit

The dark moon came the way all terrible things came to Ashvale: quietly, on a Tuesday, smelling of tallow and wet stone.

Petra felt it before she saw it. The counting house had grown colder over the past week, as though the building itself were holding its breath, and now, on the morning of the audit, the candles in their iron sconces burned with a strange, flattened light—flames that gave warmth to nothing. She stood at her desk with her hands flat on the ledger's cover and listened to the guild wake around her: the scrape of shutters, the cough of the furnace in the basement where they melted down forfeited wedding rings, the distant murmur of clerks reciting the Oath of Accurate Entry like children reciting a psalm against nightmares.

*All that is written is owed. All that is owed is written.*

Her codex lay hidden three floors below her feet, wrapped in oilcloth inside the false bottom of a salt barrel in Odell's cellar. She had copied every page by candlelight over eleven nights, her memory-thief hands moving with a fluency that frightened her—that *should* have frightened her more than it did. Eleven nights of transcribing traded shadows and bottled names and borrowed years, and each night the ink had seemed to come easier, as if the ledger recognized its own blood being drawn.

And on the twelfth page of the copy, Maren's contract sat reproduced in Petra's careful hand, the sigil rendered exactly, down to the authentication mark she had written herself in a life she could not remember.

Today Vess Corvane would demand the ledgers. Today the auditors would come with their scales and their silver styluses and their eyes like appraisal lamps. Today, if anyone looked too closely at the margins of anything, Petra Dravenmoor would be unmade in a room full of people who would call it bookkeeping.

She had rehearsed this. She had rehearsed it the way she rehearsed thefts: backward, forward, from the guard's point of view, from the lock's point of view. Surrender the master ledger without hesitation. Keep your breathing even. Let them find nothing because you have already decided what they will find.

But rehearsal was not the same as faith, and faith was not something Petra had ever been able to afford.

* * *

Vess Corvane dressed for the audit in grey silk the color of ash after rain, which was, Petra suspected, an entirely deliberate joke. She stood at the head of the long table in the Hall of Accounts while clerks arranged the ledgers in their prescribed order—oldest debts at the center, newest at the rim, the whole arrangement forming a spiral that was supposed to represent the guild's dominion over time itself. It looked, to Petra, like a snail shell. It looked like something waiting to be stepped on.

"The auditors arrive within the hour," Vess said. Her voice carried the particular smoothness of someone who had never once needed to raise it. "You will answer their questions precisely. You will volunteer nothing. You will remember that the Guild of Ashvale has survived nine hundred years of dark moons, and it will survive this one, provided no one does anything *stupid*."

Her gaze moved along the row of senior clerks and settled, briefly, warmly, on Petra.

It should have been reassuring. That was the horror of it. Vess had been kind to her since the night she'd found out about the stolen page—not openly, nothing so crude—but in the small currencies of belonging. A cup of the good tea set at Petra's elbow without comment. Her name spoken with approval in front of the under-clerks. Two days ago, Vess had rested a hand on her shoulder in the corridor and said, *You've become indispensable, Petra. I intend to see you properly rewarded.* And Petra had felt the old, starved animal in her chest turn toward that warmth like a plant toward a window.

She knew what it was. She had stolen enough memories to know how love was counterfeited. But knowing did not stop the turning.

"The god-touched seizures," one of the clerks began—"the items from the shrine quarter—"

"Are catalogued and sealed," Vess said. "The idol weeps for beggars; we do not weep with it. Next."

But Petra watched her mistress's hands as she said it, and Vess Corvane's hands were not steady. Nine hundred years the guild had operated in a world where gods were a rumor, a dead language, a market nobody traded in anymore. Nine hundred years of monopoly. And now idols bled in the fishmarket and answered prayers, and somewhere beneath everything, the foundation stones of the impossible trades had begun, very softly, to crack.

*You know,* Petra thought, watching her. *You know who holds your charter. You know whose court sits under the mountain. You know exactly what wakes when the old prayers start getting answers.*

*And you are afraid.*

It was the first comfort Petra had taken in weeks, and she took it greedily.

* * *

They came at noon, when the light through the high windows fell in pale bars across the floor: three auditors in dove-grey robes, faces powdered to a uniform pallor, carrying their instruments in lacquered cases like surgeons. The senior one was a woman with a voice like dry paper unrolling. They did not introduce themselves. Auditors of the Ledger Compact were introduced by their seals, and their seals were shown only to those about to be measured.

The ritual took the shape it always took. First, the Reading: the master ledger opened at random seven times, seven entries read aloud, each verified against its supporting documents. Then, the Weighing: the guild's aggregate obligations poured into the great brass balance and compared against the standard weight, a lump of black iron older than Ashvale itself. Then, last and worst, the Confession: each officer of the guild, one by one, declaring before the seal whether any entry in their keeping had been altered, omitted, or forged.

Petra had confessed to this ritual eleven times in her career. Each time, she had lied, because her entire position in the guild was built on lies filed neatly in chronological order. Each time, the lie had held, because the auditors weighed words, and Petra had spent a lifetime learning to make words weightless.

This time there was a codex in a salt barrel across town containing every secret the guild owned, transcribed in her hand.

"Petra Dravenmoor." The paper-voiced auditor gestured her forward. "Keeper of Impossible Trades. Step to the seal."

She stepped. The brass seal on the floor was warm—the auditors kept it warm deliberately, she'd heard, because a cold seal made men flinch, and flinching registered. She put her bare foot on it, as the rite required, and felt the faint pulse of whatever lived inside the metal taking her measure.

"Do you swear, upon your own soul's standing, that the accounts entrusted to you are true?"

Here it was. The moment she had rehearsed until it wore smooth as river stone.

"I so swear," Petra said.

The seal pulsed once. Held. Pulsed again—and released.

She let her breath out slowly through her nose. Beside the auditor's shoulder, across the hall, Vess Corvane was watching her with an expression of such serene, attentive kindness that Petra's skin crawled. *I know about the codex*, that look said. *I know, and I am letting you stand there and lie, because I want to see how well you do it.*

Or perhaps it said nothing at all. Perhaps fear was simply inventing meanings again, the way fear always did.

Then the auditor spoke again, and the meaning stopped mattering.

"A final matter." The woman drew a document from her case—a writ, heavy paper, sealed in black wax stamped with a sigil Petra knew better than her own heartbeat. The coiled serpent of contracts. The mark of the court beneath the mountain. "By instruction of the Compact, this guild's obligation under Contract Seven-Seven-Four shall be discharged ahead of schedule. Delivery of the specified asset is moved from the dark moon following next to the dark moon imminent." The paper voice paused, delicately. "Three days hence."

The hall went very quiet. Somewhere in the building, the furnace coughed.

"Asset," said one of the senior clerks, frowning. "Which asset? Seven-seven-four was entered years ago, before my appointment—"

"It concerns a minor," the auditor said. "A debt-orphan of the shrine quarter. The court has expressed... eagerness."

Petra did not move. She had learned stillness the way other people learned languages, thoroughly and young, and she wore it now like armor over a heart that was trying to climb out through her ribs. *Maren.* Three days. Not twenty-eight. Three days, and the child's soul would go into the reliquary, and the reliquary would go down the mountain road, and all of Petra's careful treason would be worth exactly nothing.

Across the hall, Vess inclined her head. "The guild, of course, complies with the Compact's scheduling."

"Of course," the auditor agreed, and rolled up her writ.

Later—much later, when the dove-grey robes had gone and the clerks dispersed in murmuring knots—Vess found Petra alone at her desk and set a cup of the good tea beside her elbow. Steam curled between them like something summonable.

"Three days," Vess said pleasantly. "You'll want to prepare the transfer documents yourself. No one else touches Seven-Seven-Four. You're the most careful pair of hands I have."

"Yes, Guild Master."

"And Petra." Vess smiled, and it reached her eyes, which was somehow worse than if it hadn't. "Whatever you may be planning—whatever late nights and private projects you've been occupying yourself with—I want you to know that my protection covers you completely. Nothing in this guild can touch you. Nothing *outside* it either. As long as you remain mine."

The tea smelled of cardamom and something underneath it, faintly metallic, like coins held too long in a fist.

"Thank you, Guild Master," Petra said.

Vess patted the desk twice, gently, as if blessing it, and left her alone with the steam and the ticking candles and the arithmetic of three days.

* * *

Odell was waiting in the alley behind his own shop, which meant he was frightened. Odell waited indoors when he was calm; he waited behind locked doors when he was nervous; he only waited in alleys when he had passed beyond nervousness into the country where fear became a practical problem requiring practical solutions.

He didn't speak until she reached him. Even then he spoke low, eyes tracking the mouth of the alley.

"They moved it," he said. "Three days. You heard."

"I heard."

"That's not scheduling, Petra. That's a *response*. The court doesn't move a delivery up twenty-five days because it's eager. Something spooked them. Something down there knows a page went missing." He ran a hand through his thinning hair. "Or something up here told them."

Petra thought of the warm tea. The gentle hand on the desk. *My protection covers you completely.*

"She knows about the codex," Petra said. "Maybe not where it is. Maybe not what's in it. But she knows I have it, and she's tightening the leash before I decide what it's for."

Odell was quiet for a moment. In the grey afternoon light he looked older than his years, a fence grown old among stolen things, a man who had spent forty years handling other people's treasures and never once keeping any.

"Tomorrow night," he said finally. "Before the transfer documents get sealed. We move the codex out of my cellar—it isn't safe, Vess's people watch this street, I've counted them—and we take it to the third location. And then..." He hesitated.

"Then?"

"Then you go down the mountain, or you burn it here and run." He said it plainly, without judgment, which was why she trusted him. "Those are the roads, Petra. There's no clever third option where everyone lives. There never is. I've looked. Forty years I've looked."

"There's a third option," Petra said. "It's just expensive."

"Everything true is." He glanced at the alley mouth again, then pressed something into her hand: a small tin of salve, plain and unremarkable, the kind clerks used for ink-stained fingers. "For the burn on your palm. From two nights ago."

Petra stared at it. She had worn gloves. She had burned herself copying the sigil pages, and she had told no one, and she had bandaged it where no one looks.

"You substituted something," she said slowly, understanding arriving like cold water. "At the audit. The Confession—when the seal pulsed the second time—"

"Your codex weighs something, girl. Soul-stuff. Anyone with the right instruments feels it in a room." Odell shrugged, embarrassed, a fence caught mid-crime. "So I made sure the room already had something heavier in it. Forged confession token, seeded with grief-memory. Cost me six years off a bottle of borrowed time I'd been saving, but the seal drank that instead of looking at you." He met her eyes. "We're past the point where either of us can pretend the other is innocent. Whatever you're doing with that child's page—you're doing it with me now. That's what the forgery means. Complicity isn't a thing you can steal back."

The alley smelled of brine and coal smoke. Somewhere above them, gulls argued over nothing. Petra closed her hand around the salve tin, and around the shape of what Odell had just done—six years of his hoarded life, spent to keep her lie alive, given freely by a man who had never once kept anything.

"Three days," she said.

"Three days," Odell agreed. "Best spend them well."

That night, Petra did not sleep. She sat at her desk with the master ledger open to Contract Seven-Seven-Four and read Maren's name until the letters blurred, and in the deepest hour, when the candles burned flattest, she took out a fresh sheet and began, in a hand she almost remembered from another life, to draft the transfer documents—except that what she wrote was not a transfer at all.

It was a list. Of everything the court beneath the mountain was owed.

And at the bottom, in the space reserved for payment, she wrote two words: *Me instead.*

The candles guttered. Far away, beneath the city, beneath the bedrock, something ancient turned over in its sleep—and dreamed, for the first time in centuries, of being answered.
