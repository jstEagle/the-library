## 9. Memories Are Currency

The fishmonger's alley smelled of brine and old blood, and the god who lived in it was picking scales out of his beard when Petra found him again.

He sat on an overturned crate behind Tallow Row, where the morning's catch had been gutted and the gutters still ran pink. Gulls screamed overhead, wheeling through the grey light like scraps of torn paper. He looked exactly as she remembered — which was to say, ragged, unremarkable, a man the eye slid off of like rain off oiled canvas. Only his eyes refused the trick of it. They were the color of deep water over drowned things.

"You came," he said. Not surprised. Gods, she suspected, were never surprised; surprise was a mortal luxury they'd traded away for something worse.

"You said you knew my name."

"I do." He smiled, and there were too many years in that smile, centuries folded up small as a moth's wing. "But names aren't free, little thief. Nothing is free. That's the first law, older than light." He patted the crate beside him. "Sit. This will take longer than you want it to."

Petra didn't sit. She stood in the alley with her arms crossed against the cold coming off the harbor, and made herself ask the question she'd been carrying since the shrine, since the ledger, since the courtroom beneath her skin that surfaced in dreams and left her gasping salt water.

"What does it cost?"

The god tilted his head. "A memory. One. Of your choosing." He held out his hand, palm up, as if she might set the thing down on it like a coin. "That's the toll. The mountain doesn't open its roots for kindness or courage. It opens for payment. Everything beneath it runs on the same arithmetic your ledgers run on. I only speak its language."

"You're asking me to cut myself open and hand you a piece."

"I'm asking you to choose what you lose. Aurelax never gave anyone that courtesy." His voice softened, and the softness was somehow more frightening than the demand. "Choose well, Petra Dravenmoor. Some memories are load-bearing."

She laughed then — a short, ugly sound that startled the gulls. "Load-bearing. You say that like I'll know which ones hold up the roof."

"No one ever does." He spread his hands. "That's the cruelty of it. And the mercy. If you knew which memories held everything else upright, you could never pay anything at all."

* * *

She walked the city for hours afterward, trying to inventory herself.

It should have been simple. She had spent ten years taking memories from other people — lifting them clean as purses, folding them flat, storing them in wax-sealed boxes in the guild vaults. She knew the anatomy of remembering better than any surgeon knew the anatomy of flesh. She knew how a memory attached itself to a smell, a season, a song hummed half-remembered. She knew how to sever them.

She had simply never done it to herself.

She walked up through Tallow Row and into the Lanternmarket, where the stalls had gone strange in the weeks since the idol wept blood. A candle-seller had hung her whole booth with red tapers, praying aloud to no particular god, just praying at the air the way you'd throw grain at birds, hoping something hungry would land. A farrier pressed his thumb to his forehead before lighting his forge. Two children played a game with chalk sigils on the cobbles, drawing doors and stepping through nothing, laughing.

The old gods were waking. Everyone felt it, the way animals feel weather. And everyone was learning the same lesson Petra had learned over a counting-house candle: prayer had teeth now. It cost. It answered.

In the square outside the Guildhall, Vess Corvane's enforcers were nailing up new proclamations. PETITION NO GOD. REPORT ALL ANSWERED PRAYERS. The ink was barely dry and already someone had scrawled beneath it, in charcoal: *the gods are owed too.*

Petra pulled her hood lower and kept walking.

Maren. She thought about Maren constantly now, the way a tongue worries a broken tooth. Nine years old, debt-orphaned, scrubbing pots in the guild's laundry hall without knowing that the most valuable part of her — the part that would keep burning after everything else stopped — had been signed away to settle her parents' fees. Payment due at the dark moon. Three days now, not four. Vess had moved the schedule up, and Petra had pretended not to wonder why, and the pretense was eating her alive.

She thought about Maren, and she tried to find a memory worth less than Maren's life.

* * *

By evening she had made three lists, scratched in charcoal on the back of a pawn ticket.

The first list was memories she couldn't give: Odell's face across the card table the night he covered for her, saying nothing, choosing her. The smell of the counting house candles. The weight of the stolen page against her ribs. These weren't memories so much as the architecture holding her upright, and she knew it, and she hated that she knew it.

The second list was memories she couldn't bear to keep: the courtroom beneath the mountain that surfaced uninvited. The god-king's voice like coins pouring down a well. Those she wanted to surrender desperately — but when she reached for them, they slipped aside like fish, and she understood with cold clarity that they weren't hers to give. They belonged to whoever had taken her past. You cannot sell stolen goods back to their owner.

Which left the third list. The short list. The almost-empty list.

Her mother's face.

She sat with it a long time in the loft above Odell's shop, a single candle burning, her charcoal hovering over the pawn ticket until the tip crumbled.

Here was the truth she circled all night: she did not remember her mother's face. She remembered *that* she had known it. She remembered the shape of the knowing — warm, certain, worn smooth by use like a river stone — but the face itself had been taken from her long ago, hollowed out along with everything else, and what remained was the memory of having once possessed it. A socket where a jewel had been. She had carried this absence so long it had become a kind of presence, the way an amputee feels a hand.

And that was why it was safe to give. It was already gone. She would be selling the ghost of a ghost, the receipt without the purchase. It would hurt — it always hurt — but it wouldn't take anything she actually used.

She fell asleep telling herself this, and dreamed of a woman singing in a language she almost understood, and woke with her pillow wet, and told herself that meant nothing.

* * *

The god was waiting in the same alley, as if he hadn't moved in two days, as if alleys were simply places he wore.

"You've chosen," he said.

"Yes."

"Tell me what, and why. The mountain listens for reasons. Payment given carelessly bounces."

"My mother's face." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Or what's left of it. It's already a hole. I'm selling you a hole."

The god was very still. Somewhere beyond the alley mouth, a fishwife was arguing about herring prices, her voice rising and falling like gulls. "Little thief," he said quietly, "I'll ask once more, plainly. Choose something else. Anything else. There are memories in you I'd take gladly instead — the taste of your first stolen purse, the first lie you told well. Give me those."

"No."

"Why?"

Because those were hers. Earned, chosen, hers. Whatever else had been carved out of Petra Dravenmoor before she ever walked into Ashvale, the thief she'd built from the wreckage was built out of exactly these small sins and small triumphs, and she would not let a god have them. "Because it's the only thing I own that's already lost. Take it. Take it and open the door."

He looked at her for a long moment, and something in his ancient eyes might have been pity, or might have been the particular grief gods feel when mortals do arithmetic correctly and arrive at ruin anyway.

"As you wish."

He didn't touch her. He only opened his hand again, palm up, and waited, and Petra understood that she had to do the cutting herself. Of course she did. Memory-thief craft ran both ways; every thief knew the route out of a mind because they'd traveled in. She closed her eyes, found the socket where her mother's face should have been — smooth, warm, worn smooth by ten years of touching it in the dark — and lifted.

It came out easier than anything she had ever taken. Too easy. Like a key turning that had been waiting to turn.

The god caught it — she saw it briefly, a curl of pale gold light resting in his palm like a moth, humming faintly, humming a fragment of a lullaby in a language she almost understood —

— and the alley *changed*.

Not visibly. Visibly it was the same alley, the same crates, the same pink gutter-water. But suddenly it was also somewhere else, the way a word is also its meaning. The stones under her feet were stones, and they were also the roots of a mountain, and the roots went down farther than stone had any right to go.

"Oh," the god said softly, staring at the golden moth in his palm. "Oh, you foolish, brave girl. Do you know what you just handed me?"

"A hole."

"A key." He closed his fist around the light, and the lullaby stopped mid-note, and the loss of it hit Petra like a physical blow — grief for something she couldn't name, grief with no object, just pure shapeless mourning flooding through her, and she staggered and caught the wall. "Your mother's face wasn't taken by accident, Petra. It was the lock on the rest. Aurelax sealed your past inside the memory of her face — the one thing he knew you'd guard even hollow. You've been carrying the door to yourself for twenty years and selling locks your whole career."

The world tilted. She put her back against the wet brick and breathed. Fish-brine and blood and the faint, fading sweetness of the lullaby.

"You knew," she whispered. "You knew when you offered."

"I hoped I was wrong." He met her eyes, and didn't flinch from what he found in them. "I told you some memories are load-bearing. I gave you every chance."

"You took it anyway."

"I did." No apology. Gods didn't apologize; apology required equals. "The door is paid for. Will you walk through it or not? Maren's dark moon comes in three days, and every hour you spend hating me is an hour she spends becoming currency."

* * *

The door, when he opened it, was not a door.

He led her to the end of the alley, where the harbor wall met the cliff face of Mount Cael — the mountain whose roots the city had grown around like moss on a bone. There, where centuries of fish-guts and tide-stain blackened the rock, the god pressed his closed fist against the stone and spoke a word that Petra's ears heard as silence and her bones heard as a bell.

The rock unfolded.

Not broke. *Unfolded*, petals of stone peeling back from a passage that had always been there, hidden the way the second list of a ledger hides between honest columns. Cold air breathed out of it, smelling of deep earth and old metal and something sweeter underneath — incense, maybe, or rot dressed as incense.

"The abandoned shafts," the god said. "The miners dug toward the court a hundred years ago and the court let them come close enough to die. What you seek is below. The reliquaries. The contract vaults. Him." He stepped back from the doorway, and his ragged shape seemed suddenly thinner, as if the opening drew substance from everything near it. "There's a road down. Petitioners used it. You'll see what became of petitioners."

"And you?" Petra's voice was raw. "You just stay here? In the alley?"

"For now. Waking gods keep small houses." He smiled, and for a heartbeat the smile was ancient and terrible and lit from below, and she saw the deep water again, and the drowned things, moving. "When you stand before Aurelax, little thief — and you will stand before him, he'll summon you himself, he knows his clerk's footsteps better than his own heartbeat — remember that the others are waking too. Every answered prayer is a god rolling over in his grave of neglect. He has eaten prayers for centuries. Prayers are owed. And we—" the smile widened, "—we keep excellent books."

He pressed something into her hand: a small grey stone, sea-smoothed, ordinary. "For the dark places. When you can't tell which memories are yours, bite down on this. Salt remembers."

Then he turned back toward the harbor, gathering his rags around him, becoming again a man the eye slid off, and Petra Dravenmoor stood alone before the unfolded dark with a hole in her chest shaped like a lullaby and no mother's face to mourn.

She had sold a ghost and lost a key. She had paid for a door into hell with the one thing that might have led her back out of herself.

*Below*, she thought. Maren was below. Three days. The rest — her past, her name, whatever child she had been before Aurelax hollowed her — that could wait in the dark with everything else the mountain kept.

Petra touched the sea-stone in her pocket one last time, tasted salt on her fingers, and stepped through.

Behind her, the stone began, slowly, patiently, to fold itself closed.
