## 10. Descent

The door the alley god had opened was not a door. It was a wound in the mountain's root, a seam of darkness where the stone had been peeled back like fruit skin, and it breathed.

Petra felt that breath on her face before she took the first step down — warm, wet, tasting faintly of copper and candle smoke and something older underneath, something like libraries left too long in the rain. The shaft beyond swallowed the last grey light of Ashvale's dusk within six steps, and after that there was only the lantern Odell carried, swinging its small yellow defiance against all that dark.

"You could still turn around," he said. Not for the first time. He'd said it at the mouth of the alley, and again when she'd bought the rope, and once more when the fishmonger's cat had spat at them both as they passed — which Petra chose to take as an omen, though of what kind she couldn't say.

"So could you," she said.

"I've got nothing below." He shifted the lantern higher on its hook-stick, and the light threw his shadow huge and crooked up the shaft wall. "You've got everything."

"That's why I'm going down," Petra said, "and you're coming with me. Someone has to carry the lantern while I steal things."

Odell laughed — a short, startled sound, quickly strangled, as though laughter were also contraband this far beneath the city. But his hand found her shoulder in the dark, brief and steadying, and then they went on.

* * *

The mine-shafts had been abandoned for a century or more, if you counted by the guild's calendars. If you counted by the dust, they had been abandoned since the world was younger. It lay over everything like grey snow: the rusted rails, the collapsed timber braces furred with white mineral bloom, the empty ore-carts sitting in their sidings like coffins waiting for occupants who had never come. Their boots left prints in it, two lines of small disturbances winding down into the earth, and Petra kept looking back at those prints and thinking of ledgers — of marks made in soft material, meant to be read by whoever followed.

She hoped nobody followed. She hoped somebody did. She could not decide which hope frightened her more.

The air changed as they descended. In Ashvale above, even at night, even in the poor quarters, there was always noise — gulls, gulls arguing over fish-guts, cart wheels, the bells of the counting house marking hours nobody wanted to keep. Here there was nothing but their own sounds, multiplied and returned to them from the stone: breath, bootfall, the creak of Odell's harness, the drip of water somewhere far off, patient as a clerk counting coins. And under it all, so low she might have imagined it, a hum. Deep. Rhythmic. Like a heart beating inside the mountain, or like a choir heard through ten feet of cathedral wall.

"Do you hear that?" she asked.

Odell stopped. Listened. His face in the lantern-light had gone the colour of tallow. "Aye," he said. "Been hearing it since we passed the second shaft. Thought it was me."

"It's not you."

"No," he said quietly. "I don't suppose it is." He wiped his palms on his coat, one after the other, a bookkeeper's gesture, a man putting his accounts in order before presenting them. "Petra. Before we go further. There's something I have to tell you, and I'd rather tell it standing up."

* * *

He would not sit, so they stood, in a widening of the tunnel where an old ventilation chamber opened off the main shaft, its ceiling lost in blackness, its walls carved — she saw now, in the full reach of the lantern's throw — with marks. Not graffiti. Marks of petition. Hundreds of them, thousands, scratched into the stone by hands at every height: names, mostly, and beneath each name a want. *Bread. A son home from the sea. My eyes. Forgiveness.* Some were ancient, worn smooth as river stones by time and touch. Some were fresh enough that the dust of their carving still glittered in the grooves.

"They came down here to ask," Odell said. "Before the guild built the counting house. Before Ashvale had a name, maybe. They came down into the mountain and asked for what they needed." He touched one of the carvings — *my eyes* — with one finger, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "And something down there answered them. Or didn't. And they either got what they wanted or they stayed."

"Stayed?"

"You'll see," Odell said, and his voice did something she had never heard it do. It cracked. Odell, who appraised stolen saints' knucklebones without blinking; Odell, who had once talked a customs officer out of a sealed crate of bottled names with nothing but a forged manifest and a smile; Odell, whose voice was the most reliable instrument in the guild's lower ranks, cracked like cheap glaze. "Petra, I need to say this now because I won't be able to say it lower down, and because if I don't say it you'll find out anyway, and then you'll know I let you walk into it blind."

"Then say it."

He looked at the lantern. At the carved walls. At his own hands.

"When I was nineteen," he said, "I sold a memory."

The words dropped into the silence of the chamber like a coin down a well, and for a long moment neither of them moved, and the hum beneath their feet went on beating, indifferent.

"Not to the court," he said. "Not directly. Through a broker in Salt Row, one of the guild's cut-outs, the kind we ourselves use when we don't want our fingerprints on a trade. It wasn't a big memory. That's what I told myself, anyway. That's what the broker told me. A small thing. An afternoon. I'd been saving for my mother's apothecary debts, and the price was good, and—" His hands made a small helpless motion, opening, closing. "It was the afternoon my father taught me to swim. In the mill pond. I remember that I remember it. I remember that it mattered. I remember the shape of it, like a coat left behind in a house you've moved out of. But the afternoon itself is gone. There's just this hole in me, worn smooth, and I've carried it thirty years, and I know exactly how heavy it is."

Petra said nothing. Her throat was too tight.

"The broker told me it wouldn't hurt," Odell said. "They always say that. They told me I wouldn't miss it, because you can't miss what you don't remember having. And he was wrong, Petra. He was lying, or he didn't know, and either way he was wrong. You miss it. You miss it every day of your life. You wake some morning with your hand half-reached toward something that isn't there anymore, and you spend the whole day pretending you weren't reaching." He turned to look at her fully, and his eyes in the lantern-light were wet and furious and old. "That's what they do down there. That's what the god-king does. He doesn't take souls whole — not usually. He takes them in pieces. He takes the afternoon your father taught you to swim, and he takes the smell of bread, and he takes the face of your mother, and he takes them one at a time, politely, with receipts, until there's so little of you left that taking the rest is barely theft at all. It's housekeeping."

"And you know," Petra said slowly, "because—"

"Because I've seen the antechambers." He said it fast, before courage could fail him. "Twenty years ago. Early in my fence-work, before I knew what half the guild's inventory was. A broker I worked with went missing, and I went looking for him, and I got as far as the first of the courts below. I saw what the petitions become. I saw the ones who asked too much, or too long, or whose asking the god decided to keep." He gestured at the walls, at the thousand carved wants. "I ran. I ran all the way back up and I told myself the guild was just commerce, just trade, that what happened below was below, that it wasn't mine to balance." He laughed, hollow. "I'm a fence, Petra. Balancing is supposed to be my whole trade. I've spent thirty years not balancing this account, and I am done with it. So. Now you know. Whatever you find below — whatever I find below — I'm not turning around. But you deserved to walk in knowing what walks beside you."

Petra reached out and took the lantern from him. He let her.

"Odell," she said. "What did you sell? What was the afternoon?"

"My father teaching me to swim. Mill pond. Summer."

"Keep it," she said. "Whatever happens down there — however far this goes, whatever they offer us, whatever we have to burn to get out — you keep that hole. Because the hole remembers the shape of what was taken, and the shape is yours, and one day — I don't know how, I don't know when, but the gods are waking, Odell, I've seen one in a fishmonger's alley and I've felt them stirring in my own skull — one day someone is going to give it back. And I want you alive and above ground when they do."

For a moment Odell's face worked through several expressions and settled on none of them. Then he nodded, once, hard, and took the lantern back, and turned down the tunnel.

"Come on, then," he said gruffly. "We've a soul to steal and a mountain to insult."

* * *

They passed the first of the salt-petitioners an hour later.

Petra smelled it before she saw it — or rather, she smelled the absence of anything, a clean blank spot in the tunnel's tapestry of rot and mineral and old wood, and then the lantern swung and there it stood, and she understood why Odell had cracked.

It had been human. That was the horror of it, the specific and deliberate horror: it had not been melted or dissolved or consumed. It had simply been *finished*. A figure stood against the tunnel wall, upright, composed, its hands folded at its waist in an attitude of patient waiting, and every inch of it was salt. White, translucent, faintly luminous salt, shot through with veins of grey where the stone behind showed through. The face was the worst part. The features remained — nose, brow, lips slightly parted, as if mid-plea — but smoothed, softened, rendered into something between sculpture and sediment. It looked less like a dead person than like a person being slowly, lovingly erased.

At its feet lay a small heap of white grains, spilled from somewhere on its body, catching the lantern-light like sugar.

"Petitioner," Odell whispered. "One of the ones who stayed."

Petra approached it the way she approached any locked thing: carefully, reading it. Up close she could see details the lantern's edge had hidden — the texture of the salt was not uniform. It was layered, like wax drippings, like deposits left over years. This person had not turned to salt in a moment. They had turned to salt across decades, standing here, waiting, asking, while the mountain took from them grain by grain. And on the figure's chest, scratched deep into the saline surface by a finger that must already have been stiffening — a name.

She read it aloud before she could stop herself.

"*Mira, who asks for her daughter.*"

The hum beneath their feet swelled, very slightly, and subsided. Like a breath drawn in recognition.

"Don't do that," Odell said sharply, and Petra realized he meant the reading. "Names are currency down here. Don't spend them."

But it was too late, and she knew it was too late, because the moment the name had left her mouth, the petitioner's salt face had changed. Not much. A degree. The parted lips had pressed closed. The waiting attitude had deepened somehow, the way a sentry settles when relieved. As if somewhere in that eroded remainder, something had heard itself named, and remembered being a person, and was grateful.

Petra's eyes burned. "How many?"

"A hundred in the first chamber alone," Odell said. "When I was here. Twenty years ago." He did not look at the salt figure. He could not. "There are nine chambers."

* * *

They walked among them for what felt like a night and no part of a day.

The second chamber held dozens. The third held hundreds. They stood along the walls and in the alcoves and clustered at the mouths of side-shafts, all of them salt, all of them waiting, all of them wearing the posture of their final question — hands cupped, arms raised, kneeling, one woman frozen mid-curtsy as though she'd been petitioning a king rather than a god, and perhaps she had. Each bore a name scratched or carved or, in the oldest cases, worn nearly to nothing. Each had spilled a drift of grains at their feet, their own slow erosion, their own ledger of loss written in what they'd shed.

Petra did not read any more names aloud. But she read them with her eyes, because she could not help it, because she was a thief of memories and these were memories in their most terrible distillation — the memory of wanting, preserved past all use. *A boat. Rain for the fields. My brother, returned from the war. To be forgiven. To be forgiven. To be forgiven.*

In the fifth chamber she stopped, because one of the figures was small.

Child-sized. Kneeling. Its little salt hands pressed together, its salt face tilted upward toward a ceiling it had never reached, and around its neck, preserved in the mineral like a fly in amber, hung a cord with a wooden bead on it — a debt-orphan's tally-bead, the kind the guild issued, the kind Maren wore.

Petra stood in front of it for a long time. Odell stood beside her and said nothing, because there was nothing to say, and the hum beat on beneath them, patient, vast, well-fed.

"We're going to burn it all," she said at last. Quietly. Not a vow, quite. More like a line entered into a ledger, awaiting only the counterweight. "Every contract. Every page."

"Aye," said Odell. "I'll hold the pen."

* * *

The ninth chamber ended at a threshold.

There was no door. Doors implied the possibility of remaining shut, and nothing in this place had ever been permitted to remain shut. Instead the tunnel simply widened, and the rough-hewn stone gave way to fitted blocks, and the fitted blocks gave way to polished marble veined with gold, and the hum resolved at last into something she could hear clearly: voices. Distant, layered, countless — murmuring, pleading, reciting, a tide-sound of petition rising from somewhere ahead and below, filtered through architecture designed to make begging sound like music.

Warm light leaked around the corner of the passage ahead, amber and gold, trembling with the flicker of a thousand candles. The smell reached them next: beeswax, ink, hot metal, and under all of it the dry sweet scent she had smelled on the contract's page, the sigil's seal, the reliquary vaults of the guild's deepest holdings.

Old paper. Old power. Old bargains.

Petra checked her thief's kit one last time in the shelter of the last alcove: picks, waxes, the vial of alley-god's ash, the folded page of Maren's contract pressed flat against her ribs where her heartbeat could keep track of it. Odell checked his: chalk, twine, the forged auditor's tokens, his face set in the expression he wore for customs men — pleasant, forgettable, utterly false.

"Together?" he said.

"Together."

They stepped around the corner, two small figures in borrowed auditor's grey, into the amber glow of the court beneath the mountain —

— and the murmuring tide of voices stopped.

All of it. Every voice, every whisper, every recited plea, silenced at once, the way a room goes quiet when the master of the house enters. Somewhere ahead, unseen, unhurried, something vast turned its attention toward the doorway, toward the two thieves standing in it, and a voice like a ledger closing spoke:

"*Enter, then. I have been balancing your account for some time.*"

Behind them, in the ninth chamber, every salt petitioner turned its head.
