## 11. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The dark ended all at once.

One moment Petra was feeling her way along a mine-shaft so narrow that Odell had to walk behind her with his breath held, and the next the stone simply opened, like a mouth deciding to speak, and they stood at the lip of a gallery carved from the mountain's living root—black granite veined with something that glowed faintly gold, as if the rock itself had swallowed candlelight and never learned to give it back.

Below them, the court spread out in tiers, descending in perfect concentric rings toward a floor of polished obsidian. It should have been beautiful. It was beautiful, in the way a spider's web is beautiful if you are not the fly. Every surface bore writing. The walls were inscribed floor to unseen ceiling with clauses and covenants, signatures and seals, some in scripts she recognized from the guild's ledgers and some in alphabets older than ink, older than paper, characters that looked less written than grown, like frost or lichen or the rings inside a tree. And everywhere, everywhere, the gold veins pulsed. Slowly. Rhythmically. Like a heartbeat that belonged to no one in particular and everyone at once.

"The whole mountain," Odell said quietly, "is one contract."

Petra didn't answer. She was listening. From somewhere far below came a sound she had no name for—a sound like a bell being struck underwater, like a coin dropped down an endless well, like a voice reading terms aloud to someone who could not refuse. It rose through the tiers in waves, and each wave made the gold veins brighten, and each brightening was followed by a dimming, a small death of light, as though the mountain exhaled grief and called it payment.

They descended by the petitioners' stair, a spiral cut into the outer wall, wide enough for two abreast, worn smooth in its center by centuries of feet. Some of those feet had belonged to people who never left. Petra saw the first of them on the fourth turning: a figure standing at the rail, robed in what had once been fine wool, hands folded, head bowed—and entirely, perfectly white. Not pale. White. Salt-white, down to the folds of the robe and the individual strands of hair, a person rendered into mineral mid-petition, frozen in the posture of asking. Her face was smooth. Whatever expression she'd worn when the last of her was taken had dissolved with her flesh.

"They waited too long," Odell murmured. "Or asked for too much."

"How do you know?"

"I've sold memories, Petra. I know what it looks like when there's nothing left to sell." He touched the salt figure's sleeve, gently, the way you touch something in a museum, and a grain of it crumbled away and drifted down into the dark. "The court doesn't kill them. It just keeps collecting until there's no one home to stop."

There were more of them as they went down. Dozens. Hundreds. A congregation of the emptied, standing at their stations around every tier, each one oriented toward the center of the court, each one still, after all this time, waiting for an answer. Petra made herself look at their faces. She told herself it was necessary—she needed to understand this place—but the truth was uglier. She couldn't stop looking. Something in her recognized the posture. The bowed head. The folded hands. The patience of the hollowed.

*You have stood like that,* whispered a memory that wasn't hers yet, stirring at the base of her skull like sediment in disturbed water. *You have stood exactly like that.*

She pushed it down. There would be time for drowning later.

* * *

At the bottom of the stair stood a desk.

It was absurd, at first glance—the whole vast cathedral of debt and divinity, and here at its threshold a plain wooden desk, scarred and ink-stained, exactly like ten thousand desks in ten thousand counting houses, with a clerk seated behind it. Except the clerk was not a person. It was roughly the shape of one, tall and thin, its skin the gray of old parchment, its face blank except where features had been pressed into it like a thumbprint into wax: two shallow dents for eyes, a crease for a mouth. It wore spectacles. It did not need spectacles. It wore them because clerks wear spectacles, because form mattered here more than function, because this entire place was bureaucracy that had forgotten it was supposed to be pretending to be something else.

"State your business," it said. Its voice was dry as a turned page.

Petra had rehearsed this on the stair, and again in the fishmonger's alley, and once, shamefully, in front of her washbasin mirror. She straightened her shoulders, arranged her face into the specific blend of tedium and authority that Vess Corvane wore like armor, and produced the token the alley god had given her—a sliver of black metal stamped with a sigil she pretended not to examine too closely.

"Guild auditor," she said. "Ashvale Guild of Impossible Trades. We're conducting a compliance review of outstanding soul-instruments. Ledger reconciliation. Nothing personal."

"Nothing," the clerk repeated, in a tone that suggested the word had been submitted for review and found wanting. "Auditors are expected."

It waved them through without another question, which frightened Petra more than interrogation would have. Expected. The guild had been coming here so long, selling so much, that the court had stopped bothering to check. Somewhere above, in a candlelit counting house, children's souls moved across a page the way grain moved across a merchant's table, and below, the machinery that received them had grown smooth with use.

The clerk's shallow eye-dents tracked them as they passed. Petra felt them like fingers on the back of her neck.

* * *

The floor of the court was warm.

Not sun-warm. Body-warm. The obsidian underfoot held a heat that had no source she could find, and it rose through the soles of her boots with a slow pulse that matched the pulsing of the gold veins in the walls, and Petra understood, with a lurch of nausea, that she was walking on something alive. Or something that had been alive. The floor was warm the way a recently vacated bed is warm.

In the center of the court, raised on three steps of veined stone, stood the bench—not a judge's bench but an altar dressed as one, a slab of black rock wide enough to lie a body on, flanked by pillars heaped with offerings. Coins, mostly. Mountains of coins, fused together by age and pressure into glittering geological strata. But threaded through the gold were other things: locks of hair tied with ribbon. Baby teeth strung like beads. Letters, thousands of letters, sealed with tears that had dried to amber. A child's shoe. A wedding ring still bearing the shape of the finger it had outlived.

And above the bench, hanging in the air without support, burned the ledger.

It was enormous—taller than two men, its pages each the size of a door, bound in something dark that might have been leather and might have been skin and Petra decided firmly not to think about which. It turned its own pages. They moved in slow rhythm, back and forth, back and forth, and wherever a page settled open, light poured off the writing on it in colors Petra had no names for, and the gold veins in the walls flared in answer, and the warmth in the floor surged, and somewhere in the tiers a salt figure crumbled a little further.

"That's the ledger of names," Odell breathed. He had gone the color of tallow. "Every soul ever bought. Every name ever signed away. It's—it's reading itself."

"It's auditing itself," Petra corrected, and hated how easily the word came, how fluently she spoke this place's language. That was the horror of it, she realized. Not the salt figures, not the fused coins, not even the ledger bound in who-knew-what. The horror was that she understood it. The horror was that it made sense. Debts entered. Debts collected. Interest accrued in grief because grief was the only currency mortals minted endlessly. She had spent eleven years balancing books full of borrowed years and bottled names, and she had told herself the guild's arithmetic was merely ugly, merely human greed wearing a clerk's collar. But it wasn't human. It had never been human. It was a theology, and Ashvale was its parish, and she had been its acolyte without knowing there was a god at the altar.

Around the edges of the court, in alcoves cut into the lowest tier, other clerks worked. Dozens of them, parchment-gray and patient, bent over desks, copying, stamping, filing. Souls, Petra realized. Actual souls, kept in vessels—stoppered vials, sealed boxes, small lead caskets—stacked on shelves behind them like inventory. Each vessel gave off a faint light, and each light moved. Flickered. Turned over in its container the way a sleeper turns in bed. She watched one vial for a long moment and saw the light inside it press against the glass, deliberately, twice, like a hand knocking.

No one opened it.

"Bureaucracy made divine," she whispered. "Divinity made bureaucratic." She didn't know which direction the corruption flowed anymore. Maybe it didn't matter. Maybe it had flowed both ways so long that the current had worn a channel between them, and now godhood and bookkeeping were simply the same river seen from opposite banks.

* * *

Finding the reliquary was almost easy. That was the part that frightened her most.

The vaults lay beyond the court proper, down a corridor lined with more salt figures—petitioners who had gotten close enough to see the altar before the last of them was taken—and through a door of black wood carved with the same sigil that authenticated Maren's contract. The sigil she had drawn herself, in a life she couldn't remember, with a hand she shouldn't possess. She had touched that carving on the way in and felt the stone hum against her palm like a struck tuning fork, like recognition, and she had snatched her hand back and told Odell it was nothing.

Inside, the reliquary vault was cold. After the body-warmth of the court, the cold felt like mercy, and she distrusted it immediately. Mercy was not something this place distributed for free.

Shelves rose to a ceiling lost in shadow, and on the shelves, in ordered rows, in labeled compartments, sat the souls. Thousands of them. Each in its vessel, each vessel tagged with a strip of parchment bearing a name, a date, a sum. Petra walked the aisles slowly, reading tags. Names she didn't know. Dates going back further than Ashvale had existed. Sums entered in currencies she'd only ever seen referenced in the guild's oldest folios: *three winters of joy; the memory of a first love; the sound of a dead man's laugh; one childhood, complete.*

And then, on a shelf at the height of her own heart, in a compartment marked with a fresh tag in handwriting she knew better than her own—

*Maren of Ashvale. Age nine. One soul, entire. Paid in full against parental debt. Delivery due: dark moon.*

The vessel was small. A vial of cloudy glass, no longer than her finger, stoppered with wax the color of dried blood. Inside it, a light no bigger than a moth.

It moved the way a trapped moth moves. Not the steady glow of the older souls on the lower shelves—those lights had gone quiet and dim, banked like coals, patient as the dead. This one was frantic. It beat against the glass in quick, soft pulses, rested, beat again, rested, beat again, as if it hadn't yet learned that the glass would always win. As if it still believed that if it knocked enough, someone would come.

Maren didn't understand what she'd lost. Petra had sat across from her in a rented room that smelled of mildew and boiled cabbage and watched the girl fold paper boats with hands that were already, invisibly, incomplete. Nine years old and missing the piece of herself that would have let her feel the size of the theft. That was the cruelest clause in the whole contract, Petra thought. It didn't just take the soul. It took the capacity to mourn it.

But the soul remembered. The soul was knocking.

Petra lifted the vial from its compartment with both hands, the way you lift a sleeping bird, and the light inside stilled for one instant—one held breath—and pressed itself against the glass on the side nearest her heart, and stayed there.

She did not cry. She had trained herself out of crying in counting houses; tears smudge ink. But her throat closed around something large and hot, and Odell, watching her, put out one hand and steadied her elbow, and neither of them said anything, because anything either of them said would have been too small.

"We can't take it yet," she managed finally. "If the vial leaves the vault before the contract's voided, the wards will—" She stopped. She was talking like a guildsman again. Like a clerk. Terms and conditions, contingencies, the language of the thing she had come to destroy. She heard herself doing it and wanted to scream. Even now, even here, standing in the cold heart of the machine, her mouth defaulted to procedure. Eleven years of bookkeeping had grown into her bones like the gold veins grew into the granite. Maybe longer than eleven years. Maybe the forgery went all the way down.

"Then we wait," Odell said simply. "We're auditors. Auditing takes time." He glanced up and down the empty aisle, and his voice dropped. "We take our notes. We make our counts. And when the moment comes—when the court is busy, when the bells ring—we move."

Petra set the vial back in its compartment, aligning it precisely with the shelf's edge, the way a careful thief returns what she means to steal. The moth-light resumed its beating. Knock, rest, knock. It did not know she was coming back. It could not know. But she knew, and knowing was its own kind of vow.

* * *

They were nearly at the vault door when the warmth returned.

It came up through the floor like a tide, sudden and total, and the gold veins in the walls blazed so bright that shadows fled the room entirely, and every soul-light on every shelf flared at once—thousands of tiny fires kindling in unison, as if the whole reliquary had drawn a single breath.

And beneath the floor, beneath the obsidian, beneath the warm and patient dark, something spoke.

It was not a voice, exactly. It was pressure. It was the sensation of a word being considered rather than said, a syllable the size of a mountain range rolling over in its sleep, and every salt figure in the corridor outside straightened by a degree, and every clerk in the distant court paused its pen, and the great ledger above the altar turned a page.

Odell grabbed Petra's arm. His fingers were shaking.

"What was that?"

Petra stared at the glowing walls, at the blazing shelves, at the vial with its small frantic tenant, and felt the not-memory stir again—that sediment at the base of her skull, that drowned thing turning over, reaching upward through eleven years of stolen silence toward a sound it recognized.

*I know that voice,* the not-memory said. *I have stood in front of that voice. I have taken dictation from it.*

She didn't say it aloud. She couldn't have explained it if she tried. But as they slipped out of the vault and back toward the tiers, hugging the shadow line, moving like two clerks late for a filing deadline, Petra caught sight of the bench at the court's center—and upon it, descending through the blaze of his own gold light, unhurried, magnificent, terrible, a figure taking his seat.

The god-king had come to hold court.

And as the light broke over her, as the warmth washed through her stolen and patched-together self, Petra felt something deep beneath the forgery shift, crack, and begin—very softly, very terribly—to remember him.
