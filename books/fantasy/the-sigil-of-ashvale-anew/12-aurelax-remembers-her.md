## 12. Aurelax Remembers Her

The court smelled of gold and old grief.

Aurelax drew it in as he settled upon his bench—a cathedral of contracts rising tier upon tier around him, each shelf of the great walls holding souls sealed in wax and glass, each reliquary humming faintly like a struck bell that had forgotten how to stop. The smell was the same as it had been for six hundred years: hot metal, tallow smoke, and beneath it all the sweetish rot of prayers left unanswered too long. His petitioners called it holiness. He knew it for what it was. Accumulation.

He had come to hold court because the mountain had begun to itch.

That was the word for it, though no mortal tongue would have chosen so small a complaint for so vast a thing. For centuries the deep roads had been silent, the salt-petitioners frozen mid-plea along his antechambers, the whole subterranean dark as obedient as a closed book. Then, season by season, something above had begun to wake. A shrine idol weeping blood in Ashvale's fishmarket. A beggar's crooked prayer answered with bread that never ran out. Small tremors. Fissures in a ledger he had believed permanently balanced.

His siblings were stirring. The old ones, the neglected ones, the ones he had fed on for so long they had forgotten their own names—and now, waking hungry and half-mad, they remembered his.

Aurelax did not fear them. Fear was a mortal instrument, useful chiefly for seasoning contracts. But he respected hunger, because he understood it. He had built an entire civilization of accounting upon it.

"Bring forward the docket," he said, and his voice moved through the court the way cold moves through water.

The clerks scurried—his dead clerks, his patient clerks, mortals who had sold themselves by degrees until nothing remained but the part of them that could keep accounts. They unrolled the day's petitions at the foot of the bench. Debts of grain. Debts of years. One widow offering her capacity for joy against her son's fever. He adjudicated in the old way, swiftly, without cruelty and without mercy, because both were inefficiencies. Gold and grief flowed upward into him in equal measure, and the ledger stayed level, and the mountain slept.

Until the thief walked in.

He felt her before he saw her. That was the thing about stolen goods—they kept a resonance. Every soul in his collection was catalogued not only by name but by absence, by the shape of what had been carved away to make it fit its contract, and there was one absence in all his holdings shaped exactly like a girl who had once stood at this very bench with ink-stained fingers and eyes too clever for her own salvation.

She came up through the tiers wearing a guild auditor's grey, hood drawn, spine bent into the servile curve clerks affected when they wished to be overlooked. Her companion was a fence with soft hands and a liar's walk. Neither of them mattered. The woman in the grey mattered the way a wound matters.

*Petra Dravenmoor.*

He almost smiled. He did not, because his face was an instrument of state, and instruments of state did not grin at the approach of catastrophe. But something in the deep chambers of him—the place where he kept the things he had loved, a very small place, a locked room at the end of a very long corridor—turned over in its sleep.

She had been his first clerk. Before the guild existed, before Ashvale had a name worth stealing, before he had learned to let mortals come to him willingly rather than dragging them down the mine-shafts in chains, there had been a god-touched child from a village that no longer appeared on maps. She had been brought to him as tribute, hollowed as all his vessels were hollowed, and set to copying names. And she had done it beautifully. Her hand was precise. Her mind was orderly. Her soul, even gutted, sang against the wax seals when she passed the reliquary shelves, and he had found himself—this was the truth he admitted to no one, not even his dead clerks—keeping her near. Listening to her breathe while she worked. It had been, he supposed, the closest thing to companionship a creature of pure accumulation could afford.

And then she had stolen from him.

Not gold. Not souls—not anyone else's, anyway. She had taken his ledger of names, the master record, the root of every contract in the mountain, and she had fled up the abandoned shafts into the grey morning light of a world that had already forgotten her village existed. And when he had finally cornered the theft in his own books—when he had traced the missing entries down through the columns and found them folded inside a single line item he had never authorized—he had understood what she had taken as payment.

Herself. Her memories. Her name. The whole of what he had spent years hollowing out, gathered up in two small hands and carried away into the world, leaving behind in his ledger a girl-shaped absence that no subsequent acquisition had ever managed to fill.

She had made herself the price of her own freedom. It was the single most elegant entry he had ever seen, and he had hated it, and he had admired it, and for six hundred years those two feelings had sat side by side in him like feuding brothers, neither willing to leave the house.

Now she stood three tiers below his bench, disguised in borrowed grey, pretending to audit a court she had once kept books for, and the resonance of her was louder than it had ever been. Because she was cracked now. Something above had begun breaking her open—memories leaking back through the forgery she had built, the courtroom surfacing in her dreams, his own voice echoing in her skull whether she willed it or no. He could hear it from here. The sound of a lock rusting open.

The bells began while she was still watching him.

They came rolling up out of the vault levels—the alarm she and her fence had tripped, clumsy after all her elegance, or perhaps not clumsy at all, perhaps meant—and the dead clerks froze, and the tiers filled with the rustle of salt-petitioners straining at their stillness, and every eye in the cathedral of contracts turned toward the intruders in the grey.

Aurelax raised one hand, and the bells died mid-toll.

"Leave them," he said, and the words dropped through the court like stones through still water. "The auditor is here on guild business. Return to your ledgers."

The clerks obeyed, because obedience was all that remained of them. The fence shot Petra a glance of naked terror; she did not return it. She was looking at the bench. At him. And her face, above the collar of the borrowed robe, was doing something complicated—fear and recognition wrestling in the same features, the expression of a woman meeting a memory she had paid everything to forget.

Good, thought Aurelax. Remember me. You will need to, before we are finished.

He let the docket proceed. Let her watch him work. This, too, was deliberate. He adjucated the widow's joy, weighed it against the boy's fever, found the exchange equitable, and sealed it. He heard the little hitch in her breath at the transaction's cleanliness—at the terrible tidiness of a grief converted to gold without waste—and he knew she was remembering that she had once found the same tidiness beautiful. That she had learned precision at this bench, under his instruction, before she had learned theft. You cannot steal well until you have kept books well. He had taught her that. Everything she was, in a sense, he had made, and then she had unmade herself out of the materials, which was either ingratitude or the highest compliment a student can pay.

When the last petitioner was dismissed and the tiers emptied, he spoke without looking up from the ledger before him.

"You may approach the bench, auditor."

Silence. He could feel her calculating—whether to run, whether the fence would run first, whether the guards at the doors were real or theater. They were theater. If she bolted, he would simply close the mountain, and closing the mountain was a gesture as effortless as blinking. But he wanted her to choose to come forward. Choice was the currency of binding contracts. He had learned that from her, too, in a way—she had taught him that the willing soul signed deeper than the chained one.

Boots on stone. Hesitant, then steadying. She stopped at the foot of the bench, hood thrown back now, and there she was. Older, of course. Harder at the edges. A face built for markets and candlelit counting houses rather than cathedral light. But underneath the wear, the same face that had looked up at him across this exact distance, six hundred years ago, with ink on her fingers and theft already flowering behind her eyes.

"Do you know," Aurelax said, "how long I have waited for someone to rob me properly again?"

Her jaw tightened. "I don't know what you mean, lord." *Lord*, delivered with a market-hag's flat contempt dressed in courtly paper. Still his student. Defiance performed correctly is indistinguishable from courtesy.

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You don't. That has rather been the trouble." He turned the ledger toward her, and let her see the page. Not Maren's contract—though that was there, the child's small soul flickering in its reliquary like a moth in amber, due at the dark moon. An older page. Yellowed. Sealed in wax that had gone the color of dried blood. An entry in a hand he would know anywhere, because he had taught it every stroke: *one (1) soul, self-delivered, payment rendered in full.*

"I have kept this page blank beside it ever since," he said. "Six hundred years, auditor, and I have bought and sold the souls of nations, and no entry has ever fit in that space. Do you know why?"

She said nothing. Her hands, he noted, had gone very still at her sides. The stillness of someone fighting not to shake.

"Because you are owed," he said. "You took yourself as payment, and the taking was lawful—it was flawless, it was the finest piece of work ever done in this court—and therefore the account remains open. I have never been able to close it. Every soul I have collected since has been an attempt, if you want the truth, and I suspect you always have. None of them fit. They were never you."

"You're lying," she said quietly.

"I am incapable of lying in my own court. It is the one law even I cannot amend. Ask any petitioner turned to salt on my stairways—they will tell you I told each of them the truth." He rose, then, and the light of him shifted through the tiers, and he saw her flinch, saw the crack in her widen, saw the memory press up against the inside of her forged self like a face against a window. "You remember the voice," he observed gently. "It has been visiting you for weeks. In the counting house. In the margins of your ledgers. You wrote my authentication mark with your own hand and did not know where you learned it. You are coming apart at the seams, Petra Dravenmoor, and I am the only tailor in the world who owns your original pattern."

"Don't say that name."

"It is yours. I held it in trust." He descended one step, then another, until the gulf between god and mortal shrank to the width of a bench. Up close, he let his aspect soften into something almost kind—he had worn kindness before; it fit him poorly, but she had never been able to tell the difference, and perhaps that had been her tragedy from the beginning. "Here is my offer, and I will make it only once, and it will be true in every particular.

"Deliver me the child's soul. Willingly—your hand, your choice, no theft, no coercion. Bring Maren of the debt-orphanage to this bench at the dark moon and lay her contract before me completed. And in exchange I will give you back everything you carried out of my mountain. Every memory. Your mother's kitchen. Your village before I unmade it from the maps. Your name—all of your names, including the first one, the one you bore before you were anyone's clerk. And more than that: the power you glimpsed and never knew you had. You stole my ledger once with nothing but cleverness and courage. Imagine what you might take with your full self behind your hands."

He watched the offer land. Watched it move through her the way frost moves through glass—beautifully, destructively. He was not lying. That was the exquisite mechanism of it. Every word was true. The restoration would be complete; his power over memory was absolute within these walls. He would give her back every year of her stolen life, every love, every grief, every hour of the childhood he had hollowed—and she would receive it all standing at this bench, in this light, having just delivered a child into the same fate she had once fled. And she would live forever afterward knowing precisely what she had purchased her wholeness with. Mercy, in his court, always came with interest.

That was the corruption of it, and the beauty, and he suspected—looking into her eyes as the offer settled over her—that some part of her already understood. She had kept books for the guild long enough to know that gifts were merely debts wearing softer clothes.

"The child," she said. Her voice was steady. He admired that. "She's nine years old. She doesn't even know what she lost."

"No," Aurelax agreed. "Most debtors don't. It is a great mercy of the trade." He inclined his head, gracious, patient, a king with all the time in the mountain. "You need not answer now. The dark moon is three nights hence. Consider what I have shown you. Consider the blank space in the ledger, and how long it has waited, and what it means that a thing such as I should wait at all."

He returned to his bench and took up the next petition, and the audience was over, and the dead clerks ushered her and her trembling fence toward the doors with perfect, courteous indifference.

But as she reached the threshold—as the light of the court released her and the shadows of the antechamber took her back—Aurelax spoke once more, softly, in the voice he knew was visiting her dreams.

"And do think carefully about the seal on that old page of mine, clerk. Wax remembers hands. So do I."

She paused. Half a heartbeat, no more. Then she was gone, up the tiers, into the dark, carrying his offer like a coin she hadn't yet decided to spend.

Aurelax sat amid his cathedral of contracts and listened to her footsteps fade, and in the small locked room at the end of the long corridor, the thing he kept that was almost affection pressed its face against the door.

Three nights, he thought. Three nights, and either the ledger would finally balance—or she would try to rob him twice.

He found, examining himself with the cold thoroughness of six centuries of bookkeeping, that he could not decide which outcome he hoped for.

That, too, was her fault. It always had been.
