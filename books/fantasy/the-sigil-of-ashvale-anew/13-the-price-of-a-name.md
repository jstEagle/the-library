## 13. The Price of a Name

The god-king's voice did not enter her ears. It entered the place where her memories had been kept, and it turned a key.

Petra had braced for pain. She had imagined restoration as a wound reopening, as fire, as drowning. What came instead was *light* — a slow dawn breaking behind her eyes, one stolen year at a time, each memory returning to its shelf like a bird that had always known the shape of its cage.

She was standing in Aurelax's court, in the cathedral of contracts beneath the mountain, and she was remembering everything.

Her name came first.

Not *Petra*. Not the name she had worn like a borrowed coat through thirty years of candlelit counting houses and careful, invisible thefts. Her true name arrived whole and warm and terrible, and it was — 

*Petra Dravenmoor.*

She laughed aloud, a small broken sound that echoed strangely off the vaulted dark. Of course. Of *course*. She had spent her life under a name so plain it was practically an apology, and all the while it had been the one thing they never thought to take, because who would believe the truth could hide in plain sight? She had been Petra Dravenmoor before she was anyone. Before the guild. Before the ledgers. Before the hollowing.

Before him.

* * *

She was four years old, and the mountain sang to her.

That was how it began — not with a contract, not with ink or gold, but with a child kneeling at a wayside shrine on the mountain road above Ashvale, praying to a stone idol with a cracked face, and the idol *answering*. Not with blood, not with weeping. With words. A voice like deep water, like coins rolling across marble, saying: *You hear me, little one. Come closer.*

God-touched. That was what the old women called children like her, and they said it the way you'd say *plague-touched*, from behind shut doors. Her mother hid her. Her mother taught her to keep her hands still when shrines hummed, to bite her tongue when prayers rose in her throat unbidden, because gods answered too easily around children like her, and answered things were taken.

But the mountain god was patient, and the mountain god was hungry, and one winter the fees came due and there was no grain and no coin, and a man in grey robes came down from the high passes with an offer written on vellum so fine it felt like skin.

*The guild will forgive your debts,* he told her mother. *In exchange, the child serves the court below. A term of service. Nothing more.*

Her mother wept. Her mother signed. And Petra — four years old, god-touched, already able to hold a stranger's memory in her cupped hands like water — was carried down into the dark.

Aurelax had wanted a clerk. That was the truth of it, stripped of all its ceremony. The court beneath the mountain ran on contracts, and contracts required a keeper, and mortal scribes forgot, and forgetting was fatal to a ledger of souls. But a god-touched child could be made into something better than a scribe. She could be made into the ledger itself.

He had not stolen her memories all at once. He had fed on them slowly, over years, the way a farmer milks a beast — taking first her childhood, then her mother's face, then her name's true weight, then the shape of the girl she'd been before the dark. Each memory he took, he wrote into his great ledger of names, and each entry made her lighter, emptier, more perfectly suited to holding *his* records instead of her own self. By the time she was grown she was a vessel of flawless recall with nothing inside it worth recalling. A book with no story of its own. His finest instrument.

And then, one night — she remembered this now with a clarity that made her knees ache — she had done the unthinkable. She had turned her gift upon her maker.

She stood in his counting hall while he slept his long geological sleep, and she opened his ledger of names, and she stole. Not gold. Not souls. She stole the *entries* — every contract he held over the living, copied into the hollow rooms of herself where her own life used to be. She made her emptied mind into a vault and filled it with his empire. And as payment for the theft — because even then, even hollow, she understood that nothing is taken without price — she took back one memory of her own choosing.

She took her name. Petra Dravenmoor. Just the name, just the sound of it, the small hot coal of who she had been before. She fled up through the mine-shafts with a god's ledger hidden in her skull and a single word burning in her chest, and she surfaced into the world above with no past but a name, and she built a life out of forgetting.

Thirty years. Thirty years of balancing impossible accounts, of hiding her small thefts in margins, of keeping her head down and her hands clean and her heart locked. She had thought she was hiding from the guild. All along, she had been hiding from *him* — and failing, because the guild had found her anyway, had given her shelter and wages and a leash, and she had never once wondered why the Guild of Impossible Trades had been so very eager to hire a woman with no history.

Now she knew why. Now she watched Aurelax across the cathedral floor — vast and patient on his throne of compressed centuries, his body a suggestion of gold and shadow and old, old hunger — and she understood the architecture of her entire life.

Vess Corvane had found her within a year of her escape. Had given her work, warmth, belonging. Had praised her meticulous hand. Had promoted her, protected her, drawn her close like a hearth-fire in winter.

Because Vess was his. Had always been his. The guild master of Ashvale knelt to the court beneath the mountain, and her kindness had been a shepherd's kindness all along — gentle only until the flock strayed, and then the crook became a hook. Every year of Petra's comfortable, careful, invisible life had been a debt accruing. He had let her run just far enough to grow valuable. Let her fill those hollow rooms with skill and craft and thirty years of stolen techniques, until the vessel that fled his court came back *full* — full of everything he'd lost, everything she'd taken, everything he meant to pour back out of her.

*I have missed you, little clerk,* Aurelax said, and his voice was almost tender, which was the most frightening thing about it. *You were my best work.*

* * *

Petra kept her face still. It was the first discipline she had ever learned, older than memory: a god-touched child survives by showing the god nothing.

Around her, the court went about its eternal business. Petitioners shuffled forward with their offerings. Clerks of pale bone recorded judgments in books that bled when closed. Somewhere far above, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone, the bells of the reliquary vault were still tolling their alarm — she had pulled Maren's soul from its case barely an hour ago, and the small flickering light of it slept now against her breastbone, wrapped in cloth, warm as a sparrow. Warm as a child's whole future.

*I imagine,* Aurelax continued, descending from his throne with the unhurried grace of something that has never once needed to hurry, *that it is quite disorienting. To remember yourself all at once. Most mortals weep.*

*I'm not most mortals.* Her voice came out steady. Steadier than she felt. Inside her skull the restored decades were still settling, still rearranging themselves around the new weight of the old ones, and grief kept surfacing in her like bubbles in deep water — her mother's face, her mother's hands, the winter the grey-robed man came, the last look her mother gave her at the mountain's mouth. *I'm yours. Isn't that what you've been telling everyone?*

His laughter moved through the cathedral like weather. The salt-petitioners in their alcoves — the ones who had prayed too long and been paid in stone — seemed to lean toward the sound.

*Yours,* he agreed. *Body, memory, and craft. You carry my ledger in your skull, Petra Dravenmoor. Thirty years I have waited for it to ripen. And now you return it to me wearing a thief's clever hands, having already demonstrated your value by recovering the Vessari soul.* His attention slid, briefly, to her breastbone, and the sleeping light against her heart flinched. *You have saved me a retrieval. I find I am inclined to be generous.*

Here it was, then. The bargain. She had known it was coming since the moment his voice had turned the key in her mind; a creature like Aurelax never restored anything for free, and mercy from a devourer was always a menu.

*Name your price,* she said, and hated how much it sounded like surrender.

*No price. A gift.* He spread hands that were mostly suggestion, mostly dark. *Your memories are returned — all of them, the whole of the girl I hollowed and the woman she became. Your name, your power, your place. Return to my court as first clerk of the ledger of names, as you were always meant to be. Rule the counting halls of the deep roads. Outlive the guild that used you. Outlive Vess Corvane, if you wish it — she is useful, but she is replaceable, and you...* the darkness of him inclined, almost courteously, *...you are irreplaceable.*

*And the child?*

*Maren Vessari fulfills her family's contract, as her parents signed and sealed. Her soul remains in my reliquary. That matter is settled law.* A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. *Deliver her to me willingly, and the gift stands. Refuse, and I take back what I have returned — piece by piece, beginning with the newest memories, the ones you have grown fond of. Odell. The codex. The alley where a ragged god spoke your name. I will leave you exactly as you were when you crawled out of my mountain: a name and nothing else. Perhaps less. I am older now, and hungrier, and my extraction has improved.*

Petra looked at him for a long moment.

Rage was rising in her — rage with a thirty-year fuse finally burning down to the powder — and she let it rise, and she let it *cool*, because rage was heat and heat was waste unless you forged it into something with an edge. She thought of the salt-petitioners in their alcoves, the prayers they'd whispered that he drank like wine. She thought of Maren, nine years old, asking Petra in the market dust whether being sold hurt. She thought of her mother's face — her mother's *face*, which she had again, which was hers again, which no contract in any court could make collateral ever again.

She thought of the ledger hidden in her skull. His ledger. The one she'd stolen once already, when she was empty. She was not empty anymore. She was the fullest thing in this cathedral, and he had just handed her back the key to her own vaults and called it generosity.

*You want an answer,* she said.

*I want my clerk.*

*Then here is my answer.* She bowed — low, deliberate, the bow of a subordinate, and she felt the court's bone-clerks record it, felt the judgment-books drink the motion like blood. *I accept.*

The silence that followed had texture. Aurelax's darkness gathered itself, pleased, coiling like smoke into the shape of satisfaction, and if gods could purr, this was the sound.

*Wise,* he said. *Kneel, then, and swear it on the ledger, and we will—*

*On one condition.* She straightened before his pleasure could harden into ritual. Keep him talking. Keep him *wanting*. A buyer who thinks the deal might slip grows generous; she had watched Odell work fences and fishmongers for years, and the principle held from eels to elder gods. *The girl. I didn't claw her out of your vault to watch her go back in. If I deliver her willingly — my choice, my hands, no coercion — then her contract is voided. Paid in full by my service. Write it into the record. You of all beings know the law you built: a willing delivery supersedes a sealed debt.*

Something moved behind the dark of him. Not surprise — nothing surprised a thing this old — but recalculation. The slow arithmetic of a predator deciding whether the bait was worth the hook.

*You drive a hard clause, little clerk.*

*I learned from the best ledger-keeper in creation.* Let him taste the flattery. Let him think the hollow girl was still hollow at her center, still his, still grateful. *Void the contract and I walk back into this court carrying her myself. Refuse, and you can dig the memory of these last thirty years out of me one at a time, and learn what it costs to search a thief's house room by room while she burns the furniture behind you.*

Another silence. Longer. In the alcoves, the salt-petitioners stood in their eternal attitudes of supplication, and Petra thought — suddenly, fiercely — *not forever.* The bells above were still tolling. Somewhere in the deep roads a ragged god was waiting. Somewhere overhead, Odell was sitting in a lamplit room with a forged codex and a guilty conscience, believing she was dead or worse.

*Agreed,* Aurelax said at last, and the word rolled through the cathedral like a verdict. *Bring me the Vessari soul willingly, and her contract burns before witnesses. You have three days. My patience, as ever, is geological — but my appetite is not.*

*Three days,* Petra said.

She bowed again. She turned. She walked up the cathedral's central aisle with her spine straight and her heartbeat slow and Maren's soul a small warm secret against her breastbone, and she did not run, though every nerve in her body screamed to run, because prey runs and clerks do not, and she had just been reappointed the finest clerk in creation.

Behind her, the bone-scribes scratched. The judgment-books drank. And the god-king watched her go with the patience of a thing that believed it had already won.

Let him believe it. She had stolen from him once with an empty head.

Now she had thirty years of craft, a partner above ground, a god in the alleys, a soul in her coat — and a memory, newly returned, of exactly where he kept the lamp that lit his ledger of names.

Three days. She climbed toward the dark of the mine-shafts, and the rage in her chest had gone quiet and cold and very, very sharp.

Above her, far above, in a lamplit counting house in Ashvale, Vess Corvane was about to ask Odell a question.

Petra didn't know it yet. But the mountain did.
