## 14. Betrayal in the Counting House

The counting house of the Ashvale Guild kept its own hours, and none of them belonged to the sun. Odell Vetch had worked beneath its low beams for eleven years, long enough to know the difference between the candle-smoke that meant ordinary business and the thicker, sweeter reek of tallow laced with myrrh that meant Vess Corvane was conducting an audit.

Tonight it was the myrrh.

He smelled it before he reached the door, and his stomach folded itself into a shape he didn't have a name for. He had spent the day doing what he always did — moving goods that officially did not exist through doors that officially were not there — and he had done it well, and he had come home at the usual hour, whistling the usual nothing-tune, because that was the whole architecture of survival in the guild's lower ranks: you were boring, you were predictable, you were furniture.

Furniture did not get asked questions under myrrh-light.

He paused with his hand on the latch. Through the frosted pane, the counting house glowed amber, and two shadows moved inside — one seated, still as a spider at rest; one standing at the great iron-bound table, turning pages. The page-turning shadow was not Vess. It moved too quickly, too hungrily. One of her enforcers, then. Brannick, probably, or the new one with the soft hands who liked to be told he was clever.

*You are furniture,* Odell told himself, and went in.

* * *

Vess Corvane sat behind a desk that was too small for her, which was a trick she cultivated deliberately. She was a broad woman, wide-shouldered as a doorframe, silver at the temples and iron everywhere else, and she liked the way she overflowed her furniture — like a tide coming in around a rock. Her hands rested flat on the ledger before her, fingers spread, as if the book might bolt.

"Odell." She said his name the way she said everything: without inflection, without mercy, without hurry. "Sit."

There was one chair. There was always exactly one chair in front of Vess's desk, no matter how many people she summoned, because she believed attention was a resource and should never be distributed. Odell sat. The chair was cold. He noticed that — cold, when every other surface in this room was warm from candles and bodies — and some animal part of him understood that the room had been arranged for him alone, tonight, hours ago, and had been waiting.

"Good work on the Marrowgate shipment," Vess said.

"Thank you, Guild Master."

"The buyer wept, I'm told. They usually do." She turned a page. The sound it made was enormous. "Tell me about the codex."

The world went very quiet and very loud at once, the way it does when a beam gives way above you and you hear both the silence where the roof was and the roar of what's coming down.

"Codex," Odell said. His voice came out steady. He was almost proud of it. "I don't—"

"Brannick." Vess didn't raise her voice. She never needed to.

The soft-handed enforcer stepped out of the archive alcove carrying a book, and Odell knew it instantly, the way you know your own handwriting, because in a sense he had written it — or rather he had written *around* it, had built the forgery the way a jeweler builds a setting: a false codex, page after patient page copied in a slantwise imitation of Petra Dravenmoor's cramped left-leaning script, aged with tea smoke and handled until the vellum felt honest. He had made it in four nights while pretending to sleep. It was good work. It was the best forgery he'd ever made, and it was going to get him killed.

Brannick set it on the desk beside the real ledger.

Vess opened it to a marker ribbon — a red ribbon, Odell noted dimly; Petra had used red ribbons; he had remembered that detail, God help him, he had been so careful with the details — and read aloud, softly, the way other people pray.

"'Entry 447. One soul, female child, nine years, sold against dark moon, court of Aurelax beneath the mountain. Sigil authenticated.'" She looked up. "This is a copy. It is a *beautiful* copy. Every quirk of the original hand reproduced. Every stain. Even the little ink-blot shaped like a fish on page sixty-two — did you know there was an ink-blot shaped like a fish on page sixty-two, Odell?"

"No," said Odell, truthfully, because Petra had never shown him the real thing. Only her memory of it.

"Neither did I," said Vess, "until an hour ago. Which means whoever made this had access to the original. And the only people with access are me, the archivist, the archivist's dead predecessor, and—" she turned another page, delicately, like a woman turning back a lover's sheet, "—the bookkeeper of record for the lower trades. You."

"I balance accounts," Odell said. "I don't touch originals."

"You touched *this* one." She closed the codex. The clap of it was like a trap shutting. "Here is what I think happened, and correct me if I stray. Petra Dravenmoor found entry 447. She took the original page — yes, we counted; the ledger is light by exactly one leaf, and don't look surprised, we count everything here, that's rather the point of us. She copied the ledger entire, because she is thorough and because she intended treason, and you helped her hide the copy, because you love her, or fear her, or fear me more than either. Then she went somewhere. And now I would like to know where."

The myrrh-smoke hung between them. Somewhere in the walls, the guild's pipes ticked and settled, counting out seconds in their own dumb metal language.

"I don't know," Odell said. "I haven't seen her since—"

"Since three nights ago, when she came down to the lower ranks smelling of river mud and borrowed time and asked you for lamp oil and four hours alone with the copying bench. Yes." Vess smiled. It did not reach anything. "We watch the oil stores, Odell. We watch everything. That's rather the point of us."

* * *

They took him down to the wet room.

He had heard about the wet room his whole career, the way sailors hear about the deep water — a place spoken of in the abstract, a thing that happens to other men. It was below the counting house, below even the archive vaults, a chamber cut into Ashvale's foundations where the old city cistern still breathed through the walls. Everything dripped. Everything was stone-slick and green-shadowed, lit by a single lantern whose flame bent constantly toward the seep, as if trying to leave.

They tied him to a chair that had clearly held many men; the armrests were worn smooth in places a chair doesn't wear naturally.

Vess dismissed the enforcers. That was worse. That told him this was going to be intimate.

"I want you to understand something before we begin," she said, crouching to his level, forearms on her knees, close enough that he could see the fine map of lines around her eyes — a face that had once perhaps been kind, or had learned kindness as a language and forgotten it. "I don't enjoy this. I want you to actually believe that, because it matters. I am not a cruel woman, Odell. I am a *thorough* one, and cruelty is inefficient. Pain produces lies faster than it produces truth. So I will ask you questions slowly, and I will listen to your answers the way a moneylender listens, weighing them, testing the weight of the metal, and I will know when you're paying me in clipped coin. Do you follow?"

"Yes," Odell whispered.

"She has the child's soul on her. Do you understand what that means? Not metaphorically. A reliquary-grade soul, loose in the world, and a contract outstanding against it, and the dark moon four days early now because the client does not wait. If she fails to deliver, the debt transfers. To the guild. To *me*. Aurelax does not accept apologies, Odell. He accepts collateral." She straightened. "So. Where did she go?"

"The mountain," Odell said, because lying about that was impossible — everyone in the guild already knew, the whole building hummed with it. "She went into the mountain."

"Yes. Through which pass?"

And here it was. Here was the moment his whole life narrowed to a single point, finer than a needle's eye.

He thought about the passes. He knew them the way a fence knows fences — the smugglers' geography of the Ashvale range lived in his head like a second skeleton. There was the Shepherd's Cut, wide and patrolled and useless for hiding. There was the Hollow Stair, the old pilgrim road, half-collapsed but passable, the obvious choice, the choice anyone would guess. And there was the Grayvein — a miner's rat-run, narrow, unmarked, opening onto the deep shafts near the court's abandoned antechambers. The Grayvein was the way a thief would go. The Grayvein was almost certainly the way Petra had gone, because the alley god had put a door in the mountain's roots and Petra did nothing the obvious way.

Which was precisely why he could not say it.

"Petra," he said carefully, "doesn't take roads anyone watches. She'll have gone the Hollow Stair."

He watched Vess receive this. Watched her weigh it. She was silent for a long moment, and the dripping filled the silence like slow applause.

"Hollow Stair," she repeated.

"It's the only approach that connects to the old shafts without passing the gatehouse. She'll have studied it. She studies everything." His voice cracked on the last word, and he hated himself for it, and hid the crack inside a cough.

Vess walked a slow circle around the chair. Her boots clicked on wet stone. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — softened, dropped into a register he had never heard her use, warm as banked coals.

"Odell. Eleven years. Eleven years you've been my man in the lower ranks, and I have never once had cause to doubt you. Do you know how rare that is? In this trade? I have been betrayed by partners, lovers, my own sister. Never by you." She stopped in front of him. "I don't want to believe you've broken. Give me one reason. Give me one true thing, and walk out of this room, and none of this ever happened. You keep your post. You keep your hands. You might even keep her, if she comes back — though I'd advise against it, sentiment being a bad investment."

The lantern flame leaned. Water ticked down the wall behind his head, patient as a clerk.

*One true thing.* He had a hundred true things and they all weighed the same as treason. *She was sold first. She was the first soul in the ledger. Vess, you've been serving the thing under the mountain since before I met you, and Petra figured it out, and I'd burn this whole guild to ash before I hand her to you.*

What he said was: "She told me if anyone came asking, to tell them the Hollow Stair."

It was a stupid answer. It was transparently a prepared answer, a lie with a bow on it, and he watched Vess understand that immediately, watched her eyes go flat and gray as slate.

"All right," she said quietly. "All right, Odell."

* * *

He would remember the next hour in fragments for the rest of his life, in the way drowning men remember weather.

He remembered that pain had a texture, that it wasn't one thing but many — white and hot, black and grinding, a thin singing wire behind the eyes. He remembered that Vess worked methodically and never raised her voice and apologized twice, actually apologized, in a tone of genuine regret, and that somehow this was the worst part. He remembered deciding, at some point past the place where decisions stop meaning anything, that he would tell her whatever she wanted, that he would confess everything, sign anything, name any name — and that underneath that surrender, buried deeper than the pain could dig, one fact would stay hidden the way a coal stays lit under ash.

The Grayvein.

Not the Hollow Stair. Whatever else he gave her, however completely he broke — and he broke, he broke like cheap crockery, he sobbed names and dates and begged — the mountain pass stayed shut. Because a broken man tells the truth about small things and lies about the large ones, and Vess knew that, and so she would sift everything he said and trust the details and doubt the architecture, and the architecture was the only thing Petra needed.

He told her about the codex — where the original pages were hidden, in the false bottom of the salt chest, that was true, let her have that. He told her about the copying nights, the tea-smoke aging, the fish-shaped blot. True, true, all true. He told her Petra had a partner above ground feeding her information, and named a fence who'd skipped town two years ago, dead to all accounts, unverifiable. Half-true. A clipped coin, weighted with sand.

And through all of it, threaded under everything like a bass note, he said again and again, in a voice that grew less and less like performance and more and less like anything at all: *the Hollow Stair, she took the Hollow Stair.*

Near the end — he couldn't say how near, time had dissolved — Vess crouched in front of him again, and took his ruined face in her strong square hands, almost tenderly, and looked into his eyes for a long time.

"You love her," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Yes," Odell said. Blood in his mouth. The word tasted like a confession of everything.

"That's unfortunate," Vess said. "For you. Because love makes men lie beautifully, and I can't tell anymore which of your truths are hers." She stood. "But here's what I know that you don't, Odell, and you may carry it with you into whatever's left of your night. There are only three ways into the deep shafts. I've sealed two. By morning I'll have watchers on the third, whichever it turns out to be. Your partner is climbing into a closed fist." She gathered her coat from the peg. At the door she paused, her back to him. "The physicians will see to you. You'll keep your position — I honor utility, even compromised utility. And Odell?" She glanced back, and for one unguarded instant something moved behind her face that might, in another life, in another woman, have been grief. "If she surfaces in Ashvale, send word. Not for the guild. For her. What's coming for her isn't mine to command anymore."

Then the door shut, and the myrrh-smoke went with her, and Odell sat alone in the wet room with the dripping and the leaning flame and the coal still burning under his ash.

* * *

They dumped him in the infirmary off the lower hall sometime before dawn. A guild physician with cold hands and colder eyes splinted what could be splinted and said nothing, because physicians in the Guild of Impossible Trades understood their clientele.

Lying on the cot, staring at the ceiling beams, Odell did his arithmetic.

The Hollow Stair was a day's hard climb from the gatehouse end, longer if you went quiet, and Vess would send her fastest enforcers up it within the hour — he had seen the order given, heard the boots in the corridor, six men, ropes and lanterns and one of those awful soul-snare hooks the enforcement teams carried. They'd reach the upper shafts by midday. Find nothing. Report nothing but wind and collapsed masonry and the droppings of things that lived in the dark.

The Grayvein bought her better than that. Two days, maybe three, before anyone thought to look at the miners' rat-run — and Vess wouldn't think of it soon, because Vess believed she had wrung him dry, and Vess was thorough but she was not omniscient, and a broken man's last lie was the one thing even she might miscount.

Two days. Three, if the gods were awake and feeling generous.

He thought about Petra down in the roots of the world with a child's soul in her coat and thirty years of stolen craft in her hands, climbing toward a god-king who had owned her once. He thought about the way she'd looked three nights ago, mud on her boots, asking for lamp oil, trusting him with the largest secret of both their lives without even saying so aloud.

*Hold the line,* he told her across all that stone and distance. *I'm buying you time. Spend it well.*

His ribs ached when he breathed. His hands, when he finally managed to lift them, shook like a drunkard's, and he laughed — a small, cracked, astonished sound — because they were shaking and they were still his, and he had kept the one thing that mattered.

Outside the infirmary's high slit of a window, the mountains east of Ashvale stood black against a graying sky, and somewhere in their roots, in the deep roads where petitioners had gone to salt and a hollowed-out thief was climbing toward her own stolen name, the dark moon was waning toward nothing.

Three days, she'd said. He had given her a head start and a wrong road and all the blood he had.

Now it was up to the mountain.
