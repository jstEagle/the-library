## 16. What the Gods Owe

The deep roads did not want her.

Petra felt it in the stone itself, a low reluctance humming up through the soles of her boots, as if the mountain's roots were closing their fists around her one knuckle at a time. The tunnels here predated the mines, predated the guild, predated perhaps Ashvale itself — passages worn smooth not by water but by something older that had crawled through them when crawling was still a new idea. Her lantern made a small, stubborn circle of light, and beyond it the dark was total and attentive.

Maren's soul rode against her breastbone in its reliquary vial, warm as a swallowed coal. It had been three hours since the vault alarms began tolling — great bronze throats somewhere above, ringing out her crime to every gallery and antechamber — and the sound had followed her down, growing fainter and stranger, until it no longer sounded like bells at all but like a heartbeat heard from inside someone else's chest.

She ran anyway. She had been running since the reliquary, since the wards came apart under her fingers like wet paper once she'd stolen their founding memories, since she'd seen what lived inside those wards — a flicker no bigger than a moth, beating itself against glass. She ran with her lungs burning and her mind full of Aurelax's voice, that patient gold-dark voice that had said *come back, little clerk, I kept your desk*.

The passage forked. She chose left because right smelled of salt.

That was how she knew, a moment too late, that the choice had not been hers.

"You're limping," said a voice behind her. "You've been limping since the second mile. You should sit."

Petra spun, lantern up, thief's knife already in her other hand. The light found him sitting on nothing — actually on nothing, on a shelf of air folded like cloth — the ragged stranger from the fishmonger's alley, still wearing his coat of patched sacking, still smelling faintly of brine and old smoke. His eyes caught her lantern-light and gave it back wrong, silvered, like coins at the bottom of a well.

"You," she breathed.

"Me." He unfolded himself and stood, and the air set him down gently, the way you'd set down something breakable. "Though in the alley I let you think of me as a beggar, and beggars are owed honesty eventually. My name is Sorrel. I am the god of gutters, gull-cries, small mercies, and fish thrown back for being too small. I was worshipped once by maybe four hundred people, all of them poor, which is why I'm alive at all while better-fed gods starved." He smiled, and it was a tired smile, an old one. "You owe me a memory, Petra Dravenmoor. But I find I've come to collect something else instead."

"I paid you," she said. Her hand rose without permission to her temple, where the absence lived now, the smooth blank place where her mother's face had been. Even reaching for it hurt in a way that had no edges, like pressing a tongue into a socket where a tooth used to be. "I paid. The door is open. I'm through it."

"You paid, and I opened it, and both those things are true." Sorrel drifted closer — he didn't seem to walk so much as agree to be nearer — and looked at the vial against her chest with an expression she could only call reverence. "And now the mountain has woken up, and the bells are screaming your theft to the dark, and you're running down my road. Because this *is* my road, Petra. The deep ways. The forgotten places. Every tunnel Ashvale stopped naming became mine. A god is wherever his name is still spoken, and no one speaks names down here anymore — except me, to myself, for six hundred years."

"That sounds less like a god," Petra said, "and more like a prisoner."

"It is precisely like a prisoner. We understand each other." He gestured, and a stone bench rose out of the wall beside them, worn smooth, as though travelers had rested there for centuries. Perhaps they had. Perhaps he remembered each one. "Sit. Please. What I have to tell you goes easier seated, though not by much."

She didn't sit. Old habit — never give ground, never give height, never give anything. But she stopped retreating, and after a moment Sorrel nodded as if that were acceptance enough.

"Aurelax eats prayers."

The words dropped into the dark between them like stones into a well. Petra waited for the splash.

"I've watched it happen," Sorrel went on, quieter now. "Down here you hear everything the mountain hears. When the court beneath us adjudicates a debt, the whole rock hums with it. And every prayer spoken in Ashvale — every candle lit at a shrine, every desperate whisper over a sick child, every coin pressed into a god-touched offering bowl — rises, and rises, and does *not* go where prayers go." His jaw tightened. "It goes to him. Down. Into the gold. He built the guild's trade routes centuries ago, did you know that? Impossible trades, traded shadows, bottled names — all of it funnels grief upward into contracts, and contracts funnel it downward into his court, and he drinks it. He has been drinking since before your guild had a name. He calls it tithing. He calls himself a steward of mortal devotion. He is a leech the size of a theology."

"The shrines woke up," Petra said slowly. "The weeping idol. The answered prayer I saw in the market."

"Because he's grown careless. Fat. Six hundred years of drinking and he sleeps deeper every year, and prayers have started pooling in the places he forgot to drain. And pooled prayer does what pooled anything does." Sorrel spread his hands. "It grows things. Us. The small ones, the gutter gods, the hearth-scraps, the patron saints of lost buttons and safe childbirth and honest bread. We are waking up, Petra, one by one, like people surfacing from a drowning sleep. And the first thing every one of us remembers upon waking is that we are owed."

"Owed."

"Six hundred years of tithes. Every prayer he swallowed was addressed to *someone*. He drank them, but the debts attached to the drinker. There is a ledger in that court," and here Sorrel's voice changed, went strange, went almost gentle, "with your handwriting in its authentication marks. You know this. You've known since the margins showed you. But there is another ledger, older, unwritten, kept nowhere and everywhere — the ledger of what the waking gods are owed. And it balances against Aurelax to the last drop."

Petra's grip tightened on the knife. "Then collect it yourselves. You're gods. Wake harder."

"We are four hundred worshippers' worth of gutter-divinity against a thing that ate pantheons." For the first time, the silvered eyes flashed with something hot. "We are mice remembering we are mice. We cannot storm his court. We cannot even enter it — he sealed the thresholds against our names before we had names again. But there is one creature who has walked his halls, signed his pages, fled his justice carrying her own soul as payment, and just now stole the one relic he cannot afford to lose."

The cold arrived then, sliding down her spine vertebra by vertebra.

"No," Petra said.

"He wrote you first," Sorrel said. "Before Maren. Before any of the sold souls. You were the prototype — the proof that a person could be entered into a ledger and owned. And you stole yourself back. Do you understand what that means to creatures like us? To *anyone*? It means it can be done. It means the ledger is not law. It means—" he laughed, short and wondering, "—it means you are the most dangerous object in this mountain, and you don't even know what you are."

"I know what I am." Her voice came out flat and hard. "I'm a thief with a head full of borrowed years and a hole where my mother's face used to be. I came here for one child. I have her soul in a bottle. In three hours I intend to be out of this mountain and gone."

"And then what?"

The question landed like a hand on her chest.

"She gets her soul back," Petra said. "There are rites. Reinstatement. The contract stays valid, technically — the guild will hunt the debt forever —"

"The guild will hunt her forever," Sorrel corrected softly. "Because Vess Corvane answers to Aurelax, and Aurelax does not forgive theft. You've seen his mercy. You told me about the courtroom in your memory, the petitioners turned to salt. That girl walks out of this mountain owing the largest debt in Ashvale's history, and every year of her life, some clerk with a ledger will come collecting." He tilted his head. "Unless there is no guild clerk. Unless there is no ledger. Unless there is no court."

The lantern flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft.

"You're asking me to burn it down."

"I'm telling you what you already decided," Sorrel said, "somewhere below the level where you keep your arguments. Why do you think you recognized your own handwriting and didn't run? Why do you think you copied the whole ledger into a codex before you ever met me? Why do you think, when I asked a price in the alley, you gave me your mother's face — the one memory that was a key?" He stepped closer, and the dark seemed to lean with him. "We didn't choose you at random, Petra. We couldn't choose anyone. We were asleep. But sleeping things dream, and we dreamed of a thief who steals memories, walking into the counting house of the god who ate our prayers, holding a pen. You dreamed yourself into this. We merely agreed with your dream."

The weight of it settled onto her shoulders — not like a burden, she realized. Like a yoke. Like harness.

"So that's what I am," she said quietly. "Not a thief. Not a clerk. A weapon. Something you sharpened while I slept and pointed at your enemy."

"Yes," Sorrel said, because apparently gutter gods owed honesty too.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you take the girl and run, and I will open every forgotten door in this mountain for you, and speed your heels, and mourn you. I am not Vess. I am not Aurelax. I do not leash." He held up a hand, patched and salt-stained. "But I will ask you one question first, and then I will ask nothing more. When you stood in that vault and unmade the wards by stealing their founding memories — when you held Maren's soul in your hands — did you feel like a weapon?"

Petra opened her mouth to say yes. To say obviously, of course, she'd been a tool since the day the guild first put a ledger in her hands, since the day — since the day —

Since the day Aurelax put a pen in her hand, in a courtroom beneath a mountain, and taught her to write her own sale.

"No," she heard herself say. The word came out cracked. "I felt like the first honest bookkeeper Ashvale's ever had."

Sorrel smiled, and this time the smile reached all the way up, and for one instant she saw what four hundred poor worshippers must have seen: not a beggar, but a small fierce brightness, a gull crying over a drowned sailor, a fish slipped back into dark water. Mercy, in its least profitable form.

"Then you know what the gods owe you," he said. "Balance comes due, Petra. Even to us. Especially to us."

Far above — so far it might have been imagination — the bells changed their rhythm. No longer alarm. Something older, slower, a summons-toll, the sound the mountain made when its king wanted everyone accounted for.

Sorrel's head lifted. His face went grim.

"He knows where you'll come out," the god said. "He's called his dogs home. The gate roads, the high passes — they'll be thick with guild iron within the hour, and Vess Corvane will be at the front of it, wearing kindness like armor." He turned back to Petra, and the silvered eyes were steady. "So choose now, weapon or bookkeeper. Run with one soul and spend the rest of your life looking over your shoulder. Or carry the fire up through the middle of them and balance the whole cursed book."

Petra looked down at the vial. Inside, the small trapped light beat against the glass — once, twice — like a moth, like a heart, like a knock on a door from the inside.

When she raised her head, her decision had already written itself somewhere behind her eyes, in a hand she was only beginning to recognize as her own.

"How many doors do you know?" she said.

Sorrel grinned, and the darkness around them filled with the smell of the sea.

"All of them, little clerk. Now walk. Your war just got an audience."
