## 20. Empty Pages

The mountain stopped screaming at dawn.

Odell had not known a mountain could scream until that night, but the sound of it — the deep tolling bells, the grinding of stone against stone, the voices of ten thousand salt-petitioners crying out with throats they hadn't owned in centuries — had followed him up through the mine-shafts like floodwater chasing a drowning man. Then, somewhere before first light, it all simply ceased. The quiet that came after was worse. It was the quiet of an empty counting house, of a ledger closed for the last time, of a page where something used to be written and now was not.

He found her sitting in the rubble of the great gate.

She was cross-legged on a slab of fallen masonry, dust to her elbows, ash in her hair turning the brown of it grey at the temples as though she'd lived twenty years in one night. Her hands lay open in her lap, palms up, empty. Not clenched. Not reaching. Just open, the way hands are when there is nothing left to hold.

"Petra," he said.

She looked at him. That was the worst part, later, when he tried to tell Maren about it — she looked at him the way you look at a stranger who has addressed you on a street corner. Polite. Faintly puzzled. Her eyes were the same grey-green they had always been, sharp even now, cataloguing him out of habit: his torn coat, the blood crusted at his hairline where Vess's enforcers had worked him over, the ledger satchel still slung across his chest because some part of him had refused to set it down even while running for his life. She saw all of it. He could feel her seeing it. And none of it meant anything to her.

"Do I know you?" she asked.

The question went through him like a pick through slate. "Odell," he said. His voice came out wrong, too high, and he had to start again. "I'm Odell. I work the lower floors. Fences mostly. You — Petra, we've been partners three years. You taught me the memory-thief's grip. You stole my hangover once because I couldn't afford to lose the morning. You—" He heard himself listing debts, because that was the only language either of them knew, and hated every word of it. "You're Petra Dravenmoor."

"Am I." She said it without wonder. That frightened him more than tears would have. She looked down at her open palms as if checking them for something, and found nothing, and looked back up. "There was a fire," she said slowly, feeling her way. "A book. I remember a fire and a book, and a voice saying my name — no. Saying *a* name." Her brow creased, the faint ghost of a frown, gone almost before it arrived. "It's all smoke after that. It's all smoke before that, too."

The bells above them gave one last shudder and went silent forever.

He crouched in front of her, careful, the way you approach a bird that might bolt. "Can you walk?"

"I think so." She stood when he offered his hand, but she didn't take it. She stood on her own, testing her legs like someone learning the trick of them, and then she looked at the ruined gate, the mountain behind it breathing its last plume of dust, and said, "Was it me? Did I do that?"

"You did that."

"Should I be sorry?"

"No," Odell said, and found he meant it with his whole bruised body. "No. Whatever you were — whoever you were — the world's lighter by one god-king and heavier by nothing at all. Come on. Ashvale's downhill. Everything important is downhill."

She walked beside him. Not with him — beside him, at the careful distance strangers keep, matching his pace without knowing why. Twice on the switchbacks she paused and pressed her hand flat against the rock as though listening for something in it, and twice she shook her head and moved on. Once she said, without looking at him, "You keep looking at me like I'm a page you've read before."

"I have," Odell said.

"Was I any good?"

He thought about it honestly, because she would have wanted honesty and because lying to her now felt like pissing on a grave. "You were the best thing this city ever produced and never deserved."

She considered that. "That sounds like a debt," she said. "I don't like owing things I can't count."

"It's not yours anymore," he said. "It's mine."

* * *

Ashvale did not believe them at first.

They came down off the mountain road in the grey of morning, two figures limping through the terraced vineyards, and the first person to see them was a boy driving goats, who took one look at their faces and ran shouting toward the lower town. By the time they reached the Saltgate, half the district had turned out. Word had come ahead of them on the wind and the wires and the prayers of the desperate: the court beneath the mountain was fallen. The god-king was ash. The contracts were unmade.

An old woman pushed through the crowd and seized Odell's sleeve. She was one of the salt-petitioners' kin — he recognized the family resemblance, the same hollow cheeks, the same stubborn jaw — and she was holding a clay jar filled with fine white powder, and her eyes were wet and furious and alive.

"He woke them," she said. "At the end. My grandmother — she went under the mountain sixty years ago to petition for my grandfather's debt, and Aurelax turned her to salt and told us she'd been refused. Last night she *woke*. The gods answered her. She remembered her own name before she crumbled. Do you understand? Sixty years as dust, and the last thing she ever got to be was *remembered*."

"I understand," Odell said quietly.

The woman looked past him to Petra. Petra looked back at her with that polite, blank attention, and the old woman's face fell a little, the way faces do when they meet a soldier home from a war and realize the war came home inside him too.

"She doesn't remember," the woman said. It wasn't a question.

"No."

"What did it cost her?"

"Everything she had," Odell said. "And she paid it glad."

Petra tilted her head at that, faintly troubled, like someone hearing a song from a room away. But she said nothing, and let him lead her through the crowd, and the crowd parted for them the way crowds part for funeral processions and coronations both, because they could not decide which this was.

* * *

Maren woke whole on the third day.

Odell was there when it happened — he had hardly left the sickroom in the shrine-house of Saint Verrin, where the sisters had taken the girl in, because guilt is a leash too and his had a long reach. For three days the child had lain in a narrow bed beneath a window facing the mountain, breathing shallow, dreaming hard. The sisters said her soul was settling back into her, finding its old rooms, hanging its old pictures. Odell said nothing. He kept the books of his own fear instead, entering each hour in neat columns, waiting for the entry that read *she does not wake*.

On the third morning, Maren opened her eyes, sat straight up, and said, "I'm hungry, and I've had the most peculiar dream."

The relief hit Odell so hard he had to sit down on the floor.

She was nine years old and entirely herself. She ate two bowls of porridge and complained about the honey ration. She asked after her parents' grave, which she visited that afternoon in a dress the sisters lent her, standing very straight between the headstones with her chin up, and Odell watched from the gate and understood that whatever else had been done to her, nobody had managed to make her small. Then she demanded to be taken to see "the lady from the ledgers," because she dreamed of her every night, a woman made of smoke standing in a burning library, holding the door open.

When they put them in a room together, Petra studied Maren the way she studied everything now: carefully, completely, without recognition.

"You're the child," Petra said.

"I'm Maren."

"You were in the book."

"I was in the book," Maren agreed solemnly. Then, with the ruthless practicality of children: "Did it hurt? Getting me out?"

Petra frowned. Somewhere far down behind her eyes, Odell saw a door strain against its hinges — a flicker, a shadow of a shadow, smoke remembering it had once been wood. "I don't remember," she said. "I think I paid for the forgetting. So probably it hurt."

Maren nodded, satisfied, as if this were the most reasonable arithmetic in the world. Then she crossed the room and took Petra's hand and held it in both of hers, the way you hold something rescued from a fire.

"Thank you," she said. "I don't know what for, exactly. But thank you."

And Petra — hollow, emptied, a house with the furniture carried out into the street — looked down at the child's hands around hers, and her face did something it had not done in three days. It softened. Whatever had been written in her had been burned away, but the shape the writing had pressed into her remained, the way letters remain in paper after the ink is scraped: *be kind to the small ones; they are always the ones sold*.

"You're welcome," Petra said, and meant it, and did not know why she meant it, and wept without knowing why she wept, silently, while Maren patted her hand and told her, gravely, that everything was going to be all right now.

* * *

Vess Corvane did not go gently.

Odell attended the guild tribunal because they summoned him — twice, actually, once as witness and once, briefly, as suspect, until the codex forgery came to light and the direction of everyone's suspicion swung like a weathervane in a storm. He sat in the public gallery of the counting hall with his ribs still taped from Vess's enforcers' attentions and watched the guild master of eleven years stand trial in the chamber where she had ruled them all.

She had lost weight. That was the first thing. The second was that the warmth was gone from her — that famous Corvane kindness, the generous laugh, the hand on your shoulder that always turned out to be attached to a leash. Without Aurelax behind it, the warmth had been revealed as what it always was: a lamp lit by borrowed oil. What remained was cold and precise and still, even now, magnificent. She defended herself for six hours without notes. She cited precedent, charter law, the founding statutes of the Guild of Impossible Trades. She argued, and not entirely wrongly, that everything she had done had been done to keep Ashvale's economy of shadows from collapsing, that the soul-contracts were few, that the mountain's appetite was ancient and had to be fed by someone, and that she had merely been the steward of a terrible inheritance.

"And whose signature," asked Magistrate Hollen, "is at the bottom of the Maren Sallow contract?"

Silence, in a hall built to measure silences.

"Mine," Vess said.

"Whose order?"

"His." And here her composure cracked, just once, just enough — a hairline fracture across the marble, and through it Odell glimpsed something that was almost grief, or perhaps only rage at having been fooled. "For two hundred years the court beneath the mountain has held our charters. Every guild in this city has fed it. I was told it was necessary. I was told it was balance. I believed—" She stopped. Straightened. "I believed the ledger balanced. I was wrong. Do what you like."

What they liked was this: stripped of rank, stripped of guild-name, her assets entered into the public record and distributed as restitution — beginning, by unanimous vote of the magistrates, with the families of every soul the mountain had taken. It was not justice. Justice would have been a rope. But Odell watched her walk out of the hall between wardens, spine straight as a ledger-line, and he thought: *she'll be someone's problem again someday, somewhere south, running smaller cons in smaller rooms.* And he found, examining himself carefully the way you check a wound for fever, that he felt no triumph. Only tiredness, and a strange pity, and the memory of her office — how warm it had been, how much he had wanted, once, to be invited into it.

The trap had been comfortable. That was what made it a good trap. He resolved, then and there, to build a world with fewer comfortable traps in it, starting with the one currently being drafted in his own head.

* * *

Winter came early that year, and gentle.

By the time the first snow dusted Ashvale's rooftops, the changes were everywhere and small: a new shrine in the fishmonger's quarter where the old idol had wept blood, tended now by volunteers, its offerings going to the poor instead of the priests. Petitioners walking openly to the mountain's sealed gates to speak their grievances aloud to the air, because the air, these days, seemed to listen. And in the counting house, a locked drawer in Odell's desk containing a codex copied in two hands — one sure, one shaking — that recorded every secret the old guild had ever kept, awaiting the day someone brave enough would know what to do with it.

He was not brave. He had learned that about himself on the mountain road, giving Vess the wrong pass with blood in his mouth, buying Petra hours with the only coin he had. Bravery was her trade. His was keeping books, and carrying things, and staying.

So he stayed. He rented a room near the shrine-house and took Petra to it, because she had nowhere else and neither, if he was honest, did he. She drifted through the winter like a guest in her own story. She was not unhappy — that was the strange mercy of it. Emptiness, Odell discovered, is not the same as grief. Grief needs something to grieve. She was quiet, and competent, and kind in a way that startled people who had known the old Petra's sharper edges, and at night she sometimes sat by the window watching the mountain and pressing her palm flat against the sill as though listening for something buried under the floorboards of the world.

Once, in the deepest part of the season, he found her in the kitchen at midnight, standing over his ledger satchel with it open in her hands.

"I wasn't stealing," she said quickly, and then laughed at herself, short and puzzled. "Why did I say that first?"

"Old habit," Odell said. "Yours, not mine."

"There's a name written here." She turned the page toward him. In the margin, in his handwriting, in the column where he recorded debts he intended to collect or die trying, was written: *Petra Dravenmoor — owed: one self.*

"That's you," he said.

"I know it's me. It says so." Her fingers trembled slightly on the page. "But I don't know what I'm owed. I keep reading it and it's like reading about a stranger. A stranger with my face." She closed the satchel gently, reverently, the way you close a coffin. "Odell. Whoever I was. Was she worth missing?"

He thought of her in the counting house by candlelight, hiding her thefts in the margins. Of her pocketing a dead child's contract against every survival instinct she owned. Of smoke and bells and a door held open in a burning library.

"Yes," he said. "Worth everything."

She nodded slowly, accepting this as data, and carried it off to bed like a coin she didn't yet know how to spend.

* * *

Spring came up the mountain road on a Sunday.

Odell was at the market buying eggs when he heard the bells — not the old tolling of the guild, but the new bell the shrine volunteers had cast from melted reliquary gold, a bright, sweet-voiced thing that rang only for answered prayers. He dropped his basket and ran, because he had learned what that bell meant and because hope, once planted in a man, grows like bindweed whether he waters it or not.

The shrine was full. He shouldered through the crowd to the doorway and stopped.

Maren stood at the altar, nine years old and growing taller, her hand resting on the new idol's knee. The idol was weeping — not blood, not anymore; the waking gods had gentler manners — and the tears ran silver down the carved stone face and pooled in Maren's cupped palms. And across the crowded floor, kneeling where she'd happened to be standing when the bell began, was Petra, both hands pressed to the flagstones, her whole body shaking.

"No," she was whispering. "No, no, I paid for—"

"Petra." Odell knelt beside her and took her shoulders. "Petra, look at me. Do you know me?"

She raised her head. And her eyes — her grey-green eyes, sharp, cataloguing, *knowing* — fixed on his face, and filled, and focused, and became, for the first time since the mountain gate, the eyes of someone home.

"Odell," she said. "You look terrible. Your ribs. Did they heal crooked? They healed crooked. You never go to the physiker, you're worse than my mother about—" She stopped. Her hand flew to her mouth. "*My mother*. I remember my mother. Odell, I remember everything, I remember the courtroom, I remember the fire, I remember—" She was laughing and sobbing in the same breath, gripping his coat with both fists. "She prayed for me. The child. She prayed for me and they *answered*, they actually answered, no one's ever—"

"The bell rang for you," Maren called from the altar, grinning through her own tears. "It's your prayer! Everybody heard it! It's the first one they ever owed anybody!"

Around them the crowd was on its feet, and the silver light of the idol's tears was spreading along the flagstones like dawn coming early, and somewhere above the snowline the mountain hummed — not screamed, not ground, just hummed, low and vast, the sound of an enormous ledger opening to a clean page.

Odell held onto Petra Dravenmoor and wept like a man repaid a debt he'd never dared enter in the books.

And far overhead, faint as a signature, the humming shaped itself — he would swear this to his grave, he would write it down that night in the margin of the new codex — into something that sounded almost like a name.

Almost like *hers*.
