## 22. A New Sigil for Ashvale

The shrine had been a fish-cellar once.

That was the joke Odell liked best about it — that the first free building in Ashvale, the one where no debt could follow you through the door, still smelled faintly of brine and old salt when the weather turned damp. He claimed it kept the gods honest. Petra suspected it kept *her* honest, which was worth more. You could forget a great many things in incense and marble. It was harder to forget them standing in a room that remembered what it was to hold dead fish and working men's grief.

She stood at the long table now, morning light coming gray through the high grate above, the way light always came in Ashvale — sifted, patient, unwilling to promise anything. The table was oak, salvaged from the counting house of the Ashvale Guild itself, pried from its moorings the week after Vess Corvane's trial. Someone had gouged initials into its edge decades ago: *T.M., 3 coppers owed.* Petra had run her thumb over the carving for a long time before deciding to keep it. A ledger should remember it was made of people.

Before her lay the book.

It was not beautiful. She had wanted it beautiful at first — she had imagined vellum, gilt corners, a sigil stamped in silver on the cover like the Guild's own ledgers wore, only inverted, cleansed. But beauty was how the Guild dressed its traps, so she'd chosen ugly instead: thick rag paper, boards wrapped in undyed linen, stitching visible along the spine like scars. Anyone could make this book. That was the point. Any child with a quill could copy its shape, and any street could keep one, and if a thousand ugly books bloomed across the city then no guild, no god, no king beneath a mountain could ever again be the sole keeper of what people owed.

The free ledger of Ashvale. Open to all. Sealed against none.

*Entry the first,* she wrote, because the codex she'd filled in the shrine's first weeks had been practice, and practice did not count. *Received into public record: one city's debts, confessed freely.*

Then she began the work.

It went slower than anyone had promised her it would. Debts were easy — debts were arithmetic, and arithmetic had always loved her, even before she'd known why, even back when the skill was a splinter of some older self lodged in her like shrapnel. But contracts were another matter. Every one had to be read aloud, witnessed by two citizens who were neither party to it nor paid by either, and entered in plain speech with no sigil-work permitted anywhere in the text. No seals that could bind. No marks that could reach. If a contract could not survive being written plainly, under daylight, in front of neighbors, then it was not a contract. It was a leash, and leashes went in the fire barrel by the door.

The fire barrel had done brisk business that first month.

Petra dipped her pen and worked through the morning stack. A widow's roof-debt, restructured at fair interest, witnessed by the cooper and the lamplighter. A journeyman weaver's indenture, voided entire — his master had sold three years of the man's sleep without consent, an entry Petra found in a Guild sub-ledger and copied here in red ink, the color she reserved for thefts. A fishwife's loan from a moneylender who had since fled the city; the debt transferred to the neighborhood common fund, to be repaid in whatever measure the woman chose, over whatever years she needed. Odell had argued for forgiving it outright. Petra had refused.

"She borrowed," Petra said. "She said so herself, first time she came through that door. Said it like it shamed her."

"And you'd rather she carry shame than copper?"

"I'd rather she carry *truth.*" Petra had tapped the page. "The old way hid what people owed until the owing became a hook in their flesh. We don't do hiding. She owes. She'll pay. And every coin she pays goes into a fund that will lend to the next woman with no questions and no teeth. That's not a debt, Odell. That's a rope thrown between boats."

He'd grumbled. He'd also carved the fund's box himself, out of the same salvaged oak, and burned his maker's mark into the bottom where no one would see it but her.

That was Odell all over. He grumbled the way other men prayed.

*

By midday the queue outside the cellar door had grown to a dozen souls, and Petra called the morning recess, as she always did, because a ledger kept by tired hands was a ledger that erred, and errors in this book were not small things anymore. Word had spread beyond Ashvale's walls. Merchants from Greyhollow had come asking whether they might establish a free ledger there. A delegation from the river-towns. Once, incredibly, a pale nervous clerk wearing the colors of the Ashvale Guild itself — the Guild, diminished and audited and forced to publish its own accounts, sending a man to learn how it was done honestly.

Petra had served him tea and answered nothing he asked. Let them send someone sincere next time.

She stepped out into the alley behind the shrine to breathe, and the cold hit her lungs like clean water. Winter was coming down off the mountain. From here she could see its shoulders above the rooftops, white-crested now, unremarkable, just stone. Six months ago it had housed a cathedral of contracts and a god who ate prayers like a man eating oysters. Now it housed silence, and snow, and — if the miners' reports were true — galleries of salt figures that had begun, impossibly, to crumble into ordinary dust. Freeing themselves, the priests said. Becoming merely dead, which was a mercy, because merely dead could rest.

Aurelax was gone. Not slain — Petra had burned his ledger of names, and a god whose names are ash is simply *unwritten*, a story no one can find again. Some nights she dreamed of the court beneath the mountain, of gold scales weighing grief, and woke with her heart slamming. But the dreams came less often. And in the waking world, when children prayed at the rebuilt shrines, something listened — something gentle, plural, new-minted — and sometimes, not always, but sometimes, answered.

They had never told her what to do with the ledger. That was the thing she kept waiting for and it never came. The waking gods had returned her memories, every stolen scrap, every self-sold year, as their first answered prayer — and then they had left her alone. As though grace, having been given, trusted her to know what to build from it.

Or perhaps, she thought sometimes, watching the mountain, they were simply learning too. Learning that the best answer to a prayer is often no answer at all, just room enough for the praying person to stand up on their own.

Odell appeared at her elbow with two cups of chicory, steam curling. He didn't ask how she was. They'd passed that stage months ago, somewhere around the night she'd woken screaming with the memory of her mother's face restored — the face she'd traded away in a fishmonger's alley for a door in the mountain's roots, and gotten back, impossibly, from the hands of gods. Odell had sat up with her till dawn then, saying nothing useful, being entirely necessary.

"Queue's muttering," he said. "Old Tammer wants to know if a debt of gratitude can be entered."

"What does he owe gratitude *for*?"

"His granddaughter pulled him out of the canal. He says he can't die easy till it's set down somewhere official."

Petra smiled despite herself. "Gratitude isn't a debt. Tell him—"

"Already did." Odell sipped. "Told him the book records what binds, and love doesn't bind, it *carries*. Then I wrote it in anyway, in the back, under 'things witnessed.' Page after that one's full of them. People keep bringing them." He shrugged, embarrassed by the softness of it. "Can't seem to stop them."

Petra looked at him — really looked, the way she'd learned to, the way you learn when you've spent years unable to see your own past and suddenly can. Odell Fence, guild-fence, keeper of secondhand everything, who had forged a codex to save her, lied to Vess Corvane under torture and given her the wrong pass, and carried her hollow-eyed body down out of a ruined mountain without knowing if anything of her remained inside it.

"The back pages," she said. "Things witnessed. That might be the most important part of the whole book."

"Don't tell anyone." He handed her the second cup. "I've got a reputation as a hard man to maintain."

*

Afternoon brought the harder cases.

A man came wanting to enter a contract selling his daughter's labor for seven years — lawful, he insisted, the Guild had allowed it for generations. Petra heard him out in full, in front of the witnesses, per procedure. Then she asked the daughter, a girl of eleven with her father's stubborn jaw, what *she* wanted entered.

"That I don't," the girl said.

"Entered," said Petra, and wrote it in plain speech: *Mira Tanner declines.* "And the father's proposed contract is rejected. Not forbidden — I don't forbid, that's the old way. Rejected. Which means no free ledger in this city will record it, and an unwitnessed contract has no force here, and every citizen knows it." She looked at the man. "You may take your daughter home, or you may take your grievance to whatever dark corner still honors the old rules. One of those places has soup."

He took his daughter home. The girl paused at the door and looked back at Petra, and something in the look — wary, measuring, a child checking whether a kindness had a hook in it — snagged on an old scar in Petra's heart. Nine years old, a counting house, a contract sealed in a language the child couldn't read. Maren had been that look once.

Which reminded her. There was one entry left today, and it was the reason the shrine smelled of baking bread, because Maren had insisted, *absolutely insisted*, that no ceremony of hers should happen without food.

*

Maren arrived at dusk with her aunt — the aunt who had taken her in after the restoration, after the soul came home to her body like a bird to a lit window — carrying a loaf the size of a paving stone and wearing her good coat, which was to say, her only coat, brushed furiously clean.

"You don't have to do this part," Petra told her, quietly, at the table while Odell saw the aunt to a chair. "The record stands complete already. Your name's been in it since the first week. Contract of sale: voided, unmade, stricken by fire beneath the mountain. Witnessed by every god that woke."

"I know." Maren set the loaf down with tremendous gravity. "But it doesn't say *paid*."

Petra blinked. "What?"

"It says voided. Stricken." The girl counted off on her fingers, a habit she'd learned, everyone teased, from watching Petra. "Voided means it never happened. But it *did* happen. Somebody bought me. Somebody carried me in a little glass box like a moth. And somebody else—" she stopped, restarted, jaw set the way it did when the words were too big for her mouth and she was going to say them anyway, "somebody gave everything to get me back and then forgot she'd ever done it. That's not nothing. That's a debt. And I'm not letting it sit in some book marked *voided*, like it was just a mistake somebody erased."

She slid a folded paper across the table. Inside were coins — real coins, worn and mixed denominations, clearly saved over months. Not nearly enough to buy back a soul. Enough to mean it.

"So write what actually happened," Maren said. "Write that I was sold, and that I was bought back, and that the price was paid — by you, mostly, and by the gods, and by Mr. Odell, and a little bit by everybody in this city who stopped looking away. And then mark it paid. Because if my name's in the book, it should be in the book *true*."

The shrine was very quiet. Even the queue at the door had gone still, listening.

Petra thought of the ledger Aurelax had kept — names in gold ink, souls as entries, everything weighed and owned. She thought of the Guild's ledgers, shadows and bottled years and children's futures filed under fees. She thought of the ugly linen-bound book before her, made so that any hand could copy it, built to hold truth instead of leverage.

Every ledger she had ever known recorded what people *owed*. None of them, not one, had ever dared to record what people were *owed* — and by whom, and whether it had been paid.

"Maren Dravenmoor," Petra said — for the girl had taken the name, last winter, to the aunt's tearful astonishment and Petra's carefully hidden ruin — "will you witness your own entry?"

"Obviously," said Maren.

Petra turned to a fresh page. Her pen hovered a moment, and she found her eyes were burning, and decided, in the spirit of the institution she'd founded, not to hide it.

*Entry the nine hundred and forty-first,* she wrote. *In the matter of Maren, once sold, once stolen, twice rescued.*

*Let it be recorded that a soul cannot truly be sold, only lent to darkness, and darkness does not get to keep what the light comes for.*

*Received this day toward balance: courage, one measure, aged nine years. Bread, one loaf, freely given. Truth, spoken aloud before witnesses.*

*Paid in full.*

She signed it. Odell signed it. Two witnesses from the queue signed it, and then a third asked to, and then half the street wanted to sign, and Odell had to restore order by threatening to enter *their* names in the gratitude pages, which apparently was mortifying enough to work.

Last of all, Maren herself leaned over the great oak table, tongue between her teeth, and wrote beneath the entry in careful, crooked, entirely her own letters:

**PAID.**

*

That night, after the queue had gone and the bread had been eaten down to crumbs and Odell had banked the fire and gone home to his rooms above the chandler's shop, Petra stayed alone in the shrine with the lamp and the book.

She did this sometimes. Audited herself, Odell called it. She turned back through the pages — the widow's roof, the weaver's stolen sleep, Mira Tanner's flat refusal, the gratitude entries filling page after back page in a dozen different hands, because the city had discovered that the book belonged to them and treated it accordingly, leaving offerings of honesty the way folk once left offerings of blood.

Near the front, the first page. Her own hand, six months younger:

*Balance outstanding: everything.*

She read it a long time. Then she took up her pen, drew a single line beneath it, and wrote the closing entry of the day:

*Entry the final, this date. Received: one city, slowly learning its own weight. Condition: worn, but honest. Outstanding balance: everything still — but henceforth, held in common.*

She blotted the page. Outside, snow was beginning to fall on Ashvale, settling soft on the mountain's shoulders, on the shrine's salt-clean walls, on the streets where no child's soul would ever again change hands in the dark. Somewhere across the city, in a hundred homes she'd never see, people were copying the ugly book into uglier ones, adding their streets' truths line by line, building the thing she'd only started.

Petra snuffed the lamp, shouldered her cloak, and stepped out into the falling snow — a woman with every memory she'd ever lost, walking home through a city that finally knew its own name, and owed her nothing, and gave her everything anyway.

The book would need a second volume by spring.

She was smiling when she thought it.
