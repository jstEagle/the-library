## 1. The Weight of Small Debts

The ledgers of the Ashvale Guild did not sleep, and neither, on the third night of every month, did Wren Joubert.

She sat at her desk in the Counting Room with a candle burned down to its last finger's width, a pot of ink gone thick as tar, and eleven books open before her in a crescent. Not paper—no one at the Guild would have called them paper, though *paper* was the closest honest word. The pages were vellum once, long ago, before the trades soaked into them. Now they were something that drank ink and gave back weight. When she wrote an entry, the page accepted it with a faint sigh, like a body settling into a chair it had been waiting in all day.

Wren had learned, in her first year, not to think about that sigh too hard.

The clock above the door read half past midnight. The hall outside was dark, the great iron lamps unlit, and the only sound was the scratch of her nib and, far below, through three floors of stone and one floor of things that were not stone, the sea-sound of the gate breathing.

Everyone else called it the undercurrent. Wren had stopped pretending it was anything but breathing years ago.

*Entry four hundred and twelve,* she wrote, in the small, immaculate hand that ten years of practice had worn into her bones. *One memory, secondhand, sold by a cooper's apprentice: the smell of his grandmother's kitchen. Received by the house of Vess, against a debt of six crowns, interest forgiven.*

She blotted it. The page sighed.

It was a small trade, as trades went. The Guild dealt in impossible things, yes—but most of what crossed her desk was small and sad rather than terrible. A night of sleep. A name nobody used anymore. The color blue, which was rarer than you'd think, and went for more than red ever had. Wren recorded them all with the same even hand and the same empty mind, because an empty mind was the only way to survive the work. Corvane himself had told her that, in her first month, standing behind her chair like weather. *You are the ledger-keeper, Mistress Joubert. You do not judge the entries. You keep them.* And then he'd added, in that soft voice he saved for his best knives: *That is why no one will ever trouble you. No one troubles the mirror.*

No one troubled the mirror. Ten years, and she had made herself so good at being unseen that sometimes she caught clerks walking through the spot where she stood, murmuring apologies to the air. She'd learned to take meals at odd hours so she wouldn't have to make conversation. She knew the names of everyone in the guildhall and was fairly certain fewer than five people knew hers.

There were worse fates. She'd written down enough of them to know.

*Entry four hundred thirteen,* she wrote. *One dream, recurring, sold by—*

The gate breathed. Louder than usual.

Wren's nib paused mid-word, a blot of ink swelling at the page's edge. She waited, counting. On ordinary nights the sound came slow as tide, in and out, a rhythm so constant you could set your pulse against it and forget it entirely. But tonight there had been three breaths where there should have been two, and the third had carried a note underneath it—a low, wet resonance, like a bell rung underwater, like a word spoken from the bottom of a well by someone who had almost remembered how mouths worked.

Then it settled. In. Out. Ordinary.

She finished the entry without reading it back. That was another rule she'd made for herself: never read an entry twice. What was recorded stayed recorded; that was the whole covenant of the ledger. To reread was to invite the entries to reread *you*, and there were old ledger-keepers' stories about what happened then, though Wren suspected the old ledger-keepers had invented those stories for the same reason she kept her own rules—to give the fear a shape small enough to carry.

She dipped her nib. Fourteen. Fifteen. Sixteen. The trades came up through the chutes in their sealed cylinders, each with its broker's mark and its wax, and she broke them open one by one and gave each impossible thing its honest line. Somewhere in the city above, people were sleeping differently tonight, or remembering less, or walking through the world a shade lighter than they'd been at dusk. Somewhere in the city above, the Guild's brokers were counting coins that would never quite cover what had been taken, and the debtors were telling themselves it was worth it, worth it, worth it.

*Entry nineteen,* she wrote. *One talent for music, incomplete, sold by a fiddler's widow against arrears. Note: seller wept throughout negotiation. Broker's mark attests consent given freely.*

Her nib hovered over *freely* for a moment longer than it should have.

Then she blotted it, and reached for the twentieth cylinder, and broke its seal, and stopped.

The broker's mark belonged to Halloway, who worked the Salt Ward, who was careful and dull and brought Wren nothing stranger than pawned lullabies. But the trade inside was written in a hand that pressed too hard, the letters gouged deep, as if the pen had been held by someone shaking.

*Seller: Maren Odile, laundress, Widow Row.*
*Goods: one shadow, entire, undivided.*
*Price received: restoration of health to Tomas Odile, age nine. Fever broken. Full recovery certified by attending physician.*
*Consent: given freely, witnessed twice.*

Wren read it three times. It was not the strangest entry she had ever recorded. In ten years she had logged a man's reflection, a woman's future children, an entire street's Tuesday. Shadows changed hands maybe once a year, usually from gamblers who'd lost everything else and wanted, they said, to feel nothing.

But this was a mother. This was a mother selling the thing that walked beside her out of doors, that stretched long and faithful across the cobbles at evening, that had been with her since the day she was born and would have been with her until the day she died—and she had done it for a boy of nine, and the broker had written *freely*, and both witnesses had signed, and somewhere on Widow Row right now a woman lay in a bed that no longer had a dark twin on the wall beside it, and a fevered child slept easy, and the arithmetic was perfect, and Wren could not stop looking at the word *entire*.

Undivided. They hadn't let her sell a piece of it. Of course they hadn't; the gate didn't deal in fractions of a soul's shape, Tamsin—the ledger-keeper before her, dead now, drunk—had told her that once. *Whole or nothing, girl. It's got no teeth for crumbs. It wants the whole bite.*

The gate breathed again. That low note beneath it, the underwater word.

Wren looked down at the cylinder still in her hand, at the shadow packed inside it in the manner shadows were always packed—which was to say, not visibly at all, the cylinder merely heavier than lead has any right to be, and cold, and humming very slightly when she held it near her ear, the way a seashell hums. She had held a thousand of these. She had never once put one to her ear before.

Inside, faintly, she heard rain. Or footsteps in rain. Or someone walking away from her down a street at night, forever.

She set the cylinder down with exaggerated care, the way you set down a sleeping animal, and made herself write the entry.

*Entry twenty. One shadow, entire, undivided, sold by Maren Odile, laundress, Widow Row. Price: life of son, restored. Consignment: the gate.*

Her nib stopped on *the gate*.

Most consignments went to buyers—houses, collectors, the rare private purchaser whose names she wrote in cipher. But some few, the ones the brokers marked with the black wax instead of red, went downward. To the vaults. To *below*. Those entries she recorded in the master ledger only, the oldest book, the one chained to her desk, the one whose pages were older than the Guild itself and warm to the touch on cold nights.

She had never asked where, exactly, *the gate* was a delivery *to*. There was a sealed door beneath the guildhall. Everyone knew that; it was part of the founding story, the romantic bit the tour-givers loved. The Door Beneath, locked since the Guild's founding, holding back whatever the founders had bound it to hold. Clerks dared each other to touch it. Apprentices left offerings—coppers, flowers, once, appallingly, a cat. Wren had keys to the corridor that led to it, because she was the keeper of the ledgers and the ledgers were the reason the door existed, according to the founding charter, which she had read so many times she could recite the relevant clause: *the Gate is fed by record, and record alone, and while the Ledger stands open, the Gate shall stand shut.*

While the ledger stands open. She used to think that meant the office hours.

She opened the master ledger to tonight's page. It was already waiting for her—that was the other thing about the old book, the thing she did not tell anyone, the thing she barely admitted to herself: it was sometimes open to the correct page before she reached for it. Tonight the page bore the date at its head in ink that matched her own hand, though she hadn't written it. The rest of the page was blank except for the single line, pre-printed in that same almost-her handwriting:

*Received this night: —*

And the blank after the dash seemed to lean toward the cylinder on her desk.

Wren Joubert had been invisible for ten years, and invisibility, she had learned, was mostly a matter of never hesitating where anyone could see you hesitate. So she did not hesitate now, in the empty Counting Room, with no one to see. She simply picked up the cylinder, carried it to the dumbwaiter at the room's heart—the brass cage that went down, always down, and never came up empty—and set it inside.

"Delivery," she said, because the dumbwaiter liked to be spoken to. Another thing she didn't admit to anyone.

The cage sank. She listened until the sound of its descent became indistinguishable from the gate's breathing, and then she listened a while after that, because the breathing had changed. Slower now. Satisfied. And beneath it, unmistakable this time, brief as a struck match: a woman's voice, or the memory of one, saying something in a language Wren didn't know but understood anyway, the way you understand crying.

Then silence, and tide, and the clock ticking toward one.

* * *

She finished the night's entries by two. Twenty-three trades, all told, and she closed each ledger, squared their edges, and chained the master book to the desk with hands that were steady in the way of hands that have decided to be steady.

Then she took the candle and went down.

This was the ritual. Ten years of ritual, performed on every working night, known to no one—or known, perhaps, to everyone, and ignored, because who notices the mirror taking a walk? She descended the east stair past the dormitories, past the scriptorium with its sleeping scribes, past the strong-room where the confiscated books slept in their iron cages, down and down along corridors that grew older as they went, the stonework shifting from dressed blocks to rough-hewn to stone so old it had forgotten it was architecture.

The corridor to the gate-chamber was forty paces long. She knew it in her feet. Thirty-eight, thirty-nine, forty—and there it was.

The door filled the end of the passage like a period ends a sentence. It was black—not painted, not iron, just black, a black that ate the candlelight a hand's breadth from its surface and gave nothing back. No handle. No hinges visible. Only the seal: a circle of white metal, bright as bone, stamped with the sigil of the Guild—an open book, and above the book a crown.

Wren stood at her usual distance, seven paces, close enough to see, far enough that she could pretend the cold she felt came from the stones.

"All quiet," she said. Her voice sounded absurdly small. "Twenty-three entries. Nothing unusual."

The door said nothing. The door had never said anything. That was the covenant, that was the whole point of the founding: *while the Ledger stands open, the Gate shall stand shut.* As long as she did her work, as long as every trade was honestly recorded, honestly delivered, the thing behind the black stayed behind the black. That was her job. Not heroism, not vigilance in any dramatic sense. Just the entries. Just the open ledger, night after night, the dullest sacrament in the world.

She turned to go.

And stopped, because the cold had moved. It was behind her now, between her and the stair, and it was shaped.

Wren did not turn around. Ten years of practice: you do not startle the dark; you address it like a colleague.

"The delivery was proper," she said quietly. "Witnessed. Sealed. Recorded." A pause. "It was a child's life. You don't get to complain."

The cold held for a moment longer—considering, weighing, the way Corvane weighed a debtor—and then it thinned, unraveled, drained away down the corridor like water finding a crack. The candle flame stood up straight again.

Wren walked back up the forty paces without hurrying. She did not run. Running was a thing people noticed.

* * *

In the Counting Room, she banked the fire, trimmed the wicks, set the ledgers in their row. Last of all she took up the day's loose papers to file them—the brokers' duplicates, the receipts, the refuse of commerce—and found, tucked among them where no clerk should have been able to tuck anything, a single sheet of heavy old stock, yellowed, brittle, smelling of the strong-room's iron cages.

Someone had confiscated it decades ago and it had somehow traveled, impossibly, upward into her tray. Or downward. She couldn't have said which direction it had come from, and that bothered her more than the words did.

Almost more than the words did.

They were written in a fine antique hand, faded to the color of tea, and they read:

*On the third night of the eleventh month, the ledger opens.*

Tonight was the third night of the eleventh month.

Below the line, in fresher ink—ink that looked, Wren realized with a cold that had nothing to do with the gate, exactly like her own—someone had begun to add a second sentence, and stopped:

*When it does, check whose hand—*

The rest was torn away.

Wren sat down slowly at her desk, in the room where she was invisible, and looked at the master ledger, chained and shut and patient, and thought: *check whose hand.*

She picked up her nib. She turned the master ledger to tomorrow's page, and wrote the date at its head in her own small, immaculate hand, and watched the ink sink in, and could not—for the first time in ten years—have sworn, watching the letters form, that they were truly hers.

Far below, the gate breathed. In. Out. Patient as a creditor.

And it sounded, tonight, almost like it was counting along with her.
