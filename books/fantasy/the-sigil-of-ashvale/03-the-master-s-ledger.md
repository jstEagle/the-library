## 3. The Master's Ledger

The prophecy-book had been in the confiscation vault for ninety-one years, and it did not want to be read.

Wren felt that the way she felt everything about the Guild's holdings—not with her hands but somewhere behind her sternum, a pressure like a finger pressed against the inside of her ribs. Some objects carried their history loudly. A wedding ring traded for passage out of Ashvale hummed with grief. A knife surrendered by a debtor who'd used it on his brother sat cold and heavy as a held breath. The book simply *pushed*, steadily, the way water pushes against a dam.

She ignored it. She was good at ignoring things.

The cataloging room occupied a wedge of the guildhall's third floor where morning light came through leaded glass and fell in grey diamonds across her desk. Around her, clerks scratched and murmured and coughed, invisible to one another in the way of people doing close work. No one looked up when Wren unlocked the vault drawer with her second key—the one no one ever asked about, because no one remembered ledger-keepers had keys at all—and lifted the prophecy-book into the light.

*Codex of the Veiled Years. Confiscated from the house of Sera Voss, cartographer, in the autumn of the Ash Interregnum. Contents: apocryphal. Value: negligible.*

That was the official entry, written in a hand two generations dead. Negligible. Wren turned the cover and felt the push behind her ribs sharpen into something almost like protest.

The pages were vellum gone the color of weak tea. The script was Tamsin-style—she didn't know the name yet, wouldn't for another day—but she recognized the discipline of it: prophecy-scribes wrote in a hand engineered to survive misreading, each letter formed so it could not be mistaken for its neighbors. Someone had cared whether this text survived.

She found the date on the fourth leaf from the end.

It was circled twice, in ink darker than the rest—a later addition, a century old or near enough, made by someone pressing hard enough to score the page beneath. The characters were cramped, urgent, written small as though the writer feared being caught mid-word:

*On this night the ledger opens. What was fed will come to collect. The door beneath the counting house remembers its true account.*

Tonight.

Wren sat very still. A clerk two desks away sneezed, apologized to no one in particular, and went back to his inventory of confiscated love-charms.

She reread it four times. Her first instinct—the instinct ten years in the Guild had ground into her like grit into a hinge—was to close the book, log it as examined, and shelve it among the other nonsense. Prophecy was contraband precisely because it was useless, Corvane said. Because it made people believe their lives were already written, and a man who believed that stopped paying his debts.

Her second instinct arrived slower and colder: *the door beneath the counting house.*

There was only one door beneath the counting house. There was only one door beneath anything in Ashvale, so far as the ledgers said, and the ledgers said nothing about it at all—which was itself an entry of sorts. Ten years of walking down the spiral stair every night, ten years of setting her palm against cold iron and feeling nothing, ten years of recording *gate: sealed* in her private log, and not once had she asked what the gate was for. You didn't ask. Asking was how you became visible, and visibility in the Ashvale Guild was how you became inventory.

But a century-old scribe had known the date. Had circled it. And tonight the iron would be cold under her palm again, and she would have to know something she could never un-know, or walk away knowing less than a dead cartographer's book.

Wren closed the codex, entered it in the day's tally—*examined, apocryphal, no action*—and began, quietly, over the next six hours, to pull records.

* * *

The trick of research in the Guild archive was to want nothing visibly. Curiosity left tracks; clerks noticed other clerks the way sheep notice wolves, dimly and too late. So Wren built her search the way she built any ledger entry: incrementally, plausibly, each request justified by the last.

She requested the founding charters because the codex referenced *the Veiled Years*, which appeared in the charter marginalia. She requested the charter marginalia because a note there referenced *the First Account*. She requested the First Account and was refused—*sealed by order of the Master, in perpetuity*—which told her more than the document itself would have, because the Guild sealed nothing in perpetuity except things that were still true.

So she went sideways. That was the ledger-keeper's whole art, after all: when a number wouldn't balance, you found what it was hiding behind.

Corvane's public accounts were open to senior staff, and Wren had been senior staff for six years without anyone appearing to notice, which suited her. They were beautiful accounts. That was the first wrongness, and she found it only because she had spent a decade learning to hear the silences between entries. Guild masters' ledgers were traditionally messy—disbursements to informants, payments to the city watch, bribes recorded in euphemism. Corvane's were pristine. Every line balanced. Every line balanced *too well*, the way a face balances when it is holding very still.

And there was a pattern in the disbursements, if you laid forty years of them side by side, which she did on her desk after closing, alone, with the lamps turned low.

Payments. Regular as tides. Not coin—coin was trivial, and Corvane spent coin the way other men spent breath. These entries were in the Guild's older unit of exchange, the one that appeared in the founding charters and nowhere in modern practice, because modern practice had (everyone believed) abandoned it as barbaric.

Years.

*To the undersigned account: three years, drawn against the life-tenure of [name redacted], per contract of voluntary surrender.*

Four hundred and eleven entries in forty years. Wren's pen hovered over her notes and did not move. Voluntary surrender contracts existed—had always existed, in theory; the Guild traded in impossible things, and years of life were merely the most impossible thing that still had a price. But she had never seen one executed. She had assumed they were vestigial, a clause kept for tradition, like the archaic blessing stamped inside every ledger's cover.

Four hundred and eleven surrenders. Three years, five years, once—her eye snagged on it and stuck—*eleven years, drawn against the life-tenure of a child of six*, and beneath that, in a different ink, someone had appended: *consent given by guardian.*

All flowing to the same destination. All flowing *downward*. The receiving account had no name, only a sigil: a circle bisected by a vertical line.

Wren knew the sigil. Everyone in the guildhall knew it, the way sailors know the shape of the sea. It was stamped on the lintel of the stairwell that descended past the vault levels, past the debtor cells, past the bottom of all recorded floors, to a landing where a single iron door stood in a frame of black stone.

The gate.

Corvane had been paying the gate for forty years. In years. In lives, converted at some rate she couldn't see, into something a door could eat.

She thought, absurdly, of the woman from two nights ago—the one who had sold her shadow for her son's fever. Wren had recorded the trade in a steady hand and gone home and slept, because that was the job: you recorded, you didn't rescue. The Guild's whole architecture rested on the principle that recording a thing and causing it were different acts, separated by a wall thick enough to live behind.

But the wall had a door in it, and the door had been eating for forty years, and the man whose signature authorized every feeding signed with the same flourish he used to approve Wren's quarterly supplies.

Betrayal was not the right word yet. Betrayal required trust, and Wren had never trusted Corvane; she had *relied* on him, the way she relied on the building not to fall down. What she felt now was closer to vertigo—that lurch when the floor proves to be thinner than you'd lived your whole life assuming. Forty years of payments meant forty years of *something on the other side accepting them*. Meant the gate wasn't a seal on a hole in the world. It was a mouth with a standing order.

She pulled the earliest entry again, needing to know when it started. Forty years ago exactly. The first payment dated—

The first payment dated nine days after the previous master's death.

Corvane hadn't inherited the habit. He had begun it. Whatever bargain he'd struck, whatever he was buying with other people's lifetimes, he had walked down those stairs in his first week as master, knocked on iron, and offered.

Wren copied the sigil-account into her notebook, then copied the dates, then—after a long moment, her pen trembling in a way it never did—copied the child's line entire. Eleven years. Consent given by guardian. She wrote it because she was beginning to understand that records were the only weapon she had, and weapons needed to be hidden where no one swept for them.

Then she heard footsteps on the marble stair.

* * *

They were unhurried. That was the worst part. Clerks hurried; debtors shuffled under guard; auditors marched. Only one man in the guildhall moved through these upper rooms at midnight as though the building were a body he owned outright, every corridor a vein, every lock a joint that bent for him.

Corvane.

Wren blew out her lamp in the same motion she used to slide the notebook beneath the false bottom of her desk—the motion she'd rehearsed for a decade without knowing why, the body keeping secrets ahead of the mind. The master's accounts lay open on her desk. She considered closing them, decided closing them was more conspicuous than leaving them, and settled instead into her chair with the confiscation codex in front of her, open to a page of apocryphal star-charts, her pen moving in slow annotation.

The footsteps came along the gallery. Paused.

"Late," Corvane said, from the doorway, without entering.

"Cataloguing." Wren didn't look up. Her voice came out level, and she marveled at it from a great distance, the way you marvel at your own hand continuing to write while a wolf watches from the tree line. "A cartographer's codex, confiscated ninety-one years ago. Mostly star-charts. Some apocrypha."

"The Veiled Years codex." He said it lightly, a man identifying a wine. "Sera Voss. She drowned, eventually. Off the Salt Quay. An accident."

"I read that in the intake record."

"Mm." Silence, long enough that Wren counted her own heartbeats and lost track at eleven. Then: "You've been pulling charter materials this week. Marginalia. Founding-era exchange rates." His voice remained mild, interested, the voice of a man discussing weather. "An unusual course of study for a ledger-keeper."

"The codex references old units. Years of tenure. I wanted to confirm the conversion rate for the valuation appendix."

Another silence. Through the doorway's edge, in the light of the corridor lamp, Wren could see the shadow his body cast into the room—long, precise, the shadow of a man who stood very straight. It reached almost to her desk.

"Of course," Corvane said. "Thoroughness. It's why I keep you, Joubert. You're the best invisible man I have." A dry sound that might have been laughter. "Don't stay too late. The building gets strange after midnight. Old buildings do." The shadow withdrew. "Leave the codex on my desk tomorrow. I'll want to look at it myself."

His footsteps receded down the gallery, unhurried, and descended the marble stair, and Wren sat with her pen touching paper and made no mark at all until the last echo died.

Then she breathed, and discovered she'd been breathing shallowly for minutes, and her hands—steady through all of it, steady through the wolf at the door—began to shake so badly she had to press them flat against the desk.

He knew. Not everything—he'd have taken the notebook and taken her otherwise, fed her downward with the rest, eleven years, consent given by guardian—but he knew *something*, and worse, he'd shown her he knew. Leaving the codex on his desk. That was a leash, not a courtesy. Tomorrow he would read what she'd read, and the day after, Sera Voss's fate would be available for consultation.

Which meant tonight was all she had.

Wren took out the notebook and looked at what she'd stolen: four hundred and eleven payments in years, flowing to a sigil she'd pressed her palm against a thousand times. A master who'd begun feeding in his first week. A conversion rate she couldn't find anywhere in the modern ledgers—which meant the receiving account set its own prices, which meant the thing below was not livestock being managed but a creditor being paid, and creditors, in Wren's long experience, eventually called in the principal.

*What was fed will come to collect.*

She needed someone who could read the codex properly—who could tell her what the Veiled Years prophecies actually said, not just the one circled date. Someone fluent in the old scribal conventions, the cipher-margins, the way prophecy-scribes hid the dangerous parts inside the boring ones. The Guild hadn't employed such a person in sixty years.

But the Guild had *imprisoned* one. Everyone knew the story, told in the low voices clerks used for ghost stories: the scribe who transcribed confiscated prophecy and was caught reading it aloud, sentenced to the deep cells indefinitely, because you couldn't execute someone who might have memorized the future.

Tamsin. The name surfaced from the sediment of ten years of overheard gossip, attached to no surname anyone remembered.

Wren closed the notebook, locked it away, and sat a moment longer in the dark with the diamonds of streetlight falling through the leaded glass across her empty desk. Below her, past six floors and a spiral stair and a landing no record acknowledged, the iron door waited with forty years of payments banked against it.

Two nights from now, the ledger opened.

She picked up her lamp, lit it, and went down—not toward the stairs home, but toward the depths, toward the cells, to bargain with the one person in Ashvale who might know what came next.

The corridors seemed to shift around her as she walked, the way they sometimes did at night, walls rearranging themselves in the dark. She chose not to notice that either.
