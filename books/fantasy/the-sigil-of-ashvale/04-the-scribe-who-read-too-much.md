## 4. The Scribe Who Read Too Much

The cell had no door. That was the first thing anyone learned about the Scriptorium's deepest room: it did not need one, because the stairs down were the lock.

Tamsin Vael counted the steps every morning. Forty-one going down, forty-one coming up, and she never came up. She had counted them four hundred and six times now—she kept the number in her head the way other prisoners scratched marks into walls, except that marks could be found, and numbers in a scribe's head were safe. Numbers, at least, no one could confiscate.

Four hundred and six mornings of gray light through a slot too high to reach, of bread with chalk in it, of ink they let her keep because a scribe without ink was a joke even the Guild didn't find funny. They gave her vellum and transcription work and expected nothing but obedience, which was the one thing she had proven, spectacularly, that she did not possess.

Her crime fit on a single line in her own hand, which was the cruelty of it. *Read what is written only; do not read what is meant.* Every prophecy-scribe swore it on induction. Every prophecy-scribe broke it eventually, because you could not copy ten thousand words of the future with your hands and keep your eyes dead. The Guild knew this. The Guild simply waited to see who broke it *interestingly*.

Tamsin had broken it interestingly.

She had been copying the *Vellum Concordance*—a century-old book of predictions so dense with hedge-language that most scribes treated it as decoration—and somewhere around the three thousandth word her eye caught a pattern the original author had hidden in plain sight. Dates. Not prophesied dates. *Recorded* dates. Events the Concordance described as future that had already come to pass, each one accurate, each one entered in a hand that predated the events by decades. Which meant either the book was real or someone was writing history backward, and both possibilities were worse than the other.

She had done the unforgivable thing. She had written a marginal note.

*Verified against Guild records. This text is not prophecy. It is accounting.*

They had taken the note, taken her post, taken her name from the rolls—her name, struck from the ledger like a bad debt—and given her forty-one steps instead. She had been Tamsin for four hundred and six days by count alone, because no one down here said it, and a name unspoken begins to feel borrowed.

On the four hundred and seventh morning, someone new came down the stairs.

Tamsin heard her before she saw her: a particular tread, soft-heeled and unhurried, the walk of a woman who had spent years making herself unnoticeable and had gotten so good at it that she'd forgotten how to be anything else. Then the lamplight bent around the last turn and Wren Joubert stood in the doorway that wasn't there, holding a lamp and a ledger, looking like an apology she hadn't yet decided to deliver.

"You're the ghost," Tamsin said. "The ledger-keeper."

"I'm the ledger-keeper." The woman's voice was flat as fresh paper. "You're the scribe who read too much."

"They gave me a cell instead of a headstone. I consider it a compliment." Tamsin set down her pen. Her current work lay half-finished on the desk—a transcription of grain tariffs from sixty years past, mind-numbing, deliberately so. Punishment transcription. "You've never come down here before. Four hundred and seven days, and you're the first visitor who isn't delivering bread or taking away my work. So either I'm finally being executed, or you want something."

Wren didn't answer immediately. She stood with the lamp raised, and Tamsin watched her do what watchers do: take inventory. The cell. The desk. The stack of finished transcriptions. The small ritual objects Tamsin had accumulated—a chipped cup, a knotted cord, a strip of vellum folded into a bird that would never fly anywhere. Reading a room the way a scribe reads a page.

"You count things," Wren said at last.

"I count everything. It's the only property they can't seize."

"How many times have I checked the gate beneath the guildhall?"

Tamsin blinked. Of all the questions. "I couldn't know that. I'm imprisoned, not omniscient."

"But you'd know how to find out. If the records existed." Wren stepped closer, and the lamp threw her shadow long up the stairwell behind her, a tall dark shape that seemed more certain of itself than its owner. "That's why I'm here. I have records. I have a great many records. What I don't have is anyone who knows how to read them."

"Then hire a reader."

"The Guild's readers are Corvane's readers."

"And I'm Corvane's prisoner, which makes me the safest reader in Ashvale, doesn't it? I have no one to report to. No one to report *for*." Tamsin leaned back, and let herself look properly at this woman for the first time—not the inventory, but the person underneath it. Thin. Careful. Eyes that had the specific flatness of someone who had seen terrible entries and recorded them faithfully anyway. Ten years, the whispers said. Ten years invisible. Tamsin had spent four hundred days imagining the people above her; she had never imagined one like this, someone who wore invisibility like armor rather than suffering it like a sentence. "What did you find?"

"That's not your concern."

"It's entirely my concern. You're asking me to commit a second crime, ledger-keeper, and the first one cost me everything. I'd like to know the price of the goods before I buy."

For a moment he thought Wren would leave. She had the stillness of it—the coiled withdrawal of a creature deciding whether the risk was worth the exposure. But then something shifted in her face, some internal ledger closing one column and opening another, and she set the book she carried down on the desk between them.

It was old. That was the first thing, even before Tamsin touched it: the age came off it like cold off stone. Calf-bound, corner-protectors of dull brass gone green, and across the cover, stamped in a blind impression that most eyes would slide past, the sigil of a crown split down the middle.

Tamsin's hands went cold. "Where did you get this?"

"Confiscation intake. It arrived two nights ago with a shipment of banned texts. It was catalogued as a common fortune-book." Wren's voice stayed level, but there was a hairline fracture running under it now, and Tamsin heard it the way she heard everything—by listening for what wasn't said. "It isn't a common fortune-book."

"No," Tamsin said quietly. "It isn't."

She opened it with the care owed to a sleeping animal. The pages were foxed and soft-edged, the ink browned to the color of dried blood, and the script was a scribe's script—professional, disciplined, but with the small human inconsistencies that machine-perfect forgery never managed. A real hand. A real century. And there, in the middle third of the book, a date circled twice in a different ink, older work pressed deep enough to emboss the following pages:

*Tonight.*

Not tonight when it was written. Tonight as in *this* night, the night now four hours from ending, the night whose moon Tamsin had seen through her high slot rising fat and white over the guildhall's western tower.

"The ledger opens," she read aloud, because some things needed air to be believed. "'When the ninth moon wanes over the counting-house, the ledger opens, and that which was paid in shall begin to be collected.'" She looked up. "You found this two nights ago. You've had two nights to convince yourself it means nothing, and instead you came down forty-one steps to a disgraced prisoner. You already believe it. You just need me to believe it louder."

"I need you to tell me what it connects to." Wren's composure was fraying at the edges now, and Tamsin found, to her surprise, that she didn't enjoy watching it fray. Fear in another person should have felt like safety—company, at least, in the cold. Instead it made her own fear stand up and stretch. "There are references. Marginal citations. They point to other texts, other records. Records I can find, because finding records is what I do. But reading them—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I read numbers, scribe. Debts, trades, prices. I have spent ten years recording what people pay for impossible things, and I have never once asked what happens to the payments after they're recorded. I think something has been asking. I think something has been *collecting*. And I think the answer is in these margins, and I cannot read them, because I was trained to see only what is written and never what is meant."

"And I was trained for exactly that," Tamsin said, "and punished for exactly that."

"Yes."

"So we arrive at the bargain." Tamsin closed the book gently and rested her palm on its cover, feeling the century under her hand like a pulse. "Say it, then. What am I buying?"

"My silence about your escape. And a way out."

"Not freedom. Not a pardon. Not my name restored to the rolls."

"Those aren't mine to give."

"Honesty. How refreshing. How *Guild*." Tamsin almost laughed. Almost. "You'll notice I haven't said yes. Let me tell you what I know about bargains, ledger-keeper, since your books apparently don't cover it. Every trade in this building is a trap wearing a smile. Someone sells their shadow, their years, their child's face, and the Guild writes it down neatly and calls it fair because both parties signed. I transcribed those contracts for six years. I know the grammar of traps. So understand that when I weigh yours, I'm weighing it against the only currency I have left, which is knowing when not to speak."

Wren met her eyes. "And?"

"And you passed," Tamsin said. "Barely. You told me the truth when a lie was easier—you could have promised me a pardon and I'd have known it for ash, but you'd have said it, if you thought it would work. You didn't. That's the only reason I'm considering this." She drew the Concordance back toward herself and opened it again, to the circled date, and felt the old thrill rise in her chest, the one that had ruined her life once already: the pleasure of a pattern resolving. "Now. Before you ask me anything else, I'm going to show you why you were right to come down here, and why you should be considerably more frightened than you are."

She turned pages. She knew this book intimately—four hundred days will do that—and she moved past the hedge-prophecies, the weather-cant, the dynastic riddles, to the spine of the thing. The account.

"This isn't one prophecy. It's a ledger of its own. Look." She flattened the page under the lamp. "Every entry has the same structure. Something is bound. Something is fed. Something waits. The language rotates—king, vessel, door, debt—but the structure never changes. Whoever wrote this wasn't predicting. They were *documenting*. An arrangement. A very old arrangement, renewed on schedule." She tapped the marginal citation in the corner, a string of characters that meant nothing to Wren, she could see it in her face. "This reference points to a binding record. A formal one, Guild-format, the kind I used to transcribe. If it exists, it exists in the restricted archive. Third sub-level, sealed stacks, access by master's warrant only."

"You know the archive codes."

"I transcribed the archive index as punishment, my first year down here. They thought copying it would bore the crime out of me. Instead I memorized it." She allowed herself the smallest smile. "Punishment is a poor teacher of obedience. It's an excellent teacher of everything else."

Wren was silent a moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed—the flatness cracking into something rawer. "There's a private ledger. Corvane's. I found it tonight. Payments in years of life, hundreds of them, all flowing toward the gate. All flowing *in*, never out. And the dates—" She swallowed. "The dates match this book. Entry for entry. A hundred years of scheduled payments, and tonight's date circled in a book written before any of them fell due."

Tamsin sat back. The lamp hissed softly. Somewhere above them, muffled through forty-one steps and a hundred tons of stone, the Guild went about its nightly business—the clink of scales, the murmur of clerks, the ordinary music of ordinary theft.

"A century of feeding," she said slowly, "on a schedule written a century ago. By whom?"

"That's what I need you to read."

"And when we know? Then what?" Tamsin spread her hands. "You're a ledger-keeper with no authority and I'm a prisoner with no name. Suppose we decode the whole conspiracy. Who do we tell? Corvane? The man whose signature is presumably all over it?" She shook her head. "You haven't thought past the finding. Neither had I, the day I wrote my marginal note. Knowing is not the same as surviving knowing."

"I know." For the first time, Wren Joubert looked tired—not the performance of weariness, but the true kind, worn down to the wood. "I've spent ten years being no one because no one survives in this building. I told myself invisibility was safety. Two nights ago I stopped believing it, and I haven't slept since, and I'm standing in your cell offering you a bargain I can't fully honor because it's the only move I have left." She straightened. "So here is the honest version. I can't restore your name. But names are entries, scribe, and entries live in ledgers, and I keep the ledgers. If we survive whatever opens tonight—or tomorrow, or whenever that circle comes due—I will write you back into the rolls myself. My hand. Witnessed. Real. Not a pardon from the Guild. A correction."

A correction. As though her entire erased existence were a clerical error, waiting patiently for the right pen.

It was, Tamsin thought, the most insulting and the most tempting offer she had ever received.

"The gate," she said. "Tell me about the gate. Not the official version—the one in the orientation pamphlets about forbidden commerce. Your version. You check it. Why does a ledger-keeper check a sealed door every night?"

Something moved behind Wren's eyes, some door of her own opening a crack. "Because it hums," she said. "Very low. Below hearing, mostly, but not below feeling. And because every year the humming starts earlier in the evening. And because last night, for the first time in ten years, it sounded like it was keeping time. Like a rhythm. Like something counting."

"Counting what?"

"I didn't stay to find out."

"You should have," Tamsin said, and immediately wished she hadn't, because Wren's face went pale in the lamplight and stayed pale, and Tamsin understood she had just handed this careful, frightened woman one more reason to lie awake. Good, some small mean part of her said. Misery keeps company. And a quieter part answered: *you're lying to yourself. You said yes the moment she opened the book.* Four hundred and seven days of gray light, and here at last was something worth reading.

"All right," Tamsin said aloud. "Here's how this works. I help you. We start with the marginal citations tonight, while the guards change and the archive seals cycle—I know the rotation, punishment teaches patience too. You get me out of this cell and into the restricted stacks. And ledger-keeper—" she held up a finger, "—one condition beyond the name. Whatever we find, however bad it is, you don't decide alone what to do with it. I've been erased once for knowing too much. I won't be erased twice for trusting the wrong person's judgment."

Wren considered her for a long moment. The lamp flame steadied between them.

"Witnessed," Wren said finally. "Both conditions. In your presence, I'll write the terms into a working ledger and sign it. If I'm giving corrections, I can give contracts."

"Spoken like a true keeper of accounts." Tamsin rose, joints stiff from four hundred mornings of stone benches, and gathered her few things: the chipped cup, the knotted cord, the paper bird. The pen she wrapped in cloth and tucked into her sleeve, because a scribe without ink was a joke, but a scribe without a pen was nothing at all. "One warning before we climb. Whatever's behind that gate, whatever Corvane's been paying—it wrote this book. Or had it written. Prophecy this precise doesn't grow wild. And texts that document arrangements usually include contingencies for the people who read them."

"What kind of contingencies?"

Tamsin glanced at the circled date, at the deep-pressed ink, at the blind crown split in two on the cover.

"The kind," she said, "where the book falls open to exactly the page the reader needs—and the reader never wonders why it was so easy to find."

She followed Wren to the bottom of the stairs, lamp first, shadow climbing the wall ahead of them like a tall thin accomplice. Forty-one steps up, Tamsin told herself. Forty-one steps out of four hundred and seven.

She was on step thirty-eight when she realized she had never actually asked what Wren had been doing in the archive at midnight—and that Wren had volunteered nothing about it at all.

* * *

The upper levels of the Scriptorium were silent in the way only Guild buildings achieved: not empty-silent but *attended*-silent, as though the architecture itself were listening. They moved through it quickly, Wren ahead with the lamp shuttered to a blade of light, Tamsin behind with the Concordance strapped flat against her ribs beneath a borrowed clerk's coat that smelled of someone else's sweat.

At the junction before the restricted archive, Wren stopped so abruptly that Tamsin walked into her shoulder.

Ahead, spilled across the flagstones where no spill should be, lay a scatter of loose vellum pages—archive stock, official watermark, dozens of them, fanned out from the sealed stacks like a hand of cards thrown down in surrender. Tamsin crouched, careful, and lifted the nearest into the light.

It was an entry. A trade record. Standard format. A name she didn't recognize, a payment in years, a destination marked only with the Guild's glyph for the gate-chamber. Dated ninety years ago.

She lifted the next. Same format. Different name. Different decade. The next. The next. A century of payments, torn loose from their bindings, lying in the corridor like leaves shaken off a tree.

"Wren," she said quietly. "These are the master ledger's pages. These are the *payments*."

"I know," Wren said. Her voice had gone strange and thin. She was staring past the scattered pages toward the archive door, which stood open—an impossibility, Tamsin knew, sealed stacks didn't open without a master's warrant—and beyond it, into the darkness of the third sub-level, where the shelves stretched back further than lamplight could follow.

"Someone's been here tonight," Tamsin said. "Someone's been *removing*—"

"No." Wren's throat worked. She picked up one of the fallen pages, and Tamsin saw her finger tremble against the vellum, saw her read the same entry three times as though hoping it would change. "No, you don't understand. These aren't removed. Look at the edges. Look at the ink."

Tamsin looked. The edges weren't torn. They were *unwritten*—faded, bleached, the ink dissolving outward from the center like frost retreating from glass, as though the words themselves were being called home.

The entry she held was vanishing as they watched. The name went first. Then the sum. Then, last of all, patient and unhurried, the date.

In the dark of the open archive, somewhere far back among the shelves, something turned a page.
