## 5. What Wears the Crown

The cell had no door. That was the first thing anyone needed to understand about Tamsin Vael's imprisonment: the Guild did not believe she required one.

She had a room in the deepest archive tier, three levels below the counting floor, where the air tasted of cold iron and older paper. A cot. A lamp that never ran dry of oil because the Guild could afford generosity in the small things. Shelves of transcription work, brought down to her on a schedule, and taken away again when finished. And no door — only an archway hung with a curtain of grey felt that the guards on the landing pretended was sufficient, and that everyone involved understood was not a kindness but a message. *You may walk out at any time. There is nowhere outside these walls you will be permitted to remain.*

Tamsin had spent four years proving them right. She stayed because staying meant lamplight and books, and leaving meant streets where a disgraced scribe with her particular crime could not buy bread under her own name. Her crime was simple and unforgivable and written into her file in Corvane's own hand: *read what she transcribed.*

A scribe's duty was to copy, not comprehend. Every prophecy-book confiscated from street-churches and hedge-mages passed through the Guild's scriptorium before it passed into the vaults or the fire, and every scribe who touched it swore the same oath — eyes on the letters, never behind them. Tamsin had broken the oath the way water breaks stone: slowly, nightly, one line at a time, until comprehension was simply what her hands did while they copied. She had read forty-one forbidden texts before a supervisor noticed that her marginalia — meant to be mechanical notes on ink condition and page damage — contained cross-references. Connections between prophecies that were supposed to have been read by no living person all together.

They had burned her marginalia in front of her. They had kept the rest.

So when the ledger-keeper came down the archive stairs after midnight with a book wrapped in her shawl like a stolen infant, Tamsin knew two things at once. She knew why the woman had come to *her*, of all the prisoners in Ashvale. And she knew, with the sick certainty of someone whose whole life has been a preparation for a single bad hour, that whatever was in the book would be worse than anything she'd already read.

"You're Joubert," Tamsin said. "The quiet one. Ten years on the trade floor and nobody remembers your face."

"That's generally considered my best quality."

"It's a terrible quality." Tamsin moved the lamp closer. "It means nobody's watching you. Sit down."

* * *

The prophecy-book was a hundred years old and had been handled badly — water-stained along its lower edge, its spine re-sewn twice by amateur hands. Tamsin read the way she always read, which was to say she let the text arrange itself around its own center of gravity, the way iron filings find a lodestone. Most prophecies were dross: local fears dressed in borrowed symbols, a hedge-mage predicting a hard winter and calling it doom. But this one had structure. This one had been *composed*, by someone working from older sources, and composition left fingerprints.

"The hand changes here," she said, turning pages. "First third is one scribe, careful, educated. Then it degrades — different writer, less skilled, copying from the first. And then this." She flattened the final quire. "A third hand again, but hurried. Corrections in the margins. Whoever wrote this section wasn't transcribing. They were arguing with their source."

Wren Joubert leaned over the table. Up close she had the look of a woman who had taught herself not to react to things — level brows, steady hands, eyes that catalogued rather than wondered. But her breathing had gone shallow somewhere around the second page, and it hadn't come back.

"Read it," Wren said. "All of it. Out loud if you have to. I've stared at it for six hours and I can't make the pieces hold still."

"They won't. Not until you know what the pieces are." Tamsin pulled her transcription kit toward her — habit, comfort, the ritual motions that made reading safe. "Prophecies about gates are common. Prophecies about *doors* are rarer; a door implies a threshold someone built, a boundary with intent. This one uses both words interchangeably, which means the composer didn't distinguish them, which means—" she tapped the page, "—whoever bound this thing wanted it understood as architecture. Something made. Not summoned. Made, and sealed, and *keyed*."

"Keyed to what?"

"To a name." She found the line she'd been circling since midnight and set the lamp directly over it. The ink had gone brown as old blood, but the letters held:

*When the crown forgets its head, the ledger opens.*

"There," Tamsin said. "That's tonight's date circled below it, yes? In the margin?"

"Yes."

"Circles in margins are additions. Someone added that date *after* the text was complete — within the last year, judging by the ink. Which means someone alive today knows this prophecy well enough to date it." She looked up. "Who confiscated this book?"

"A debt-collection clerk. It came in with a defaulted trade."

"Then ask yourself why a debtor was carrying it. Ask yourself whether it was carried *to* the Guild on purpose." Tamsin watched the thought land — saw it in the way Joubert went very still, the way a person does when the floor shifts under assumptions they've stood on for a decade. Good. Fear that arrived early was worth ten times fear that arrived late. "Now. The rest of it."

She read. She read the binding verses — fragmentary, half-destroyed by water, but enough. A king — unnamed in the text, called only *the crowned one* and *the head* — had done something at the century's turn. Not a war. Not a conquest. A *transaction*. The prophecy was obsessed with transaction language: price, payment, consideration given and received, terms, default. It described a bargain struck between the king and something that existed on the far side of a made thing, a door built in the deep foundations of a city that the text refused to name directly but referred to, over and over, by a title instead: *the Counting Place.*

"And here." Tamsin turned to the damaged leaf she'd saved for last, handling it like a bird with a broken wing. "This is the part that matters. The part I'd bet my remaining years on. Listen to the grammar."

"'And the soul of the head shall be entered in the book,'" she read, "'and the body of the head shall remain upon the seat, and that which enters the body shall keep the crown warm, and the Counting Place shall keep the accounts current.'"

Silence. Somewhere above them, through three floors of stone, the Guildhall settled and creaked like a ship at anchor.

"Entered in the book," Wren repeated. Her voice was carefully flat. "You're saying the king's soul was *recorded*. Like a trade."

"I'm saying whoever wrote this used the vocabulary of commerce because that's the only vocabulary that fits what happened. A soul isn't destroyed in a binding. It's *held*. Collateral. And the thing holding it—" Tamsin pressed her palms flat against the table, grounding herself, because the next part deserved steadiness even if she didn't feel any, "—the thing holding it took the empty place. The body stays on the throne. The crown stays warm. And the accounts stay current, which means—"

"Payments." Wren said the word like she was spitting out a seed. "Ongoing payments. To the door."

"To whatever's behind the door. Yes."

* * *

After that neither of them spoke for a long time, and the silence had texture, layers, the way deep water does.

Tamsin used the time the way she used all time — reading. She went back through the text a second time, then a third, because horror that isn't verified is just imagination wearing a prophecy's clothes, and she had learned that lesson at cost. But the verification changed nothing. If anything, each pass made it worse, because the details she'd skimmed on the first reading now assembled themselves into a shape, and the shape was a century long.

A hundred years. That was the arithmetic sitting on the table between them like a corpse.

A hundred years of trades. A hundred years of the Guild recording exchanges in impossible things — years of life, shadows, names, luck, love, memory — and routing them, she would wager everything, toward a single account. She had transcribed enough confiscated material in four years to know how such systems were structured. Nothing fed itself. Every sustained working in every tradition she'd ever copied required fuel, and fuel of that quality — souls adjacent, identity-adjacent, *name*-adjacent — did not trickle. It flowed. It had to flow. Something had been drinking from Ashvale for a hundred years, and the pipe had a name, and the name was the Ashvale Guild, and the Guild had a master.

"Corvane," Wren said at last. She said it the way you test ice.

"He'd have to know. Maybe not from the beginning — maybe he inherited it the way you inherit a debt, thinking it's an estate. But he knows *now*." Tamsin sat back. "Joubert. Look at me. Do you understand what this prophecy actually says? Strip away the poetry. What it says is that the man on Ashvale's throne has been dead for a hundred years, and no one noticed, because the thing that replaced him is very good at wearing him."

"The king died a decade before I was born."

"The king died a decade before *anyone now living* was born. That's the elegance of it. There's no one left alive who remembers his face except from portraits — portraits painted after, by artists serving the court. The thing on the throne has had a century to curate its own likeness." Tamsin heard her own voice climbing and forced it down. Panic was a luxury; she'd learned to live without luxuries. "Every witness is dead. Every record passes through the same hands — your hands, Joubert. Your Guild. Who audits the audit? Who checks the ledger that keeps the ledgers?"

"No one." Wren's face had gone the color of the lamp-lit paper. "The ledger-keeper answers to the Master. The Master answers to the crown. And the crown—"

"—answers to the door. Yes. It's not a chain of command. It's a circle. And circles don't have exits."

* * *

It was Wren, surprisingly, who broke first — not into panic but into fury, which Tamsin privately rated as the more useful fracture. The ledger-keeper started pulling records from memory, naming trades she'd logged herself, dates and counterparties recited in the flat tone of a woman discovering that her life's careful work was a confession she hadn't known she was making.

"The shadow trades," Wren said. "There've been more than anyone would think possible. Shadows, memories, names — the trades everyone calls exotic. I logged eleven last year alone. I thought it was fashion. People go through fashions."

"It's not fashion." Tamsin was already writing, fast, her transcription hand doing what it did best — imposing order on chaos, building a concordance. "Exotic trades are the ones that touch identity. A shadow is a memory the body keeps. A name is a self made portable. Whatever's behind that door isn't eating years of life, Joubert — that's the small change. It's collecting *personhood*. It's trying to assemble one."

"Why would it need to assemble one?"

And Tamsin stopped writing, because the answer had been sitting in the prophecy's grammar all night, waiting for the question.

"Because it doesn't have one," she said quietly. "Think about what we're describing. A thing that takes bodies. A thing that needs payments of names and shadows and memories to stay... coherent. A thing that wears a dead king like a coat because it has nothing underneath." She turned the damaged leaf toward the light. "Here — the third hand, the hurried one, the one who argued with his source. He added a gloss. Look."

Below the binding verses, in ink a shade darker than the rest, someone a century dead had scrawled a single line of commentary, the letters cramped with haste or terror:

*It is not the king. It is the crown. It was never anything but the crown.*

Tamsin read it aloud, and then read it again, and watched Wren Joubert arrive at the same place she had arrived at alone, hours ago, in the dark of her own skull: the understanding that the enemy was not a creature that had seized power. The enemy was power — the office, the weight, the hunger of dominion itself — and it had been wearing Ashvale's kingship for a hundred years the way a leech wears blood.

"The trades feed it," Wren whispered. "Every impossible trade the Guild has ever recorded. Every shadow sold, every name signed away. It's been *building* itself out of us. And Corvane—"

"Corvane pays the bill," Tamsin said. "And passes the cost down. Always down. That's how debts like this survive a century — somebody at the bottom keeps paying and never sees the contract."

* * *

The lamp oil shifted color as the night deepened, and still they worked. By the fourth hour Tamsin had filled nine pages of her own hand — a concordance, a timeline, a map of the trap drawn from a single water-damaged book and one ledger-keeper's memory. It was more than she'd managed in four years of secret reading, and it terrified her how quickly it had come together. Some truths want out.

But it was incomplete, and the incompleteness had a specific shape.

"There's a gap," she said finally. "The prophecy describes the binding — the soul entered in the book. But it never says *which* book, or *where*. 'The Counting Place keeps the accounts.' That's a location. That's a room. And the original record of the binding — the actual entry, the collateral line — that record exists somewhere, because bindings like this are contracts, and contracts survive their signatories. Find that entry and you don't just prove all this. You find the mechanism. You find how to unmake it."

Wren was silent for a moment. When she answered, her voice had gone strange — careful, controlled, the voice of someone standing at the edge of something.

"The oldest ledgers aren't in the archive tiers. They're in the Master's private keeping. Sealed stacks, under his personal seal. I've catalogued around them for ten years. I've never opened one."

"Then tomorrow night, you open one."

"And if he notices?"

Tamsin looked at her — really looked, past the level brows and the steady hands, at the woman who had spent ten years being invisible and had come down into the dark carrying a stolen prophecy like a torch she didn't know how to hold. She thought about doors without locks, and oaths without sense, and the long slow crime of reading what you were told not to read.

"He'll notice eventually either way," Tamsin said. "Prophecies with circled dates don't get wrong, Joubert. They get *fulfilled*. The only question left is which side of the ledger you're standing on when it happens."

Wren gathered the prophecy-book back into her shawl, and for a moment her hands paused on the wrapping, and Tamsin saw her hesitate — the hesitation of someone about to say something that couldn't be unsaid.

"There's one more thing," Wren said. "The prophecy mentions an heir. 'The blood of the head, cast out beyond the walls, who returns when the accounts come due.' I checked the succession records tonight, before I came down. King Aldric had a son. The son was exiled twenty-five years ago — officially for treason. Unofficially, no one will even say his name anymore." Her eyes lifted. "He's alive, Tamsin. The records list a pension paid quarterly, to a fishmonger in the salt market, under another name."

Tamsin sat back slowly. Outside the archway, the felt curtain stirred in some subterranean draught, and the lamp flame bent, and for one heartbeat the shadows on the wall looked like a figure wearing a crown — and then straightened, and were only shadows.

"An heir selling fish," she murmured. "While a crown with nothing inside it eats his father's kingdom one trade at a time." She blew out a breath. "Then we'd better hope he's braver than his fishmonger's name. Because come dawn, Joubert, you're going to the salt market — and you're going to tell a stranger he's the last honest line in a century of lies."
