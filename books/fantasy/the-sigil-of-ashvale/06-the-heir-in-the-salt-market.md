## 6. The Heir in the Salt Market

The prophecy gave me a name, a city, and nothing else.

*The heir walks unmarked among the salt-stalls, known to the gulls and to no man.* Tamsin had traced the line with a cracked fingernail, her voice flat the way voices go when they're trying not to hope. *Unmarked* was scribe's cant—she explained it to me twice, patiently, the way one explains a thing to someone who keeps hoping she's wrong. It meant the boy had been struck from every record. No baptism entry. No tax roll. No guild registration. A person who existed only in the way a stone exists at the bottom of a river: present, and invisible to everything above the water.

I knew the feeling intimately. I had built a life out of it.

Which is why I hated that she was right, because I also knew exactly where a man like that would go. Not the Salt Market itself—too watched, too many guild eyes counting crates and collecting duties. But the market had edges, and edges were where the unrecorded lived. The fishwives' row along the canal behind the tanneries, where the catch came in stinking and silver at dawn and no ledger in Ashvale ever asked who sold it. The Guild recorded trades in impossible things. Fish was merely possible, and therefore beneath us.

So at an hour when honest people were still asleep and I was pretending to be one of them—plain wool dress, ledger-keeper's ink scrubbed from under my nails, hair bound back like any dockside widow—I walked down through the fog to the water and started looking for a dead man's son.

* * *

The fishwives' row at first light was a country unto itself. Gulls screamed over the gutting tables. Ice cracked and shifted in the carts. The canal moved slow and green beneath the pilings, carrying its cargo of fish heads and tannin and the occasional drowned rat with the dignity of a river that has seen worse. Women in rubber aprons called prices in a dialect of numbers and insults. Somewhere a bell rang the hour, and nobody listened.

I had a description, of a kind. The old portraits in the guildhall's restricted gallery showed King Aldous at forty—heavy-jawed, dark-haired, with the particular stillness of a man who has learned that every expression will be painted and argued over. The prophecy's marginalia, in Tamsin's careful reconstruction, said the heir favored him. Dark hair. The jaw. Eyes that the old scribe had annotated, in faded iron-gall ink, *too watchful for his years*.

That was not much. But I had spent ten years reading faces across a counting table, cataloguing the exact moment a seller decided to lie. I knew what watchful looked like. I went stall to stall, buying things I didn't need—a twist of smoked herring, a paper of salt I had no kitchen to use—listening.

The third stall from the tannery wall was run by a young man with his sleeves rolled to the elbow, hands red with cold, laying out mackerel in ranks with a precision that snagged at me before I understood why. Not the precision of a man arranging fish. The precision of a man arranging *entries*. Each fish angled the same way, each price chalked in a small, disciplined hand, no flourish, no waste. I had seen ten thousand hands write numbers. I knew what a hand that had been *trained* to write them looked like, even when it was writing prices on slate.

And he was too watchful for his years.

He looked up when I stopped at his stall, and his eyes did the thing that confirmed everything: they went to my hands first. Not my face, not the coin purse at my belt. My hands. Checking, the way I checked every customer who approached the sealed ledgers, for ink stains, for guild rings, for the small telltales of official attention.

"Smoked herring's sold out," he said. "I've mackerel. Good ice on them."

"Then I'll take mackerel." I counted out coppers, and let my sleeve slip back just enough—deliberately, this time—to show the faded ink stain at my wrist that no amount of scrubbing ever fully lifted. A ledger-keeper's mark. His eyes caught it and something behind them went very still, the way a whole house goes still when it hears a floorboard creak.

He wrapped the fish in brown paper with hands that did not shake, which was itself an answer. Men with nothing to hide wrap fish like men with nothing to hide.

"Your chalk," I said quietly. "You hold it like a pen. Like you were taught a formal hand. Dock boys don't get taught formal hands."

"Monastery school," he said. "Before it burned."

"Which monastery?"

"The one that burned." He set the wrapped fish on the counter between us and looked at me fully for the first time, and I saw the jaw of the dead king looking back at me from a face twenty-five years younger, and I understood why the portraits had been moved to a restricted gallery. Some resemblances are dangerous to leave lying about. "Look, mistress. Whatever you're selling, I'm not buying. Whatever you're asking, the answer's no. Take your fish."

"I haven't asked anything yet."

"You were going to." He wiped his hands on his apron. "People who find me are always going to ask something. It's why I keep moving."

*Keep moving.* The words dropped into place like a coin into a slot. Tamsin's marginalia, another line I'd made her read three times: *the heir walks unmarked*, and beneath it, in the same century-old hand, smaller, almost an apology: *and cannot stop walking, for the crown has marked him, and the mark does not fade.*

"You're the one they're looking for," I said. Not a question. I kept my voice under the gull-noise, under the market-noise, a private frequency between the two of us. "Not the Guild. Not the magistrates. The other one. The thing on the throne."

The change in him was not dramatic. That was the frightening part. He didn't flinch, didn't glance around, didn't do any of the things a guilty man does. He simply went quiet in a way that made the market noise seem to bend around him, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped its dockside roughness entirely, and what was underneath it was educated, precise, and cold as the ice on his fish.

"You have until I finish wrapping this order," he said, "to walk away. I'd take the offer. Everyone who's ever stayed to talk to me has ended up worse for it."

* * *

His name, in this country of the unrecorded, was Tam Voss. Fish-seller. Lodger in a room above a rope-maker's shed, paid weekly, no questions. He had been in Ashvale eight months, which meant he'd be gone within the month, because he never stayed longer. He told me none of this willingly. He told me because I sat on the upturned crate beside his stall for two hours, buying nothing, saying nothing, until the morning market wound down and he ran out of customers to hide behind, and then I told him who I was.

Not my name. I'm not a fool. I told him what I *was*—ledger-keeper, third rank, sealed archives, the gate. I watched him decide whether to believe me the way a man decides whether to believe a storm warning: reluctantly, and with his eyes on the horizon the whole time.

"The prophecy book," I said. "Confiscated a century ago. Catalogued and forgotten until I pulled it last week. It names you. It says you're alive. It says—" I hesitated, and then decided he deserved the whole weight of it at once, the way you pull a splinter: fast. "It says your father's soul was bound beneath the guildhall a hundred years ago, and that what sits on the throne wearing his face is not him. And it says the gate under the Guild opens tonight."

He wrapped the last of another customer's order in absolute silence, took her coins, smiled at her—a real smile, warm, practiced, a mask that fit him so well it was almost a face—and waited until she was gone before he spoke.

"Say the last part again."

"The gate opens tonight."

"And you came to a fish stall." He laughed, and there was no humor in it, only a kind of exhausted wonder. "This morning. With the day half gone. To find a man who has spent twenty-five years making sure no one could find him, to ask him to help stop the end of the world, and you gave yourself until—" he checked the sky, "—mid-morning."

"I had to establish the pattern first. Where you sell, how you watch, whether you'd run." I shrugged. "You didn't run. That told me more than an hour of questions."

"I didn't run because running from a woman at a fish stall is how you end up in a canal." He pulled off his apron, folded it, and set it on the counter with the same terrible precision he'd given the mackerel. "You want to know what I know about my father? I know that he died when I was four. I know that my mother wept for a week and then stopped weeping all at once, like a tap shut off, and never said his name again. I know that when I was seven, men came to our house in the dark, and my mother put me in a fish cart—fish, always fish, there's a joke in that somewhere if I ever live long enough to laugh again—and sent me south with a man I'd never seen, and I never saw her again. I know that every few years, someone finds me. A kind old priest in Vellmark who asked too many questions about my birthmark and was dead of a fever within the month. A woman in Corrin who swore she'd been my nurse and wanted to help me claim my birthright, and who I left at an inn at midnight and heard, from the next town, had been found in the river." His voice stayed level the whole way through, which was somehow worse than shouting. "I know that I am the only person in the world who benefits from my father staying dead. Because the day anyone proves he isn't—proves his soul is sitting in a cage under your guildhall, proves the thing on the throne is wearing him like a coat—that's the day every knife in Ashvale comes looking for the heir. Do you understand? You're not offering me hope. You're offering me a target painted on the only thing I have left, which is my own unremarkable life."

"I'm not asking you to claim anything."

"You're asking me to *exist*. Publicly. In a room with the gate. In a room with *Corvane*." He said the name the way sailors say the name of a reef that has sunk better ships than theirs. "You think I don't know what Corvane is? I've been running from his agents since I was seven. The Guild's reach is the whole reason I never stop moving. And you want me to walk into the guildhall."

"I want you to walk into the gate-chamber," I said. "There's a difference. Corvane's accounts show payments flowing into the gate—years of life, drawn from the debtors, drawn from trades. He's been feeding it. The prophecy says the binding can be unmade, but the ritual needs royal blood. Not royal *claim*—blood. Your actual body, standing in that chamber, speaking your father's name. That's all. After that you can go back to selling fish until the end of your quiet, unrecorded days. I'll even buy the mackerel."

He looked at me for a long moment. Behind him the gulls fought over a gutting table; the canal slid past, green and patient; the fog was burning off toward a pale, ordinary noon. Somewhere in the city above us, a bell rang the hour, and I thought about the prophecy's date circled in century-old ink, and about how many hours were left in it, and about how I had walked out of the guildhall this morning with Corvane's own key-ring jingling in my pocket and his trust resting on me like a hand I couldn't shrug off.

"Show me the entry," he said at last.

"What?"

"The binding. My father's binding. You said Corvane's accounts show the payments. I want to see my father's entry. What was paid, and by whom, and—" His voice cracked, just once, a single hairline fracture in all that precision, and he pressed his lips together until it sealed. "I want to know what my mother paid. Because she stopped weeping all at once, ledger-keeper, and I have spent twenty years telling myself that was grief. Show me the entry, and I'll know whether it was something else."

I had it. Of course I had it—I'd copied it out by hand before I left, in my own cipher, because I no longer trusted anything that stayed in the guildhall overnight. I took the folded page from inside my coat and held it out, and for a moment I didn't let go when he took it, and we stood there in the fish-smell and the gull-cry like two people passing something hot.

He read it. I watched him read it, and I watched the exact moment he reached the line item that mattered, and I will carry the sound he made for the rest of my life, however long the Guild or the gate or anything else decides that life should be. It was a small sound. A man does not scream when he learns his mother paid thirty years of her own life into a door to buy her son a clean exile. A man makes a small sound, like something breaking in another room, and then he goes very quiet, and then he folds the page along its existing creases with hands that have finally, at long last, learned to shake.

"My mother's name isn't in any record," he said, when he could speak. "They erased her. I looked, for years. I thought—I thought she'd abandoned me. I thought she'd taken the coin and started a new life somewhere." He laughed, that same airless laugh. "She paid thirty years. She'd have been—she'd have died when I was—"

"Twenty-two," I said quietly. I had done the arithmetic days ago, alone in the archive at night, and hated myself for it. "The entry's dated. She'd have died when you were twenty-two. Eleven years ago."

He stood there holding the page, and the market went on around us—the fishwives shouting their prices, the gulls screaming their hungers, the whole indifferent commerce of a city that did not know what was under it—and I watched a man finish grieving his mother eleven years late, in public, beside a gutting table, and I understood something that Tamsin had tried to tell me in the dark of the archive and that I had been too proud to hear: that this was why the prophecy needed three of us. Not for the ritual. For this. For the moments when the truth was too heavy for one pair of hands, and someone else had to stand close enough to catch the weight.

"I'll do it," he said.

I blinked. "Just like that?"

"No. Not just like that. I've been deciding since you sat down on that crate two hours ago, and I decided before you ever showed me the entry, and the entry only told me *why*." He tucked the page into his shirt, against his skin, like something that needed to be kept warm. "But I want it understood that I'm not doing this for a crown. I don't want the crown. If we unbind my father and he lives, he can have it, and I'll sell him fish at a fair price for the rest of his reign. If he dies, and the council starts looking at me with that particular hungry look councils get, I'll be on a boat before the bells finish ringing. I am not a king, mistress. I am a man whose mother was murdered by an accounting entry, and I am going to make the thing that killed her look at what it did. That's the whole of my ambition. Write that in your ledger."

"It's already written," I said, and it was true; I had written it in my head the moment I read the entry, in the private ledger I keep behind my eyes, the one no one can erase.

He closed the stall. It took him ten minutes—fish into ice, ice into the canal-chute, chalk board wiped, apron folded, coins into a belt-purse, every motion done in the order of a man who has abandoned a hundred rooms in a hundred towns and has a liturgy for leaving. Then he stood before me in his plain shirt with his sleeves rolled up and the dead king's jaw set hard, and said, "The archive. Your scribe. Where do we meet, and when, and how do we get in without Corvane's people seeing three faces instead of one?"

I told him. And as I said it—as I gave him the time, the door, the service stair, the words that would braid his life into mine and Tamsin's past all unpicking—I felt the thing I had been dreading all morning settle over me at last, cold as canal water.

I had spent ten years being safe because I was alone. Alone, I was invisible. Alone, no one could be traded for my mistakes, no one could be erased on my account, no one's mother could pay thirty years for my survival. I had told myself it was discipline. Standing in the fish-smell with the heir's eyes on me, waiting on my word, I finally admitted what it actually was. It was cowardice with a ledger's neat hand. And I had just spent my last morning of it.

We walked up from the canal together, two unrecorded people in a city of records, and the fog was burning off, and the bells of Ashvale rang out the noon hour over the rooftops—and beneath the bells, faint, from the direction of the guildhall, I heard the second bell. The one that only rings in the archive levels. The one that means a breach has been found.

Corvane knew.
