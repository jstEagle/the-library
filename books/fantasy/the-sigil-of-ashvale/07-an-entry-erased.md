## 7. An Entry Erased

The breach bell was still ringing when I came up out of the salt market fog, and by the time I reached Guild Square it had stopped—which was worse. A bell that rings and rings means panic. A bell that stops means someone has taken control of the panic.

I had perhaps an hour. Maybe less.

I went in through the clerks' door on the canal side, the one with the warped lintel that everyone else avoided because it scraped like a dying animal. Ten years of using that door had taught me its moods; tonight I used them back, letting the scrape cover the sound of my boots on the stair. The guildhall at midday was all noise and traffic—traders arguing over valuations, porters hauling crates of things that had no business existing in crates—but the archive levels were mine, and mine alone, and I moved through them the way I'd moved through them for a decade: unseen, unremarked, a smudge in the corner of everyone's attention.

That invisibility had always been my armor. Standing at the top of the archive stairs, listening to the silence below, I understood for the first time that it could also be my shroud.

* * *

Corvane's study occupied the third level, behind a door of black ashwood that opened, in theory, only to his key. In practice, ten years of ledger-keeping had taught me every lock in this building, because locks were just ledgers made of iron—they recorded who belonged where, and every record could be read, and most could be persuaded to lie.

His private ledger sat on the desk where he'd left it. That was the first wrong thing. Corvane did not leave things. Corvane kept his accounts the way a miser keeps coins: against his skin, in locked rooms, in places no honest light ever reached. A ledger abandoned on a desk was not carelessness.

It was bait.

I stood in the doorway a long moment, breathing the smell of the room—ink and tallow and something older underneath, something like wet stone, like the air that seeped up from beneath the guildhall floor. From the gate. Then I crossed to the window instead of the desk, and looked down into the courtyard, and there he was.

Master Corvane, standing in the gray noon light with three of his senior factors around him, giving quiet instructions. Even from here I could read the shape of it in their bodies—the way they kept their eyes on his collar rather than his face, the way they nodded too quickly. He sent two of them toward the archive stairs. The third he held a moment longer, and whatever he said made the man go pale and walk away fast.

Then Corvane looked up. Not at the window—at the building itself, the way a man looks at a horse he suspects has gone lame. His gaze traveled the stone face of the guildhall as though reading it, line by line, and I stepped back from the glass before his eyes could reach mine, though I knew, with cold certainty, that they hadn't needed to see me. He already knew. The breach bell had told him *where*, and the ledgers would tell him *who*, and the only question left was how long he intended to let me run before he closed the book.

I should have fled then. I want to be honest about that, even if no one will ever read this honesty. I should have gone down the service stairs, out the canal door, away from Ashvale and its bells and its hungry gate, and never looked back.

Instead I went back to the desk, because the ledger was open, and I am what I have always been: a woman who cannot leave a page unread.

* * *

The entries near the front were ordinary, or what passes for ordinary in Corvane's accounts. Payments in years—not coin, but life itself, measured and rendered in the Guild's own ciphered notation. *From Aldous Vey, factor, eleven years, condition of continued preferment.* *From Maren Sallow, widow, four years, condition of her husband's debt discharged.* Page after page of them, decades deep, and every entry bore the same counterweight notation in the margin: *to the door. to the door. to the door.*

He'd been feeding it since before I was born. Perhaps since before the binding. The sums grew larger as the pages turned, the way a debt grows when the creditor grows impatient, and the last entries—the last few months—were written in a hand that shook.

But it wasn't the payments that stopped my breath. It was what came after.

A new section, begun recently, headed in Corvane's precise hand: *Reconciliation.* And beneath it, a list of names being struck through, one after another, each strike so firm it had torn the paper.

I knew some of those names. Halveric Ost, who had traded his singing voice for his brother's pardon, twelve years ago. Bette Crowan, who sold the memory of her mother's face to pay a rent she couldn't meet. Names from my own ledgers, from my first years in the archive, entries I had made myself with a steady hand and a numb heart.

Except—

I pulled my own early ledger from memory and checked the dates, and the dates didn't sit right. I remembered recording Halveric's trade. I remembered the way he wouldn't look at me afterward. But when I reached for the details—the season, the room, the exact words of the contract—everything slid, like trying to hold water in a fist. Twelve years ago. Or fifteen? Had it been his brother, or his son?

And Bette Crowan. I had written her name a hundred times in my head over the years, a small guilt I carried like a stone. But when I tried to picture her face, there was nothing. Not a blur. Nothing. As if the space where her face should have been had never held anything at all.

The strikes through her name in Corvane's ledger seemed suddenly very loud.

I understood then, in the way you understand a thing your body understands before your mind consents to it: erasure was not a metaphor. When Corvane struck an entry, the trade didn't merely vanish from the record. It vanished from the world. The person unraveled backward through their own history until even the memory of them was a hole shaped like a person. He wasn't cleaning up evidence of my snooping.

He was deleting witnesses. Deleting debts. Deleting, possibly, people who had never existed at all except as lines in his books—and how would anyone know the difference? How would *I* know?

My hand went, without my deciding it, to the last page of the reconciliation list. There was space there. Blank ruled lines waiting for more names.

And at the bottom, in pencil, faint, provisional—a name that made the room tilt.

Not mine. Not yet.

*Tamsin Vale.*

* * *

I got out of the study with the lock none the wiser and my heart going like a debtor fleeing a collector, and I did the only thing I knew how to do with fear: I took it to the records.

The archive levels at night were a cathedral of paper, ten floors of shelving descending into the earth toward the sealed levels, toward the gate, toward everything I'd spent ten years not thinking about. I lit one lamp, shielded it with my body, and started pulling.

If Corvane could erase people, then somewhere there had to be the master record of erasure itself—the log of what had been removed. Even a man as careful as Corvane needed to know what he'd deleted, or he'd delete himself one day by accident. Deletion leaves a scar. You can sand a page smooth, but the pages beneath remember the pressure of the pen.

I found the scars in the oldest registry, the one kept in the days before ciphered notation, when the Guild still wrote plainly because it believed in itself. Dozens of entries marked *voided* in red ink. And here was the thing that turned my blood to canal water: among them, entries voided within the past week. Fresh ones. Corvane had been working fast since the bell.

I read the fresh entries twice, because the first time my eyes refused to accept them.

They weren't names of witnesses. They weren't trades at all. They were *people*. Whole lives, entered in the old plain style—births registered, apprenticeships recorded, deaths noted with dates and causes—and now each one stamped VOID across the face of it, the ink still sharp enough to bite.

A chandler named Osric Pell, born sixty-one years ago, died fifty-eight years later of a fever. Voided.

His wife, Annis. Voided.

Their daughter, born in the spring of a year I could look up but somehow couldn't bring myself to. Voided, voided, voided.

Three entire lives, erased down to the roots, and no debt attached, no trade, no payment to the door. Just—removal. Cleanup. As if Corvane were not striking out evidence of a crime but pruning a tree.

And I might never have noticed, except that erasure is a hungry thing, and hunger spreads. The void-stamps had bled. Whoever had applied them—or whatever process the ledger itself used, because I no longer believed the ledgers were entirely inert—had pressed too hard, and the red had ghosted onto the neighboring entries. Ghosted, and *taken*. The entry beside Osric Pell's birth record, an unrelated registration, had gone faint. Faded. Its ink graying out year by year, or week by week, or hour by hour, I couldn't tell. The name at its head was barely legible now, letters dissolving like sugar in rain.

I leaned close. I read it.

*Wren Joubert, admitted to the Guild archive, age sixteen, sponsor—*

The rest was gone. Not blotted, not struck. Simply absent, the way a tooth is absent from the gum, leaving you tonguing a hole that aches with the memory of what filled it.

Sponsor. Someone had sponsored me. Someone had stood in this hall sixteen years ago—I did the arithmetic and my hands went cold—and vouched for a girl with no family and no history, and that someone had been erased so thoroughly that even the word *sponsor* had rotted out of my own admission record.

I had always known I came to the Guild with nothing. No family, no origin story I could bear to tell, a childhood like a room with the furniture covered in sheets. I had told myself for ten years that the blankness was grief, or poverty, or the ordinary cruelty of being nobody.

It was none of those things. It was a struck entry. My earliest self had been deleted, and I had lived inside the deletion without ever feeling its edges, the way a fish does not feel the water.

How much of me was missing? Sixteen years old, admitted—that was real, the ink still held there. But everything before it was fog, and now I knew the fog had a cause, and the cause had a hand, and the hand held a pen that was still writing.
