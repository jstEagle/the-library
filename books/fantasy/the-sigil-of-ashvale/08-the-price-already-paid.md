## 8. The Price Already Paid

The salt market woke before the sun, as markets of the poor always did—by smell rather than light. Brine and smoke and the sweet rot of yesterday's catch that no one had sold. Emeric Vael had learned to wake with it, the way he had once learned to wake with bells and the rustle of silk against marble, though the two educations shared almost nothing else.

He gutted mackerel with a knife older than his exile and did not think about crowns. He was very good at not thinking about crowns. Ten years of practice.

"Three for a copper," he told a woman whose hands were red from the washing-tubs. "Five if you take the ugly ones too."

"The ugly ones taste the same."

"They taste better," Emeric said. "Ugly things have more salt in them. Ask any fisherman's wife."

She laughed, took five, and moved on into the grey morning. Around him the market assembled itself out of the dark: awnings snapping open like wings, braziers coughing to life, the first carts grinding in on iron-rimmed wheels. A boy of perhaps nine worked the row opposite, shouting prices in a voice still too high for the words. Emeric watched him sometimes when the crowd was thin. The boy had his mother's jaw. That was all. It meant nothing. He made himself believe it meant nothing, the way he made himself believe a great many things before breakfast.

He was wrapping the last of the herring in oiled paper when she came back.

The ledger-woman. Wren Joubert. She had come twice before, standing at the edge of his stall with her ink-stained fingers and her eyes that catalogued everything and gave away nothing, and both times she had bought nothing, said little, and left. He had marked her the way he marked everyone: by how they walked, where their eyes went, whether their hands stayed loose or drifted toward knives. Hers stayed loose. Her eyes went everywhere. And she walked like someone who had spent her life being overlooked and had turned it into an art.

"You again," he said, without looking up.

"Me again."

"If you're going to keep buying nothing, you could at least buy something small. A sardine. For appearances."

"I'll take a sardine." She counted out the copper precisely, set it on the wet boards, and then didn't leave. She stood there while he wrapped the fish, and the market flowed around them, and he felt the shape of what she hadn't said yet pressing against the air between them like weather coming off the sea.

"Whatever you're selling," Emeric said quietly, "I'm not buying. I've told you."

"You've told me you don't want a crown." She handed the sardine back to him, absurdly, so that he was holding his own fish again. "I haven't offered you one."

"Everyone who finds me eventually offers me one. It takes them longer than it took you, but they get there. First they ask if I'm really him. Then they look sorry. Then they start talking about destiny." He tossed the sardine into her basket. "You're ahead of schedule. Congratulations."

"I'm not here about destiny." Her voice dropped, low enough that only he could hear it under the market noise. "I'm here about a ledger entry dated the eleventh of Deepwinter, ninety-nine years ago. Entry four thousand one hundred and twelve. Do you know what your father paid that night, Emeric? Do you know who paid it *for* him?"

The world went narrow. The market sounds flattened into a single distant hum. He heard himself say, in a voice he didn't recognize as his own: "What are you talking about?"

* * *

They ended up in the back room behind the smokehouse, because it was the only place he knew where no one would come looking. It smelled of oak shavings and old brine and it was cold enough that their breath showed. Emeric lit the lamp himself because his hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking, and even so, the flame wavered.

Wren unwrapped a book from oilcloth. Not the prophecy-book—he'd seen that one, glimpsed it on her second visit, old and cracked and full of circles drawn around dates. This was different. This was a ledger, bound in grey leather gone soft as cloth at the corners, its pages furred at the edges with a century of handling. Guild paper. He knew Guild paper; every contract in the kingdom carried its watermark, the interlocked rings.

"This is a copy," she said. "I couldn't bring the original. Corvane would notice the weight missing from the shelf. But I copied it exactly. Every word. Every figure."

"You copied a hundred-year-old entry," Emeric said, "in your own hand, and brought it across the city to show a fishmonger."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because I thought you deserved to read it in someone's honest handwriting." She laid the book on the barrel between them and opened it to a ribbon marker. "And because I need your help, and I won't trick you into giving it. Whatever else happens, you'll have read the truth first. That matters to me."

He looked down.

The entry was written in a clerk's hand so precise it might have been engraved. He read it once quickly, the way you skim past a wound, and then forced himself to go back to the beginning and read it slowly, every line, letting each one cut.

*Entry 4112. Eleventh of Deepwinter, Year 99 of the Interregnum.*

*A binding performed at the sealed gate beneath the guildhall, witnessed and recorded per charter.*

*Subject: King Aldric Vael, called Aldric the Just. Soul-bound at his own petition.*

*Terms: The crown and body remain above. The soul remains below.*

*Duration: Until such time as a name is written into the door willing to be forgotten.*

*Price paid by the petitioner: none. Price paid by proxy: Queen Isolde Vael, twenty years of life, remitted immediately and in full.*

His father's name. His father's actual name, in ink, in a stranger's book, followed by the word *petition*. As if—

"He asked for it?" Emeric's voice came out cracked. "That's what this says. He *asked*."

"That's what it says," Wren said gently. "There's a marginal note. Read the marginal note."

Below the main text, smaller, cramped against the edge of the page as though the original clerk had been afraid to write it larger:

*Clerk's annotation: The petition was not freely given. The thing that wears the king dictated it, and the king's mouth spoke it. The queen wept through the whole of the transaction and thanked us at the end. I record this note because no one else will, and because someday someone must.*

Emeric put his hand flat on the page. He didn't remember deciding to do it. The wood grain of the barrel pressed up through the paper, through the ink, through the words, and he stared at the lamplight lying yellow across the entry and understood that he was waiting—for it to change, for the words to rearrange themselves into something survivable—and they didn't.

"She died that winter," he said. "My mother. I was six. They told me it was lung-rot. They told me—" He stopped. Started again. "They gave me a lock of her hair in a silver box. I kept it for years. I used to think the box smelled like her."

"Twenty years of life," Wren said softly. "Remitted immediately and in full. Your father's binding cost her two decades, taken that night. You were born three years after this entry."

"So I never—" He laughed, one short ugly sound with nothing in it. "I never met the woman in my memories at all. Or I did, and those years were stolen ones, and I've been grieving on credit." He pressed his palm harder against the page. "Who wrote this note? Who was honest enough to write it?"

"A clerk. There's no signature. There never is, in our house. We're trained out of signatures." Something flickered across her face—something old and tired. "I found this three nights ago, following references in the prophecy-book. I've been sitting with it ever since, trying to decide whether showing anyone was worse than showing no one."

"And you chose a fishmonger."

"I chose the heir."

"I'm not the heir. I'm the exiled prince. Those aren't the same thing, whatever the pamphleteers say." He straightened. The grief was still there—it filled him floor to rafters—but underneath it, rising like water finding its level, something colder was arriving, and he recognized it with a kind of relief. Purpose was easier than grief. Purpose had edges. "Tell me the rest. All of it. Don't arrange it for me, don't soften it. Tell me what's on the throne right now."

* * *

So she told him.

She told him about the prophecy-book and the circled date, tonight's date, and the phrase *the ledger opens*. She told him about Corvane's private accounts—not coin, never coin, but payments in years, decades, lifetimes, all flowing downward toward the gate beneath the guildhall like blood toward a wound that never closes. She told him about the woman who had sold her shadow for her son's fever, and about the debtor cells, and about the trades in impossible things that had been feeding something behind a sealed door for a century.

And she told him the part Tamsin had deciphered. The part that made the lamplight seem suddenly insufficient, that made the smokehouse walls feel close.

"The king's soul has been under the guildhall for ninety-nine years," Wren said. "Bound. Caged. Alive, in whatever way souls are alive. And the thing that sits on the throne wearing his face and his crown and his body—" she hesitated, and he saw that even now, after everything she'd uncovered, saying it plainly cost her something— "it has been ruling Ashvale since before you were born. Everything you remember of your father, every audience, every festival, the day he lifted you onto his horse in the palace yard—that was it. That was the crown walking around in him."

"No."

"Emeric—"

"*No.*" The word came out of him like something breaking. He was on his feet without remembering standing, the barrel rocking, the lamp guttering. "I remember his hands. I remember his laugh. I remember him teaching me the names of the rivers from the east balcony, pointing, *that one's the Sorrel, that one's the Weep*, and he got the Weep wrong on purpose to see if I'd correct him, and I did, and he laughed—" His voice failed. He gripped the edge of the barrel and breathed. "Are you telling me that thing laughed? Are you telling me it *learned to laugh*, wearing my father, so that I would trust it?"

"I'm telling you it wore him well enough to fool a child," Wren said. "I'm telling you it fooled a whole kingdom for a century. I don't think that makes it kind. I think it makes it hungry and patient, which is worse."

"And my exile?"

"That's why I'm really here. That, and the entry." She reached into her coat and produced a second paper, thinner, a single page. "Tamsin found this cross-referenced in the prophecy margins. The entity marked you early. Before you could be a threat. Look at the date."

He looked.

The date was eleven days after his fourth birthday. A notation in the same cramped hand as the other margin notes—or near enough, a later hand, a keeper of the same terrible honesty:

*Note appended to account 4112, ongoing: The vessel-body shows signs of resistance. The bound soul stirs when the child is near. Recommendation transmitted to the throne: remove the child from proximity. Method: engineered disgrace. Cost to be borne by whichever minister can be arranged to bear it.*

Engineered disgrace. Four words. His whole life, folded flat into four words.

The theft they'd accused him of at fifteen—the treasury seals found in his rooms, the witnesses so perfectly placed, the council's faces arranged in that terrible tableau of sorrow and certainty. The night ride to the border with two guardsmen who wouldn't meet his eyes. The ten years since. The false name. The fish. The mornings waking to brine instead of bells, telling himself he had earned this, telling himself some part of it must have been true, because the alternative was unbearable—

"It was never true," he said. His voice had gone very quiet, and very level, and somewhere in the leveling he felt the last of the boy who had ridden out of Ashvale in disgrace finally die, and felt no mourning for him at all. "There was no theft. There was no disgrace. There was a *recommendation transmitted to the throne.*" He looked up at Wren, and saw her flinch slightly at whatever was in his face, and did not care. "It exiled a four-year-old's future because my father's ghost stirred when I came into a room. Because I made the caged thing restless. That's all I was. A draft under a door."

"That's not all you were." Wren's voice was steady, but her hands weren't; he noticed them, ink-stained, gripping the ledger's cover as if it were a rail over deep water. "That's not all you are. Listen to me. The prophecy needs three things: a reader, a key, and a name. Tamsin reads. I hold the key—I've held the gate records for ten years. But the unbinding requires the king's true name spoken by blood. Spoken by *you*. No one else in the world can do it. That's why I'm standing in a smokehouse instead of running. That's why I brought you the truth instead of a trick."

"And if I say no? If I gut my fish and live my life and let the date pass?"

"Then tomorrow the gate opens anyway, and Corvane feeds it the debtor cells to buy another year, and the year after that he empties the streets, and eventually there won't be a city worth ruling and the thing on the throne will wear your father's face in an empty kingdom until the sun burns out." She said it flatly, without theater. That was somehow the worst part. She said it like a ledger entry. "Or you come with me, and we try to free him. Knowing what it costs. Knowing the vessel dies either way."

The vessel dies either way.

He made himself hold that sentence. His father's body—worn for a century by something hungry, something patient, something that had stolen his mother's twenty years and his own name and his whole remembered childhood—could not be saved. Freeing the soul killed the shell. There was no version of this where he got his father back. There was only a version where his father finally, at long last, got to die.

"I was six years old at my mother's funeral," Emeric said slowly. "They wouldn't let me see her. My uncle said it was because of the lung-rot, that seeing would frighten me. I stood in the crypt afterward, alone—they let me have one minute alone, gods know why—and I promised her I'd find out the truth of everything, someday, when I was big enough. I'd forgotten that promise. Ten years of fish will do that." He closed the ledger gently, the way you close a coffin. "I'm big enough now."

"Emeric—"

"Here is what I am, Wren Joubert, so we're clear with each other, because you gave me the truth and I'll pay in kind. I am not doing this for a crown. If the throne survives what's coming, burn it for all I care. I am not doing this for justice, because justice is a word ministers use while they arrange witnesses. I am doing this because a clerk a hundred years dead was honest enough to write a margin note, and because my mother's twenty years are *owed*, and because somewhere under that guildhall my father has been screaming into a locked door since before I could talk." He picked up the lamp. His hand was steady now. It would stay steady. He would make it stay steady. "Grief is a debt like anything else in your books. And I intend to collect."

For a long moment Wren just looked at him, and something passed over her face that he would spend years trying to name—a recognition, maybe, of a debt of her own.

"Tamsin is waiting at the sign of the Drowned Bell," she said. "We move tonight. Before Corvane realizes what tonight is." She paused at the door, the cold air knifing in. "Bring warm clothes. And whatever you're good at besides fish."

"Being underestimated," Emeric said, and snuffed the lamp.

Outside, the salt market roared on in the dark, unaware, and the first snow of the season had begun to fall on Ashvale—fine and dry and silent, falling on the palace where a dead king's body sat a throne it had not chosen, falling on the guildhall where a ledger waited with tonight's date already written in a century-old hand.

Falling, Emeric noticed, like ash.

He pulled his hood up and followed Wren into it.
