## 10. The Guild Turns Its Eye

The summons came at dawn, folded inside a routine requisition for ink.

*All senior clerks to the Great Hall. Attendance is not optional.*

Wren read it three times, her thumb pressed flat against the paper as if she could hold the words still. In ten years she had never once been summoned to the Great Hall. Senior clerks worked in the counting rooms and the archive galleries; they did not gather. The Guild did not gather. The Guild was a thousand hands moving in a thousand small rooms, each one ignorant of the others, and that ignorance was the mortar that held the whole edifice together.

She dressed carefully. Grey wool, plain collar, ledger-keeper's chain worn visible at her throat—the small brass token stamped with her number, 114, because names were currency here and you did not wear yours where it could be stolen. She checked her appearance twice in the spotted mirror above her basin. She looked like exactly what she was supposed to look like: tired, obedient, unremarkable.

*You accessed his ledger,* said the voice in the back of her skull, the one that had not stopped talking since the night in Corvane's private accounts. *He knows. He has to know. And now he is going to do something about it, and you are going to stand in a crowd of clerks and watch him do it.*

She had considered running. She had stood at her window in the grey hour before dawn, looking down at the cobbled lane below, calculating distances to the salt gate, to the river stairs, to the tannery district where no Guild man would follow without a writ. She had gotten as far as imagining the cold air on her face.

Then she thought of Tamsin, locked two floors beneath her feet. She thought of the prophecy-book hidden inside the false spine of her mattress, its century-old ink naming tonight's date in letters gone brown as dried blood. She thought of Emeric, who smelled of fish and salt and stubbornness, waiting in a market stall for word that might never come.

Running solved nothing. Running only meant she wouldn't be there when the end arrived—and the end was coming regardless. Tonight's date did not care whether Wren Joubert watched it happen from Ashvale or from some ditch outside the walls.

So she buttoned her collar and went down to be invisible one more time.

* * *

The Great Hall of the Ashvale Guild had been built by men who wanted money to feel like a religion. Vaulted ceilings ribbed in black oak. Columns carved with scales, coins, keys, doors—all the iconography of trade rendered with the solemnity of saints. Light fell through high windows in long amber shafts, and in those shafts dust drifted like slow golden snow.

Two hundred clerks stood in ordered rows on the marble floor, arranged by rank and department exactly as entries were arranged in a ledger. Wren found her place among the record-keepers, third row, and fixed her eyes on the back of the head in front of her—a broad, balding skull belonging to Hemm, an old inventory clerk who snored through every audit.

At the far end of the hall, on a dais beneath the carved seal of the Guild—a door with a keyhole and no key—stood Master Corvane.

He looked, Wren thought, terrible. Not weak. Never weak. But there was a new quality to him, a brittleness behind the composure, like fine porcelain with a hairline crack running through it that only showed when the light caught at the right angle. He wore his silver-grey coat immaculately. His hands were folded before him. His face wore the expression of a man doing arithmetic.

Beside him, flanked by two enforcers in the Guild's dark livery, stood a young man.

Wren knew him. Of course she knew him—she had processed his intake papers six years ago, a boy of seventeen with ink-stained fingers and a stammer he tried to hide by speaking very fast. Pell Ostrewey. Junior reconciliation clerk, second gallery. He sorted misfiled entries and fetched tea and once, memorably, had spent an entire afternoon helping Wren hunt down a duplicated debt-entry that turned out to be a moth crushed flat against the page, its wings reading like a smudge of figures. He had laughed about it afterward, that quick nervous laugh of his, and bought her a honey-bun from the cart on Copper Lane.

Now he stood on the dais with his wrists bound in front of him, and his eyes were already red-rimmed, and he was looking out across the assembled clerks as though searching for one face among them. A face that might save him.

Wren kept her gaze forward and her breathing even and did not let herself be that face.

"Brothers and sisters of the ledger," Corvane began, and his voice carried through the vaulted space with practiced ease, warm as a hearth, reasonable as interest accruing. "For a hundred years this Guild has kept faith with Ashvale. We have held the city's debts and its credits, its fortunes and its failures, in perfect balance. Balance is our covenant. Balance is why Ashvale eats while other cities starve, why our streets are lit while theirs go dark."

A pause. His eyes moved along the rows.

"Three nights ago," he said, "someone breached the private accounts."

The silence that followed had weight. Two hundred clerks not breathing, all at once.

"The private accounts are the heart of this institution," Corvane continued. "They record obligations of the most sensitive kind—arrangements made between the Guild and persons whose trust we are bound to protect. What was taken—or rather, what was *seen*—could unravel arrangements upon which the safety of this entire city depends." He unfolded his hands, folded them again. "I will not pretend the breach was sophisticated. It was clumsy. It left traces. And those traces led us here."

He turned to Pell.

"Pell Ostrewey. Junior reconciliation clerk. In whose gallery the traces terminate. In whose drawer was found a copied fragment of a restricted account. In whose hand—" Corvane lifted a piece of paper between two fingers, and even at a distance Wren recognized the cramped, hurried script, the way Pell crossed his sevens, "—the copy was made."

"That's not—" Pell's voice cracked, pitched too high. "Master, I copied it because I found it, I didn't know what it was, it was *misfiled*, it came up in a routine sort and I thought—I thought someone had made an error, I was going to report it, I swear it on my entry—"

"You accessed a restricted account," Corvane said, gently, the way one corrects a child's arithmetic. "Without authorization. Without reporting it. You concealed the copy in your personal effects." He shook his head, sorrowful. "Intentions are not entries, Pell. Only acts are entries. You know this. We taught it to you."

"I didn't conceal anything, I was going to—"

"*Silence.*"

It was not loud. That was what made it terrible. The word came out of Corvane at conversational volume, and yet it crossed the hall like a blade laid flat against every throat, and Pell stopped as if a hand had closed around his windpipe. The enforcers shifted. Somewhere in the upper galleries, a door closed softly.

Wren's heart was slamming against her ribs so hard she feared it might be audible.

*He knows it wasn't Pell.* The certainty settled over her like frost. *The traces don't lead to a junior clerk. They lead to a master record-keeper with access to the sealed stacks. Maybe they lead nowhere conclusive at all—but they can't lead to me, so they lead to whoever is easiest to feed to the thing he actually fears.* Her mind flashed, unbidden, to the ledger entries she'd found in Corvane's private accounts: payments flowing toward the gate, years of life measured out in neat columns. Corvane understood feeding. It was the only language he had left.

"This breach cannot go unanswered," Corvane said to the assembly. "Not because I am cruel. Because the alternative—the *merciful* alternative—is to teach two hundred clerks that the private accounts may be opened without consequence, and then to stand by while the next curious hand opens something that should never be opened, and the thing behind that something comes through into your streets." He let that sit. Let the word *thing* do its quiet work. "Mercy, applied at the wrong moment, is how cities die. I have seen the arithmetic. I am asking you to trust it."

He nodded to the enforcers.

Pell made a sound then that Wren would carry for the rest of her life, however many or few days that proved to be. Not a scream. Something smaller than a scream—the sound a person makes when they understand, finally and completely, that no one is coming. He twisted toward the assembled clerks, toward the faces he had worked beside for six years, and his mouth formed words: *help*, or *please*, or perhaps just a name.

No one moved. Hemm's bald head did not turn. The amber light lay in its long bars across the marble floor, indifferent as sunlight falling on a battlefield.

Wren stood in the third row with her hands at her sides and her face arranged into appropriate solemnity and her nails biting four crescents into her palms hard enough to draw blood, and she did not move either.

*Say something,* screamed the part of her that was still human. *Stand up. Confess. Give them the truth: it was me, it was me, he's innocent—*

And the rest of her, the part ten years of invisibility had built, answered with cold clarity: *And then?* Then Corvane would have two traitors instead of one, and Tamsin would rot in her cell forever, and the prophecy would arrive tonight to find its named instruments scattered or dead, and the gate would open anyway, and Pell would still be fed through it, because Corvane needed a demonstration more than he needed justice. Her confession would buy nothing except company on the way down.

That was the arithmetic. She hated it. It was Corvane's arithmetic, learned in his school, and she was using it to keep her hands at her sides.

*This is what complicity feels like,* she thought. *It doesn't feel like evil. It feels like being sensible.*

The enforcers walked Pell off the dais, down the length of the hall, past the ranks of clerks who studied their own shoes with tremendous diligence. As he passed the third row, close enough that Wren could smell the fear-sweat on him, could see the ink stains still on his fingers after everything, his searching eyes swept across the record-keepers' faces—

— and caught hers.

One heartbeat. Half of one. Recognition flickered in his face—*you, you helped me find the moth, you'll say something, anyone, please*—and Wren gave him nothing. Not a blink. Not a breath. Ten years of practice compressed into half a second: the perfect blank surface of the invisible woman.

His face changed. It was worse than despair. It was understanding. He had learned, in that half-second, the same lesson she was learning: what the Guild's silence cost, and who paid it.

Then they took him through the doors beneath the seal, and the doors closed, and Corvane resumed speaking as smoothly as if a hymn had merely paused between verses.

"Let this be entered in the day-ledger," he said. "Let it be remembered. The Guild protects Ashvale. The Guild asks, in return, only obedience." He smiled then, brief and warm and horrible. "Return to your work, brothers and sisters. Balance must be maintained."

* * *

Wren did not return to her work.

She walked the corridors on legs that felt borrowed, past counting rooms where quills scratched and clerks murmured figures to one another, the ordinary machinery of the Guild grinding on as though a boy had not just been marched beneath the building to be unmade. She passed the tea alcove where Pell used to hide the good leaves from the inventory counts. She passed the stairwell down which they had both carried a mislabeled crate of shadow-contracts, laughing about the client who'd written his own name wrong.

Her body moved. Her mind kept replaying the half-second. *Anyone. Please.*

In the privacy of the archive gallery—her territory, her kingdom of dust and shelving—she stopped, gripped the edge of a shelf until her knuckles went white, and let herself shake. Silently. Efficiently. Even her grief had learned to keep the accounts balanced.

Because here was the truth she could no longer outrun: her cover story had a price, and the price had a face now. Every day she spent unseen, unremarked, safely anonymous, was a day purchased with someone else's visibility. Pell had died—not died, but been *erased*, which was worse—because Wren Joubert had chosen to survive quietly one more day. And she would choose it again tomorrow. She knew she would. Knowing it changed nothing. That was the horror of it. Virtue, she understood now, was easy when the bill hadn't arrived. The bill had arrived, and she had paid it with a boy's life and kept the change.

"No more," she whispered to the empty gallery, to the shelves, to the ledger-god the Guild secretly worshipped. "Whatever it costs. No more."

The words felt true and useless at once. Words were entries. Only acts were entries. Corvane had taught her that much this morning, in front of everyone.

She wiped her face, straightened her collar, and went down.

Down past the reconciliation galleries, past the debtor cells where the poor waited in the dark to be valued, down to the deepest level where the cells had no numbers on their doors—only sigils, old ones, older than the Guild's charter. Down to where a disgraced scribe sat in the dark with a century of forbidden text folded up inside her memory like a knife.

Tamsin looked up when the lamp-light reached her cell. Her eyes went from Wren's face to Wren's hands, which were still trembling, and something in her expression sharpened.

"It's started," Tamsin said. It wasn't a question.

"They took a clerk this morning. Pell. They're saying he breached the private accounts." Wren's voice came out flat, scraped clean. "They're trading him through the gate tonight. As punishment. As a *lesson*."

Tamsin was silent for a moment. Then: "And you watched."

"And I watched."

"Good," Tamsin said, and the word was brutal and kind at once. "Watch everything. Remember everything. That's what you're for, ledger-keeper. You're the memory this place never wanted." She rose, and the lamplight caught the old scars on her wrists where the Guild had once tried to burn the prophecies out of her, letter by letter. "Which means you know we have less time than we thought. Corvane doesn't punish unless he's afraid. And a frightened Corvane, on the night the prophecy named—"

"—won't wait for the date to come to him," Wren finished. "He'll move early. He'll start emptying the debtor cells. He'll feed it everything he has if he thinks it's slipping away from him."

Tamsin nodded slowly. "Then we need the heir tonight. Not tomorrow. Tonight." She stepped closer to the bars, and her voice dropped. "There's one more thing in the prophecy I haven't told you. About what the gate does with what it's given. About how the king's binding can be undone—if we move fast enough, and if we're willing to pay for it in the only coin it accepts."

"What coin?"

Tamsin smiled, and there was no comfort in it. "Names," she said. "Come closer, ledger-keeper. It's time you heard what your name is worth."

Above them, muffled through fathoms of stone, the Guild bell rang the noon hour—and rang it wrong, one stroke too many, a sound Wren had never heard in ten years of service. Old clerks said the extra stroke sounded only when the Guild itself was counting down to something.

Somewhere far below, behind a door with no key, something began—very softly—to laugh.
