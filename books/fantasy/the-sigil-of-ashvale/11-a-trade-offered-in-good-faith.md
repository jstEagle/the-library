## 11. A Trade Offered in Good Faith

Corvane had always believed that the difference between a merchant and a monster was arithmetic.

A monster took. A merchant traded. And a trade, properly conducted, left both parties better than they had been—this was the first law of the Guild, older than its charter, older than the guildhall itself, carved into the lintel above the counting floor in letters worn nearly smooth by two hundred years of hands passing beneath them: *What is given freely is never stolen.*

He repeated it to himself now, standing at the window of his private office with a cup of cold tea going bitter in his hand, and watched the lamplighters work their way down Copperlane Row. One by one the little flames bloomed in the blue dusk. Somewhere below, a fishwife was arguing about herring. Somewhere beyond that, past the salt market and the tanners' yards and the tenement warrens where the city kept the people it preferred not to look at, a door waited under six feet of stone, and behind that door something patient was waiting for him to keep his accounts current.

One more year. That was all he needed. The prophecy's deadline was close—he'd known the date as long as Wren had, longer; he had commissioned the book that Tamsin Vael had transcribed, a century ago, when his hands were young enough to hold a pen without trembling—but deadlines were only prices with calendars attached. Everything had a price. He had spent ninety-seven years learning what this one cost, and he could pay it. He would pay it. He simply required one more year of solvency.

The tea had gone entirely cold. He drank it anyway, because waste offended him even now, especially now, and crossed to his desk.

The evidence lay before him in three neat piles, because Corvane could not think in disorder. In the first pile: the access log from the deep archive, showing a ledger-keeper's key used on four consecutive nights in the restricted stacks. In the second: the copy of a page from his private accounts, reproduced in a clerk's quick, precise hand—the hand of someone who copied figures for a living, who wrote numbers the way other people breathed. In the third, smallest pile: a single hair, grey-streaked brown, found caught in the lock of his ledger case.

Wren Joubert.

He had suspected her for eleven days, and known for three, and had spent those three days doing something far harder than accusation: he had been listening. He had walked the counting floor at odd hours and watched her work. He had stood behind her while she entered a trade of three memories against a winter's coal, and her pen had not shaken. He had watched her check the gate-seal at midnight, as she did every night, her little ritual, her candle and her chalk mark and her whispered count—and he had seen, in the set of her shoulders, not greed, not ambition, not any of the appetites that made clerks useful or dangerous.

He had seen fear. Carefully managed, beautifully contained fear, but fear nonetheless. She had looked at the door the way a woman looks at a debt she knows she cannot pay.

Good, Corvane had thought. Fear is the beginning of every honest negotiation.

* * *

He sent for her at the ninth bell of evening, after the counting floor had emptied, through the internal post—a folded slip carried by a junior clerk who did not read what he carried because reading what one carried was the fastest way to stop carrying anything at all.

*Ledger-keeper Joubert. My office. Tonight.*

He arranged the room himself. This mattered. Negotiations were won in rooms before anyone spoke. He moved the lamp so that it lit the guest's chair fully and left his own face half in shadow—not to intimidate, he had learned long ago that intimidation produced lies, and lies were worthless currency, but so that she would feel observed without being able to observe him back. He laid out no documents. Accusations were crude. Instead he opened the good decanter—the Ashvalan red, the vintage from the year the old king died, though she would not know why that year mattered—and set out two glasses.

Then he sat, folded his hands, and waited.

She came at the ninth-and-a-half bell, exactly as he predicted. Punctual. Of course punctual. He had read ten years of her time-slips; the woman was never early, never late, never sick, never absent. Ten years of perfect attendance, and in ten years he had spoken to her perhaps forty times, and remembered none of them. That had been the point of her. It occurred to him now, with a small cold discomfort he declined to examine, that invisibility was a skill, and skills were cultivated, and he had never once wondered *why* she had cultivated hers.

"Master Corvane." She stood just inside the doorway, hands clasped, spine straight. Her eyes went to the wine and then away from it, deliberately, the way one looks away from a trap. "You wanted me."

"I wanted to thank you," Corvane said.

Something flickered across her face—there and gone, fast, but he caught it. Good. Let her wonder.

"Ten years," he said, rising, pouring the wine whether she wanted it or not, because refusing poured wine required a boldness he doubted she'd spend tonight. "Ten years, and I have never thanked you. Not once. Do you know why?"

"No, sir."

"Because thanking a servant acknowledges that she sees the machinery. And a servant who sees the machinery starts to have opinions about it." He handed her the glass. Their fingers did not touch; she took the stem by its base with a precision that told him she had noticed everything about the gesture. "But tonight I find myself wanting to thank you anyway. So sit down, Wren Joubert, and let us be, for one hour, two people who see the machinery."

She sat. She did not drink. She set the glass on her knee and held it there like a small amber shield.

"I don't know what you mean, sir."

"No," Corvane said pleasantly. "You wouldn't. You're very good at not knowing things. It's your finest quality. You don't know how a copy of my private accounts came to be made in a ledger-keeper's hand. You don't know how your key opened the deep archive on the fourth, fifth, sixth, and seventh nights of this month. You don't know whose hair this is—" he lifted it delicately between finger and thumb, let it fall back to the desk— "though I'll confess the last one may be unfair. Hair is treacherous evidence. It goes everywhere. It once convicted an innocent man in Vellmark, or so I'm told." He smiled. "I looked into you, by the way. Forgive me. I look into everyone. And do you know what I found? Nothing. Ten years of service and *nothing*. No debts, no vices, no family, no lovers, no letters, no history worth the name. You arrived at my gate with a reference letter from a house that burned down two years later, and you became the most reliable clerk I have ever employed, and I never asked myself the only question that matters."

Wren's face was very still. Under the stillness, he could see the effort of the stillness, like water straining against a pane of ice.

"Which question is that, sir?"

"*Who erases themselves so thoroughly, and why?*" Corvane leaned back. "You see, ordinary people want to be seen. They want wages raised, names praised, grievances heard. They make noise. But you—you have spent a decade making yourself a blank page. And I have spent rather longer than a decade learning what blank pages are *for*." He tapped the desk. "They are for writing on."

For a long moment the only sound was the settling of the fire.

"You could report me," Wren said at last. Her voice was level. Impressive, actually; he noted it with professional admiration. "To the assessors. Breaking the master's seal is a hanging matter."

"I could. And then the assessors would come, and ask questions, and pull threads, and within a month they would be standing in front of a certain door asking *it* questions, and I would be dead, and you would be dead, and worse—" he let the pause stretch— "the payments would stop. Do you understand what happens if the payments stop, Wren? Have you read enough of my accounts to know?"

Her jaw tightened. There it was. She had read them. She knew.

"Tell me what you think happens," he said softly.

"I think," Wren said, "that you've been feeding a person to a door for thirty years and calling it bookkeeping."

"A *person*." Corvane laughed—genuinely, briefly, before he could catch it. "No. No, and this is precisely why I wanted this conversation, because you've read the numbers without understanding what they *are*. Sit. Listen. I am going to tell you the truth, which is a thing I do perhaps twice a century, so attend to it."

He rose and went to the window. Below, Copperlane had gone quiet; the lamps made islands of gold in the dark, and between them the shadows pooled like ink awaiting a pen.

"When I was a young man," he said, "I was not the master of this Guild. I was its second accountant. There was a man above me—Master Aldery—and above Aldery there was the king, and above the king there was peace, and I believed, as you believe, that the world ran on rules. Then Aldery showed me the door." He turned. "And he showed me the ledgers that came before him, and the ones before *those*, and I learned that the Guild has exactly one client, one creditor, one counterparty, and that every trade we have ever recorded—every shadow, every memory, every year of life, every name—flows in the end to the same account. And I learned what happens when the account runs dry."

"And what happens?" Wren asked. Her voice had gone quieter.

"The door opens," Corvane said simply. "Not metaphorically. I have seen the preliminary drafts. Aldery saw the real thing, once, in the year of the Sallow Fever, when the plague gutted the debtor cells and there were too few bodies to feed it and it *tasted the hinges*. Eleven days the city shook. Eleven days of dreams—everyone in Ashvale dreaming the same dream, a corridor with no end and a voice at the bottom of it saying *mine, mine, mine*. Aldery restored the payments and it closed. And Aldery grew old, as I will not grow old, and passed the accounts to me, and I have kept them current ever since. Ninety-seven years, Wren. I have fed it slowly, carefully, in measured installments—the condemned, the dying, volunteers who sold their remaining decades at fair market rate. Never a child. Never an innocent. I have kept the price as low as it can be kept. And in exchange, Ashvale stands. Two hundred thousand souls go about their stupid, beautiful, ordinary lives, arguing about herring, lighting their lamps, and not one of them knows they are alive *on credit*." He spread his hands. "So. Tell me. Who is the monster in this transaction?"

Wren looked down at the wine in her lap. When she raised her head, her eyes were bright and hard and he thought, with a strange lurch he did not care for: *she is almost with me. She wants me to be wrong and cannot prove it.*

"The woman," Wren said. "Last month. She sold her shadow for her son's fever. You signed that entry."

"She signed it. Freely. First law—"

"—is *what is given freely is never stolen*, yes, I know the lintel." Something moved in her voice, some banked heat. "Did you watch her leave, Master Corvane? Did you see her stand in the courtyard trying to remember which way the sun comes up? Because I did. I record the trades. I write down what people sell. I am the one who makes it *real*, so don't lecture me about the first law. I know the first law better than anyone alive." She stopped. Breathed. And he saw her decide, consciously, to put the anger away, folding it up like a document returned to its drawer, and he admired that too, God help him, admired it and feared it. "Say your piece," she said. "You didn't summon me here to confess. What do you want?"

Straight to it. Good.

"What I want," Corvane said, "is a partner."

* * *

He laid it out the way he laid out all his best trades: plainly, in order, each term following from the last like a proof.

"The deadline in that book you read—it's real. I've known the date since before your grandmother was born. In nineteen days, the account must be paid in full or the door opens wide, and no installment plan covers what it takes then. I can pay it. Barely. I have been arranging it for a decade—the reserves, the contracts, the volunteers bought and stockpiled at ruinous expense. But paying it will cost me nearly everything I have accumulated, and I will need to rebuild, immediately, from nothing, alone, with an entity behind that door that now knows my scent and my schedule and my limits." He stepped closer. "Unless."

"Unless," Wren repeated flatly.

"Unless the ledger-keeper becomes the co-signer. Two names on the master accounts. Two hands feeding it, watching it, balancing it. You think I haven't been drowning under these books for thirty years? You think I haven't dreamed of someone who reads them the way you read them—who found in three nights what three generations of assessors never found? You are the best mind this Guild has produced in a century, and I have wasted you recording coal trades because I didn't know what you were." He let the sincerity come through—not performed, never performed, that was the secret of his life, he meant every word, that was what made him dangerous. "I offer you permanence, Wren. The same arrangement I have. Years added, not subtracted. Your name written into the master roll in indelible ink—do you understand what that means? *No one can erase you.* Not the Guild, not the assessors, not whatever power it is you've been hiding from since before you came to my gate. Whatever blankness you built your life out of—I will make you permanent. Unremovable. Real, in the deepest sense this world offers. All I ask is that you help me save this city, slowly, carefully, humanely—one honest installment at a time—instead of tearing the whole arrangement down and killing us all."

He stopped. The fire ticked. Down in the dark of the city, somewhere, a dog barked twice and gave up.

"And if I refuse?" Wren said.

Corvane sighed, and for a moment let himself be tired, let the weight show, because she deserved to see it—she of all people deserved to know what the accounts cost the man who kept them.

"If you refuse, I will erase you," he said gently. "Not kill. Erase. Every entry in your hand re-attributed. Your wage records dissolved. Your room reassigned. Your name struck from the rolls until there is no Wren Joubert for anyone to hang, and no crime for anyone to remember. I have done it before. It is not cruel. It is, in its way, a mercy—you'll simply wake elsewhere as someone else, owing nothing, hunted by nothing. But you'll also lose this." He touched the desk, the ledgers, the whole invisible architecture of the Guild. "And I think—forgive me, I have watched you for ten years—I think you would rather die than lose this. The order of it. The truth of it. You're like me, Wren Joubert. We are the only two people in this building who believe the records should be true."

"That's not true," she said quietly.

"No?"

"No. Because if it were, you'd have told the city what it's living on credit *for*." She stood, setting the untouched wine on the desk with a soft click, precise as a signature. "You've decided for two hundred thousand people that they don't get to know what they're paying. That's not keeping accounts, Master Corvane. That's forging them. And I—" her voice caught, barely, a flaw in the metal— "I have given my whole life to not being a forged line."

She reached the door.

"Wren." He said her name and she paused, hand on the latch, and he saw her shoulders brace. "Nineteen days. Think about what I've offered. And think about this, too: whoever you were running from, whatever blankness you bought and paid for—you should ask yourself who taught you that disappearing was safety. Someone erased you long before I ever could. I'm merely offering to undo it." He smiled, sadly, honestly. "That is a trade offered in good faith. It stays open until the deadline. Both of them."

The latch clicked. She was gone. Her footsteps faded down the stair, steady, unhurried—she would not run, of course she would not run, running was noise—and Corvane stood alone in the lamplight among his neat piles of evidence and felt the thing he always felt after speaking truth aloud: clean, and hollow, and afraid.

She would refuse. He had watched her face. She would refuse, and she would go to the scribe and the boy, and in nineteen days there would be either a miracle or an ending.

He poured her untouched wine back into the decanter. Waste offended him.

And far below, under six feet of stone, something that wore a dead king's patience turned over in its sleep, and dreamed, for the first time in a century, of a woman with a ledger and a pen—and woke hungry, and began, very quietly, to practice saying her name.
