## 13. What the Shadow Remembers

Tamsin had spent four years in a cell learning that memory was the only currency no one could confiscate. Now, walking the crooked lanes of Saltrow with Wren and Emeric three paces behind her, she was about to test how far that currency would spend.

The woman's name was Maret Vosse. Wren had found it in an entry dated ten years back — *one shadow, whole and unworn, traded for one life, male child, age six. Paid in full. No remainder.* Tamsin had read the line three times before understanding what it meant: somewhere in Ashvale there was a woman who had given away her own shadow to buy her son's fever down, and somewhere in the dark beneath the guildhall, something was wearing it.

Or keeping it. That was the distinction that mattered.

"The gate doesn't eat," Tamsin said, for the fourth time since they'd left the tunnels. She couldn't stop turning it over. It kept rearranging itself into shapes she didn't trust because they were too hopeful. "It stores. Every trade in those ledgers flows one direction — toward the door. Nothing comes out. Corvane thinks he's paying tribute. He's actually making deposits."

Emeric pulled his fishmonger's cap lower as a pair of Guild runners passed, their ledger-chains clinking at their belts. "Deposits of what?"

"Of everything. Years of life. Names. Shadows." She glanced back at him. "You don't burn an archive, Your Highness. You build one."

"Don't call me that here."

"Then give me another word for a man whose father's soul is locked behind a door in the cellar of the world."

He didn't answer. He'd been not answering for two days, ever since Wren had shown him the binding entry and he'd gone very quiet in the way that frightened Tamsin more than shouting would have.

Saltrow smelled of brine and coal smoke and the particular sourness of fish left too long on ice. The lane narrowed. Laundry lines sagged overhead like rigging, strung with shirts gone grey from salt air. And there, at the corner where the street bent toward the water, a woman sat mending nets with hands that moved without looking at them.

Maret Vosse was perhaps forty, thin in the way of people who gave their food to children first. Her son — tall now, sixteen, hauling a barrel of herring up from a skiff — moved through the morning light beside her, and Tamsin saw it immediately, the way you see a missing tooth in a familiar smile.

The boy cast no shadow.

The sun was high and hard off the harbor, and everyone else on the dock stood in pools of darkness sharp enough to cut paper with. The boy walked through light that fell straight through him and struck the boards unbroken. Nobody on the dock seemed to notice. That was the horror of it — ten years, and the whole street had learned not to see.

But Maret noticed. Tamsin watched her eyes track her son across the bright planks, watch the small flinch when his heel came down inside where his shadow should have been, and Tamsin understood that this woman had been flinching for a decade and had never once stopped.

"Mistress Vosse," Wren said quietly, and the woman's head came up like a bird dog's.

"I've paid," Maret said. Her voice was flat, worn smooth by repetition. "Whatever it is, I've paid it. My entry's closed. I have the receipt."

"You do," Wren said. She set the ledger page down on the net-mending stool between them — the copied line, *paid in full, no remainder*. "That's why we're here. Full means nothing was kept back. But we think something was kept anyway. Not taken. Kept."

Maret looked at the page a long time. Then at her son, laughing at something across the dock, walking through sunlight like a hole cut in the world. When she spoke again her voice had dropped to almost nothing.

"Come inside. Not in front of Tam."

* * *

The room above the bait shop was clean and poor and smelled of tar. Maret lit no lamp even though the shutters made it dim; Tamsin wondered if that was habit or superstition, then realized it was neither. It was shame. Ten years of arranging your life so that your own absence of shadow mattered less.

"They told me it was painless," Maret said. She sat with her hands folded tight enough to whiten the knuckles. "The Guild clerk who came. He said a shadow weighs nothing, costs nothing, that half the city would trade theirs tomorrow if they understood the price of medicine. He said my boy would live and I'd feel lighter. Lighter." A sound that wasn't quite a laugh. "I did feel lighter. That was the worst part. For three days after, I felt wonderful. Like something had been peeled off me that I hadn't known was heavy."

"And then?" Emeric asked. He'd wedged himself into the corner, big man in a little room, and he kept his voice low and careful, the voice of someone asking questions in a house where grief lived.

"Then I started dreaming wrong." Her eyes fixed on the middle distance, on something none of them could see. "Not nightmares. Dreams of places. A corridor under the guildhall, older than the guildhall, stone that isn't stone. A door. And every night the dreams got longer, like I was being shown more of it each time, like a tour. Like someone was giving me a tour." Her knuckles went white. "I told the Guild. I wrote to them, I went to the counting house, I begged a clerk to write it down. And he did write it down — I watched him. And two nights later the dreams stopped. Completely. And I thought, thank the saints, whatever it was has passed."

She looked up at them, and her face was terrible with the thing she'd understood too late.

"It wasn't passing. It was filing. They wrote my dreams into a ledger, and once the ledger had them, the thing behind the door had them, and it didn't need to show me anymore. It keeps what it's fed. It keeps everything it's fed. My shadow isn't just — a shape. It's ten years of me. Everything my shadow saw while it was attached to me. Every night I slept beside my son's cradle. Every debt collector at the door. Every—" Her breath hitched. "Everything. It has all of it. Filed away in the dark under the city, next to everyone else's."

Tamsin felt the idea land in her chest like a dropped coin — cold, ringing, enormous.

An archive. The entity had spent a century eating trades, and every trade was a piece of a life, and pieces assemble. She thought of the prophecy-book, of the careful century-old hand that had circled tonight's date, of the scribe who had written *the ledger opens* and died knowing what opened with it. The gate didn't hunger the way beasts hungered. It collected the way archivists collected — patiently, completely, with an eye toward a future use.

And if it kept everything its meals had seen—

"Mistress Vosse," Tamsin said slowly, "your shadow was attached to you for thirty years before you sold it. Where were you, thirty years ago? What did it see?"

Maret frowned. "What does that matter? I was nobody. Fisherman's daughter, then a fisherman's wife. We weren't rich, we weren't—"

"Where were you?"

"Ashvale. Here, mostly. But—" She stopped. Something moved behind her face, some long-shelved memory climbing down off its shelf. "My mother worked at the guildhall. Before I married. Laundress, in the old wing, the closed wing. I used to go with her sometimes, when I was small, when they still let servants bring children through the west corridors. There were doors down there that even she wouldn't touch. Doors that were warm."

"Doors that were warm," Tamsin repeated. Her mouth had gone dry.

"And once — I couldn't have been more than eight — I saw something I shouldn't have. In the old wing. My mother caught my arm and dragged me out and told me if I ever spoke of it the Guild would write me out, and I didn't know what that meant but I knew it was worse than dying, so I never spoke of it." Maret's hands had come apart; they trembled now in her lap. "Why are you asking me this? Why does any of it matter? I'm nobody. I sold the only interesting thing I ever owned."

"No," Tamsin said. "You sold the most interesting thing anyone in this city owns. You just don't know it yet."

Wren turned to look at her, and Tamsin saw the question forming — the same question Wren always asked, the ledger-keeper's question, *what is the entry, exactly* — and Tamsin answered it before it was spoken.

"The entity keeps what it eats. Whole. Unspoiled. It doesn't consume memories, it *files* them — Maret's dreams proved that, the way they stopped once the Guild recorded them. Which means everything it has ever been fed is still in there. Organized. Preserved. Including—" she let it hang, let them arrive at it themselves, because saying it aloud felt like tempting fate and she'd been a prophecy-scribe long enough to respect that instinct, "—including anything any of its meals ever witnessed. Including a laundress's daughter who wandered the old wing a century too late and a hundred years too early, depending on how you count."

Emeric had gone still. "The binding," he said. "Someone saw the binding. Someone who was fed to the gate afterward."

"Or someone whose shadow saw it," Tamsin said. "We don't need a living witness, Your Highness. We need a *kept* one. And the gate has been keeping witnesses for a hundred years, filed and cross-referenced, waiting for someone to ask the right question."

"That's assuming we can get answers out of it," Wren said. Her voice was flat, practical, but Tamsin heard the strain underneath it — the sound of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. "It's behind a sealed door. Corvane feeds it. It whispers. None of that suggests a reading room with open hours."

"No," Tamsin agreed. "But it suggests something better. It suggests the archive wants to be read." She tapped the prophecy-book in her satchel, the confiscated volume she'd transcribed with her own traitor's hand. "Why else keep everything? An archive that's never consulted is just a grave. This thing has been collecting for a century — names, shadows, years, dreams — and it circled tonight's date in a book before any of us were born. It's been waiting for a reader. Corvane thinks he's feeding a god. I think he's feeding a librarian."

The word dropped into the dim room and lay there.

"Then we're going to the library," Emeric said.

"We're going nowhere yet," Wren said sharply. "If the entity can show Maret her shadow's memories, it can show us things too — and it will show us exactly what it wants us to see. That's not an archive. That's a lure with a card catalog."

Tamsin met her eyes. "Yes. Probably both. Archives and lures look identical from the outside; the difference is only in what happens when you reach for the shelf." She shrugged, and hated how calm she sounded when her pulse was hammering. "So we don't reach blind. We ask Maret's shadow a specific question. One question, narrow, verifiable. If the answer matches the prophecy-book independently — if it tells us something we can check against records that exist outside the gate — then it's telling truth, or at least telling *accurately*, and we can risk deeper questions."

"What question?" Maret whispered.

Tamsin looked at the woman who had bought her son's life with her own darkness and had spent ten years flinching at sunlit docks, and Tamsin — who had read too much, been imprisoned for it, lost her name and her rank and her place in the world for the crime of writing true things down — felt something she had not felt since the day the Guild clerks had carried her down into the depths: hope.

Fragile. Terrible. Probably a trap.

Hope anyway.

"One night," Tamsin said. "One night, soon, we'll sit at the edge of that door and we'll call your shadow home to testify. And we'll ask it what it saw in the old wing when you were eight years old."

Maret's son chose that moment to come clattering up the stairs, herring-scaled and laughing, casting no shadow across the floorboards, and Maret rose to meet him with her face arranged into a mother's smile, and Tamsin looked away because some griefs deserved privacy.

Outside, the afternoon had begun to fail. Emeric stood in the lane pulling his cap low again, and Wren was already recalculating routes and risks, her lips moving faintly around numbers only she could hear.

"Tamsin." Wren's voice was quiet. "When we ask that question. Whatever the shadow shows us. How do we know it's showing us the past?"

Tamsin looked back at the bait shop, at the lit window where a shadowless boy was making his mother laugh.

"We don't," she said. "That's why we go tonight. Because if the date in the prophecy is right, we don't get a second asking."

Above Saltrow, the first bell of evening rang from the guildhall tower — and Tamsin, who knew bells the way sailors knew weather, counted the strokes and went cold.

There were supposed to be seven.

The tower rang eleven.
