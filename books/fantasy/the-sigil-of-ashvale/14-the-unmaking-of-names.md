## 14. The Unmaking of Names

They kept her in the counting house's old scriptorium, three floors above the gate-chamber and yet somehow closer to it than the walls allowed—a trick of the Guild's architecture that Wren had catalogued a hundred times in other people's accounts and never expected to feel from the inside. The room remembered being important. She could tell by the way the candlelight pooled where desks had once stood, marking absences the way water marks the beds of vanished rivers.

Tamsin slept against the far wall, prophecy-book open on her chest, rising and falling with her breath like something alive. Emeric had taken the door. He sat with his back against it, sword across his knees, and when Wren looked at him he looked back without pretense, which was still new enough to unsettle her.

"You should sleep," he said.

"I've been sleeping for ten years." She turned back to the window. Below, Ashvale lay under a bruised sky, the salt market dark, the debtor cells' lamps burning their last oil before the night Corvane would empty them. "I'm not going to waste the last ones unconscious."

"The last ones," Emeric repeated quietly. "You say things like that now. Like a woman reading her own final entries."

"Occupational habit."

He didn't laugh. Neither did she. Somewhere beneath them, faint as a heartbeat heard through a floor, the gate pulsed. It had been pulsing since the night they'd taken the ledger hall—slower now, patient, the rhythm of something that had waited a century and could wait out one more insomniac clerk.

Wren went to the desk nearest the window and opened her satchel. Her own ledger was there—the small one, the private one she'd kept since her first year, recording not trades but observations: the way Master Corvane's hands shook on fast days; the smell of the gate-chamber after a feeding, sweet as overripe pears; the number of times she had checked the sealed door each night (three; always three). She read the earliest pages and felt nothing.

That was the horror of it. Not grief, not loss. Nothing. Pages in her own handwriting describing a childhood she could not access, a mother whose name she'd written out in careful clerk's script—*Maren Joubert, née Vale*—and felt about as much recognition as if she were transcribing someone else's inventory.

"Show me again," she said.

Emeric crossed the room. He didn't ask what she meant. He took the book from her, found the page—he knew it by heart now too—and held it so the candle caught the ink. *Mother: Maren Joubert. Sold: one memory, complete. Purchased: passage north for her daughter, plus ten years' interest, compounding.*

"She sold your memories of her," he said, the same way he said it every time. Gently. Like a man reading a eulogy to someone who couldn't remember the deceased. "To get you out of Ashvale. And then you came back anyway."

"I came back because I didn't know what I was leaving." She took the book back. The words swam. "That's the joke, isn't it? The trade only works if the thing traded can't mourn itself. She made sure I couldn't miss her. Efficient. Very Guild-standard drafting."

"Wren—"

"It's fine." It wasn't fine. It was a hole in her shaped exactly like a woman, and every day since Tamsin had decoded the entry, she'd been pressing her tongue against it the way you press a missing tooth. "Go back to the door."

He hesitated, then went. That was the arrangement between them now: no comfort offered twice. They were all of them holding themselves together with procedure.

The candle burned down. The city stayed dark. And at some hour past midnight, when even Tamsin's breathing had gone slow and deep, the gate below stopped pulsing—

—and spoke.

*Wren.*

Not aloud. Not through the walls. Through the ledger in her hands, the ink stirring like something waking at the bottom of a pond. She looked down and the words of her mother's entry had rearranged themselves, letters crawling into new formations, and the formation was a sentence, and the sentence was:

*You want to see her.*

She should have woken the others. Every plan they'd made, every rule Tamsin had drilled into them—*never answer it, never negotiate, never let it think you're listening*—said she should wake them. Instead she sat very still, the way she had learned to sit for ten years while powerful people said terrible things above her head, and she listened, because the entity had found the one currency she could not pretend not to covet.

*I keep what I am fed,* it said, and its voice was not a voice but a sensation, like cold air moving across an open ledger page. *Every shadow. Every memory. Every name struck from every book. Your master has been generous these hundred years, and his generosity is my archive. I remember everything Ashvale has sold. Including her.*

"I don't make bargains," Wren whispered. Even that felt like answering. Even that felt like opening a door.

*This is not a bargain. This is a courtesy.* Something almost like amusement moved through the ink. *A demonstration of good faith, as your master would say. Look.*

The candle guttered though there was no draft. The darkness above the flame gathered, thickened, took on depth and grain—and became a room.

Not her room. A kitchen, low-ceilinged, smelling of tallow and river-damp, in some tenement she did not recognize and recognized completely, the way the body recognizes a home the mind has lost the address for. A woman stood at the table with her sleeves rolled, salting fish with quick, economical movements—fish, of course, everything in this city circled back to fish—and humming something tuneless under her breath. She was plain and tired and her hair was coming loose from its knot, and when she paused to push it back she left a smear of salt at her temple, and Wren's whole body lurched forward as if yanked by a hook behind the sternum.

*Maren,* the entity said, tender as a knife. *Your mother.*

"I know." Wren's voice cracked on it. "I know that."

*Do you? Or do you know what the ledger told you? There's a difference. The ledger gives you facts. I can give you the hum. The way she sang flat on purpose because her own mother had sung flat and she'd promised never to correct it. The way she counted the coins three times before hiding them, always three, and blamed the cat when she lost count. The way she cried in the bath so you wouldn't hear, and you did hear, and you never told her, and that secret between you was the truest thing either of you owned.*

"Stop." The word came out shredded.

The image did not stop. It widened. The woman turned from the table—slowly, terribly, like a figure in a dream obeying dream-logic—and looked directly out of the darkness at Wren, salt on her temple, eyes the exact gray-brown that Wren saw in her own reflection every morning without ever having known where they came from. Her mouth moved. No sound came. But Wren had been a ledger-keeper long enough to lip-read debtors through confession-glass, and the shape of the words was unmistakable, was the one phrase in all the world she had been engineered never to hear:

*Run, little bird. Don't look back.*

Then the kitchen folded up like a page closing, and the candle stood straight again, and Wren was on her feet without remembering standing, both hands braced on the desk, breathing like a woman who had surfaced from deep water.

Across the room, Emeric was watching her, hand on his sword. "What happened?"

"Nothing." She straightened. Lied smoothly, automatically, with the old Guild fluency—and hated how easily it came. "It tested the walls. Nothing got through."

He studied her a moment too long. Then nodded and returned to the door, and Wren sat back down and put her palms flat against her ledger as if holding down a lid.

*You see what I offer,* the ink murmured, patient, warm now, the cold gone out of it. *Not power. Not gold. Those are Corvane's appetites, and I find them vulgar. What I offer is restoration. Everything that was taken from you—every erased year, every struck line—I will write back. You will remember her whole. Her face, her voice, the sound of the door when she came home. You will remember yourself before Ashvale made you invisible. Is that not worth more than anything the Guild ever traded?*

"And the price." She kept her voice flat. Clerk's voice. Reading terms aloud.

*So direct. I admire that in you—you're the first one in a century who reads contracts properly.* The ink rippled, almost appreciative. *The price is the door. Open it fully. Not a crack for shadows to slip through, not a feeding-slit for your master's cowardly installments. All the way. Let me step through wearing this face I've grown fond of, and take what a century of appetite has earned.*

"Ashvale dies."

*Ashvale is already dying,* it said, with terrible reasonableness. *Slowly, in cells and ledgers and salt markets, a piece at a time, and no one weeps because no one watches. I am simply proposing to do openly, in one night, what your Guild has done proudly for a hundred years. The difference between us, Wren Joubert, is that I have never pretended to be a benevolent institution.* A pause. The ink settled. *And here is my honesty, freely given, as hers was freely given: open the door, and I will restore your mother's sale in full—hers AND yours. She sold your memories to save you. I will give them back to both of you. She lives still, did you know? Two streets from the salt market, older, poorer, and unable to remember why she cries every year on the anniversary of a trade she also cannot recall. You could knock on her door tomorrow with a decade of her in your arms. Or you can close this conversation and remain a blank page walking around in a borrowed life, and Ashvale keeps its slow death, and everyone stays comfortably unremembered together.*

Silence. The candle burned. In the corner, Tamsin murmured something in her sleep—an old scribe's counting-rhythm, numbers keeping nightmares at bay.

Wren looked at her own hands. Ledger-keeper's hands. Hands that had recorded a woman selling her shadow for her son's life and had felt nothing, because feeling was inefficient, because invisibility was safety, because the Guild taught that the person who watched and wrote was different from the person who bled.

She thought about the clerk Corvane had fed to the gate. About the sound the crowd hadn't made.

*Well?*

"No," Wren said.

The word came out quiet and absolute, and she found, examining herself for the terror that should have accompanied it, that the terror was there but underneath something else—something harder and cleaner, forged in chapter after chapter of watching what silence cost.

*No?* The ink went very still.

"No. And here are my terms, since you admire a proper reading." She leaned toward the ledger, and her voice steadied as she found the shape of the thing she'd known since the kitchen folded away. "You showed me her to break me. But you made a mistake. You showed me that she *sold* me—that love, in this city, looks like a line in a ledger with the price paid in full. Everyone in Ashvale who ever traded did it for someone else. The shadow-woman. My mother. Half the names in your archive aren't greed. They're grief with the serial numbers filed off. And you keep them all. You hoard the proof that this city is worth more than you say it is."

The ink did not move.

"So no. I won't open your door. And I promise you this instead: we are going to unbind the king whose face you wear, and then we are coming for every single thing in your archive. Every shadow, every memory, every name. You've spent a century eating this city's love. We're going to make you give it back."

For a long moment, nothing. Then, softly, with something that might have been respect or might have been hunger sharpening itself:

*Corvane said you were the best clerk he had.*

And the ink went inert, ordinary, just words on a page, and the pressure in the room lifted like a hand withdrawn.

Wren sat alone with the candle and shook—finally, thoroughly, the delayed tremor of a woman who had stared into the exact shape of her longing and refused it. When the shaking passed, she woke Tamsin with a hand on her shoulder.

"It talked to me," she said. "It knows about my mother. And Tamsin—it *keeps* everything. Every trade. If the king's binding is in there, if any of what was sold still exists inside it—"

Tamsin was already reaching for the prophecy-book, sleep burning off her face like mist. "Then the gate isn't just the enemy's house," she said slowly. "It's the enemy's *evidence*. Everything we need to destroy it, it's been storing for us." Her fingers found a passage near the end, one they'd marked and failed to solve: *what the devourer keeps, the keeper may claim.* "We need the shadow-woman. Tonight, Wren. Before Corvane empties those cells and feeds her to the thing she sold her shadow to."

Below them, faint and unmistakable for the first time, the gate began to pulse faster—hungry now, no longer patient—as somewhere in the dark city, the debtor cells began to empty.
