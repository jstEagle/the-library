## 15. Emeric's Choice

The candle had burned down to a stub the color of old teeth before Emeric spoke.

"Say it again."

Tamsin did not look up from the prophecy-book. Her finger rested on a line of cramped script, and she read it aloud a third time, her voice flat in the way of someone who has learned that flatness is armor: *"When the soul is loosed from its cage of flesh, the flesh shall fail, for it was never the king's to keep. The vessel dies with the binding broken. So it is written; so let it be weighed."*

*So let it be weighed.* As if the words were coin and someone might dispute their count.

Emeric stood at the narrow window of Tamsin's borrowed room above the salt market, watching the harbor fog swallow the masts below. Somewhere out there, Corvane was still master of the Guild. Somewhere beneath the guildhall, his father's body — the body Emeric had mourned at thirteen, had wept over, had carried the weight of for eleven years of exile — sat on a throne or walked through marble halls, wearing the crown like a glove worn by no hand at all.

"The vessel dies," he repeated. "Meaning him. Meaning *it*. Meaning—"

"Both." Tamsin closed the book gently, as though it were sleeping. "That's the cruelty of it, Your Highness. There's no version where you get your father back. There's only a version where you set something free and watch it end."

"Don't call me that."

"It's what you are tonight. Tonight you're not a fishmonger."

He turned from the window. The room was small and honest — a bed, a desk, the prophecy-book, three cups of tea gone cold because none of them remembered drinking any. Wren had gone down into the market an hour ago to meet the shadow-seller again, and her absence filled the space like another person. He found himself wishing she were back, and hated the wishing. He had spent eleven years needing no one. Needing people was how princes died.

"How certain is it?" he asked. "The reading. Could it mean something else? Vessel could mean the gate. Vessel could mean the crown itself. Prophecies are slippery — you told me so yourself, when we met. You said a prophecy-scribe who reads literally dies poor and disgraced."

"I said that," Tamsin admitted. "And I've spent two days trying to make it mean anything else." She opened the book again, turning pages with a scribe's reverence, stopping at a diagram Emeric had seen once and dreamed about since: a man-shaped figure bound in seven lines of script, the seventh line drawn straight through the heart. "Look at the marginalia. This hand — the same century-old hand that circled your date — wrote here. 'The seventh line runs through the heart of the vessel, not the soul. Cut the line, spill the blood.' It's unambiguous. The binding isn't anchored to the entity. It's anchored to the body. To the flesh. Unbind the soul, and the flesh has nothing left holding it together."

Emeric sat down slowly on the edge of the bed. The rope frame creaked under him, a small honest sound in a night full of enormous dishonest ones.

"So my father is already dead."

"He's been dead a hundred years."

"No." Emeric heard his own voice harden and did nothing to soften it. "My father died eleven years ago, in a bed, holding my mother's hand, while I was forbidden to enter the city to see him. That's what they told me. That's what I buried. What's sitting on the throne now—" His hands curled against his knees. "What's wearing him now, whatever it is, it isn't dead. Dead things don't sign treaties. Dead things don't raise taxes. Dead things don't send soldiers to drag a boy across the border in the middle of the night and call it mercy."

"You think it killed your mother too?"

The question landed like a hook behind his sternum. He looked up sharply. "I didn't say that."

"You didn't have to." Tamsin's eyes were kind in the way Wren's weren't — Wren's kindness was always arithmetic, cost counted first — but kindness could cut deeper than arithmetic. "You've thought it for years. You just never had anywhere to put the thought. Now there's a book that says the thing on the throne marked you from childhood. Marked you, exiled you, and then both your parents die within four years of each other, conveniently childless, conveniently heirless, conveniently leaving a throne with no living claimant but one it already owns."

"She died of fever."

"People die of fever all the time," Tamsin said softly. "Especially queens whose sons are inconvenient."

Emeric stood. He crossed to the window because standing still felt like drowning. Below, the salt market was closing for the night, awnings folding, lamps guttering one by one along the quay. A woman argued cheerfully with a fishmonger about the price of cod, and the ordinariness of it made him want to scream. Eleven years he had sold fish beside people like her. Eleven years he had convinced himself that anonymity was peace, that a man with salt-cracked hands owed nothing to a kingdom that had discarded him.

And underneath, all along, like a stone in a shoe he'd stopped noticing: the knowledge that the throne had never really let him go.

"If I do this," he said, to the fog rather than to Tamsin, "and the ritual works — if we pull his soul out of that thing's mouth and speak his true name and free him — then the body falls. Right there. In front of whoever's watching. And every eye in that chamber turns to me, because I'm the one who killed the king."

"The entity," Tamsin corrected. "You'd kill the entity. Or evict it. The distinction will matter to history, if history survives."

"And the crown passes to me." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Do you understand? Do you see the shape of it? The trap isn't the gate. The trap isn't even Corvane. The trap is *me*. They built this whole century so that the only way out ends with a Joubert — no. Not Joubert." His mother's name, the false one he'd lived under. He said his real name instead, quietly, like testing a blade: "A Valcrist. On the throne. With the thing that wore my father knowing exactly which strings it left behind when it went."

*

He had been nine years old the last time he'd truly spoken with his father.

Not the formal audiences — those were theater, his father behind the high desk with the crown catching lamplight, dispensing justice in a voice pitched to carry through marble. Emeric meant the other times. The early mornings, when the king would summon his son to walk the palace gardens before council, and they would talk about nothing: the health of the orchard bees, the merits of one hunting dog over another, whether the river ran higher than last spring. His father had loved the bees. Emeric remembered that with a suddenness that hurt — the king of Ashvale, on his knees in wet grass, showing a prince how to find the queen in a humming box of ten thousand subjects.

*"A hive knows its queen by scent alone,"* his father had said, lifting a frame of comb into the light. *"If you give them a stranger, they'll ball her and smother her. But give them time, feed them right, and they'll accept almost anyone. Remember that, Emeric. Most things that serve a ruler don't serve the ruler. They serve the smell of the office."*

He had thought it a lesson in statecraft. He had been a child. Now, thirty years too late, he understood his father had been telling him the truth in the only way a watched man could tell it — that the thing giving him bee lessons some mornings might not be the man who'd tucked him in other nights, and that a king could leave messages for his son inside ordinary sentences, hoping someday the son would be old enough to hear them.

Had his father known? Had the real Valcrist, caged behind his own eyes for decades, chosen those mornings deliberately — the mornings when the entity slept deepest, or hunted furthest abroad in its crown-work — to press his palm to his son's shoulder and say *remember this, remember me, remember the bees*?

Emeric pressed his forehead against the cold glass until it ached.

"They balled her," he said aloud. "The hive. When the keeper gives them a stranger."

"What?" Tamsin said.

"Nothing. Bees." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand and turned around. "Show me the entry again. My father's binding. The price my mother paid. I need to see it before I decide."

"Wren has the ledger pages—"

"No. The prophecy. Your part. Show me what it says about the binding rite — who performed it, what it cost." He crossed back to the desk. "Because here's what I keep circling, Tamsin, and I want you to tell me I'm wrong. Someone bound my father a hundred years ago. Someone chose to do it. Which means someone knew the price going in — the vessel dies, the soul is caged, the crown goes hungry — and paid it anyway. And if the prophecy names the price so clearly, then the people who did it wanted it written down. Wanted it legible. Wanted some future heir to sit exactly where I'm sitting, read exactly these words, and understand precisely what was done to his family and why."

Tamsin was quiet a long moment. Then she turned to the final folio, the one whose vellum had darkened as if scorched, and laid it flat between them.

The script here was different — older, harder, each letter carved rather than written. Emeric couldn't read it, but he had learned in these two days to read Tamsin reading it, and what passed over her face now frightened him more than anything the book had said yet.

"Tamsin."

"The binding wasn't performed against the king," she said slowly. "It was performed *by the succession*. The prophecy calls it 'the coronation beneath the coronation.' Every Ashvale monarch for a hundred years — every crowned head, every regent, every Guild master sworn at the accession — took part. It's not that someone stole the throne from your family, Emeric. It's that your family's throne was built on top of a prison, and everyone who ever swore to protect the realm also swore to keep the prisoner."

"My father—"

"Took the oath knowing. Or half-knowing. Or knowing and choosing not to look, which is the same sin with better manners." She tapped the page. "And here — the last line. Listen. '*When the heir of the bound blood stands before the gate and speaks the true name freely, the cage opens and the debt of crowns comes due. Let him weigh well what he inherits: a kingdom fed on trades, a guild fed on lives, and a hunger that does not end when the wearer does.*'"

Silence. The candle gutted. Down in the market, the last lamp went out.

"*Freely,*" Emeric said. "It says freely."

"That's the clause that matters most. The unbinding only works if you choose it without coercion, without bargain, without being deceived about the price." Tamsin met his eyes. "Which means everything I've just told you, I had to tell you. If you'd gone in ignorant, the ritual would fail. Whoever wrote this wanted the heir to know the whole cost before he spoke. They wanted you to choose with open eyes."

"Then why write it at all? Why not just — spare the heirs? Leave the throne empty of truth?"

"Because a lie can hold a door shut for a century, but only a truth can open one." She said it simply, without drama, and it settled over him like snowfall.

*

Wren came back near dawn, smelling of fish brine and secrets, and found them both exactly where she'd left them.

"Well?" she asked, setting down a wrapped bundle that smelled of bread. "Has he decided?"

"He hasn't asked the right question yet," Tamsin said.

Emeric looked at her. "Which is?"

"Why you're hesitating." Wren unwrapped the bread, tore off a piece, offered it around. Nobody took any. "You've known since yesterday that freeing your father kills the vessel. You're not stupid, and you're not squeamish — I've seen you gut fish without blinking. So it's not the dying. It's something else."

He should have lied. Eleven years of exile had made lying his native language. But these two had a way of standing inside his silences like they owned the lease.

"When he dies," Emeric said finally, "the moment his soul comes free — however long that lasts, however brief — he'll know. He'll know what was done to him, and he'll know that his son is the one who did it. The one who ended him. And then the crown lands on me, and I spend the rest of my life ruling the prison my father rotted in, on a throne that ate him, in a kingdom that watched and called it reign." His voice frayed. He kept pulling the thread anyway, because Tamsin had said *freely*, and freely meant all of it. "I keep thinking: what would he want? Would the man who showed me the bees want to be pulled out of the cage just to die in front of his son? Or would he want us to leave the lock alone, keep the lie running, keep the city safe another hundred years — the way Corvane argues, damn him, the way Corvane *almost* argues well?"

"Corvane feeds the gate children's shadows to buy himself decades," Wren said flatly. "Don't you dare put yourself in the same sentence."

"I'm not putting myself in his sentence. I'm asking whether his sentence has any true words in it." Emeric stood, restless, pacing the width of the little room — four steps, turn, four steps. "Because here's what I know in my bones, and I need one of you to tell me if I'm wrong. A kingdom built on a secret doesn't survive the secret. It just gets worse at hiding it. Corvane's slow-feeding, the entity's patience, the careful ledger entries — it's all decay management. The rot spreads anyway. It reached my mother. It reached the debtor cells. Last week it reached a clerk whose name I never learned, and I watched the whole hall pretend a man hadn't been devoured in front of them." He stopped pacing. "The choice isn't between my father's life and the city's safety. That's the lie Corvane sells. The choice is between a truth that costs everything now, and a lie that costs everything later, plus interest. And I am *so tired* of interest."

Tamsin had gone very still. "You've decided."

"I decided the moment you read me the word *freely*." He turned to the window. The sky over the harbor had gone the gray of unpolished silver; gulls were waking; somewhere a bell rang the hour, indifferent and clean. "My father spent a hundred years in a cage, and the worst part — the very worst part — is that nobody ever spoke to him. Not once. Everyone talked *about* him, *around* him, *through* him. Corvane negotiated with his jailer. The Guild recorded his captivity in columns. Even the prophecy writes him like an entry in a ledger." His throat worked. "Someone has to speak *to* him. His name. Out loud. By someone who loved him. If that's the price of the crown landing on my head — fine. Take the crown. I'll break it apart and melt it down for church bells, but I'll take it, because the alternative is letting him stay unspoken for another century, and I won't. I won't do to him what everyone else did."

He realized he was crying only when Wren silently held out a cloth — rough market linen, fish-scaled — and he laughed wetly at the absurdity of it, a prince weeping into a fishmonger's rag, and took it anyway.

"For what it's worth," Tamsin said quietly, "the marginalia agrees with you. The same hand, last page: '*Let the heir come grieving and go crowned. Grief is the only honest key.*'"

"Grieving and go crowned." He blew his nose inelegantly. "Charming. Did your prophecy-scribe colleagues ever write anything cheerful?"

"Not once in the entire archive. It's why they were disbanded."

For a moment — one small, stolen moment — the three of them almost smiled together in the gray light, and Emeric understood that this was what he had been starving for through eleven years of exile: not the crown, not the revenge, not even the truth, but this. Two people who knew the worst of it and stayed in the room.

Then Wren unfolded the scrap of paper she'd carried up from the market, and the smile died in the cradle.

She spread it on the desk beside the prophecy. It was a Guild chit — official stock, the embossed seal unmistakable — and Emeric read it upside down twice before the meaning finished assembling itself.

**EMERGENCY RECLAMATION NOTICE. All outstanding debts called in effective immediately. Debtor holdings to be transferred to central custody by nightfall. Attendance compulsory.**

"Nightfall," Wren said. Her voice had gone thin and fast, the voice of a ledger-keeper reading numbers she didn't want to total. "Every debtor cell in Ashvale. Every person who ever traded the Guild so much as a memory. He's not buying himself one more year, Emeric. He's emptying the shelves. The prophecy's deadline is tomorrow night, and Corvane knows it, and he's going to feed the gate an entire district of the city to keep his bargain alive."

Outside, the bell rang the half-hour, and down in the streets of Ashvale, the first of the summoned began to walk toward the guildhall — thousands of them, ordinary people carrying their small debts like stones, walking willingly into the dark because a notice told them attendance was compulsory.

Emeric picked up the fish-scaling knife from the table where he'd left it eleven years of habit ago, and put it in his belt.

"Then we've got until nightfall," he said, "to storm a counting house."
