## 16. The Night of Ledgers

The bells of Ashvale rang wrong that night.

Wren heard it from the garret window of Tamsin's hiding place—a room above a dye-shop in the Salt Market that smelled of mordant and old fish—and she knew before her mind finished the thought what it meant. Guild bells did not ring at midnight. Guild bells rang at dawn, at dusk, and at the closing of accounts on quarter-day, and never otherwise. The last time they had rung outside their hours was the night of the great fire, forty years ago, when half the counting house had burned and the ledgers had been carried out into the street by clerks who wept as they worked.

Three strokes. A pause. Three again.

*Debt called,* said the old signal-code every clerk learned in their first year and prayed never to hear. *All debt. Called now.*

"They've started," Emeric said behind her. He had not been sleeping; he sat by the cold hearth with his boots on and his father's signet ring—retrieved from its hiding place beneath a loose flagstone in the salt cellar where his mother's people had kept it for twenty years—turned over and over in his fingers like a coin he could not decide to spend. "That's the call, isn't it. I remember it. From when I was a boy."

"You shouldn't remember it," Wren said. "You were four when you were exiled."

"I remember bells." His voice was flat. "I remember being carried through a gate at night, and I remember that someone was singing, and then I remember nothing for a long time. Memory is a ledger too, apparently." He looked up at her, and there was something terrible in his face—not fear, not grief, but the cold arithmetic of a man who had stopped hoping to be wrong. "It's begun. Corvane is emptying the cells."

Below them, the Salt Market should have been silent. It was not. Doors were opening along the crooked row of tenements, and lamplight spilled out onto the cobbles, and voices rose—first confused, then frightened. Wren leaned out and saw why.

Guild wardens were walking the street.

Not two of them, as on any other night of collection. Not six. Dozens, in pairs, moving door to door with lanterns hooded so that they threw light only downward, only at the thresholds, like men checking a list against the ground itself. And behind each pair came a clerk with an open ledger and a stylus, reading names aloud.

"Vessa Marlowe, spinner, arrears of nine years' tithe—"

"Old Tam, no surname given, debt of record since the flood year—"

"The widow Ansel, three children entered under her account—"

Each name was followed by silence, and then by the sound of a door opening, because the poor of Ashvale knew better than to refuse the Guild at its own door. Wren watched a woman come out into the street with her coat over her nightdress and her hands folded in front of her like a penitent, watched the clerk write something, watched the woman nod as though agreeing to terms. Watched them walk her away down the hill toward the guildhall, toward the sealed gate beneath it, toward the thing wearing the dead king's crown.

No one screamed. That was the worst of it. Screaming would have meant surprise, and there was no surprise left in Ashvale's poorest streets. They went quietly because they had always known, in some wordless way, that this was how it would end: not with fire or plague but with an entry written in fair hand and a knock at midnight.

"We have to go now," Wren said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her, because inside she was shaking apart. "Tamsin. Get up."

Tamsin was already dressed. She had been dressed since sundown, sitting cross-legged on the floor with the prophecy-book open across her knees, tracing and retracing the circled date in its century-old ink as though she could wear through the page to whatever truth lay beneath. She looked up now, and her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely clear.

"Tonight's the night," she said. "The ledger opens. Not the gate—the ledger. Listen to me, Wren. I got the parsing wrong. Everyone did. It doesn't say the *gate* opens tonight. It says the *ledger* opens—all of them, every account, everything ever traded through that door becomes visible, becomes *claimable*. Corvane thinks he can buy one more year before that happens. One more payment, big enough to reset the clock." She stood, and the prophecy-book slid shut with a sound like a mouth closing. "He's not buying a year for himself. He's buying a year for all of them. For the whole rotten arrangement. And he'll pay with anyone he can reach before dawn."

"Then we reach them first," Emeric said.

They went down through the dye-shop, past the sleeping dyer and his family who had been paid, in honest coin, to ask no questions—and Wren hated herself a little for using the Guild's own methods even for mercy—and out into the street where the wardens' lanterns were already two doors away and closing.

There was no time for cleverness. There was only time for nerve.

Emeric walked straight up to the nearest pair of wardens, and Wren felt her heart stop, until she saw what he was doing: squaring his shoulders, dropping his chin, putting on the accent of the salt-dock districts he'd lived in for twenty years, and holding out his hand as though he had every right in the world to stop them.

"Wardens. Counting-house business." He let the words drop low and bored. "Master Corvane's auditors are doing spot-checks tonight. Every collection gets verified before it goes down the hill. Names first, then take them to the corner post for confirmation. You know the new protocol."

Wren held her breath. The wardens were young, tired, and afraid—she could see it in the way their lanterns trembled, in the way they glanced at each other. On a night like tonight, no one wanted to be the one who hadn't followed protocol. The elder of them consulted his list. Emeric read it upside down without appearing to, nodded gravely, and said, "Marlowe's confirmed. Take her to the post. Next name?"

And while they walked the widow Marlowe to the corner—while they *walked her away*, and Wren's nails bit into her palms at the theater of it—Tamsin slipped across the street and spoke softly through the next doorway, and a family came out into the dark and vanished down the alley behind the tannery, carrying nothing, told only: *run north, past the shrine of the drowned saint, don't stop until the bells stop.*

It went on like that for hours. It was the longest night of Wren's life, and it was made longer by the arithmetic she could not stop doing. For every debtor they turned aside, three more were collected somewhere else in the city. The wardens moved faster than lies could. By the second hour, the pairs had stopped consulting lists at corners and simply worked their way down the streets like scythes, and by the third hour the streets themselves had changed—there were gaps in them, absences walking around in the shape of lit windows and closed doors, whole households gone so cleanly that the dark seemed to lean into the spaces they left.

And still the bells rang. Three strokes, pause, three. Debt called. All debt. Called now.

* * *

They reached the guildhall district at the fourth hour, moving with a crowd—which was itself an obscenity, because there *was* a crowd, dozens of the collected poor being funneled up the processional road by ranks of wardens, and the three of them simply joined the flow, heads down, part of the river.

From the outside, Wren thought, it might almost look orderly. Humane, even. Lanterns burning at intervals, clerks calling names in calm voices, blankets handed to the shivering. The Guild was good at this. The Guild had always been good at dressing a knife in ceremony.

Inside the gates of the guildhall courtyard, the true scale of it opened up before her, and her stomach turned over.

The debtor cells were in the undercroft, but they weren't being used. Instead, rows of wagons stood in the yard, and the debtors were standing beside the wagons in lines sorted by ledger-number, waiting with the terrible patience of people who had long ago stopped expecting anything from the world. And at the head of the yard, on the steps of the hall, stood Corvane.

She had not seen him since the night he offered her permanence and she refused him, since the ledgers began rewriting her history and she fled through the service tunnels past the whispering door. He looked older than eleven days should allow. His coat was immaculate but his collar was open, and his hair, always oiled and ordered, hung loose at one temple as though he had dragged his hand through it and forgotten to repair the damage. He was speaking to the assembled clerks in a voice pitched to carry, and it was not cruel. That was the horror of it. It was gentle.

"—and let no one call this a cruelty," he was saying. "Every soul here carries debt the city cannot forgive and cannot carry. Tonight we settle those debts in full. No more interest accruing. No more children born into their parents' accounts. Tonight ends it. You may tell your grandchildren—" and here his voice caught, or performed catching, Wren could no longer tell the difference with him— "that on the night of ledgers, Master Corvane paid Ashvale's burdens down to nothing."

The clerks bowed. The wardens bowed. The debtors stood in the cold and waited to be fed to the door.

Wren found that her hands had become fists so tight her knuckles had gone white, and that Tamsin's hand was clamped around her wrist, hard enough to bruise, holding her back from something she had not consciously decided to do.

"Not yet," Tamsin breathed. "Not like this. If you rush him now, you die in this yard and the rest of us hang tomorrow, and the door eats everyone in these lines anyway. *Not yet.*"

"He's going to kill them," Wren whispered. "All of them. He's standing there making a speech about mercy while he—"

"Yes."

"And we're going to stand here and watch."

"*Yes.*" Tamsin's grip did not loosen. Her face was gray in the lantern-light. "Because if we move now, it changes nothing except adding our names to the payment. We need the counting house, Wren. The master ledger is in the counting house, and the ritual pages, and the seal-records. We storm the yard, we get maybe thirty seconds before the wardens close, and we spend those thirty seconds dying. We wait. We find the moment. I know—" and here her voice cracked for the first time, just once, quickly repaired— "I know what waiting costs. I spent six years in a cell learning what it costs. Wait anyway."

Emeric said nothing at all. He was staring at the steps, at Corvane, with an expression Wren had seen only once before—on the face of the shadow-seller, Vessa Marlowe, when they had asked her what she remembered of the night her shadow was taken. It was the expression of someone watching a wound reopen on purpose.

Then Corvane raised his hand, and the first line of debtors began to move.

They were marched down the outer stair, the one that spiraled into the ground beside the hall's foundation, the one Wren had climbed a thousand times on her private ritual visits to the sealed gate. The line descended into the earth and the lanterns followed, and the light grew smaller, and smaller, and then the last glow sank below the level of the yard and was gone, and the yard was quieter, and the clerks began writing entries.

Wren counted. She couldn't help it. It was what she was. Forty-one souls went down in the first descent, and then a pause while the clerks compared notes, and then another line formed, and another forty-one, and between each descent the bells above the hall tolled three strokes, recording, she understood suddenly, each payment received. The gate was signing for its delivery.

By the fifth hour, the lines were thinning. By the sixth, the wardens had gone back out into the streets for a final sweep, and the yard held only clerks, and wagons, and the enormous patience of the guildhall itself—and that was when Tamsin pulled them back into the shadow of the boundary wall and laid out the plan in a whisper fast and flat as a ledger entry.

The counting house stood at the yard's far end, connected to the main hall by the long gallery. Its doors would be barred, but its loading arch—the one used for bringing in strongboxes on quarter-day—would be open tonight, because tonight was, in Corvane's own theology, the greatest quarter-day in the Guild's history. Three of them, plus whoever among the freed clerks could be trusted—Maret, the young man from records, whose brother had gone down in the third descent; he was waiting at the wall breach with a crowbar and a face like flint, and he had brought two others whose names Wren didn't catch and whose faces she would remember forever.

"Tamsin holds the gallery," Tamsin said. "If the building shifts—if the corridors start rearranging—you hold the book open and you keep reading aloud, and the halls will hesitate, because the Guild hates nothing so much as being *witnessed*. Emeric takes the stairs to the gate-chamber and does what we planned, if it comes to that. And Wren—" Tamsin turned to her, and in the dark her eyes were very bright. "Wren takes the master ledger. Whatever happens, whatever you see, whatever he says to you—Corvane will be there, and he will talk, because talking is his weapon the way the crown is the entity's—you get to the ledger and you put your hand on it and you do not take it off. Can you do that?"

Wren looked back across the yard, at the steps where Corvane had promised mercy, at the descending stair where forty-one souls had gone down and not come up, at the bells hanging silent now in the tower, waiting for the next payment.

Somewhere beneath her feet, faint as a heartbeat through stone, the gate was feeding. And somewhere beneath her feet, a small caged soul that had once been a king was listening to it all happen, one hundred years of listening deep.

"My earliest entries are already blurring," Wren said. "My mother's face is already blurred. Everything I am is written in books that man controls, and tonight he taught me exactly what he does with the people in his books." She rolled her shoulders back the way she did before a long night of entries, the small ritual of a small life, repurposed. "Get me to that ledger."

* * *

They crossed at the change of the watch, when the yard's lanterns were trimmed and refilled and the light went briefly dim. Maret's crowbar took the loading-arch padlock in one stroke that sounded, to Wren's straining ears, like the crack of the world. Then they were inside, in the smell of paper and lamp-oil and dust that had been the only incense of her adult life, and the counting house rose around them, shelves upon shelves upon shelves, ten thousand ledgers holding ten thousand lives, all of it quiet, all of it complicit, all of it *theirs*, for the next few minutes, if they were swift.

They were swift. Tamsin broke for the gallery with the prophecy-book hugged to her chest and began to read aloud in a voice that filled the vaulted dark like water filling a hull. Somewhere ahead, the corridors groaned—actually groaned, timber and stone shifting in their joints, the Guild waking to the violation—and then hesitated, caught between its hunger and its hatred of witnesses, and Tamsin read louder.

Emeric went for the inner stairs with Maret and the others, and Wren heard their boots fade upward toward the gate-chamber, toward the door, toward the crown.

Which left the master ledger, chained to its lectern at the center of the hall, glowing faintly in the lamplight like something alive. Which left the walk across the open floor alone. Which left—

"Wren."

His voice came from the shadows by the hearth, where a single lamp burned low, and she nearly stopped, nearly obeyed, ten years of habit pulling at her spine like a hook. Corvane stepped into the light. He was not armed. He did not look afraid. He looked tired beyond the reach of tiredness, and he looked at her the way a father looks at a child who has come home bleeding.

"You're erasing yourself," he said gently. "Can't you feel it? Your edges going soft? Come here. Put your hand on the master ledger with me, and I'll write you back. I'll write you *permanent*. One signature, and no power in Ashvale can strike your name again. Not mine. Not theirs. Not the thing under the floor." He extended his hand across the distance between them, across the lectern, across everything. "The bells ring all night, Wren. But dawn is coming, and dawn forgives everything that survived it. Be one of the things that survives."

Behind her, far off in the dark of the gallery, Tamsin's voice rose, reading the old words, holding the halls. Above her, faintly, steel rang on steel—Emeric had reached the stairs, had met the guard. The whole night balanced on its edge.

And Wren Joubert, ledger-keeper, invisible woman, keeper of small debts, walked forward—not to Corvane, but past him—took the master ledger's edge in both hands, and said:

"No. But I'll write *you*."
