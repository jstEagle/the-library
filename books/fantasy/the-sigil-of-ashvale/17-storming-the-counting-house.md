## 17. Storming the Counting House

**Wren**

The debtor cells opened like a wound.

Wren had never heard the sound before—ten years in the guildhall and she hadn't known the cells *had* locks that could be forced from outside—but she would carry it to her grave: iron shrieking against iron, and beneath it the thin, confused cries of people who had spent their lives learning not to hope. Forty clerks and debtors and salt-stained porters, freed by Tamsin's stolen keys and Emeric's sword, standing blinking in the torchlight of the under-halls as if light itself were a trick they'd be punished for believing.

"Listen," Wren said, and her voice carried farther than she expected. Perhaps the building let it. Perhaps, tonight, the building wanted to see what they would do. "Corvane is emptying the city into the gate. Street by street. The poor quarters first—he's already begun. Every hour we spend planning is an hour of names."

An old woman in a debtor's smock raised her hand as if asking permission to exist. "They took my daughter's family off Tanner's Row at dusk," she said. "Carried them through the counting house doors. There was no screaming. That's the part I can't—" Her voice folded.

"There will be no screaming when we take it back either," Emeric said quietly. He stood among them still smelling of fish brine, still wearing the oilskin apron he'd refused to remove, because—Wren understood now—it was the last honest thing he owned. "But there will be an accounting."

*There will always be an accounting,* Wren thought. That was the one truth the Guild had taught her that was worth keeping.

Tamsin spread the prophecy-book on an overturned grain cart, weighting its corners with candle stubs. The old ink seemed darker tonight, or perhaps the light was worse. "The binding ritual requires three hands," she said. "A reader to hold the words open. A blood-heir to speak the name. And—" her eyes found Wren's, and she didn't finish, because they all knew. A keeper of ledgers.

"The master ledger has to be reached first," Wren said. "Everything Corvane has fed the gate runs through those accounts. If we unbind the king while the payments are still live, the entity keeps its anchor. We sever the accounts, then we sever the king's chains." She looked around at the faces, gaunt and frightened and furious. "The counting house is guarded by men who are paid in years of life. They will fight harder than any soldier you've faced, because for them every minute of this night is literally worth months."

"Then we should stop wasting theirs," Emeric said.

Wren gave them the only thing she had ever given anyone freely: the layout. Ten years of walking those halls unseen, ten years of memorizing which corridors remembered and which forgot, poured out in a quarter hour. The service stair behind the third archive. The gap where the building always hesitated between two versions of itself—the seam she'd slipped through since she was nineteen. The counting house proper: a vaulted hall where forty scribes had once worked in silence, now emptied for the night's great trade.

And above it all, behind black iron, the gate-chamber door.

She did not tell them about the whispering. Some debts a person carries alone until the very end.

* * *

**Tamsin**

The guildhall fought them.

Tamsin had suspected it for years—that the building was not merely built but *bound*, that its corridors were less architecture than argument—but knowing and feeling were different animals entirely. As they moved up from the under-halls in three columns, the walls began to lie.

The grand stair that should have risen toward the counting house delivered them instead into a chapel she had never seen, all dust and dead candles, where portraits of Guild masters hung with their eyes scratched out. When she turned back the way they'd come, the doorway showed her the cellar. Again. Again.

"It's rearranging," she called, and hated how her voice shook. "It knows what we want and it's giving us everything else."

"Then want something else," Emeric said.

It was the shadow-seller who solved it—Maren, the woman from chapter one of all their troubles, the woman who had sold her shadow for her son's life and spent eleven days learning what the gate kept. She walked the false chapel slowly, barefoot, her shadowless feet making no prints in the dust, and stopped before the scratched-out portrait of a master dead two centuries.

"It lies in shapes," Maren said. "My shadow told me—told the part of me that remembers being kept. It can't invent, only rearrange. So find the thing that doesn't fit." She pressed her palm flat against the wall beside the portrait. "This wall is warm. Stone in a sealed chapel shouldn't be warm."

Behind the warm wall: the true stair, narrow and ugly, smelling of lamp-oil and old paper. The building hissed—there was no other word for it—as they pried the stones loose, a sound like a ledger closing on a finger.

Tamsin climbed with the prophecy-book chained to her wrist. That had been her own addition; she trusted the building's honesty less than she trusted her own grip. The pages whispered against each other as she walked, turning themselves, the century-old hand seeming eager now, as if the book too had waited a long time for this date to arrive.

Halfway up, the stair ended.

Not blocked—*ended*, in midair, four steps of stone hanging over darkness that breathed. Below, faintly, she could hear water moving where no water was. The building had simply decided this passage did not exist anymore, and was watching to see whether she would accept the revision.

"Tamsin!" Emeric's voice, distant, already climbing another way.

She looked down at the breathing dark. Then she opened the book to the circled date—to *the ledger opens*—and held it up so the old ink faced the emptiness.

"You don't get to erase tonight," she told the guildhall. "I read you your own history. I know what you did to the king. I know what you did to *me*. And I am telling you, as a scribe of the Ashvale Archive, that this entry stands."

The dark shivered. And the stair, grudging as a debt collector, extended itself one stone at a time beneath her feet.

She ran up it before it could change its mind.

* * *

**Emeric**

Emeric had fought exactly twice in his life before tonight, and both times had been tavern disputes settled with broken furniture and apologies. Nothing had prepared him for the guards at the counting house doors.

They were clerks. That was the horror of it—men with ink-stained fingers and the pallor of people who worked by lamplight, holding pikes and shortswords, and fighting like the damned. Because they *were*, he realized, cutting low and backing away rather than pressing in. Each of them had sold Corvane years. Each of them was spending those years right now, burning life like lamp-oil, and every second Emeric delayed was a second they could feel leaving them.

"Fall back!" he shouted to the freed clerks behind him. "Don't kill them—they're dying fast enough!"

A guard came at him with a pike and tears streaming down his face. "You don't understand," the man gasped as Emeric caught the shaft and twisted. "He'll trade our *children*. He showed us the entries. Our children are collateral. You can't—"

"I know," Emeric said, and drove him down gently, unconscious rather than dead. "I know what collateral means in that building. I was mine."

The counting house doors burst inward on the third ram-strike, and the hall beyond opened like the inside of a great heart: vaulted ceiling ribbed with black oak, forty empty desks arranged in perfect rows, and at the far end, on a dais that had not been there in any of Wren's descriptions, the master ledger itself—huge as a coffin lid, chained shut, its pages glowing faintly from within like something alive and dreaming.

Corvane's voice rolled down from somewhere above, from the gate-chamber stairs, amplified by the building itself so that it seemed to come from the walls, the floor, the ribs of the roof.

"Wren Joubert." Almost fond. "You brought guests to my accounting."

Emeric fought toward the gate-chamber door because someone had to hold the stairs, and because his father—his father's *body*, the thing wearing his father's crown—was somewhere above it, and because grief that cannot speak finds work instead. The freed clerks flowed past him into the hall, and here was the thing he would remember all his life: they did not go for the ledger first. They went for the desks. Forty clerks pulling their old chairs close, sitting down at their old stations, opening drawers, finding pens, as if the most radical act imaginable was simply to do their jobs honestly, in the open, for themselves.

One of them—a young man Emeric vaguely recognized, hollow-cheeked, with the particular stillness of someone who had been traded through a gate and returned—climbed onto the dais and put his hands flat on the master ledger's chains.

"This is mine," the young man said, to Corvane, to the building, to everyone. "You wrote my name in here when I was nine. You wrote my mother's death as a *transaction fee*. Tonight I'm writing back."

He tore the chains free with his bare hands, and the skin of his palms split, and he did not stop, and Emeric understood that some prices already paid buy more than their purchasers ever know.

* * *

**Wren**

Wren reached the master ledger as the chains fell, and the sight of it open nearly undid her.

Every entry she had ever made was there, but *annotated*—her neat columns surrounded by Corvane's marginalia in that careful, reasonable hand she had once admired. Beside the shadow-trade from her first unsettling night: *Necessary. One shadow purchases forty years of the boy's productivity. Efficient.* Beside the debtor lists: *Sad, but the alternative is worse. See annex: the city, whole.* Page after page of monstrous arithmetic justified in the tone of a man balancing a household budget.

At the bottom of the current page, the live account pulsed: *Payments received, this night: 214 souls. Balance owing to the Gate: deferred. Term: one year.*

One year. All those vanished streets, bought him twelve months.

Her pen was in her hand before she decided anything. It had always been in her hand; that was the whole of her.

She struck the line through. Not erased—struck, in the old way, the way honest clerks marked a voided entry, so the record showed both the debt and its cancellation. She wrote beneath it in letters deep enough to scar three pages down:

*Account closed by authority of the Ledger-Keeper. Payment refused. The Gate holds nothing. The Gate is owed nothing.*

The ledger screamed. There was no other word. The pages thrashed beneath her hands like netted fish, and the glow guttered, and somewhere far below them the sealed gate felt the severance and began—she felt it in her teeth, in the marrow of the building—to come awake.

Above, through the iron door of the gate-chamber, came a sound she had heard only once before, ten years ago, on the worst night of her apprenticeship: the slow, deliberate grinding of a lock turning from the inside.

"Wren!" Tamsin, bursting onto the dais, the prophecy-book open and blazing with its own old light. "It's time. The reader holds, the heir climbs—but the keeper must stand at the ledger when the name is spoken, or none of it binds!"

Emeric was already on the stairs, sword drawn, face wet, going up toward the thing that wore his father.

And the door at the top of the stairs began, softly, patiently, to open—and from the darkness beyond it, in a voice like dry paper and patient hunger, something spoke her name. Not the name the Guild knew. Not even the name she had lost.

The name her mother had used, the one no living soul remembered.

The gate had been keeping that, too.
