## 19. Unbinding

The gate-chamber had no right to be this cold.

Emeric felt it through his boots, through the leather of his father's old riding gloves—gloves he'd stolen from a pawnshop in Saltmarket eleven years ago and never once worn until tonight. The cold came from beneath the floor, rising like water filling a hull, and it carried a smell he knew from childhood: candle smoke in a closed room, ink, and something sweeter underneath that his body remembered before his mind did.

The king's bedchamber. The last night before the exile.

"Stand on the sigil." Tamsin's voice was steady, but her hands were not. She held the prophecy-book open against her chest like a shield, its pages fluttering though there was no wind. "Both of you. The binding was laid in three parts—word, witness, and will. We have to give it all three back."

Wren moved first. She always moved first now; Emeric had noticed that about her over the past weeks, the way she stepped into danger the way she must once have stepped into rows of figures, as if everything were simply an entry waiting to be recorded. She took her place at the western point of the sigil burned into the flagstones—a circle of interlocking ledgers, each page rendered in stone, each line of text a groove deep enough to catch blood or candlewax or a man's heel.

He took the eastern point. Opposite her. Between them, the door.

* * *

It did not look like a door anymore.

When Emeric had first seen it, days ago, in the service tunnels with Wren's lamp shaking in her grip, it had been iron and oak, bound in bands of black metal, sealed with a sigil that hurt to look at directly. Now the wood had gone grey as ash. Now the sigil pulsed. And now, as they stood into position, the whole surface breathed—once, slow, like the flank of a sleeping animal—and he heard it speak.

Not words. Not yet. A sound like many voices under water, like a choir heard through the wall of a drowned church. It said his name. It said his father's name. It said Wren's name, and Tamsin's, and names he didn't know—hundreds, thousands, a ledger of the eaten recited to itself in the dark.

"Don't listen to it," Wren said quietly. "It collects. That's all listening is, to it—an opening."

"It knows my voice," Emeric said.

"It knows everyone's voice." Her eyes were red-rimmed. He'd watched her refuse the entity's bargain hours ago, watched it offer her a mother she couldn't remember, and she had said no with tears running down her face and her spine straight as a ledger-line. "That's how it hunts. That's how Corvane fed it for thirty years—one familiar voice at a time."

Above them, somewhere in the guildhall, the fighting went on. He could hear it muffled through stone: the crash of the counting-house doors, clerks' voices, the freed debtors moving through corridors that had finally stopped rearranging themselves. The Guild was dying tonight. He could feel it in the walls, a great slow shudder, like a beast realizing the hand that fed it meant to cut its throat.

"Tamsin," he said. "Read."

* * *

She read.

Her voice started small and grew, the way fire takes a room. The prophecy's language was older than Ashvale itself, full of sounds that seemed to bend the air around them, and as she spoke the sigil began to wake. The grooved lines filled with light—not gold, not white, but a colour Emeric had no name for, the colour of a memory you can almost reach. The circle brightened. The cold deepened.

And the shadow came.

It poured out of the gate's seam like smoke finding a draught—but wrong, inverted, a darkness that gave off rather than took. It spread across the floor between them, thin as spilled ink at first, then thickening, gathering, until it stood: the shape of a woman, roughly drawn, edges trembling. Marta Veyne, who had sold her shadow for her son's life, and whose shadow had spent weeks learning what the thing behind the door had learned when it swallowed her.

The entity screamed.

There was no other word for it. The choir under the water became a single vast voice, and the voice became rage, and the rage shook dust from the ceiling and cracked one of the ledger-stones clean through. *THIEF*, it said, and the word landed in Emeric's chest like a blow. *MY LEDGER. MY ENTRIES. WHAT IS FED TO ME IS MINE.*

"Not yours." Tamsin didn't flinch. Emeric marveled at that—at the woman who had been locked in a cell for reading aloud, standing now in front of a god-thing and reading louder. "Nothing was ever given freely. That's the flaw in every entry you hold. Coerced consent. Stolen payment. You are nothing but bad bookkeeping, and I am here to audit you."

The shadow raised its head. It had no face—not truly—but Emeric felt it look at him, and in that looking he saw what Wren had promised him it would show: images, memories, the entity's own hoarded plunder turned inside out.

He saw a hundred years ago.

He saw a throne room lit by tallow candles, and a king kneeling—not before a priest, but before a circle of men in merchant's robes, the founders of the Guild. He saw his father—younger, whole, wearing a crown of simple silver—and he saw them take the crown and set upon it a working so old and so foul that the air around it blackened. He saw them bind the king's soul down into the deepest cellar of the newest building in the city, and he saw what climbed up out of that cellar wearing the king's body, smiling the king's smile, signing the king's decrees.

And he saw his mother.

He hadn't expected that. The shadow gave it to him without warning—the queen, alive, furious, standing in these same tunnels with a knife she never used because they didn't need to kill her. They traded her instead. Her years, her name, her place in every record and memory, poured into the gate to buy silence for the binding. She hadn't died of fever in the northern provinces, as the court histories claimed. There were no court histories. There was no fever. There was only a woman erased so thoroughly that even her son had grown up unable to recall her face, loving a grief he couldn't name.

"Mama," he said, and the word broke in half.

The shadow reached toward him. Its touch was cold and gentle and smelled of salt markets at dawn, and Emeric understood: it wasn't the entity showing him this. It was the shadow. Marta Veyne's stolen darkness, carrying what the devourer could not digest—truth. The thing behind the door ate names and years and shadows, but it could never digest testimony. It could only store it. And stored things, Tamsin had told him, could be called as witnesses.

"The record stands," Tamsin said, and her voice rang against the stones. "The binding is witnessed. Now, Emeric—"

"Now," he agreed, and stepped to the center of the sigil.

* * *

The entity stopped raging.

That was worse, somehow. The vast voice gathered itself, compressed, and when it spoke again it wore a shape he knew better than his own heartbeat.

*"Emeric."*

His father's voice. Warm, wry, tired in the way kings get tired—with the weight of other people's lives. He had heard it last when he was fourteen years old, from a horse, in a courtyard, saying *go north, boy, go north and live*. Eleven years he had built himself around that voice. Eleven years of telling himself his father still lived, still reigned, still loved him across the distance, if only the ministers and the wars and the distance itself could be overcome.

"Come home," the entity said, in his father's voice, through the mouth-shaped dark of the gate. "Open the door, and we'll go home together. I've missed you. I've watched you sell fish, boy. I've watched you grow into a man who haggles honestly and feeds the gulls. Come home, and no one will ever make you leave again."

"That's not my father."

"It is the closest thing you will ever hold again."

"I know," Emeric said, and found, to his surprise, that he was weeping without any drama at all—tears simply falling, the way rain falls, while his voice stayed level. "That's exactly what I know. And I'm done choosing the closest thing."

Behind the entity's voice, faint as a candle behind a shuttered window, something else flickered. Something small. Wren saw it too—he watched her see it—and she nodded once, barely.

"He's still in there," she whispered. "The real king. Caged. He's been caged a hundred years, Emeric. He's so tired. But he heard your voice just now. I saw the cage move."

Emeric knelt at the center of the sigil. The light rose around him like water. Somewhere above, the guildhall groaned; somewhere below, the entity pressed against its door; somewhere in his chest, a boy who had sold fish for eleven years under a false name stood up straight for the first time since the courtyard, since the horse, since *go north and live*.

Tamsin turned to the final page. "Speak his name," she said, "the true one, the one they bound him by, and the binding breaks."

"But the vessel—" His throat closed. They had told him. Wren had made him say it aloud, days ago, in the salt-smell of the safehouse: unbinding would free the soul, and the body that had hosted the devourer for a century would fail. Even freed, even restored, his father would have minutes. Perhaps less. "If I say it, he dies."

"If you don't," Wren said softly, "he dies anyway, alone, in the dark, wearing a monster. At least this way—" her voice caught, steadied— "at least this way someone writes the ending honestly."

Emeric looked at the gate. Past the entity's borrowed face, past the rage and the hunger and the terrible patience of a thing that had ruled a kingdom by wearing its king—he looked for the small light behind the shutters.

And he said the name.

Not *Aldric*. Not *His Majesty, King Aldric III of the Ashvale Throne*. The true name, the hearth-name, the one his mother used to call across a garden when supper cooled, the one the binding had been laid upon because a soul answers fastest to the name love gives it.

"*Alley.*"

* * *

The world went white.

No—the world went *true*. That was the only way he could ever describe it after. Colour flooded back into the grey chamber; sound returned with a rush like surf; and the gate did not explode, did not burn, but simply *opened*—swung wide on hinges that had waited a hundred years, and what came through was not the devourer.

What came through was a king.

Small. That was the unbearable part. The soul that emerged from the gate was small as a child, luminous as dawn, and it stumbled into the sigil's light looking around with eyes that had not seen anything but the inside of a cage for a hundred years. Around it, shrieking, the entity unraveled—stripped of its stolen shape, its borrowed voice, its century of careful feeding, until all that remained was what Tamsin had promised it was all along:

A crown.

Just a crown, spinning slowly in the air, gold and hollow, turning on nothing, wanting a head. It lunged for Emeric. It lunged for Wren. It found no purchase—every name in this room was spoken, witnessed, written—and with a sound like a coin dropped down an endless well, it fell, and the sigil drank it, and the gate began to close.

Emeric ran to the small light and it ran to him, and for one impossible moment his arms held both things at once: the weightless blaze of his father's freed soul, and the sudden, mortal heaviness of his father's body, which appeared as the soul entered it—appearing out of nothing, out of the entity's last stolen shape, collapsing onto the stones of the gate-chamber as an old man in a plain shirt.

An old man. His father had vanished at forty-one. The body that lay gasping in Emeric's arms was ninety-three, because the devourer had spent the king's years the way it spent everything.

"Papa." The word came out in the child's language, the garden language, the one no one had spoken to this man in a hundred years. "Papa, I've got you. I've got you."

His father's eyes opened. They were clouded and clear at once, the way eyes are at the very end, seeing two worlds. They found Emeric's face, and the king smiled—not the court smile, not the smile from coins and portraits, but the crooked private one from the garden, the one Emeric had spent eleven years failing to forget.

"There you are," Aldric rasped. "North. You went north."

"I went north. I lived. Papa—I'm sorry, I'm sorry it took so long—"

"Took?" His father laughed, a ruin of a laugh, wet and delighted. "Boy. I heard you selling fish. Worst haggler in the market. Let a woman cheat you twice for eels." His hand, feather-light, gripped Emeric's sleeve with a ghost of the old strength. "I listened every day. A hundred years is long. But you made it shorter. You made it—"

The breath failed. Came back. Failed again.

"Your mother," the king whispered. "You remember her?"

Emeric bowed his head over his father's hands. "I do now," he said. "The shadow showed me. She fought them, Papa. She fought them to the last."

Something eased in the old face, some debt paid, some terror ended. "Then we were both—both in the ledger. Both kept." His gaze drifted past Emeric's shoulder, to where Wren stood with tears streaming silently down her face, and Tamsin with the open book cradled like a sleeping bird. "Who keeps the books now?"

"She does," Emeric said. "The honest one."

"Good." The king's eyes were closing. "Good. Then write it true. Write that the king—write that Alley Joubert's boy came home—"

He didn't finish. He didn't need to. He died the way the tide goes out—slowly, gently, and entirely himself, his hand in his son's hands, his face at peace, wearing no crown at all.

Emeric held him as the light faded from the sigil. Held him as the gate ground shut behind them, stone sliding over stone, the hungry dark sealed away at last. Held him as Tamsin sank to her knees and Wren stood unmoving, watching, memorizing—because that was what she did, he understood suddenly. That was what she had always done. Someone had to keep the record. Someone always had.

Above them, muffled and distant, a bell began to ring—the counting-house bell, rung by freed hands, tolling the end of the Guild.

Wren touched his shoulder. When he looked up, her face was pale with something new, something he had never seen her wear in all their weeks of flight and fury.

"Emeric," she said quietly. "There's one more entry left. And it needs your name on it."
