## 1. Chapter 1

The king's supper arrived on silver that had been polished so many times it had begun to forget it was ever ore.

Wendell Yarrow stood at her station beside the high table's shadow, hands folded, face arranged into the mild attentiveness expected of a royal taster. The great hall of Caerlum rose around her in pale limestone and ironwork, every arch carved with the same admonition repeated in the old tongue: *that which is unmade cannot be unmade again*. The Edict of Unmaking, rendered in stone for those too slow to read the placards. No magic. No charms. No workings of any kind within the city's walls, on pain of the quiet rooms beneath the inquisitors' hall, from which people did not so much emerge as stop.

The soup was first, as always. A footman lifted the lid with ceremonial slowness, releasing a breath of leek and cream and something beneath it — green, sharp, faintly sweet, like crushed stems.

Wendell stepped forward, took the tasting spoon, and let the liquid sit on her tongue for the count of five.

There. At the very end, under the dairy fat, a whisper of bitterness shaped like an almond's ghost. She swallowed, set the spoon down, and waited. One hundred heartbeats. Two hundred. The hall watched her with the bored patience of people who had seen this ritual ten thousand times and hoped, perhaps once, to see it go wrong.

Nothing came. Of course nothing came.

"Foxglove," she said to the steward, quietly, the way one remarks on the weather. "Extracted in wine, I'd think, to cut the bitterness. Crude work. Whoever sent it wanted it found."

The steward's mouth tightened. He signaled, and the soup went out to the dogs, and somewhere in the kitchens a boy of perhaps fifteen began to weep before anyone had even accused him. Wendell felt the familiar small ache open behind her ribs — not guilt, exactly. Guilt would have required her to have done something. This was older than guilt: the dull grief of watching the machinery of suspicion grind, knowing she could not stop it and had long ago stopped trying.

The second course was venison in a dark sauce. She tasted it. Nothing but juniper and salt and the long pepper the crown imported at ruinous expense. The third course, a custard, carried a trace of bitter almond again — someone in the kitchens was recycling their poisons, which spoke either of laziness or desperation, and Wendell made a mental note to mention it to no one, because mentioning things got kitchen boys taken to the quiet rooms.

By the fourth course her body had settled into its evening complaint.

It always did this. Twelve years of swallowing death by appointment had built her into a fortress, yes, and fortresses were cold inside. The immunity the court prized — the reason a taster could survive foxglove and hemlock and arsenic and the blue-ringed venom of the marsh eel, all before the fish course — had not come free. It had been purchased drop by drop, year by year, each dose a little larger, each recovery a little slower, until her blood itself had become something other than blood. Her mentor, old Master Corvane, had described it once as paying taxes to your own survival. Then he had died of a dose he swore he'd measured correctly, and Wendell had learned that even fortresses fell, given time and sufficient patience on the part of the besiegers.

Now the toll was constant. A ringing in her ears like distant bells. A metallic taste that no amount of honeyed water washed away. Her fingertips, numb at the edges, so that she read the world through texture rather than touch. And beneath all of it, deep in the marrow, a low continuous ache — the sound of a body doing arithmetic, calculating what tonight's doses would cost tomorrow.

She kept her face pleasant. That was the first rule of tasting, Corvane had said. Not courage. Not vigilance. *Pleasantness.* A taster who flinched taught the court to be afraid, and a court that was afraid found new things to fear, and fear in Caerlum always ended the same way: with someone being asked what they had hidden, and no answer being good enough.

So Wendell smiled at the venison. She pronounced the custard excellent. She drank the king's wine, all three cups, and felt the foxglove ghost of the soup finally dissolve into her armored blood like a debt quietly forgiven.

*Twelve years,* she thought, watching the candlelight swim. *And what do you own that isn't poisoned?*

* * *

The palace released her at half past nine, as the servants' bells rang the hour when honest folk banked their fires.

Caerlum after dark was a different city wearing the same bones. By day it was white limestone and proclamation boards, the Edict carved over every gate, inquisitors in grey walking the streets with the unhurried confidence of men who had never once been wrong in their own estimation. By night, when the fog rolled up off the river and the watch retreated to the lit thoroughfares, another city woke underneath — the night market, three streets of stalls in the old tannery quarter where the smell of lime pits still haunted the cobbles and no one looked too closely at anyone else's hands.

Wendell changed her clothes before she left the palace grounds. The grey servant's livery went into her satchel; out came a plain brown coat, a scarf, and the small canvas roll she wore strapped against her spine like a second vertebra. In the roll were thirty-one luck-knots, tied from red thread and horsehair and — this was the secret, though she'd have denied it under torture — nothing else at all. Thread. Hair. Patience. And the smallest, most deniable scrap of working, the kind of magic so minor that Wendell herself sometimes doubted it qualified as magic at all, which was precisely why it slipped past the Edict like water through fingers.

A luck-knot didn't summon anything. It didn't transform anything. It only *leaned*. It leaned on the odds the way a friend leans on a doorframe — casually, persistently, until the odds tilted. A fisherman's knot might mean one more catch per week. A mother's knot, sewn into a child's collar, might mean fever passing by the house instead of through it. Nothing anyone could point to. Nothing anyone could prove.

Which was fortunate, because proof was the whole architecture of the Edict. You couldn't hang a woman for thread.

The tannery quarter received her with its usual fog and murmur. Lanterns hung from stall-poles on wires strung between leaning buildings, and the light they cast was the color of weak tea. Wendell took her corner — the one between the bone-button seller and the widow who sold eels — and laid out her stock on a cloth the color of dust: knots, headache charms (copper discs, hammered thin, worked with a pattern that encouraged blood to flow away from pain), sleep-salves, a jar of fever-tallow. Prices chalked low. Lower than she needed, honestly. The palace paid her well to risk her life at dinner, and she had never learned how to spend money on herself, so she spent it here instead, on red thread and copper and the small outlawed kindnesses of strangers.

Her first customer came before she'd finished arranging the cloth: a dockhand with a hand wrapped in filthy linen, asking for something for the swelling. Wendell unwrapped the hand, clucked at the state of it, cleaned it with spirits, and sold him a poultice and a knot to tie at his wrist — *for the ache*, she said, *the rest is just good dressing*, which was true in the way most of her sentences were true: technically, and not at all in spirit.

Then the fishermen. Then a girl of maybe nineteen, pregnant, terrified, buying a birth-charm with coins counted out twice. Then an old man who wanted a knot for his wife's memory, which was going, and which no charm of Wendell's could hold — but she sold him one anyway, and told him to talk to her, talk to her every day, because the hearing lasted longest, and the old man held the knot like it was made of glass and thanked her four times.

This was the part no one warned you about, Wendell thought, tying a new knot while she waited — fingers moving in patterns her teacher had shown her, patterns older than the Edict, older probably than Caerlum. This was the part: that outlawed kindness felt more real than legal kindness. At the palace she was furniture that swallowed poison. Here, in the fog and the eel-smell, she was a person. Every charm she sold was a small act of treason against a law she had never believed in, and every transaction was also, if she were honest, a transaction against her own loneliness. People needed her here. They said her name. They came back.

At the palace, no one had spoken her name unprompted in years.

* * *

Isolde came at the far edge of midnight, as she always did, when the market was thinning and the lanterns burned low.

Wendell saw her before she saw the archivist's face — saw the particular way Isolde walked, unhurried, head slightly tilted as though the world were a document she was still reading. Isolde Amberdale was tall where Wendell was compact, pale where Wendell was weather-brown, and moved through the night market like someone visiting a foreign country she intended, eventually, to learn the language of. She was the royal archive's junior cataloguer, keeper of restricted folios, a woman whose family name opened doors in Caerlum that Wendell's could not have knocked upon. None of that mattered here. Here she was just the woman who brought bread.

"You're late," Wendell said, not looking up from her knot.

"The archive was locked down. An inventory of restricted holdings." Isolde set a wrapped loaf on the cloth, next to the luck-knots, with the seriousness of a diplomatic offering. "Inquisitors requested access to the sealed collections. Hale himself."

"Hale." Wendell's fingers paused on the thread. "What does Hale want with sealed books? He can barely tolerate printed ones."

"He wants everything, lately. That's what frightens the senior clerks." Isolde sat down on the upturned crate that served as Wendell's second chair, arranging her skirts with the automatic care of someone raised in a house with rules. Up close, in the lantern light, Wendell could see the tiredness under her eyes, the ink stain on her left ring finger that never quite washed out. "He asked about prophecy holdings specifically. Old ones. Why does an inquisitor want prophecies, Wendell?"

"Because the Edict says the future isn't permitted to happen without a license," Wendell said, and was rewarded with the laugh she'd been fishing for — quick, low, quickly suppressed, the laugh Isolde saved for places where laughing was dangerous. It was, Wendell thought, the best sound in either of the cities, the day city or the night one. It made the ache in her marrow recede by some measurable degree. It always did. She had stopped examining that fact too closely, the way one stops counting a lucky coin.

They ate the bread together, tearing pieces from the loaf, passing a flask of cidered water back and forth. Around them the market wound down: the eel-widow banking her coals, the bone-button seller counting his tray. Somewhere a fiddle started up two streets over and gave up almost immediately.

"I sold a birth-charm tonight," Wendell said. "To a girl named Sefa. If you ever meet a girl named Sefa with a baby that sleeps through the night, act surprised."

"I'm always surprised," Isolde said. "That's my whole personality. Ask anyone in the restricted stacks." She turned the luck-knot over in her long fingers — the one Wendell had just finished, still warm from her hands. "Red thread again?"

"Red is for keeping things. Blood-color. Things want to stay near what they recognize."

"That's not in any of the texts."

"The texts don't know everything."

"No," Isolde agreed softly. "They really don't. That's the trouble." And there was something in her voice then, a hairline fracture under the words, that made Wendell look up properly. Isolde was watching the lantern flame, and her expression had gone somewhere Wendell couldn't follow — somewhere archival and careful, a face used to handling fragile things deciding whether it was holding one.

"Isolde."

"Mm."

"What is it?"

For a moment — Wendell would remember the length of it later, and curse its brevity — Isolde seemed about to say something. Her mouth opened. The lantern guttered in a draft from the river. And whatever it was, whatever had risen in her like a bubble through deep water, she swallowed it, and smiled, and said:

"Nothing. Inventory week. Everything feels like it's staring back at me."

Wendell knew better than to press. That was another thing twelve years had taught her: when to taste and when to wait, when a flavor needed time to declare itself. So she reached across the cloth and tucked the finished luck-knot into Isolde's hair, above her ear, where the red thread caught the light like a small deliberate wound.

"For keeping things," Wendell said.

Isolde's hand rose to touch it. "You're going to get us both taken to the quiet rooms someday."

"Not someday. I've been getting us taken there daily for three years. We're very experienced at not being arrested."

And Isolde laughed again, and the market closed around them like a book, and neither of them knew — how could they know — that in a sealed folio in the restricted stacks, in a margin of vellum a century dead, a pen was already dry that had already written Isolde's face among the brides of the court beneath the mountain.

The moon, that night over the tanneries, was a coin worn nearly smooth.

Eleven turns gone. One to spend.
