## 2. Chapter 2

The night market woke the way a fever wakes: slowly, then all at once.

Wendell felt it before she saw it, a tremor in the cobbles beneath the Tanners' Bridge, a hum that traveled up through her boot soles and settled somewhere behind her sternum. By day, Caerlum's streets belonged to salt and law and the Edict of Unmaking, its stones scrubbed so pale they seemed to apologize for themselves. But after the ninth bell, when the lamplighters had made their rounds and the inquisitors' patrols turned toward their barracks and their suppers, the city remembered what it had been before the Edict. The market rose out of the alleys like breath on cold glass — there, and then not, and no record of it anywhere but in the hands of the people who needed it.

She came down through the tannery district with her hood up and her satchel against her ribs, past the lime pits breathing their sour white stink into the dark, past the vats where hides steeped in oak bark and iron. The smell was a mercy, in its way. It clung to everything here — hair, wool, memory — and it buried the other smell she carried: the faint green tang of working charms, which no honest nose could name but every guilty one could fear.

Twelve years of tasting had given her a catalog of smells that meant danger. Arsenic was garlic and rotting apples. Belladonna was sweet, treacherously so, like wine left too long in the sun. Hemlock smelled of mouse nests. The night market smelled of none of these things. It smelled of tallow smoke and wet wool and frying onions from Maren's cart, and under it all, faint as a hymn heard through a wall, the hum of small magics being made by people who should have been afraid to make them.

Wendell was one of those people. She had been for six years now, since the winter she'd discovered that the same hands which held poison twice a day could also hold a knot of thread and pull luck into it like drawing water up a well.

Her stall — if three planks balanced across two barrels deserved the word — stood in the shadow of the bridge's second arch, where the lamplight didn't quite reach. She set out her wares with the economy of long practice: a shallow basket of luck-knots, each one tied from red-dyed cord, seven turns, the last turn left loose so the luck had somewhere to enter; a row of headache charms stitched onto scraps of linen, little flat diamonds of willow bark and feverfew sewn inside with a word murmured over them that wasn't a word at all, just a shape she made with her mouth and meant with her whole chest; jars of salve for burns and chilblains; twistings of dried mugwort for women whose courses came cruel or not at all.

None of it was grand. That was the point, and the danger, and the strange pride of it. The Edict of Unmaking did not concern itself with greatness — great magic had died screaming a century ago, burned out of the world in the Reclamation, and no one in Caerlum could raise it if they tried. What the Edict hunted was this: the knot, the murmur, the charm sewn into a hem. Small magics. Kitchen magics. The kind that kept a fisherman's nets mended and a mother's fever down and a widow's rent paid one silver at a time. The kind the crown had decided, in its wisdom, that no one needed anymore.

*Unmaking*, they called it. As though a luck-knot were a wound.

Wendell arranged her charms in their rows and felt the familiar double pulse in her blood: the lift of it, the guilt of it. Both at once, always both. It was like tasting the king's supper and finding it delicious — the pleasure itself the transgression.

The first customer came before the market had fully filled, an old netmender named Sorrel who smelled of pitch and river mud.

"Wendell." Sorrel's voice was a gravel-whisper. She glanced over both shoulders with the thoroughness of the truly frightened, then again, because once is never enough. "Two knots tonight. And a headache charm if you've got one."

"Head's bad?"

"Like a nail driven backward." Sorrel laid coins on the plank — copper, worn smooth, counted twice. "My granddaughter sits her weaving trial next week. I want her hands steady and her mind clear and something watching over her that isn't just me praying."

Wendell chose the knots carefully, feeling each cord for the weight of what she'd tied into it. "This one for calm," she said, pressing the first into Sorrel's palm. "This one for steadiness. Tell her to hold it in her left hand while she works. Left hand's nearer the heart."

"And the charm?"

"Under the pillow. If the head still aches by Thursday, come back and I'll re-stitch it. No charge."

Sorrel's face did something complicated — gratitude and grief tangled together, the look Wendell knew best in all the world, the look of someone receiving a kindness they'd been taught to be ashamed of needing. "You're a fool, girl," Sorrel said softly. "A good fool. You know what they'd do to you."

"I know what they do to everyone," Wendell said. "I'd rather be hanged for the knots than live clean and let your granddaughter shake her way through the trial."

It sounded braver than it felt. It always did. The truth was that she sold charms for coin as much as kindness — a royal food-taster's wage was better than a dockhand's but worse than comfort, and there was Isolde to think of now, dinners and small gifts and the dizzy extravagance of courting someone. But standing there with Sorrel's copper warming her palm, Wendell let herself believe the finer version for a moment. Belief, like luck, could be tied into a thing if you held it long enough.

* * *

The market thickened around her as the night deepened. Wendell worked her stall and watched the crowd move, and the crowd was Caerlum's other body, the one it grew only after dark: fishermen whose boats the sea had cheated, laundresses with knuckles cracked raw from lye, a bricklayer missing two fingers, a boy of maybe twelve selling stolen candle stubs, a woman in a fine cloak gone shabby at the cuffs who bought a luck-knot without meeting Wendell's eyes. Everyone wanted something small. Everyone wanted it desperately. Desperation, Wendell had learned, was the true currency of the night market; the coin merely stood in for it.

She tied knots between customers, her fingers moving through the seven turns without her mind's help. Seven turns, the last left loose. Her grandmother had taught her the rhyme that went with it, half-nonsense, half-instruction:

*One for the want, and two for the way,*
*three for the work of the waking day,*
*four for the wind that finds the crack,*
*five for the road that won't lead back,*
*six for the debt, and six for the due,*
*and the seventh turn loose for the luck to come through.*

Her grandmother had sung it over bread dough and fishing lines and the cradle of every child on their street, back when such things were merely old rather than criminal. Wendell sang it silently now, her lips barely moving, and felt the cords warm slightly under her fingers — or imagined she did. After twelve years of poisons, a body learned to distrust its own reports.

That was the other inheritance the tasting had left her. Not just the hardened gut, the tongue that read a broth like a priest reads scripture, the stomach lining calloused against half the apothecary's shelf. It was this: a permanent, low-grade doubt about sensation itself. She tasted almond and could not simply taste almond; she tasted almond and asked it questions. Where from. How old. What are you hiding. The night market demanded a similar discipline. Every warmth in a cord might be real or wishful. Every flicker at the edge of vision might be a warding-sign or a lantern guttering. She had built her livelihood on the difference, and she trusted it the way sailors trust a chart drawn by a dead man.

Maren came by with fried onions wrapped in paper and gossip bundled tighter underneath.

"Inquisitors were at the Weftgate end last night," Maren said, too casually, handing over the onions and taking nothing in return, which from Maren was a warning in itself. "Not raiding. Buying."

Wendell kept her eyes on her cords. "Buying what?"

"What do you think? Charms. Luck-knots. A headache charm off old Piotr, paid him double what it was worth." Maren wiped her hands on her apron, a gesture Wendell had never seen her make nervously before. "Paid him double, Wendell. When did anyone in a gray coat pay double for anything?"

The onions had gone cold in their paper. Wendell ate one anyway, mechanically, tasting it the way she tasted everything: onion, oil, salt, and beneath it, faintly, the ghost of fear. Fear had a flavor. She'd found it in the king's courtiers' sweat more than once, thin and metallic, like a spoon left in the mouth too long.

"They're building a case," she said.

"They're building *something*. Hale's men, some of them. You know the one. Tall, patient, smiles like he's doing you a favor." Maren leaned closer. "Piotr sold them his whole stock afterward, every charm he had left, swore he was done with the life. And here's the thing — they let him keep the money. Didn't arrest him. Just wrote things in a little book."

Wrote things in a little book. Wendell's hands went still over the seventh turn. An inquisitor who arrested was a storm; you ran from storms, you boarded up, you survived. But an inquisitor who *bought*, who paid fairly and recorded patiently, was a ledger. Ledgers didn't rage. They accumulated. Somewhere in a gray office above the Salt Court there was a page growing heavier with names, and every charm sold tonight was a line added to it in a fair, unhurried hand.

"You should pack up early," Maren said. "Just tonight."

"I will."

"You won't." Maren sighed, and it turned into almost a smile. "I'll leave the extra onions anyway. Call it a bribe for good sense you don't have."

* * *

They came near midnight, and they came shopping.

Wendell marked them the moment they entered the market, the way her tongue marked arsenic: not by any single feature but by the whole pattern refusing to fit. Two figures in undyed wool, cloaks plain as penance, moving through the crowd with the wrong rhythm — stall to stall, unhurried, asking questions with their heads tilted at the angle of men listening for something. Honest buyers haggled. These two nodded at every price.

Her hands did not stop tying the seventh turn. Twelve years at the king's table had taught her that the deadliest response to poison was to swallow it smoothly and smile. Panic was a flavor. Never let them taste panic.

The taller of the two drifted toward her stall with the aimless gait of a man browsing, and stopped, and smiled, and Wendell recognized him even before he spoke: Inquisitor Hale, or near enough his likeness that her skin crawled with confirmation. She'd seen him at court, at a distance, at functions where her role required her to stand against the wall with the other servants and taste nothing but boredom. He had a face designed to be forgotten — pleasant, unremarkable, forgettable as water — and eyes that ruined the design entirely, eyes that looked at things the way an appraiser looks at goods.

"Luck-knots?" he said. "What's the going price these days?"

"Depends what you want it for," Wendell said. Her voice came out level, market-warm, exactly as bored as a lawful seller's should be. "Cord's red. Knots are seven turns. Last one's loose."

"Loose? Doesn't that spoil it?"

"That's where the luck gets in." She said it lightly, the way she'd say it to any customer, and watched him write nothing down, and understood suddenly that he would remember it anyway. Men like Hale didn't need notebooks for everything. Some things they carried in their heads, filed under faces.

He picked up a knot, turned it over. His fingers were careful, scholarly. "My sister's ill," he said. "Fever. I thought — some folk swear by these."

"Some folk do." Wendell selected one, her calmest knot, tied for ease and sleep and nothing that would interest a man reading evidence. "For fever, hold it while you sit with her. Won't cure anything. Just keeps the hands busy so the heart doesn't wear itself out."

Something moved behind his forgettable face — approval, perhaps, or appetite. He paid her full price in clean silver, thanked her, wished her a dry night, and drifted on toward the next stall, and Wendell stood in the shadow of the arch with his silver in her palm and her heart beating slow and enormous, like a drum struck underwater.

She had just sold a charm to the inquisition. She had taken their money. Her hands were in their ledger now, tied into it as surely as the seventh turn, and the worst part — the part that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone — was that the knot she'd given him was *good*. Whatever game he was playing, whoever his sister was or wasn't, the knot would do what knots did. Her kindness had gone into it. It would work on him. She had charmed the wolf and meant it.

The market thinned around her. She packed her planks and barrels with hands that stayed steady because she ordered them to, the way she ordered them to lift the king's cup, and she thought about Piotr, paid double and spared, written into a little book. She thought about how the crown's justice had learned patience the way she had learned immunity: one small dose at a time, until the thing that should have killed was merely part of the blood.

By the time she reached the Tanners' Bridge, the market was dissolving behind her — stalls folding, lamps dying, the whole bright fever of it fading back into ordinary stone — and ahead, up the hill, the archive district lay dark and quiet, where a light still burned late in a third-floor window because a certain archivist never trusted anyone else to reshelve the evening's returns.

Isolde. The thought arrived the way it always did now, unbidden and warm, and for one moment it burned through the dread like brandy through a chill. Wendell climbed toward that window through the cold, rehearsing how she'd say it — *someone bought charms from me tonight, someone with gray-coat eyes, and I took his silver because refusing would have been its own confession* — and rehearsing, too, how Isolde would listen, head tilted, one finger marking her place in whatever folio lay open, and say something precise and unexpected that would make the fear manageable, the way she made everything manageable just by looking at it squarely.

The lamp in the third-floor window went out as Wendell watched.

Then, after a pause — deliberate, unmistakable — it lit again. Twice. Three times. Their signal, invented a month ago over stolen wine: *come up, we're alone.*

Wendell stood in the dark street below with an inquisitor's silver in her pocket and her lover's signal burning above her, and felt the night split cleanly in two: the life below, outlawed and warm, and the life above, where the archives kept every secret Caerlum had ever tried to bury.

She went up the stairs two at a time, and neither life knew yet what was waiting for them both in the shelves.
