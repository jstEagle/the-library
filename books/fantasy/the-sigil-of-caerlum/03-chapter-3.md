## 3. Chapter 3

The restricted archive smelled of iron and cold candlewax, a smell Isolde Amberdale had come to think of as the smell of other people's secrets.

She worked alone on the fourth sublevel of the Royal Athenaeum, three turns of the stair below the reading rooms where sunlight was permitted and scholars were trusted. Down here there were no windows, only lamps in green glass shades that turned every surface the color of drowned things. Down here the books were kept that the Edict of Unmaking had not yet burned — not because they were harmless, but because they were useful. Prophecies mostly. Genealogies with gaps in them like missing teeth. Contracts between the crown and parties whose names had been scraped from the vellum so thoroughly the pages had gone thin as breath.

Isolde catalogued them. That was her work, her small unremarkable work: to number each forbidden thing, describe its binding and its condition, record the date of last inspection, and lock it away again in a cabinet whose key hung at the waist of a man named Undermaster Corvel, who never smiled at her and never spoke unless spoken to and always, always counted the books twice.

She had been doing it for six years. She was good at it. She could date a manuscript by the smell of its tanning; she could tell a forged seal by the way wax cooled too evenly, without the tremor of a living hand. The archive did not frighten her. It was a library, after all, even down here — and libraries were the one place in Caerlum where Isolde had never once felt like a guest in her own life.

The book arrived on a Tuesday, though no one told her it arrived. That was the first strangeness, though she did not know it for strangeness until much later. She came down the stair at her usual hour, lit the lamps at their usual sequence — outer ring first, then inner, then the long gallery — and there it was, lying on her worktable as if it had grown there overnight like a mushroom in a cellar.

A folio bound in boards faced with grey leather gone soft as skin. No title stamped on the cover. A seal of pale wax across the clasp, unbroken, though the wax had yellowed to the color of old teeth, and pressed into it a sigil she did not recognize: a crescent moon with thirteen notches cut into its curve, like a knife that had been sharpened twelve times and waited for a thirteenth.

There was no accession slip. There was no entry in Corvel's ledger. There was nothing in the world to say this book belonged here except the fact that here it was.

Isolde stood over it for a long time, hands folded, the lamp-light making slow green rings on the table around it. She thought about calling for Corvel. She thought about how he would count the books twice, and how his face would do whatever it did when a book existed that he had no memory of receiving, and whether that face would look afraid.

She thought about Wendell, who would have laughed — *a book that walks, love, careful, next it'll want wages* — and then thought about Wendell more seriously, because Wendell tasted the king's food for poisons and knew better than anyone in the city that the most dangerous things arrive looking ordinary, sitting quietly, waiting to be swallowed.

But Isolde was an archivist. And an archivist's whole faith, the faith she had chosen over the ones her family had failed to give her, was this: that things hidden are still real, and that to catalogue a thing is to make it answerable to the light.

She put on her cotton gloves. She broke the seal.

* * *

The wax parted along the thirteenth notch of the moon with a sound like a held breath released.

Inside, the book was worse than the outside promised. The paper was vellum, fine-grained, and the hand that filled it was a beautiful one — a scribe's trained hand, patient and sure, working in iron-gall ink that had browned over a century or more into the color of dried blood, which is what iron-gall ink does, which is what everything in this city eventually does. The text was a chronicle of the court beneath the mountain. Not the folk-tale version, not the nursery rhyme version her nurse had sung — *down, down, under stone, the bridegroom counts and won't sleep alone* — but something older and drier and far more terrible: a ledger-voice, a contract-voice, listing obligations in the flat grammar of law.

*The court beneath requires sustenance. The court beneath requires service. The court beneath requires, upon each completion of the moon's great turning, a soul freely rendered.*

And then, further on, a passage that made Isolde's gloved finger stop above the page without quite touching it:

*Nine brides have been rendered. Three remain owed. The debt shall be discharged in full before the moon completes its turn.*

She read it four times. The words did not change, which was somehow the worst part. Words in the archive usually changed — that was the dirty secret of prophecy, that it rewrote itself in small ways, shifting its pronouns, migrating its details, the way a river shifts its banks while insisting on the same name. This text sat still. It sat still the way a trap sits still.

She began, then, properly, professionally, to catalogue. Binding: quarter-leather over pasteboard, rebacked. Collation: sixty-one leaves, the last three excised. Hand: single scribe, early period. Provenance: unknown. Acquisition: unknown. Condition: sealed, now broken, by I. Amberdale, fourth sublevel, Tuesday.

It was while writing that last line that she noticed the margins.

The scribe had left generous margins, as good scribes do, and later readers had used them — not many, just two or three, in different centuries' worth of ink. One margin note near the front was a simple tally, nine strokes crossed through. Another, halfway through, in a crabbed and hurried hand, said only *it lies about the willing part.*

And on the leaf describing the third remaining bride — the thirteenth bride, the last owed — there was a drawing.

Not a coat of arms. Not a sigil. A face.

Small, done in pen, in confident strokes that understood cheekbone and jaw the way a good hand understands letters. A young woman in profile, head slightly bowed over a book, hair pinned back with a few strands escaping at the temple. Inked lashes. The particular stubborn set of a mouth that has been told to be quiet and is composing its reply.

Isolde looked at it the way you look at your own reflection in a shop window when you have forgotten, for a moment, that you are walking past.

Then she took off one glove, because archivists are superstitious in precise ways, and touched her own jaw, and felt the same bone the ink had found.

Her hair was escaping at the temple right now. She could feel it, one strand, tickling her cheek, exactly where the drawn strand escaped.

The lamp hummed. Somewhere above her, muffled through six floors of stone and shelving, the great clock of the Athenaeum struck the hour, and the sound came down the stairwell like a coin dropped into a well, falling and falling and never quite landing.

*The thirteenth bride*, said the caption beneath the face, in the scribe's lovely obedient hand. And beside it, smaller, in that other crabbed century-old script, someone had added: *she will find this page.*

Isolde closed the book very carefully, the way you close a door on a room where someone is sleeping.

Then she hid it.

* * *

She told herself the hiding was procedure. An unregistered item in the restricted archive could not simply be reported up the chain — chains led to Corvel, Corvel led to the Office of Edicts, the Office of Edicts led to inquisitors, and inquisitors did not ask gentle questions of archivists who opened seals they should not have opened. So she wrapped the folio in an unmarked dust-cloth and placed it inside the false back of the map-case, behind the rolled surveys of harbor works that no one had consulted since the harbor silts up, and she locked the case, and she wrote in the day-log: *No acquisitions. Routine inspection. All items accounted for.*

All items accounted for. Her handwriting looked normal. That seemed obscene somehow — that her hand could be steady, could produce its neat professional letters, when the rest of her felt like a page that had been written on by a stranger.

She went home. She ate. She slept badly, and dreamed of counting, of a voice under stone saying *nine* in a tone of mild dissatisfaction, like a steward finding a cellar short.

And the next morning she told herself she would report it. And the morning after that. And instead, on the third day, at an hour when the reading rooms above were crowded and Corvel was at his midday meal and would not descend until the bell rang twice more, Isolde took the key from her own belt — she had copied it months ago for reasons she had told herself were efficiency — and let herself down the green-lit stair to open the map-case again.

Just to check. Just to confirm the condition of the item. That was all. Cataloguing required verification. Verification required return visits. There was a whole vocabulary of respectable words for what she was doing, and she leaned on them like a woman leaning on a door that is trying to open inward.

The book lay where she had left it, warm-seeming in the cold air, which was impossible, and she unwrapped it with hands that shook only a little, and she turned to the leaf with the margin, and the face was still hers.

That was when the vertigo started.

It came slowly at first, a faint wrongness like a floor that tilts a half-degree off true — noticeable only when you stand still. She looked at the drawn mouth and her own mouth ached with sympathy, the way teeth ache when another tooth is struck. She read *the thirteenth bride* and heard it in her mother's voice, in the particular flattened tone her mother used for facts about Isolde's future — the marriage being arranged and unarranged, the position at the Athenaeum being suitable, the family name being carried properly. Her mother had a gift for speaking of Isolde's life as though it were a document awaiting signature.

Now some dead scribe had signed it a hundred years ago. Some dead scribe had known her bowed head, her escaping hair, her stubborn mouth. Someone had sat with this book open on their knees and drawn her — drawn her precisely, tenderly almost — decades before she was born, and written underneath, in the calm of certainty: *the thirteenth bride.*

And below that: *she will find this page.*

She had found the page.

Isolde sat down on the worktable stool, because her legs had opinions of their own. The green light moved very slightly on the walls, though the flames did not flicker. She thought: I am being authored. The thought arrived complete, fully dressed, like a visitor, and she could not send it away. All her life she had believed — had needed to believe — that she was the writer of her own small story: the daughter who declined the arranged match, who took the archive post no respectable Amberdale wanted, who loved whom she loved in the narrow safe hours the city allowed. Every choice had felt like ink from her own pen.

And here was proof of an earlier draft. Here was her face in a margin, captioned, predicted, *owed.* Nine strokes crossed out. Three remaining. Before the moon completes its turn.

How many nights until the turn? She did not know. Nobody in Caerlum marked the great lunar calendar anymore; the Edict had abolished the old reckoning along with the old magic, and the official almanac printed only the plain civil months. But she was an archivist, and archives remember what cities are made to forget. There were coronation records with marginal dates. There were tide-tables in the harbor surveys. If she wanted, she could reconstruct the moon's great turning from a hundred years of dry bureaucratic entries, the way you can reconstruct a murder from laundry lists.

If she wanted. As if wanting were still available to her.

* * *

She hid the book again. She went home. She did not sleep.

She returned the next day. And the next.

It became a ritual with the shape of an illness. In the mornings she performed her legitimate work with flawless care — she dated a forged indulgence to within a decade, she re-housed a water-damaged psalter, she watched Corvel count the books twice and felt the lie sit in her chest like a swallowed stone — and every afternoon, in the green hour when the archive emptied of even the memory of people, she unlocked the map-case and read.

Because there was more than the one page. Of course there was. She read about the tolls and the terms, about memories accepted as currency, about the treasury of souls and the warden who weighed payments "as souls weigh, not as gold weighs." She read the crabbed annotator's scattered warnings — *it lies about the willing part; ask what the family received; the name matters, the name always matters* — and copied none of them down, memorized all of them, recited them on the stair like prayers said backwards.

And every day the drawn face waited for her with its escaping hair and its composing-a-reply mouth, and every day Isolde looked at it and felt the vertigo deepen from a tilt into a fall: the sensation of standing inside a story that had been finished before she began, of moving through her own life like a character walking through rooms already described, checking each detail against a text she'd never been allowed to see.

On the ninth day — she was counting now, she couldn't stop counting — she caught herself doing something unforgivable. She had brought a mirror. A little hand-glass, silver-backed, the kind ladies carry. She knelt at the worktable with the book open and the glass propped against the inkwell, and she compared, feature by feature, line by line, the woman in the margin and the woman in the glass, as if hoping to find a difference. A different angle to the brow. A softer chin. Any discrepancy, any flaw in the scribe's certainty, any crack in the sentence she had been written into.

There was none. The scribe had seen her truly. Whoever they had been, however long dead, they had looked across a hundred years and drawn Isolde Amberdale with the accuracy of love, and that was the most frightening thing of all: not hatred, not malice, but recognition. The book was not threatening her. The book was *expecting* her.

She put the mirror away. She pressed her palms flat against the vellum, feeling its cool animal smoothness, and whispered to the empty green room, to the scribe, to whoever listened from under the mountain:

"Whoever you think I am — whoever you say I'm promised to — I am not yours. Do you hear me? I am not a payment. I am a person."

The lamps burned. The clock dropped its hour down the stairwell. Nothing answered, which she had expected, and which disappointed her anyway.

And then, because she was an archivist, because cataloguing was the only weapon she had ever owned, she did what she had sworn she wouldn't do. She took out her private notebook — the one she kept for thoughts that were not the Athenaeum's business — and she wrote, in her neat professional hand, the first entry of a new catalogue:

*Item: one prophecy, sealed, unregistered, self-updating. Subject: myself.*

*Status: contested.*

She underlined *contested* twice. It was a small defiance, a clerk's rebellion, four syllables of ink against a century of certainty. But her hand did not shake when she wrote it, and that steadiness surprised her, and the surprise felt almost like hope.

She was wrapping the folio back in its cloth when she remembered the annotator's last marginal note, the one she'd read a dozen times without weighing: *ask what the family received.* Ask what the Ambersdales — what the Amberdales — had been given, and when, and why their fortune had risen so conveniently, so catastrophically, in exactly the era this book described.

Her mother's house had fourteen rooms. Her mother never spoke of her grandmother's grandmother. Her mother flinched, very slightly, every time the moon was mentioned, and Isolde had always taken it for piety.

She blew out the lamps in reverse order — inner ring, outer ring, gallery — and stood a moment in the dark with the book's shape pressed against her ribs through her coat, listening to the building settle around its secrets.

Tomorrow, she decided, she would go home to her mother's fourteen silent rooms and begin asking questions.

And tomorrow evening — this thought arrived unbidden, warm, absurd, indispensable — tomorrow evening she would find Wendell at the night market, and put her hand in her lover's, and tell her everything. Because if she had been written into a prophecy, then she intended to bring her own witnesses. Because the margin had drawn her alone, bowed and solitary, and Isolde refused, absolutely and forever, to be alone on this page.

She climbed the stair toward the ordinary lamplit world, carrying a forbidden book against her heart, and behind her in the dark the map-case held only the smell of iron and cold candlewax — and, faintly, if a person were foolish enough to believe such things, the sense of something below, patiently, politely, beginning to count.
