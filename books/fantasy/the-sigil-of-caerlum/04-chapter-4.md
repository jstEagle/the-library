## 4. Chapter 4

The moon over Caerlum had begun to wear its thirteenth face, though most of the city still counted twelve. Wendell knew because she'd learned to read the sky the way other people read ledgers — out of necessity, and with a constant low dread of what the numbers might add up to.

She climbed the archive stairs two at a time, her boots leaving damp half-moons on the stone. The tanneries' reek clung to everything in this quarter, and even the royal kitchens couldn't quite scrub it from her skin. But here, above the restricted stacks where Isolde worked, the air smelled of vellum and lamp-oil and the faint iron tang of old ink — the smell Wendell privately thought of as *home*, though she'd never said so aloud.

Isolde was waiting for her at the reading table, and Wendell knew at once that something was wrong. Not because Isolde's face betrayed it — Isolde's face was a locked cabinet, an archivist's face, trained by decades of guarding secrets that could hang a person. It was the hands. Isolde's hands were always moving when she waited for someone: straightening papers, squaring books, aligning the world into order. Tonight they lay flat on the table, palms down, as if pinning something in place.

On the table between them sat a book.

Wendell had seen enough restricted volumes in her years of quiet bribes and borrowed keys to know one at a glance. This one wore a binding of grey leather gone green at the edges, stamped with a sigil she didn't recognize — a circle broken by thirteen notches, like a moon that had been bitten thirteen times. A lead seal hung from the spine by a rotted cord.

"You opened it," Wendell said. Not a question. Her stomach performed a slow, cold revolution. "Isolde. That seal is crown-wax."

"I opened it eleven days ago." Isolde's voice was steady the way a frozen river is steady. "I've been trying to decide whether to tell you ever since."

"Eleven days?" Wendell pulled out the opposite chair but didn't sit. Sitting felt like surrendering the ability to run. "You sat on this for eleven days?"

"I sat on it because I needed to be sure I wasn't mad." Isolde finally looked up, and Wendell saw the thing beneath the archivist's composure: exhaustion, deep as well-water, and fear worn smooth from handling. "And then I needed to be sure it wasn't some inquisitor's trick. And then I needed to work up the courage. Courage is not my trade, Wendell. Cataloguing is my trade."

"What's in it?"

Isolde turned the book around. Her fingers trembled — actually trembled — as she found her place among the ribbon markers, and Wendell felt her heart begin to pound in the rhythm of bad news.

The page showed a court rendered in faded gilt and brownish ink: a hall of salt-white pillars descending into darkness, figures seated in ranks, their faces left deliberately blank — just ovals of untouched parchment, as if the artist had refused or been unable to paint them. Above them, a moon in thirteen phases circled like a clock face.

And at the bottom of the page, kneeling before the blank-faced throne, was a woman drawn in finer detail than anything else on the page. Dark hair pinned up carelessly. A long jaw. A small scar through the left eyebrow, inked in with loving precision — the scar Isolde had gotten falling out of a cherry tree at nine years old, the story she told at every dinner party, the story Wendell kissed every time.

Beneath the figure, in a spidery antique hand: *the thirteenth bride.*

The room tilted. Wendell gripped the table's edge and made herself breathe, made herself do the thing she did when tasting the king's supper — cataloguing, separating, refusing to let the body panic before the mind had all the evidence.

"That's you," she said. Her voice came out flat and strange.

"That's me." Isolde touched the inked eyebrow-scar with one fingertip, so lightly it might have been a blessing or a warding gesture. "Down to the scar. Down to the way I pin my hair when I'm tired. Whoever painted this has never met me, Wendell. The book was sealed before my grandmother was born."

"Could it be an ancestor? A resemblance—"

"I checked the family portraits. All of them." Isolde's laugh had no humor in it at all. "There are resemblances. There's a great-aunt with my chin. But none of them have the scar, and none of them are wearing this." She turned the page, and there was the same woman again, larger now, standing in a doorway of woven bone, dressed in a wedding gown whose hem dissolved into written lines — actual text, running down the dress and off the edge of the page like the gown itself was unraveling into prophecy.

Wendell read the text. She read it twice, because the first time her eyes slid off it the way they slid off poison labels she didn't want to parse.

*When the moon wears its thirteenth face, the debt comes due beneath the mountain. The bride promised shall walk down of her own blood and bone, and the court beneath shall take its due, which was sold and not given, pawned and not paid.*

"Soul-debt," Wendell said quietly. "This is a soul-debt contract."

"You know the terminology."

"I know more than I should." Wendell finally sat, because her legs had decided the matter for her. In the night market she sold luck-knots to girls who wanted to marry well and headache charms to clerks who wept behind their ledgers, but she also — quietly, rarely, at triple price — took questions about darker things. Debts. Bargains. What happened to people who went looking under the mountain. She knew the shape of the folklore the way a taster knows the shape of arsenic: by its symptoms. "Tell me you haven't gone to anyone about this."

"I came to you."

"Isolde—"

"You're the only person I trust," Isolde said, and the words landed between them with the weight of a vow, and Wendell understood suddenly why Isolde had waited eleven days. Not for courage. Because saying it aloud made it real, and real things could be taken away.

Wendell reached across the table and took her hand. Ink-stained, cold, familiar. "Then we'll be real about it together. Start at the beginning. When did you find it?"

"Reshelving the restricted collection. The Vellum Reorganization — the whole eastern wall was being recatalogued, and this volume had been misfiled for — possibly centuries, honestly, the old system was chaos." Isolde spoke faster now, retreating into procedure the way she did. "It shouldn't have been accessible. Someone misfiled it, or someone *wanted* it found. I keep coming back to that."

"Why would anyone want it found?"

"Because prophecies don't work unless they're witnessed," Isolde said simply. "That's the whole theory of them. A prophecy sealed in a dark room does nothing. It needs eyes. Maybe the book chose me. Maybe someone chose the book for me. I can't determine which, and it's making me insane."

"And the moon count? Thirteen faces?"

Isolde pulled a lunar almanac toward her, its pages soft with use, annotated in three different inks. "The current lunation is the twelfth turn since the last conjunction recorded in the text. See here—" her finger traced a marginal calculation, "—the prophecy counts from the winter solstice four years ago. Twelve full turns complete. We are inside the thirteenth now. It ends in nineteen days."

Nineteen days. Wendell did the arithmetic the way she did everything: quickly, coldly, and with her stomach dropping through the floor. Nineteen days to understand a century-old contract, find the counterparty, and break a bargain with a court that existed mostly in nursery rhymes and grandmother's warnings.

"Then we don't waste another hour on whether you're mad," Wendell said. "We assume you're sane, the book is true, and we treat it as a legal document. Because that's what it is, isn't it? It says *sold*. Sold means a seller. Sold means terms."

Isolde stared at her. Then, slowly, the terror in her face rearranged itself into something Wendell recognized from long nights in this same archive: the hunger of a researcher handed an impossible question. "A legal document," Isolde repeated. "Yes. Yes — contracts have parties, consideration, terms of redemption. If my soul was sold, there's a record. Records live in archives." She was already pulling ledgers toward her. "My family's fortune came from somewhere, Wendell. My mother guards those ledgers like relics. I used to think it was pride."

"And now?"

"Now I think it might be guilt."

* * *

They worked until the lamps burned low and the tallow smell gave way to dawn-grey seeping through the arrow-slit windows. Isolde sent a runner — a boy she trusted, deaf, uninterested in gossip — to her mother's townhouse with a forged authorization slip, and by midnight he returned with a wrapped bundle of family ledgers that Isolde swore she'd return before her mother noticed, and that both of them knew she wouldn't.

The Amberdale — no. Isolde's family name was different; Wendell kept having to correct herself. The **Vessant** ledgers went back six generations, and the earliest volume was bound in the same grey-green leather as the prophecy book, which made Wendell's skin crawl in a way she couldn't articulate.

"Here." Isolde's finger stopped on a page near the front, where the handwriting shifted from careful clerk's script to something hurried and blotted. "Great-great-great-grandmother Serafina Vessant. The year everything changed. Look at the entries."

Wendell leaned close. The ledger recorded a household in decline: debts, pawned silver, wages withheld. Then, over the space of a single week, entries that made no commercial sense whatsoever. *Received, per agreement.* *Consideration rendered in full.* *The mountain is satisfied.* And then, abruptly, the household's fortunes reversed — purchases of land, a townhouse, a commission in the guard, dowries that married Vessant daughters upward for a hundred years.

"There's no source," Wendell said slowly. "Every other windfall in these books lists where it came from. Land sale, inheritance, marriage portion. These entries just say *received, per agreement.* Agreement with whom?"

Isolde turned the page. Folded into the binding, hidden for generations, was a single loose leaf — thinner than the rest, and written not in ink but in something reddish-brown that Wendell's trained tongue identified by sight alone and had to swallow against.

Blood-ink. Or something pretending to be.

The leaf bore two columns. On the left, in the clerk's hand: *Serafina Vessant, of the house of Vessant, conveys unto the Court Beneath one soul of her line, to be chosen at the court's pleasure, redeemable only by the debtor's own bloodline ere the moon completes its thirteenth turn.* On the right, in a different hand entirely — looping, elegant, and utterly inhuman in its patience — a single line of acknowledgment, and below it a signature that looked less like letters than like a crack in stone.

"One soul of her line," Wendell read aloud. "To be chosen at the court's pleasure." Her mouth had gone dry in the professional way it did around certain alkaloids. "Isolde. Your ancestor didn't sell *her* soul. She sold one from her descendants. She let *them choose*."

"And they chose me." Isolde's voice was very quiet. "A century later, they reached down the family tree and picked the one with the scar."

"Redeemable," Wendell said. She seized the word like a rope thrown to a drowning woman. "It says redeemable. Only by the debtor's bloodline — that's you, you're Vessant blood even if the name's changed — *ere the moon completes its turn.* That's a redemption clause. Contracts like this always have a redemption clause, it's — it's the law of bargains, even the ugly ones honor their own terms, it's how they bind themselves—"

"Wendell." Isolde caught her hands, and Wendell realized she'd been pacing, had knocked a lamp, was talking too fast the way she talked when the poison-count was wrong and the king might die. "Breathe. You're doing the kitchen-voice."

Wendell breathed. Held Isolde's hands. Felt their warmth and thought, with a clarity that frightened her more than any prophecy: *nineteen days, and I have spent twelve years learning how to keep other people alive, and none of it will matter if I can't keep this one.*

"All right," she said. "Kitchen-voice off. Tell me what redemption requires."

"I don't know yet. The leaf doesn't say. Which means the answer is either somewhere else in this archive, or—" Isolde hesitated.

"Or it's beneath the mountain," Wendell finished. "In the court itself."

Neither of them said anything for a moment. The lamp guttered. Somewhere far below, Caerlum was waking — cart wheels on cobbles, the first bells, the ordinary machinery of a city that didn't know one of its archivists was mortgaged to the dark.

"Then I'll go down and ask them," Wendell said.

The words were out before she'd fully decided to say them, and she watched them land on Isolde like a blow.

"No." Flat. Immediate. "Absolutely not."

"You have a better idea?"

"I have the idea that the court beneath the mountain collects people, Wendell. Every rhyme agrees on that. Grandmothers don't invent warnings like that from nothing. You go down there, you don't come back, and I lose—" Isolde stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "I lose you. That's the whole plan. That's what your plan costs."

"It's not a plan yet. It's a direction." Wendell turned Isolde's hands over, examined the ink stains on her fingers as if they were evidence. "Listen to me. I have spent twelve years putting things in my mouth that should have killed me, because somebody had to stand between the king and the cup. I am very hard to kill, I am very good at noticing what doesn't belong, and I have spent my whole life being invisible in rooms where visibility gets you hanged. If anyone in this city can walk into a court of dead bargainers and come back out, it's me."

"And if it's not enough? If they want something you can't pay?"

"Then I'll negotiate. I'm told I'm ruthless at market."

Isolde laughed — a wet, cracked sound, halfway to tears — and pulled Wendell's hands to her lips. "You're impossible."

"I'm yours," Wendell corrected. "It makes me impossible."

For a long moment Isolde just held on, and Wendell felt the trembling in her slowly still, the way water settles after a stone. When Isolde spoke again, the archivist was back — spine straight, eyes dry, mind already moving.

"Then we do this properly. Together. I research, you infiltrate. I find the terms of redemption before you set foot past any gate, because walking into that court without knowing the exact wording of the contract is suicide, and I refuse." She held up a finger, ticking points. "One: I pull every text in this archive touching the under-court, the Edict of Unmaking's original drafting, and bargain-law. Two: you tell me everything you know about the night market rumors — the tolls, the gates, the rhymes. Three: nobody else learns any of this. Not your fence, not your customers, not the boy who fetched the ledgers beyond what he already knows. Inquisitors buy secrets, Wendell. If Hale's people hear the word 'bride' in connection with my name before we understand this, we're both ashes."

"Inquisitors," Wendell said. The name landed in her chest like a swallowed nail. She'd felt eyes on the night market lately. She'd been meaning to mention it. "Yes. About that."

"Tomorrow," Isolde said. "Tonight we pact."

She held out her hand, formal and absurd and deadly serious, the way scholars seal agreements when there's no wax worth trusting. Wendell took it.

"To breaking a century-old contract," Isolde said.

"To stealing back what was stolen," Wendell said.

"To nineteen days."

"To us," Wendell said, because someone had to say it plainly, and because she wanted the words to exist somewhere outside her own head in case the days ahead got dark. "Whatever's under that mountain, whatever this costs — we fight it together. Both of us. No one goes alone, no one lies to protect the other, no one pays a price the other doesn't know about. Swear it."

Something flickered across Isolde's face then — quick, unreadable, gone before Wendell could catalogue it. Later, much later, she would understand what that flicker had been: the future, arriving early, wearing a warning. But tonight she read it only as fear, and fear was fair.

"Sworn," Isolde said. "Together. No prices paid in secret."

Outside the arrow-slit window, the moon hung over Caerlum's rooftops, fat and white and counting down. Nineteen days. Wendell looked at it and felt, beneath the terror, a strange and unfamiliar steadiness — the steadiness that came after the poison was identified and before the antidote was poured, when dread became simply work.

She had a contract to break, a lover to save, and a city full of outlawed kindnesses to fund the journey.

First, though, she had charms to sell — and someone in the night market owed her news about the roads beneath the mountain.

*She squeezed Isolde's hand once, blew out the lamp, and went down into the dark of the city to make preparations — unaware that three streets away, a man in inquisition grey had already begun buying luck-knots.*
