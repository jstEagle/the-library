## 5. Chapter 5

The ledgers of House Amberdale lived in the east study, behind a door that locked with a key her mother wore on a ribbon at her throat, and Isolde had been stealing into them for three days.

Not stealing, precisely. Her mother had gone to the coast — *to breathe sea air, you look pale, Isolde* — and had left the key in the drawer of her writing desk as if she had forgotten it. Isolde did not believe her mother forgot anything. The key sat there the way a trap sits there, patient and gleaming, and Isolde had taken it anyway, because the alternative was to sit in her own house feeling like a guest at someone else's funeral.

She worked by lamplight with the shutters closed. The ledgers were bound in calfskin gone soft as old bread, their spines cracked into rivers, and they smelled of dust and tallow and something faintly sweet beneath it all, like fruit rotting somewhere deep in the house. She had catalogued ten thousand documents in the restricted archive; she knew how records lied by omission, how the most important line in any ledger was the one written smallest, in the hand of whoever hoped no one would read it.

So she read everything.

Her family's fortune unfolded before her like a slow, obscene flower. Great-grandfather Aldous Amberdale, who had arrived in Caerlum with nothing but a name too grand for his coat, who within five years held tanneries on the river road and a townhouse on Sable Street and a pew in the Basilica close enough to the altar that the incense reached him first. The ledgers recorded every purchase, every loan, every shrewd acquisition. What they did not record — what appeared nowhere in any column of figures — was where the starting capital had come from.

Except.

On the third night, wedged inside the back binding of a ledger dated ninety-one years past, she found a folded paper so old its creases had begun to fur, like bark. It was not written in ink. Or rather, the words were ink, ordinary brown-black iron gall, but beneath them ran a second script, pressed into the paper as if from the far side — thin silver lines that moved when she was not looking directly at them, the way water moves under ice.

She recognized the handwriting of the visible text. It was her great-grandfather's. She knew it from the deed to this very house, which hung framed in the front hall like an icon.

*Received of the Court Beneath, one sum, in consideration of which I do pledge—*

The rest was in the silver script.

Isolde set the paper down on the blotter. Her hands were not shaking. She noted this distantly, the way she noted catalogue numbers, the way she noted anything: *item, trembling absent; condition, subject calm beyond reason.* She had learned in the archive that the moment before understanding is often the last quiet moment a scholar gets, and she made herself sit in it. She listened to the house settle. She heard the clock in the hall count the quarter hour. Somewhere below, the cook banked the kitchen fire, and the smell of ash rose through the floorboards like a warning.

Then she picked the paper up again and turned it toward the lamp, angling it until the silver script caught the light and threw its letters onto the wall behind her, huge and wavering, so she could read them reflected rather than direct. A trick of the trade. Scholars of restricted texts learned early that some writings bite if you meet them face to face.

*...I do pledge one soul of my bloodline, the thirteenth born or the thirteenth named, to be surrendered upon the completion of the moon's thirteenth turning, redeemable only by the debtor's own blood before said turning is complete. Failing redemption, the pledge passes wholly to the Court Beneath, together with all issue, interest, and accrual thereof.*

There was more. There was always more; contracts were her profession, in a way — she had spent six years cataloguing bargains between the crown and powers it preferred not to name. The clauses went on in that dry legal voice that makes horror sound like bookkeeping. *The pledge shall be marked.* *The pledge shall be known to itself only when the hour grows short.* *No member of the debtor's bloodline shall speak of this instrument to any authority of the Crown of Caerlum, upon penalty of forfeiture accelerated.*

Forfeiture accelerated.

She thought, absurdly, of Wendell's hands. Of how Wendell tied a luck-knot around Isolde's wrist three weeks ago, laughing, saying *it won't work, I'm a fraud, but you'll sleep better believing in me,* and how Isolde had slept better. How she was sleeping better still, these nights, waking before dawn with the taste of someone else's future in her mouth, reaching across cold linen to find warm fingers already waiting for hers.

The moon was on its twelfth turn. Nine days remained.

Isolde did the arithmetic she had been avoiding since the archive, since the margin, since the inked face that wore her own eyes looked up at her from a book older than her grandmother. Thirteenth born or thirteenth named. Her mother had lost two infants before her — she knew this now, knew it the way you know a room you've never entered, suddenly and completely — two small unnamed graves in the family plot at Fenmarsh. And Isolde herself had been the thirteenth child *named* into the Amberdale rolls, the registry her mother kept in her own hand, thirteen entries counting the dead, the miscarried, the stillborn, each one given a name posthumously because her mother could not bear an unlabeled grave.

Thirteen names. And the thirteenth was hers.

*Item: one soul, pledged. Item: one daughter, purchased.*

She thought about the house. She thought about how it had never once felt like hers, though her name was on half the furniture. She thought about the nursery that had become a sewing room that had become a room that was simply kept shut, about her mother's habit of standing at windows watching the moon as if expecting it to deliver a bill.

And then, with a lurch that finally reached her hands, she thought about her mother's coldness. All of it, every year of it, rearranging itself in her memory like a sentence whose final word has just been revealed. The way her mother had refused to celebrate Isolde's thirteenth birthday — *we don't mark that number in this house* — and had taken her instead to Fenmarsh, to stand among the graves, and had gripped her shoulder hard enough to bruise and said, *never let them know your true age, never tell anyone the order of your birth, promise me, promise me.* Isolde had thought it vanity. Families were strange about lineage; Caerlum was strange about everything.

It was not vanity. It was a woman hiding her daughter from a creditor, badly, for thirty years, and hating the daughter a little for being the debt, and loving her too much to say so, and choosing silence over confession because confession would have meant admitting what the comfort was bought with. Every silk gown. Every winter's coal. Every lesson in languages and calligraphy and the careful, bloodless arts of a lady — paid for. Everything Isolde had ever touched in this house was purchased goods, and the coin had been herself.

She laughed. It came out wrong, a single syllable with no humor in it, bouncing off the shutters.

The silver script shifted. She looked down and saw that new lines had surfaced along the bottom edge of the paper, faint as breath on glass, appearing because — she understood this with archival certainty — the hour was growing short, exactly as the contract promised, and the document was waking up. It wanted to be read. It had been waiting ninety-one years for someone in the debtor's bloodline to hold it under lamplight and understand.

*Note of accrual,* the new lines read. *Interest compounding per annum since date of instrument. Current balance: one soul, plus ninety-one years' weight thereof.*

Ninety-one years' weight. She thought of Wendell again, unbidden — Wendell who tasted poison for a living, who carried twelve years of arsenic in her bones and called it competence. Wendell would look at this paper and see terms. Wendell would look at these terms and start hunting for the flaw, because that was what Wendell did with dangerous things: she put them in her mouth and found out what they were made of.

*Redeemable only by the debtor's own blood.*

Only by the debtor. Not by the pledge. Not by the bride herself. Whatever was owed could only be reclaimed by an Amberdale walking down into whatever court waited beneath the mountain, before nine days ran out, carrying payment of equal weight to ninety-one years of compounded soul.

Isolde folded the contract along its old creases, carefully, the way one handles anything wounded. Then she took a sheet of plain paper and began, in her best archival hand, a full transcription — visible text and silver both, the silver rendered in her own mixed technique of graphite and powdered mica so it would catch light the same treacherous way. If the original woke further, grew teeth, tried to return to its hiding place or call out to its owner, she would have a copy. Cataloguing was not glamorous work. It was the work of people who assumed the world would try to lie to them.

She was grinding the mica when she heard the front door.

Her mother was home four days early. Isolde heard the key go back into the drawer of the writing desk — heard it placed, not dropped, placed with the deliberate gentleness of someone checking whether it had moved — and then the footsteps on the stairs, slower than usual, heavier, stopping twice before they reached the study door.

They stopped outside it for a long time.

When the door opened, her mother stood in the frame with her travel cloak still on and her face arranged into its usual composure, except that the composure was a day old now, stale, worn through at one corner. Her eyes went to the desk. To the open ledger. To the folded paper beside Isolde's transcription, sitting in the lamplight like a small dark heart.

Neither of them spoke. In the silence Isolde watched her mother's gaze move over the silver-inked copy, and watched thirty years of practiced indifference fail, all at once, the way a dam fails — not dramatically, but completely.

"You weren't supposed to find it," her mother said, "until after."

"After," Isolde repeated.

Her mother came into the room and closed the door behind her, and when she turned around, she was not the woman who had raised her. She was smaller than that woman. Older. A debtor.

"The moon turns the last time nine days from tonight," she said. "Sit down, Isolde. We have less time than you think, and I have been a coward for thirty years, and I am done."

* * *

But it was not her mother's confession Isolde remembered most, afterward. It was the last thing her mother said before dawn grayed the shutters, when the whole ugly architecture of the bargain lay open between them: that there was another copy of the contract, or something worse than a copy, sealed in the restricted archive of the royal library — Isolde's own workplace, her own territory — filed under a seal she now recognized, having seen its twin pressed into the wax of the original.

"And whoever holds that instrument can contest yours," her mother whispered. "Or enforce it."

Outside, the moon was setting, fat and nearly whole, one turn from done. Isolde dressed quickly in the dark, took her archive credentials, and slipped out of the house while her mother slept at last — truly slept, the sleep of the emptied — toward the library, toward Wendell, toward the only person alive who could help her read the enemy's paperwork before the moon finished counting.
