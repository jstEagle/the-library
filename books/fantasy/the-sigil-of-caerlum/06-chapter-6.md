## 6. Chapter 6

The night market smelled the way it always did on a Thursday: tallow smoke and wet stone, fried onions from Maret's cart, the copper-sweet tang of a hundred small magics being passed hand to hand like contraband kisses. Wendell had been working her corner for three years now, the one beneath the broken arch where the tanners' runoff made the cobblestones glisten even in drought, and she knew the market's moods the way she knew the king's palate — intimately, superstitiously, by aftertaste.

Tonight the aftertaste was wrong.

She couldn't have said how at first. The crowd was its usual weave: laundresses buying luck-knots against the fever that ran through the wash-houses every autumn, a stonemason with the gray look of a man who'd dreamed of the mountain three nights running and wanted something — anything — to hang over his door. Old Sefa was there with her tray of bottled breaths, each vial holding one exhalation of someone who had died contented, which was not magic anyone could prove but which sold steadily all the same. A child was chasing a paper bird Wendell had charmed an hour ago; it flapped in lazy circles around the arch, refusing to land.

Wendell stood behind her folding table with her hood back and her sleeves rolled, tying knots into colored thread while she talked. That was the trick of it, the whole secret of her trade: she never sold anything. People bought. They picked up a knot or a sachet or a twist of wax, they held it, they told her what hurt, and when the thing left her hands it left warmed, carrying just enough of her intent folded inside it to do one small kindness. A headache dulled. A fever broke a day early. A gambler lost slightly less catastrophically. Nothing the Edict of Unmaking would trouble itself to name — except that the Edict named everything, because that was what edicts were for.

"You've got the tired hands tonight," said Maret, leaning across the gap between their tables. He was seventy if he was a day and he'd been selling onions in the night market since before the Edict had teeth, back when it was only a sermon nobody enforced. "Tired hands make weak knots."

"Tired hands make honest knots." She bit the thread. "The luck goes in easier when there's less of me in the way."

He snorted. Somewhere down the lane a fiddle started up, badly, and the paper bird wheeled toward the sound like it had opinions about music.

That was when Wendell noticed what was wrong. It came to her the way poison knowledge came — not as thought but as taste, a wrongness on the air she'd been breathing without listening. The market was too quiet at its edges. Not empty; *arranged*. There were men standing at the mouths of two alleys who were not buying anything. They wore no livery, but they stood the way palace guards stood when they were dressed as nothing: weight forward, hands loose, eyes moving in patterns.

She had spent twelve years learning to read rooms for a living. The king's supper required it; a taster who noticed things late died mid-notice. And so Wendell did what she had trained herself to do, the thing that had kept her alive through arsenic breakfasts and antimony feasts and the long slow apprenticeship of swallowing death in polite quantities: she went still, she breathed shallow, and she catalogued.

Six of them. No — seven, one already drifting along the cloth-stall row, lifting scarves to his nose as though shopping, though no one shopped for scent at midnight in the rain-shadow of the mountain. And beneath the onion-smoke and the fiddle, faint as a memory of a memory, she caught it: salt and iron and something older underneath, something that smelled the way a sealed crypt smells when you crack the door. Grave-cold. Courtly.

She had smelled it once before. Three weeks ago, in the restricted archive, on Isolde's gloves, after Isolde had carried a certain book up from the deep stacks with shaking hands. *The thirteenth bride.* The moon hung outside the broken arch now, fat and low, eleven nights shy of its final turn, and Wendell felt its pull like a hook behind her ribs.

"Hale," she said, very quietly. Not a question. She didn't know why she was sure. She simply was, the way she was sure about hemlock.

"Who?" said Maret.

"Inquisitor Hale. He's here, or his people are. Pack your tray."

Maret looked at her, and then — this was the thing Wendell would carry afterward, the stone she would swallow and swallow again for weeks — Maret did not pack his tray. He laughed. "Girl, I've seen scares come through this market since before you were weaned. They post watchers, the watchers get bored, everyone buys onions out of nervousness and we all go home richer. Stay put. You'll spook your customers."

"I'm telling you—"

"And I'm telling you," he said, gentle as a grandfather, foolish as a saint, "that old men don't run."

She should have grabbed him. She understood that later, turning it over at four in the morning like a coin she couldn't spend. She could have taken his arm, tipped the tray herself, scattered the bottled breaths into the gutter — anything to move him. Instead she hesitated one breath too long, because hesitation is what invisibility costs and she had been paying it for years, and in that breath the fiddle stopped.

It didn't falter. It stopped, mid-phrase, the way a heart stops, and the silence that followed had edges.

Then the lanterns went out.

Not all at once — in sequence, from the mouth of the lane inward, snuffed by hands or by wind or by something that walked, and the dark came rolling down the market like a tide, and with it came voices calling names. Not *in the king's name*. Hale never wasted words on the king. Just names, flat and unhurried, read off a list: "*Sefa Venni. Maret Osk. The knot-woman.*" That last one was hers, and it went through her like a needle finding a vein. *The knot-woman.* He had come for her specifically. He had come knowing what she was called when she thought no one important was listening.

Chairs scraped. Someone screamed, briefly, and was quieted. The paper bird burst into flame above the arch — uncharmed itself, unraveled, its little magic eaten — and in its dying light Wendell saw them clearly for the first time: gray coats, gray faces, moving through the market with the terrible tidiness of men doing arithmetic. And ahead of them, unhurried, lantern in hand, a tall figure whose face she had seen twice before from the gallery of the Assize Hall, watching sentencings with the patient expression of a man reading scripture he already agreed with.

Inquisitor Hale.

*Move,* said twelve years of training. *Now.*

But her feet betrayed her — not with slowness but with direction. They took her one step toward Maret, toward Sefa, toward the whole web of small kindnesses she'd knotted together over three years, because you do not leave your people, you do not leave your people, you do not—

A hand closed on her wrist. Sefa. The old woman pressed something into her palm — one of the bottled breaths, cool glass, warm from her pocket — and shoved her backward into the shadow of the arch with a strength that belied her age. "Go," Sefa said. "You're the one he named last. Last means he wants you walking out under your own power, girl, and that means you can disappoint him. Go be disappointing."

"Sefa—"

"*Go.*" And then the old woman turned toward the gray coats, lifted her chin, and said, in a voice pitched to carry, "Sefa Venni. That's me. What kept you?"

Wendell ran.

She hated herself for it with a completeness that surprised her. She had thought herself past cowardice, or rather she had thought her particular courage — the daily courage of swallowing poison — was the only kind, and now she learned otherwise in the space between heartbeats, learning it through her soles as she fled up the tanner's stair with the market burning its light out behind her. She heard Maret shouting, indignant more than afraid. She heard a table go over, the bright scatter of vials, and then — worse than any scream — she heard nothing from either of them at all.

* * *

She went to ground the way water goes to ground: downward, patiently, along channels worn by repetition. Through the dye-yard where the reeking vats hid her scent from any hound they might loose. Along the culvert that smelled of the river's oldest regrets. Into the cellar beneath the ruined bathhouse where she kept a change of clothes, a purse, a knife, and — because she was who she was — a bundle of unfinished charms she could not bring herself to abandon, wrapped in oilcloth like a dead infant.

There she sat in the dark with her back against wet brick, and shook.

Not gracefully. Not poetically. She shook like a woman coming off poison, because that was essentially what fear was, she'd long ago decided — a toxin the body brewed for itself — and she rode it out the way she rode out antimony, letting it crest and pass, counting her breaths the way her mentor Corvin had taught her before the night he drank for her and taught her the rest of the lesson instead, the one about how a taster's immunity is a debt collected slowly from everyone nearby.

When the shaking passed, the thinking came, and the thinking was colder.

Hale hadn't raided the market for charm-sellers. Charm-sellers were a harvest, not a hunt; the inquisition swept them seasonally, the way you cull fish. But he had come with her *name*. Her night-name, her true trade, known only to perhaps forty people, none of them talkers — talkers didn't survive the night market. Which meant someone had talked, or someone had watched, or — and here the cold spread from her mind down into her chest — someone had *told* him. Someone who moved in places where names were currency.

She turned Sefa's gift over in her hands. The little vial held a breath of someone dead and contented, and in the dark it seemed suddenly less like a keepsake and more like a message, the way everything seemed suddenly like messages. She held it to her lips without uncorking it, absurdly, as if she might inhale the comfort secondhand.

And beneath the river-smell and the brick-mold, faint but unmistakable, the cellar air tasted of salt and grave-cold. Of the court beneath the mountain.

She had told herself, these past days, that the two worlds were separate — that the inquisition was Caerlum's dog and the under-court was Caerlum's nightmare, and dogs and nightmares did not conspire. But sitting in the dark with her stock abandoned and her friends arrested, Wendell let herself finally think the shape of the thought she'd been circling:

Why would the inquisition care whether Isolde Amberdale reached her wedding-day beneath the mountain? They wouldn't. Prophecy was superstition; the Edict burned superstition. Unless the prophecy wasn't superstition. Unless the Edict's power — the raw authority of Unmaking, the weight that let seven gray coats extinguish a market with their footsteps — was itself *bought*, paid for in the only coin the under-court accepted. Souls. A pact as old as the city, renewed on terms Wendell could only guess at, and guess she did, sitting in the dark: *the crown keeps the mountain sleeping; the mountain keeps the crown unanswerable.*

Which meant Hale wanted her failure. Or worse — Hale wanted her *success*, delivered into his hands: Isolde's soul-shard pried loose from the treasury and claimed for the crown, the debt transferred, the pact refreshed. Either way, every step Wendell took toward the gate was a step along a road someone else had paved.

She laughed, once, quietly, and it came out wrong.

Isolde would know. Isolde, who read ledgers the way priests read psalms, who had found her own inked face in a century-old book and hidden the horror under cataloguing discipline — Isolde would find the thread connecting Hale to the deep stacks, if a thread existed in paper. Wendell had to reach her. Had to warn her that the archive might not be safe, that the restricted section might have eyes, that the woman Wendell loved had become the most dangerous object in Caerlum simply by being legible.

She stood. Her legs held. Twelve years of poison had built her body into a fortress, but fortresses, she thought, gathering the oilcloth bundle, were just targets that had survived longer than most.

At the top of the bathhouse steps, the night waited, moon-fat and close, eleven turns from done. Somewhere below her feet, beneath the foundations and the bones and the bargains, something ancient adjusted its ledger and made room for a new entry.

Wendell pulled up her hood and started toward the archive district, moving like smoke, carrying a dead stranger's breath in her pocket and the growing certainty that the only person in Caerlum she could trust with the truth was the one person the truth was trying to eat.

Behind her, far off, a single bell tolled from the Assize Hall. One stroke. An arrest confirmed, or a sentence begun.

She did not let herself wonder whose.
