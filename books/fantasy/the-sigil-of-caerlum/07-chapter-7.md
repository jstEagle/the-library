## 7. Chapter 7

The rhymes came back to her in the bathhouse steam, the way old things always did — unbidden, unwelcome, arriving like a debt collector at dinner.

*Down where the tanneries rot and the river forgets its name,* her grandmother used to sing, wringing washing until her knuckles went white as candle wax, *there stands a door that is no door, and walking through it is no shame, unless you carry what you owe, unless you owe what you can't pay, unless the moon has turned twelve times and thirteenth turns to look your way.*

Wendell had been six. She had thought it was a song about laundry.

Now she sat in the public baths on Salt Lane with her arms wrapped around her knees, watching the water bead on skin that had refused to blister for twelve years, and understood that every lullaby in Caerlum was a warning wearing ribbons. The city was built over something. Everyone said so the way they said the river was older than the king — a fact too large and too damp to be worth discussing. But her grandmother had sung about a door, and her grandmother had grown up in the Tanneries before the Edict of Unmaking scrubbed the district's history off the stones, and grandmothers who sang warnings usually knew why.

*Unless you carry what you owe.*

Isolde owed a soul. Wendell intended to carry it home like a stolen loaf of bread under her coat.

She tipped the attendant a copper not to refill the tub, dressed in her plainest wool, and went out into the evening with the rhyme stitched behind her teeth.

* * *

The Tanneries after dark smelled the way grief would smell if grief were rendered into tallow — lime pits and rotting hide and the sweetish undertone of the river where it pooled stagnant beneath the drying frames. The Edict had closed most of the yards a generation ago; the great stone vats stood open to the sky now like a field of empty eye sockets, and the wind moving across them made a sound Wendell chose not to name.

*There stands a door that is no door.*

She walked the rows slowly, one hand trailing the vats' cold rims, reciting under her breath. Her grandmother's version had a second verse, half-lost, worn smooth by decades of repetition until it barely made sense: *where two walls lean but never meet, where water runs both up and down, count the arches, spare the seventh, turn your back upon the crown.* She'd always assumed the last line was seditious nonsense. Now she wasn't sure anything her grandmother sang was nonsense.

Where two walls lean but never meet.

It took her an hour. The moon was high and fat, one night short of some threshold Isolde could calculate and Wendell could only feel — a pressure behind the sternum, like the moment before poison announces itself — when she found it at the far edge of the abandoned yard: a place where the boundary wall of the old Marrow Yard and the wall of the shuttered dye-house leaned toward each other across a gap of perhaps four feet, their tops almost touching, their bases held apart by nothing Wendell could see. Between them, the ground ran with water. It ran uphill.

Not fast. Not dramatically. Just a thin silver thread crawling the wrong way along the channel cut between the stones, patient as a debt accruing interest.

Wendell crouched and put two fingers in it. Cold — colder than any water had a right to be in autumn, cold like the inside of a lock. Above the gap, where the leaning walls nearly met, the darkness was thicker than the night around it. Not black exactly. *Unlit*, the way the inside of a mouth is unlit.

"Count the arches," she murmured. There were no arches. Then she looked again, properly, the way she looked at a suspicious pheasant — not at what was presented but at what was arranged — and saw that the shadows between the two walls fell in curves. Seven of them, overlapping like scales, deepening toward the center. Seven arches made of nothing at all, holding up a doorway made of less.

*Spare the seventh.* She counted her steps carefully and stopped at the sixth shadow, close enough that the cold coming through it raised the fine hairs on her forearms. Close enough to hear — or thought she heard, or told herself later she had heard — a faint sound from within, like a very large room where many people were breathing politely.

Her hands were shaking. She noted this with the detached professionalism of long practice, the same way she noted a bitter almond note in the king's wine: *symptom present, source unknown, proceed with caution.* Twelve years of tasting had taught her that fear was just another flavor to be catalogued and swallowed.

But this — standing at a door out of her grandmother's songs, about to knock on the underside of the world for a soul that someone had pawned like a coat against winter — this was a flavor she didn't have a shelf for yet.

"I've come to bargain," she said to the dark.

Nothing answered. Which was, she would learn, an answer.

* * *

The toll-keeper arrived without arriving. One moment the sixth arch held only shadow; the next there was a figure seated on a low stool in the gap between the walls, as though he had been there since the stones were laid and had merely been waiting for her eyes to catch up.

He looked like a clerk who had been left in a cellar for three hundred years. Grey robes gone the grey of old dishwater, a ledger across his knees, ink-stained fingers, and a face that was pleasant the way a well-made mask is pleasant — all the features present and correct, arranged around an absence. His eyes, when they found hers, were the exact color of the uphill water.

"You're not supposed to know I exist," he said. His voice was dry paper. "The Edict was very thorough. We spent good money on that thoroughness."

"We?"

"The court below and the men above." He smiled without engaging any of his face. "Inquisitions are inexpensive when you own the treasury. But." He tilted his head. "You said *bargain*. That word still works down here. Sit."

There was no second stool, but when Wendell lowered herself onto the wet stones, they were dry where she sat, which she decided not to think about.

"Terms first," the toll-keeper said, opening his ledger. "Passage through the gate is not given. It is sold. Coin is useless to us — we minted your coin, in a sense, or your coin mints us, the metaphysics are tedious. What we accept—" he turned the ledger so she could see, and the pages were filled not with numbers but with small moving images, like pressed flowers of light: a woman dancing in a kitchen. A boy falling from a pear tree. A funeral. A first kiss, over and over, in a hundred different hands, "—is weight. Memory has weight. Joy weighs more than sorrow, despite what your poets say; sorrow everyone keeps anyway, free of charge. We take the memory entire. You will not remember that you remembered. The hole closes over, smooth as water."

Wendell looked at the pressed flowers of other people's lives and felt her stomach perform a slow, professional assessment, the way it did before a risky dish. "And if I refuse to pay?"

"Then the gate stays shut, and the girl whose face is in the prophecy book marries the court beneath the mountain on the thirteenth turn, and serves us for a century or a millennium, depending on how well she takes to it." He said it gently, the way a physician delivers a prognosis. "You came here knowing the price of entry. Entry is always paid in yourself. That is what a door is."

"How much?" she asked. "For passage. Just passage."

He studied her then, really studied her, and something flickered behind the mask-face — surprise, maybe, or recognition. "You want a quote. Like a fishmonger." A dry rustle that might have been laughter. "Very well. Proof of earnestness, minimum: one minor memory. Something true, something yours, something you can survive losing. After that, deeper passage costs deeper coin. And the treasury—" his eyes went briefly distant, "—the treasury sets its own rates. They will not be mine. Mine are charitable."

Charitable. From the keeper of a door that ate lives. Wendell almost laughed.

She began inventorying instead. This was her discipline, the one skill twelve years at the king's table had honed to a razor: triage under pressure. What could she afford to lose? Her mother's face — no, she'd already lost most of that to time, and what remained was load-bearing. The taste of burnt honey — trivial, but she suspected trivialities weren't accepted; the ledger-images had all pulsed with significance. Her first year of apprenticeship, the terror of it? No — that terror had made her careful; careful kept her alive. The night market, the charms, the customers who whispered their desperate little wishes — those were her livelihood, her other self, and she would sooner sell a hand.

Which left—

"There's a morning," she heard herself say, "about nine years ago. My mentor, Master Corvin, was still alive. He caught me practicing a charm on a kitchen cat — a luck-knot, harmless, the cat adored it — and instead of reporting me, he sat down on the flour barrel and taught me the proper binding. Four hours. He called it *the family trade*, joking, because neither of us had family worth the name. Then he made me breakfast. Eggs with too much pepper because he believed pepper was prophylactic against everything including boredom." Her throat tightened. "He died two winters later. Of my breakfasts, though neither of us ever said so."

The toll-keeper's pen had paused above the page.

"That one," Wendell said, before she could reconsider, before the arithmetic of loss could finish itself. "That morning. Take that one."

"It's a good weight," he said softly. "A little heavier than minor, honestly. Are you certain? Once the ink dries—"

"He's dead," Wendell said. "He's dead and the morning is dead and I am not, and the living have debts." She thought of Isolde's face inked in a margin a hundred years before Isolde was born, and repeated, quieter: "Take it."

The toll-keeper looked at her for a long moment with his water-colored eyes, and Wendell had the sudden, vertiginous sense of being read — thoroughly, past the poison-hardened body, past the outlawed small magics, down to whatever raw ledger lay underneath.

"My predecessor," he said, "took memories from a man who came selling his daughter's future. He paid cheap and laughed about it. I have watched him regret it for longer than your city has had its name." He dipped his pen. "I'm telling you that so you understand I know what I'm taking, and what it becomes afterward. Hold still."

He reached out and touched two ink-stained fingers to her forehead, just above the bridge of the nose, and it did not hurt.

It was worse than hurt. It was *subtraction*.

The morning lifted out of her like a splinter drawn whole. She felt it go — the flour-barrel creak, the cat's rumbling purr, the pepper-bright eggs, the words *the family trade* spoken in a voice she suddenly could not place, could not hear, could not prove had ever existed — each detail sliding away in order, gently, terribly, the way the tide takes a sandcastle grain by grain until only the idea of a castle remains, and then not even the idea. She grabbed for it. She actually grabbed, hands rising uselessly toward her own temples. Too late. The ink dried. The hole closed over, smooth as water.

She was kneeling on dry stones between two leaning walls, and there was a faint ache above her nose, and somewhere in the middle of her life there was a gap shaped like a morning, and she could not remember what had filled it. She only knew — the way you know a tooth is missing — that something had been taken. That she had *given* it. That she had chosen this, and the choosing itself was already going grey at the edges.

"Well?" she said. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Did I pass?"

The gate between the arches had changed. Where the unlit dark had been there was now a stair, descending, lit by nothing, each step worn concave by feet older than kingdoms. Warm air rose from it, smelling of salt and candlewax and, beneath that, faintly, of the king's own banquet hall — that same undertone of too many bodies in a gilded room.

"Proof received," said the toll-keeper, closing his ledger. He was watching her with an expression she couldn't parse, and wouldn't, until much later, when understanding would arrive too late to help. "Earnestness confirmed. Passage granted to the outer court — no further. Beyond the vestibule, others do the pricing." He stood, joints cracking like green firewood, and gestured at the stair with something horribly like courtesy.

"One kindness," he added, as Wendell got to her feet and looked down into the warm dark, "freely given, since you paid fair: the court below loves a collector. Say you've come about a defaulted account. Never say *bride*. Never say *prophecy*. And whatever they offer you to eat—" the mask-face almost smiled, "—you'll be tempted. Your body has strange talents, little taster. But nothing that grows below the mountain is food."

"How do you know what I am?"

"I keep accounts," said the toll-keeper. "I know everyone who owes. And everyone who owes—" he stepped backward into the sixth arch, folding into the shadow like a letter into an envelope, "—is food, eventually. Mind the seventh step. It minds you."

Then there was only the stair, and the warm air rising, and the moon overhead beginning its slow work toward fullness, and Wendell Yarrow standing alone in the ruin of the Tanneries with a hole in her memory shaped like a kind man's voice and a debt in her pocket she meant to collect.

She thought — she was almost sure she thought — *I'm doing this for someone. Someone with dark hair. Someone who laughs at her own footnotes.*

The name wouldn't come. She frowned at the gap where it should have been, decided the fog in her head was only exhaustion, and started down.

Behind her, at the top of the stair, the seventh arch quietly opened.
