## 8. Chapter 8

The gate did not open so much as admit her.

Wendell had expected a door, a threshold, some clean line between the world above and whatever waited below. Instead the passage simply stopped resisting her, the way a throat stops resisting when it has decided to swallow. The toll-keeper's memory-payment still sat cold behind her eyes — the taste of her mother's plum wine, gone now, an absence shaped exactly like a spoon — and she wondered if the under-court had tasted it too, and found her sweet enough to let pass.

She descended by stairs that had not been stairs a moment before. They were cut from something pale and faintly translucent, like tallow, and each step gave slightly beneath her boot as if the mountain were chewing thoughtfully. The air changed as she went down. Caerlum's air was smoke and salt and tannery-rot, all of it sharp, everything above ground insisting on itself. This air was older. It smelled of candlewax burned in a closed room, of coins handled by ten thousand hands, of the particular sweetness that gathers around fruit just before it turns.

And there were bells. Far off, muffled, ringing in no pattern she could name — or rather, ringing in a pattern she almost could, the way one almost recognizes a face across a crowded market, the way she had almost recognized Isolde's face inked on vellum a hundred years dead.

*Don't think of Isolde.* She put the thought away carefully, the way she'd been taught to set aside a dish she suspected. There would be time below for grief, if grief survived what was coming. Right now she needed the other thing her twelve years had built into her: the flat, cataloguing attention of a woman who tastes poison for a living and cannot afford to flinch at the first bite.

The stairs ended at a wall of salt.

Not salt water — salt itself, a vast crystalline face rising out of darkness, veined with light that moved through it slowly, like blood through skin. Wendell stood before it and waited, because her grandmother's rhyme had been clear about waiting: *Knock with nothing, knock with none; the court will ask you what you've done.* But there was no door, and no knocker, and after a long moment she understood that the wall was the knocker — that the light moving in its veins was looking back at her, weighing her the way she weighed the king's supper, checking her for bitterness, for rot, for anything that might sicken the court.

Then the salt opened along a seam she couldn't see, and Caerlum looked back at her.

It was her city. It was not her city.

She stepped through onto cobbles she knew — the same worn granite of Tanner's Row, the same crooked gutter-stones — except every stone was smooth here, polished by feet instead of roughened by them, and the gutters ran with clear water that smelled of nothing at all. The tanneries rose on either side in their familiar leaning rows, but they were white as bone, their vats full of light instead of lime, and where the drying-frames above should have hung skins they hung instead things she chose not to look at too closely: shapes like garments, like banners, like shed selves.

Above her, where Caerlum's grey sky should have pressed down, was a ceiling of stone veined with the same slow light as the wall. The mountain's belly. The whole inverted city hung inside it like a pearl inside a shell.

*The court beneath the mountain,* the prophecy said, and the prophecy had not lied. It had only declined to say that the court beneath the mountain was a mirror held up to the one above, showing everything backward: wealth without weight, streets without dust, and people —

That was the thing. The people.

They came out to meet her the way a market crowd comes out for any new arrival, with curiosity and calculation braided together. Courtiers, the toll-keeper had called them, and she saw now that he'd meant it precisely. They wore court dress — doublets and gowns of a cut two centuries out of fashion, brocade gone strange with age, lace yellowed to the color of old teeth — but they moved through their mirrored streets doing market-work. One carried a basket of light as if it were fish. Two argued over a ledger at a stall that sold, so far as she could tell, shadows sorted by length. A woman leaned in a doorway turning a ring around and around on her finger, faster and faster, never stopping.

Their faces were the trouble. Their faces were beautiful the way wax is beautiful, smoothed and softened, features present but indistinct, as though someone had loved them once and then handled them too much. And their eyes —

Wendell had spent twelve years learning to read eyes across a supper table, watching for the pupil-tremor of aconite, the glassy calm of hemlock, the particular panic that arrives before pain does. These eyes held none of those things. They held recognition. Every single one of them looked at her and *recognized* her, and smiled with the patient satisfaction of creditors.

"The new collector," someone said, and it moved through the crowd like oil through water. "The new collector has come."

She hadn't decided on the lie yet. It decided itself out of self-preservation, out of the deep animal instinct of a woman who survives courts: agree with the story being told about you until you understand its shape.

"I'm expected," Wendell said.

"Of course you are." The speaker stepped forward — a tall figure in a coat the color of dried wine, whose face was smoother than most, as though he had been dead longer and the polishing had more years to work with. He bowed with perfect, dreadful courtesy. "Vessin. Under-chamberlain. We have been counting the days since your grandmother's grandmother's day." His smile widened by a degree that did not touch his voice. "The ledgers do grow untidy. Debts called in from above, payments owed from below, souls mislaid, souls misplaced, souls *mislabeled*—" a small pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on silk, "—and no one above ground who thinks to audit us. Until now. You collect for the treasury?"

"For the treasury," Wendell agreed, and felt the word *treasury* land somewhere ahead of her like a lantern thrown into dark water, lighting the way and marking her position to everything that watched.

"Then walk with me, collector, and I will show you your accounts."

* * *

He walked her through the mirrored city, and Wendell made herself look. That was the discipline, the one her mentor Corvin had died teaching her: *look at what poisons you, girl, look closely, look lovingly, because the thing that kills you is also the thing that names itself.* So she looked.

The under-court was Caerlum with the hunger taken out and the accounting left in. Where the night market above sprawled in canvas and contraband, here stood permanent stalls of white wood selling permanence: bottled echoes, lengths of yesterday measured by the ell, names pressed flat between panes of glass like flowers. Where the palace above sat fat and blind on its hill, here a palace of the same silhouette rose downward from the stone ceiling, its spires pointing at the earth like roots, its windows lit with a color Wendell had no name for — not gold, not green, something that made her teeth ache faintly, the way sugar aches in a hollow tooth.

And everywhere, everywhere, the courtiers.

"They were all collectors once," Vessin said, following her gaze with something that in a living man she would have called fondness. "Or debtors. Or lenders. Or lovers who lent themselves unwisely. Everyone here bargained, collector. That is the only citizenship we recognize. You cannot be born into the under-court. You can only *owe* your way in."

"And when the debt is paid?" Wendell asked, though she already knew, the way she knew the aftertaste of arsenic before the tongue confirmed it.

Vessin turned his smooth face to her, and behind its vagueness something moved, briefly, like a fish under ice. "Paid debts are what we keep in the treasury," he said. "Would you like to see what a paid debt looks like? It is very beautiful. It weighs nothing. It burns blue."

Her heart did not stutter. Twelve years of practice kept it level, kept her breath even, kept her hands loose at her sides. But some part of her — some part that had lain quiet since the gate — began to scream in a small, controlled, professional way, the way Corvin had screamed during his last lesson, politely, while the poison took him, so that she would know what dignity cost and decide early whether she could afford it.

A woman passed them carrying a tray of small glass bells. Each bell held a curl of smoke. As she passed, the bells rang without being touched, and Wendell heard — faintly, unmistakably — voices inside them. A child laughing. A man reciting numbers. Someone singing a lullaby in a dialect dead four hundred years.

"What are they?" she asked, because asking was safer than understanding.

"Payments," Vessin said. "Memories mostly. Memories are the small coin of this place — everyone has them, few miss them rightly. A soul is the great coin. Rarely surrendered entire, usually pawned, usually inherited, usually forgotten by the family that spent it." He glanced sideways at her, and the fish under the ice turned over. "You would be astonished, collector, how many fortunes above ground are built on a soul pawned three generations back and never redeemed. Your king's own grandfather — ah, but that is treasury business. Forgive me. I gossip."

They crossed a square Wendell knew from above — Saltmarket Square, where she'd sold luck-knots not six nights past — and in its center stood the mirror-image of the Edict Stone, the great grey monolith on which the Edict of Unmaking was carved in letters a man could read from horseback. Here the stone was white, and blank. Perfectly, deliberately blank.

"Our edict is simpler," Vessin said, catching her stare. "*Everything may be bought. Everything may be sold. Everything may be collected.* No unmaking here, collector. Nothing is ever destroyed below. It is only ever transferred." He spread his smooth hands. "Which is why we find the Edict above so — useful. A city that forbids magic must send its magic somewhere. It runs downhill, like everything else. Like water. Like debt. Like you."

Wendell said nothing. She had learned long ago that silence was the only dish she could serve a court that could not be poisoned afterward.

But Vessin seemed satisfied with her quiet, and turned toward a street she recognized with a lurch: Archivist's Lane, where Isolde worked, where Isolde had first — 

No. Careful. Careful. She banked the thought like a fire at bedtime, coals banked, flame killed, heat kept.

Archivist's Lane below was not lined with archives. It was lined with doors, hundreds of them, narrow doors packed shoulder to shoulder up both walls, each door labeled with a name in script that shifted when she tried to fix her eyes on it. And out of certain doors, at intervals, came the sound of voices saying single sentences over and over — a name, a date, a promise — the way a clock says the hour.

"Debt-doors," Vessin said. "Every bargain struck with the court gets its door. Behind each one, the terms are kept forever. Some of our residents live quite comfortably inside their own contracts. Some of them are the contract." He paused before one door, smaller than the rest, its label worn nearly smooth. From within came a voice, thin and patient, saying: *I will pay. I will pay. I will pay.* "This one has been paying for ninety years. Very faithful. Never once late." He sighed, and the sigh had dust in it. "Of course, the interest compounds faster than the voice can count. It always does. That is not cruelty, collector. That is arithmetic."

"Is there a door," Wendell asked, and her voice came out steady, thank the twelve poisoned years, steady as a scale, "for the Amberdale accounts? I'm told my line collects old debts. Family debts. Inherited paper."

Something happened to Vessin then. Not a change in his face — his face was incapable of change, that was its horror — but a change in the quality of his attention, the way a room changes when a window opens. He regarded her for a long moment, and around them the market-noise of the dead seemed to lean in, bells and ledgers and the endless patient voices all going quieter, as though the whole city were an ear.

"Family debts," he repeated. "Yes. The Amberdale paper is old. Older than the family name, in fact." And there it was again, that knife laid flat on silk: *older than the name.* "You will want the treasury, then. All old paper comes home to the treasury eventually." He turned and walked on, and Wendell followed, and behind them the little debt-door went on whispering, *I will pay, I will pay*, and she thought: *that's what I'm doing, that's all any of us are doing.*

* * *

The treasury announced itself by temperature.

The mirrored streets grew warmer as they climbed — for the city was inverted, and climbing meant descending, and Wendell's legs ached with the wrongness of ascending toward the mountain's heart — and the light in the stone veins thickened from white to amber to a deep arterial red, and then the street opened into a cavern, and Wendell forgot, for one full breath, how to breathe.

The treasury was a vault of salt the size of a cathedral, and every surface of it burned.

Flames stood in their thousands upon thousands in niches carved into the walls, floor to unseen ceiling, ranked like candles in a church, like bottles in an apothecary, like the population of a city — because that was what they were. Most burned the ordinary gold of ordinary fire. But scattered among them, rarer than saffron, burned flames of blue. Small, contained, perfectly still — flames that did not flicker, that gave off light without heat, that burned with the terrible composure of things that intended to go on burning exactly so forever.

Souls. Pawned souls. Paid debts. *Very beautiful. It weighs nothing. It burns blue.*

There were perhaps forty of them. Forty blue flames in a cathedral of gold, and Wendell's eyes went along them one by one with the methodical horror of a taster working down a banquet table, and stopped.

Ninth niche from the western arch. Small blue flame, no taller than a thumb. And beneath it, on a brass plate gone green with age, words in a fine archival hand — the hand she knew better than her own, the hand that annotated margins, that wrote her name on folded notes left in library books:

*Isolde Vane-Marlowe. Pawned 1731 by Ambrose Marlowe against the fortune of his house. Redeemable by debtor's bloodline only, before the moon completes its turn.*

The handwriting was Isolde's. Not similar to Isolde's. *Was* Isolde's — the exact slant, the exact way the descenders curled, the exact pressure of a pen held by someone who had been writing since she was six years old and would write the same way a century before she was born.

The vertigo hit Wendell like a wave hitting a seawall. She had known — she had *known* since the archive, since the margin, since Isolde's white face over the open book — but knowing was one substance and seeing was another, and this was the seeing. Her lover's soul, burning in a niche. Her lover's handwriting, labeling her lover's captivity, written before her lover's grandmother was born, as if Isolde herself had come down here in some earlier life and signed the inventory.

*Or as if the court writes in her hand,* whispered something cold in the back of Wendell's mind, *because the court wrote her first. Because the book came before the woman. Because she has always been theirs, and everything since — the archive, the lane, you — has been the flame arranging its own wick.*

"Ah," said Vessin softly, beside her. "I see the paper you've come to collect."

"It's mine," Wendell said. Her voice was steady. She was distantly proud of that, and infinitely disgusted. "Family paper. I've authority to redeem."

"Undoubtedly. The bloodline clause is quite clear." He gestured, and a figure detached itself from between the niches — the warden, though Wendell did not learn the title until later, a squat shape wrapped in keys that clinked with the sound of the glass bells, of voices, of everything small and paid. The warden looked at her, and looked at the blue flame, and looked at her again, and Wendell watched the arithmetic move behind its eyes like a moneylender's abacus.

"Redemption requires payment of equal weight," the warden said. Its voice was the sound a ledger makes closing. "The shard is small. But souls weigh more than gold, collector. Souls weigh more than anything. Come. Let us weigh you, and see what you are worth."

Behind her, far above and impossibly audible, the bells of the under-court rang out a pattern she finally, horribly, recognized: the pattern of Caerlum's moon-clock, marking the twelfth turn nearly done.

One turn remained. And the warden was holding out a pair of scales.
