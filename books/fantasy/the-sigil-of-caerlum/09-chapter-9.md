## 9. Chapter 9

The treasury of souls did not look like a treasury.

Wendell had imagined vaults — iron doors, chains, the grim architecture of hoarded things. What she found instead was a garden gone wrong: a field of pale grass that whispered when it moved, though there was no wind beneath the mountain, and rising out of that grass in rows upon rows upon rows, small blue flames.

Each flame stood about the height of a candle's tongue, no taller than her hand, and each burned without heat, without smoke, without flicker. They burned *steadily*, and it was their steadiness that frightened her most. Fire was supposed to dance. These flames had been standing at attention for so long they had forgotten they were ever anything else.

She counted the nearest row out of habit — cataloguing was how she kept fear at arm's length, the same way she catalogued poisons by aftertaste, the bitter almond of cyanide, the garlic ghost of arsenic, the copper coin of something rarer and crueler. Forty flames to a row. She could see perhaps sixty rows before the dark swallowed the field, and the dark went back further than sight could argue with.

Thousands of souls. Tens of thousands.

*Every courtier was once someone who bargained,* she reminded herself, and then, because the thought demanded its twin: *and every flame was once someone who couldn't pay.*

The grass parted for her feet as she walked, closing behind her heels like water. It made no sound now, only when the flames breathed — and they did breathe, she realized, a slow in-and-out of light, the rhythm of sleep or of patience. Somewhere among all this blue, one flame wore Isolde's name.

She had come down through the mirrored city with a debt-collector's seal pressed from candle-wax and grave-dirt, wearing a coat she'd stolen from a dead man's closet in the inverted market, and she had told herself a dozen times that she was here as a functionary, an instrument of the court's own ledger-law. But walking into the treasury, past the whispering grass and the patient flames, Wendell Yarrow stopped pretending. She was not a collector. She was a thief of the only kind the under-court respected: someone come to steal back what should never have been sold.

A path opened ahead of her — the grass bowing aside in a long aisle — and at the end of it stood a small stone pavilion, and in the pavilion sat a warden.

* * *

The warden was neither old nor young, which is to say it had been both long enough ago that the distinction had worn smooth. Its face was like a coin rubbed featureless by centuries of handling. It wore robes the color of wet slate, and before it, floating at the height of its chest, hovered a scale.

Not a merchant's scale. The beam was bone-white and longer than Wendell was tall, and from each end hung a pan of woven silver, empty, waiting.

"You are not a collector," the warden said. Its voice was quiet and vast at once, like the sea heard through a wall. "Collectors do not walk the treasury aisles reading labels."

Wendell's hand did not go to the knife at her hip. In Caerlum that gesture meant negotiation; down here she suspected it meant confession. Instead she bowed the way she'd seen the courtiers bow — shallow, precise, hands open.

"I've come to redeem a debt," she said. "Paid in full, by the bloodline, before the moon completes its turn. Your own law allows it."

"Law." The warden turned the word over like a coin it suspected of being clipped. "You have been reading our statutes, taster."

The title landed like a hand on the back of her neck. Wendell kept her face still. Twelve years at the king's table had taught her stillness worth more than any charm she'd ever sold. "Then you know I understand contracts," she said. "And you know I know that yours are binding on both parties. The debt of the Amberdale line — the soul pawned by Aldous Amberdale, four generations past — may be redeemed by his blood before the moon's thirteenth turn closes. I am here to close it."

"Aldous Amberdale." The warden's featureless face inclined, and somewhere far out in the blue field, a thousand flames breathed in unison, one slow pulse of light. "Yes. He sat where you stand. He wept rather beautifully, if I remember. They usually do, the ones selling. It is the ones buying back who arrive dry-eyed and dangerous."

"I'm not dangerous," Wendell said.

"No," the warden agreed mildly. "You are *prepared*. That is worse."

It lifted one hand, slate sleeve falling back from fingers too long by half, and gestured. The grass aisle ahead of them lit — not all at once but in sequence, row by row, flame by flame waking as the light ran down the field like a finger drawn along a shelf of books, until it stopped.

Three hundred paces away, give or take, one flame burned a shade different from its neighbors. Not brighter. Bluer, somehow, the way deep water is bluer than shallow — a blue with weight behind it. And around its base, instead of the bare pale grass, grew a single ring of small white flowers that Wendell did not recognize and suspected did not grow anywhere in the world above.

"There," said the warden. "Your redemption."

Wendell was walking before she decided to walk. The grass parted, the aisle unspooled, and the nearer she came the more certain she became, in the way that has nothing to do with evidence and everything to do with the body — the way she knew arsenic by its ghost of garlic before any test confirmed it. The flame stood in its ring of white flowers and breathed its slow light, and beside it, planted in the earth like a grave-marker, stood a small tablet of black glass.

She knelt. The label cut into the glass was fine as engraving, deliberate as penmanship:

*Isolde Vane-who-was-Amberdale. Pawned 4 generations. Interest accruing.*

But it was not the words that undid her. It was the hand. She knew that hand the way she knew her own heartbeat — the little upward kick on the *l*, the *d* that always leaned slightly drunk to the right, the crossbar of the *t* that never quite touched the stem because Isolde wrote fast and thought faster. Isolde's handwriting, on a soul-shard buried under a mountain since before either of their grandmothers drew breath.

"She labeled herself?" Wendell's voice came out cracked.

"The shards carry the name their owner will bear at the hour of claiming," the warden said, arriving soundlessly beside her. "The contract writes itself forward as well as back. Yours is a clever lover, taster. She annotated her own damnation. Archivists do tend toward thoroughness."

Wendell reached out — she couldn't not — and cupped her palm above the flame. No heat. No cold either. Something between, something like the air over a letter just written, like breath held before good news. The flame leaned toward her hand, a fraction, the first unsteady thing it had done in four generations, and Wendell felt it the way you feel a sleeping animal stir against you.

"Oh," she said softly. "Hello, love. I'm here. I'm sorry it took me so long to find you."

The flame pulsed once, twice, quick as a caught breath, and settled.

Behind her, the warden made a sound that might have been a sigh in some century older than this one.

"That," it said, "is going to complicate the arithmetic."

* * *

They walked back to the pavilion together, and the scale waited for them, pans swaying gently as if pleased by company. Wendell stood with her hands flat on the stone counter while the warden produced — from nowhere, or from inside itself, she genuinely could not tell — a book. Black glass covers, pages of something thin and grey and soft as moth-wing.

"Redemption is permitted," the warden said. "I will not insult us both by pretending otherwise. The statute stands. Bloodline pays, moon turns, shard returns. But." 

There is always a *but*, Wendell thought. Down here the *but* was probably load-bearing.

"But a pawned thing is not a lost thing," the warden continued. "It is held against value. And value must be matched. You cannot simply lift a soul from my treasury the way a thief lifts a ring. The scales—" it nodded at the great bone-white beam, "—are older than I am, and I am older than your Edict, older than your city, older than the word *city*. When a debt is redeemed, the treasury must be made whole. A soul leaves; a soul's weight enters. That is not cruelty. That is balance. The alternative is that everything down here comes apart, and believe me, taster, you would not enjoy the arithmetic of *that*."

"So I pay," Wendell said. "Gold. The Amberdales have money. Their fortune was bought with this soul — let it buy her back."

"Gold." The warden almost smiled, if a smoothed-over face can be said to do anything almost. "Do you know why we accept memories at the gate? Why the tolls below are paid in recollection rather than coin?"

"Because gold is worthless underground," Wendell said. "Everyone knows that story."

"Everyone tells that story wrong." The warden laid one long finger on the counter. "Gold is not worthless. Gold is simply *light*. Everything you people dig up and mint and hoard weighs almost nothing against what matters. A king's ransom might balance a single afternoon. Souls—" it glanced out at the endless field of breathing blue, "—souls weigh more than mountains. Memories weigh somewhere in between. Which makes memories the only currency most mortals can afford."

The floor did not tilt, but Wendell felt it tilt anyway. "How much," she said. "For hers."

The warden opened the black-glass book. The grey pages turned themselves, whispering, and stopped on an entry written in that same familiar slanting hand — no. Not Isolde's. Older. Aldous Amberdale's, four generations downstream into hers. The contract, self-writing forward.

The warden read in silence. Then it looked up at her with its coin-blank face, and Wendell had spent twelve years reading faces across a poisoned dinner table, and what she read there was not malice. It was pity, which was infinitely worse.

"Four generations of interest," the warden said. "Accrued against the family fortune that was purchased, against the years the shard has stood in my keeping, against the nearness of the claiming hour — value rises as the deadline approaches, taster; scarcity is scarcity everywhere. The redemption price, payable in memory-weight—"

It turned the book toward her. Numbers crawled up the page like insects and arranged themselves, and Wendell, who could calculate poison doses in her head faster than any physician in Caerlum, read them three times and felt the numbers refuse to shrink.

"That's—" Her voice failed. She started again. "That's a life. That's every memory I have. That's more than—"

"It is approximately one childhood, one apprenticeship, and one love affair," the warden said, with terrible gentleness. "Weighted, of course, by intensity. Intense memories weigh far more than dull ones. The happiest years of a mortal life are, I regret to inform you, the most expensive."

The scale hung above them, swaying its empty silver pans, patient as the flames in the field.

"And if I can't pay it all?"

"Then partial payment purchases nothing. The shard does not leave this field until the scales balance fully. Half a redemption is a coin dropped down a well." The warden closed the book. "Though I will note — and I note this because in four thousand years I have learned that mortals deserve to know what they are walking into — that your body is an oddity, taster."

Wendell went very still.

"I have weighed ten thousand debtors at these scales," the warden said. "Poison-eaters interest me. You have spent years making yourself into a vessel that cannot easily be claimed, filled, or altered. The court's magic slides off you like rain off wax. Whatever you decide to pour onto these pans—" the blank face tipped toward the scale, "—it will have to be poured honestly. There is no bargaining with the beam. It weighs what is truly there."

Somewhere out in the blue field, three hundred paces away, a small flame with a ring of white flowers around its base breathed its slow, patient light — and leaned, Wendell would have sworn it leaned, toward the woman who had called it *love*.

The warden folded its long hands.

"So, taster. What is she worth to you?"

And Wendell Yarrow, who had survived twelve years of arsenic breakfasts and nightly crimes of kindness, who had crossed the mirror-city and lied to a toll-keeper and knelt in a field of the damned, opened her mouth to answer — and found, for the first time since she was a child, that she had absolutely no idea what she was about to say.

* * *

She asked for one night.

The warden granted it with the indifference of an institution — the treasury did not close, time below ran sideways anyway, and the moon overhead was turning regardless. "Sit with her, if you wish," it said. "Debtors often do. It makes the arithmetic harder afterward, in my experience. But the heart insists, and I have stopped arguing with hearts. They always lose, and they always insist."

So Wendell went back down the aisle the grass made for her, and knelt in the white flowers, and stayed.

She talked to the flame for a long time. About the night market, and the luck-knots, and the customer who'd warned her about inquisitors buying charms as evidence. About Hale, whose name she spat like a seed. About the taste of the king's soup that evening, weeks ago now, the faint bitterness she'd flagged and swallowed and never reported, because some poisons were only ambition wearing a sauce. The flame listened the way Isolde listened — completely, head tilted, waiting for the part that mattered — and when Wendell finally ran out of reportage and sat in silence with her hand hovering over the light, the shard did the thing that broke her.

It pulsed in the rhythm of Isolde's laugh. Quick, quick, slow. The laugh she made when Wendell said something wicked about the court, the one she tried to smother in archive dust and never quite managed.

"They're going to ask me to pay in memories," Wendell told her. "And I have a horrible feeling I already know which ones weigh the most."

The flame breathed. Quick, quick, slow.

Far above, beyond miles of mountain, the twelfth turn of the moon rolled toward its thirteenth like a coin spinning toward rest, and Wendell began, quietly and methodically, to make a list of everything she could not bear to lose — knowing even as she wrote it in her mind that the scales would want exactly those things, and nothing else, and all of it.
