## 10. Chapter 10

The first poison Wendell ever swallowed was honey.

That was how Master Corvane had taught her, in the cold back room of the taster's quarters where the light came through horn windows the color of old bruises. He had set a spoon of honey before her, golden and ordinary, and told her to eat it. She had been nine years old and starving, newly arrived from the tannery rows with her grandmother's rhymes still stuck in her teeth, and she had eaten it without hesitation because it was honey, because she was hungry, because a child does not yet know that kindness can be a curriculum.

Then he had shown her the vial. Aconite, steeped in the honey for three days until the sweetness became a lie with a body count.

"You'll be sick," he said, not unkindly. "You may wish you were dead. But you will not die, if I have measured rightly, and tomorrow you will taste that flower on your tongue for the rest of your life. Every time. That is the gift I am giving you: the knowing."

She had vomited into the copper basin until her ribs ached and her eyes wept and her small hands shook against the floorboards. And when it was over — when the convulsions had wrung themselves out and left her lying sweat-slick and hollow as a gourd — Corvane had crouched beside her and pressed one dry palm to her forehead, the way her grandmother used to when fever came to the tenements.

"Now," he said, "you are beginning."

* * *

Twelve years. She counted them now the way other people counted coins or sins or children, by their weight rather than their number.

Year one was arsenic. It was always arsenic first; Corvane said the palate had to learn humility before it learned anything else. Arsenic had almost no taste at all — that was its genius and its cruelty — only a faint sweetness like a candle guttering, and then nothing, and then hours later the cramping began, the vomiting, the burning in the gut as though someone had poured embers down her throat. She learned to feel it not with her tongue but lower, in the soft warning place beneath her sternum, a flutter like a bird trapped behind her ribs. The court physicians called it fancy. Corvane called it the second tongue, and made her trust it above all others.

By year two she could take a grain and a half and remain upright through supper, smiling her taster's smile at the king's table while her blood quietly fought a war no one else could see. By year three she had stopped vomiting most mornings. Her body had learned what Corvane promised it would learn: not immunity — there was no such thing, he was adamant about that, no true immunity, only tolerance, only a fortress built one stone of suffering at a time — but something close enough to pass for it in a court full of men who wanted the king dead and did not much care who stood between.

The breakfasts were the worst. Poisoners loved breakfast. Morning was when the kitchen changed hands, when the night scullions went off duty and the day cooks arrived sleepy and unwatched, when a vial could slip from a sleeve into a porridge pot and no one would be the wiser until the king turned blue at his prayers. So Wendell ate breakfast eleven times out of ten, every morning of her life from age nine onward, spooning up whatever the royal kitchens produced after she had drawn a sample into her testing cup and found nothing in it that her tongue or her second tongue could name.

There were mornings — she remembered them with a tenderness that surprised her now — when the porridge was simply porridge, salted with honey from the southern apiaries, and she ate it slowly in the grey light of the tasting room and felt, briefly, like a girl instead of an instrument. Those mornings were rarer than they should have been.

* * *

Corvane died in her sixth year, and he died teaching.

It happened the way everything happened at court: quietly, in plain sight, with everyone agreeing afterward that it must have been inevitable. There was a banquet for the Ambassador of Vell — fish, seven courses, wine from the coastal presses — and somewhere between the fourth course and the fifth, between the sea-bream in sorrel sauce and the quails in pastry coffins, something entered the wine that should not have been there. Wendell tasted it first. Of course she tasted it first; that was the whole architecture of her life, she tasted things first so that others might never taste them at all. A bitterness at the back of the throat like almond skin, like peach pits crushed and left to rot, a ghost of cyanide wearing perfume.

"Stop," she said, and her voice cracked across the banquet hall the way a whip cracks, and forty nobles froze with cups halfway to their lips.

But Corvane had already drunk. He had drunk because that was his role in the choreography — the old master drank first so the young taster's word would carry weight, so no one could say the girl was hysterical, ambitious, mistaken. He had drunk his portion of the fifth-course pour thirty seconds before she raised her own cup, and by the time she spoke the bitterness was already blooming in him like a night flower opening.

He did not fall dramatically. He set down his cup, straightened his napkin on his knee, and looked at her across the length of the table with an expression she would carry for the rest of her life — not fear, not reproach, but *attention*, fierce and final, the look of a man spending his last minutes on the only lesson left worth giving.

"Breathe," he told her, while his lips went grey. "Note it. Almond, bitter, late-rising. Write it in your book tonight. And Wendell—" His hand found hers on the tablecloth and gripped it with a strength that frightened her, a strength being spent all at once from some last reserve. "Never drink after you've spoken. Promise me. The moment you know, you stop. Your life is the tool. Keep it sharp."

They were the last coherent words he said. He lived four more days in the infirmary, mostly unconscious, his body doing exactly what hers had been trained to do and failing anyway, because tolerance is not salvation, because twelve years of his own fortress had only bought him four days where another man would have had four minutes. Four days during which the palace hummed around him and the king sent flowers and no one was ever punished, because the poisoner was never found, because poisoners are never found, that is the whole grammar of the trade.

She kept his promise. She kept his book, too — his little leather ledger of tastes, decades of poisons catalogued in his cramped hand: *hemlock, mousey, numbs the tongue's edge first; cantharides, hot, a liar that mimics spice; thallium, sweet as sugar, the kindest and cruelest of them.* On the last page, in fresher ink, he had written a single line that she did not understand for years:

*A taster's body is a ledger too. Everything written on it stays.*

* * *

Everything written on it stays.

She understood it finally in her ninth year, the winter the under-court's whispers first reached even the palace kitchens — stories of the mirrored court beneath the mountain, of bargains struck in salt and shadow, of souls weighed against gold in a treasury where the dead served wine. A scullery girl swore her cousin had sold six months of his life for a luck-charm and woken with his hair white. A carter claimed the toll-keeper at the old gate took memories as payment and gave back nothing but passage.

Wendell had listened with her professional ear, the way she listened to everything — sorting truth from flavor, flavor from threat. And what she heard, beneath the superstition, was mechanism. The under-court's magic worked, if it worked at all, by *taking*. It reached into a person and drew out what it wanted: years, memories, soul-stuff, the soft interior wealth that every living person carried whether they knew it or not. Every bargain was a kind of poisoning in reverse — instead of putting something harmful into a body, it pulled something essential out.

And her body, she realized slowly, sitting very still in the tasting room with Corvane's ledger open on her knees, was the most thoroughly defended vessel in Caerlum.

Not against knives. Not against grief. But against *taking* — against the slow extraction, the dissolving, the thing that magic did when it unpicked a person thread by thread. Twelve years of arsenic and aconite, of hemlock and heavy metals, of swallowing death in measured doses until death lost its grip on her — her cells had rebuilt themselves around the assault the way bone knits around a break, harder at the fracture, denser at the wound. She was a woman made of scar tissue all the way down. And scar tissue, she thought, staring at her own hands on the ledger's cover, is the body's answer to invasion. Scar tissue does not open easily. Scar tissue does not let go of what it holds.

She tested it once, cautiously, in the way of her profession: a single minor charm, bought from a trembling hedge-witch in the night market, a forgetting-charm meant to erase a bad hour. She held it against her palm and felt it press and slide and find no purchase, like a key fitted to a lock that had long since been bricked over. The witch had stared at her and crossed herself with both hands.

Whatever the under-court wanted from her, it would have to work for it. She was not a door. She was a wall that walked.

* * *

Now, on the eve of her descent — though she did not yet know she would descend; now was still the night of Isolde's discovery, the night the prophecy lay open between them like a wound — now, sitting in Isolde's archive room with the lamp turned low, Wendell flexed her hands and felt the whole strange history of her body settle into purpose.

Isolde was watching her across the table, face pale, the thirteenth bride inked in the margin of a century-old book looking up at them both with Isolde's own steady eyes.

"The moon turns twelve times more," Isolde said. Or fewer — the counting was confused, the dread making arithmetic unreliable. "And then they come for—"

"They will not find me easy to collect." Wendell heard her own voice come out flat and certain, the taster's voice, the voice she used at the king's table when forty lives hung on her palate. "I have spent twelve years teaching my body not to surrender what it holds. Arsenic. Hemlock. Things whose names I only say inside my head. Whatever waits beneath the mountain takes from people — I've heard the stories, I believe them. Let it try to take from me."

Isolde reached across the table and took her hand. Her thumb traced the calluses there, the burn-scars, the faint yellowed ridges along the fingers where a hundred small poisons had left their signatures — Corvane's ledger written in flesh.

"It hurt," Isolde said softly. Not a question.

"Every day of it." Wendell turned her hand over and laced their fingers together. "And I thought it was only survival. Eating death so the king could live. Keeping myself alive out of stubbornness and habit." She looked at their joined hands, at the lamp, at the prophecy with its cruel patient ink. "But maybe nothing is wasted. Maybe every morning I didn't die was a stone in a wall I'd need later. Maybe I was building this fortress for a siege I couldn't see coming — a siege over you."

Outside the archive window, the moon hung low and swollen over the mountain, one turn closer to full than it had been the night before. Wendell watched it and felt, beneath the fear, the quiet hard certainty of a woman who has been poisoned on schedule for twelve years and never once missed a meal.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow she would go looking for the old gate, and the toll-keeper, and the road down.

Her body, at least, would know what to do. It had been preparing all her life.
