## 11. Chapter 11

The warden's scales hung from a chain of knucklebones, and they weighed nothing Wendell could see.

She stood in the treasury of souls with her palms pressed flat against her thighs, the way she stood before the king's table when the first course arrived and every eye in the hall expected her to swallow. The posture of a woman whose body was her instrument. Twelve years of tasting had taught her that fear lived in the hands first — they trembled, they sweated, they betrayed you to any observer worth fearing. So she kept them still.

The shard floated in its niche of salt: a flame no larger than a sparrow's heart, burning blue and patient, labeled in Isolde's own looping hand. *Isolde Amberdale. Held in trust.* The handwriting undid her more than the fire did. Somewhere in a life she could not yet imagine losing, Isolde had sat at a desk with an ink-stained nib and written her own name onto a ledger line as if it were nothing, as if she were cataloguing a shipment of candles.

"Terms," the warden said again, because Wendell had not answered.

The warden had been beautiful once, perhaps. Now beauty on it was like embroidery on a shroud — evidence of care applied to something past caring for. Its skin had the grey translucence of tallow, and where its eyes should have been there were two small flames, banked low, the color of old coals. It wore the robes of a court chamberlain, cut in a fashion two centuries out of date, and it moved the way water moves under ice: without hurry, without mercy, because hurry is a thing only the living require.

"A soul for a soul," it said. "That is the first term. You have a soul, food-taster. Small and scorched and stubborn, but the court beneath weighs all souls equally. Bring yours to the scale and walk away carrying hers."

"And the second term?"

The warden's coal-eyes brightened by a degree. "Memories," it said, and the word came out soft, almost tender, the way a poisoner says *harmless*. "Of equal weight to the shard. Weighed and measured here, on the scales of the under-court, which do not lie and do not forgive. Pay us what she is worth in what you remember, and the debt dissolves, and the bride goes free, and you climb back up into your city of salt and iron none the poorer — except for what you paid."

Wendell looked at the scales. She thought about weight.

A soul, she knew from the ledgers Isolde had copied out for her in the archive, in that last golden week when they had still believed research was a weapon: a soul weighed whatever the holder said it weighed. That was the obscenity of the under-court. No fixed measure, no honest coin. The court beneath set the price after it saw the customer.

But memories — memories had texture, and texture could be judged. And Wendell Yarrow had spent twelve years learning to judge what entered her body, drop by drop, course by course.

"How does it work?" she asked.

* * *

It worked like this: the warden produced a pair of cups.

They were made of horn, worn smooth at the rims by centuries of mouths, and one of them was already full. The liquid inside was dark and still as a well at midnight, and when Wendell leaned over it she saw not her reflection but a kitchen — her grandmother's kitchen, the blue enamel kettle, the window with the crack shaped like a river running through the glass. A memory, poured out and waiting.

"You drink," the warden said. "What you drink, we keep. The scale will tell us when the weight is met. You will feel each memory leave the way one feels a tooth leave the jaw: briefly, and then forever."

"And I choose which ones?"

"You may guide the pouring." The warden's mouth did something that on a living face would have been called a smile. "Most debtors begin carefully. They offer the small things. The name of a childhood dog. The taste of a fruit they disliked. And then, when the scale demands more, they discover that the small things were never theirs to spare — that everything in a life is load-bearing, once you start pulling out the beams." It gestured at the empty cup. "You are a poison-taster, yes? The court knows your profession. The court finds it amusing. You have spent years swallowing deaths so another might live. Now you will spend yourself the same way. There is poetry in it. The court enjoys poetry."

"I don't," Wendell said.

"No," the warden agreed. "The living rarely do."

She thought of Isolde's face when she'd shown her the prophecy page — that terrible vertigo, the inked margin holding a face she saw in her mirror every morning. She thought of the ledger entry: *a soul pawned, redeemable by bloodline.* She thought of the moon above them both, invisible now behind a mountain's worth of stone, grinding through its twelfth turn like a millstone with her lover caught between the stones.

She picked up the empty cup.

"What happens to me," Wendell asked, "if I pay too much?"

The warden regarded her with its banked little fires. "You mean: how much can a woman lose and remain a woman? I cannot say. Some who pay heavily walk out whole, missing only what they gave. Some walk out hollow, unable to say why the world tastes of ash. Some—" it paused, and the pause had weight, "—some forget why they came. They stand at the gate with their purchase in their hands, looking at it as though it belonged to someone else. We call those the unmoored. They wander the upper passages until the toll-keepers find them, and then—" it spread its tallow hands, "—the court finds uses for everyone."

"And if I stop partway? If I pay some and the weight isn't met?"

"Then the payment is forfeit and the shard remains, and you will have unmade yourself for nothing. Partial payments are not refunded. The court does not deal in fractions of grief."

Wendell set the cup down on the salt shelf with a click that echoed strangely, as though the treasury were much larger than it appeared, as though a thousand niches full of small blue flames were listening.

"Show me the scale," she said. "I want to see how it measures."

* * *

The scales of the under-court did not lie and did not forgive, and they were also, Wendell discovered, entirely indifferent to her.

They stood in an alcove off the treasury proper: two pans of black iron suspended from a beam of bone-white wood, and neither pan held anything, and yet the beam tilted, minutely, toward the left, as though the emptiness itself had opinions. When the warden lifted Isolde's shard from its niche with tongs of green glass — handling it, Wendell noticed, more gently than it handled anything else — and set it in the right pan, the left pan sank. Sank to meet a weight that wasn't there.

"There," the warden said. "That is her weight. In memory. What you must pour until the pans rest level."

"It looks like nothing."

"Everything looks like nothing, down here. That is rather our condition." The warden withdrew the shard and returned it to the salt. "You may begin whenever the moon permits. Which is to say: now."

Wendell closed her eyes.

She built the list the way she built a defense against a banquet: strongest poisons last. She would not touch Isolde. Not first. Not second. There were decades of Wendell Yarrow that had nothing to do with an archivist with ink on her fingers, and those decades would buy the time she needed. Her grandmother's rhymes. Her apprenticeship. The taste of the first poison she'd ever swallowed, almond-sweet and lying. The night market. The luck-knots. Her mother's voice singing off-key. All of it expendable. All of it, she told herself, ballast.

She opened her eyes and took the horn cup.

"The taste of my grandmother's kitchen," she said. "The bread she baked on feast days. The smell of it. How the crust cracked. What she said when she gave me the first piece."

The warden inclined its head, and poured from the full cup into hers, and the darkness in the cup swirled, and for one moment Wendell smelled warm bread so vividly her mouth flooded, her chest ached, she was eight years old and safe—

She drank.

It left her the way the warden had promised: briefly, and then forever. There was a moment of warmth going down, a moment of something bright flaring behind her ribs like a struck match, and then a coldness spreading outward from the place where the warmth had been, smooth and complete, like ice sealing over a pond. She reached for the memory of the bread and found a shape where it had been — she knew, abstractly, that her grandmother had baked bread, that this was a fact about the world — but the smell was gone. The crack of the crust was gone. The words spoken over the first piece were gone, and worse, she could no longer know what kind of words they had been. The space where they'd lived was polished smooth, like a stone in a riverbed.

On the scale, the left pan rose. A fraction. An inch of iron climbing toward level.

"Well?" she said, and her voice sounded strange to her, thinner.

"That was a heavy one, for a beginning." The warden made a note on a slate with a stylus of yellowed bone. "Grandmothers weigh more than kings, in this court. Continue."

* * *

She paid in installments.

The taste of river water on a summer day when she was nine and someone — a friend, a cousin, the name gone even then — had dared her to drink straight from the current. Gone. The pan rose.

Her mother's lullaby, the melody stripped out of her head so completely that when she tried to hum it afterward her throat produced only silence, only the shape of humming without any song inside it. The pan rose. She hadn't known a melody could be surgically removed, leaving the longing for it intact. That was the cruelest part: the wanting stayed. She wanted to hear the lullaby with a desperation that had no object, like hunger in a dream of eating.

The face of her first schoolmistress. The layout of the house she grew up in — she kept the knowledge that she had grown up somewhere, but the rooms went dark one by one as she drank, the hallway last, the hallway she ran down a thousand times, and then the hallway was gone too and she was a person who had simply begun, unrooted, like a flag with no pole.

The night she earned her first coin in the night market, selling a luck-knot to a laundress with cracked red hands, and the laundress had wept and kissed her knuckles and called her *little saint*. She remembered the coin. She remembered the weeping, distantly, as a reported fact. But the feeling of being called a saint — the sudden terrifying sweetness of being good at kindness — that drained out of her like color from cloth, and she sat on the treasury floor holding the empty horn cup and wondered why her eyes were wet, since clearly nothing sad had happened.

The pan climbed. The pan climbed. The warden noted each installment on its slate with tiny bone scratches, and Wendell began to hate the sound the way she hated the sound of the king's fork touching his plate: a small noise that meant everything.

"Rest," the warden said, at some point that might have been an hour or a night later. Time below moved like syrup. "The scale grows impatient but not yet demanding. Rest, and consider your next pours."

Wendell considered.

She sat against the salt wall with the shard burning its quiet blue a few feet away, and she tried to take inventory of herself, the way she'd inventory her stock of charms before market night. Name: Wendell Yarrow. Profession: taster. Purpose: standing in a hole under a mountain, buying back a soul.

Whose soul?

The thought arrived without drama, the way rot arrives in a wall — quietly, behind the plaster, discovered late. She looked at the little blue flame and felt... recognition, yes. Obligation, certainly. She had come here for this; the purpose was stitched into her like a label. But when she reached for the reason, the reason was a door painted on stone. She knew the word *Isolde* the way she knew the word *prophecy*: important, load-bearing, hers. But the face would not assemble. She could remember that Isolde had a face — of course she had a face — but when Wendell reached for it, she found herself reaching for the memory of a portrait described to her once, secondhand, by a stranger.

*Not yet,* she told herself, and the telling shook her. Because that phrasing implied a schedule. Because some part of her, some deep and practical part trained by twelve years of banquets, had already begun triaging. Had already begun setting aside the memories she could afford to lose, and marking the others, and among the marked ones — protected, ringed with warning like a dish she'd refused at table — was a rainy afternoon, and a library, and a voice saying something she could no longer hear but somehow still felt in her sternum, the way you feel a bell after it stops ringing.

"Warden," she said. "How many installments remain?"

The warden consulted its slate. The coal-fires of its eyes flickered, and Wendell understood suddenly, with a taster's certainty, that the warden was pleased. That the court beneath had seen thousands of debtors sit exactly here, doing exactly this arithmetic, and that the arithmetic always ended the same way, and that the ending was, in some sense, the product. The court didn't want her gold or her soul entire. The court wanted precisely this: a woman paying herself out coin by coin, unmooring, becoming lighter and easier to keep.

"Four," the warden said gently. "Perhaps five, depending on weight. Choose wisely, food-taster. The heaviest memories go farthest."

* * *

She chose three that night, because waiting cost her something too — every hour she sat, the shard burned and the moon turned and somewhere above, a bride was being measured for a wedding she'd never agreed to.

She paid the taste of snow, the first snow of her sixth winter, tongue-out, catching flakes, and the specific cold-sharp joy of it, and the pan rose and she felt the winter go dim behind glass.

She paid the sound of the night market at its peak — the fiddles and the haggling and the rain on canvas awnings, the whole roaring music of the only place she had ever been free — and when it left her she retained the knowledge that markets existed and that she attended one, but the music was gone, and she grieved it without knowing what she was grieving, tears sliding down her face while the warden scratched its bone stylus and observed her with its patient banked fires.

She paid her mentor. Master Corwin, who had taught her the craft of surviving plates, who had died at a banquet table with his own cup in his hand so that she could live and learn from his autopsy what killed him. She paid his face, his voice, his terrible jokes about arsenic. She kept the lesson — the body keeps what it needs, the lessons had sunk into her very blood, they could not be drunk out — but the man was erased, and she sat afterward staring at her own scarred, hardened hands, these hands that were a fortress built by a dead man whose name she could no longer find anywhere in herself, and she said aloud, to the empty treasury:

"Who taught me?"

No one answered. The pan rose anyway.

And then there was one installment left, maybe two, and the warden set down its slate and looked at her with something that in a living creature she would have named pity.

"One more, food-taster. Perhaps two. The scale is nearly level. And you have been careful — I'll grant you that, the court grants you that — you've been very careful about which doors you never opened. But the scale knows what you're keeping. The scale always knows. It leans toward the heaviest thing in the room, and right now, debtor, the heaviest thing in this room is not my shard." The coal-eyes banked lower, softer. "One pour of the true weight, and you are done, and she goes free. Or refuse, and lose everything you've spent, and watch the moon finish its turn."

Wendell stood. Her legs were steady — the body keeps what it needs, the body was a fortress, the body had not been asked to forget anything and remained loyal, remaining loyal the way it had remained loyal through every poisoned plate while the mind flinched.

She walked to the niche and looked at the small blue flame, and read the label in that looping hand — *Isolde Amberdale, held in trust* — and the name went through her like a fishhook finding flesh, catching on something deep that she had no memory of hooking there.

"Before I pay," she heard herself say, and her voice came out low and rough, a stranger's voice, "tell me one thing. Tell me what she was to me. Not the memory — just the answer. Just the shape of it. Does the scale weigh answers?"

For the first time in what might have been centuries, the warden hesitated.

"The court deals in memories," it said slowly, "not in summaries. But I will tell you this much, debtor, because the court finds it poetic: you did not come here for a stranger. No one crosses the gate and pays a grandmother's kitchen for a stranger." It rose, robes whispering like ash settling. "Pour the next cup, and be quick. The moon will not wait, and neither—" the coal-eyes flickered toward the treasury's far entrance, where somewhere in the black passages, faint but unmistakable, boots were striking stone in a soldier's cadence, descending, "—will whoever else has just begun to climb down."

Wendell turned toward the sound, the horn cup cold in her fist, and recognized the stride before she could possibly have known it, some instinct older than memory tightening her shoulders.

Someone from above had followed her into the dark.
