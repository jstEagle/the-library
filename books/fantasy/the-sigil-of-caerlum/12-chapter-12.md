## 12. Chapter 12

The moon hung over Caerlum like a coin someone had bitten and found wanting.

Isolde watched it from the archive window, counting the sliver of shadow creeping across its face the way other women counted stitches. Twelve turns gone. The thirteenth was nearly full of itself now, swelling night by night toward completion, and somewhere beneath the mountain her soul sat in a treasury like a pawned ring waiting for its owner to die so the broker could sell it honestly.

*Three days*, she thought. *Perhaps four.*

Below her, the city went about its evening as though nothing were owed. Lamplighters worked their way down Archive Row with long brass poles. A fishwife argued cheerfully about herring. Somewhere a dog barked at nothing, or at everything, or at the thin place in the world that Isolde had learned to feel with her teeth — a pressure behind her molars, faint but constant, growing stronger as the moon fattened.

She had stopped sleeping in her own bed. The bed faced the window, and the window faced the moon, and twice now she had woken standing on the cold flagstones with her hand already reaching for the door, her body trying to answer a summons her mind refused to hear.

So she worked. Work was a rope; work was an anchor thrown into daylight. She catalogued. She shelved. She copied marginalia until her wrist ached, and when the archivists had gone home and the restricted stacks stood silent around her, she did what Wendell would have done if Wendell were here: she made a list.

*What I know.* The ink caught the lamplight and shone wetly.

*One: the contract names the debtor's bloodline, not the debtor. The debt follows the name Amberdale-adjacent line through whatever branch survives.*

*Two: redemption must be claimed by the bloodline before the turn completes. Wendell has gone to claim it. Wendell has been gone two nights.*

*Three: the prophecy requires a bride delivered by the thirteenth turn. It does not require my cooperation. Prophecies, in my professional experience as a keeper of them, are notoriously bad at accounting for stubborn women.*

She underlined that last sentence twice, pressing hard enough to emboss the page beneath.

Then she heard the boots.

They came up the marble stair of the archive in unison — not the arrhythmic scuff of scholars but the drilled tread of men who had practiced walking together, which in Caerlum meant only one kind of man. Isolde closed her notebook, slid it beneath a folio of grain tariffs from the reign of a king nobody remembered, and turned to greet the door with the pleasant, vacant expression she had spent three days polishing.

Four of them entered. Inquisitors wore grey the way other institutions wore color — thoroughly, joylessly, as a form of armor. The one in front was tall, dry-looking, with the patient eyes of a man who enjoyed waiting more than finding. Hale. She knew him by reputation only, but reputations in the archive were a currency she dealt in daily, and his was heavy: Inquisitor Hale, who had once kept a suspected charm-seller under observation for eleven months before arresting her over a knotted thread.

"Archivist." He inclined his head without warmth. "Working late."

"The archive never closes for those who love it," Isolde said. "Though I notice it's usually empty for everyone else. How may I help the Edict?"

"We're conducting an inventory." He said it lightly, the way a man says *we're conducting a search* when he wants you to stand very still. "Of restricted materials. There have been... irregularities. Materials consulted without proper authorization."

"How alarming," Isolde said, and let a small frown gather between her brows, the frown of a woman who cared deeply about authorization. "Which materials?"

Hale produced a slip of paper. He did not consult it. He recited from memory, watching her face as he did so, and she understood that the recitation was the test and her face was the instrument he was reading. "*The Sigil of Caerlum. Folio thirteen. Sealed binding, wax of the old rite.*"

Her heart performed a slow, deliberate somersault and settled back into place. Three days ago that title would have cracked her open like an egg. But she had spent those three days practicing, alone, in front of the darkened window — saying the words to herself, *the book with your face in it, the book that owns you* — until they passed through her like water through sand.

"Folio thirteen." She said it musingly, as though tasting an unfamiliar wine. "That would be in the sealed cases. Those require a second key besides mine. You'd want Master Corvane, or — well." She paused delicately. "Master Corvane has been ill."

"He has been *unavailable*," Hale corrected. "Since the night this folio was accessed. His key was used."

"Then someone stole his key, or he lent it unwisely, or—" she spread her hands, "—I'm sure the inquisition is far better equipped than I am to determine which. Shall I show you where the case stands? It's the third alcove. Mind the step; the stone settles oddly there. The whole building does. Archives sink, you know. All that weight of paper going down into the earth year after year." She smiled. "Everything in Caerlum goes down eventually. That's practically our civic motto."

Something flickered behind Hale's eyes — not suspicion exactly, but the recalibration of a man who had expected flinching and received a guided tour instead.

"You're very helpful, Archivist."

"I'm paid to be organized. Helpfulness is organization applied to people." She lifted the lamp. "This way."

She led them to the third alcove, chattering about humidity and acid-free bindings and the scandalous practice of some previous century's archivist who had used prophetic folios as coasters. She unlocked what needed unlocking with hands that were, thanks to twelve years of copying in cold rooms, perfectly steady. And when Hale drew out the Sigil of Caerlum and opened it to folio thirteen — to the margin where a woman's inked face waited, a face that was hers, a face that had been hers for a hundred years before she was born — Isolde looked at it the way she might look at a stranger's portrait in a market stall.

"Hm," she said. "Unflattering nose."

Hale looked up sharply. "You recognize the subject?"

"It looks a bit like half the women in the Tanners' Quarter. Prophetic art is like that. Vague faces are cheap; you can hang any meaning on them." She reached past him — slowly, openly, letting him see every inch of the motion — and tilted the page toward the light. "Besides, look at the pigment. This margin was painted later than the text. Someone added it. Someone with an agenda and a brush and, I'd wager, a grudge against whichever family this bride was meant to shame." She straightened. "Forgery is also in my professional experience, Inquisitor. I catalogue forgeries the way physicians catalogue poisons. You learn to see them everywhere."

It was, of course, almost entirely true, which was the best kind of lie. The margin *was* later. The hand *was* different. She had spent two sleepless nights establishing that much before Wendell ever saw the page, because Isolde Amberdale trusted documents the way other people trusted gods, and if her own doom was written anywhere, she wanted to know the provenance of the handwriting.

Hale studied her for a long moment. Then he closed the book.

"Archivist Amberdale." He pronounced her surname with peculiar care, weighing each syllable like coins. "Your family has been generous patrons of this archive for generations."

"My family has been *taxed* into patronage for generations. It's less noble but better documented."

"And your mother. Lady Vessia. I understand she keeps a household of considerable... tradition."

"She keeps a household of considerable *debt*," Isolde said pleasantly. "Tradition is what you call debt after the creditors stop coming. Was there anything else? Only, the restricted stacks need locking before the damp comes off the river, and mold waits for no one."

They left. Their boots descended the marble stair in their terrible unison, and Isolde stood in the alcove listening until the sound dissolved, and then she put her hand flat against the cold stone of the shelf and breathed until her lungs forgave her.

*Vague faces are cheap.* She had said it to an inquisitor's face while looking at her own.

She laughed — a small, cracked sound, quickly smothered — and went to fetch her notebook.

* * *

Her mother was waiting at the house.

Not in the parlor, where mothers waited, with tea and plausible excuses. In the front hall, standing beneath the amber-glass lantern with her hands folded, in the posture Isolde privately called *the effigy* — the stillness Vessia Amberdale assumed when feeling had been judged inappropriate and locked away somewhere in the cellars of herself.

"You've been at the archive all day," her mother said. "And the day before. And the day before that."

"I'm employed there."

"You've stopped sleeping. Marta tells me you pace your room. Your window shutters are open to the moon, which you know I cannot abide." Vessia's voice stayed level, but underneath it ran something Isolde had never heard there before, and it took her a moment to name it, because her mother had never once permitted it into the house: fear.

"Say it," Isolde said quietly. "Whatever it is you came down here to say. Say it plainly, Mother. For once in either of our lives."

For a moment she thought the effigy would hold. Then Vessia crossed the hall — quickly, ungracefully, in a way Isolde had never seen her move — and gripped her daughter's wrists hard enough to leave marks, and the words came out of her in a low, urgent rush, like water finding a crack in a dam that had held for thirty years.

"Do not go near the tanneries gate. Do not go near any gate. When the moon turns — and it will turn, Isolde, it always turns — you stay inside, you keep every iron nail on your person that I sewed into your coat last winter, and above all you do not *answer* it. Whatever calls you. However it calls. It will call in your own voice, do you understand me? It will call in your own voice, and you will want to go, and you must not—"

"Mama." Isolde's voice came out strange. Gentle. Her mother's hands were shaking. Her mother's hands had never shaken, not at funerals, not at audits, not the day the bailiffs came. "How long have you known?"

The silence stretched. In it, Isolde heard the house settle, heard the amber lantern tick as it cooled, heard thirty years rearrange themselves around a new center of gravity.

"I signed the household ledgers at sixteen," Vessia said at last. "I found the contract the way you found it. I read the terms. I did the arithmetic of my own life." Her grip tightened. "I married a man who would give me daughters. Two daughters. I told myself one would be enough to satisfy it. I told myself the court forgets. They don't forget, Isolde. They are *patient*, and patience is worse than hunger, and I have spent every year of your life being cold to you because I could not bear to be anything else. If I loved you — if I let myself —"

"—you couldn't have given me up," Isolde finished. "So you gave me up early. By degrees. So it wouldn't hurt."

Vessia's face did something complicated and ruinous, the mask of thirty years failing all at once, and Isolde discovered that she had imagined this moment so many times — the confession, the collapse, the apology — that actually receiving it felt strangely like grief for a version of her mother who no longer existed. She had wanted to be angry. Anger required energy she no longer had to spare.

"I'm not going to be taken," Isolde said. "Listen to me. I'm not going to be a bride and I'm not going to be a sacrifice, and I say that not out of hope but out of *plan*. There is a difference, Mama, and the difference is a woman named Wendell Yarrow, who is beneath the mountain right now buying back what our family sold, and who I intend to have home for supper before the moon finishes its arithmetic."

"Yarrow." Her mother went very still again, but it was a different stillness now. Sharper. "The taster. The little charm-witch from the night market." Something moved through her face that Isolde, cataloguer of marginalia, reader of hidden hands, identified with a small shock as respect. "Then the ledger entry last month. The withdrawal of the heirloom silver. That was for her passage. That was *you*."

"I sold the silver. I sold the second-best clock. I'd have sold the house if the buyer weren't the under-court itself." Isolde freed her wrists — gently — and held her mother's shaking hands instead. "Here is what happens now. In three days, maybe fewer, men in grey will come asking questions, because they've been asking questions everywhere, and you will tell them the truth, because the truth is safe: that your daughter is a dull, dutiful archivist who smells of dust and corrects people's citations. You will not defend her too warmly. Warmth draws eyes."

"And you?"

"I'll be where I can be most useful. The prophecy needs a bride *delivered*. Delivery requires doors, and thresholds, and moments of transition — and transitions, Mother, are the one thing an archivist understands better than anyone alive. Things pass from one state to another. Records change hands. Names change owners." She smiled, and felt her own mouth make the shape of Wendell's crooked grin, borrowed and worn like a charm. "Nothing in any contract ever accounted for a woman whose entire profession is knowing how documents fail."

* * *

The call came on the second night.

She was in the restricted stacks, working by a shaded lamp she wasn't strictly authorized to use, when the pressure behind her teeth bloomed into a voice. It did not come through her ears. It came up through the floor, through the soles of her feet, through the bones the way a bell comes through its own bronze — and it was, exactly as her mother had promised, her own voice, pitched soft and reasonable, saying:

*Come down. Come and see what they've done with your name. It's kept very nicely. Come and check the record, Isolde. You of all people should verify the record.*

She set down her pen. Her hand was trembling, but her voice, when she answered aloud into the empty stacks, was not.

"Noted," she said. "Objection filed. The record is disputed, and the dispute is pending, and pending means *no one touches it*." She pressed her palm flat against the shelf, against a hundred years of sealed bindings, as though she could hold the whole archive between herself and the mountain. "And if you take her instead — if you hurt her down there — I will spend the rest of my life doing to your contracts what water does to paper. Slowly. Thoroughly. From the edges inward."

The voice said nothing more. But the pressure behind her teeth eased — fractionally, grudgingly, like a creditor acknowledging receipt — and Isolde sat back down, and dipped her pen, and wrote at the top of a fresh page, in her neatest archival hand, the heading she had chosen for the rest of her life:

*Case file: the estate of Isolde Amberdale, deceased-in-law only.*

Beneath it: *Exhibit A. The contract, and everything wrong with it.*

Outside the high window, the moon hung a hair's breadth from complete, and far below the city — below the fishwives and the lamplighters and the barking dog — something in the deep was counting down. Isolde counted with it, unafraid, sharpening her pen.

Two days. Perhaps three. Long enough for one archivist to find every flaw in a hundred-year-old document, and short enough that she had better begin.
