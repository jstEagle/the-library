## 15. Chapter 15

The treasury of souls did not smell of anything at all, and that was the strangest thing Wendell had learned about it. Every other place in the under-court had a scent — the tallow-and-iron of the courtiers' hall, the wet slate of the stairways, the salt-sweet rot of the gardens where nothing grew but grew anyway. The treasury was scrubbed clean of sensation, as though even the air had been pawned and never redeemed. She stood before the warden's counter on the ninth night below the mountain, and the silence there was so total she could hear her own heart working, patient and stubborn, like a mill grinding grain no one would eat.

"Four remain," the warden said.

It did not look at her when it said it. The warden had no face in the way she understood faces — its head was a smooth oval of grey stone, featureless except where two shallow dents suggested eyes — but she had learned to read it anyway, the way she had once learned to read the king's cooks by the set of their shoulders. It tilted slightly toward her now. It was, she thought, almost gentle.

*Four remain.* Her hands were shaking. She put them flat on the counter to still them and felt the blue flame of the shard through the glass, warm as a cheek, faintly pulsing. Isolde's soul. Isolde's handwriting on the label, that looping, careful script Wendell knew better than her own: *Isolde Amberdale, given in trust, to be claimed.*

She could not remember why the handwriting made her chest hurt.

That was the trick of it, she was learning. The payments did not take everything at once. They took the edges first. She had come down here carrying Isolde the way you carry a lantern — every detail lit, every angle known — and now whole corridors of that light were simply dark, and she kept walking into walls that used to be doors.

"The fourth installment," the warden said, and extended one stone hand, palm up. On the palm, a small depression formed, cupped and waiting, like a spoon.

Wendell breathed out slowly, the way she did before tasting. *Name the poison. Name it, and it has less power over you.* That discipline had kept her alive for twelve years at the king's table; it would keep her whole enough now to finish this.

"What will you take?" she asked.

The warden turned its hand over, considering. "The winter your mother taught you to gut fish," it said. "You were nine. There was blood on the ice."

"No." The word came out of her before she thought, sharp as a snapped wire. "Not that one. Take something else."

The warden's head tilted. Somewhere in the stone, something ground softly, like a millstone turning. "You are permitted preferences," it allowed. "But the weight must balance. The shard does not care which memories you love. Only how much they weigh."

"Then give me a moment to choose."

"You have been choosing for three nights," the warden said. "The moon turns tonight."

---

She sat down on the cold floor with her back against the counter and let herself remember — deliberately, methodically, the way she catalogued poisons by aftertaste. Bitter almond. Garlic and copper. Sweetness with a rotten edge underneath.

There was the memory of learning to tie luck-knots, her grandmother's knuckles brushing hers, red thread and the rhyme that went with it. That one weighed heavy — it was the knot-tying that had saved her life in the market raid, the quick fingers, the instinct. But she could feel, when she probed it, that it was already thinning, already spent somewhere in the earlier installments. What remained was the shape of knowing without the knowing itself.

There was the taste of honeyed wine on a rooftop, someone laughing beside her. She reached for the someone and found only warmth, a presence like sun on skin, no face, no name. That payment had gone out three nights ago and she hadn't even felt it leave. That was the cruelest arithmetic: you only discovered what you'd paid when you went looking for it and found the shelf bare.

There was the rainy afternoon. She held onto that one the way a drowning woman holds a spar. She did not know yet what it was worth or when the warden would come for it, but she understood with an animal certainty that it was the keystone. Everything else about Isolde — the archive dust in her hair, the way she said *wendell* with the second syllable soft, the mole beneath her left ear — all of it stood on that afternoon's foundation. Take it away and the whole house came down.

*Keep it,* she told herself. *Pay everything else first. Keep it until the end, and maybe—*

Maybe what? Maybe the warden would be moved to mercy? Maybe souls had change-counters who gave back what they didn't need? She had read enough of the under-court's law, in Isolde's own research notes, copied out in that looping hand — she could not remember whose hand, only that it was beautiful and that reading it made her throat tight — she had read enough to know the court did not do mercy. It did balances. It did weights and measures. It did exact.

"Wendell Yarrow."

She looked up. The warden had not moved, but its voice seemed to come from inside the counter now, from the wood itself, from the whole room.

"You are weeping," it observed.

"I'm not." She touched her face. Wet. "Huh," she said, and laughed, a small broken sound. "I can't find the reason. Isn't that something. I'm crying and I don't know why."

"The body remembers longer than the mind," the warden said. "It is the last thing to be spent. Some debtors find that cruel." A pause, the length of a held breath. "Some find it merciful."

"The fourth installment," Wendell said, standing up, wiping her face with her sleeve like a child. "Take the fish. Take my mother's kitchen. I'll pay the weight difference after."

The warden considered her for a long moment. Then: "Accepted."

The memory went out of her the way water goes out of a cracked jar — she felt it drain, felt the cold of it leaving, and then there was simply a hole shaped like a winter morning, nine years old, her mother's breath steaming, blood on the ice. Gone. In its place, a strange lightness, a gap she kept prodding with her tongue like a missing tooth.

Three remained. And the moon would turn tonight.

---

* * *

The third installment was her grandmother's funeral.

She chose it herself, which surprised her. She had expected to fight harder, to haggle, to claw at every memory like a miser defending coins. But standing at the counter with the shard glowing against her ribs, she had done the accounting — twelve years of poison-hardened pragmatism doing what it did best — and the funeral was dead weight. Her grandmother had lived long and died easy and been buried in good soil. The grief had long since composted into something useful. The luck-knots remained, the rhymes remained, the red thread between her fingers remained. Only the rain on the coffin lid and the specific ache of that specific day would go.

"Are you certain?" the warden asked, and for the first time it sounded genuinely curious rather than procedural.

"No," Wendell said. "Weigh it."

The warden weighed it. The stone hand filled with a pale shimmer, like frost forming in fast-forward, and then the memory was gone and Wendell staggered, catching herself on the counter, gasping as though she'd surfaced too fast from deep water. Her grandmother existed now only as a function: the source of knots, the source of rhymes. A woman-shaped tool in the workshop of her past. She tried to summon a face and got only hands. Hands were enough. Hands had to be enough.

Two remained, the warden said. Two installments, and the shard would be free.

She carried it to the little alcove behind the treasury where she had been sleeping — the under-court did not require sleep of its courtiers, but Wendell's body was still stubbornly, gloriously mortal, and demanded its hours — and she sat with the flame cupped in both palms and talked to it, because she had heard that souls could hear, even caged ones. Especially caged ones.

"I don't know who you are," she whispered. "Isn't that the joke. I've spent three nights paying for you and I don't know who you are anymore. But my hands know. My hands won't stop shaking when I think about giving you up, and my body keeps turning toward you like a compass needle. So whoever you were to me — you mattered. Whatever they've taken, they haven't taken that."

The flame pulsed. Once, twice. Like a heartbeat answering. Like someone, far away, pressing their palm to glass.

She slept holding it, and dreamed of nothing at all, which frightened her more than any nightmare would have. Her dreams had been full all week — fragments, echoes, the feeling of being loved by someone whose name dissolved every time she woke. Now even the fragments were going quiet. The well was running dry.

---

* * *

The second-to-last installment nearly broke her.

The warden named it, because by now the court knew her inventory better than she did: the night market. All of it. The stall behind the tannery wall, the luck-knots and headache charms, the desperate customers, the outlawed kindnesses, ten years of daily treason committed in the name of mercy. The whole double life. The whole self she had built in the dark while the city slept.

"That's not a memory," Wendell protested. "That's who I am."

"It weighs accordingly," the warden said.

And here was the trap she had seen coming since the third night and walked into anyway: if she refused, the moon completed its turn, the contract activated, and some girl she couldn't picture was dragged into this stone court as a bride. If she paid, she survived as a person who had never sold charms, never broken the Edict, never been brave in the small illegal ways that had made descending possible at all. The warden had told her, early on, in its flat mineral voice: *the under-court prefers debtors who arrive already hollowed.* She hadn't understood it then.

She understood it now. Every payment made the next one easier. That was the design. Grief, once started, ran downhill.

"I'll pay it," she said, "in pieces. Not all at once. Give me the pieces."

The warden paused. Stone did not sigh, but something in the room did.

"Accepted," it said, "as a courtesy. You are the first debtor in four hundred years to negotiate terms."

"My mentor taught me to always read the contract," Wendell said, and then frowned, because she could not remember her mentor's face either, or his name, only the lesson. The lessons were outlasting everyone. Soon she would be nothing but a bundle of instructions walking around in a poisoned body.

They took the market apart piece by piece, and each piece was a small death. The smell of frying onions from the chandler's cart. The sound of dice in the alley. The old woman who bought headache charms with buttons because she had no coin, and always pressed one extra button into Wendell's palm, always said *for your sweetheart*, and Wendell — she realized with a lurch — had always brought those buttons home and given them to—

To.

The word was a door and the door was bricked up. She stood in the middle of the treasury with her fists clenched and her eyes screwed shut, pushing against the brick, and the brick did not move, and behind it something was screaming without sound.

"There was a person," she said aloud. Her voice sounded wrong in the scentless air, too loud and too small at once. "There's a person all through these memories. You're taking the memories but the person is still — the shape of them is still—" She opened her eyes. The shard in her hands was burning brighter than she had ever seen it, flickering fast, agitated, like a bird throwing itself against a window. "It's you," she said. "Whatever's behind the bricks. It's you, isn't it?"

The flame steadied. Pulsed slow. Yes. Yes. Yes.

"They'll take that too," Wendell said quietly. "Won't they. Tonight. The last installment. They'll take the shape of you, and I'll be standing here holding a stranger's soul and wondering why my hands won't let go."

The warden, from across the chamber, said nothing. Which was answer enough.

---

* * *

She asked for an hour before the final payment. The warden granted it — courtesies were accumulating around her now, she noticed, like snow around a warm stone; even the under-court's servants were curious about the woman who argued with contracts — and she spent the hour walking the treasury aisles, past the shelves of labeled flames, thousands upon thousands of them, each one a person someone had sold or gambled or mourned.

She talked as she walked. To the shard. To herself. To whatever part of her would still exist tomorrow morning to inherit these words the way you inherit furniture from a dead relative.

"If you ever get back to yourself," she said, "and if you ever find me — and apparently I'm told I'm findable, apparently I come here, apparently this is the kind of thing I do — then here's what I want you to know. I came down a mountain for you. Twelve years of swallowing poison so my body would be a fortress, and I spent it all on you, and I'd do it again, and tomorrow I won't remember saying this, and that's fine. That's the whole point. Love isn't the remembering. I figured that out — somebody figured that out with me, recently, I think, there was a conversation, I can't—" She stopped walking. Pressed her knuckles to her mouth. Breathed. "Love isn't the remembering. Remembering is just the record. The love is the thing itself, and the thing itself is in my hands right now, and it's blue, and it's warm, and it knows me even though I don't know it."

The shelves of flames flickered as she passed, hundreds of small lights leaning toward her voice like grass toward wind. She chose to believe they were listening. She chose to believe that somewhere in this stone city, the story of the taster who paid herself away would travel, and that someday, some other desperate debtor would hear it and take heart.

She went back to the counter with twenty minutes left on her hour and sat down cross-legged on the floor, the shard against her sternum, and did the last inventory.

The rainy afternoon. She could feel it waiting, wrapped in grey light, smelling of wet wool and old paper. An archive. Rain on tall windows. Someone looking up from a book — someone looking up at *her*, and the look on that face was the single most valuable thing she owned, worth more than the family fortune that had bought all this trouble, worth more than the crown, worth more than the Edict and the city and the mountain entire. Someone had looked up from a book and seen her, and everything after that had grown from that look like a tree from a seed.

"Last call," the warden said gently, from the doorway. "The moon stands at the edge of its turn. Will you pay freely, or shall I take it?"

"Freely," Wendell said. "Always freely. That was — that was the rule. We had a rule about that." She smiled, and her face was streaming. "Gods, I wish I remembered who taught me that."

She held out the memory the way you hold out your tongue for medicine. The warden's stone hand rose. The grey afternoon lifted out of her like mist off a pond at sunrise, and she watched it go — watched the rain, the window, the book, the face, all of it thinning, paling, dissolving into the warden's palm — and the last thing she managed, the very last act of the woman who had walked down the mountain, was to say:

"Take care of her. Whoever she is. She's worth everything. I checked. I did the math myself."

And then it was gone, and the silence where it had been was enormous, and Wendell Yarrow sat on the treasury floor of the court beneath the mountain, breathing, empty-handed in every way that mattered, with a blue flame resting against her heart.

She looked down at it. Warm. Pulsing. Labeled in handwriting she did not recognize, though something in her fingertips ached at the sight of it, some ghost of muscle-memory, some thread pulled taut between her hand and those letters.

"Whose are you?" she asked the flame.

It flared — bright, urgent, blazing like a signal fire on a hilltop — and pressed itself against her palms, and would not, though the warden waited with the redemption papers drawn, let her put it down.
