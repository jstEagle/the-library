## 21. Chapter 21

The kettle sang before the fire did.

Isolde had learned to read the small omens of their borrowed rooms in the weeks since the tanneries gate sealed behind them: the way the chimney drew badly when rain came off the mountains, the way the floorboard by the window swelled and groaned like an old ship, the way Wendell woke — always — a full minute before her body opened its eyes, as though some part of her still stood watch against poison that no longer came.

She was watching it happen now. The grey light of early morning lay across the bed, and Wendell lay beneath it with her hands curled at her sides, fingers slightly spread, the posture of a woman who had spent twelve years readying herself for the taste of arsenic in her sleep. Then her eyes opened — brown, clear, and utterly, perfectly empty of Isolde Amberdale.

"Good morning," Isolde said, for perhaps the two hundredth time.

Wendell blinked at the ceiling. Blinked at her. There was nothing hostile in it, nothing cold. That was the worst part, Isolde thought. A stranger's indifference she could have borne. But Wendell looked at her the way one looks at a kind landlady, a helpful clerk — with polite, unburdened gratitude, the gratitude of someone who owed her nothing.

"Good morning," Wendell said. Her voice was rougher than Isolde remembered. Or perhaps Isolde simply remembered wrong; memory was a treacherous archivist, and hers had spent weeks cataloguing a woman who no longer existed. "You made tea."

"I make tea every morning."

"I know." Wendell sat up slowly, pressing her palm to her sternum as she did, that gesture Isolde had come to dread and cherish in equal measure — the body checking itself, taking inventory of what remained. "I know because you told me. And because my hands remember the cup before I do."

*Her hands remember.* Isolde poured the tea and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that would not wound them both. The under-court had taken memories by weight, the warden had told her, and weight was not order. It had not eaten Wendell's past from the outside in, oldest first, like peeling an onion. It had taken what weighed most — and what weighed most in Wendell Yarrow's life, apparently, was not her grandmother's rhymes or her years in the king's kitchen or even her own name's history, but a rainy afternoon in the restricted archive, and everything that had grown from it.

The courtiers below had taken the whole harvest. Every conversation. Every argument. Every night market walk home through the tallow-smell streets. Every time Wendell had tied a charm into Isolde's hair and called it practice.

Isolde set the cup in Wendell's reaching hand. Their fingers brushed. Wendell flinched — not away, but *inward*, a small contraction, as if the touch had struck a bell somewhere deep and she could not find where the sound came from.

"Sorry," Wendell muttered.

"Don't apologize."

"It's just—" She frowned into the tea. Steam climbed between them. "When you touch me, I feel like I'm late for something. Like there's an appointment I've missed, and it mattered."

Isolde's throat closed. She drank her own tea to hide it. It had gone bitter; she'd let the leaves sit too long again, thinking about other things.

"You're not late," she said. "We have all the time there is."

* * *

In the afternoons, Isolde told stories.

It had started as a practical measure. Dr. Vess, who came three times a week to check Wendell's heart and lungs and the old scars on her forearms where the poisons of twelve years had written their biography in silvered skin, had said: *The mind is a muscle of association. Surround her with the shape of her life and the life may regrow around its shape, like bone knitting to a splint.*

So Isolde had built a splint out of words.

"The night market," she'd say, winding wool around needles because Wendell seemed to listen better when Isolde's hands were busy, "opens at moonrise behind the chandler's row. You go down the stairs that smell of beeswax, and there's a man who sells candles shaped like saints, only they melt into shapes that are definitely not saints, and everyone pretends not to notice."

"What kind of shapes?"

"Indecent ones. Very popular."

And Wendell would laugh — startled, delighted, a laugh like water finding a new channel — and Isolde would feel the laugh land somewhere in her chest and stay there, warm and terrible, because she had heard that exact laugh a hundred times before and this Wendell was laughing at the joke for the first time, and both things were true at once, and Isolde did not yet know how to hold two truths that heavy without spilling.

She told her about the luck-knots. About the headache charms woven from willow-bark thread and whispered over. About the Edict of Unmaking, which had forbidden all of it, and about the inquisition whose pact had now been dragged into the light, so that the charms were merely illegal instead of damnable, and the city's magistrates were arguing in public about whether an Edict bought with souls could stand at all.

"Did I—" Wendell paused, cup halfway to her mouth. This happened often now: questions surfacing like drowned things. "Was I good at it? The charms?"

"You were the best in Caerlum," Isolde said. "A widow in Tanner's Lane swore your luck-knot kept her son alive through the fever winter. A dockhand bought a headache charm every week for six years and never once asked what was in it. He just said your knots *smelled like his mother's kitchen*, whatever that means."

Wendell looked at her hands. Turned them over. They were clever hands; they had not forgotten their cleverness, only the reasons for it. "I'd like to see it," she said. "The market. When it opens."

"It doesn't open yet. Not until—" Isolde hesitated. "Soon. They're rebuilding it. There's going to be a reopening."

Something moved behind Wendell's eyes — not recognition, exactly. More like a fish passing under ice, a shadow where no shadow should be. "I feel like I've been there," she said slowly. "Not remembered. Felt. Like the word *tallow* is heavier for me than other words. Like the smell of beeswax makes me sad in a way I can't explain."

"That's enough," Isolde said softly. "For now, that's more than enough."

* * *

Some evenings the splint failed.

It failed on the ninth evening, when Wendell found Isolde crying in the kitchen — quietly, stupidly, over a burned loaf, of all things, because Wendell had once told her, in another life, that burnt bread was the truest test of a household, that any fool could bake well but only the brave baked often — and Wendell stood in the doorway holding her own teacup and watched Isolde weep over bread with an expression of gentle, helpless concern.

"Here," Wendell said, and crossed the room, and took the ruined loaf from her hands, and set it aside, and then — after a visible hesitation, the hesitation of a woman consulting a map of herself and finding the region blank — she put her arms around Isolde anyway.

It was not the way she used to hold her. Isolde knew the old embrace the way she knew her own handwriting: one arm low across the back, chin hooked over Isolde's shoulder, a small squeeze at the end like a signature. This was different. Careful. New. A stranger comforting a stranger who happened to be crying.

But her heart was beating against Isolde's ear, steady as a metronome, and Isolde thought: *the warden took the pages, but not the binding.* The book of her was gutted, but the spine held. Somewhere in that poison-fortressed body, the shape of loving remained, waiting for content.

"Why does it hurt you?" Wendell asked into her hair. "The bread? It's only bread."

"I know."

"You're grieving something. I can tell. You grieve the way people grieve — sideways, at objects." A pause. "Are you grieving me? Is that what the doctor won't say in front of me? Did I die?"

"No," Isolde said into her shoulder. "No, you didn't die. You saved me. And saving cost more than either of us knew it would."

Wendell was quiet a long moment. Then, in a voice smaller than Isolde had ever heard from her:

"When you say *me* like that — the me you lost — I'm jealous of him. Of her. Of the one you miss. Isn't that awful? You're sitting right here holding me and I'm jealous of myself."

Isolde pulled back far enough to look at her. Wendell's eyes were wet and furious at being wet.

"It's not awful," Isolde said. "It's the truest thing you've said all week. And Wendell — listen to me. Listen." She took Wendell's face in her hands, the way she used to, or nearly. "I am not missing someone else. I am sitting here with you. Whatever I grieve, it isn't a person who's gone. It's a person who's *here*. Do you understand the difference?"

"Not really."

"Neither do I, most days. But I believe it. One of us has to believe it, and I have the archive training, so I'll take the believing shift."

That startled the laugh out of her again — wet, reluctant, real — and Wendell said, "You're strange, Isolde Amberdale," and Isolde's whole body ached at the sound of her name in that mouth, because it was the first time Wendell had said it like it belonged there.

* * *

The nights were hardest, and Isolde solved them with architecture rather than courage.

They had shared a bed since the first week — not from romance, at first, but because Wendell woke screaming twice in the dark, hands scrabbling at her throat as if tasting something vile, and after the second night Isolde had simply brought her blankets to the same room and said, "I talk in my sleep, apparently, and it might be useful for you to hear it." A lie wrapped in a truth. Wendell had accepted it with the grave courtesy of the convalescent, and since then the nightmares had faded to almost nothing.

Almost.

Tonight, near the hour when the mountain above Caerlum went black against the stars, Wendell stirred and murmured, half-asleep, "Don't pay for me."

Isolde went still.

"It's too much," Wendell mumbled, eyes shut, brow furrowed. "Whatever it costs. Don't— it's not worth—" Her voice frayed into breath. "Tell her the knot goes left over right. Left over right, or it unravels..."

Then she was asleep again, breathing slow, gone.

Isolde lay in the dark with her heart hammering and her eyes wide open, staring at the ceiling where the rain wrote its long grey sentences. *Left over right.* The luck-knot. Wendell's hands had known it before her mind did; Isolde had seen it three days ago, when a loose ribbon in her hair had bothered Wendell so visibly that finally, without appearing to decide anything, Wendell had reached over and tied it — quick, sure, left over right — and then looked at her own fingers as if they'd spoken a language she'd been told she'd never learned again.

The body keeps the faith, Isolde thought. The body, poison-proofed, bargain-scarred, emptied of every page — the body still ties the knot.

She did not sleep. She lay listening to Wendell breathe and rehearsed, silently, the story she would tell tomorrow: not the night market this time, not the saints' candles. Something older. The rainy afternoon in the archive, maybe — the real one, the one whose weight had been paid out coin by coin below the mountain. Isolde remembered every detail of it. She had been the archivist; remembering was her trade. She could rebuild it brick by brick in words: the smell of vellum and lamp oil, the sound of rain on the restricted wing's high windows, the moment a food-taster with ink-stained fingers had looked up from a misfiled ledger and asked her why anyone would alphabetize a lie.

Tomorrow, she would tell Wendell how they met. Tomorrow, Wendell would hear her own love story for the first time, and it would belong to someone else entirely, and Isolde would give it to her freely, without receipt, without expectation of return.

Love as a practice. Love as a craft, like knots, like tea, like telling stories at the right hour. Not a thing you have. A thing you do, daily, until the doing becomes the having.

Outside, the rain eased. Somewhere down the hill, behind the chandler's row, carpenters were working by lantern-light on stalls and awnings, hammering the night market back into existence plank by plank. She had heard them all week, faint through the walls — a heartbeat under the city. The reopening was set. Everyone in Caerlum who had ever bought a luck-knot in the dark was talking about it.

Beside her, asleep, Wendell turned toward Isolde without waking — a slow, blind gravitation, her forehead coming to rest against Isolde's shoulder, seeking warmth the way a plant seeks a window.

Isolde did not move for a very long time. She was afraid that moving would end it, whatever unnamed thing it was — this instinct, this leftover gravity, this love with the memory scraped off but the marrow intact.

"Left over right," she whispered to the dark. "I'll remember for both of us."

Above the mountain, thin as a paring, the new moon was beginning its next turn.
