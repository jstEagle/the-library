## 22. Chapter 22

The night market reopened on the first warm evening of spring, and Caerlum came out to meet it like a tide coming home.

Wendell felt it in the cobbles before she saw it — the vibration of feet, hundreds of them, pouring down from the tannery district and the lampwrights' rows and the narrow streets above the harbor, all of them drawn by the same rumor that had traveled the city for a fortnight: *the Edict is dying.* The inquisition's writ had been torn up in the Council Hall three days ago. Hale's name was spoken now only in the past tense, and mostly in the tone people used for weather disasters — something enormous, something ruinous, something survived.

She set out her wares on the same scarred table she'd used for twelve years, in the same crook of the lamplit lane between the candle-seller's stall and the woman who fried honey-pastries, and the table looked smaller than it used to. Or perhaps the world around it had simply grown larger.

Beside her, Isolde unrolled a bolt of blue cloth over their shared counter-space and laid out her own inventory with an archivist's precision: ink-stained paper packets of luck-knots, each one tied in the pattern Wendell's hands remembered making even when her mind did not; small glass vials of headache-ease; folded charms against nightmares, against drowning, against the specific loneliness of being new to the city.

"Left side is yours," Isolde said, tapping the cloth. "Right side mine. We agreed."

"We agreed," Wendell said, and found herself smiling at the word *we*, the way she'd been smiling at it for weeks now. It was a good word. It fit in her mouth like a stone worn smooth by someone else's river — no, she corrected herself, not someone else's. *Their* river. She was learning the difference.

The first customer arrived before the lanterns were fully lit: a dockhand with salt-cracked knuckles, wanting a charm for his daughter's fevered dreams. Then a widow. Then two apprentices from the chandler's guild, giggling, buying luck-knots for each other with the transparent shyness of the newly smitten, and Wendell tied the knots while Isolde told them — deadpan, scholarly — about the knot's lineage in the market records, its appearance in three centuries of trade inventories, and the girls left glowing as though they'd been handed a piece of history rather than a loop of red thread.

"You enjoy that too much," Wendell said.

"Citation is a love language." Isolde adjusted her spectacles. "You'll learn."

"I'm learning everything," Wendell said. "That's rather the situation."

It was the joke they made now, the one they could afford to make because they had made peace with its underside. She was learning everything. The taste of Isolde's tea — chamomile and something sharper, fennel maybe, no, *anise* — the way she pushed her spectacles up with one knuckle, the particular cadence of her voice when she read aloud, rising at the ends of sentences like questions even when the text was declarative. All of it learned again since the under-court sealed itself behind them, since Isolde had carried her through the tanneries gate with the shard warm against both their hearts and Wendell's memory of her gone down into the dark like a coin dropped in a well that would never be dredged.

Some nights Wendell grieved it. She had learned that grief was allowed, that Isolde insisted on it — *you lost something real, Wendell, you're permitted to mourn it* — and so some nights she lay awake cataloguing what was missing: a rainy afternoon, a first conversation, years of small accumulated mornings. A whole architecture of knowing, collapsed. She mourned the woman she had been who loved this woman, mourned her like a sister, like a stranger whose grave she tended.

But other nights — most nights, now — she noticed instead what remained. The instinct of her hands. The way her body turned toward Isolde in a crowded room like a compass finding north. The way she woke at dawn already reaching across the bed before her eyes opened, certain of warmth there, and was never once wrong.

*Love persisting as instinct after memory is gone* — she didn't know where the phrase came from. Perhaps from one of Isolde's stories. Isolde told her stories every evening, patient as water wearing stone: how they met, how Wendell had sold her a charm against heartbreak and then, in Isolde's telling, proceeded to cause a fair amount of it herself before making amends; how they had pored over prophecy books together; how Wendell had gone down beneath the mountain. Some of the stories Wendell could almost feel, like heat coming off a hearth in a room she'd left. Most were simply beautiful tales about a woman who sounded brave and tired and kind, and whom she was steadily, deliberately, becoming again.

A boy bought a nightmare-charm with coins counted out one by one. An old woman pressed Wendell's hand and said, "I knew your grandmother sold these, in her day. Good hands run in families." Wendell thanked her and did not say: my family is a woman I met six weeks ago, or met twelve years ago, depending on which half of me you ask.

* *

The moon rose early, nearly full but not quite — the thirteenth turn long completed, the prophecy spent, its thirteenth bride standing behind a market table selling luck to anyone who wanted it. Wendell caught herself looking up at it and had to laugh.

"What?" Isolde asked.

"I keep expecting it to demand something of us." She shook her head. "Old habit. For a while there, the sky was full of paperwork."

Isolde's laughter was one of the things Wendell had relearned fastest and loved most completely — the way it started in her shoulders before it reached her mouth, the way she covered her face afterward as if embarrassed by her own delight. "The sky's ledger is closed. I checked the archive twice."

"Of course you did."

"Three times."

The Edict's collapse had changed the market's texture as much as its legality. Before, every charm sold here had been contraband, passed hand to hand like a secret, priced with fear built in. Now people lingered. Now children ran the lanes between stalls. Now, on the corner opposite, a man played a fiddle badly and nobody minded, and the smell of frying honey mingled with woodsmoke and river damp and the green smell of spring pushing up through the cracks in Caerlum's ancient stones.

An inquisitor came through the crowd near midnight — a young woman in the gray-and-silver of the reformed order, which was to say the much-reduced, much-humbled, mostly-clerical order — and Wendell felt her body go taut before her mind caught up. Old reflexes. Twelve years of tasting poison taught a person to flinch beautifully.

But the inquisitor only bought a charm against bad dreams, paid fairly, thanked them, and moved on, and Wendell exhaled slowly.

"You'll always flinch," Isolde said quietly. It wasn't a criticism.

"Probably." Wendell turned the coin over in her fingers. "My body keeps its own archives. You'd appreciate that, actually. Very thorough collection. No index, though."

"We could write one together." Isolde said it lightly, but her eyes were serious, the way they went serious over ledgers and contracts and other documents that decided people's lives. "Your body remembers things your mind doesn't. Mine remembers things nobody else bothered to write down. Between us—" She gestured at the table, the market, the moonlit lanes full of people buying small magics openly for the first time in a century. "Between us we seem to make one complete person. Or possibly two adequate ones."

"Two adequate ones," Wendell agreed. "With excellent joint documentation."

* *

Near the night's end, when the crowd had thinned to stragglers and the honey-fryer was scraping her griddle, Isolde poured the last of the tea from the dented kettle they kept under the table — chamomile and anise; Wendell knew it now, knew it the way she knew her own heartbeat — and they drank sitting on stools in the lamplight, shoulder to shoulder, watching the market fold itself up around them.

"Do you remember," Isolde began, and stopped, and Wendell watched her choose a different door. "May I tell you another story?"

"Always."

"It's short." Isolde wrapped both hands around her cup. "Once there was a food-taster who poisoned herself on purpose for twelve years so that no harm could reach the king through her, and everyone thought she did it from duty, and she let them think it, because the truth was stranger. The truth was that she couldn't bear for anything meant to hurt to succeed. Not on her watch. Not through her body. She turned herself into a wall between the world's cruelties and whatever stood behind her, and she never once asked who would stand behind her in turn."

Wendell listened with the peculiar double attention she'd learned to hold — hearing the story fresh, and feeling underneath it the ghost-warmth of having lived it. "And then?"

"And then a woman came to her stall wanting a charm against heartbreak, and the wall discovered it had a gate." Isolde smiled into her tea. "The end. Or rather — the middle. The middle is still being written. I'm given to understand it's being written by two people at a market table, very slowly, in ink that hasn't finished drying."

"That's a terrible metaphor," Wendell said. "Archivists should be ashamed of themselves."

"Noted for the record."

Wendell set down her cup. She had been circling something all evening, all week, all month — a thing she kept approaching and retreating from, the way one approaches a hot stove. Now, in the amber lamplight, with the market going quiet and the moon climbing over the tannery roofs, she let herself say it.

"I feel like I've loved you longer than I can prove."

The words came out steadier than she expected. She kept going, because stopping now would be worse.

"I know the facts. Six weeks since the gate sealed. Twenty-two days since I moved my things into your rooms above the archive. But it doesn't feel like twenty-two days, Isolde. It feels like—" She searched for it and found, of all things, a sensation from her tasting days: the way a poison's aftertaste lingered long after the mouth forgot the meal. "It feels like something that was already in me when I got back. Like I came up out of the ground carrying it. Like loving you isn't something I'm learning from scratch after all. It's something I'm remembering with the wrong tools."

Isolde had gone very still. In the lamplight her eyes were bright and wet and she wasn't hiding it, hadn't hidden her tears in weeks, having declared early on that after everything they'd both paid, secrecy about feelings was officially retired from their household economy.

"Wendell," she said. Her voice cracked on the second syllable and she laughed at herself for it. "Do you know what the prophecy book actually says? The margin note. I've read it a hundred times since. A hundred and— well. I've read it many times."

"Tell me."

"It doesn't say *the thirteenth bride will be taken*. That's how everyone reads it, how I read it, how my mother read it with terror in her eyes for thirty years. But that's not what's written." Isolde reached into her coat — she carried it everywhere now, the little notebook where she transcribed the old texts, the contract, Hale's confession, all of it, the paper trail of their survival — and found the page without looking. "'On the moon's thirteenth turn, the court below shall claim its bride.'" She looked up. "*Claim.* And further down — the part the archivists missed for a hundred years, because the ink faded — 'unless claimed first.'"

Wendell frowned. "Claimed first. By whom?"

"That's the beauty of it. It doesn't say. Prophecies are contracts, Wendell — you taught me that, or I taught you, or we taught each other, the provenance is disputed — and contracts have gaps. Loopholes. Room for interpretation." She closed the notebook. "The court claimed me. But something else got there first. Something paid before the deadline. Something walked down into the dark and gave away every memory it had of me so that when I arrived — a bride, a sacrifice, a soul walking willingly into the deep — there would be someone waiting who loved me without knowing why."

The lamplight flickered. Somewhere down the lane the fiddler played his last tune and packed up.

"So when you say you've loved me longer than you can prove," Isolde said, and her voice had gone soft as ash settling, "you can prove it. You just can't remember doing it. There's a difference, and I am professionally obligated, as an archivist, to point out that the record exists even when the witness has forgotten reading it." She took Wendell's hand across the table, over the bolt of blue cloth, among the unsold charms. "A century of prophecy says so. A contract written before either of us was born has you in it, loving me, in the margins. You've loved me longer than either of us has been alive. Longer than the Edict. Longer than Caerlum's walls. You just took the scenic route back to it."

Wendell laughed — wetly, unexpectedly, her own eyes stinging now — and squeezed her hand.

"That's the most romantic citation I've ever heard."

"I have others. Volume two is forthcoming."

* *

They packed the stall together as the market died down to embers: knots and vials into baskets, the blue cloth rolled, the kettle emptied and cooled. The other sellers called goodnight to them by name — by *both* names, as a pair now, the way married tradesfolk were named in Caerlum, and neither of them corrected anyone.

Walking home through the emptying lanes, their shoulders bumping, Wendell found herself doing the thing she did most evenings now: taking inventory. Not of poisons anymore. Of certainties.

The tea was chamomile and anise. The house was three flights up above the archive, and the third stair creaked. Isolde read aloud in the evenings and hummed while she catalogued and slept on her left side and stole blankets with the shameless entitlement of a cat. Wendell tied knots without thinking, tasted everything before trusting it, and loved this woman with a love that had no beginning she could find — only depth, only continuation, only the strange and stubborn persistence of a thing that had survived its own erasure.

She had come back from beneath the mountain without her memories. She had expected, in the dark weeks after, to be a stranger to her own life forever.

But identity, she was learning, was not a vault that could be robbed. It was a practice. It was tea made daily and knots tied nightly and stories told until the listener's heart grew a second copy of what was lost. It was choosing, every morning, the same direction.

At the foot of the archive stairs, Isolde paused under the lamp and turned, and Wendell reached up without thinking — the way she reached at dawn, the way her hands knew things her mind was still catching up to — and gathered a loose strand of Isolde's hair and tied it in a knot. Red thread. Luck. The old pattern, older than the Edict, older than the prophecy, hers before she was hers.

Isolde looked at her for a long moment, the knot glinting against her hair.

"You do that when you're happy," she said. "Did you know? You've done it eleven times since the gate sealed. I've been counting."

"No," Wendell said. "But I'm glad somebody's keeping the record."

Inside, the kettle went on. Outside, the moon rose whole and undemanding over Caerlum, and in the margins of an old book in the archive overhead, faded ink held steady: *claimed first.*

The middle of the story went on being written. Slowly. In ink that had finally, at last, finished drying — and begun, in its place, to shine.
