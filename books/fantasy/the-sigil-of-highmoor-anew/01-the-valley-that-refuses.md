## 1. The Valley That Refuses

Wren Okafor woke before the sun, as keepers always had, and lay in bed for exactly four minutes longer than they should have, as Wren always did.

The ceiling of the gatehouse was a map of water stains shaped, if you squinted, like nothing at all. Grandmother Ivet had once claimed the largest blot looked like the old royal crest of Aelthmar. Grandmother Ivet had also claimed she could taste lies in her tea and that the moor hens were gossiping about her, so Wren took most of what she'd said with a grain of salt and a cup of strong nettle brew.

Still. Four minutes. Then up.

The morning ritual hadn't changed in three hundred years, and it hadn't changed this morning either: boots, coat, iron key on the cord around the neck, out into the blue-grey cold of Highmoor valley with a lantern that was mostly habit, since Wren knew every stone of the path to the gate by the feel of it under their feet.

The gate stood at the valley's northern throat, where two hills leaned toward each other like tired old friends. It wasn't much to look at — a granite arch strangled in ivy, a door of black oak banded with iron, and around the arch seven seals carved into the stone, each one no bigger than a dinner plate.

Each one humming, very faintly, if you pressed your palm flat against it and held still.

Wren pressed their palm to the first seal and waited.

*Hm.*

That was the sound. *Hm.* Not a word, not a chime, just a low vibration in the stone that traveled up through the wrist bones and settled somewhere behind the sternum, like a cat purring through a wall. Grandmother Ivet had called it "the gate saying good morning." Wren had never been sure whether that was sentiment or doctrine.

First seal: humming. Second seal: humming. Third—

Wren paused.

Third seal: humming, but wrong. Softer. The way a kettle sounds when the water's running low, or the way a voice sounds when someone's falling asleep mid-sentence. Wren pressed harder, closed their eyes, counted to sixty the way the ledger said to do.

*...hm.*

Softer still. Or maybe the same. Maybe Wren was imagining it, the way you imagine your own heartbeat going irregular just because you thought about it.

They checked the fourth, fifth, sixth, seventh. All fine. All *hm*-ing along at full strength. Wren stood back, looked at the third seal in the early light, and made the decision they would later think of as the first stone in an avalanche:

They decided to say nothing about it.

Not lying, exactly. Just — omitting. There was a difference, Wren told themselves, hauling the ledger book out from its niche behind the ivy. Lying was when you said a thing was well when it wasn't. Omitting was simply failing to mention that a thing might be slightly less well than usual, pending further observation, which any reasonable person would agree was just caution.

The ledger was written in Grandmother Ivet's hand, which slanted hard to the left as though even her handwriting were trying to escape the page. Every keeper before her had kept one. Three hundred years of daily entries in a dozen different scripts, all saying more or less the same thing:

*Seals checked. All humming. Nothing crossed. Nothing will.*

Wren dipped the pen, wrote *All humming,* hesitated over the third seal, and wrote *Third seal noted for observation.* Then, after a moment, they scratched out *noted for observation* and wrote *fine.*

There. History preserved. Nobody needed to know.

Because here was the thing about being the last keeper of a gate nobody believed in: if the seals were failing, then the duty mattered, and if the duty mattered, then the Council would send someone else to do it. And Wren had seen what happened the one time a Council inspector came up from Lowmarsh, twenty years ago. Master Ellery Voss, a round man with ink-stained cuffs and the eyes of someone who reads contracts for pleasure. He'd spent three days tapping the seals with a little silver hammer, pronounced them "structurally inert," recommended the keeper's stipend be reduced to cover "administrative redundancy," and left with the gatehouse's only comfortable chair.

If the seals were failing, the Council wouldn't send a stipend. They'd send a replacement. And the replacement wouldn't love the gate. Nobody loved the gate. But Wren did, in the way you love a thing you've talked to every morning of your life, and the thought of some hammer-tapping functionary pressing their soft palms against the stones made something twist hot in Wren's chest.

*It's fine,* they told the gate. *You're fine. We're both fine.*

The gate hummed at them, faintly, and Wren chose — generously — to interpret it as agreement.

* * *

Market day came once a month, when the lowlanders climbed the switchback road from Lowmarsh with their carts and their suspicion, trading salt, lamp oil, and news for Highmoor wool, honey, and the particular sour apples that grew nowhere else because nowhere else had Highmoor's soil.

Nobody in the lowlands could quite explain why they kept trading with Highmoor instead of simply absorbing it, annexing it, paving it. They just didn't. It was understood, in the way deep things are understood without ever being said aloud, that Highmoor was a place you went to but not a place you stayed. A place that was *on* maps, technically, though usually near the edge, usually small, usually misspelled.

Wren set out their stall by midmorning: apples in three baskets sorted by degree of stubbornness, jars of honey, six fleeces, and the good scales that didn't cheat because Wren had personally filed down the thumb-weight Marta Tull used to palm off on them two winters back.

"Morning, Keeper." Tam Broadstairs arrived first, as always, because Tam believed in getting the best of everything and had long ago concluded that the best way to do that was to arrive before anyone could see him doing it. He was seventy if he was a day, with a beard like winter scrub and hands like root balls.

"Morning, Tam."

"Heard anything from the Council?"

"Not a word."

"Hm." Tam examined a fleece with the intensity of a man defusing a bomb. "Council's quiet lately. Too quiet, my wife says."

"Your wife says the moon is too quiet lately."

"She's right there too." He sniffed an apple, bit it, chewed with his whole face. "Fine apples. What's new up at the gate?"

"Nothing crosses," Wren said, which was the answer to every question anyone asked about the gate, and the answer everyone wanted. "Nothing will."

Tam nodded, satisfied, paid without haggling for once — a bad sign; Tam always haggled — and moved off. The rest of the morning passed in the usual rhythm: Old Senna arguing that honey should cost less because bees worked for free (the bees, Wren felt, might disagree about the *free* part), the Pell twins daring each other to touch the boundary stone at the lane's end and both pretending they had, and a young couple from Lowmarsh asking, shyly, whether it was true that wishes came true in Highmoor if you whispered them to the wind.

"No magic comes into the valley," Wren told them, in the flat tone of a person who has answered this question ten thousand times. "That's the whole point of it. Whatever you whisper to the wind stays exactly as whisperable as it started."

But the young woman smiled anyway, and whispered something into her scarf before they walked on, and Wren found they couldn't entirely blame her. It was human nature, wanting a thing to be enchanted. It was why people climbed the switchback at all. The valley refused magic so thoroughly that people had started treating the refusal itself as a kind of miracle.

Which was, Wren supposed, fair. It *was* a kind of miracle. Just one that required daily maintenance.

By late afternoon the carts were rolling back down the hill and Wren was packing up, fingers stiff with cold, when Old Senna lingered at the stall, the way she did once or twice a year when she'd had enough elderwine to be brave.

"You look tired, Keeper," she said. "More than tired. You look like a person carrying something heavy and pretending it's a hat."

"I'm fine, Senna."

"Mm." The old woman looked north, up the valley, toward the hills and the gate hidden between them. "My grandmother said her grandmother said the gate used to sing. Before the forgetting. Did you know that? Used to sing loud enough that the whole valley heard it, and nobody minded, because the song meant *safe.*" She shrugged her shawls. "Then one day it stopped singing and started humming instead, and everyone agreed that was better, quieter, and within a generation nobody could remember what the song had sounded like at all."

"That's a nice story, Senna."

"It's not a story, it's a memory, and memories are worth more than stories." She patted Wren's arm with a hand like a dried apple. "Mind your seals, Keeper. Things hum quieter right before they stop."

She wandered off toward her cart, and Wren stood in the cold with the empty baskets and felt the words settle somewhere inconvenient, right next to the memory of the third seal's thin, drowsy *hm.*

*Things hum quieter right before they stop.*

"Nonsense," Wren said aloud, to nobody, and loaded the cart.

* * *

That night the frost came early, silvering the window glass, and Wren sat by the fire with the ledger open on their knees, reading backward through three hundred years of entries looking for — they weren't sure what. Something about weak seals. Something about a keeper who'd noticed and fixed it.

Instead they found this, in faded brown ink, in a hand older than Grandmother Ivet's, dated in a calendar system the ledger offered no key to:

*The heir will come barefoot. Do not bar the door.*

Wren read it twice. Read it a third time. It was almost certainly nonsense — half these old entries were recipes, complaints about the weather, one memorable passage that appeared to be a curse directed at a horse named Buttercup. Barefoot heirs. Ridiculous.

Outside, the wind changed direction, swinging around from the south to the north, from the lowlands toward the gate, and it carried on it a smell Wren had never smelled in Highmoor before: snow, yes, and pine, but underneath it something older, something like woodsmoke from a hearth in a house that had burned down centuries ago.

And beneath that, faint and far off, carried impossibly across miles of frozen dark —

singing.

The gate was singing.

Wren sat very still, the ledger sliding forgotten from their knees, listening to a sound that had supposedly died three hundred years ago, and thinking, with a rising, dizzy certainty: *that is not a seal failing. That is a door opening.*

Someone knocked at the gatehouse door.

Small knuckles. Twice. Then again, patient as snowfall.
