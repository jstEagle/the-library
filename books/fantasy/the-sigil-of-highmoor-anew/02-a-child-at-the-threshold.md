## 2. A Child at the Threshold

The frost moon came to Highmoor once a year, on the longest night of the deep cold, and it did two things that no other moon in Wren's long experience could manage. First, it turned every fencepost and gatepost and gravestone in the valley into something that looked carved from bone. Second, it made the seals sing.

Not loudly. Not anything a lowlander would notice, or even most Highmoor folk, who had learned three centuries ago to hear nothing at all. But a keeper heard it. Wren heard it every year: a thin, high note like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass, rising from the seven stones that ringed the valley's edge, from the iron band set into the gatehouse lintel, from the buried rootwork beneath the threshold stone where Wren had slept every night of their life.

Tonight the singing was wrong.

Wren stood at the window of the gatehouse with a cup of barley tea going cold in their hands and listened to the note bend. It should have been steady — one clean tone held from moonrise to moonset, the sound of a door well shut. Instead it wavered, dipped, caught, the way a song catches when the singer forgets the words and tries to fake their way through the verse.

"Stop that," Wren told the stones, because telling things what to do was most of a keeper's job, and the other half was being ignored.

The stones did not stop.

Wren had spent the day doing everything a keeper does on the eve of the frost moon. They had walked the boundary at first light with the copper rod, tapping each of the seven stones and listening for the answer-tap that meant *still here, still sealed*. All seven had answered, though the sixth had answered slowly, the way old people answer when you call their name across a room. They had swept the threshold, oiled the hinges of the little iron gate that hadn't swung open since Wren's grandmother's grandmother, and checked the ledger for the rite of renewal, which turned out to involve honey (one spoonful), salt (three pinches), and a sentence spoken backward in Aelthric that Wren pronounced the way you'd pronounce a word you'd only ever seen written down: carefully, and slightly ashamed.

They had also done the thing they always did after checking the seals, which was stand in the middle of the empty road and consider reporting the weakness to the Council in Lowmoor, and then not do it.

It wasn't cowardice, exactly. It was arithmetic. The last keeper who reported seal-weakening had been replaced within a season by a nice young man from over the mountains who wore his badge polished bright and reported, every year, that all was well. The seals under him weakened further. Nobody knew, because nobody asked, and eventually the man went back over the mountains with his polished badge and his untroubled conscience, and the Okafor family took up the duty again without comment, as though duty were an heirloom that kept getting left on doorsteps.

So Wren kept the secret the way they kept everything else: alone, and slightly resentfully, and with a great deal of tea.

The singing bent again — lower this time, almost a groan — and Wren set down the cup.

"Right," they said to the empty room. "That's enough of that."

* * *

Outside, the snow had stopped falling and started simply existing, deep and blue-lit and utterly silent, the kind of silence that comes when the whole world holds its breath to listen. The frost moon hung fat and white above Torvane Fell, and its light lay on the moorland beyond the boundary like spilled milk.

Wren wrapped their grandmother's coat around their shoulders — it smelled of cedar and old ink and someone else's life — and trudged out to the gate.

The gate itself was nothing much to look at. Two stone posts, an iron band, a stretch of low wall running off into the dark on either side until it became suggestion rather than structure. Visitors from Lowmoor sometimes laughed at it, politely, the way you laugh at a scarecrow. Wren never laughed. You didn't laugh at a lock just because it was small; you asked instead how big the thing behind it was.

The singing was coming from the iron band now, and it had stopped pretending to be a single note. It sounded, Wren thought, like humming. Like someone humming to themselves while they worked, or waited, or tried to remember.

Wren pressed a palm flat against the iron.

Cold. Colder than iron had any right to be on any night, cold enough that the skin stuck faintly, and beneath the cold, a tremor — a fine fast shiver, like a struck bell deciding whether to keep ringing.

And then the light changed.

It came up the road from the moor side — the outside side, the side where there was supposed to be nothing but unmapped heather and the long slow centuries. A small light, low to the ground, wavering. At first Wren thought: lantern. Then: fox eyes. Then, as it drew closer through the moon-silvered snow: neither.

It was a child.

A child of perhaps eight years, walking up the forgotten road toward the gate of Highmoor at midnight on the frost moon, barefoot in snow that came to her ankles, dressed in nothing Wren recognized — a tunic of some coarse grey wool, dyed with something that wasn't madder or woad but something older, a color that seemed to have been chosen before colors were properly invented. Her hair was dark and cropped short and frosted white at the tips. She walked steadily, deliberately, the way people walk when they are following something invisible, and she did not shiver, which frightened Wren more than anything else about her.

"No," Wren said, to the night in general. "No, absolutely not."

The child looked up. She saw Wren — clearly saw them, her eyes finding theirs across thirty yards of moonlit snow — and she said something.

It was not Lowmoor speech. It was not the clipped border-talk of the traders, nor the drawl of the shepherds on the south slopes. It was liquid and formal and strange, vowels rolling like water over stones, and though Wren had never heard it spoken aloud in their life, they understood every word, the way you understand a face in a photograph of your own house.

*"I am come to the door,"* the child said, in Aelthric, the language that had been dead for three hundred years, dead so thoroughly that scholars argued about whether it had ever truly lived. *"Open to your own."*

Wren's mouth went dry. "No," they said again, uselessly, in Lowmoor speech, and the word fell into the snow between them like a dropped coin.

The child reached the gate.

Wren watched it happen — watched her put out her small bare hand toward the gap between the posts, the crossing-point, the exact place where the valley ended and the rest of the world began — and watched the wards rise to meet her.

It was not dramatic. That was the horror of it. There was no flash, no thunder, none of the theater that magic was supposed to provide. The air simply thickened. The child pushed forward and the air refused her, gently, firmly, the way a doorway refuses a splinter too wide for the crack — and she slid sideways along the boundary as if along glass, her feet leaving no prints now, her expression not surprised but sad, terribly sad, the sadness of someone who has expected this and hoped otherwise anyway.

She tried again. And again. Three times she pressed herself against the edge of Highmoor, and three times the valley declined to have her, smooth and seamless as still water, and each time she made a small sound — not crying, worse than crying, a small patient exhale, the sigh of a child who is very tired and very used to doors not opening.

"Don't," Wren heard themselves say, and crossed the threshold line without thinking, which no keeper should ever do lightly, and crouched down in the snow on the outside of their own home like a fool, like a traitor, like somebody's neighbor.

Up close the child was thinner than she'd seemed, and there was a smudge of ash on her cheekbone, and her feet — Wren looked at her feet and felt something in their chest turn over — her feet were not even red with cold. They were pale and clean as if the snow were a warm bath, as if the cold simply declined to touch her, and Wren thought: *the wards aren't keeping her out because she's dangerous. They're keeping her out because she isn't natural. Because she's already half something else.*

Because, said a voice in the back of Wren's mind, a voice that sounded unpleasantly like the ledger, *the seal-work doesn't reject harm. It rejects magic. And that child is carrying magic the way other children carry fleas.*

"What's your name?" Wren asked. Lowmoor speech again. Stupid. But the child cocked her head, listening to the shape of it, and then answered — and here was the terrible thing — she answered in kind, her accent clumsy, her grammar assembled like furniture from instructions, but comprehensible:

"Sef," she said. "My name Sef." Then, reverting to the flowing old tongue with visible relief, a whole spill of words, urgent and musical, of which Wren caught fragments — *door*, *home*, *they burned the—*, *you are the keeper, you must—* — before the wards pulsed once, softly, warningly, and the words died in her mouth as if snuffed.

Sef blinked. Touched her lips. Tried again. Nothing came.

"They're stopping you," Wren whispered. "The seals are stopping your words."

The child nodded. And then, because children are children everywhere and always, in every century and every country including ones that officially do not exist, she shivered — finally, belatedly, absurdly — and looked up at Wren with enormous dark eyes, and pointed past them, at the gatehouse, with its lit window and its cooling tea and its small human warmth.

"Please," she said, in careful Lowmoor speech, the single hardest-won word in her mouth. "In?"

Everything in Wren Okafor that was keeper said no. Everything that was keeper recited the rules: nothing crosses, nothing magical, report all anomalies, trust the seals, trust the maps, trust the absence where a country used to be. Everything that was keeper remembered the polished badge of the replacement from over the mountains and the arithmetic of secrets.

But everything else — the part that had eaten supper alone for eleven years, the part that talked to stones because there was no one else to talk to, the part that had just watched a barefoot eight-year-old press herself against a wall of air three times and sigh — that part reached out and took her hand, and found it warm, warm as bread, warm as a hearth, warm as something that had traveled a very long way to be exactly here.

"You can't come in," Wren said helplessly. "You understand? Whatever you are, whatever you're carrying — the valley won't allow it."

Sef's face crumpled and reassembled itself, quick as weather. She nodded. And then she lifted her other hand, the one Wren wasn't holding, and pressed it against the empty air beside the gatepost — and where her palm touched, the frost on the old stone melted in a perfect small circle, and beneath the melting frost Wren saw lines. Carved lines. Ancient lines, worn nearly to nothing, lines that Wren had walked past ten thousand times without once seeing.

Lines that matched, stroke for stroke, the sigil stamped on the inside cover of the keeper's ledger, in their grandmother's hand, beside four words written in fading ink:

*If she comes — run.*
