## 3. What the Seals Remember

The keeper's ledger lived in the third drawer of the gatehouse desk, beneath a false bottom that was only false if you didn't know to press the knot of oak where the wood remembered being a branch. Wren had learned that trick at seven years old, standing on a stool, their grandmother's hand wrapped around theirs.

"Press where it hurts," Maren had said. "That's true of drawers and most other things."

Wren pressed. The drawer sighed open like an old woman settling into a chair by the fire, and there it was: *The Book of Highmoor*, bound in grey leather gone soft as moth wing, its spine stitched with thread that had once, Wren suspected, been something more interesting than thread.

They had not opened it in four days. Four days since the child had appeared on the threshold, barefoot in frost, speaking a language that made the wards itch. Four days of feeding her porridge she ate with the solemn gravity of someone receiving tribute, of watching her trace patterns in spilled salt on the table—patterns that made the lamp flame stand up straighter, attentive, like a soldier called to attention.

Four days of telling themselves they would look in the ledger tomorrow.

Tonight, with the child asleep in the loft and the frost moon pouring silver through the east window, tomorrow had run out of road.

Wren set the book down, lit two lamps instead of one—for courage, though they'd have denied it under oath—and opened to the first page.

Maren Okafor's handwriting filled it: sharp, slanted, impatient with margins. The opening lines were ones every keeper memorized before they could read anything else:

*This valley is sealed. This seal is kept. This keeping is a kindness done to a wound, so that it may close. Do not mistake the bandage for the skin.*

Below that, instructions. Rituals for the seasonal reinforcement of the wards. Recipes for the tallow-and-ash paste that fed the boundary stones. And then—the part Wren had skipped as a child because it was boring, all lists and diagrams—a full accounting of the original seal-work laid into the gate itself.

The original sigils were drawn in ink so dark it seemed to drink the lamplight. Seven of them, arranged in a ring. Wren knew them by heart from years of pressing their palm to the gate's cold iron each dawn, feeling them hum faintly under the skin like a sleeping animal's breath.

Except.

Wren turned back three pages. Then forward again. Then set both pages side by side and felt something cold walk up their spine and settle at the base of their skull like a hand deciding whether to grip.

One of the seven sigils—the third, the one at the gate's heart, the anchor—was wrong. Not damaged. Not faded. *Altered.* Someone had redrawn it generations ago, a careful forgery in old ink, and the difference was small enough that only a keeper who had spent twenty years touching the real thing every morning would ever feel the lie.

But Wren touched it every morning. And lately, the hum had changed pitch. They'd told themselves it was weakening. Decay. Age.

It wasn't decay. It was *correction*. The original sigil underneath the forged one hadn't died—it had waited, patient as a seed under snow, and now it was pushing up through the falsification like grass through a floorboard.

And the shape it was pushing into being—the shape Wren had seen traced in spilled salt by an eight-year-old who couldn't have known what she was drawing—

Was the same.

Wren sat down. Their legs made the decision without consulting them.

"No," they said aloud, to the empty room. "No, no. Gates don't call people. Gates are doors. Doors don't—" They stopped, because even as they said it, they heard how foolish it sounded. Everything else about Highmoor was strange; why should the strangeness stop at the threshold of sense?

They read on, faster now, hunting through Maren's marginalia. And there, squeezed sideways in the gutter of page forty-one, in handwriting smaller and shakier than the rest:

*The seal is not a wall. It is a promise, and promises want keepers with blood in them. If the line of the first makers survives anywhere, the gate will know its own. God help whoever is keeper when that day comes, because it will not be a gentle homecoming.*

Wren looked up at the loft, at the small shape curled under three blankets, breathing slow.

"You're not lost," Wren murmured. "You're *expected*."

The word sat in the room like a stone dropped in still water. Expected. Summoned. Called home by a door in a country that officially did not exist, in a valley whose whole purpose was to make sure it stayed that way.

Which raised the question Wren had been circling for four days without letting it land: who had wanted the valley to forget? And what would they do when they noticed it remembering?

*

The census taker came on the fourth morning, riding a dun mare up the lowland track with the unhurried air of a person paid by the day rather than the mile.

Wren saw her from the gatehouse window and felt the small habitual dread of any valley dweller confronted with officialdom. Censuses came maybe once a decade, when the lowland magistrates remembered Highmoor existed long enough to count it. Wren had answered three of them in their lifetime. They were tedious and harmless, like weather.

She was perhaps forty, grey-streaked hair pinned severely, dressed in good wool the color of undyed flax. She carried a leather satchel and a slate board, and when she dismounted at the boundary marker she did it with the precise economy of someone who had dismounted at a great many boundaries.

"Morning," she called, cheerful as fresh bread. "Ilsa Vane, Office of Records and Enumeration, out of Correnvale. I'm told there's a household here?"

"One household," Wren said, coming out. "Keeper Wren Okafor."

"Wonderful." She produced a pencil and a printed form, and began writing with brisk little strokes. "Name, age, occupation. You keep the boundary, yes? Lonely work."

"It has its moments."

"I imagine." She glanced up, and her eyes were pale grey and entirely pleasant and did not blink quite often enough. "And others in the household? Family? Staff?"

"A housekeeper comes up from Millbrook fortnightly."

"Mm." Pencil strokes. "Children?"

The question landed lightly, the way a needle lands lightly, right up until you notice the thread attached to it.

"Why would a census care about children?" Wren asked. "We're counting heads, not futures."

Vane smiled. It was a warm smile. It reached her eyes the way a lantern reaches the far end of a barn—not at all, but convincingly if you weren't looking closely. "Oh, children are terribly important to records. Births, deaths, movements. A population is just a story we tell with numbers, Keeper Okafor, and children are how the story goes on." She tilted her head. "You sound educated. Valley schools must be better than they say."

"My grandmother taught me. She believed in reading everything, including things meant for other people."

"Sensible woman." Vane closed her slate with a snap and looked past Wren, up at the gatehouse, at the smoke rising from its chimney, and Wren watched her gaze snag—just slightly, just for a heartbeat—on the east window, where the frost patterns on the glass had formed overnight into shapes that no frost had any business forming. Spirals. Rings. A sigil, drawn in ice, by a child's sleeping breath against the pane.

"Interesting weather you have here," Vane said softly.

"We get odd frosts. Something about the moorstone."

"Mm." She remounted the mare with that same economical grace, gathered the reins, and paused. "One last thing, purely administrative. There's been some confusion in the lowland archives—flood damage, you understand—and certain older place-names have become... unclear. You wouldn't happen to know if this region went by any name before 'Highmoor'? Anything historical. Even secondhand."

The wind moved through the valley grass behind Wren, and for half a heartbeat it sounded almost like speech—an accent, a cadence, a language with its bones showing.

"No," Wren said. "Nothing like that. We've always been Highmoor."

"Always," Vane repeated, tasting the word. She smiled again, and this time Wren thought: *that's not warmth, that's a trap closing gently, the way good traps do.* "Well. Thank you for your candor, Keeper. The Office does appreciate honest households." She turned the mare. Then, over her shoulder, casual as commenting on the sky:

"Do mind the child. Winters are cruel to little ones who wander."

She rode off down the track. Wren stood very still until the bend of the road took her, and then somewhat longer.

Winters are cruel to little ones who wander.

Wren had never mentioned Sef. Not to the census form, not aloud, not once.

Back inside, the ledger lay open on the desk where they'd left it, Maren's warning staring up from the gutter of page forty-one. Wren read it again, and beneath it found one more line they'd missed in the lamplight, written so faintly it might have been intended only for paper to hear:

*If the Cartographers come asking names, give them nothing. Especially not yours.*

Wren closed the book. Up in the loft, Sef stirred in her sleep and murmured something in a dead language, and outside, far down the lowland road, a dun mare walked unhurried away, carrying the news that a country everyone had agreed to forget had just sent up a shoot of green.

The gate wasn't failing, Wren thought. It was calling. And something out there intended to answer the call with ink and fire.

Three days, Wren decided. Three days to learn what breaking the first seal would cost—and then they were leaving, taking the child with them, out of the only home either of them had ever known, onto roads that no longer existed.

Assuming, of course, that three days was time enough.

From the direction of the boundary stones, faint but unmistakable, came a sound Wren had never heard in twenty years of keeping: the gate, humming a note of anticipation.

It sounded almost like a knock.
