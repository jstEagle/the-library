## 5. The Road That Isn't There

The first seal was a knot of iron and elder-wood set into the gate's western post, and Wren had polished it every morning of their life without once understanding it.

That was the trouble with inherited duties. You learned the motions before you learned the meanings, and by the time the meanings arrived—if they ever did—you'd been doing the motions so long that questioning them felt like pulling your own teeth. Wren's grandmother had taught them the polishing song, the oiling of the hinges, the words spoken at dusk to settle the wards like a farmer settling restless sheep. She had never taught them what to do if the seals began to *hum*.

They hummed now. Had been humming for three days, low and persistent, in time with Sef's breathing. When the child slept in the loft above the hearth, the iron sang softly to itself. When she woke and wandered the gatehouse touching things—she touched everything, always, with the careful greed of someone who had owned nothing—it sang louder.

"It likes you," Wren said, not looking up from the ledger. "Which is inconvenient."

Sef looked at them with those grave dark eyes and said something in Aelthric, three syllables falling like water over stones. Wren had begun keeping a list of her words in the ledger's back pages, phonetic guesses beside their meanings when context supplied one. This one they already knew. It was the word she said most often.

*Home.*

"No," Wren said gently. "This is my home. Whatever it is you're homesick for, it isn't here." But even as they said it, their hand drifted to the ledger page where their grandmother's cramped script described the original seal-work: *seven knots, cut from one sigil, the sigil being the door and the door being the name.* And beneath it, in ink so old it had gone brown: a drawing of that sigil.

It hung around Sef's neck on a leather cord. She'd had it when she arrived, barefoot in the frost moon's snow. Wren had spent two chapters of sleepless nights telling themselves it was coincidence, or ornament, or anything other than what it plainly was.

The valley would not let her in. The wards rejected her like a splinter working toward the heart. Every morning Wren walked her to the boundary stones and every evening she appeared again at the gatehouse door, patient as weather, unable to leave and unable to arrive. Stuck in the seam between.

There was only one way to unstick a seam, and Wren had been avoiding thinking about it the way you avoid a letter you know contains bad news.

* * *

"You'll want your boots," Wren told Sef on the fourth morning, "and you'll want to not ask questions, because I don't have answers, and lying to children is a habit I'd rather not start this late in life."

Sef put on the boots. They were lowlander boots, traded from a shepherd who'd asked no questions about why the keeper wanted child-sized footwear, and she clomped around the gatehouse in them like a small determined horse. Then she stood by the door with her arms crossed, waiting, which was somehow more unnerving than questions would have been.

Wren took down the seal-pick from its hook—the long iron rod their grandmother had used for maintenance—and stood before the western post for a long moment.

"Grandmother," they said under their breath, "if there's a chapter in that ledger I missed, now would be an excellent time to mention it."

The ledger, being a book, said nothing. But the seal hummed, and Sef reached out and laid her small hand flat against the iron, and the humming resolved into something almost like speech—almost like the language she spoke, though stretched and slow as a song heard through a wall.

Wren fitted the pick into the knot. Their hands were steady, which surprised them. Perhaps terror and steadiness were neighbors rather than opposites.

"I am breaking the first law of Highmoor," they announced, mostly for their own benefit. "Let it be recorded that I hesitated."

Sef patted the iron, twice, encouragingly.

The knot turned easier than it should have. Three centuries of sealed iron, and the pick went in like a knife into warm bread. Something inside the post *exhaled*—Wren felt it against their face, a breath of air that smelled impossibly of summer, of cut hay and river water and woodsmoke from a country that existed nowhere on any map. The smell hit Wren so hard behind the eyes that they staggered, because they had never smelled it before and yet some buried layer of them recognized it instantly, the way you recognize a face in a dream.

"There," Sef whispered—in Common, actually, her first word of Common since arriving. She pointed past the boundary stones.

Wren looked, and the world came apart politely.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the worst part. There was no tearing, no thunderclap. The moorland beyond the boundary simply became *more*—the heather thickened into grass that grew in rows, and the rocks arranged themselves into the foundations of walls, and a hedge appeared where no hedge could be, old and enormous and heavy with white blossom out of season. A lane ran through it all, worn pale by feet, curving away westward into hills that Wren knew were not there, could not be there, because Wren had walked this boundary their whole life and beyond it lay only empty moor.

"The valley's edge," Wren said faintly, "is dissolving."

No—not dissolving. Being *remembered*. That was worse. The land beyond the wards was unfolding like a letter kept folded too long, creases still visible, and everything it revealed belonged to somewhere else. Somewhere erased.

Sef stepped across the boundary line and nothing rejected her. Nothing burned, nothing pushed. She simply walked onto remembered ground and stood there in her borrowed boots, filling her lungs with that impossible summer air, and for the first time since the frost moon she smiled.

It undid Wren completely. They had prepared for fear—they had prepared arguments and comforts and a whole speech about courage—but not for a smile that small and that vast at once. They picked up the travel pack they'd packed three days ago while pretending to pack nothing, and followed her across.

* * *

The road only Sef could see properly.

To Wren it flickered—a pale ribbon that solidified when they stopped believing in the moorland around it and thinned to nothing when doubt crept in. Walking it felt like walking on a memory of walking, each step landing a half-heartbeat after they decided to take it. Twice Wren's foot went through the lane entirely and sank into real heather up to the ankle, and both times Sef caught their sleeve and pulled, patient as a fisherman with a stubborn net.

"How," Wren managed, on the third such stumble, "does a child of eight navigate better than a keeper of—" they did the arithmetic and winced "—never mind how old I am. How?"

Sef considered the question with great seriousness. Then she knelt and pressed her palm flat to the road, and spoke one soft syllable of Aelthric, and the road *answered*. It firmed beneath her hand like a dog leaning into a familiar touch. She looked up at Wren and shrugged, in the universal shrug of children who have explained something perfectly and cannot comprehend adult failure.

"She remembers it," Wren translated for themselves. "You remember it. The ground remembers you." They laughed—a slightly unhinged sound, they'd admit later. "I have lived forty-one years in a valley whose entire purpose is forgetting, and I'm being out-walked by a girl who treats geography like a pet."

They walked. The hours went strange. The sun moved wrong—too slow, then too fast—and the blossom-heavy hedges gave way to stone walls, and the walls to open country dotted with ruins that Wren refused to look at directly, because every ruin resolved, when glanced at sideways, into something whole. A millwheel turning over dry air. A chimney smoking with smoke that wasn't there. Once, unmistakably, a bell rang somewhere behind a hill, and Sef stopped dead and listened with tears running silently down her face, and Wren pretended very hard not to notice, because noticing would have required acknowledging that they too had heard it, deep in the ear, in the place where inherited memories live.

By late afternoon the vertigo had settled into something almost companionable. Wren had, they realized with a jolt, never been farther from the gate than the lowland market town. Thirty years of duty, and this was the first horizon of their life. It should have been terrifying. It was, a little. But underneath the terror ran something warmer and more shameful: *wonder*. The world was bigger than the valley. The world had always been bigger than the valley, and nobody had told them, or perhaps everybody had told them constantly and called it danger.

Sef tugged their sleeve and pointed.

Ahead, where the remembered road crested a hill, the land changed. Behind them: the half-real country, blossom and bell-smoke. Ahead: moorland again, but wrong—flat, gray, featureless in a way that made Wren's eyes slide off it. Not empty. *Blanked.* Like a page scraped clean for reuse. And standing at the border between the remembered road and the scraped land, small in the distance, was a figure. Watching. Not moving.

Wren's stomach dropped through the hill and kept going.

"That's not one of ours," they said quietly. "Nobody from Highmoor walks like that. Nobody stands that still."

Sef's hand found theirs. Her grip was cold.

The figure raised one arm—not waving, Wren realized. *Measuring.* Something glinted in its fist. And across the blank moor, as Wren watched, thin lines began to draw themselves in the gray, straight and precise and utterly wrong, like ink bleeding through paper from the far side.

A map was being drawn of a place that had been erased.

And it was being drawn toward them.
