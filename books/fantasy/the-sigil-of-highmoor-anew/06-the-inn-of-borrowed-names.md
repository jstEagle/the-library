## 6. The Inn of Borrowed Names

Sef counted Wren's footsteps so she would not have to count her own.

Wren walked heavy, like the ground owed them money. Sef walked light, because her feet were still cold and cold feet liked to be quick. The road under them was not a real road. Sef could see it, a pale ribbon through grey grass, but when she looked away and looked back, it had shifted a little, like a cat finding a new spot in the sun.

"Are we there yet?" she asked. She knew it was an annoying question. She asked it anyway, because annoying questions were one of the few things she had plenty of.

"No," said Wren.

"Are we *almost* there?"

"I don't know. There isn't an 'almost' on a road that keeps moving."

That made Sef laugh, and then she felt bad for laughing, because Wren's face was doing the thing it did — the folded-up face, like paper creased too many times. Since the first seal broke, Wren had been quieter than quiet. Not angry-quiet. Sad-quiet. Sef knew sad-quiet from the inside.

The air changed before anything else did. It got warm. Not sun-warm. Fire-warm, bread-warm, the kind of warm that has people inside it. Sef smelled woodsmoke and onions and something sweet she didn't have a word for.

Then the inn was simply *there*.

It stood alone in the grey moorland as if it had been dropped from the sky by someone who wasn't looking where they were dropping things. Three stories, crooked chimney, windows glowing amber. A sign swung above the door on old iron hooks, and the sign said nothing at all — just a blank board, sanded smooth, waiting to be written on.

"The Inn of Borrowed Names," Wren read — except they didn't read it off the sign, because the sign was blank. They read it out of the air somehow, or out of their own head. "My grandmother wrote about this place. I thought she was being poetic."

"Maybe she was," said Sef. "Maybe everything is."

"That's a very tiring way to live."

Inside, the inn was all low beams and candle-glow and a fire big enough to bathe in. And the people — Sef stopped on the threshold and stared. She had never seen so many different kinds of faces. A woman with skin like birch bark. A man whose shadow sat in a chair beside him while he ate, eating its own small plate of shadows. Two children who were clearly brother and sister except one had green eyes and one had eyes like coins, and both of them were staring at Sef the same way she was staring at them.

Nobody here looked surprised by anything. That was how Sef knew they were all far from home, the way she was.

A woman behind the bar came toward them wiping her hands. She had flour on her cheek and a smile that was kind but careful, the way you're careful near a bird with a hurt wing.

"New walkers," she said. "Welcome to the inn. Sit anywhere that doesn't already have a memory sitting on it."

Sef looked at the benches. Some of them seemed to shimmer faintly, like heat over a road in summer.

"What does that mean?" she whispered.

"It means some memories are territorial," the woman said cheerfully, as if this were normal, which Sef was beginning to understand it might be. "Stew or bread? Oh — no. Wait." Her smile flickered. She looked at Wren, then at Sef, then back. "You'll be paying in coin, will you? Or...?" She let the word hang.

"We don't have coin," Wren said.

"Then you know what we take." The woman's voice stayed gentle, but something underneath it went very still. "Sit by the fire. I'm Maren. I run the house. We'll talk when your bones stop shaking."

* * *

The stew was the best thing Sef had ever eaten, and it was free, which confused her until Maren explained: "Food's for anyone who walks in. Nobody pays for food. Food is what makes us people instead of ghosts." She said it lightly, but Sef saw her hands pause on the ladle, just for a moment.

It was after the stew, with the fire popping and Sef's toes finally, gloriously warm, that the trading began.

She watched it happen at the next table. An old man with a sailor's rolling gait even though he was sitting down slid something across the table to a young woman in a coat too big for her. What he slid was nothing visible — just his cupped hands, moving slowly, like he was carrying water he didn't want to spill. The woman leaned in and breathed in, the way you'd smell bread. Then her whole face changed. She laughed, sudden and wet-eyed, and pressed her own cupped hands back across the table.

"What are they doing?" Sef asked.

"Trading memories," Wren said quietly. "That's the currency here. This inn sits between erased places. Everything around it was unwritten — countries, towns, roads. The people who escaped those places can't carry gold out, because the Cartographers unmake anything with a name attached to it. But a memory..." Wren's jaw worked. "A memory doesn't need a name to be real."

"So they sell remembering."

"They *trade* it. Sell sounds too simple." Wren stared into the fire. "Give up your mother's voice and get someone else's wedding day. Give up the smell of your grandmother's kitchen and receive a stranger's first snow. You stay rich in feelings and poor in facts."

Sef thought about that. She thought about how much of her she'd already lost — a whole country, a language everyone else had forgotten, a family whose faces lived only behind her eyes. She had so little left. If someone asked her to pay with more...

"Don't," Wren said.

"Don't what?"

"You've gone stiff. Like you're guarding your pockets." Wren almost smiled. "Nobody's taking from you tonight. Eat your bread."

But Sef kept watching the tables anyway, because that was what she did — watched, and kept things. That was mostly all she was: a watcher and a keeper of things nobody else remembered existed.

* * *

Maren came to their table when the fire burned low, carrying three cups of something honey-colored and a candle stub. She sat down without asking, which Sef decided she liked about her.

"You'll want the door," Maren said. No greeting, no circling. "Word came down the road ahead of you. A keeper and a child walking the old remembered ground. Everyone knows why you walk. Everyone's been asking me if it's true."

"Is what true?" Wren asked.

"That the gate in Highmoor is failing." Maren turned her cup in her hands. "And that the child is the last of the bloodline that built it."

Sef felt everyone in the inn go quiet at once — not silent, because the room was still full of murmurs, but quiet *inside*, the way a held breath is quiet. Even the man's shadow stopped chewing.

"And what do you want for telling us about the door?" Wren asked carefully.

"Nothing for the telling. Telling's free; it's *knowing* that costs." Maren set down her cup. "Here's what I know for free: the door out of Aelthmar exists. It stands in the ruins of the capital, in the middle of the monument they built there to celebrate the forgetting. The Cartographers think it's decoration. It isn't. It opens. But—" she paused, and Sef saw her choose her words like a woman crossing stepping stones, "—it won't open for keys or locks or force. It wants a name. A name spoken before kingdoms. Before Aelthmar had borders, before it had a king, before anyone drew a line around it and called it a thing. Say such a name to the stone, and the stone remembers what it was before it was a wall, and it steps aside."

"A name spoken before kingdoms," Wren repeated slowly. "Whose?"

"That," Maren said, "is the part that costs."

* * *

What it cost was this: Maren had seen the door herself, long ago, when she was younger and braver and hadn't yet learned that bravery was a currency too. She knew how the names worked. But knowing it fully — seeing it in her mind again, pulling the memory up out of the deep cellar where she stored it — that was a trade.

"I'll show you the memory," she told Wren. "You'll see exactly what I saw. The inscription, the mechanism, the warning carved underneath that everyone ignores. In exchange—"

"In exchange you take one of mine," Wren finished. "I know how this works."

"Wren." Sef grabbed their sleeve. She didn't have many words for big feelings yet, in any language, so she just gripped the wool and pulled.

"It's fine, little one."

"It isn't fine. I know how it works too. I watch." Sef pointed at the other tables with her chin. "They give away the small ones first. A birthday. A song. But the ones that buy big things—" she lowered her voice, "—the ones that buy big things cost mothers."

The inn had gone truly quiet now. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

Maren's face softened. "Clever child," she said. "Yes. To learn what you need to learn, the price is a keystone memory. Something load-bearing. Take a corner brick out of a person and the whole house leans." She looked at Wren with real sorrow. "I don't set the prices. I just keep the till."

"There must be another way," Sef said. Her voice cracked in the middle. She hated that. "There's always another way. There's — I could pay. I have — I have my mother's hands. I remember her hands. And her humming. I could—"

"No." Wren's voice came out flat and hard, a door slamming. Then, gentler: "No, Sef. Your memories aren't for spending. Yours are the whole point of this walk. You're the last person alive who carries Aelthmar inside them without cracks in the jar. If we pour you out to buy a road, then what was the walk for?"

"But you'll pour *yourself* out."

"That's different."

"It isn't different! It's the exact same jar!"

For a moment neither of them spoke, and the fire popped, and somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked like the inn itself was shifting in its sleep.

Then Wren sighed — a long, folding-up sigh — and turned to Maren.

"Her mother's hands," Wren said. "Mine instead. My mother's voice. Singing. The lullaby from the valley, the one every keeper learns before they learn their letters." Wren closed their eyes briefly. "I haven't heard it since I was six. It's the oldest thing I own."

"Oh, Wren." Maren's voice was barely above the fire. "You understand what you lose? Not just the song. The whole shore around it. Her hands braiding your hair. The kitchen. The smell of whatever she cooked while she sang. Memories come in weather systems, keeper. You don't get to hand over one raindrop."

"I know." Wren's voice didn't shake. Sef admired that and hated it. "Take it. Show her the door."

* * *

The trade happened by the hearth, and Sef made herself watch, because someone should witness it and she was the only one whose job was keeping things.

Wren cupped their hands. Maren leaned close, breathed in — and Sef saw it leave. She couldn't say how she saw it, but she did: something silver and warm lifted out of Wren's face, the way breath lifts off a window in winter. For one heartbeat Wren's expression went strange and open, like a house with all its doors blown wide.

Then it closed, and Wren blinked, and frowned slightly, and rubbed their chest with one hand as if an ache had settled there.

"Do you remember?" Sef whispered.

"Remember what?" Wren said. And then, hearing themselves, they went very still. "...No. I remember that I used to. There's a hole shaped like singing." They laughed, a small broken sound. "Well. That's that, then. What did I get?"

Maren touched Wren's shoulder, and the memory poured across like water finding its level — and Sef watched Wren's eyes widen as the capital rose inside them: the ruined monument, the blank-faced statues, the door hidden in the heart of the stone, and beneath it all, carved in letters older than any kingdom Sef had ever been told about, the warning.

Wren exhaled slowly. "The inscription," they said. "*A name spoken before kingdoms.* And underneath — oh. Underneath." Their face drained pale in the firelight. "Sef. The door doesn't just *want* a name spoken before kingdoms. It takes the speaker's name as payment. Whoever says the name to the stone gives it away. Forever. It unravels you into the hinge."

Sef felt the words land in her stomach like stones dropped down a well.

Because she already knew her name. She had always known it, the way you know a heartbeat. It was old. It was *so* old. Older than Aelthmar, older than maps, passed mother to daughter back past the first kings. She was the only living person who carried it.

And she understood, suddenly and completely, what she was walking toward — and what Wren had just paid to make sure she'd know it in time.

Outside the inn's small bright windows, far off across the dark moor, something moved. A line of lanterns. Orderly. Patient. Coming along the remembered road like ink spreading along a page.

Maren saw them too. Her face went white as flour.

"Cartographers," she said. "They've found the inn. You have until morning."
