## 7. Ilsa Vane's Confession

Ilsa Vane had caught fugitives in nine provinces, and she had learned long ago that the trick was never speed. Speed was for soldiers and hounds. The trick was patience — you simply walked the road the fugitives would walk, because erased people always followed remembered ground.

She was walking it now: a track of packed earth that appeared on no map in the Order's possession, winding up through moorland that smelled of heather and old rain. The Cartographers called such places *residue* — country left behind when a name was scraped away, like wax left in a seal after the stamp is lifted. Residue held no danger. It only held *weather*, endless weather, weather that seemed to be remembering something.

Ilsa hated residue. Not because of the Order's rules about it, but because of what happened to her there.

Because every time she crossed unmapped ground, she dreamed of a harbor.

Not at night. That would have been manageable. At night you could wake, light a lamp, write your report, and remind yourself that dreams were unregulated territory and therefore beneath professional concern. No — these came in daylight, walking, wide awake. A smell of fish and tar, a bell buoy, her mother's voice singing a counting song about gulls. A town called—

And there it stopped. Every time. The name stood at the edge of the memory like a door painted onto a wall. She could put her hand flat against it. She could feel the draft coming through the cracks.

She could not open it.

"Vane." Her junior, Pell, trotted up beside her, ink-stained to the elbow as always. He was twenty-two and believed completely in everything, which made him either the best or the worst possible companion; Ilsa hadn't decided. "The trail bends here. See? The grass is bent where the child walked."

"The child," Ilsa said, "walks like a child. The keeper walks like someone who has read every manual on evading pursuit and found them all disappointing."

Pell laughed, then caught himself, because laughing at a senior Cartographer was technically a breach of protocol. "Do you really think they know we're behind them?"

"I think the keeper has known since the census office," Ilsa said. "I think they took the child and ran anyway, which tells us something important."

"That they're foolish?"

"That they love the child more than they fear us." She said it lightly, but Pell went quiet, and she watched him turn it over — the idea that people might arrange their whole lives around something other than the Registry. He'd been raised in the Archive Halls. He thought everyone did.

They crested the hill, and below them, tucked into the fold of the moor like a coin dropped in grass, stood an inn.

Ilsa knew the inn. Everyone in the Order knew the inn, in the way you know a stain on a ceiling — it was called the Inn of Borrowed Names, it existed between erased places, and it was officially classified as *nonexistent*. Officially, one did not enter nonexistent buildings. One filed a Form 11 and waited six years for a survey team.

Unofficially, the keeper and the child were almost certainly inside it.

"We should surround it," Pell said. "Call the rest of the cohort from the ridge."

"Yes," Ilsa said.

Neither of them moved.

The wind came up the valley, carrying woodsmoke and bread, and underneath both — faintly, unbearably — the smell of fish and tar. Ilsa closed her eyes for exactly one second. In that second she saw the harbor again, the gulls wheeling over slate roofs, her mother's hand pointing out at the water, saying the name of the town, saying it, saying—

"Pell," she said. "How many places have we erased this year?"

"Eleven," he said proudly. Then, hearing something in her voice: "Twelve, if the valley counts."

"The valley counts."

"Then twelve." He said it the way you'd say *twelve loaves delivered*. That was the training. That was the whole horror of it, Ilsa thought — that the Order didn't make monsters, it just made clerks, and clerks could erase a country before lunch and complain about the tea afterward.

"Pell. Do you remember any of the eleven?"

He blinked at her. "Remember them how?"

"Their names. Their songs. What the harbors smelled like." She kept her voice level, curious, a senior officer making conversation. "You were present for the erasure of Solenne-in-the-Marches. Tell me what it looked like before."

"Solenne-in-the-Marches," Pell recited, "was a market town of approximately four thousand souls, notable for its dyeworks and its—"

"No. Before the *file*. What did it look like? Did the dyeworks smell of ammonia? Were there swallows nesting under the bridge? Was anyone there playing a fiddle badly?"

Pell stared at her. And Ilsa watched something happen on his young face — watched him reach for the memory and find, where a town should have been, a smooth blank wall with a door painted on it. She watched him touch the wall for the first time and notice that there was a draft.

"It's gone," he said, and his voice cracked in the middle like a boy's. "Why is it gone? We *did* that. We did that and I can't even—"

"Now you understand the job," Ilsa said gently. "We are the only ones who could remember, and we made ourselves the first ones to forget. That's not erasing a place, Pell. That's eating it."

* * *

She left Pell on the ridge with orders to wait — orders he obeyed gratefully, because his hands were still shaking — and walked down to the inn alone. This was against protocol. Nearly everything she intended to do next was against protocol, and she found, to her mild surprise, that the prospect made her lighter rather than heavier, like setting down a ledger she'd been carrying since childhood.

The inn's door opened before she knocked. Inside: low beams, a fire, and refugees of countries that no longer existed playing cards with memories instead of coins. She felt their eyes take in her grey coat and mark her, and she did what she had learned to do in nine provinces: she took off the coat, folded it over her arm, and became simply a tired woman looking for a fire.

The keeper sat near the hearth with the child asleep against their shoulder. Wren Okafor looked up, and Ilsa gave them credit — they didn't flinch, didn't gather the child, didn't reach for a weapon. They just looked at her the way you look at weather you've been expecting.

"You're the census taker," Wren said quietly.

"Census taker, field assessor, junior registrar." Ilsa sat down across from them, uninvited. "I've worn a lot of hats. They're all the same hat."

"And now?"

"Now I'm going to commit treason," Ilsa said. "But first I'd like some of that bread, if it's being shared, because I've been walking your residue for three days and residue has terrible inns."

Something moved at the corner of Wren's mouth. Not quite a smile. The beginning of the machinery of one. They tore the loaf and handed half over, and for a moment the two of them ate bread by a fire while a nation slept against Wren's shoulder, and it was so absurd that Ilsa nearly laughed.

"The Order knows about the girl," she said then, low. "Not just that she speaks Aelthric. That she *is* what she is — heir-blood, gate-born. There's a writ. My superior signed it yesterday." She reached into her shirt and drew out a slip of paper, and set it on the table between them, face down, like a card she was ashamed of. "They're not coming to erase the language, keeper. Languages are easy; you burn a few grammars and the words die of loneliness. They're coming to erase *her*. Personally. A full Unnaming. Name, bloodline, memory. When they're done, you'll look at that child and see a stranger you can't account for, and she'll look at you the same way, and somewhere a door will stand forever locked because no one remembers whose door it is."

Wren's hand had come to rest, without seeming to decide to, on the child's back. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because I was born in a place my order erased."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the room a card player laid down a memory of rain and sighed happily.

"There was a harbor town," Ilsa said. "Fishing. Slate roofs. Gulls that would steal the teeth right out of a herring. My mother sang a counting song about them. I remember the song. I remember her voice. I do not remember the town's name or her face, because the Order got there when I was four and did its work, and I grew up in the Archive Halls being told that places without registry entries cannot be missed." She turned the card over at last — the writ itself, seal intact, her own countersignature at the bottom — and pushed it across the table. "Every time I cross unmapped ground, I dream of that harbor awake. And last night, following your trail, I realized something that has ruined my career in advance."

"What's that?"

"If they erase the girl," Ilsa said, "then someday a Cartographer will walk through Highmoor and smell woodsmoke and feel a draft through a wall where a feeling used to be, and never know why. Same as me. Same as Pell, who forgot a town he personally helped unmake and cried about it on a hilltop like a child. Your valley isn't the last place on the map, keeper. It's the last *memory* on the map. And I am done helping to eat memories."

Wren studied her for a long moment. Then: "This is a trap. It's a very good trap. If it is, congratulations — I genuinely believe you."

"It's a worse thing than a trap," Ilsa said. "It's a confession."

The child stirred, opened her eyes, and looked at Ilsa with the frank, ancient appraisal of the very young. She said something in Aelthric — soft, questioning.

"She wants to know," Wren translated, "if you're the wolf."

"Tell her," Ilsa said, "that the wolf is three days behind me with twelve riders and a writ, and that this particular wolf is about to become a sheepdog, and that if she values her neck she'll get you both out of this inn before dawn."

Outside, far off on the ridge, a signal lamp began to blink — Pell, dutiful even in distress, reporting that his senior officer had entered the nonexistent building alone.

Ilsa watched the light and felt, with strange calm, the last of her old life click into place like a seal closing.

"They'll be here tomorrow night," she said. "So tonight, keeper — we run."
