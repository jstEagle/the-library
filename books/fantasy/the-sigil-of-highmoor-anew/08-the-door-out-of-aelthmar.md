## 8. The Door Out of Aelthmar

The road ended where roads are supposed to end — at a city.

Except it wasn't a city anymore. It was the idea of one, the way a footprint in ash is the idea of a foot. Walls stood in places, slumped like tired shepherds. Streets ran straight for a while and then simply stopped, as if the masons had been called away mid-thought and never returned. Grass grew through the flagstones in soft green rivers, and somewhere ahead a bird sang a song with no name for it, which struck Wren as fitting.

"Careful," they said, catching Sef's sleeve before she could bound down the slope. "Ruins have opinions about feet."

Sef gave them the look — the one every child apparently learns at birth, the universal expression of *obviously I know that* — and pulled free anyway, skipping down into the dead capital of Aelthmar like she owned it. Which, Wren was beginning to understand, she rather did.

Three days on the remembered road had changed them both. Wren's boots were ruined; their knees sounded like an old gate hinge; they had eaten the last of the hard cheese two mornings ago and had been mourning it ever since. But Sef had bloomed out here, past the valley's edge, past the wards that had once rejected her like a splinter. She spoke more now — not aloud, never aloud, but her hands talked constantly, quick Aelthric shapes in the air, and Wren was slowly learning to read them the way one learns to read weather.

*What is this place?* her hands asked now, sweeping at the ruins.

"It was called Cael Amarth," Wren said. "The capital. Before."

She frowned. Shook her head. Made a small, frustrated gesture Wren had come to dread: *no.*

"No? It's in your ledger—"

She grabbed Wren's hand and pressed it flat against a fallen stone, then traced letters above it, slow and deliberate, spelling out a word Wren's mouth didn't know how to say but their bones seemed to recognize. Not Cael Amarth. Something older, rounder, warmer. The city's first name, from before kingdoms, before maps, before anyone decided that names were things you could take away.

Wren sat down heavily on a broken wall and let that settle.

"Right," they said. "So we're not even standing in the right country. We're standing in the country underneath it."

Sef beamed, as if Wren had passed some sort of examination.

They walked deeper. And there, in what must once have been the heart of everything, they found the monument.

It was a tower — or had been. Now it was a hollow ring of pale stone, open to the sky like a cupped hand, and its walls were carved floor to shattered top with writing. Wren circled it slowly, reading. At first they thought it was a list of kings. Then heroes. Then they understood, and their stomach turned over gently, like a boat in bad water.

It was a list of erasures.

*Here stood the bridge of Lethen, unmade by decree.*
*Here sang the choir of Vessa, unnamed by decree.*
*Here was the field where the harvest-dance was danced. Let no one dance.*

Line after line, centuries of it, the country cataloguing its own forgetting in beautiful, careful script — as though whoever commissioned it had believed that writing a thing down honestly was the same as keeping it. As though shame, recorded neatly enough, became history instead of crime.

At the base of the tower, where a door ought to be, there was a door.

It was smaller than Wren expected. Plain oak, iron-banded, utterly ordinary-looking, set into the monument's foundation like a servant's entrance to a palace. Except the air around it hummed. Wren felt it in their teeth, in the old keeper-sense they'd spent a lifetime pretending wasn't there: pressure, attention, *waiting*. The same feeling as the gate in Highmoor, but older. Hungrier. A gate that had been shut so long it had forgotten what it was for, and was hoping someone would remind it.

Sef pressed both palms against it and looked up at Wren with an expression of such naked hope that Wren had to look away and pretend to study the carvings.

*Open,* her hands said. *Please. Home.*

"Not yet." Wren crouched, running fingers along the iron bands. There was no lock. No keyhole. Only a shallow recess at chest height, shaped like nothing so much as a mouth. "Doors like this don't want keys, love. They want words. And I'd bet my last boot this one wants a very particular word."

They spent the afternoon finding out how particular.

Wren tried the keeper's formal invocation first — the one from the ledger's back pages, the one their grandmother had made them memorize alongside the seals and the boundary-rules and the recipe for nettle soup (the grandmother had been a practical woman). The door did not care. They tried the valley's founding oath, the lowland trade-tongue, and, in a moment of desperation, a rude word they'd learned from a drover at the autumn market, which at least made Sef laugh soundlessly until she hiccuped.

Nothing. The mouth-shaped recess stayed closed and patient, like a librarian waiting for you to stop wasting everyone's time.

Then Sef touched Wren's arm.

She pointed at herself. Then at the recess. Then she took a breath — and began to speak. Aloud. For the first time since the frost moon.

Her voice came out rusty and small, a bell unused for years, but the language was unmistakable: Aelthric, the true old tongue, flowing off her tongue like it had never died anywhere except everywhere else. She was offering her name. Wren understood that with horrible clarity even before the door began to glow — a faint, warm light seeping around the oak like dawn under a curtain — and before Wren saw what the offering was doing to her.

She was fraying.

It started at her fingertips. Her hands went translucent at the edges, like paper held too near a candle, not burning but *unwriting*, the small bones going vague. She smiled up at Wren the whole time, radiant, certain, a child who had walked barefoot through snow to get home and was perfectly willing to come apart at the seams to do it.

"*No.*"

Wren hauled her back from the door. The light dimmed, sulky. The fraying slowed — her fingertips solidifying again, thank all the quiet gods — but she fought them, twisting, signing furiously: *it wants me, it's mine, I'm the heir, I can pay—*

"You can't pay if you've been spent," Wren said, and heard their own voice crack on it, and hated that, and kept talking anyway because someone had to be the adult in the ruins and apparently it was going to be them, again, always. "Listen to me. You are eight years old. You are the last person alive who speaks a whole country. If you unravel, that's it — that's the second death of Aelthmar, and this time it's final, and I will not stand in this grass and watch it happen. Sit *down*."

To Wren's lasting astonishment, she sat down.

And then, because sitting down left Wren alone with the humming door and the glowing mouth-recess and their own traitor thoughts, the thought arrived. It arrived the way important thoughts do: quietly, sideways, dressed as something obvious.

*The door wants a name spoken before kingdoms.*

Not *her* name. Any name old enough. Any name that predated the maps, the decrees, the erasure-lists on the tower walls.

And Wren Okafor — last keeper of a sealed gate in a valley that refused magic, orphan of orphans, inheritor of a duty no one had asked them to want — possessed exactly one thing that old.

Their inherited name.

The one their grandmother had spoken only once, at Wren's naming, in a whisper, with tears in her eyes, saying: *this comes down the keepers' line from before there were lines, from before anyone drew a border and pretended the world agreed to it. Guard it. One day it will be worth more than you.*

Wren had assumed she meant it would buy respect. Land, maybe. A pension.

They looked at the plain oak door, glowing patiently. They looked at Sef, whole again, watching them with those enormous eyes, already starting to sign *what? why are you making that face?*

They looked at their own hands, steady, keeper's hands, hands that had oiled hinges and checked seals for thirty years without ever once being thanked.

"Oh," Wren said softly, to nobody. "Oh, you absolute door."

Because here was the shape of it, unavoidable now, terrible and clean: the door would take a keeper's name. Theirs. All of theirs — not just the word itself but whatever it carried, the long chain of memory it had been passed down inside, the keepers' line entire, unwinding backward into the dark like thread pulled from a hem. Wren would step out of history so Sef could step into it. And the worst part — the truly unforgivable part — was that some small, warm corner of Wren felt *relieved*. Thirty years of lonely duty, and here at last was a job with a clear ending.

"Keeper," Sef signed, tugging their sleeve. Her face had gone sharp and frightened, because children always knew. "Keeper. Your face is doing the snow-face. The face from the night we met."

"I know," Wren said.

They sat down beside her on the cold flagstones, in the shadow of a tower built to celebrate forgetting, and put an arm around her thin shoulders, and told her the truth, because she'd earned it and because Wren suspected they wouldn't get many more chances to say things.

"There's another way to open it," they said. "But it costs me, not you. And I need you to hear me clearly: I haven't decided anything yet. So don't make the sad face. Eat half this biscuit. We'll think better with crumbs."

Far behind them, down the remembered road, the wind changed.

Wren felt it before they heard it — a wrongness in the air, the particular hush of a place being *edited* — and when they turned, the horizon to the south had gone subtly wrong. Not dark. Just... less. A strip of moorland that had been there that morning was now a strip of nothing-that-had-ever-been-there, and it was coming toward them at the speed of a determined walk.

Ink, rolling across the world like weather.

Sef grabbed Wren's arm. Wren stood, knees cracking, heart hammering, and faced the door that wanted everything they were.

"Well," they said. "I suppose we'd best hurry."
