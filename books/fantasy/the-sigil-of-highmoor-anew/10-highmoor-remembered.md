## 10. Highmoor, Remembered

The morning the mapmaker's letter arrived, Sef was teaching eleven children to count in Aelthric, and one of them — Bram Oakley, eight years old and allergic to sitting still — had turned the word for *seven* into a sneeze.

"Again," Sef said.

"*Seva*!" Bram bellowed, sneezing magnificently on cue. The other children dissolved. Even old Maren, who sat at the back of the schoolhouse ostensibly to mend nets but actually to make sure nobody was teaching her grandchildren anything dangerous, snorted into her thread.

"You're a menace," Sef told Bram, but she was smiling, because *seva* really did sound like a sneeze, and it had taken her three winters to stop laughing at it herself.

That was when the pigeon came through the open window — pigeons could do that now; most things could do that now, gently, without the world flinching — and dropped a letter into the chalk dust. The wax seal bore a compass rose with no north marked.

Sef's hands went still around the chalk.

Maren saw. Maren always saw. "Bad news?"

"I don't know yet." Sef broke the seal. Inside was a single sheet, and on it, drawn in careful cartographer's ink, was a map of the valley — Highmoor, whole and named, its roads and its river and its little schoolhouse all rendered lovingly, precisely, by someone who had clearly walked every foot of it. In the lower corner, where maps once said *HERE THERE IS NOTHING* or simply left a blank white ache, someone had written in flowing Aelthric script: **Aelthmar.**

And beneath that, smaller: *We are sorry. We would like to help. — I.V.*

Sef read it four times. Then she laughed, which startled the children, and then she cried a little, which startled them more, and then she held up the map and said, in Aelthric, "Class, today we learn the word for *homecoming*."

* * *

It had been nine years since Wren stepped into the gate, and the valley had learned to breathe differently.

Magic came back the way spring comes back — not all at once, not dramatically, but in small stubborn green things. Foxgloves grew in patterns that spelled out old blessings if you knew how to read them, which Sef did, and taught. The well water tasted faintly of starlight on cold nights, which the lowlanders who came up to trade pretended not to notice and quietly filled extra jugs. Hedgewitchery worked again, the humble domestic kind: bread that rose no matter the weather, fevers that broke when you sang to them, a charm Maren's granddaughter wove into a fishing net so that it never caught anything too small to have lived a full and happy fish life.

Nothing blazed. Nothing towered. Highmoor had been starved of wonder for three hundred years, and it gobbled up magic like a child given sweets after a long illness — greedily at first, then more sensibly, then with a kind of settled gratitude. The valley seemed to understand, in the way valleys do, that it had nearly lost everything once, and that the second helping should be savored slowly.

The gate itself stood open.

That had been the hardest thing, those first years — walking past it daily, seeing the archway unsealed, the old sigils glowing soft as banked coals instead of locked as iron. Every instinct Wren had drilled into the valley said *a gate must be shut or we are all undone*. But this gate was not shut, and it was not unguarded either. It was guarded by Wren.

Or rather, by what Wren had become, which was harder to say and easier to feel. The stones of the archway hummed when you touched them, a deep contented note like a cat the size of a cathedral. And sometimes — this was the part Sef loved, the part that made her throat tight even now — sometimes the hum answered.

You had to speak Aelthric. That was the key. Lowland speech slid past the stones like water off slate, but Aelthric sank in. Say *good morning* in the old tongue and the hum would shift, warm, attentive. Ask a question and the silence before the answer felt exactly like Wren pausing to think — that same little skeptical pause, the pause of someone deciding whether your question deserved an honest answer or a sigh.

Sef visited every evening. She always would.

* * *

On the evening after the map arrived, she climbed the path to the gatehouse carrying two cups of cider and the map rolled under her arm. The frost moon was rising, fat and pale, the same moon under which a barefoot child had once stood in the snow unable to cross a threshold. Nine years, and Sef still took off her boots at the boundary line out of habit, though the wards had welcomed her since the night they finally understood whose blood walked through them. She kept doing it anyway. Some rituals are how you say thank you.

"Wren," she said to the archway, in Aelthric. "*Good evening. I've brought cider and news.*"

The stones hummed. It was a particular hum tonight — lower, wry. Wren-hum had developed a vocabulary over the years, or perhaps Sef had simply learned to hear it. This one meant something like *you only visit when you want something, child.*

"That's slander. I bring cider."

She set one cup on the threshold stone, where it steamed, and the steam curled in a way steam didn't usually curl — toward the archway, eager, and then settled, and the cup was empty when she looked back. The first year that had happened she'd cried for an hour. Now it just made her smile. Wren had always liked cider and had always pretended not to.

"The Cartographers wrote." She unrolled the map against the gatepost, weighting the corners with pebbles. "Ilsa Vane. She's — I think she's head of the whole order now. She deposed the ones who wanted to erase me. Did I tell you that? She says they spent six years quietly undoing erasures. There are forty-one places back on the maps. Forty-one." Her voice caught, the way it still did. "Aelthmar is going to be last. She wants us to choose what gets written. The borders, the names, everything. They're sending a survey team in spring, if we'll have them."

The hum went long and thoughtful. Steam rose again from the threshold stone, briefly, like a breath drawn in.

"I know," Sef said softly. "I know what you'd say. You'd say be careful. You'd say trust them exactly as far as you can throw the entire order. You'd say—" she imitated, gently, the flat keeper's tone she remembered from another life, "*the rule is nothing crosses the boundary that we haven't checked twice.*"

The hum did something new then. Something Sef had never heard it do.

It laughed.

Not the warm hum, not the wry hum — an actual ripple of amusement through the stones, brief and unmistakable, and then a pulse of pressure against her mind that carried, wordless but perfectly clear, the shape of a sentence she had heard Wren say a hundred times:

*The rules were for keeping things out. It's time somebody learned the rules for letting things in.*

Sef stood very still. Then she pressed her palm flat against the gatepost, feeling the hum travel up her arm like a hand squeezing hers, and she said, "All right. Survey team in spring. And I'm writing the name myself. My handwriting's terrible, so they'll just have to live with it."

The hum wrapped around her, brief and fierce, and let go.

She drank her cider on the wall below the gate, watching the valley lights come on one by one — the farmhouses, the inn, the schoolhouse where Bram Oakley had presumably gone home to sneeze Aelthric at his long-suffering mother. Somewhere down there, forty-one places were waking up on maps, blinking in the light, remembering themselves.

* * *

Winter came, and Sef taught.

This was her work now, and she had come to understand it was the true work, the work the door out of Aelthmar had always been waiting for. Not escape. Not sealing. *Teaching.* Because a country does not live on maps. A country lives in mouths — in children saying *seven* like a sneeze and grandmothers humming lullabies whose words they suddenly, miraculously understood, and traders from the lowlands picking up greetings because it was useful and then picking up jokes because they were funny and then, one day, catching themselves dreaming in a language that was supposed to be dead.

Her classroom had grown. Eleven children had become thirty, plus Maren, plus half the adults in the valley who claimed they were only there to fetch their kids but stayed, sitting in the back rows, mouthing the words. She taught the numbers and the seasons and the names of the stars, which in Aelthric were all names of ancestors, so that looking up at night was a kind of family reunion. She taught the old songs, and the hedgerows learned them too — foxgloves blooming in verse now, which the bees appreciated enormously.

And once a season, she taught the lesson that mattered most. She would roll out Ilsa Vane's gift — updated each year now, more roads added, more erased neighbors restored — and she would point to the blank spaces that remained, the places still unmapped, still unmade, and she would tell the children about the Cartographers, and about the cost of forgetting, and about a keeper who had traded everything so that forgetting wouldn't win.

"Why did Wren do it?" Bram asked, every single time, the way children ask the questions adults circle around. "Why their name? Why not yours?"

Because mine was the key to the door, Sef thought. And theirs was older than any kingdom. Because inherited names are heavy, and someone has to finally set them down somewhere they can rest.

But what she said was: "Because Wren Okafor was the last keeper of a closed gate, and the first keeper of an open one, and being both at once is the loneliest job there ever was. So when you walk past the archway — and you will, every day of your life — you say good morning in our language. You make sure the hum never has to wonder whether it's remembered."

"And it answers?"

"It answers."

* * *

Spring came early that year, the way it had learned to, politely. The survey team arrived on the first mild day — five cartographers with mud on their boots and terror and hope fighting openly on their faces, led by a gray-haired woman who stopped at the boundary line, took off her hat, and waited to be invited in.

Sef went down to meet her.

"Ilsa Vane," the woman said. "We corresponded."

"You look tired," Sef said.

"I've spent nine years un-erasing the world. It turns out that's slower than erasing it." Ilsa looked past her, up the valley, at the green and the lights and the archway on the hill, and her voice went rough. "Is it true? What the reports say. That the stones answer."

"Come and see," said Sef, and stepped aside, and opened the way.

They climbed together through the foxgloves, past the schoolhouse, up the old road to the gate, and when they reached it Sef put her hand on the stone and said, in Aelthric, the oldest greeting she knew — the one that meant, literally, *I see you, and you are not forgotten.*

The valley held its breath.

And the stones of the gate at Highmoor hummed — deep, and warm, and glad — and answered.
