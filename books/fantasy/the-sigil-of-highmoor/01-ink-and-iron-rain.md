## 1. Ink and Iron Rain

The rain came down on Highmoor like a tax collector — relentless, impersonal, and arriving exactly when it was least wanted.

Dominic Dravenmoor felt it in her teeth before she heard it on the roof. That was the trouble with being what she was: the weather never let her have a quiet morning. Somewhere over the Grayfell mountains a front was building, all pressure and promise, and it pressed against the inside of her ribs like a cat demanding to be let out. Ten years of practice had taught her to ignore it. Ten years of practice had not, unfortunately, made it stop.

"You're clenching your pen again," said Marta from the next desk.

"I am *holding* my pen."

"You're strangling it. Yesterday you snapped a nib clean in half and blamed the goose that made the quill. Geese don't make defective quills, Dom. You just hate them personally."

Dominic loosened her grip with the deliberate care of a woman defusing something explosive, which, in a manner of speaking, she was. "The goose and I have an understanding," she said. "It provides quills. I provide grudges. It's a very old arrangement."

Marta snorted and went back to her column of figures, and Dominic turned to the manifest in front of her and felt the small warmth of an ordinary morning gutter out like a candle in a draft.

*Item forty-one,* said the manifest, in the tidy hand of the night foreman. *One (1) live specimen, crated. Handle after dark only.*

That was never good. In eleven years as a guild clerk, Dominic had learned that anything requiring darkness fell into one of two categories: smuggled brandy, which was boring, or magic, which was not. And the guild's ledgers had been trending sharply away from brandy of late.

The receiving floor of the Merchants' and Carters' Guild of Highmoor smelled of wet wool, tallow, and sawdust, and sounded like the inside of a very argumentative bell. Porters shouted numbers at each other. A cart axle screamed somewhere near the tarpaulins. Rain drummed the high windows, gray light falling in long dusty sheets across crates stacked like a city built by drunkards. Dominic threaded through it all with her ledger under her arm and her ink-stained fingers laced together, the picture of a clerk, because she was one, because she had made herself one, stitch by careful invisible stitch, for ten years.

The crate sat apart from the others, in the corner where the lamplight didn't reach, which told her the foreman already knew what he had.

"Forty-one," she said to the porter standing guard over it, a big placid man named Osk whose primary talents were lifting heavy things and remaining unbothered by everything, including, apparently, whatever was scratching softly inside the crate.

"After dark," Osk said. "Foreman's orders."

"I'm cataloguing it now or I'm cataloguing it never. The crown inspectors want manifests current by week's end, and I refuse to spend my Sunday listening to you describe a crate from memory. You'll say 'wooden.' You always say 'wooden.'"

Osk considered this. Osk considered most things. "It is wooden," he offered.

"Open it."

He did, prying the lid with a crowbar, and Dominic leaned over the edge with her pen poised, and then she did not write anything for a very long time.

Inside, packed in straw, was a cage. Inside the cage was a moth.

She knew moths. Highmoor bred them in its wool lofts; they got into everything, ate the ledgers if you let them, were generally gray and apologetic. This was not that. This moth was the size of a dinner plate and it was made of moonlight — there was no other way to put it, though Dominic spent several seconds trying, because *made of moonlight* was the sort of phrase that got people hanged. Its wings were panes of pale silver fire, and where they beat, slow as a sleeping heartbeat, the straw beneath them glowed, and the shadows in the crate moved wrong, bending toward the light instead of away from it.

And it was looking at her. Moths don't look at things. This one looked at her the way a dog looks at a whistle it can hear and no one else can.

Dominic became aware, distantly, of a sound like a struck tuning fork beginning somewhere behind her sternum.

No, she told it. No. We are not doing this.

The storm in her chest — she thought of it as a storm because she'd never found a better word, and she'd had ten years and a great many sleepless nights to look — pressed upward, delighted, *recognized*, the way it always did when it met its own kind. The rain against the windows stuttered. For half a breath every drop on the glass hung motionless, suspended, like the whole sky had paused to listen.

She breathed out, slow, through her nose, the way Master Corvane had taught her in the first awful weeks: *count the ledger columns, count them backward, count the errors.* Nine entries, eight, seven. The raindrops remembered their business and fell. The moth dimmed its wings to embers and watched her with an expression she could only call polite interest, which was frankly worse than alarm.

"Ink," she said aloud, because Osk was watching her. Her voice came out level, and she was quietly astonished by that. "I've got ink on my face again, haven't I."

"Both cheeks," Osk said. "Like whiskers."

"A distinguished look."

"Very clerical."

She wrote the entry. *One (1) live specimen, luminous, winged. Origin: unlisted. Value: pending assessment.* Her hand did not shake, and she took a moment to be proud of that, because pride was cheaper than terror and lasted longer.

* * *

The problem, she reflected later at her desk, was not that she had a gift. The problem was that she had gotten *good* at hiding it, and being good at hiding things meant nobody ever checked whether you still wanted to be hidden.

Ten years ago she had been someone else — a name with a sigil attached to it, a family, a house with weather-vanes that spun when the children laughed. Then the king's men came with iron collars and a list, and the sigil was scraped off the door, and the name was scraped off the registers, and a girl who could taste lightning on the air was walked out of the smoke by a guild master who owed her father money and paid the debt in forged papers instead.

*Dominic Dravenmoor,* the papers said, and so Dominic Dravenmoor she became: a drab, competent, faintly sarcastic clerk with ink on both cheeks and a gift folded up so small inside her it sometimes felt less like power than like a stone she'd swallowed.

The discipline of it had a rhythm, almost a comfort, the way a corset does once you stop fighting it. Count the columns backward. Breathe through the nose. Never touch iron during a thunderstorm. Never stand too close to open flame, because fire made her careless. Never, ever let anyone see you look at the sky the way other people look at lovers.

And in exchange: a desk. Warm bread. Marta's terrible jokes. Master Corvane's gruff, undiscussable protection. A life.

It was a fair price. She told herself it was a fair price. She had gotten very good at telling herself things.

"Dom." Marta's head appeared around the partition, wearing the expression of a woman delivering gossip and enjoying it. "You're wanted upstairs. Corvane wants the irregular-stock manifests on his desk within the hour."

"All of them?"

"All three years' worth." Marta lowered her voice with relish. "There's criers in the square. Crown proclamation. Something about a *royal hunt* — and inspectors coming with it. Old Corvane's been pacing his office like a bear with a pebble in its boot since dawn."

Dominic kept her face pleasant and empty, the face she wore for the world, and behind it her stomach dropped through the floorboards, past the cellar, into the aquifer.

A royal hunt. For the gifted. She had read enough between the lines of the last two years' manifests to know what the crown hunted them *for*, even if no page ever said it plainly: crates marked *handle after dark*, cages marked *live specimen*, values *pending assessment*. Cargo that breathed. Cargo that glowed. Cargo, occasionally, that wept.

And now the hunt was coming here. To Highmoor. To her.

"Within the hour," she repeated. "Tell him I'm compiling."

She waited until Marta's footsteps faded, then drew the top manifest toward her and began, mechanically, to check the columns. Item forty-one stared up at her from the fresh page, luminous, winged, alive.

Outside, the rain thickened, and far off over the Grayfells something rumbled that was not yet thunder but was thinking about becoming it. Dominic pressed her ink-stained hands flat against the ledger to keep them steady, and felt the storm press back, patient as spring.

Somewhere in the square below, a crier's bell began to ring.
