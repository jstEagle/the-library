## 2. The Crown's Proclamation

The bell over the Mercantile Guild of Highmoor rang at the wrong hour, and everyone in the counting room knew what that meant before anyone said it.

Dominic Dravenmoor — though no one had called her that in ten years, and no one alive but one boy knew to call her anything at all — set down her pen with the deliberate calm she had practiced until it stopped being a performance and became a spine. Bells rang at nine for the opening of trade, at noon for the meal, at dusk for the closing of the gates. They did not ring mid-morning on a grey Tuesday unless something had gone wrong somewhere, or was about to.

"Proclamation," said Pell, the junior clerk, poking his head through the doorway of the records annex. He was nineteen, freckled, and constitutionally incapable of delivering bad news without grinning, which made him either very brave or very foolish depending on the news. "Criers on the steps. Crown business. Everyone's meant to come out and listen."

"Then I suppose we'll come out and listen," Dominic said, and capped her ink.

Her hands did not shake. She checked them the way other people checked their buttons — quickly, automatically, a dozen times a day. Steady. Ink-stained. Ordinary. Ten years of practice lived in those hands, ten years since a girl with storm-light in her veins had died in a fire along with her family name, and a quiet guild clerk named Dom had been born from the ashes with nothing more dangerous than good penmanship.

The square outside was already filling. Guild clerks in their sober grey, fishwives with their baskets set down, children perched on the mounting block where they'd be swatted later for it. And above them, on the guildhall steps, three royal criers in scarlet-and-gold livery that looked, in Highmoor's perpetual drizzle, like wet blood on wet rust.

The middle crier unfurled a scroll with theatrical slowness.

"By order of His Majesty Aldric the Second, rightful sovereign of the Nine Provinces—" A pause here, long enough that everyone understood the *rightful* was doing work. There'd been trouble over succession two winters back; everyone pretended not to remember. "—let it be known throughout his realm: a great hunting is declared. All persons harboring, concealing, or practicing unlicensed magic are hereby commanded to surrender themselves to crown authority within forty days."

A murmur went through the crowd like wind through barley.

"All such persons will be treated justly according to their gifts and their cooperation," the crier continued, in the tone of a man reciting a recipe he didn't intend to cook. "Those who shelter them will be charged as accomplices. Rewards are offered for information leading to the discovery of the gifted. By the king's grace, by the king's law, by the king's—"

He never got to the third *king's*. The fishwife nearest Dominic spat, loudly and with evident professionalism, and half the crowd laughed, and the criers' scarlet backs stiffened, and the moment curdled pleasantly for everyone except, Dominic thought, the people in the crowd who could not afford to laugh.

She kept her face arranged into the mild, slightly bored expression of a clerk who found proclamations tedious. Inside her chest, beneath the breastbone, under ten years of careful masonry, something turned over in its sleep and listened.

*Unlicensed magic.* As if there were a license. As if there were a window in some ministry where you queued with your little gift in a jar and paid your fee.

Forty days. Then inspectors. Then doors kicked in across the provinces, starting, if history held, with the poor quarters and working up toward the merchants who could pay to be overlooked.

Dominic's eyes drifted, as they always did, to the third-floor window of the guildhall. Behind its leaded glass, she knew, stood Master Corvane's office, and behind Master Corvane's desk, she also knew, sat ledgers. Her ledgers. The ones she kept in her own fair hand, recording every crate and cask and cage that moved through Highmoor's warehouses under the guild's seal.

*I'll need you sharp this season,* Corvane had told her only last week, resting one hand briefly on her shoulder in the way he did when he wanted to seem paternal and mostly succeeded. *Big movements coming. Crown contracts.*

She had assumed grain. Grain, tin, salt. Things that were merely illegal because of taxes, rather than illegal because of what they *were*.

She was beginning to suspect otherwise.

* * *

Corvane summoned the senior staff within the hour, which was fast even for him. He stood at the head of the counting table with the proclamation itself spread out before him, weighted at the corners with inkwells, as though it might blow away or bite.

"You've all heard it," he said. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, silver-bearded, with the permanent faintly apologetic look of someone who had once been idealistic and had recovered. "Inspectors from the capital within the fortnight. Which means, gentlemen and ladies, an audit of our holdings. Everything in our warehouses must be accounted for, catalogued, and — where necessary — relocated."

"Relocated where?" asked Mistress Halloway, who ran the wool contracts and feared nothing on earth except damp.

"To our southern partners." Corvane's eyes swept the table. "Specifically, the irregular stock."

There it was. The phrase landed in the room and sat down among them like an unwelcome guest. Dominic watched the senior clerks exchange glances — Halloway's chin lifting, old Tam Vess suddenly fascinated by his own fingernails. They knew. Some of them had always known. She had suspected it for a year now, in the way you suspect a wall has rot behind sound plaster: you press it gently and then you stop pressing, because you don't want to know.

"Irregular stock," Halloway repeated flatly.

"The crown pays well for discretion," Corvane said. "And pays better still for speed. Nothing leaves this warehouse after the inspectors arrive that shouldn't be here when they do. Master Dom —" and his gaze found hers, steady, unreadable, "— I'll want full manifests by Friday. Every lot, every seal number, everything marked with a blue ribbon in the east annex."

Blue ribbon. She had tied those ribbons herself, months ago, on crates whose manifests read *ceramic goods* and *textiles, assorted* and, memorably, *live songbirds, twelve*. Twelve songbirds that had cried out in human voices at night, softly, when they thought no one heard.

"Yes, Master Corvane," she said.

"Good man." He paused. "Good clerk," he corrected, without apology, because he was the only person in Highmoor permitted to make that joke, and he made it precisely often enough to remind her he could.

The meeting broke apart into murmuring clusters. Dominic gathered her notes with steady hands and walked back to the annex at exactly her usual pace, which took considerable effort, because every instinct she owned was screaming at her to run.

* * *

The east annex smelled of cedar shavings and lamp oil, and in the afternoon light it held its secrets poorly. Dominic unlocked it with her own key, sat at her own desk, and pulled the blue-ribbon ledger toward her with fingers that were, she noted distantly, finally beginning to tremble. Well. Better here than in the square.

She worked backward, because that was how dread worked — from the present into the past, peeling layers. The most recent lots: *crates, sealed, contents unknown, transit south*. Before that: *cages, iron, six units, contents listed as 'livestock.'* Before that—

She stopped.

The entry was dated fourteen months ago. Lot 77-C, received from the provincial assizes at Marrowfield. Contents: *one (1) individual, female, age approx. 30. Sigil class: weather-working. Disposition: transferred to crown custody, fee rendered.*

And beside it, where the receiving clerk was meant to record the source house or family mark of any bonded goods, someone had stamped a sigil in faded violet wax.

Dominic knew the sigil the way you know your own heartbeat. A lightning-fork crossed with a river-bar, inside a circle of seven stars. She had drawn it in the frost of windows as a child. She had watched it burned off her father's door-lintel when she was fifteen, along with the door, along with much of the house, along with — the record said — her mother, her father, and her name.

Dravenmoor. Lot 77-C. Fee rendered.

The lamp hissed. Somewhere overhead, rain began against the roof, soft as an apology.

They hadn't taken her whole family. That was the thing her mind circled, refusing to land, refusing to accept. When the hunters came to Marrowfield, the story everyone told afterward was that the Dravenmoors had been executed entire — father, mother, daughter, all three dead in the fire, all three avenged by nobody, mourned by nobody, because mourners got investigated. But this entry said *female, age approx. 30*, singular. One woman. Weather-working. Transferred.

Which meant either the record was wrong, or the fire had been staged around an absence, or —

Or her mother had been taken first. Months before the fire. Had spent her last year in a crate marked with a violet seal, shipped south by the same guild that would later give a frightened girl with singed eyebrows a false name and a job keeping books.

*You owe me nothing,* Corvane had said, the night he'd found her half-dead on the high road, ten years ago, and wrapped her in his own coat. *But if you work hard and stay small, I'll ask nothing of you either.* And she had believed it, because grief makes excellent soil for believing things. Stay small. Keep the books. Don't ask what's under the blue ribbons.

She had asked nothing for ten years. She realized now that she had known, in the wordless way of the complicit, and had let the not-knowing be her rent.

Dominic sat very still while the storm rose in her chest — not the metaphorical one. The real one. It pressed against her ribs like a bird testing bars, and the lamp flame leaned, and the rain on the roof thickened from whisper to drum, and somewhere in the rafters the building groaned as the wind found it. Ten years of discipline said *put it down, put it down, put it down.* She breathed the way she'd taught herself to breathe. In for four. Out for four. The pressure receded, sullen, and settled back into its cell below her heart.

Then she picked up her pen.

Because here was the arithmetic she could not escape: the proclamation gave the hunt forty days. Corvane wanted the irregular stock moved before the inspectors came — moved *south*, to the capital, to whatever waited there for caged women and men and children with violet seals on their crates. And the ledger in front of her recorded, in her own fair hand or her colleagues', every shipment, every route, every fee. If she could read the pattern forward, she might learn where the stock went. If she could read it backward —

She turned to a fresh page, and began copying. Names where names existed. Dates. Seals. Routes. Her handwriting stayed beautiful throughout, because it was the one part of her that had never learned to be afraid.

Outside, the rain kept falling, and far off over the moors, thunder answered something she hadn't said aloud.

She was halfway through the second ledger when she noticed the shadow in the doorway — a boy, thin as a whipstock, with mud on his boots and a smuggler's easy slouch, watching her copy stolen pages with an expression of dawning, terrible interest.

He raised one finger to his lips.

Dominic, clerk of the Mercantile Guild of Highmoor, keeper of the blue-ribbon manifests, felt her whole hidden life tilt on its axis — and did not stop writing.
