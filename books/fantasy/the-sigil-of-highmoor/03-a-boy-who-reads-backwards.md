## 3. A Boy Who Reads Backwards

Wren Ashfoot had three rules for surviving in Highmoor, and he'd learned all of them the hard way, which was the only way anyone learned anything worth knowing.

Rule one: never be the most interesting person in a room.

Rule two: never steal from the same pocket twice.

Rule three: if you find something that looks like trouble, put it back exactly where you found it and walk away whistling.

He was breaking rule three at this very moment, sitting cross-legged on the floor of the guild's records annex at an hour when honest people were asleep, holding a ledger page up to the light of a stolen candle stub and reading it backwards.

To be fair, he hadn't gone looking for it. That was his story and he intended to stick to it. He'd been in the annex to lift a wax seal stamp — the good one, with the twin-tower sigil, the kind of thing that turned a forged bill of lading from a gamble into a guarantee — and the ledger had simply been *there*, open on the clerk's desk, its pages written in that strange flowing mirror-script that made ordinary readers squint and give up.

Ordinary readers gave up because they thought the letters were wrong. They weren't wrong. They were just written right-to-left, the way Wren's grandmother had taught him to write, back when she was alive and the family still had a name nobody bothered to hunt. Left-handed folk wrote like that sometimes, she'd said, or folk who wanted their letters kept private from prying eyes. Prying eyes see reversed writing and think: *cipher*. Prying eyes are lazy. It had never once occurred to Wren that other people couldn't read it. Finding out, at age seven, that his trick was actually a gift had been one of the great disappointments of his life, ranking somewhere below the time he'd discovered the "magic ring" he'd shoplifted only worked on credulous tourists.

So he read the page. And then he read it again, because the first time he was sure he'd held the candle at a bad angle.

*Lot fourteen. Female, approx. thirty years. Gift: mending (minor). Condition: subdued. Delivered to capital escort, third bell. Price settled.*

Not contraband. Not silk, not brandy, not unlicensed spellpaper. A woman. With a price. And beneath her entry, more entries, columns of them, dates and descriptions and sums, each line ending in that same phrase — *delivered to capital escort* — like a refrain in a song nobody should have been singing.

Wren sat very still, because rule one was suddenly the most important rule in the world, and he was fairly sure he had just become the most interesting person in the building.

The candle guttered. Somewhere above him, the guildhouse creaked through its nighttime bones. And Wren Ashfoot, who could talk his way past a customs officer, sweet-talk a harbor matron, and lie fluently in two languages and one dialect, realized there was no clever exit from what he now knew. You couldn't unknow a column of names with prices beside them.

He did the only sensible thing. He copied the page — quickly, in his own hand, forward-reading, hidden inside what looked like a shipping manifest — folded it small, tucked it into his boot where he kept the really important things (his knife, his mother's button, and half a honey-cake of uncertain age), put everything back precisely as he'd found it, and walked away whistling.

It was a terrible whistle. His nerves always made it flat. But nobody stopped him, which he chose to interpret as mastery rather than luck.

* * *

The trouble with knowing a secret in Highmoor was that the town was small enough that secrets got bored of being kept and went looking for company.

Two days later, Wren was in the guild courtyard ostensibly delivering a crate of "Kellish glassware" that was, in fact, mostly sand and optimism, when he saw the clerk.

She came out of the records office with an armful of ledgers, and everyone in the courtyard did the same thing they always did around her, which was nothing at all. Dominic, they called her. Just Dominic, no surname, the way you'd call a cat or a well. She had ink on her fingers the way other people had scars — everywhere, permanent, earned — and she walked like someone carrying a full cup across a crowded room: careful, level, giving nothing away to the slosh.

But here was the thing Wren noticed, the thing that had been itching at him since he read that page. When she passed the new porter, a big fellow with a stutter and a habit of dropping things, a crate slipped off his shoulder, and before it hit the ground it *stopped*. Hung in the air a half-second too long, like a bird deciding whether to land, then settled soft as a feather into the man's arms.

Nobody else saw it. The porter himself blinked at the crate like it had apologized to him.

Dominic's hand was half-raised, fingers spread, and then it closed, slowly, and went back to gripping her ledgers, and her face did absolutely nothing at all.

That face doing nothing was, in Wren's professional opinion, the loudest thing he'd seen all week.

He set down his crate of sand. "Mistress Clerk," he called, cheerful as a market bell. "Got a delivery problem. Need a signature."

"I signed your manifest at the gate," she said, without looking up.

"Ah, but you signed it *forward*," Wren said. "And I've recently developed a passionate interest in backward writing."

That got her to look up. Her eyes were gray, storm-colored, and for one unguarded instant something moved behind them like weather. Then the shutters came down, smooth as a drawer sliding closed, and she said, "I don't know what you're talking about."

"No," Wren agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't. Nobody does. That's sort of the point of it." He drifted closer, hands loose at his sides, smile fixed at the exact width that said *harmless* without saying *stupid*. "Funny thing about mirror-script. Most folk take one look and decide it's a cipher. Takes a special kind of education to just... read it."

"Special kinds of people get hunted," she said quietly.

"And yet," Wren said, "here we both are."

* * *

She didn't run. That surprised him. Most people, offered an accusation wrapped in a compliment, either fled or swung. Dominic did neither; she stood in the courtyard with her armful of ledgers and studied him the way she probably studied suspicious entries — patiently, thoroughly, looking for the forgery.

"You've been in my office," she said at last.

"The guild's office," Wren corrected. "I'm told the guild serves the crown, and the crown serves the people, and the people pay for the privilege. Very circular. Very tidy." He glanced around — porters, carters, nobody close — and lowered his voice. "*Lot fourteen.* Mending, minor. There were forty-one lots on that page alone. I stopped counting after that because counting them made me want to be sick in a hedge."

Something happened to her face then. The shutters didn't come down; they cracked. Just a hairline, just for a moment, and what showed through wasn't guilt exactly, though there was plenty of that, but something older and tireder. Grief that had been carried so long it had become posture.

"You don't understand what you took," she said.

"I understand numbers," Wren said. "My gran taught me figures before letters. Forty-one lots, times however many pages, times however many books. That's not smuggling, mistress. Smuggling moves things people want. This—" he tapped the spine of the topmost ledger in her arms, gently, almost politely— "this moves people nobody wants to admit exist. There's a difference. The difference is everything."

"They'll hang you for that page."

"They'll hang me for the seal stamp," Wren said. "Or the honey-cake. Or for having a face like mine, which every watchman in three towns has memorized. I was already going to hang, mistress. Now I get to hang *informed*." He grinned, and even he could hear how thin it was. "There's a freedom in it."

For a moment he thought she'd laugh. She didn't, but something in her jaw loosened, and she said, "Wren, isn't it? The Ashfoot boy."

"Infamous," he confirmed. "Though I notice you didn't ask how I knew my own name was in your head. Clerks gossip?"

"Clerks remember." She shifted the ledgers. "What do you want?"

Honest question, honestly asked, and it deserved better than the answer he'd prepared (nothing, nothing, just a look, forget me). What came out instead was true: "I want to know if any of them were children."

Her breath caught. Just barely. But Wren had built a life on catching barely — the barely-weighted purse, the barely-ajar window, the lie that barely held — and he caught hers.

"Yes," she said.

* * *

They ended up in the annex after dark, because she said if he was going to be a disaster he would at least be a disaster where she could supervise him, and Wren decided that was as close to friendship as he was likely to get this year.

She lit two lamps instead of one, which he understood as ceremony. Then she pulled the ledger — the one, the real one — across the desk and opened it, and watched him read.

He read aloud, translating as he went, because he wanted her to hear it in plain speech, forward-facing, undeniable: names, gifts, conditions, prices. *Subdued. Compliant. Damaged, reduced rate.* Each word landed in the little room like a stone dropped in a well, and neither of them reached for the next one quickly.

Halfway down a page his hands started shaking.

He hadn't noticed until they knocked against the lamp base and rattled it, and he snatched them back into his lap, embarrassed, thirteen years old and pretending to be a man of business and apparently made entirely of water. "Sorry," he muttered. "Cold in here."

"It isn't," Dominic said.

"No."

And then she reached over and put her hands over his.

They were ink-stained and steady and warm, and they pressed his trembling fingers flat against the desk the way you'd press down papers in wind, and the shaking slowed, and then stopped, and Wren looked up at her and felt the whole shape of his understanding rearrange itself with an almost audible click.

Because it wasn't comfort. Or it wasn't *only* comfort. Her hands were doing something — some small, controlled, deliberate thing, a thread of warmth running through her palms into his, precise as penmanship — and the tremor in his bones obeyed it.

"You're one of them," he whispered. "A lot. You're a lot that got away."

"Don't." The word came out sharp and quiet at once. Her hands withdrew, folded, vanished into her sleeves. "Don't ever say that word near me again."

"All right." He meant it. He raised both palms, placating. "All right. But you steadied me. That's not clerking, mistress. That's not a trick you learn from a ledger."

For a long moment she said nothing. The lamps hissed. Outside, Highmoor murmured its evening murmur — carts, gulls, a drunk singing three notes of a song he'd forgotten the rest of — utterly indifferent to the two most dangerous people in the city sitting three feet apart.

"My family had a name," she said finally, so quietly he had to lean in. "It's in those books, once. Erased. Struck through in the master copy. If you can read backwards, you can read a strike-through, if the hand underneath is stubborn enough." Her gray eyes fixed on him. "So now you know what I am. Which means we have a problem, you and I."

"Mutual blackmail," Wren said brightly. "Gran always said it's the strongest bond there is. Stronger than love. Love lets go. Blackmail holds on forever."

Something twitched at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. The ancestor of a smile, maybe, preserved in amber. "You're impossible."

"I'm *alive*," Wren corrected. "It's the same thing, done with more effort." He sobered, laid his finger on the page, on the last legible line — *delivered to capital escort, third bell* — and let the humor drain out of his voice entirely. "Where do they take them, Dominic? Where does the caravan go after the gates? Because I've watched those wagons roll out, and I've watched the road east, and nobody comes back down it. Ever. Goods come back. Crates come back. People don't."

She was silent long enough that he thought she wouldn't answer. Then she rose, crossed to the shelf behind her, and drew out a slim volume he'd never seen — bound in gray, unlabeled, its lock already hanging open as if she'd been expecting to need it tonight.

"There's a place under the guild," she said. "The old vaults, from before the guild was the guild. The ledgers say the deliveries stop there first." Her hand rested on the cover without opening it. "I've kept the books for six years, Wren. I've told myself I was keeping my head down, keeping safe, keeping *useful*. Tonight I'd like to find out what I was actually keeping."

Wren stood, checked his boot-knife, patted the page-copy over his heart, and grinned the grin that had gotten him thrown out of four respectable establishments and one church.

"After you, mistress clerk," he said. "I've always wanted to see where the guild keeps its conscience."

"It doesn't have one," Dominic said, lifting the lamp. "That's why we're bringing ours."

The stairs down were older than anything in Highmoor, and colder, and they went much further than stairs had any right to go.
