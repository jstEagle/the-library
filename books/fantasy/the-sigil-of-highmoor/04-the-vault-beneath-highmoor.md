## 4. The Vault Beneath Highmoor

The ledgers said the sigils were stored "below," and below was a word that meant different things depending on who wrote it.

To a guild clerk, *below* meant the cellar where they kept the cheap wine and the cheaper paper. To a guild master, it apparently meant a stair that didn't exist on any floor plan, hidden behind a rack of shipping manifests in the counting room's back corner, behind a lock so old its key had to be coaxed rather than turned.

"You realize," Wren whispered, crouched beside me in the dark while I wrestled with the mechanism, "that if this opens into a wine cellar, we're breaking into the guild for nothing."

"Then we'll have stolen wine instead of answers," I said. "I've had worse evenings."

"I haven't. And I've been arrested twice."

The lock gave with a sound like a knuckle cracking. Beyond it, stairs descended in a spiral so narrow my shoulders brushed stone on both sides, and the air changed — grew cold in the way deep places are cold, but also strange, charged, like the hush before lightning. My chest tightened around the familiar pressure I had spent ten years teaching to sit still.

Wren felt it too. He always felt weather before I did; I'd never decided whether that made him perceptive or unlucky.

"It's like standing under a storm cloud," he murmured. "Underground."

"Mm." I lit no lamp. The manifest rack above us had been dusty; whoever came down here did not come often, and did not come carrying light they'd want seen from the stairwell. Instead I let my eyes adjust and felt my way down by the wall, one step at a time, Wren's breath quick and shallow behind mine.

The stair ended in a door banded with iron. No lock this time — just a latch, and beyond the latch, a glow.

Not lamplight. Not torchlight. A pale silver radiance seeping through the gap beneath the door like water finding its level, and when I put my eye to that gap, what I saw made me forget how to breathe for three full seconds.

"Dom?" Wren whispered. "You've gone the color of ledger paper. That's usually my job."

I opened the door.

* * *

The vault was not large. That was the first obscenity of it — that something this vast could fit into something so small. A round chamber perhaps forty feet across, ceiling lost in shadow, shelves cut directly into the living rock. And on those shelves, row upon row upon row, in wooden cradles lined with wool —

Sigils.

Crystallized magic. I had read about them the way one reads about distant countries: academically, with a certain morbid fascination. Every gifted soul carries a sigil at their core, the shape their magic takes when it's finally pulled out of them — willingly or otherwise. The crown's hunters knew how to pull. The crystals before me were each the size of a fist, some smaller, faceted and glowing faintly from within, and each one pulsed with a slow rhythm like a heartbeat slowed to the pace of grief.

Moon-silver ones that hummed with illusions. Ember-red ones that flickered like dying hearths. One, near the door, a deep sea-green that rippled even in stillness, and I understood without being told that its owner had spoken with rivers.

Hundreds of them. Hundreds of people, reduced to shelf stock, sorted by intensity and quality the way the guild sorted pepper and saffron.

"They're labeled," Wren said hoarsely.

They were. Small tags, tied with cord, in the neat clerical hand I knew intimately because it was my own hand, trained into me by guild tutors until my letters marched in perfect rows. Lot numbers. Acquisition dates. Grades. *Volatile — handle with tongs. Dormant — stable. Fading — discount.*

Discount. Someone had priced a person's soul at a discount.

I heard myself make a small sound, and Wren's hand found my sleeve. "Dominic."

"I'm fine."

"You're not. Nobody's fine down here. But you're upright, and that's what matters right now."

He was right, and his matter-of-factness was the only rope I had, so I held it. We moved along the shelves together, reading tags by the light the sigils themselves provided, and it was Wren — because he could not help himself, because he was Wren — who found the pattern.

"Dates cluster," he said, tapping a tag. "Here — six acquisitions in one week, all from the Marches. Here, nine, all marked 'Highmoor circuit.'" His finger stopped. His voice did something complicated. "These are local. These are people from here. From the market. From — that's the miller's daughter's lot number, Dom. The one who went visiting her aunt in Vell and never came back. Everyone said she'd run off with a carter."

"She didn't run off with a carter," I said. My voice came out flat and terrible.

"No," Wren said. "She ran off with a discount tag."

We stood in the silver-dark among the heartbeats of the harvested, and I did the arithmetic I had been refusing to do since the night I first found the coded entries. Ten years of ledgers. Ten years of shipments I had catalogued as *wool*, as *salted fish*, as *agricultural implements*. I had inked the entries. I had balanced the columns. I had noticed, once or twice, that certain lots weighed oddly little for their volume, and I had told myself it was miscounted freight, because telling myself anything else would have required me to become a different person, and becoming a different person meant losing the only life I had left.

My silence had funded this room. My neat, disciplined, invisible little life had oiled the wheels that rolled these people down into the dark.

"Dominic," Wren said quietly. "Your hands."

I looked down. Frost was creeping across the nearest shelf edge, feathering out from under my fingers in delicate white ferns, and the air had gone sharp with the smell of an oncoming storm, and every sigil on every shelf within reach of me had begun to pulse faster — brightening, waking, answering.

My storm knew them. Or they knew mine. Either way, the whole vault was turning toward me like flowers toward a sun, and flowers do not care who sees them bloom.

"Stop it," Wren hissed. "Stop it stop it stop it—"

"I'm trying—" I wrenched my hand back and pressed both palms against my skirts, against my ribs, against the old scar over my heart where I had once, at thirteen years old, driven a needle through my own sigil's tether to muffle it. Breathe. Count. Ledger columns. Seven crates wool, four barrels salt, three souls sold at discount. Breathe.

The frost stopped. Slowly, grudgingly, the crystals dimmed back to their sleeping rhythm, though the nearest few seemed to watch me go with something like hope, and I could not bear that, so I stopped looking at them.

"That was nearly very bad," Wren said. He wasn't scolding. He was shaking. "If a guard comes down tonight, if anyone comes down—"

"Nobody comes down. The dust on the stairs is weeks old."

"The dust doesn't report to Corvane."

But he was already moving, doing the thing he did best, which was reading — except now there was almost nothing written, only the arrangement itself to decode. He paced the chamber's circumference, counting shelves, muttering. "Storage's organized by grade, but look — the innermost ring, the oldest shelf. Those aren't sorted by grade. They're sorted by *type*. All storm-affine. All lightning, thunder, weather-work." He turned to me, and in the sigil-light his face was very pale and very determined. "Someone's collecting a matched set."

"A matched set of what?"

"Of *you*, Dominic." He swallowed. "Or — no. Not of you. Of whatever they're building *with* you. This isn't a treasury. Treasuries get visited. This is a pantry. Something's being fed."

The word landed in my stomach like a stone. Fed. I thought of the crown's new warehouses outside the capital, built fast and windowless. I thought of the hunt's schedule, which I had transcribed last month without thinking, noting only that it accelerated toward midsummer. Toward coronation day.

"We need one," I said.

Wren blinked. "One what?"

"One sigil. Unharvested enough to still remember its owner." I was already pulling off my glove. "If these things can answer my storm, then they can tell me things. Where the rest have gone. What's being fed. Ledgers lie, Wren. I should know; I write them."

"That is the single worst idea you've had, and I once watched you eat a pickled egg you found in your desk."

"Wren."

"Three years old, Dominic. It had opinions."

"*Wren.*"

He threw up his hands. "Fine. Fine! But touch one from the outer ring — a dormant one, a quiet one — and be quick about it."

I chose a small crystal, ember-dim, tagged *dormant — stable*, and lifted it from its wool cradle.

It was warm. That undid me more than anything else in the vault — that after everything, after being pried out of someone who loved with fire, it was still *warm*. And the moment my bare skin met its facets, my storm rose in my chest like a dog hearing its name, joyous and enormous and utterly uninterested in discipline, and the crystal flared crimson-gold, and the flare leapt shelf to shelf to shelf, a chain of answered light racing around the vault, hundreds of sigils blazing awake at once, singing a chord I felt in my teeth—

And somewhere above us, faint through forty feet of rock, iron rang against iron. Once. Twice. An alarm, or a door, or boots.

Wren grabbed the crystal out of my hand — pain flashed across his face as it bit him, but he pocketed it anyway, wrapped in his scarf — and shoved me toward the stair.

"Later," he said. "Grieve later. Climb *now*."

* * *

We made the counting room as the bells above the guildhall began to ring the hour — midnight, ordinary, innocent — and stood gasping among the manifest racks while the dust resettled around our ankles and pretended we had never disturbed it.

Wren pressed the wrapped crystal into my hands. Through the wool I felt it pulse, slow and stubborn, keeping its dead owner's time.

"Whoever they were," he said quietly, "they wanted to be found. Everything in that room wanted to be found."

I nodded, because speech was beyond me.

And then, from the corridor outside the counting room, came footsteps. Measured. Unhurried. Familiar — the particular deliberate tread of a man who had taught me to keep books, to keep secrets, to keep my head down.

Guild master Corvane's voice drifted through the door, low and pleasant, addressing someone I couldn't see:

"No, no trouble at all. The irregulars are already boxed for transport. You'll find them exactly as promised — including the two who broke into my vault tonight."
