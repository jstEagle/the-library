## 5. What Corvane Owes

Master Aldous Corvane had kept three rules for thirty years, and he recited them the way other men said prayers: never lend what you cannot afford to lose, never drink with a creditor, and never — *never* — learn the true name of anyone you shelter.

He'd broken the third rule within an hour of meeting her.

She had come to him at fourteen, soaked to the bone, soot in her hair, with a burned half-ledger clutched to her chest like a shield. The Dravenmoor house was still smoldering behind her, and the king's riders were still picking through its ashes for survivors. She stood in his doorway and did not beg. That was the first thing he noticed. The second was that the rain around the doorway fell in a perfect circle around her — not one drop touching her skin — and she hadn't noticed it herself.

"Girl," he'd said carefully, "what's your name?"

A long pause. Long enough for lightning somewhere over the hills, though the sky had been clear all day.

"Dominic," she said. "Just Dominic."

And Corvane, who had spent thirty years not learning names, heard the lie and let it stand, because some lies are load-bearing walls. "Dominic," he repeated. "You'll need ink under those nails if you're going to pass as my clerk. Scrub first."

That had been ten years ago. He had fed her, taught her, taught himself not to flinch when thunder rolled on cloudless days or when her ledgers came back with scorched margins. He had told himself, every single year, that he was a good man doing a hard thing, keeping a storm bottled up inside a clerk's coat where no one would think to look for it.

He was still telling himself that, on the morning the inspectors came.

---

The letter arrived by royal courier, which meant it arrived sealed twice and smelling faintly of the palace incense that Corvane privately called "expensive guilt." He broke the seal in his office above the counting floor, read it once, and set it down with hands that did not shake because thirty years of practice is worth something.

*By order of the Crown Assize, the Guildhouse at Highmoor will submit to inspection of all irregular stock prior to the Feast of the Coronation.*

Irregular stock.

He knew precisely what irregular stock meant. He had signed the manifests himself. Cages, mostly, and crates with breathing holes drilled in careful patterns, and once — he still dreamed about it — a crate labeled *fragile, do not tilt* that had wept light through the seams for two days before the caravan took it south.

Corvane poured himself a measure of plum brandy at an hour he would normally have called disgraceful, and sat with the letter, and did his arithmetic.

The crown held his debts. Not the guild's debts — *his*. Old ones, from the bad years, from before he'd built the Highmoor chapter into something respectable. Debts to men who were now palace men, whose ledgers had long memories and longer knives. Every year the interest came due, and every year the payment was the same: keep the books, move the stock, ask nothing about what happened after the caravans crossed the southern bridge.

In exchange: Dominic stayed hidden. Ten years of hiding, purchased annually, like firewood.

"That's not how I'd frame it," he said aloud to the empty office, and the empty office, being honest, declined to argue.

Because here was the truth he circled every night and never landed on: he had not bought her safety. He had rented it. And the landlord could raise the rent whenever he pleased, and this year the landlord wanted more than silence.

The letter's postscript was four words long: *All irregular stock. All.*

---

He found her in the counting room, as he always found her, hunched over a ledger with her sleeves pushed back and ink to her elbows. She looked up when he came in — she always knew it was him; he'd never figured out how, unless the air itself announced him, which, given what she was, it might.

"The inspectors," he said. "They come in nine days."

Her pen stopped moving. A bead of ink swelled at the nib and fell, landing precisely on the blotter rather than the page, which was the kind of small impossible tidiness she produced without thinking, like breathing.

"All of it?" she asked.

"All of it. They want everything moved south before they arrive. Clean books for the coronation."

"And the stock in the vault?" Her voice was level. Too level. He'd learned to hear the weather underneath it — the low pressure before the squall. "The sigils. Those go too?"

"Yes."

She looked down at her ledger. For a moment he thought — hoped — she would say nothing, file her fear away the way she filed everything else. Then she said, quietly:

"Aldous. What are we *for*?"

It was the first time in ten years she'd used his given name, and it went through him like a splinter. He gave her the answer he'd been polishing for a decade, the one about survival and patience and the long game, about how the guild endured because it bent. He watched her listen politely, the way one listens to an old dog barking at nothing, and he understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that she had outgrown the answer. Perhaps she'd outgrown it years ago and had simply been too kind to tell him.

"I need time," he said instead. "Nine days. If I can delay the transfer—"

"You can't delay the crown."

"No," he agreed. "But I can slow it. Slow is sometimes enough."

He didn't know yet, that morning, what he meant by *slow*. He would find out. That was the curse of being the sort of man who solved problems: eventually he ran out of good solutions and had to use the others.

* * *

Wren came to the guildhouse on the sixth evening, smelling of river mud and stolen apples, with that quick ferret grin he aimed at everyone over fifty as if age entitled them to be charmed.

Corvane liked the boy. That was the problem. He'd liked him from the start — the cheek, the cleverness, the way Wren could read a manifest upside down faster than Corvane could read it right side up. In another life he'd have taken the boy as an apprentice and been proud of it.

In this life, Wren had been seen reading a ledger page in a mirror. Worse, he'd been seen talking to Dominic in the counting room at hours clerks don't talk. Worst of all, one of the crown's informants — a soft-bellied factor named Pell who smiled too much and reported too often — had marked the boy as *curious*, and curiosity, in the season of the hunt, was a disease that spread.

Corvane learned all this on the seventh morning, from Pell himself, delivered over tea with the gentle menace of a man describing weather.

"Strange thing," Pell mused, stirring honey into his cup. "That smuggler boy. Always underfoot. Always near your head clerk. You know how reports travel, Master Corvane. A name in a report becomes a name on a list, and lists get collected along with everything else." He sipped. "Unless, of course, the name proves to be a misunderstanding. Misunderstandings can be... managed. For friends."

There it was. The rent, raised.

Pell wanted the boy taken — quietly, deniably, as part of the general sweep — and in exchange, Pell would lose a certain report in a certain drawer, the one that mentioned a head clerk whose paperwork was impeccable but whose presence raised questions Corvane could not afford. Questions like: *where does she sleep, who vouches for her, why does the master favor her?*

One boy, weighed against ten years of sheltering. One stray against one storm.

Corvane sat very still and hated Pell with a thoroughness that would have impressed a scholar, and then — this was the terrible part, the part he would carry forever — he began, almost immediately, to think of reasons.

The boy was already compromised. Pell had proof of the mirrored reading; refusing wouldn't save Wren, only spend the leverage pointlessly. The boy was a smuggler, a criminal by any honest accounting. The boy was reckless and would have gotten himself caught eventually regardless. The boy was nobody's son, nobody's charge, whereas Dominic —

Whereas Dominic was the whole of what remained of his conscience, wearing ink stains.

*If I lose her,* he reasoned, in the warm reasonable voice he kept for exactly such moments, *then these ten years meant nothing. But if I keep her safe, there is still time to fix the rest. There is always time to fix the rest. That is what time is for.*

It was remarkable, he thought later, how quickly a man could furnish a betrayal. Like a room. One rationalization per corner, and suddenly it was comfortable enough to live in.

"Pell," Corvane heard himself say, and his voice was steady, which seemed obscene. "Your discretion does you credit. As for the boy — I make no promises about my clerks' acquaintances. But if the assize finds irregular persons on guild premises, well. The guild cannot be held responsible for strays that wander in off the road."

Pell smiled like a door opening onto a cell. "Of course. Strays." He set down his cup. "The hunters ride tomorrow, I believe. Early. Best that no one warns the little animals."

* * *

He told himself he would give the boy a chance. That was the final furnishing, the last cushion in the room: *I'll warn him obliquely. I'll clear the roads. I'll look away.* He composed three versions of an oblique warning during supper and abandoned all of them, because every version contained information only a guilty man would have, and a guilty man was exactly what Corvane could not appear to be.

So instead, on the eighth morning, he did the cruelest efficient thing: nothing. He rose early, dressed, and sat in his office with the shutters open, listening to the hooves of the hunter patrol come up the King's Road, and he did nothing at all.

He heard them take the boy at the gate. Heard Wren's voice — bright, indignant, profane in three dialects — and heard the chains, and heard Pell's unctuous assurances that it was all a formality, all routine, nothing personal, Master Corvane, nothing at all personal.

Through the shutter slats, Corvane watched the cart roll south with its small cargo of one, and he performed the arithmetic one last time. One boy, bought and paid for. Ten years preserved. Time secured.

Then he saw Dominic.

She was standing at the edge of the yard, ledger still tucked under her arm, watching the cart go. She wasn't looking at the hunters. She was looking at the boy — at the boy's face turned back toward the guildhouse, toward *her* — and the wind in the yard had gone perfectly, unnaturally still, the way air goes still before it decides to become something else.

And Corvane understood, with the sick lurch of a man reading a ledger line he should have caught months ago, that he had miscounted. That the boy knew things. That whatever passed between those two was bigger than a warning, bigger than a suspicion — and that he had just handed the crown the thread that, pulled gently, would unravel Dominic entirely.

He had sold time. But time, he now saw, was running in both directions.

Down in the yard, the stillness broke. A gust slammed the gate open hard enough to crack a hinge, and Dominic turned and walked back inside, spine straight, face white, and Corvane recognized the walk. He'd seen it once before, ten years ago, in a soaked girl in his doorway with ash in her hair.

It was the walk of someone done hiding.

Corvane closed the shutters and reached for the brandy, and found, for the first time in thirty years, that his hand shook.
