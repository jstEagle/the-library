## 6. Chains on the King's Road

Wren had been in a great many cages in his sixteen years, and he had developed a taxonomy of them.

There was the small cage — a barrel, a crate, the hold of a smuggling skiff — which required patience and flexible joints. There was the locked room, which required either a talent for locks or a friend who had one. And then there was this: an iron wagon on the King's Road, bars spaced too narrow for even his shoulders, bolted to a frame of oak that groaned like an old man climbing stairs, with six other prisoners inside it and four hunters riding alongside.

This cage, Wren decided, was a thinking man's cage. Which was fortunate, because thinking was the only tool they'd left him.

They had taken everything else. His knives, naturally. His boots, which stung more than he expected — a boy's feet remember every road they've ever run down, and now those roads were between him and cold iron. They'd taken the folded ledger page too, pried it out of his collar seam with fingers far gentler than their voices. That one still made his stomach turn over. Not because of the page itself, but because of what it meant: they knew he could read what the guild wrote. And people who know you can read their secrets tend to want to know *who else* can.

But they hadn't taken the knock.

* * *

The knock had come at dusk on the first night, when the caravan halted at a waystation and the hunters went to drink themselves brave. Three soft taps against the wagon's floor, from beneath. Then a pause. Then two more.

Wren's heart did something complicated in his chest.

Because that was *his* code. The code he'd taught exactly one person, three years ago, crouched behind the guild's coal sheds, teaching her to signal when the night clerk came round early. *Three means wait. Two means go.* He'd invented half of it himself and felt very clever about it, and she had looked at him with those storm-grey eyes and said, "You know this is just knocking, Wren," and he had said, "It's *coded* knocking, Dominic, there's a difference," and she had smiled the way she only ever smiled when she thought no one could see.

He knocked back. Two. *Go.*

Nothing happened, obviously, because she was out there in the dark somewhere and not, in fact, inside the wagon with him. But the answering silence felt different after that. Less empty. He put his back against the bars, closed his eyes, and let himself believe she'd heard him across forty miles of King's Road, because believing it was the only thing standing between him and being a boy in a box.

Then he got to work.

* * *

"Right," Wren said aloud, because the others had been staring at him since the waystation and silence was beginning to curdle. "I'm going to say something, and I'd ask you not to scream, faint, or inform on me until I've finished."

Six faces turned toward him. A woman with silver threaded through her braid — he'd marked her the first hour, the way her hands kept moving, weaving invisible thread through the air. A big man with burn-scarred forearms who sat like a wall that had learned to sit. Twin girls, maybe twelve, pressed together shoulder to shoulder, whose eyes glinted faintly green whenever they blinked — which, Wren noted, they were doing far less often than most people blinked. And a thin old fellow at the far corner who hadn't spoken, but who hummed, constantly, under his breath, tunes that didn't quite exist.

"I'm Wren," he said. "I'm a smuggler by trade, which sounds worse than it is, mostly because I'm bad at it. And I think we've all got the same problem, so I'll be quick before the guards get bored of their ale."

The silver-braided woman's hands stopped moving. "And what problem is that, boy?"

"We're not prisoners." He tapped the ledger entry he could still recite from memory, letter by letter, backwards if they liked. "We're inventory. There's a page in the guild's books that says what we cost per head, and what gets taken off us when we arrive, and I don't mean our boots." He watched the twins' hands find each other. Gently: "I read the ledgers. That's my whole crime, as far as I can tell — I read something I wasn't supposed to. What about the rest of you?"

Silence, the kind that has weight. Then the burn-scarred man said, quietly, "I warmed a miller's bread for twenty years. Never lit a fire in my life. They called it witchcraft when the ovens followed my mood." He flexed scarred fingers. "Tam. My name's Tam."

"Bess." The silver-braided woman lifted her chin. "My thread finds lost things. Or it used to. It's been quiet since they collared me." She touched the dull iron band at her throat, and all of them touched theirs, the little synchronized gesture of the collared, and Wren's chest tightened, because he'd spent years around magic without ever once understanding what losing it might feel like.

The humming man opened his eyes. "Pell," he said. "I sing things awake. Or asleep. Depends what's wanted." A pause. "Mostly asleep, lately."

"And you two?" Wren asked the twins.

The left one said, "Merrin." The right one said, "Sorrel." Then, together, perfectly unison, "Don't ask."

"Wasn't going to," said Wren. "Right. Merrin, Sorrel, Tam, Bess, Pell. Five gifted, one reader, one wagon, four hunters, and a coronation coming fast enough that whoever's buying us is in a hurry. Hurrying men make mistakes." He held up his knuckles. "So here's what I need from you."

* * *

What Wren needed from them, over the next two days, was rhythm.

The code spread down the wagon the way warmth spreads through a shared blanket: tap-tap-tap for *listen*, tap-tap for *now*, a long drag of a fingernail for *guard*, a double-rap for *count*. Tam counted the hunters' rotations — four riders, changing watch every sixth mile-marker, sloppy on the second watch, drunker each night. Bess counted the collars' weaknesses, pressing her thumb to each seam and listening with her whole body. Pell hummed, low, and told them which stones in the road sang back to him, and therefore where the road was hollow, and therefore where the ground might be persuaded to argue.

The twins, it turned out, could count anything instantly — birds, trees, the exact number of links in the chain between Tam's wrists — "Don't ask," they said again, in unison, and Wren decided he liked them enormously.

By the third dawn, Wren lay against the bars doing arithmetic that would have made his old fence mistress proud. Four hunters, tired. One hollow stretch of road near the ferry crossing, where Pell said the earth hummed back in a minor key. Six prisoners, five gifts, zero weapons, and one smuggler with a plan shaped roughly like a bad idea.

It was a terrible plan. It was also the only plan, and Wren had learned long ago that terrible plans executed loudly beat good plans never executed at all.

That was when the raven landed on the wagon roof.

* * *

Ravens follow caravans; carrion always does. But this raven landed twice, tapped its beak on the roof — three times, pause, two — and then sat there looking, Wren could have sworn, smug.

His breath stopped in his throat.

She wouldn't. She couldn't. Dominic Dravenmoor, the most careful person in the world, the woman who ironed creases into her own personality for a living, following a convict caravan across half the province disguised — badly, surely, *badly* — as a bird? No. Ravens weren't her gift. Her gift was thunder given hands. If she were here, the sky would—

The sky, at that moment, grumbled. Distantly. Over a horizon with not one cloud on it.

"*Oh, you absolute fool,*" Wren whispered, with a feeling that was equal parts fury, terror, and a warmth so fierce it nearly embarrassed him. "*You glorious, impossible fool.*"

He glanced at the hunters. None had noticed; the sky-grumble had rolled off them like weather, which it technically wasn't. He pressed his palm flat to the roof above him and knocked, slow and deliberate: *wait.*

The raven cocked its head. Tapped once — *no.*

Two days, then. She'd been shadowing them for two days while he'd been lying in the dark feeling sorry for his boots. He knocked again, faster, the old argument-code from the coal sheds: *go home, it's not safe, I mean it.*

One tap. Firm. *No.*

"You are the single most stubborn—"

"Wren." Bess's voice, quiet. "Who are you arguing with?"

"The sky," said Wren honestly. Bess exchanged a look with Tam, and Pell's humming changed key into something almost amused.

* * *

That night, camped at the ferry crossing, Wren pressed his face to the bars and watched the dark past the fires. Somewhere out there she was crouched in wet grass with the guild's ledgers strapped to her ribs, alone, with every hunter in the province hunting a storm-caller who had spent ten years learning to be invisible — and she'd thrown it away. For him. For a boy she'd fed soup to and taught card games and never once, in three years, let closer than arm's length.

He should have felt flattered. Mostly he felt afraid, and underneath the fear, something harder taking shape.

Because here was the thing Wren understood about Dominic Dravenmoor, better perhaps than she understood it herself: she would not leave. She'd tell herself she was waiting for a better moment, gathering information, being sensible — and she'd wait until the walls of whatever city swallowed them swallowed her too, because that was what ten years of hiding taught a person. Hiding became a country, and countries have borders you stop noticing.

Unless someone gave her a reason to cross.

Wren turned back to the wagon's shadows, to the five faces that had become, in three days, something dangerously close to *his*. He raised his knuckles to the floorboards, and knocked the new pattern they'd built together, the one that meant *tomorrow, at the water, all of us.*

Five sets of hands answered in the dark.

Outside, the raven ruffled its feathers, and far to the south, over the capital where a crown was being polished for a coronation, the clouds began, very quietly, to gather.
