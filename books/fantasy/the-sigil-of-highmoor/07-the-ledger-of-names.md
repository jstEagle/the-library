## 7. The Ledger of Names

The rain had followed her all the way from Highmoor, which Dominic chose to take personally.

It came down in thin gray sheets over the ruined millhouse where she'd made camp, drumming on the collapsed roof like a clerk tapping a quill — *hurry, hurry, hurry* — and she pulled the oilcloth tighter around the satchel of ledgers and told it, out loud, "I'm going as fast as I can."

Talking to weather was, of course, exactly the sort of thing that had gotten her family killed. She did it anyway. Ten years of silence earned a woman the right to grumble at rain.

Wren was due back before dark with bread, candles, and — if his grin at parting had meant what she feared it meant — news he absolutely should not have gone looking for. She'd sent him into Ashford for supplies, strictly supplies, and he'd kissed her knuckles with theatrical solemnity and said, "Yes, my lady storm-caller," which was his way of promising nothing.

So while the boy played spy, Dominic worked.

She'd built herself a little fortress of ledgers on the miller's old counting table: nine volumes, each fatter than a hymnal, each one a quiet horror bound in calfskin. She'd spent three days cross-referencing them by candlelight, building her map of the hunt — routes, quotas, delivery points. The guild's handiwork was meticulous. That was the worst of it. Somebody had sat at a desk very much like hers, with ink-stained fingers very much like hers, and turned human beings into columns.

*Batch 41, six units, condition: subdued. Sigil yield: satisfactory.*

*Units.* She'd stopped flinching at the word somewhere around batch thirty.

She was halfway through reconciling the Ashford deliveries against the caravan schedules when she found the discrepancy. It was small — the kind of thing only a person who'd spent a decade making numbers lie convincingly would ever notice. Every ledger recorded sigils delivered to the Office of Royal Works. Every ledger recorded payment received. But the payments didn't scale with the yields. Batch after batch, the crown paid for less than the guild harvested.

Which meant something was skimming the difference. And things that skimmed magic kept books of their own.

She went back to the beginning of the ninth volume and read it properly this time, not as an accountant but as a thief, looking past the neat entries to what lived between them. And there — tucked behind the final page's paste-down, a leaf so thin it might have been a watermark — was a folded insert, written in a cipher she recognized because she'd invented a cousin of it at fourteen, hiding her own name from her own memory.

It took her until well past midnight to crack it. Her hands were steady. Her heart was not.

* * *

When Wren came back, dripping and triumphant, swinging a paper-wrapped loaf like a trophy, he found her sitting in the dark with the insert spread open before her and no candle lit.

"You look," he said carefully, wringing rain out of his sleeve, "like you've been having a conversation with God, and God lost."

"Sit down, Wren."

"That bad?" He sat.

"The sigils aren't tribute." She kept her voice flat, clerk-flat, because if she let any feeling into it she would never get through the rest. "They're fuel. There's a ledger within the ledger — Corvane keeps it, or someone above him does. The harvest feeds something called the Vigilant. A war-construct. Old war. Before your grandmother's grandmother."

Wren frowned. "Constructs are stories. Brass giants to frighten children near the border."

"This one's real, and it's buried under the capital, and it has been sleeping for two hundred years because nobody could feed it." She tapped the page. "Sigils don't just hold magic, they *are* magic — identity given form. Enough of them, and something dead wakes up. The hunt isn't a hunt. It's a larder."

The rain filled the silence for a moment. Then Wren said, very quietly, "And the end date?"

"Coronation day." She heard her own voice crack on it, hated that, pressed on. "Three weeks. They mean to wake it as a coronation gift. A kingdom with a god of war under its feet."

Wren whistled low. Then, because he was Wren, he reached over, tore the loaf in half, and handed her the bigger piece. "Eat first. End-of-the-world business goes better with bread. My mother said that."

"Your mother sounds wise."

"She was a fence. Same thing, mostly." He chewed thoughtfully. "So we take this to the magistrates—"

"The magistrates report to the crown."

"—to the temples—"

"The temples blessed the hunt."

"—to literally anyone, then, because I'm hearing a lot of doors closing and I don't love it."

Dominic looked down at the insert, and at the nine ledgers stacked around it, and felt the true weight of her problem settle onto her chest like a second storm.

Because here was the thing she hadn't said yet. Here was the thing that had kept her sitting in the dark.

Every entry in every ledger carried a name. Not *units*, not *batch numbers* — beneath the guild's cold shorthand, in the cipher, someone had recorded who these people had been. Mira of Fenlow, whose gift was mending broken things. Aldous Parr, who could make a seed bloom in frost. The Vess children, ages seven and eleven, taken off the King's Road in spring.

Someone — some clerk, some predecessor wearing her own ink-stained sins — had refused to let them be numbers entirely.

"If we use these ledgers," she said slowly, "if we take them to anyone who can act — a rival lord, a foreign envoy, whoever will listen — the names become evidence. Evidence gets read aloud. In courts, in squares. These people get paraded as victims, their gifts catalogued, their families questioned. The crown will want to know who else in their bloodlines might burn bright enough to harvest."

"So we burn them," Wren said simply.

"And then there's no proof. No map of where the vaults are, which caravans run when, where the captives are held *right now*. Burn them and the Vigilant wakes on coronation day with no one the wiser, and every name in here dies twice — once in a cellar, once in smoke."

Wren set down his bread. For once, he didn't have a quip ready. The firelight — she'd finally lit the candle — caught the fine bones of his face, the smudge of soot he'd never fully wash out of his collar, smuggler's tattoo. Seventeen years old and already carrying more grief than most gravediggers.

"My sister's in one of those caravans," he said. "You know that."

"I know."

"If burning the book saves her, burn it. If keeping it saves her, keep it. But Dom—" He looked up, and his eyes were fierce and wet and utterly certain. "Whatever you choose, choose it fast, and then stop apologizing to them in your head. They're names. Names want to be *said*. Not worshipped. Said."

She almost laughed. Almost cried. "When did you become the wise one?"

"I always was. You were too busy being dramatic about rain to notice."

* * *

She worked through what remained of the night, and what she built was neither burning nor surrender.

She copied. By candle stub and memory, in the mirrored script only she and Wren could read, she transcribed the names — all four hundred and eleven of them — into a new book, plain and unadorned, a ledger of names and nothing else. No routes. No vault locations. No sigil yields. Just who they had been: their towns, their gifts, the small human details the original clerk had sometimes added in a shaky hand (*sang to the other prisoners; asked twice about her cat*).

Then she closed the original ledgers and bound them shut with cord and wax, and did not burn them, though her hands hovered over the candle longer than she liked to admit.

"Compromise," Wren said, watching her seal the last knot. "The coward's favorite meal."

"The survivor's," she corrected. "There's a difference. It's mostly sleep."

He grinned, but it faded quick. "Coronations need hostages made examples of. You know that, right? They'll sentence the ones they've caught publicly — assizes, the square, all very ceremonial. My sister's caravan is headed for the capital. If they're sentenced before coronation day—"

"Then the square is where we'll be," Dominic said, and heard the storm answer her voice, low under the floorboards of her chest, patient as thunder deciding whether to bother.

She stood, wrapped the ledger of names in oilcloth beside the sealed originals, and blew out the candle.

"Wren. When this is done — whatever happens to me — the names get read. All of them. In daylight. Even if I have to read them myself standing on the wreckage of the guild hall."

"That's the spirit." He shouldered his pack. "Also, terrifying. Very much both."

Outside, the rain had stopped at last, and the clouds over the eastern road — the road to the capital — were breaking apart around a thin, rising light.

Three weeks to coronation day.

Two, if the assizes came first.
