## 8. Storm over the Assizes

The assizes were held in the market square of Highmoor, because the crown wanted witnesses and Highmoor had never once refused to watch.

Dominic arrived before dawn, dressed as a guild courier with a satchel of forged delivery manifests against her hip and Wren's mirrored page pressed flat beneath her heart like a second, braver heartbeat. The square had been transformed overnight. Bunting in royal blue. A scaffold draped in cloth the color of a bruise. And at its center, arranged in two neat rows like merchandise awaiting appraisal, the cages.

Iron-barred, wagon-borne, each one holding three or four of the captured. She found them by their stillness — that particular hush of people who have learned that noise invites attention. In the third cage from the left sat a woman who could coax fire out of damp straw, hands folded in her lap as though she were simply waiting for a coach. Beside her, a boy no older than twelve whose gift was, so far as Dominic could tell, making flowers open early. The crown had chained him anyway. Chains, it seemed, were cheaper than discernment.

*Ten years,* Dominic thought, and her chest did the thing it always did — that low rolling pressure, like weather deciding whether to be born.

*Not yet.*

She took up a position near the well, where clerks clustered and nobody looked twice at ink-stained fingers, and she counted. Twelve cages. Forty-one captives. Sixteen hunters in crown scarlet, crossbows strung. Magister Hale of the Royal Assize, a small dry man with a voice like paper tearing, reviewing his sentences on a lectern. And at the edge of it all, standing apart in his good coat with his hands clasped behind him like a man attending a funeral he'd helped plan —

Corvane.

He had not looked at her since she'd slipped the ledger copy into his study and watched his face go gray as old tallow. He knew what she intended. Perhaps he even understood why. Understanding, she had learned, was not the same as forgiveness, and she was not here to be forgiven.

A hand touched her elbow. She nearly put the man through the well.

Wren stood beside her wearing the livery of a water-carrier's apprentice, which was perfect, because everyone ignored water-carriers the way they ignored rain — until it ruined something. His eyes were red-rimmed. He hadn't slept; neither had she. Between them they had spent the night distributing forty-one small folded slips of paper through the bars of eleven cages, each slip carrying instructions in mirrored script, because Wren had discovered that half the hunted could read backwards if you taught them, and teaching them had been *his* idea, delivered breathless at three in the morning:

"They read everything else we write about them. Let them read something *for* themselves."

"You're certain?" she'd asked.

"I'm certain they've been waiting ten years for someone to ask," he'd said, and she had loved him a little, in the fierce sideways way you love a person who is younger than you and braver than you and doesn't know enough to be afraid.

Now he murmured, "Hale starts at the top of the hour. Knock-code's set. On your signal."

"My signal is going to be very hard to miss."

"That's what I'm counting on." He almost smiled. Then, quieter: "Dom. When it starts — don't look for me. I'll be where I need to be."

"Where you need to be is *behind me.*"

"And stand in front of a storm-caller? No thank you. I've seen your ledgers. I know how you file things." He squeezed her wrist once — the clerk's grip, the smuggler's goodbye — and melted into the crowd.

Dominic breathed. In through the nose, out through the teeth, the discipline of a decade performed one final time. Her chest rolled again, and this time she let it roll, the way you let a wave carry you rather than fight it, and somewhere deep beneath her ribs the buried country of her childhood stirred and turned over and *woke.*

*Soon,* she told it. *Be patient. We've been patient long enough.*

* * *

Magister Hale mounted the scaffold at the stroke of nine and began to read names.

He read them the way the ledgers recorded them — stripped, numbered, filed. "Prisoner fourteen, irregular stock, gift unverified, sentence: transfer to the capital, custody of the Crown Office of Sigilic Affairs." The fire-woman stood when called. She did not tremble. Dominic marked her face and thought: *I will know your name again before sundown. Your real one.*

They went down the list. Prisoner fifteen. Prisoner sixteen. The flower-boy, prisoner nineteen, who stood on tiptoe to see over the cage rail and scanned the crowd — scanning, Dominic realized, *for someone*, and her heart cracked cleanly down the middle when she understood he was looking for a mother who would not come.

At prisoner twenty-three, Hale paused, licked his thumb, turned a page.

And Dominic stopped listening, because Corvane had moved.

He crossed the square with the unhurried stride of a guild master with every right to be there, mounted the scaffold steps, and leaned in close to Hale. She could not hear the words. But she saw Hale's expression change — the small dry man going smaller, drier, glancing toward the hunters, then toward the cages, then back at Corvane with the look of a man being handed something heavy and unpleasant. Corvane stepped back. Hale nodded. And a runner in crown scarlet peeled away from the hunters' line, moving fast, moving toward—

Toward the guildhouse.

Toward the vault.

"No," Dominic whispered. Corvane was buying time — spending the sigils, scattering the evidence, trading the last of his complicity for whatever mercy the crown still pretended to offer him. Or worse: warning them. Either way, the clock had just been wound tight around all their necks.

It didn't matter. It couldn't matter. Whatever happened in that vault would happen; what happened *here*, in the next hundred heartbeats, belonged to her.

Prisoner thirty. Prisoner thirty-one. The sun climbed. The crowd murmured and ate roasted chestnuts and looked away at the right moments, the way crowds do, and Dominic stood among them with a decade of silence rising in her throat like floodwater.

*Prisoner thirty-seven.*

The flower-boy stood up. And before the guards could touch him, he reached between the bars and opened, impossibly, a single white bloom out of the crack between cobblestones — just one small defiant flower growing where no seed had been planted — and the whole square went quiet, because it was beautiful, because beauty is the most alarming thing a caged person can produce.

The nearest hunter raised his crossbow.

Dominic's discipline ended.

* * *

Later, she would try to remember choosing it — the moment, the decision, the words. She never could. What she remembered was only the *release*: ten years of swallowed lightning leaving her body all at once, and her voice, her own voice, the one she had buried with her family name, tearing out of her across the square:

**"THEN LET THE SKY TESTIFY!"**

The clouds answered.

They came in off the moors like a cavalry charge — black, boiling, *fast*, swallowing the mild morning in three breaths. The temperature dropped. The bunting snapped. Every hunter in the square looked up, and every gifted prisoner in those cages felt it too — Dominic saw it pass through them like wind through wheat — the sudden electric knowledge that the sky had come *for them*, that they were not abandoned, that the world itself had finally picked a side.

Then the rain hit, warm and violent, and the first thunderclap knocked four crossbows from sixteen hands.

"HOLD THE PRISONERS!" Hale shrieked, paper tearing. "HOLD THEM—"

Dominic walked into the center of the square. Rain streamed off her; steam rose where her skin met the storm, because she was done suppressing, done hiding, done being a clerk-shaped hole in the world. Lightning crawled down her arms and pooled in her palms and she let it, *invited* it, and when she raised both hands the sky came down the rope of light she offered and struck the first cage with a sound like the world's spine breaking.

The lock fused. The bars sagged glowing orange. Inside, the prisoners flinched — then saw the gap, and *moved.*

Knock-code rattled through the remaining cages — Wren's pattern, three-and-two, three-and-two — and Dominic understood with a wild soaring joy that they weren't waiting to be rescued anymore. They were *escaping.* The fire-woman had already burned through her cage's rear axle; the boy with the early-flowers was doing something to the padlocks that made them wilt open like buds; and along the crowd's edge, water barrels burst in sequence — Wren, working the line, drowning torches and slicking cobblestones under charging hunters with the cheerful chaos of a boy who had spent his whole life reading the world backwards and finding it made better sense that way.

"THE GIFTED ARE NOT STOCK!" Dominic shouted, and thunder carried it to the rooftops. "THEY HAVE NAMES!"

She struck the second cage. The third. Each time she reached deeper, and each time the storm gave more gladly, as though it had spent ten years rehearsing for exactly this audience. Her hair had come loose and lifted around her head; her clerk's disguise was soaked to nothing; and somewhere in the roaring bright center of it she heard herself laughing — actually *laughing* — because it turned out that the thing she had feared most, the thing she had chained and starved and smothered for a decade, had only ever wanted to be *useful.*

The hunters regrouped at the square's north end, forming a line, leveling crossbows at her through the rain.

She was reaching for the fifth cage when the bolt came — fast, flat, aimed true at her heart.

It never landed. A shape slammed into her, took the bolt high in the shoulder, and went down with her into the flooded cobblestones: Wren, gasping, white-faced, an arrow-shaft jutting from the meat below his collarbone, grinning up at her through the deluge.

"Told you," he wheezed, "not to look for me."

"You *idiot*—"

"Forty-one names," he said. "You promised. Read them *out loud.*"

So she did. With the hunters closing and the cages breaking and the sky pouring down like judgment, Dominic Dravenmoor stood in the market square of Highmoor and did the one thing the guild had taught her never, ever to do.

She claimed the ledger.

"Not prisoners," she cried, and her voice was thunder now, was *hers*. "Not stock! *People!* Alenna Voss, who warms orphanages with her hands! Tam Merrilees, who makes gardens bloom! Every name in these cages has a name, and every name was written down in guild ledgers, and I—" her voice broke, and rebuilt itself, "—I *kept those ledgers.* I am Dominic Dravenmoor, daughter of the executed house, storm-caller, and I am *done hiding!*"

The square went silent except for rain. Forty-one faces turned toward her. Somewhere behind the hunters' line, she saw Corvane standing motionless in the downpour, watching his life's careful architecture drown, and she could not read his face at all.

The last cage broke. The last prisoners ran — north, east, into the maze of streets where Wren's smuggler friends waited with carts and cellars and routes out of the city. The fire-woman paused at the square's edge, looked back, and gave Dominic a single fierce nod: *we will remember this.*

Then a horn sounded from the direction of the guildhouse. Long, urgent, wrong.

Wren struggled upright against her shoulder, clutching his wound, following her gaze. "That's not the assize horn," he said. "That's the deep-vault alarm."

And beneath their feet — faint, distant, felt more than heard, a vibration rising through the cobblestones like something enormous turning over in its sleep — Highmoor began to *hum.*

Dominic looked at the empty cages, at the scattered sigil-ledgers darkening in the rain, at the north road where the runners had gone.

"It's not the vault," she said quietly. "Something's *waking up.*"
