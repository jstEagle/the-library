## 9. The Construct Wakes

The bells began at dawn, and they did not stop.

Dominic heard them from the rooftop of the chandlers' guild, where she had spent the night with the ledger of names strapped to her chest like a breastplate. Below, the capital dressed itself in bunting and lies. Banners of gold thread snapped from every balcony. Children waved paper crowns. Somewhere beneath the flagstones of the Coronation Square, fed on ten years of stolen sigils and still hungry, something old was turning over in its sleep.

"It's going to be a long day," Wren said beside her. He was dressed as a bell-ringer's apprentice, which mostly meant he had stolen a hat.

"You're supposed to be with the others at the east gate."

"I'm supposed to do a great many things. I find I do better when ignored."

She almost smiled. Almost. Her hands would not stop trembling — not with fear now, but with the storm itself, which had woken fully two nights ago when she broke the cages at the assizes and had refused, since then, to be quiet. It moved under her skin like a second pulse. Like a guest who intended to stay.

Below them, the ritual began.

* * *

Corvane stood in the square because he could not think of anywhere else to stand.

He wore his guild master's chain of office for the last time; he had left his resignation letter on his desk where the inspectors would find it by noon, weighted under a copy of every ledger Dominic had not burned. He had spent forty years building an institution, and he had spent one night deciding to bury it, and the strange thing — the thing he kept turning over like a coin that might prove counterfeit — was how light he felt.

The high priest raised the coronation crown. The king-elect knelt. And beneath the square, Corvane felt it: a hum through his boot soles, deep and patient, the sound a vault makes when it opens.

The escaped sigils — the ones Dominic's storm had shattered free — had scattered across the city like startled birds. He had assumed they were lost. He had been wrong. They were being *called*.

The flagstones split along seams no mason had ever cut.

The first thing out of the ground was a hand, if you could call it that — a fist of black iron and pale stone, big as a carriage, its knuckles set with sockets where crystals should sit. Half those sockets were dark. Half of them blazed with harvested light, sigils ripped from the hunted and never returned, still burning with the shape of the people they'd been taken from.

The crowd did not scream at first. Crowds never do; they wait for permission. The construct gave it to them by standing up through the coronation dais, shrugging off the king-elect like a coat, and turning its eyeless head toward the palace.

Then everyone screamed at once, and Corvane started running toward the thing instead of away from it, because apparently forty years of caution ended like this.

* * *

Wren whooped so loudly that three pigeons left the roof permanently.

"That's our fault," Dominic said flatly.

"That's *half* our fault," Wren corrected. "The other half is the crown's, and I intend to say so loudly and often." He was already counting — the way he counted everything, backwards, sideways, inside-out. "Half-fed. Look at its shoulders, Dom. The dark sockets. It's running on half a meal."

"So we starve it."

"Or," said Wren, with the particular cheerfulness he reserved for terrible ideas, "we fill it up so fast it chokes."

Dominic looked at her own hands. The storm pressed against them like water against a dam, and for the first time in ten years she understood what the dam had actually been for. Not protection. Storage.

"It eats magic," she said slowly. "That's what the harvest was. That's what the vault was. It doesn't destroy sigils — it drinks them."

"And if someone poured in more than it could swallow—"

"—it wouldn't choke. It would crack." She closed her eyes. She could see it already, the way she could see weather coming: the construct's lattice of stone and iron overcharged, seams splitting, the stolen light inside finally going home all at once. Every name in her ledger released together.

"And the someone doing the pouring?" Wren asked quietly.

She didn't answer. He didn't ask twice. That was one of the thousand small reasons she loved him, though she had said so exactly zero times, because she had spent ten years learning that everything you named could be taken.

"Dom." He took her shaking hands in his steady ones — the same gesture she'd once made to him, mirrored back, backwards boy reading her forwards. "There'll be another way."

"There won't. But go down anyway. Get the prisoners clear when it breaks. Someone has to read the names aloud after, and you're the only one who can do it in mirror-script, which will confuse absolutely everyone."

"That's my whole strategy, yes." His grin wobbled. Then, seriously: "I'll be at the east side. If you're not there by sundown, I'm telling everyone your real name is Bartholomew."

"Get out of here, Wren."

He went. She stayed, and let the dam open.

* * *

The storm came up through her like a confession.

She walked into the square through a crowd fleeing the other way, rain beginning around her in a circle that widened with every step, lightning threading the clouds overhead into a crown nobody had ordered. Guards saw her and hesitated — a clerk woman in ink-stained cuffs, walking calmly toward a colossus — and their hesitation bought her the thirty steps she needed.

The construct turned toward her. It *felt* her, the way a starving man feels bread. Its dark sockets flared with wanting, and Dominic felt the pull in answer, her storm leaning toward those empty places like a river toward the sea.

*Come and take it,* she thought. *But take all of it.*

She raised both hands, and gave it everything she had hoarded for ten years — every suppressed thunderhead, every swallowed flash, every night she had bitten down on lightning until her gums bled. It went out of her in a torrent, silver-blue and shrieking, and the construct drank greedily, sockets igniting one after another after another—

—and kept drinking, past fullness, past sense, past design. Cracks spidered up its legs. Light leaked from its seams like blood from a wound. It reached for her with that enormous hand, and she stood her ground inside her own weather, laughing, actually laughing, because it had never once occurred to her that the end of hiding would feel like this—

A blade came out of nowhere and took the construct's reaching arm at the elbow.

Corvane stood between her and it, guild sword drawn, guild chain still glinting at his throat, an aging man in a merchant's posture facing down a war-god's leftover toy.

"Guild master," she managed, "you're late."

"I've been early my whole life," Corvane said. "Thought I'd see how it felt." Behind him, six royal hunters in crimson arrived at a run, crossbows rising — not at the construct, which was visibly dying, but at her, at the storm-caller, the unlicensed gift, the crime walking.

"Stand down," Corvane told them, in the voice he used on inspectors. "This woman is under guild protection."

"She's a rogue talent—"

"She is," said Corvane, dropping the chain of office into the mud between them with deliberate ceremony, "*the Sigil of Highmoor*, and the last person alive who knows where every body from your harvest is buried, and I have just finished writing it all down. Arrest me instead. I insist. I've always wanted to be interesting."

The crossbows wavered. And behind them all, the construct screamed — a sound like a cathedral tearing — and broke.

* * *

It came apart from the inside out.

Light first: every stolen sigil bursting free at once, hundreds of small bright shapes spiraling up through the rain — moths of moonlight, sparks with names, each one burning briefly with the face or voice or signature of the person it had been torn from. Dominic felt them pass through her storm like birds through wind, and each one left a word behind, a name, and she caught every single one and held them, because someone had to.

Then the shell: iron and stone folding inward, collapsing into the pit it had climbed from, taking the coronation dais, the throne's empty shadow, and forty feet of royal pavement down into the dark.

When the dust cleared, the rain stopped falling upward and began falling normally, and Dominic Dravenmoor stood in a crater in the middle of the capital, empty-handed, empty-chested, and utterly, completely ordinary.

She felt the absence like an amputation. Ten years of held breath, finally exhaled — and nothing left inside her but a woman's heartbeat, slow and human and quiet.

Wren found her at the crater's edge. He didn't hug her. He just stood close and read her face, forwards, and whatever he saw there made him take off the ridiculous hat.

"Well," he said. "Bartholomew."

"Shut up, Wren."

Behind them, Corvane was surrendering himself to the hunters with the air of a man arranging a particularly satisfying business deal, and across the square, in the sudden hush, Wren opened the ledger of names and began to read aloud.

Dominic listened to the first few names, then turned east, where the road ran out of the city through fields just greening, and started to walk.

The rain followed her only as far as the gate. Then even the sky let her go.
