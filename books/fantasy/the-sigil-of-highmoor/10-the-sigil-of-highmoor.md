## 10. The Sigil of Highmoor

The construct's chest was a cathedral of hollow iron, and it wanted her storm the way a drowning man wants air.

Dominic stood on its outstretched palm, forty feet above the coronation square, lightning crawling down her arms like old friends come home. Below her, the city screamed and scattered. Above, the sky she had spent ten years refusing opened like a wound.

*Come,* the thing hummed, in a voice that was not a voice but a pressure behind the eyes, a hunger with grammar. It had eaten sigils for years — small ones, stolen ones, crystallized grief pried from the hunted — and every one of them had only sharpened its appetite. *You are the largest. Come.*

"No," Dominic said, conversationally, as though declining an offer of tea. "But I'll tell you what I'm going to do instead."

She pressed both hands flat against its breastplate, over the place where a heart would be if anyone had bothered to give it one, and let go.

Not all at once. That was the trick of it — the thing no ledger had ever taught her, that Corvane's ledgers could never have recorded. A storm is not a purse to be emptied. A storm is a river, and rivers do not pour; they *choose their course*. So she chose hers, and the ten years of swallowed thunder came up through her spine and out through her palms in a long, silver torrent, and the construct drank it, because it could not help itself, because drinking was the only thing it knew how to do.

And what it did not know — what none of them had known, not the crown, not the guild, not the clever men who had built a god out of stolen names — was that a storm given freely does not behave like a storm taken by force.

Taken magic sat in the construct's veins like stones. Given magic ran through it like weather.

"Thank you," Dominic whispered, as the light began to spread from the inside out, seams of white opening across the iron like dawn cracking an egg. "For holding my family's sigil. For keeping it somewhere I could find."

The last thing the Sigil of Highmoor heard, before it came apart into rain, was her laughing.

*

She woke on her back in three inches of muddy water, staring at a sky washed so clean it looked new-minted, and the first thing she felt was —

Nothing.

Not pain. Not emptiness, exactly. Just... quiet. The particular quiet of a house after a guest who has lived there a very long time finally leaves, taking their boots with them. She lay there and listened to the absence where the storm had been, and found, to her genuine surprise, that she was not grieving.

Wren's face appeared above hers, upside down, filthy, tear-streaked, and grinning like an idiot. "You're alive."

"Barely," she said. "Help me up before someone writes an elegy."

"You laughed." He hauled her upright, gripping her arm hard enough to bruise. "Corvane says you *laughed*. The whole square saw you standing on that thing laughing while it — " He stopped. Swallowed. "We thought it ate you."

"It did eat me," Dominic said. "That was rather the point. You can't poison a thing that insists on swallowing everything you give it." She flexed her fingers. They were just fingers now. Ink-stained, cracked at the knuckles, ordinary. She turned her hand over and watched rain bead on her palm and run off, and nothing in her answered it, and she thought: *oh*. And then, quieter, in the place where dread should have been: *oh good.*

"Dominic?" Wren's voice went careful. "Do you feel it? The storm?"

She considered lying. She had gotten quite good at lying; it had been her trade for a decade, right alongside bookkeeping. But Wren read mirrored script and backwards ledgers and people most of all, and he was looking at her hands the way she used to look at other people's hands, hunting for the tremor.

"Gone," she said. "Spent. All of it. Every last drop I hoarded for ten years went into that thing's gullet, and I don't feel so much as a rumble left." She smiled, and meant it, which startled her more than anything else that day. "I'm nobody now, Wren. Just a woman with terrible posture and excellent penmanship."

He stared at her. Then he did something she did not expect: he took her hand — the ordinary one, the empty one — and kissed the knuckles like she was something still worth bowing to.

"Best nobody I know," he said.

*

They rebuilt the square's dais into a reading platform, because Wren insisted, and because no one had the heart to argue with a boy who had organized forty prisoners with coded knocks through cage-bars while the guards played dice.

The ledger of names was heavy. Dominic carried it to the platform herself, though her arms shook, and set it down beside the lectern where Corvane stood — Corvane, who had arrived at the battle too late to save her and just early enough to put his body between her and the hunters' crossbows, and who bore two quarrel scars now and wore them like penance.

"The guild of Highmoor," he announced, in the voice he used for closing contracts, "is dissolved. Its assets are forfeit to the families listed herein. Its master—" his jaw worked— "its master submits to whatever judgment this city sees fit. I only ask to hear the names first."

So Dominic read them.

It took four hours. Her voice gave out twice and Wren took over, and then a one-eyed woman from the caravan whose gift had been sight across a hundred miles, and then a boy who could make candles gutter, whose contribution to the reading was enthusiasm rather than stamina. Name by name, sigil by sigil, the dead and the caged were spoken back into the world. Some of the named stood in the crowd and wept. Some had no one left to weep for them, and the crowd wept anyway, on principle.

When she reached the entry bearing her own erased family sigil — *Vasquez, line of Highmoor, stock: one (1), disposition: escaped* — she paused, looked up at several thousand strangers, and said clearly:

"My name is Dominic Vasquez. My family called storms. I am the last of them, and my storm is spent, and I am — " her breath caught, and she let it, "— I am glad to finally be introduced."

The silence lasted three heartbeats. Then the one-eyed woman began to clap, slowly, and the sound rolled through the square like the front edge of weather.

*

By evening the guildhouse doors had been thrown open and its cellars emptied, and the hunted — those who wished to — began to scatter along the roads like seeds off a shaken blanket. Some went north. Some went home to towns that might or might not remember why they'd left. The candle-boy asked Dominic to bless his journey, and she told him honestly that her blessings were probably worthless now, and he said that wasn't the point, and hugged her hard enough to crack a rib she was fairly sure had already been cracked.

Corvane found her at the guildhouse threshold, a single satchel packed at her feet.

"They'll want a trial," he said. "Mine. Possibly yours, though I suspect the crowd has ruled on that already."

"I'll testify to everything," she said. "Except the parts that would hang you. You get those redacted. Consider it professional courtesy between clerks."

His mouth twitched. "You always were a generous accountant." He looked at her for a long moment, this man who had sheltered her out of guilt and betrayed a boy out of fear and chosen, at the end, to stand in front of crossbows with no gift at all except stubbornness. "Where will you go?"

"I haven't the faintest idea." She said it the way other people said *I've inherited a fortune*. "Somewhere with weather. Somewhere where nobody's ever heard of Highmoor and nobody will ever again hand me a ledger and ask me to keep it balanced."

"Terrible plan," Corvane said. "No structure. No oversight." His voice roughened. "Good."

Wren was waiting at the east gate when she came down at dawn, a pack on his shoulder and the mirrored half-ledger tucked under his arm like a favorite novel.

"I'm coming with you," he said. Not asking.

"Wren." She stopped in the gateway, where the road unrolled ahead of them through wet green country, going wherever roads go when no one is chasing you down them. "There's no guild to rob anymore. No ledgers to decode. No hunt to dodge. I'm just a woman walking. There's nothing left to smuggle."

He grinned at her, that crooked grin that had started all of this, in a cellar, over a page written backwards.

"Sure there is," he said. "You just haven't written it yet."

And Dominic Vasquez — clerk, storm-caller, last of her line, first of her name to walk free under an open sky — laughed, adjusted her pack, and stepped onto the road, unhiding as she went, into whatever weather came next.
