## 1. The Ledger of Tonight

The accounts never balanced, and that was the point.

Edmund Kavanagh had learned this in his first week at the Guild of Impossible Things, when he'd tried to reconcile a ledger entry for *one miracle, minor* against the receipt for *three pounds of starlight, spoiled*, and found the numbers refused each other like oil and water. Ashka had watched him struggle for an hour before taking the quill from his hand.

"You're balancing them wrong," she'd said. "You keep trying to make the impossible behave. It won't. You balance around it, the way water balances around a stone."

Seven years later, he balanced around stones for a living. The guild's back office smelled of tallow and old vellum, and on ordinary evenings — evenings like this one, with rain ticking against the shutters and Wren's laughter drifting down from the kitchen where she was supposed to be asleep — Edmund could almost pretend the guild was something sensible. A charitable house. A strange family with strange bookkeeping.

He was halfway through the quarterly tally when the dead clerk started whispering.

Edmund set down his quill. He'd learned that trick early too: the dead spoke more readily to stillness than to motion, the way shy animals came out for quiet. The whisper came from the corner by the filing cabinets, where a man in guild livery stood half-faded into the plaster. Hollis, the records-keeper who'd died forty years ago and never quite stopped working. His ghost was gray as pencil lead and twice as fussy.

"Page eleven," Hollis said. "Eleven, eleven, eleven."

"Hollis. Good evening."

"It isn't." The ghost's hands wrung themselves. "Eleven. No one has read page eleven in a hundred years. I counted. I count everything now; it's all there is to do."

"The prophecy ledger?" Edmund asked, though he already knew. There was only one book in the guild's keeping that old, locked in the iron cabinet behind him, its key worn smooth by generations of guildmistresses who consulted it the way farmers consulted weather. "What about it?"

"Someone should read page eleven." Hollis drifted closer, and the candleflame bent away from him as it always did. "Before it's too late. Before tonight."

"Tonight's nearly done," Edmund said, glancing at the clock. Quarter past eleven. "Whatever's on the page can wait until morning."

The ghost looked at him with an expression Edmund had seen only on the dead and the recently bereaved — a pity so vast it seemed almost rude. "That's what the last one said. The archivist, the cowardly one. *It can wait until morning.* And then morning came, and he falsified the record instead, and a hundred years went by, and here we are." Hollis pressed a finger — or the memory of one — toward the iron cabinet. "Open it, bone-carver. Read it yourself. I'm not allowed to say more. The dead have rules too, worse ones than yours."

Edmund sat very still for a moment. Rain ticked. Above him, Wren's laughter had gone quiet at last.

Then he rose, took the key from its hook, and opened the cabinet.

* * *

The prophecy ledger was heavier than any book had a right to be, bound in hide no beast would claim and clasped with iron that bit cold even through his sleeves. He carried it to the desk, laid it flat, and turned to page eleven.

The paper was cream-white. That was the first wrong thing. Every other page had yellowed to the color of weak tea, but eleven looked freshly cut, as if the century hadn't dared to touch it. The ink was black and wet-looking, though no hand had written there in a hundred years.

At the top of the page, in a script Edmund recognized from the guild's oldest contracts — the hand of some founder long dust — was a date.

Tonight's date. Not the day, not the month. The full date, year included. Inked a hundred years ago.

Below it, two lines:

*A soul sold beneath Ironhollow shall be devoured at moonset.*

*Unless one comes who hears the dead.*

And beneath that, a name.

His name. *Edmund Kavanagh,* written in ink older than his grandfather, in a book sealed before his mother was born.

The candle guttered. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked — Wren, restless, or the house settling, or something else entirely. Edmund found that he was holding his breath and made himself let it go, slowly, the way he did when carving the fine work, the memorial pieces, the bones that had to hold a name without cracking.

He heard the dead. That was no secret; it was his trade and his curse both, the reason a struggling carver had been taken in by a guild of impossible things. But hearing the dead meant the line on the page fit him like a key fits a lock, and keys are only cut for doors someone means to open.

"Who is she?" he asked aloud. "The soul that was sold."

Hollis had drifted to the window. He didn't turn around. "You know her," the ghost said. "That's the worst of it. You know her, and you love her, and the court below has been waiting a hundred years for the bill to come due."

"Wren?" The name left his mouth before he could stop it, small and absurd in the big quiet room. Wren, who'd arrived at the guild a scrawny orphan of six and grown into the sharpest-tongued, quickest-laughing member of their strange household. Wren, who brought him tea while he worked and stole his good chisels and put them back duller. Wren, who was upstairs right now, alive and breathing and fifteen feet of floorboards away.

"She was sold before she was born," Hollis said. "Decades before. A debt inherited like furniture. The guild owed the court beneath Ironhollow, and the court collects in souls, and the contract named the next child raised under this roof." At last the ghost turned, and his gray face held something that might once have been shame. "I kept the records. I saw the entry. I said nothing. Dead men pay their debts in warnings — consider this mine paid in full."

Edmund's hands were steady on the ledger. He noted that distantly, the way he noted grain direction in a fresh piece of bone. Steady hands, cold blood, a name in a hundred-year-old book. The comfortable order of his evening lay cracked down the middle like a badly fired bell, and through the crack he could hear the mountain breathing.

"Moonset," he said. "When?"

"Half past midnight." Hollis was fading now, thinning into the plaster as the dead did when they'd spent themselves. "Read the rest of the page, Kavanagh. All of it. And then decide quickly what kind of man you are — the kind who balances accounts, or the kind who settles them."

The candleflame straightened. The corner by the filing cabinets was empty.

Edmund looked down at page eleven. Below his name, in smaller script, ran a final line he'd missed:

*The debt may be broken only at its signing place, by one willing to pay passage in the coin of himself.*

Coin of himself. He touched the edge of the page and felt — faintly, unmistakably — the paper drink warmth from his fingertip, the way bone drinks oil before it takes a carving.

Above him, the floorboard creaked again. Then silence.

He closed the ledger, and the clap of its covers sounded horribly final, like a door shutting on a cell. Half past midnight. Two hours to learn what the guild had sold, why Ashka's ledgers never balanced, and what it would cost to buy a soul back from the dark beneath the mountain.

Edmund blew out the candle, took up his coat, and went to find the Guildmistress.

She was awake, of course. She was standing at the top of the stairs, holding a lamp, and she'd been crying — a thing he had never once seen her do in seven years.

"I hoped you wouldn't find the page," Ashka said. "I hoped every year, on this night, for thirty years."

"What did we sell?" Edmund asked. "And don't tell me the accounts won't show it. I know they won't. That's how they balance."

Ashka looked at him for a long moment, lamplight trembling across her face. Then she stepped aside, opening the door of her study behind her.

"Come in," she said. "There are things this family did that you need to hear from me, tonight, before you hear them from the dead."

* * *

Later — much later, when the study fire had burned to embers and Edmund's hands had finally stopped being steady — he climbed the stairs to check on Wren. Habit, or something deeper than habit. She slept curled tight as a fern, one arm flung over her eyes, utterly unaware that somewhere below the mountain a lamp was being trimmed for her.

He stood in her doorway until the clock downstairs struck the quarter hour, and then he went down to the workshop, took out his finest chisel, and began, without quite deciding to, carving a key out of a piece of whalebone.

He didn't know yet what it opened. He only knew that somewhere below Ironhollow, a hundred years of debt was coming due at moonset — and that he intended to be there when it did.
