## 2. What the Guild Sold

The ledger did not let me sleep.

I tried. I banked the fire, I climbed the narrow stair to my room above the workshop, I lay in the dark listening to the guild settle around me the way it always did — timbers ticking, rain on slate, old Master Corvus's ghost muttering inventory numbers in the attic because even dead he could not stop counting. But every time I closed my eyes I saw the ink. *Edmund Kavanagh.* Tonight's date, a hundred years before tonight. And beneath it, in that same patient antique hand: *the debt comes due.*

So at some black hour past midnight I lit a lamp again and went looking for Ashka.

Guildmistress Ashka Voss kept her own quarters at the top of the east tower, which was less a privilege than a sentence — she said so herself, often, with a laugh that dared you to disagree. Her door was whalebone and iron, older than the tower around it, carved with running figures whose faces had been worn smooth by centuries of hands knocking. I knocked.

"Enter," she called, "before you wake the whole house."

She was awake, of course. Ashka was always awake at strange hours; I'd told myself for eleven years it was insomnia, or devotion, or the ordinary burdens of running an institution devoted to impossible things. She sat by her window with tea going cold and a book open facedown on her knee, silver hair loose, looking — for once — exactly as old as she was.

"You've read it," she said. Not a question.

I set the prophecy ledger on her table between us. My hands were steady. I remember being proud of that later, when nothing else about me was.

"Page one hundred and twelve," I said. "My name. Tonight's date. Written by a hand that's been dust for ninety years." I pulled the other chair out and sat, because my legs had opinions about standing. "And one more line. *The soul sold to the court beneath shall be devoured at moonset unless someone steals it back.*"

Ashka looked at the ledger the way you look at an old dog that has done something unforgivable but is too old to punish. Then she looked at me, and what was in her face frightened me more than the page had. Not surprise. Recognition. The face of someone who has been waiting decades for a knock at this door.

"Whose soul?" I asked. Though some part of me already knew. Some part of me had known since I read the line, the way you know a bone is broken before the pain arrives.

Ashka poured herself cold tea anyway, for something to do with her hands. "You should understand how debts work," she said, "in houses like this one. Impossible things don't come free, Edmund. They never have. Every wonder the guild has ever performed was purchased. And purchases must be paid for."

"I asked you a question."

"And I'm deciding how much truth you can carry without dropping it." She drank. Set the cup down. Her hands, I noticed, were not steady at all. "The court beneath Ironhollow keeps accounts, same as we do. Older ones. Crueler ones. A century ago, this guild owed them — owed them catastrophically, for workings I will not describe to you tonight because they'd curdle your tea worse than it's already curdled. And the court does not accept coin. Coin means nothing to things that live under a mountain. They accept souls. Or they accept promises of souls, which accrue interest."

The lamp flame leaned in a draft that wasn't there. In the corner of the room, faint as breath on glass, I felt the dead stir — they do that around secrets, like moths around a lamp — but none spoke. Even the dead, apparently, knew when to be quiet in this room.

"A promise of a soul," I repeated.

"The contract named no one," Ashka said. "That was the mercy of it, if you can call it that. It bound *the guild* — its heirs and assigns, its children unborn. A debt against the house itself." Her eyes came up to mine, grey and level. "Wren was born into this house. Raised under this roof. Fed at this table. By the terms of a contract signed forty years before her mother met her father, she belongs to the court beneath Ironhollow. And tonight, at moonset, the court collects."

The room went very far away. I heard myself breathing. I heard the rain, the timbers, the silence of the dead who would not speak. Eleven years I had eaten beside that girl. Taught her to carve. Watched her fall asleep over her practice pieces and carried her to bed like a sack of flour, twice her size and half her weight in stubbornness. And all that time —

"All that time," I said, and my voice came out wrong, scraped, "she was *collateral.*"

"She was family," Ashka said, sharp as a chisel slipping. "Don't you dare reduce her to a line item. Everything this house is — the warm beds, the full bellies, the freedom to chase impossible things — exists because someone, a hundred years ago, signed away something they prayed would never come due. Every orphan we sheltered after that was bought with that bargain. Including you, Edmund. You think the guild found you starving in Ironhollow town by accident? We keep watch for children the ledger might touch. We took you in because you were *already written in it.*"

That landed harder than anything else she'd said. I sat with it. Betrayal, it turns out, is not one wound. It's a dozen small ones arriving in sequence, each with its own specific shape — the lie, the concealment, the arithmetic underneath everything, and worst of all the discovery that the arithmetic had been done *for* you, long ago, by people who loved you, or said they did.

"Who signed?" I asked.

"Guildmaster Ellery Vane." Something flickered across her face at the name. "He died believing he'd bought his successors time enough to find another way. He was wrong, or he lied, or both. His records were archived. Sealed."

"Sealed," I said. "Or falsified?"

The silence lasted three heartbeats. Long enough.

"Tobias," I said. Tobias Vane, our archivist, great-grandson of the signer himself, keeper of the sealed stacks, the man who blushed whenever Wren teased him and catalogued impossibilities with trembling reverence. "Tobias has been sitting on this for years, hasn't he. Keeping the archive quiet. Keeping *me* quiet."

Ashka rose and stood at the window, back to me, a silhouette against the rain. "Tobias did what I told him to do," she said. "When I inherited this office, the first thing I read was the contract. Do you know what I did then, Edmund? Nothing. I raised her. I fed her. I taught her letters and carving and how to swear properly. I gave her fourteen years of a life, and I told myself that when the bill came due, I would find a way to pay it differently. That's not treachery. That's love, doing arithmetic."

"It's trafficking," I said quietly. "With extra steps."

Her shoulders flinched. She didn't turn around.

"There's a way," she said at last. "There's always a way, that's the whole obscene point of this guild. Contracts can be broken where they were made. Debts can be stolen back from creditors. The court beneath has gates, and gates take payment, and payment—" She stopped. Turned. Looked at me with an expression I couldn't read then and understand completely now.

"Payment in what?"

"You're a bone-carver who hears the dead," Ashka said. "You tell me what a thing like that would want from you."

Downstairs, somewhere below the floors and the foundations, beneath the mountain's roots, a clock I could not see began counting toward moonset. And in the wall behind Ashka's window — I swear this, I swear it now as I swore it then — something knocked. Three times. Patient. From the inside.

I turned toward the sound, and Ashka followed my gaze to the old whalebone door set into her tower wall, sealed with iron since before either of us was born, and her face went white as bone.

"That door," she whispered, "has been silent for sixty years."

Behind the whalebone, something royal cleared its throat, and asked, in a voice like frost forming:

"*Which one of you is going down?*"

* * *

I did not answer right away. You don't rush answers to doors like that.

Ashka moved first — of course she did, she'd spent fourteen years practicing courage in secret and it showed in how fast she put herself between me and the whalebone. "Seraphine," she breathed. "No. No, she's supposed to be dormant. Tobias renewed the seals in spring—"

"You know what's behind there," I said. It wasn't a question anymore either. It seemed I'd spent my whole life asking questions I already knew the answers to, and tonight the ledger had simply torn the last page off the habit.

"I know what's behind *everything* in this house," Ashka said, and there was exhaustion in it now, the deep kind, the kind that comes from carrying one secret too many up too many stairs. "A queen who wouldn't kneel to the Bone King. They took her throne and her name and her death, and what was left over we sealed in whalebone because she refused to leave quietly. She's been behind that door since before my grandmother held this office. And she hates the court below with a hatred so pure it could cut glass."

The knocking came again. Three times. Then, dryly, almost amused:

"*I can hear you describing me, little guildmistress. Flattery will get your orphan nowhere.*"

Every hair on my arms stood up. Ashka closed her eyes as though praying for patience from a god she no longer believed in.

"Edmund," she said carefully. "Whatever you are planning—"

"I'm planning to go down there," I said. "Tonight. Before moonset. With or without your blessing, with or without a map, with or without a ghost who remembers being royal." I picked the prophecy ledger up off her table and held it against my chest like a shield made of paper. "You did your arithmetic, Ashka. Fourteen years of love against one signature. Here's mine: I'm not letting this house sell that girl twice."

For a long moment the only sounds were the rain and the slow tick of the tower and, beneath us all, faint and patient, the mountain counting.

Then Ashka crossed the room, took the iron key from around her own neck — she wore it always, and I had always assumed sentiment — and held it out to me on her open palm.

"If you wake her," she said, "you cannot put her back to sleep. And she will name a price. Ghosts always do. Whatever she asks — *whatever* she asks — decide beforehand what you won't pay, because once you start bargaining with the dead, 'no' stops being a word you know how to say."

I took the key. It was colder than iron has any right to be.

Behind the whalebone door, Seraphine laughed — soft, delighted, hungry — as if she'd been listening to every word.

"*Come in,*" she said. "*I've been dying to meet the man from the ledger.*"
