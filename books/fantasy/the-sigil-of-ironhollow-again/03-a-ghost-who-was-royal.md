## 3. A Ghost Who Was Royal

The whalebone door stood at the end of the guild's deepest cellar, behind a rack of pickled impossibilities and a shelf of jars labeled only in Ashka's handwriting: *do not open, do not shake, do not apologize to*.

Edmund had walked past it for eleven years. Every carver did. It was part of the architecture of the place, like the crooked stairs and the smell of lye — a door made of curved white ribs lashed together with iron bands gone orange with rust, taller than any man, set into a wall of raw stone where no room should be. Children dared each other to touch it. Old Marta claimed she'd once seen it breathe.

Tonight Edmund put his palm flat against it, because his life had already cracked open like bad ivory, and what was one more fracture.

He'd spent the hours since leaving Ashka's study not thinking. That took work. He'd sharpened every knife in the shop. He'd re-carved a knucklebone dice set that didn't need carving. He'd checked on Wren twice — once through the door of the dormitory where she slept tangled in blankets like a nest of her own making, once just standing in the corridor listening to her breathe, memorizing without meaning to the small wet snore she'd deny to her grave.

Then he'd come down here, because there was one name left in the ledger that might help him, and it wasn't written in ink.

*Tobias Vane*, Ashka had said, when he'd asked who else knew about the sale. *Tobias kept the records.* But Tobias was tomorrow's problem, or an hour from now's. Tonight, while the guild slept and Wren's soul sat in a cage beneath a mountain he'd never seen, Edmund needed something else. He needed a guide. And there was exactly one being in this building older than the guild itself, sealed behind whalebone by people who had clearly been very afraid of it.

The bone was cold under his hand. Colder than stone had any right to be, cold like deep water, like the inside of a thing that had once swum and now endured.

"I know you can hear me," he said. "You're a door. Hearing is mostly what doors do."

Nothing. The cellar lamp guttered in a draft that came from nowhere.

"The Guild of Impossible Things," Edmund went on, keeping his voice level, conversational, the voice he used to soothe frightened clients when he told them what their dead mothers wanted said at the funeral. "We keep accounts that don't balance, prophecies nobody reads, and souls we apparently sell. I found my name in a book tonight. Yours isn't in any book at all, which tells me someone went to a great deal of trouble to erase you. I'd like to know why. And I'd like your help."

Silence. But it was a different silence now — attentive, weighted, the silence of a held breath.

"I'm going down into Ironhollow," he said. "Under it. To the court. I'm told the dead down there remember things the living prefer forgotten." He paused. "I'm also told you were royal."

The temperature dropped.

Not gradually — all at once, as if winter had been waiting just behind the wall for someone to say the right word, and here it came through the seams of the door like breath through teeth. Frost bloomed across the whalebone ribs in feathered whorls. The lamp flame shrank to a blue bead. Edmund's exhaled fogged.

"Who," said the door, "speaks of royalty?"

The voice was a woman's, low and precise, with an edge on it like a blade that had been sharpened on grief. It seemed to come from everywhere the cold touched.

"A dead clerk," Edmund said honestly. "And the dust on a prophecy ledger. And the fact that a door doesn't get built out of whalebone unless someone wants to keep something very large and very angry on the wrong side of it."

A long pause. Then, dryly: "You are either brave or simple."

"Carvers learn there's not much difference."

Another pause — shorter, assessing. When the voice came again it had shifted, the way a person shifts when they decide to stop performing. "Eleven years you have walked past me. You and your knives and your little guild of hoarders. Why tonight?"

Edmund looked at the frost-feathers on the bone. "Because tonight I learned that the family I love sold a child's soul before she was born. Because tomorrow night at moonset, that soul gets devoured. And because everyone I might ask for help is either complicit, grieving, or eight years old." He spread his hands. "So I'm asking a door."

The cold receded slightly — not warmth, nothing so kind, but a kind of interest.

"You want passage through the court below," Seraphine said — and he realized he hadn't given her the name, that it had simply arrived in his head along with the cold, the way a title arrives with a crown. "You want to steal from the Bone King. You are a bone-carver with a lamp and no plan."

"I have a ghost."

"You have a rumor wearing a ghost's confidence." Something moved behind the ribs — a darkness shifting, vast and slow, like a whale turning in deep water. "Let me tell you what I am, carver, since honesty is apparently the currency of this conversation. I was queen of that court, before it ate its own heart and called the eating wisdom. I ruled the dark under Ironhollow when it still had laws. Then my court decided a crown was safer empty, and they unmade me — not killed, unmade, which is worse. They pulled my name out of every record, my face out of every memory, and nailed what was left of me into this door so that even forgetting me would require effort." A sound that might have been laughter, if laughter could freeze. "They succeeded. Do you know my name? Truly?"

"No," Edmund admitted.

"No one does. That was the sentence." The frost crept closer to his hand; he didn't withdraw it. "And now you stand there wanting my help to walk into the mouths of the creatures who did that to me, carrying nothing but sentiment and a trade skill."

"That's most of what anyone carries."

This time the laugh came easier, sharp and almost warm. "You are either brave or simple," she said again, "and I am beginning to fear you are both."

* * *

They negotiated for an hour, though it hardly felt like bargaining. It felt like two people circling a fire, each checking the other's shadow.

She would guide him. She knew the court — its gates, its tolls, its appetites. She knew how the bone gates demanded payment, and what form the payment took, and which memories were cheap and which were ruinous. She knew the Bone King's habits, his hungers, the schedule of his table. She knew, she said, where the King kept the things he loved best: caged, lit, and close enough to gloat over.

"But hear the shape of the bargain," she said, and the whole door leaned toward him, ribs creaking like a ship's hull. "I will lead you. I will spend my hatred freely — it has kept me fed for three hundred years and it keeps well. In return, you owe me a price. Not named now. Named later, by me, when I choose. Whatever I ask, you pay. That is the term. Refuse it and knock on some other door."

Edmund was quiet for a long moment. He thought of Ashka's face when she'd said *we brokered it*, the way guilt had sat on her like a coat too heavy to remove. He thought of the ledger's neat entry in his own handwriting-shaped future. He thought of Wren, asleep upstairs, breathing.

"That's not a bargain," he said finally. "That's a hook with no bait visible."

"Yes," Seraphine agreed pleasantly. "It is. Most bargains are. At least mine is honest about its teeth."

"What would you ask? Honestly. If you asked right now."

Silence again — but this silence was hers alone, private, and when she spoke her voice had lost its blade-edge and become simply tired, ancient, human in its exhaustion. "Right now? Tonight? I would ask you to sit on the other side of this door and talk to me until dawn, about anything, everything, the price of candles and the smell of rain and whether children still sing in the streets above. Because I have not heard a living voice speak kindly in three centuries, carver, and I find myself starving." The cold pulsed once, embarrassed. "Which is precisely why I will not ask that. Need makes poor currency. So: a price unnamed, payable in full, and you may trust me exactly as far as that honesty carries you."

Edmund rested his forehead briefly against the whalebone. It was strange — the bone felt less like a barrier now and more like a brow pressed to his own.

"I'll take it," he said. "One condition of mine, then, to balance yours. Whatever you ask of me — whatever the price turns out to be — it comes out of me alone. Not Wren. Not the guild. Not anyone sleeping upstairs. Me."

A long stillness. Then, softly, with something that was almost wonder: "You would shield strangers from a debt you haven't measured."

"They're not strangers. That's rather the point."

"Hm." The frost withdrew from around his hand, folding back like a curtain. "Agreed. Now listen, because the descent is not kind and I will say this once. The first gate takes a small memory — a taste, a color, something you won't miss till later. The second takes larger. They always take larger. By the last gate you will be paying in things you built your self out of, and you will smile while you sign, because that is how the gates are carved." A pause. "Bring a man who owes the guild a confession, if such a man exists. Guilt opens doors below that courage cannot."

"Tobias Vane," Edmund murmured. The archivist. The falsifier. Tomorrow's problem becoming tonight's.

"If he weeps easily, leave him topside. Weeping attracts the wrong kind of attention down there."

"And if he doesn't?"

"Then he's lying, and liars make excellent guides." The ribs groaned; the darkness behind them turned over like something settling back to sleep. "Go, carver. Wake your archivist. Come back at moonrise with your lamp lit and your debts arranged. Knock three times and say my name — the name you do not know, the one that tastes of winter and drowned bells. Your mouth will find it. Names always do, when they're owed."

Edmund stepped back from the door. At the foot of the stairs he stopped, unable to help himself.

"Seraphine," he said — testing it, giving it back to her, the first syllables of her name spoken aloud in three hundred years.

The cold flared — startled, hungry, grateful — and then, quietly, in a voice that had forgotten how to hope and was trying anyway:

"Until moonrise."

He climbed toward the light, toward the sleeping guild, toward the room where Tobias Vane kept his falsified ledgers and his unslept nights. Behind him, in the deep cellar, a door made of whalebone held its breath and began, for the first time in centuries, to count the hours.
