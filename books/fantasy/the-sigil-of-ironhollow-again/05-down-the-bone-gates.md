## 5. Down the Bone Gates

The entrance to Ironhollow was not a cave mouth. It was a mouth, full stop — a fissure in the mountainside shaped like a lower jaw, teeth of granite worn smooth by centuries of things going in and nothing coming out.

Tobias stood at the threshold with his ledger clutched to his chest like a breastplate. "There's still time," he said. "We could petition the Guild. File a formal objection. There are procedures—"

"The procedures sold her," Edmund said. He stepped through the teeth. The dark inside smelled of cold stone and old marrow. "Come on."

Seraphine went last. She had taken a body for the journey — the ghost had ways of pressing herself into borrowed flesh when she wished to walk among the living, and she'd chosen a shape that was beautiful the way frost on a window is beautiful: precise, transient, faintly cruel. She looked up at the jaw of the mountain and smiled.

"I remember this door," she said. "It used to sing when it opened. They've let it go quiet." Her voice carried the particular grief of someone revisiting a house that has burned down. "Sentimental kings. It's always sentimental kings who ruin everything."

The passage descended in long spirals, cut by no human hand. After an hour Tobias's lantern began to burn green instead of yellow, and after two hours the walls changed. Stone gave way to bone — not scattered bones, not arranged bones, but bone as architecture, femurs load-bearing, ribs arched into vaulting, skulls set into the mortar like stones in a wall, their eye sockets facing inward, watching them descend.

"Whose?" Edmund asked quietly.

"Everyone's," Seraphine said. "The court doesn't bury its dead. Why would it? Dead men make excellent roads."

* * *

The first gate stood where the spiral narrowed, and it was not a gate so much as a wall that had decided, temporarily, to be elsewhere. Fused skeletons — dozens of them, melted together at the joints into a single lattice — hung across the passage like a curtain of knucklebones. As they approached, the lattice stirred. Every skull turned toward them at once, a sound like rain beginning.

"Payment," said the gate. It had one voice made of many, layered and dry. "Passage is bought. Passage is always bought. Nothing descends for free; nothing ascends for free. What do you carry?"

"Memories," Seraphine said, before Edmund could speak. She glanced at him sideways. "That was going to be my warning, bone-carver, and you were about to learn it the expensive way. The gates don't take coin. Coin is worthless down here. They take what you remember — and once they take it, you don't get it back. You won't even know what's missing. You'll just be lighter, and poorer, and glad of it."

"Glad of it," Tobias repeated, horrified.

"That's the cruelty of it. The court takes your worst memories too, if it likes them better. But mostly it takes the good ones. Power down here runs on love. Love is fuel. The court burns it."

The gate's skulls leaned closer, patient as tide.

Edmund thought of Wren — nine years old, standing in the guild kitchen with flour on her nose, asking him to carve her a bird that could fly if she believed hard enough. He thought of the prophecy ledger with his own name inked beside tonight's date. He thought: *I am walking down into the earth to steal back a soul, and I have not yet lost anything. That will not last.*

"What do you want?" he asked the gate.

The skulls considered him. "Something warm," the many-voice said. "Something held often. A face, perhaps. Faces keep well."

He knew which face. He had known since the gate spoke.

His mother had died when he was eleven, but he still had her — every morning of her, really. The particular way she squinted when she laughed. The scar above her eyebrow from a fall off a hay cart. The way she hummed while kneading bread, off-key, unbothered by it. Her face was the first thing he saw when he closed his eyes at night and the last thing he saw when he opened them in the morning, and he had built his whole quiet life on the foundation of it without ever noticing he was building.

"If I give it," he said, "I won't grieve her anymore? I'll forget she existed?"

"You'll know a woman bore you," the gate said. "You'll know she died. You won't know her face. You won't hear her humming. You will feel the shape of the loss without the content, forever, like a coat with nothing inside it."

"And I'll pass?"

"You'll pass."

Seraphine said nothing. That was the worst part — her silence, letting him choose without the mercy of being talked out of it.

Edmund closed his eyes. He looked at his mother's face one more time, deliberately, the way you look at a room before leaving a house you'll never return to. The squint. The scar. The humming.

"Take it," he said.

It did not hurt. That felt like a betrayal all its own. Something passed through him — cold, thorough, polite, like a customs officer patting down a traveler — and then it was gone. He opened his eyes and tried to find her and found only a warm blankness where she should have been, a door painted onto a wall. He remembered loving someone. He could not remember whom, or how, or why the memory of loving hurt worse than any memory of anything else.

"Well?" His voice came out flat and strange. "Did I pay enough?"

The gate dissolved. Skeletons drifted apart and pressed themselves flat against the walls, bowing, making a corridor of themselves.

"First gate paid," Seraphine said softly. "Six more. The price rises as you go deeper. That one took something old. The next ones will want something living."

* * *

They walked. Tobias kept trying to talk — about guild history, about the architecture of the descent, about anything — because silence made the missing pieces audible. Edmund let him talk. It was easier than thinking about the coat-shaped absence where his mother had been.

The second gate was smaller, and older, and it spoke before they reached it.

"Warm thing," it said. "Held often. Held *recently*. Give."

And here was the problem: Edmund had spent the whole descent trying not to think about the wedding day that never was.

It hadn't been much of a daydream, as such things went. A girl named Maren who worked the dye-works in Ironhollow town, who laughed at his bad jokes and had once fallen asleep against his shoulder during a guild recital. He'd never spoken to her of marriage. He'd never worked up the courage. But he had built the day anyway, over four years, brick by brick, in the workshop's quiet hours: the small ceremony behind the guildhall, Ashka officiating badly, Wren scattering petals and getting most of them in Tobias's hair. Maren in blue, because she'd said once she hated white. Himself, nervous, happy. A future, ordinary and warm, that he had visited so often it had worn a path in him like a stair.

The gate wanted that. He could feel it wanting — the way a dog watches a hand near food.

"Not the mother-face again," the gate said. "Spent. Spent. Give fresh."

"There's a girl," Tobias started, and stopped, because he understood at the same moment Edmund did what Edmund was reaching for, and there are some understandings that are unbearable to say aloud.

"Don't," Edmund said. Not unkindly.

"You could refuse," Seraphine said. "Turn back. Find another road."

"There is no other road."

"No," she agreed. "There never is, below. The court builds the roads that way on purpose. Every path to it is paved with what you love. It's how the court stays fed." She studied him with those frost-precise eyes. "You should know that the King learned that trick from me. Or I from him. It hardly matters anymore who sinned first."

Edmund stood before the second gate and held the wedding day in his mind one final time. Maren turning toward him in blue. Wren's petals in Tobias's hair. Ashka fumbling the words. The whole impossible, ordinary happiness of it, worn smooth by four years of visiting.

He had thought giving up his mother's face would be the hardest thing he ever did. He had been wrong, because his mother had actually lived, and grief at least had the decency to be finished. This was a day that had never happened, being taken away, and it somehow left a bigger wound — proof that the court didn't just eat your past. It ate your futures too. It ate the man you might have been, and left you standing in the wreckage of a life you now couldn't even mourn properly, because mourning requires remembering what you lost.

"Take it," he said.

This time it lingered. This time the cold thing moving through him seemed to *savor*, and he understood that the gate wasn't a gate at all, not really. It was a mouth. All of it — the fused skeletons, the watching skulls, the court waiting below — was one organism, and it was eating its way down toward him course by course, and everything it took made the next taking easier, because he was becoming a man with fewer reasons to stop walking.

When it finished, he reached for Maren's name and found it gone. Reached for the color blue and found it just a color. Reached forward, into tomorrow, and found no one waiting there.

He swayed. Tobias caught his arm.

"How many memories does a man have?" Edmund asked the dark. His voice sounded like someone else's.

"Fewer than you think," Seraphine said. "Far fewer than you think. Which is why we must hurry." She pointed down the corridor of bowing bones, where a third gate glimmered ahead, taller than the others, its skulls gold-capped and hungry. "Because I have seen what the deep gates take, bone-carver, and I need you to understand something before we reach them."

"What?"

"The third gate doesn't want your past or your future." Her voice dropped low, and for the first time since the whalebone door, the forgotten queen sounded afraid. "The deep gates want your *present*. And I have watched men pay that price and walk out the other side as strangers wearing their own faces — hollow men the court uses, afterward, as doors."

Ahead of them, the gold-capped skulls turned in unison, and the third gate began, softly, to hum.
