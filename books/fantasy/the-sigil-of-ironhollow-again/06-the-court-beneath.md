## 6. The Court Beneath

I had forgotten how the court smelled.

Not rot — rot would have been honest. It smelled of lilies steeped in cold fat, of candlewax and old blood gone sweet, a perfume engineered by something that had once been alive and had never forgiven itself for it. The moment the third bone gate sealed behind us, the scent wrapped around me like a shawl I had burned a hundred years ago and found waiting still, folded and warm.

"Smile," I murmured to Edmund.

He did not smile. He was looking at the corridor with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he already dreaded — counting gates, counting payments, tallying what remained of himself behind his eyes. He had paid twice on the way down. I had watched him hand over his mother's face through the first gate, watched the memory lift out of him like steam off tea, and he had not flinched until it was gone, and then he had flinched at nothing at all, because there was nothing left to flinch *at*. That was how they took things here. You didn't lose what you loved. You lost the knowing that you'd loved it.

Tobias Vane clutched his satchel of falsified ledgers to his chest like an infant. "The court will see through us," he whispered. "They see through everything."

"They see through fear," I said. "Wear something else."

And then the doors opened, because down here the doors always know when you've finished being afraid enough to be interesting.

* * *

The throne room of the Bone King is not a room. It is a throat.

Ceilings of rib and vaults of knucklebone arched away into dark, and chandeliers hung from them — actual chandeliers, crystal and gold, absurd as jewelry on a corpse. That was the court's whole grammar: beauty nailed onto bone. Courtiers drifted across the floor in gowns of moth-wing and suits stitched from spider-silk, and every one of them was lovely, and every one of them was wrong. Their beauty sat on them the way paint sits on wood. Underneath, if you looked — and I looked; I had spent a century learning to look — underneath there were shapes that had agreed to be beautiful so they could keep existing.

They remembered me before they remembered themselves. I felt it move through the crowd like wind through wheat: a ripple, a turning, whispers folding around my name — or the name I'd worn when I wore flesh. *Seraphine.* Some bowed too deeply. Some drew back too quickly. A woman in a dress of frozen breath pressed her fingers to her mouth as though I were a ghost, which was funny, since she was the one who had no reflection anymore.

"You said they exiled you," Edmund said quietly at my shoulder.

"They exiled my soul from the throne." I kept my chin high and let them stare. "My name stayed behind. Names are currency here. Mine has been accruing interest for a hundred years."

"And now?"

"Now they don't know whether to kneel or to eat me. Which means for one evening, nobody moves." I turned to him. "You are Lord Kavanagh, a debt-collector from the surface guilds, come to audit accounts the court has neglected. Tobias is your clerk. I am your translator, because the dead speak a language the living mangle."

Edmund looked at me for a long moment. There was bone-dust on his sleeve and grief under it that he couldn't name yet, and still he managed a dry note in his voice: "You enjoy this."

"I was crowned at nineteen," I said. "I have been dead longer than I was alive, and I remember enjoying exactly one thing." I let my gaze sweep the glittering horror of the court. "This. Being underestimated by monsters."

* * *

The Bone King received us beneath a canopy of fused vertebrae, and he was beautiful, which I had warned them about, but warning does nothing against it. He had been a man once — they all had, down here, that was the court's founding shame — and he had kept the man's face and added to it: antlers of pale ivory sweeping back from his temples, eyes like coins left on a dead man's lids. When he smiled, the smile fit him the way a crown fits a usurper. Perfectly. Recently. On top of someone else's claim.

"Lord Kavanagh," I announced, in the formal cadence of the surface guilds. "Debt-collector, bearing the seal of the Guild of Impossible Things. He comes regarding an account opened in this court's name — a soul, contracted and unpaid."

A murmur went through the assembled courtiers like a blade being drawn slowly.

The Bone King's coin-eyes fixed on me last of all, and stayed. "And your interpreter speaks with the accent of a queen we unmade," he said. His voice was warm. That was the worst thing about him. Everything cruel he ever said arrived warm, like bathwater, like a hand at your back guiding you somewhere you shouldn't go. "Welcome home, Seraphine. Or do you prefer the name you use among the living's leftovers?"

"I prefer," I said, "*Your Majesty,* delivered without irony, which I know is difficult for you, since everything you wear is borrowed."

The court laughed — sharp, delighted, terrified laughter, the sound of people laughing at whatever the King laughs at, and praying the aim stays elsewhere. The Bone King laughed too, and inclined his great antlered head, and I understood that he had decided to find me entertaining rather than treasonous, and that this reprieve would last exactly as long as I remained amusing.

"An audit," he mused. "How tedious. How *mortal.* Very well, Lord Collector. Ask your questions. The court keeps immaculate books." He gestured, and I saw then what hung beside his throne, and my whole dead self went still.

Lamps.

Dozens of them, suspended on silver chains from the rib-vault above the dais, each one glowing with a soft, shifting light that was not fire. Soul-light. Some burned steady. Some guttered. One near the end of the row flickered in a rhythm I knew — quick, stubborn, arrhythmic, like a heart refusing to keep proper time. Like a child who had grown up among impossible things and learned that impossible was just a word adults used for *not yet.*

Edmund had gone very white. Tobias had made a small wounded noise and converted it, bless his coward's instincts, into a coughing fit, hunching over his satchel like a clerk overcome by dust.

"The account in question," Edmund said, and his voice held, barely, "is a girl-child's soul, sold by contract some decades past. We require to inspect the asset. Verify condition. Assess outstanding balance."

"Inspection is permitted." The Bone King rose, robes whispering like sand through an hourglass, and drifted toward the row of lamps with the unhurried grace of a man showing off a wine cellar. "Condition varies. Grief dims them. Hope burns them out faster than despair, oddly — hope flickers, spends itself in bursts. Despair is a slow oil." He stopped before the stubborn, arrhythmic flame and cupped his hands around it almost tenderly. "This one is neither. This one is *defiant*, which connoisseurs prize. It burns hot and refuses to gutter. Do you see how it leans? Toward the door. They always lean toward the door, the young ones. Even after a lifetime down here, even after they forget there is a door." He glanced back at us, smiling. "It hasn't forgotten."

Edmund made a sound I felt more than heard, a fracture running through him, and I stepped smoothly between him and the lamp before the King could read it.

"The asset appears intact," I said. "Its purpose?"

"Dinner lighting." The Bone King said it the way a mortal says *ambiance.* "It hangs above my table. Tonight, in fact — moonset. There is a dish I serve only by soul-light. The flavor is said to be..." he searched for the word, savoring it, "...*nostalgic.*"

Above his table. At moonset. The hour the prophecy had named, the hour when the devouring came due, and he had said it all in front of us with the serene pleasure of a host describing his seating chart.

Tobias gripped my arm. I could feel his pulse hammering through his sleeve, fast and useless and alive. "The contract," he breathed. "If it's destroyed at its signing place—"

"It was signed on the throne dais," I murmured back, "a hundred years before your girl was born, by a guild clerk with ink-stained fingers and a spine of wet paper. I watched it happen." I watched Tobias absorb that — watched him understand that the cowardice in those records was older than he was, inherited, a debt of its own — and something moved through his face that I had seen once before, on the face of a young queen deciding not to cry in front of her court.

Resolve. Ugly, terrified, magnificent resolve.

The Bone King turned back to us, lamp-light swimming in his coin eyes. "You'll stay for the feast, of course, Lord Collector. All debts are honored at table. All *accounts settled.*" His gaze slid past Edmund, past trembling Tobias, and landed on me with the weight of a century. "Especially yours, Seraphine. We have so much unfinished business, you and I. Your name has been sitting in my treasury all this time. Growing."

Around us the court bowed, beautiful and hollow, candles guttering in their sockets, and above the dais the small defiant flame leaned hard toward the door — toward Edmund — burning like a heart that refused, refused, refused to keep proper time.

Moonset was four hours away.

I smiled at the Bone King, warmly, the way everything down here was warm, and began to plan a theft.
