## 7. The Price Named

The court beneath Ironhollow never slept, but it pretended to. Lamps of caged soul-light burned in alcoves the way candles burn in a chapel — patiently, as if waiting to be needed — and somewhere far off, water dripped through stone with the rhythm of a heart that had forgotten what it was counting.

Edmund stood at a window of the guest wing that wasn't a window. It was a mirror polished so fine it showed the court above them, inverted: the guild hall, the kitchen where Wren burned the porridge every morning and swore she'd done it on purpose, the bone-yard where he taught her to carve sparrows from lamb shanks. He watched it for a long time. He was memorizing it without knowing why.

Behind him, Seraphine moved through the room like weather.

She had not slept either. Ghosts didn't, though she'd spent three days pretending otherwise out of some old royal habit of dignity. She wore the body the court had lent her — flesh again, or something shaped like flesh, given back for the duration of her visit like a coat returned from storage. It fit her badly. Not in size. In patience.

"You're going to ask me," she said, "what my price is."

Edmund turned. "I assumed you'd tell me when it suited you."

"It suits me now." She sat on the edge of the bed that no one had slept in, arranging her borrowed hands in her lap with the care of someone handling instruments she didn't trust. "I've been thinking about it since the whalebone door. I've had a hundred years to think, down there in the dark, and then three days up here among the living, watching you all."

"Watching us."

"The way Tobias checks the locks twice. The way you count your tools before bed." Her eyes came up. They were the eyes of a queen and also the eyes of something much older and much hungrier. "Family," she said. "That's what you have. That's what I lost before I understood I'd had it. A kingdom is not a family, Edmund Kavanagh. I learned that too late, and the learning cost me everything, and now I remember everything and feel nothing, and the remembering is worse."

He said nothing. The dead clerk's whisper from a week ago echoed in him — *the page no one has read* — and he thought: this is how it starts. This is how a comfortable life cracks. Not with thunder. With a ghost asking politely for what you can't imagine giving.

"I watched the girl," Seraphine went on. "Wren. She laughs at your failures. She brings you tea you don't drink because she forgets you take it black, and you drink it anyway, sweetened, because correcting her would end the ritual. Do you know what that is? That is the entire architecture of love. Small wrong things, kept." Her voice did something he hadn't heard it do before — it frayed. "I want it. Not the memory of it. I have ten thousand memories of courts and coronations and a mother who kissed my forehead exactly twice in my whole life. I want the *feeling*. And feeling lives in the living, bone-carver. It cannot be copied. It can only be taken whole."

Edmund's mouth had gone dry. "You want—"

"Her," Seraphine said. Simply. Terribly simply. "Every memory you have of Wren. From the day you met her to this moment. All of it. That is my price."

* * *

He made her say it twice. Then a third time, because the first two times his mind had flinched away from the words like fingers from a hot iron, and he wanted to be sure he'd actually heard them.

They were standing by the false window now. Below, in the inverted image of his life, Wren was hanging laundry in a wind that didn't reach this deep into the mountain. She was arguing with a sheet.

"If you take those," Edmund said slowly, "I won't know her. I'll look at her and see a stranger. A stranger living in my house, eating our food, calling me—" His voice caught on the word. *Edmund.* Or maybe *Uncle.* Or maybe just the sound she made when she needed him and couldn't find the right word, which was a kind of whistle through her teeth, which was its own name, which was theirs alone.

"Yes," said Seraphine.

"And I'll let her? Some part of me — whatever's left — will just let a strange child live with me?"

"That part I cannot promise. But I will make you one vow, bone-carver, and ghosts keep vows better than kings ever did." She rose, and the borrowed flesh made her tall, taller than she'd been as a haunting. "What you give me, I will carry well. Every memory of her will be tended in me like a grave-garden. I will not waste your love. It will be the first thing I have felt in a century, and I will treat it accordingly."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we leave tonight, and I lead you out through gates that know my name, and Wren's soul burns as a lamp on the Bone King's table until moonset swallows her forever." She tilted her head. "You've already paid the gates. Your mother's face. Your wedding day. You are practiced at loss, Edmund. I am only asking you to lose the thing you love best — and gain, in exchange, the knowledge that it still exists somewhere, felt, alive, in me."

"There's another exchange," he said. "Tobias. The contract. If he destroys it at the signing place, the claim unravels and no one has to pay anything more."

Seraphine smiled, and it was the saddest thing in the court beneath Ironhollow, which was saying a great deal. "Yes. If he finds it. If he reaches it. If the Bone King lets him burn it. So many ifs between here and salvation. My price requires only one word from you."

* * *

He walked the corridors for an hour after that. Maybe two. Time ran strangely down there, stretched thin over the dark like skin over a drum.

He passed alcoves where soul-lamps flickered. He stopped at one — small, guttering, the flame inside it shaped almost like a girl, almost like a bird, almost like the way Wren moved when she danced to music only she could hear. The Bone King's table lamp. He knew it now. He'd seen it at dinner, set in the place of honor, and he'd had to grip the table's edge to keep from reaching for it while the court's beautiful, rotten faces watched him with amusement.

*Tonight*, the ledger had said. *Edmund Kavanagh.* Moonset was coming whether he agreed to anything or not.

He found a bench carved from a single femur, polished smooth by centuries of other people's despair, and he sat on it and did what he always did when the world broke open: he worked the problem like material.

Option one: refuse. Lose Wren entirely, soul devoured at moonset, and keep his memories of her — spend the rest of his life carving little birds no one asked for and weeping over a grave he'd helped dig.

Option two: agree. Lose her from himself, but free her. Wake tomorrow beside a stranger and begin — what? Grief without an object. Love with no address.

There should have been an option three. There wasn't. The court beneath Ironhollow did not offer thirds.

And underneath the arithmetic, quieter than the arithmetic, a truth he kept trying to bury: he loved her. Not as a father loves, exactly, not as a brother, not as anything with a clean name. He loved her the way bone-carvers love grain — knowing its flaws, working with them, finding the shape already hidden inside. And love, real love, had never once in his life been about keeping. His mother's face had gone into a gate and the world had continued. The wedding day that never was had gone into a gate and he had survived it. Love was not the having. Love was the giving, even when the giving emptied you.

Even, he thought, when the giving took the giver.

He stood up. His legs were steady. That surprised him.

* * *

He told Tobias nothing. Tobias was in the guest wing, hunched over stolen copies of the sale records, cross-referencing signatures with the frantic energy of a man doing penance in advance, and Edmund looked at him — at the archivist who had falsified a ledger out of cowardice and followed them into the dark out of shame — and thought: *you would try to stop me. You would offer your own memories instead. And hers aren't yours to offer, old man. Only mine.*

So he said, "Rest. We move at moonset," and Tobias nodded, and that lie sat in Edmund's chest like a swallowed stone.

Wren's lamp was in its alcove. He passed it once more on his way to find Seraphine. The little flame leaned toward him as he went by, the way a plant leans toward light, the way a child leans toward a door opening, and he did not stop, because stopping would have ended him.

He found her on a balcony of rib-bone overlooking the Bone King's gardens, where flowers grew that were not flowers, fed on things he refused to identify.

"One condition," Edmund said.

Seraphine turned. "Ghosts don't negotiate."

"Then hear a demand." His voice was level. He was proud of that, later — of the levelness, in the memory he would not keep. "When you feel it — all of it, the porridge, the tea, the birds, the laundry wind — you'll understand what I gave up. And when you understand, you'll do one thing for me. You'll tell her. Everything. Every memory, in order, starting with the day she arrived at the guild with frostbite and a stolen spoon and wouldn't speak for a month. You'll teach me to myself. Because I'll be a stranger to her, Seraphine, and strangers don't get trusted, and she deserves better than to grieve alone in her own house."

The queen was silent a long moment. Wind moved through the balcony that had no source, carrying cold from deeper places.

"You're making me a keeper," she said finally. "Not of your memories. Of your *love*. Do you know what you're asking?"

"I'm a bone-carver. We know exactly what we're asking. We just do it anyway."

"Then yes," Seraphine said. "Vowed and witnessed." The dark itself seemed to lean in as witness. "Now. Here. While your courage holds."

She raised her hand — the borrowed one, the royal one — and pressed two fingers to his forehead, and Edmund felt the first memory rise like a splinter being drawn.

It was the arrival. Frostbite. The stolen spoon. The silence that lasted a month and broke one morning over burnt porridge, when Wren laughed — a cracked, rusty, astonishing sound — and pointed at the pot as if accusing it of treason, and he had laughed too, and that had been the beginning of everything.

He held onto it for one second longer than he had to. Then he let go.

* * *

The taking took longer than a moment and less than an hour, and afterward Edmund stood on the rib-bone balcony with gaps in himself he could feel like missing teeth. He remembered a girl existed whose soul they were stealing back tonight. He remembered duty. He remembered the ledger, the prophecy, moonset. He did not remember porridge. He did not remember a laugh like a rusted gate swinging open. He did not remember why his hands smelled faintly of lavender polish, or why his left thumb kept moving in the motion of carving something small, something bird-shaped, over and over, a habit with no story attached.

Below, in the gardens, a bell began to toll. Once. Twice.

Moonset was rising.

From the shadowed end of the balcony, Tobias Vane stepped forward, pale as paper, holding the stolen copies against his chest — and Edmund realized, with the flat calm of a man reading his own obituary, that the archivist had heard everything.
