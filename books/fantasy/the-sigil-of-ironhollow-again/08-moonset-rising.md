## 8. Moonset Rising

Tobias Vane had spent forty years learning that the difference between a lie and a record was only which one people believed, and in all that time he had never once done anything brave. He thought about this now, pressed flat against the cold wall of the Bone King's antechamber while Edmund Kavanagh's hands hovered over the soul-lamp like a man defusing something with his heart.

The lamp was smaller than Tobias had imagined. That was the obscenity of it. Wren — who had once climbed into his archive window at fourteen to reorganize his misfiled prophecy scrolls by *feel*, because she said he sorted them like a coward, alphabetically instead of by danger — was a flame the size of a thumb. She sat inside a cage of carved ribs, and she burned a soft gold that made Tobias's eyes sting, because it was the exact color of the reading lamps in the guild library, the color of every evening he'd ever pretended was enough.

"Third bell," Seraphine breathed beside him. The dead queen's voice had a new edge down here, honed against old hatred. "When it rings, the King's cupbearers come for the table wine. We have the length of their walk."

Edmund didn't answer. His fingers were shaking. Tobias watched him work the rib-cage's clasp — bone-carver's hands, patient even in terror — and felt the familiar acid of his own guilt rise in his throat. He had written this lamp into existence. Not the soul, no; the court had taken that long before him. But he had inked the record. *Received in good order: one soul, orphan-born, per contract.* Forty years ago, a younger Tobias Vane had signed his name under a guild seal he did not yet understand was a brand.

"I have it," Edmund whispered.

The clasp gave. The flame flared — and Wren's light *recognized* him. Tobias saw it happen: the gold bent toward Edmund like a stem toward sun, trembling, reaching. The whole antechamber brightened for half a heartbeat, and half a heartbeat was one too many.

Above them, through the vaulted dark, something vast exhaled.

"The King wakes," Seraphine said. "Move."

* * *

They moved.

Tobias had planned the escape route himself, from the maps he'd stolen out of his own falsified archives, and the plan lasted exactly as far as the first corridor. The plan said left. The corridor said otherwise: where the map showed passage, there was instead a wall of fused vertebrae, fresh, glistening, grown overnight like coral. The court rebuilt itself around its treasures. It had known they were coming since the moment Edmund paid the first gate with his mother's face.

"The other way," Seraphine snapped, already turning, her crownless head high, her dead queen's bearing the only weapon she carried openly.

The other way was worse. The other way was the great gallery, where the court's nobility hung from hooks of iron like garments waiting to be worn, hollow courtiers-in-waiting, beautiful and empty. As the three of them ran the length of it, the empty things began to turn on their hooks, slowly, tracking them, and Tobias understood with a lurch that they were not decorations. They were *options*. The court kept spare bodies the way the guild kept spare quills.

"Don't look at their faces," Seraphine said. "Some of them are wearing people you've read about."

Tobias looked anyway, because he was Tobias Vane, and looking was the only sin he'd never managed to quit. In the fourth hook, wearing a face he knew from an oil portrait in the guild's founding hall, hung a woman with the Guildmistress Ashka's jawline. An ancestor. A debt inherited like property ran both directions.

Then the third bell finished ringing, and the gallery lit up all at once, chandeliers of knucklebone and amber flaring to life, and the hunting began.

* * *

It is difficult to run with a stolen soul in your arms. Tobias learned this as a fact of the body before he learned it as a fact of the heart: Edmund carried the lamp cradled against his chest, which slowed him, which made him turn corners wide, which made him — Tobias saw it coming with horrible clarity — hesitate at a junction to check whether the light was still burning.

"She's fine," Tobias gasped. "Keep moving."

"She flickered when we turned—"

"*She's a soul, not a candle, keep moving!*"

Behind them, the Bone King came. They did not see him at first; they heard him, which was somehow worse. He did not sound like footsteps. He sounded like a ledger being balanced — a dry, patient scratching, the sound of accounts being checked line by line, and wherever the sound paused, the walls behind them grew new bone. He was closing the corridors as he walked. He was editing the mountain the way Tobias had edited records: removing what should never have been permitted to exist.

Seraphine led them down through a colonnade of teeth so large each one stood taller than a man, and there the sound stopped, and the silence was more terrible than the scratching, and then the wall at the end of the colonnade simply opened like a mouth and the King was there.

Tobias had expected a monster. What he saw was an accountant. The Bone King wore a crown of fused finger-bones and robes the color of old vellum, and his face was a face designed for reading over spectacles, except he had no eyes — only smooth hollows, and in the hollows, small patient flames, the color of souls kept for lamps. When he spoke, his voice was the voice of every clerk who had ever told Tobias *that's just how the records are kept.*

"You are holding my property," the King said to Edmund, reasonably. "Return it, and I will return what you have paid me. Your mother's face. Your wedding day. Fair trade. I am nothing if not fair."

Edmund's arms tightened around the lamp. "Her name is Wren."

"Her name," said the King, "is line item eleven." And he raised one hand, and the entire colonnade leaned inward like a book closing.

That was when Tobias Vane reached into his coat and took out the original contract.

He had carried it for forty years. Folded eight times, worn soft as cloth, hidden in a false bottom of his document case through every audit, every inspection, every night he woke sweating at the thought of someone finding it. He had falsified the sale records because he'd been a coward, yes — but a coward keeps evidence. Cowards know that someday the bill comes due. He had rehearsed this moment in his head a thousand times and in every rehearsal his hands were steady.

His hands were not steady. But they were moving.

"Tobias—" Seraphine saw it, understood instantly, and her dead eyes went wide. "Not here — the signing place, the prophecy says—"

"The prophecy says the tunnels collapse either way," Tobias said. "I read it. Twice. While you two were being heroic, I read it." He laughed, a cracked, awful little laugh. "Forty years I filed things correctly because I was afraid of what happens when you don't. Well."

He held the contract up between himself and the Bone King, and the King's hollow eye-flames fixed on it, and for the first time the vast patient scratching sound of him went silent — truly silent — and Tobias understood that he was looking at fear. Real fear. The court beneath Ironhollow was built on paper. Every horror here had a signature at the bottom of it.

"It has to burn at the signing place," the King said quickly, smoothly, reasonably, the way you talk someone off a ledge. "Burn it here and you unmake nothing but yourselves. The tunnels—"

"I know," Tobias said. "I've read the geological surveys. I *wrote* some of the geological surveys." He fumbled at his belt for the striker Edmund had given him back at the third gate — the carver marked everything, even tools, with tiny careful runes — and his fingers were shaking so badly he dropped it, and it skittered across the tooth-floor, and the Bone King moved toward it with terrible unhurried grace.

And Tobias Vane, disgraced archivist, forty years a liar, did the least likely thing in his unlikely life.

He struck the contract against the King's own crown.

The fused finger-bones were old, older than the court, older than the guild, and somewhere in their marrow there lived a spark — the same fire, perhaps, that burned in soul-lamps, that had burned once in a forgotten queen. The vellum caught. The flames ran up the folded lines of ink like they had been waiting forty years for permission, and the King screamed — not a monster's roar but a clerk's shriek, the sound of a ledger catching fire — and the whole mountain heard the arithmetic come undone.

Line item eleven ceased to exist. Somewhere above them, Tobias knew, a falsified record in his own archive was quietly blanking itself, honest at last. And the tunnels—

The tunnels began to fall.

* * *

Afterward, Tobias would remember the collapse in fragments, the way he remembered everything worth remembering: badly, in pieces, lit wrong.

Seraphine seizing his collar and hauling him sideways as a column of vertebrae came down where he stood. Edmund running with the lamp blazing now, blazing *free*, the gold light no longer caged but pouring out of the broken rib-cage like water from a tipped jug, lighting corridors ahead of them a heartbeat before the ceilings behind them became floors. The King pursuing them through his own disintegrating kingdom, screaming line numbers, screaming *item seven, item twelve, item eleven, WHERE IS ELEVEN* — a god of accounting watching his books burn.

A bridge of spine-bones giving way under Tobias, and Edmund — without hesitating, without checking whether the lamp still burned — throwing himself flat and catching Tobias's wrist with one hand while the other arm cradled the light. "I've got you," Edmund said, and Tobias, dangling over a dark that went down further than dark should go, thought: *he doesn't even know what I did. If he knew what I signed, what I hid—*

"I know," Tobias called up. "I know what you're carrying. Both things. I've got them too."

He meant the confession. He would make it later, properly, with the records open in front of them. But Edmund only hauled, and Seraphine pulled, and they came up out of the falling dark together as the last of the Bone King's galleries folded shut behind them like a book no one would read again.

* * *

They surfaced through a fissure in the mountain's northern flank as the moon touched the horizon — moonset, the devouring hour, the hour the prophecy had named. The sky above Ironhollow was going grey at the edges. The lamp-light poured out of Edmund's arms and rose, and rose, and for one suspended moment it hung in the cold air shaped like a girl — arms, laughter, the ghost-outline of a shrug — looking at the three of them looking up at her.

Then it tore itself apart into sparks and streamed away east, toward the guild, toward home, faster than grief could follow.

"We have until dawn to reach her body before—" Seraphine started.

But Edmund had gone very still. He was staring at the place where the light had been, and his face was doing something Tobias had seen only once before, on the face of a man reading his own name in a ledger of prophecies.

"Edmund?" Tobias said carefully. "Edmund, do you know who that was?"

The bone-carver looked at him with polite, complete, absolute emptiness, and said:

"Should I?"

Behind him, Seraphine closed her eyes. Above them, the moon slid under the horizon, and the devouring hour ended, and somewhere down in the ruined dark, faint and patient and not nearly finished, the scratching sound began again.
