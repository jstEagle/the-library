## 9. The Devouring Hour

The moon was going down like a coin dropped into a well.

Edmund felt it in his bones before he saw it — the light through the fissures above Ironhollow thinning from silver to grey to nothing, and beneath his feet the whole court shivering awake with appetite. Moonset was the devouring hour. The hour when debts came due.

He stood at the edge of the Bone King's hall with Tobias's burned contract still smoking in his fist and Wren's soul-lamp heavy under his arm, and he understood, with the terrible clarity of a man who has already paid too much, that it was not enough.

The lamp fought him. That was the thing no one had told him about souls — they were not quiet. The glass was warm as a sleeping child's cheek, and inside it something turned and turned, restless, trying to remember which way was up. He had carried it three corridors through collapsing dark while the Bone King's hounds sang behind them, and every step of the way it had pressed against his ribs like a heartbeat that wasn't his, asking a question he couldn't answer.

*Is it time? Am I going home?*

"Not yet," he whispered. "Almost."

The hall opened around them like the throat of some enormous patient beast. Walls of femur and rib, polished until they gleamed like ivory mirrors, and in each mirror a reflection that wasn't quite right — reflections that showed you not as you were but as you would be when the court finished with you. Edmund did not look at his. He had seen enough of himself going missing, piece by piece, gate by gate. First his mother's face. Then a wedding day that had never happened and somehow hurt worse for it. Then the smell of rain on the guild roof, the sound of Ashka laughing, the name of the street where he'd learned to carve. A man could be spent like coin, he'd learned, and still walk and talk and hold things in his hands. What remained was not exactly a person. It was the shape a person leaves in a chair.

At the far end of the hall, on a throne of interlocked vertebrae, the Bone King waited.

He had stopped pretending to be beautiful. That was the last kindness of the night — the mask cracking off like eggshell, revealing what wore it: something tall and jointed wrong, crowned in black bone, its face a suggestion rather than a fact. Around it the court knelt — Seraphine's old courtiers, gorgeous and hollow, their jeweled faces turned up toward their king with the devotion of moths toward a lamp that eats.

"You have my property," the Bone King said. Its voice was the sound a skeleton makes settling in the earth.

"I have *her*," Edmund said. "She was never yours. Vane burned the contract. Your claim is ash."

The Bone King tilted its head, and the crown clicked against itself, vertebra sliding on vertebra. "Paper burns. Debts do not. The debt was signed in blood older than your guild, carver. The girl's soul was promised before her grandmother drew breath. Come. Sit. We will discuss terms."

Behind one of the bone mirrors, Tobias made a small wounded sound. Edmund didn't turn. If he looked at Tobias now — at the archivist gray-faced and soot-blackened, holding the stub of a candle he'd used to commit the bravest act of his cowardly life — Edmund would lose the cold thing in his chest that was doing all the work of keeping him upright.

"There are no terms," Edmund said. "There's only this."

And he set the lamp down between himself and the throne, and he said the words he'd been carrying since the third gate, the ones he hadn't told Seraphine or Tobias because saying them aloud would have made them real too soon:

"Take me instead. Everything I have left. All of it."

The court went silent the way a forest goes silent — everything listening at once.

"Every memory remaining to me," Edmund said, and his voice held, barely, the way a bridge holds. "Not traded at your gates, not parceled out passage by passage. Wholesale. All at once. Every face I've ever loved. Every name. The guild. My hands' knowledge — let that go too, if it has a price. I'll be an empty room walking out of here. And you will open your hand, and she goes free, and the debt dies with the payment. Paid in full by the only currency this place respects."

The Bone King rose from its throne. It took a long time, joints unfolding like a folding screen being opened, and when it stood it filled half the hall.

"You would forget her," it said. "You climbed into the dark of the world for a child whose face you carry in your chest like a coal, and you would hand me the coal."

"Yes."

"And why would I take such a bargain? I already have her soul. Why buy what I own?"

"Because paper burns," Edmund said, "and you're afraid of that. Because a stolen soul is a wound in your kingdom that never closes — I've seen it, she leaks, she *fights*, she's been fighting you for years from inside a lamp. But a soul given freely, bought and paid—"

"—is owned absolutely," the Bone King finished. "Yours freely given, sealing hers forever released." Something moved beneath its suggestion-of-a-face that might have been hunger or might have been respect. "You have been in the dark too long, carver. You bargain like us."

"No," Edmund said. "I bargain like someone who loves her more than being anyone at all."

* * *

"Edmund."

Seraphine stepped out of the shadow of the mirrors, and the whole court flinched, though none of them knew yet why.

She had been quiet since the tunnels collapsed behind them — too quiet, Edmund had thought, grief-quiet, a queen walking back into the house that ate her. Her gown of mothwing grey was torn. Her hands were steady.

"You don't have to do this," she said. "There is another way. There has been another way since we entered this hall, and I have been a coward about naming it, because naming it means giving up the last thing I own."

"Seraphine—"

"My name," she said, and the word went through the court like a bell through glass.

Every courtier screamed at once. Not loudly — horribly, softly, the scream of people remembering something they'd spent centuries forgetting. Because names were currency down here too, and Seraphine's had been seized when her soul was exiled, locked in the Bone King's crown with all the other stolen sovereignty, and without it she had been only a ghost with excellent posture. With it—

With it she was a queen.

Light came off her like heat off a forge. The mothwing gown burned away and beneath it was armor of whalebone and dawnlight, and the courtiers who had knelt for the Bone King now cowered from her, because they remembered — Edmund watched them remember — the reign before the reign, the queen who had built the bone gates as *doors* and not walls, who had ruled the dead with mercy until her own court sold her name to the thing that replaced her.

"The contract," Seraphine said, walking forward, and the floor of femurs parted for her, "was signed in *my* court, under *my* seal, by a king who had no right to hold either. The debt was never lawful. It was theft compounded on theft. And I—" she reached the throne, reached the Bone King, reached up with both burning hands toward the black crown — "am taking my property back. All of it."

The Bone King struck at her. She caught its wrist the way you catch a child reaching for a knife.

"Every name you stole," she said. "Every soul you caged and called a lamp. I remember what this court was. Let me show it what it can be again — starting with your ending."

She closed her hands on the crown.

The sound it made was not a sound. It was the absence of one, a hole punched in the world's hearing, and then the crown came apart in her grip like rotted wood, vertebra unknitting from vertebra, and every stolen name inside it flew free — Edmund heard them go, hundreds of them, thousands, a storm of syllables streaming up through the fissures toward the surface, toward graves and memorials and mothers who had never known why they couldn't stop grieving.

The Bone King came apart with its crown.

It did not fall so much as *unagree to exist*, joints loosening, suggestion-of-a-face smoothing into plain bone, plain dust, and the dust did not even settle — the updraft of freed names took it, carried it, scattered it through the halls it had ruled. The courtiers knelt again, but differently now, kneeling the way people kneel at a shore.

The lamps along the walls began to go out. Not dying — *leaving*. Souls rising out of glass like sparks off a struck flint, streaming upward after the names. The hall emptied itself of light and filled itself with motion, everything going home at once, everything except—

Except the lamp at Edmund's feet, which flickered, and steadied, and stayed.

Because its debt had been real. Because paper burned but the ledger remembered, and somewhere in the arithmetic of the world, Wren's soul still owed, unless—

"Unless," Edmund said aloud, and laughed, a cracked awful laugh, because of course. Of course it came down to this. Of course the gates had been teaching him the price all along, memory by memory, so he'd know exactly what he was holding when the final bill arrived.

He knelt beside the lamp. Put his palm flat against the warm glass.

"Hey, Wren," he said. "I'm going to tell you something, and then I'm not going to know it anymore, so listen carefully."

Inside the glass, the turning slowed. Listening.

"I found you in a book. Isn't that funny? You were ink before you were anything. And I came down here because the thought of a world with you missing out of it was unbearable, and I want you to know that even after I don't know why, even when I look at you and see a stranger — there'll be a shape in me where you were. Rooms in me with the furniture gone. And empty rooms are still rooms. Someone can live in them."

His vision was already going soft at the edges. The Bone King was dead but the debt lived in the oldest bones of the mountain, and the mountain was collecting, gently, the way snow falls.

"The guild raised you," he said, faster now, words tumbling. "Ashka pretends she's stern but she cries at weddings. Tobias falsified your sale and burned the proof and he'll apologize for both forever. Seraphine was a queen and she chose us. You like your eggs burnt. You whistle when you think no one's listening. You carve little animals out of scrap bone and hide them in my workshop and I have never once thrown one away, I keep every single—"

He couldn't remember what he kept.

He couldn't remember why his hand was on a lamp.

The lamp was warm. That seemed important. He stayed kneeling beside it a moment longer, frowning at it the way you frown at a word that's been on the tip of your tongue all day.

Then a girl's voice — no, a young woman's, coming down the shattered hall, running — crying his name, and he turned, and he did not know her, and the last thing the old Edmund Kavanagh felt, in the half-second before the new one took his place, was not fear.

It was the strangest relief. As if some load he'd been carrying for miles had finally been set down by someone else's hands.

"Who—" Edmund began.

Wren caught him before he fell.
