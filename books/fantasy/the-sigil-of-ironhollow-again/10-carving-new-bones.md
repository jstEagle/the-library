## 10. Carving New Bones

The first thing I learned about the dead is that they don't leave all at once. They leave the way heat leaves a room after the fire dies — slowly, in layers, and you keep turning toward the warm spot where they used to be.

Edmund came up out of Ironhollow at dawn, carried between Tobias Vane's shoulders and Seraphine's ghost-light, and when I ran to him he looked at me the way you look at a stranger who's standing too close.

"Hello," he said. Polite. Kind, even. Edmund was always kind; that much of him had survived the mountain. "Are you hurt? You're crying."

"You don't know me."

"No." He said it simply, without apology, because how could he apologize for something he didn't know he'd lost? "Should I?"

Tobias made a sound like a man swallowing glass. Seraphine's light dimmed to almost nothing, which I would learn was her way of weeping.

I had rehearsed this on the long climb back down from the ridge where we'd watched the moonset burn over Ironhollow. I'd rehearsed anger — *you absolute fool, you could have taken me with you, you could have paid with anything else* — and grief, and even gratitude, laid out neat as ledger lines. But none of the rehearsals survived contact with his face. Because it *was* his face. The same crooked nose he'd broken falling out of the guild's apple tree when he was nineteen. The same ink stains on his fingers. He was entirely himself, except for the hole where I used to be, and he couldn't even feel the edges of it.

"I'm Wren," I said. "You carved my first bone whistle. You told me the guild kitchen haunted itself just so I'd stop being scared of the dark. You taught me letters by carving them into bread crusts so I could eat my alphabet."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not memory. Memory was gone, spent coin by coin through the bone gates, poured out wholesale at moonset. But maybe an echo of the shape a memory leaves. Like the dent in a chair.

"That sounds like someone I'd have liked to be," he said.

* * *

The guild took him back the way you take home a bird with a broken wing: gently, and with no expectation it will ever sing the same song again.

Guildmistress Ashka met us at the door. She looked at Edmund — at what he'd paid for Wren's soul, for *my* soul, for a debt she'd inherited and never lifted off our family — and whatever she meant to say died in her throat. She settled for, "Welcome home, Kavanagh."

"Do I live here?" he asked.

"You do now."

He slept that night in the narrow room above the workshop, the one with the whalebone door, because Seraphine had asked to be near it and nobody argued with queens, even forgotten ones. I sat outside his room until well past midnight, listening to him breathe, hating the mountain, loving him, not knowing which of those was heavier.

It was Tobias who gave me the idea. He found me on the stairs, hollow-eyed, still smelling of burnt contract paper. He'd been the guild's archivist before his cowardice unmade him; falsifying records was his sin, but organizing truth was his gift.

"The ledger," he said. "The prophecy ledger. It recorded what hadn't happened yet. Well." He laughed, a cracked little sound. "Most of what happened hasn't been written down anywhere. And you were there for all of it."

"What are you saying?"

"I'm saying memory is just story told twice. He can't remember you. So tell him. Every day. Tell him until the telling becomes its own kind of remembering."

* * *

We started small, because bone-carvers know you don't begin a piece with the fine work.

I brought him breakfast the first morning and put the bone whistle in front of his plate.

"Yours?" he asked.

"Mine. You made it. I was six, and I cried every night because the orphanage before the guild had a dog named Turnip, and I missed him. So you carved me a whistle out of goose bone and told me if I played it at the window, Turnip would hear it wherever dogs go when they die." I turned it in my fingers. "I played it every night for a year."

"Did it work?"

"It worked better than it should have. One night something answered. Ashka says it was a fox. I say it was Turnip." I pushed the whistle across the table. "Keep it. You should hold things you've made."

He held it like it might break, or like he might. That became the shape of everything after.

Each day I gave him one story, and only one. I learned quickly that more than one drowned him — he'd nod along, agreeable as ever, and by evening they'd all blurred together like wet ink. But one story, told slow, carved careful, stayed. Not as memory. As something deeper than memory. As grain in wood.

I told him about the winter he taught me to skate on the millpond and fell through the ice and came up laughing, and how I screamed, and how he said, "Now you know exactly how cold it is, and you'll never fear guessing again." He frowned at that one for three days, then started laughing at nothing, twice, and couldn't say why.

I told him about the time he forged Ashka's signature on a permit for fireworks and nearly burned down the guild's east wing, and how she made him copy the entire accounts ledger as punishment, which is how he discovered he loved balancing numbers, which is how he became the guild's bookkeeper at all. When I finished, he looked down at his own hands like they'd confessed something. "That explains," he said slowly, "why ledgers feel like coming home."

I told him about my mother — the woman the guild sold before I was born, whose soul-debt hung over my cradle like a blade. I told him how he'd found her name in a dead clerk's whisper, how he'd walked into the dark to buy back what was mine. He listened without blinking. At the end he said, quietly, "Whoever he was, that man must have loved you very much."

"He did," I said. "And so do you. You just don't remember why yet."

* * *

Seraphine left on the ninth morning.

She'd grown brighter since leaving the court beneath Ironhollow — since she'd stood before the Bone King's shattered crown and spoken her own name aloud, the name the court had erased, and the whole mountain had groaned around the sound of it. Queens shouldn't be able to die twice, but she'd come close, and she'd done it anyway, for people she'd known for less than a week.

She gathered herself at dawn by the whalebone door, a column of pale light with a woman's face inside it, more visible now than she'd ever been below ground.

"You're going," I said.

"I was royal once. Then I was a prisoner. Then I was a guide." Her voice was the sound frost makes forming. "I find I'd like to try being nothing at all. Just light, going somewhere."

"Will you come back?"

"Ghosts don't make promises. Promises are for the living, and living is your department now." She drifted closer, and where her light touched me I felt warm, absurdly warm, like sun through a window in winter. "You rebuilt him out of stories, little wren. Do you understand what you did? The court devoured memories because it believed they were the self. You've proven the opposite. The self is what *keeps telling*. Keep telling."

Then she was gone, up through the dawn, and I swear the birds went quiet for her, and I swear the light bent a little to let her pass.

Tobias watched from the doorway, holding the burnt corner of the original contract he'd saved from the fire like a relic. "She was royal," he said wonderingly. "And she chose us."

"She chose free," I said. "We just happened to be pointing that direction."

* * *

By autumn, Edmund had thirty-one stories, and I kept count because I am my mother's daughter and my father's bookkeeper both.

He'd begun adding to them himself. Small ones, new ones. He remembered the *telling*, even when he couldn't remember the events — remembered my voice shaping each one, the way the fire popped on certain nights, that Tobias always cried during the sad ones and pretended it was pipe smoke. Those were his memories, freshly minted, and he guarded them jealously.

One evening in the workshop, he set down his knife and studied me across the lamp.

"Wren. Can I ask you something strange?"

"You ask me strange things constantly."

"When you tell the stories. About the man I was." His hands, always restless, turned a half-carved sparrow over and over. "Do you miss him? The one who knew you? Is it — does it feel like talking to a stranger wearing his face?"

I thought about lying. I thought about it for exactly as long as it takes a candle flame to gutter.

"Yes," I said. "Every day. Some days I grieve him like he died, because in a way he did. He gave himself away piece by piece to get me back, and I got to keep everything and he got to keep nothing, and that's not fair, and I'd do it all again, and I hate that I'd do it all again."

He nodded slowly. Then he picked up the sparrow, blew the bone dust from it, and set it in front of me. It was crude. The wings were wrong. It was unmistakably the work of a beginner, of a man relearning his own craft with hands that remembered skill but not the ten thousand hours behind it.

"Then here's what I think," he said. "I think whoever I was, he'd be furious if you spent the rest of your life mourning him when you could be annoying me instead. And I think—" he hesitated, and I saw him reach for something past the edge of what he knew, "—I think love isn't the remembering. I think the remembering is just where love used to live. The house burns down. The love finds a new address."

I laughed, and it came out wet and ugly and real. "Did you just invent philosophy from a bone sparrow?"

"I'm told I'm full of surprises. You would know."

"I do know," I said. "I have thirty-one stories that say so."

"Thirty-two," he said, and began to carve.

* * *

Winter came early that year, the way it does in the hills above Ironhollow. We sealed the whalebone door against the cold, though sometimes at night I swear I felt warmth seeping through it, faint and regal, like a hand resting briefly on the other side.

On the longest night of the year, Ashka called the household together — me, Edmund, Tobias, and the two younger orphans who'd arrived in spring — and opened the prophecy ledger to a fresh page.

"A century ago, this book told us tonight would happen," she said. "So it seems only fair we tell it what happens next." She dipped the pen and held it out. "Wren. You were there. Write it."

My hand shook. Not from cold.

*In the year of the broken crown,* I wrote, *a soul was stolen and stolen back, and the man who bought it home forgot the reason he'd gone. This is not a tragedy. Read on.*

Below it, in Edmund's careful bookkeeper's hand, he added: *Read on indeed. She tells it better than I lived it. — E.K.*

Tobias added an annotation, because Tobias could no more leave a document unannotated than fly. Seraphine's name went in last, written in letters that seemed faintly luminous no matter what ink we used, so that anyone reading the page a hundred years from now would know a queen had passed that way, and had finally, at the end of everything, been free.

Ashka closed the ledger. Outside, snow was beginning to fall, soft as bone dust, covering the road down to Ironhollow, the gates, the dark court with its beautiful cruel faces, burying all of it a little deeper.

Edmund touched my shoulder. "Tomorrow's story," he said. "What will it be?"

I smiled, and reached for the beginning, which is the only place anything ever truly starts.

"You were twenty-two," I said, "and you came home soaked to the skin carrying a box you refused to open, and you said, 'Whatever's in here, we're keeping it,' and that is how you found me—"

And upstairs, the ledger lay closed on its shelf, its pages quiet, waiting — as all true books wait — for the next impossible thing to walk in and demand to be written down.
