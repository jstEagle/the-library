## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The lamps of Ironhollow had to be lit before the mist thickened at dusk, and Wren Brightwater had never once been late.

Nine years he had walked the same circuit — from the lamp-house at the valley's northern lip, down the switchback stair cut into the chalk cliff, along the shore of Lake Vessel where the water lay flat as poured pewter, and out again to the smaller lights strung on their iron posts like a rosary of fire. There were forty-one lamps in all. He knew each one's temper. The third lamp on the lake path sputtered if the wind came off the water wrong; the lamp above the tide gate needed its wick trimmed twice as often as any other, though no one could say why; and the great beacon in the lamp-house itself — the one the valley had built long before Wren was born, for reasons no one quite remembered — burned with a flame that leaned, always, toward the center of the valley. Never outward. Toward home, the older lamplighters used to say. Though *home*, in Ironhollow, was a word with soft edges, worn smooth by handling, like a coin passed through too many hands.

Wren climbed the last of the stairs two at a time, his oil can swinging against his knee, and let himself into the lamp-room as the first grey fingers of mist began curling over the ridge to the south.

The lamp-room sat at the top of a tower that had no business existing. That was the thing about Ironhollow that visitors — the rare, brief, soon-forgotten visitors — always remarked on before they remarked on nothing at all: a landlocked valley, ringed by mountains, drowned in permanent fog, and yet here stood a lighthouse. A great stone tower with a gallery and a Fresnel lens the size of a cartwheel, polished until it threw rainbows across the rafters when the sun occasionally punched through. Built to guide ships that would never come, up a channel that had never existed, through water that lay a hundred miles away in every direction.

*It guides something else,* the oldest of the elders used to say, when she still said anything. But the oldest of the elders had died when Wren was nineteen, and whatever it guided, the valley had forgotten, and forgetting — Wren had learned this slowly, the way you learn the shape of a stone in your shoe — was not an accident in Ironhollow. It was a function. The valley forgot things the way a body sweats: constantly, involuntarily, and for its own protection.

He knew this because he kept records.

Not official ones. Officially, the lamp-room contained ledgers of wicks trimmed and oil consumed, columns of neat figures going back — well, going back about nine years, which was as far as any ledger in Ironhollow went, because paper did not keep here the way paper kept elsewhere. Ink faded. Pencil ghosted. Even carved letters in wood softened at their edges after enough seasons, as though the grain itself grew embarrassed by permanence. Wren had tested it once, morbidly curious, scratching his own name into the lintel above the door with a knife. Three years later it read *Wr*n B**ghtw*t*r*, and now it read nothing at all, just a shallow scar in the timber that might have been anything.

So he kept his true records in the archive below — the dry, lamp-warmed cellar beneath the tower where the valley's books went when they stopped being useful and started being dangerous — and there he wrote in a code of his own devising, numbers standing in for names, so that even if the valley reached into the pages and smoothed them blank, the arithmetic underneath might survive. It was a foolish project. He knew it was a foolish project. But a man who has lost everything once develops a superstitious tenderness for the practice of keeping.

Wren set his oil can down, lit the great beacon with a taper from the eternal flame — the one fire in the valley that never guttered, never wavered, and whose origin nobody questioned, because questioning was the first thing Ironhollow taught you not to do — and then, instead of descending the outer stair toward the lake path as he should have, he paused.

The light swept around. Once. Twice. Its shadow wheeled across the shelves of the little gallery, across the chart table with its map of a coastline no one could find, across the narrow door set into the eastern wall that led down into the archive — and stopped, or seemed to stop, or perhaps only Wren's eye caught on it because tonight of all nights his attention wanted catching.

Tomorrow evening, Tam Ilsa's boy Corven was coming to supper.

No — Corren. He corrected himself, as he had been correcting himself all week. *Corren.* A stranger, technically, though stranger was a generous word for a man who had washed up at the tide gate three weeks ago with no history, no papers, and no memory of how he'd arrived, which was normal enough for Ironhollow — people arrived the way weather arrived, unbidden and half-forgotten before they'd finished unpacking — except that Corren hadn't forgotten anything. That was the strange part. That was the part that had made Wren look twice, and then keep looking.

Most newcomers arrived already blurred. They'd smile at you across the market and you could see it happening behind their eyes, the soft erosion, the outside world draining out of them like color out of cloth left in rain. Within a season they were valley-born, or near enough. But Corren — Corren remembered roads. Corren remembered a word he said was the name of a kingdom, though he pronounced it uncertainly, like a man reading it off a scrap of paper: *Vel Saren*. And when he said it, three separate elders left the room.

And Corren had looked at Wren across the fish stall on Tuesday, with those dark restless eyes and that old scar cutting through the stubble along his jaw, and said, "You're the lamplighter. You're the one who keeps things." And Wren, who had not been asked to share a meal by anyone but Elder Maren in four years, heard himself say yes.

His first date in nine years. He turned the phrase over and found it both absurd and terrifying. He was twenty-five years old and he had lived in Ironhollow since he was sixteen, and in nine years the valley had eaten every memory he owned of the world beyond the mist — he knew this the way you know a tooth is missing, by the gap, by the tongue returning again and again to the absence — and tomorrow night a man who *remembered* would sit across his table and eat his stew, and what would they possibly find to say?

Everything, probably. That was what frightened him.

Wren shook himself, trimmed the beacon's wick, and took up his oil can to make the circuit — and then stopped again, because the eastern door was open.

He never left it open. Nine years of habit does not bend easily. The archive below was damp-sensitive, draft-sensitive, flame-averse; he kept that door shut and barred the way a miser keeps his strongbox. Yet it stood ajar now, breathing out its smell of cold paper and tallow and something older underneath, something mineral, like the air inside a cave that has been closed since before anyone living was born.

He told himself the bar must have slipped. He told himself this while his heart performed a slow, heavy percussion against his ribs, and he went down anyway, because curiosity in Wren Brightwater had always outrun caution, and everyone in the valley who knew him knew it, and it had cost him, once, everything.

* * *

The archive was a single low-vaulted room, dug into the tower's foundation, shelved floor to ceiling with the valley's discarded past. Ledgers of harvests whose years no longer matched any calendar. Sermons by preachers whose names had gone illegible. Children's copybooks. A tax roll. Three identical maps of the valley itself, each drawn by a different hand, each labeling the surrounding peaks with names that disagreed with one another completely — as though three different worlds had tried to describe the same prison.

Wren lit the wall sconce and stood a moment, listening. Dust hung in the lamplight, undisturbed. Nothing had been taken. Nothing had been touched, apparently, except—

One book lay on the reading table in the center of the room.

He knew every object in this room the way he knew his own hands, and that book had never been on the table. It had never been in the room. It was a slim volume bound in grey leather gone greenish with age, its spine cracked, its corners rounded like river stones, and it lay closed and patient in the pool of light as though it had been waiting for him specifically, which was impossible, which was exactly the sort of thing the valley did not permit him to think clearly about, because the moment he tried to wonder *who put it there*, he felt the thought go soft in his mind, the edges blurring, the question dissolving like sugar in warm water —

— and Wren, who had spent nine years practicing this, did what he always did: he wrote it down. Pulled his notebook from his coat, scratched in his private numerals: *Book appeared. No explanation. Do not lose this thought.* And the act of writing anchored it, the way a mooring anchors a boat, imperfectly, temporarily, but enough.

Then he sat down at the table and opened the cover.

The pages were dense, close-written in a hand that slanted hard to the right, brown ink gone sepia, and the opening lines were a record-keeper's preamble, dry as chaff: *Being a true account of the visions of Sera Dovel of the Lampkeepers' Order, set down in the year 812 of the Kingdom of Vel Saren.*

A kingdom name. In writing. In Ironhollow, where ink forgot.

His skin prickled. He turned the page, and another, skimming past the pious throat-clearing, past dates and dreams and a long complaint about the quality of the archive's candles — and there, on a page headed by a small precise drawing of the lamp-tower itself, the handwriting changed. Larger. Pressed harder into the paper, as if the writer had been afraid of being interrupted.

*This is what she saw, and I write it plainly so that no hand can soften it later. She saw the valley's ending and its beginning, both at once, which is the way such things are seen. She saw a storm-caller come home. These are her words:*

*When the stranger crosses the tide gate on the eve of the turning — the eve written in no calendar but this one, the ninth day of the waning moon in the month the valley calls Quietfall — the exile who remembers will know him by the mark upon his jaw, and the storm will wake in him as it woke before, and the door that was shut will be asked to open.*

*A century I give it, no more. Let the one who reads this count the years and tremble.*

Wren stopped counting at nothing, because he didn't need to count. The ninth day of the waning moon in Quietfall. He knew the valley's calendar the way he knew his own heartbeat; he had tended its lamps through thirty-one Quietfalls, and the ninth day of the waning moon this year was —

Tomorrow.

The lamp overhead hummed. Somewhere above, the great beacon swept its slow circle, its shadow passing over the ceiling of the archive like the hand of an enormous clock. Tomorrow evening, Corren was coming to supper. Corren, who remembered a kingdom. Corren, who had a scar along his jaw — Wren had noticed it the first time, of course he had, had wondered idly what knife or accident had left such a clean line through the dark stubble, and now the wondering curdled into something colder.

And *the exile who remembers*. Wren read the phrase again. It slid off him the first time, the second time, the way certain thoughts slid off everyone in Ironhollow, and he felt the valley working at him even now, gentle as erosion, telling him the phrase meant nothing, meant someone else, meant no one. But he had his notebook in his hand, and he wrote it down, and the writing held it fast, and holding it fast he made himself look at it plainly:

There was exactly one person in Ironhollow who had ever been exiled. Not exiled *from* — exiled *within*. Sentenced at sixteen, by the council of elders, to remain in the valley forever but to be set apart from it, unbelonging, unnamed in the festival rolls and unmourned in the memorial lists, a resident ghost: Wren Brightwater, lamplighter, keeper of the archive, keeper of everything the valley chose to forget.

He had never learned why. That was the sentence within the sentence. Whatever crime had earned it, the punishment included the erasure of the reason — the valley took the memory of his offense as payment for his sanctuary, Maren had told him once, gently, her hand on his hair like a mother's, *it's a mercy, Wren, you wouldn't want to carry it* — and so he had grown to adulthood around a wound shaped like a memory, pressing on it some nights the way the tongue presses a missing tooth.

*The exile who remembers.*

But he didn't remember. That was the whole point of him. Unless — and the thought arrived with a sensation like the first drop of a storm he could not yet hear — unless the remembering was what was beginning. Unless the prophecy wasn't describing something that had happened, but something happening *now*, starting now, in this room, in this chest, in the slow thaw of a thing frozen for nine years.

He sat very still for a long time. The dust turned in the light. Above him the beacon swept, and swept, guiding whatever it guided.

Then, carefully, he closed the grey book, carried it up out of the archive — the first book ever to leave that room in his keeping — wrapped it in oilcloth, and hid it inside the housing of the third lamp on the lake path, the temperamental one, the one no one visited but him. Then he stood on the shore of Lake Vessel in the thickening dusk and lit the remaining lamps one by one, forty-one flames leaning all together toward the center of the valley, toward home, and tried to feel hope about tomorrow instead of dread, and found that the two had become, somewhere in the last hour, indistinguishable.

The mist came down off the ridges and swallowed the mountains. Somewhere beyond it — a day's walk, a lifetime's walk, he no longer knew — there was a world with a kingdom in it called Vel Saren, or there had been, once.

Tomorrow, a stranger with a scar on his jaw was coming to dinner, and Wren Brightwater had a terrible feeling that he had set an extra place at the table without knowing why.
