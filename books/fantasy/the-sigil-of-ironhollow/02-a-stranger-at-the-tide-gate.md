## 2. A Stranger at the Tide Gate

Wren had lit the lamps a hundred times before, but never with shaking hands.

He told himself it was the cold. The mist off the lake had teeth tonight, and the wick of the third lamp fought him, guttering twice before it caught and threw its small gold circle against the fog. Ironhollow's lamps were not like other lamps. They were old work, older than the valley's houses, older than its bridges — glass and iron wrought in shapes no living smith could name, and when they burned they did something to the mist. They didn't push it back so much as persuade it, gently, endlessly, to stay where it was. To keep the valley folded in on itself like a letter no one would ever open.

Nine years he'd tended them. Nine years since the council had led him down from the high pastures with his hands bound in wool — not rope, never rope; Ironhollow was gentle even in its punishments — and set him inside the valley's edge and told him this was his life now: exile within exile, the only man in Ironhollow who could not leave.

He checked the fourth lamp. The fifth. His reflection swam in each one, a thin young man with ink-stained fingers and hair the color of wet slate, and behind that reflection, faintly, the archive room of the lamp-house, where an old book lay open on his desk with a date written in it that had kept him awake until the birds started.

*On the eve of the ninth year,* the prophecy said, *a stranger comes through the tide gate.*

The tide gate was not really a gate. It was a gap in the valley's western rim where the mist ran thin over black stones, and once an hour, when the pressure of the world beyond pressed hardest, the water of the outflow stream reversed itself for a few breaths and flowed backward, uphill, into the fog. The children called it the valley breathing in. Wren had watched it a thousand evenings and thought nothing of it, the way you think nothing of your own heartbeat.

Tonight he thought of nothing else.

*You're being foolish,* he told himself, climbing the lamp-path toward the meeting house, where he'd agreed — gods help him, actually agreed, out loud, with witnesses — to meet Corren Vale at sundown. A stranger who'd arrived three days ago on the eastern road with a pack, a scar, and a smile that made Wren forget the ends of sentences. Maren Voss had vouched for him, which surprised everyone, because Maren vouched for almost no one. *He's tired and far from home,* she'd said. *Let him rest a season.* And Wren, who had spent nine years being careful the way other men were brave, had heard himself say, *I'll show him the lamps.*

A date. Tam had laughed himself sick about it. *Nine years, Brightwater, and you pick a man who appeared out of thin air.*

But there was nothing else in Ironhollow to pick. That was the truth of the valley nobody said plainly. Two hundred souls, and every one of them had been born inside the mist or had come so long ago that the coming had worn smooth as river stone. Nobody remembered another country. Nobody missed one. When Wren asked — carefully, casually, the way you ask questions you've been punished for — what lay beyond the ridge, people looked at him with mild, kind puzzlement, as if he'd asked what lay beneath the ground they stood on. *Mist,* they said. *More valley, maybe. Who can say?*

Only Wren knew there had ever been anything else, and only because the valley itself had left him the memory as a wound: he remembered *that he had forgotten*, the way an amputee remembers a hand. Somewhere in him was a door painted shut. He'd stopped knocking on it years ago.

Mostly.

The meeting house square was lamplit and nearly empty. Corren was already there.

He stood under the eave of the bakehouse, out of the drizzle, and he was watching the mist come down the street the way a sailor watches weather — not afraid, exactly, but reading it, cataloguing it, measuring it against some private chart. He wore a traveler's coat gone soft at the elbows and boots repaired more than once by someone who loved him enough to do it badly. The lamplight found the scar first, as it always did: a pale seam along the line of his jaw, left side, running up toward the ear like the tail of a sentence cut short.

Wren stopped walking.

The book. The illustration. He'd told himself it meant nothing — woodcuts were woodcuts, faces were faces, a hundred years was long enough for coincidence to grow comfortable and old. But he had stared at that page by candlelight until the wax drowned, and he had memorized the way the artist had drawn the jawline scar, the tilt of the shoulders, the coat. And here it all was, breathing, dripping rain, waiting for him under the eave of the bakehouse as though it had always been there and the world had merely now gotten around to it.

*On the eve of the ninth year, a stranger comes through the tide gate, marked by iron and loss.*

"Evening," Corren called, easy and warm, and raised a hand. "I wasn't sure you'd come. People say a lot of things on the road."

"I say them too," Wren heard himself answer, and his voice came out steady, which felt like a betrayal of everything currently happening inside his chest. "Come on, then. I promised you the lamps."

They walked the lamp-path together, and Wren performed the tour he'd given a dozen times to visitors who never stayed: the first lamp, the founder's lamp, the glass blown thick as a knuckle; the little shrine of river-stones where the keepers left offerings; the view from the second rise where all two hundred lamps of Ironhollow showed at once, strung along the streets and terraces like a constellation someone had lovingly dropped. Corren listened. That was the thing about him — he *listened*, entirely, with his whole face, asking questions that were sharper than casual, and Wren caught himself talking more than he had in years, telling him things about the valves and the wick-oils and the way the mist thickened near the eastern quarry, and every time their eyes met he forgot the ends of sentences again.

It was good. It was, absurdly, the best evening Wren had had in nine years, and that was precisely what made his skin crawl, because he kept seeing the woodcut every time Corren turned his head, and the book was still open on his desk, and the date was still today.

They reached the tide gate at full dark.

Here the path ended in black stone shelves above the outflow, and the mist hung low and slow, and the sound of the valley breathing filled the hollow — that long reverse-sigh of water climbing back uphill into the fog. Corren went very still when he heard it.

"That's not a tide," he said quietly.

"It's what we call it." Wren leaned on the rail. "Once an hour. Nobody knows why."

"I know why." Corren came to stand beside him, close enough that Wren could smell woodsmoke and road dust on his coat. His voice had changed — the warmth banked down to coals, the listening-face turned inward now. "Do you want the honest answer or the comfortable one?"

"There isn't a comfortable one, is there."

"No." Corren looked out at the mist, and the lamplight caught the scar, and Wren thought: *say it isn't true, say you're just a man from somewhere, anywhere.* "Three days ago I walked through a door," Corren said. "Not a gate, not a pass. A *door*. Standing alone in a burned forest, frame and all, no wall around it. My sister was behind me. The riders were behind her. I went through, and the door took me instead of her, and it put me out on your eastern road, and I have spent three days trying to understand how a place can exist that doesn't exist."

Wren's mouth had gone dry. "Doesn't exist."

"On any map. In any record. In anyone's *memory*." Corren turned to look at him then, and his eyes in the lamplight were dark and level and frightened in a way that had nothing to do with Wren. "I came from Vel Saren, Wren. Have you ever heard that name?"

And Wren opened his mouth to say *no*, because that was the right answer, the only answer, the answer everyone in Ironhollow would give—

—and found that the word wouldn't come. It sat in his chest like a swallowed coal. *Vel Saren.* The syllables fit somewhere in him, fit a shaped absence the way a key fits a lock that's been bricked over, and his hands began to shake, and Corren saw it, and Corren's face went strange.

"You have heard it," Corren whispered. "Gods. You have."

"I—" Wren gripped the rail. Below them the water sighed backward, uphill, the valley breathing in. "There's a book," he said. "In the lamp archive. A century old. It names a stranger coming through the tide gate, and it has a drawing, and the drawing has your scar, and I told myself it was coincidence, and I have been lying to myself since sundown." He made himself look up. "What are the riders?"

Corren's jaw tightened. The scar pulled white.

"Wardens," he said. "Of the Ashen Court. They ride gray horses that make no prints, and they carry lanterns that burn without flame, and if you look into their eyes you'll see they've been emptied — everything they were scooped out and replaced with duty. They hunt what the Court has erased. Books. Names. People." He paused, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped to almost nothing. "My sister was taken four years ago. She's one of them now, maybe. Maybe worse than one of them. And the door I came through — Wren, doors like that don't stand open by accident. Something let me in. Something *wanted*—"

He stopped.

Wren followed his gaze up the western rim, where the mist lay thickest, a pale shoulder of the world — and saw the light.

It was small and far and wrong. Every lamp in Ironhollow burned gold; everyone knew that, the way you know the color of your own blood. This light burned white. Flameless, smokeless, steady as a held breath, it moved along the ridge-top with a rider's unhurried rhythm, and where it passed, Wren saw — he would swear afterward, on anything, on everything — that the mist did not part around it. It *shrank*. Drew away in threads, like frost retreating from a warm hand, leaving a corridor of bare, cold air behind the lantern.

Corren made a sound low in his throat. Not a word. Older than words.

"They shouldn't have found it," he breathed. "Nobody finds it. It's not *on anything*—" And then, horribly, softly, with the guilt arriving in his voice ahead of his understanding of it: "Unless they followed me. Unless the door didn't hide me. Unless it *led* me."

Down in the valley, the lamps of Ironhollow burned on, gold and trusting, lighting suppers and bedtime stories and an old woman pulling bread from an oven, and none of them — not one soul in two hundred — had ever seen a light that wasn't theirs. None of them knew there was a world to be hunted from.

Wren did. He had known since sundown, since the book, since the date written a century ago in a dead hand:

*A stranger comes through the tide gate, and after him the hunters, and the valley that forgot must learn to remember or be forgotten with the rest.*

The white light crested the ridge and stopped. Turned. Looked down into the valley with eyes Wren couldn't see and felt anyway — patient, empty, certain.

"Wren," Corren said quietly, hand finding his arm. "We need to get to the elder. Now."
