## 3. What the Mist Keeps Out

Corren had never seen a place that refused to be found before.

He had heard of such things — every child raised on the road-edges of the Ashen Court had, in the furtive whispers that passed for bedtime stories once the lamps were doused and the wardens had finished their rounds. A valley behind a wall of mist. A door with no handle. A country that remembered itself when no one else would. He had filed those stories away with all the other impossible things: dragons, rain that fell without permission, a king's name spoken aloud in daylight.

Now he sat in a lamplit kitchen at the bottom of one of those stories, holding a cup of tea he could not bring himself to drink, while a man with storm-grey eyes waited for him to explain why riders were burning the world.

The kitchen belonged to Wren Brightwater. Corren knew the name now — had known it since the tide gate, when the stranger from the prophecy had said it like an apology. The house was small and crooked and smelled of lamp-oil and dried lavender, and there was a shelf of books along one wall that made Corren's chest ache in a way he did not have words for yet.

"You said you walked through a door," Wren said. He sat across the table with his hands wrapped around his own cup, not drinking either. "Doors don't exist here. I've lived in Ironhollow nine years. There's the valley mouth, and there's the mist, and nothing comes through the mist."

"This one did." Corren set the cup down. His hands were still shaking; he doubted they would stop until the shaking stopped meaning something. "It wasn't a door when I came to it. It was a crack. In a cliff face, up past the tree line where nothing grows anymore. There was light coming through it — wrong light, gold light, like the last hour of a day that never ends." He looked down at his knuckles, at the scratches the rock had left there. "I put my fingers in the crack because the riders were coming, and I thought, better to be crushed inside a mountain than caught by them. And then the crack was a corridor, and the corridor was a stairway going down into dark, and then—"

"Then the mist," Wren said quietly.

"Then the mist. It parted for me. Like it knew I was coming." Corren laughed, and hated how thin the sound was. "I know how that sounds."

"I found a book tonight," Wren said. "In the lamp-room archive. It predicted you. Down to your face." He turned his cup slowly on the table, around and around, a small orbit. "So no. It doesn't sound impossible. It sounds like Tuesday."

* * *

Corren told him everything. That was the strange part, looking back on it later — how easily the whole terrible architecture of the outside world came pouring out of him, once he started, as though nine years of silence in this kitchen had left a shape exactly the size of his grief, waiting to be filled.

He told him about the Court first, because everything else grew out of it like roots out of rotten soil.

"There was a kingdom called Vel Saren," he said, and watched Wren's face for any flicker of recognition. There was none. Of course there was none. "East of the mountains, south of the salt marshes. Two hundred years old when it fell. They had — gods, they had *everything*. Universities. Libraries that took a day to walk across. Rain-mages who negotiated with the weather instead of commanding it. A fleet. A calendar of festivals so long that somewhere in Vel Saren it was always someone's feast day." His voice cracked on the word *always*, and he pressed on before Wren could offer him kindness, because kindness right now would undo him entirely. "And then the Ashen Court came out of the north, and they didn't conquer it. You have to understand that. Conquest would have been mercy. They didn't take the kingdom. They *unwrote* it."

Wren had gone very still. "Unwritten how?"

"Burned first. Then burned again — the second fire was worse. They burned the records. The maps. The gravestones. The ledgers of debts owed and paid, the marriage registers, the ship manifests, the recipes grandmothers kept in kitchen drawers. Every document that contained the name Vel Saren was gathered into squares in the town centers of three countries and set alight, and the Court's criers stood by the fires and read new histories aloud while they burned, histories where the land had always been empty, where the rivers had no names, where nobody had ever lived there at all." Corren's hands had closed into fists without his noticing; he made himself open them. "And then they burned the people who *remembered*. Not all at once. They were thorough about it. Teachers first. Then priests. Then archivists, genealogists, anyone whose trade was memory. And the ones they didn't kill—" He stopped. Started again. "The ones they didn't kill, they hollowed."

"The riders," Wren said. "With the empty eyes."

"Wardens." The word tasted like ash; everything did, these days. "They were people once. The Court takes them — volunteers, mostly, at first, though the volunteering has gotten less voluntary as the years go on. They drink something, some alchemy of the Regent's own devising, and it burns everything out of them except duty. Names go first. Then faces. Then love, if they had any. What's left is very obedient and very patient and does not sleep and does not stop." He met Wren's eyes. "I ran from six of them for eleven days. They don't tire. They don't eat. They follow a scent like hounds follow blood — except what they're scenting isn't you. It's whatever the Court wants found. And right now, what the Court wants found is this valley."

"Why?" Wren asked. But Corren could see, in the way the lamplight moved across his face, that he already half-knew — that some part of him had known since the moment Corren stumbled through his door smelling of smoke and fear. People who lived behind walls always half-knew what the walls were for.

"Because of the mist," Corren said. "Because the mist doesn't just keep things out. It keeps things *in*. Memory can't cross it. That's why you've never heard of Vel Saren — the valley eats the outside world's names at the boundary. Nothing gets remembered in here that wasn't already here." He laughed again, that awful thin sound. "You have a library, Wren. An archive. A hundred years of prophecy and record and god knows what else, sitting in a lighthouse at the edge of a drowned lake, untouched, unburned, unindexed by men who burn indexes. Do you understand what that makes it?"

Wren's cup had stopped turning. "The last one."

"The last unburned library in the world." Corren spread his hands, helpless. "Every name the Court erased still exists somewhere in this valley, if it exists anywhere. Every record they thought they'd destroyed. And they've been looking for it for a century. The Regent calls it the Unfinished Fire — the one archive that never made it onto a pyre. Whole expeditions have gone into these mountains and never come back. I used to think the mist killed them. Now I think the mist simply declined to let them in." He looked down at his scratched hands. "Until tonight."

* * *

The silence that followed lasted long enough for the tea to go cold in both cups.

Corren got up because he could not bear to sit still inside the shape of what he'd done, and crossed to the window, and looked out at the valley. It was beautiful in the way of things that do not know they are dying: terraced fields silvered by moonlight, the dark spine of the lake, and above it all the great slow breathing of the mist-wall, rolling and unrolling against the peaks like a tide that never reached shore. Lamps glowed along the ridgeline paths — the famous lamps of Ironhollow, Wren's lamps, each one tended by hand.

Somewhere under that calm water, or behind those stone shelves, or in whatever deep room the elders kept their secrets, lay the only proof left on earth that two million people had ever drawn breath.

And he had led the wolves straight to it.

"They followed me," he said to the glass. His reflection looked wrecked, hollowed in its own smaller way, and he thought: *a week more of running and I'd have matched them.* "That's the part you need to understand, Wren. They didn't find the valley despite me. They found it *through* me. I was carrying a compass — a dragon-scale compass, stolen off a warden I killed near the border, back when I still believed one dead warden mattered. The scales point toward what the Court seeks. I thought I'd broken it. I threw it in a river three days ago." His breath fogged the cold pane. "But the crack in the cliff opened for me anyway. Which means either the compass worked at a distance, or—" He couldn't finish it.

"Or the door wanted you here," Wren said from behind him. "Because the prophecy needed you here."

"That's worse," Corren said. "Don't you see? If the door chose me, then the door chose *them* too. I'm the key that opened the lock, and I walked through it thinking only of saving my own skin, and now there's a century-old archive full of dead people's names sitting thirty miles from six things that never stop walking." He turned. "I should leave. Tonight. Lead them away from the valley the way I came — if they'll follow me out—"

"They won't follow you out." Wren rose too, and there was something in his voice Corren hadn't heard before, something older than his years, worn smooth like a stone in a current. "If the Court wants what's in this valley, they'll want it whether you're here or gone. Running won't un-ring the bell. Believe me." A shadow crossed his face then, quick as a fish under water, and Corren understood there was a story there — an exile inside an exile, a man punished by the place that saved him — and that he did not have the right to ask about it yet.

"So what do we do?" Corren asked. It was not a rhetorical question. He genuinely did not know. He had spent three years being a courier of forbidden memories — memorizing fragments of Vel Saren's records, songs, street names, a children's counting rhyme, anything small enough to carry in a skull — and smuggling them to pockets of the unremembered in the Court's outer provinces. He knew how to hide a poem in the meter of a work song. He did not know how to save a library from the end of the world.

"We tell the council," Wren said. "There's an elder — Maren Voss. She's—" Something complicated moved through his expression, tenderness braided with doubt. "She'll help. She's helped me since the day I arrived. And the archive has to be protected, or moved, or—" He stopped. Looked toward the window, toward the mist. "Corren. How long do we have? Truly. How fast do they move?"

"Through open country, faster than a horse over days. Through mountains—" Corren calculated, and hated the number he arrived at. "Two days. Three if the terrain fights them. They're already at the valley's edge, Wren. I saw the lantern on the ridge before you brought me down. They don't need the crack in the cliff anymore. They know where the door is."

Outside, the wind changed. Corren felt it before he heard it — a pressure shift, a wrongness in the air, like the held breath before lightning — and both of them turned to the window together.

Far up on the eastern ridge, where the mist thinned to gauze against the stars, a light had appeared. Not a lamp. The valley's lamps burned warm, amber, alive. This light was white. White and utterly still, like the eye of something that had been watching for a long time and had finally decided to blink.

Then a second light answered it from the north ridge. Then a third.

Wren's hand found his arm. "How many?" he whispered.

Corren counted, and the counting took longer than he wanted it to, and when it was done he said the number aloud in a voice that did not sound like his own:

"Nine. They're not sending a hunting party." He swallowed. "They're sending an army."
