## 5. Ash on the Wind

Corren had been in Ironhollow for two days when the mist began to die.

He noticed it first as a wrongness in the light. The valley lived in a perpetual soft morning — mist diffusing everything, so that shadows were suggestions rather than statements — but on the third dawn the shadows came back. They lay across the cobbles of the lampwrights' quarter like spilled ink, hard-edged and certain, and Corren stood in Wren Brightwater's doorway with a cup of barley tea going cold in his hands and thought: *they're through the outer veil.*

"You feel it too," Wren said behind him. Not a question. He'd learned that about the exile in their brief, wary acquaintance: Wren asked questions the way other people fired arrows, only when he meant to hit something.

"The light's wrong," Corren said.

"The light's *sharp*." Wren came to stand beside him, and this close Corren could see the sleeplessness written under his eyes — a bruised half-moon, the same one Corren wore. Two nights since the wardens' lantern had appeared on the ridge and vanished. Two nights of the valley pretending, with the practiced calm of people who had never once been invaded, that it hadn't seen anything at all. "Mist is supposed to blur things. That's what it's for."

"It's thinning." Corren set down the tea because his hands had started to shake, and he didn't want Wren to see. "It thins near them. The Court's wardens carry ash from the burning of Vel Saren — ground bone and page-ash, pressed into everything they wear. It eats ambiguity. It eats *memory*. Where they walk, the world stops being able to hold more than one truth at a time." He looked out at the valley — the terraced orchards going gold, the lighthouse-lamps along the ridge road, each one a small stubborn flame against the fog — and felt the guilt settle over him again like wet wool. "I led them here, Wren. I know I keep saying it. But I need you to understand what I actually did. There were a hundred ways into this valley and I walked the one they could follow."

"Then we have perhaps a day to be clever," Wren said. "Less, if they're fast."

* * *

They were not fast. They were worse than fast. They were *inevitable*.

The alarm came at midday, and it came not as a bell but as a silence. Ironhollow's lamps all burned with a low hum, a sound so constant that Corren had stopped hearing it within an hour of arriving — but he heard it stop. All across the valley, the hum guttered, and in that sudden acoustic void every other sound became enormous: a dog barking, a shutter banging, a child crying somewhere near the well-house.

Then the horn from the north ridge, where Elder Maren Voss kept her watch-station, and the valley stopped pretending.

"They're at the Grey Ford," Wren said, appearing at Corren's elbow already dressed for work, sleeves bound, the lamp-tending hooks hanging from his belt. "Maren's runners say three riders crossed the ford and the water went—" He stopped. Swallowed. "She said the water went grey. Not muddy. Grey, like ash stirred into milk. And it stayed grey."

Corren closed his eyes. He knew what greyed water meant. He'd seen a river do that outside Vareth Mole, the day the Court took the city's records — all of them, every deed and birth-line and recipe for bread, carted out and burned in a pyre that gave off no heat, only a smell like cold candle-snuff. "Dragon-scale," he said quietly. "They found a compass cut from dragon-scale. It points not north but toward sealed things — hidden doors, veiled valleys, anything the old kingdoms warded away. The Court's been hunting one for years. I'd hoped it was a rumor."

"And it led them here." Wren was already moving toward the door, pulling Corren with him by the wrist, and there was something in his grip that Corren would think about later — not anger, exactly. A kind of terrible clarity, a man deciding that fear was a luxury his schedule didn't allow. "The archive. That's what they want. You said it yourself the first night — the last unburned library. If they take the valley's records—"

"Then Vel Saren isn't just dead. It's *unhappened*." Corren kept pace with him down the lane, past lamplighters running the opposite way with buckets and iron bars, past old Tamsin the baker standing in her doorway holding a peel like a halberd, confused and unafraid because she had never in her life needed to be afraid. "The Court doesn't conquer, Wren. Conquest leaves witnesses. They *erase*. A kingdom that no book remembers, no map marks, no song names — that kingdom can't rise again, can't even be mourned properly. Its people become ghosts in their own skins." His voice cracked on the last of it and he let it. "My family came from Vel Saren. I know that much because my mother wrote it down and hid the paper inside a doll. That scrap of paper is the entire surviving literature of my homeland, as far as I know. One sentence in a doll."

Wren stopped walking. For a moment he just looked at him, and whatever he saw in Corren's face made him reach out and grip his shoulder, brief and hard. "Then today we add a library to your sentence," he said. "Move."

* * *

The archive sat beneath the great lamp-house at the valley's heart, down stairs worn hollow by a century of feet. Corren had glimpsed it the night before — Wren had shown him, almost shyly, the way a man shows a stranger his mother's garden — but he hadn't understood. No one who hadn't grown up in a country of ash could understand.

Shelves. Ranks upon ranks of them, carved from the pale local stone itself, holding ten thousand documents: birth-rolls and weather-ledgers, seed almanacs and love letters confiscated in some ancient scandal, maps of coastlines that existed nowhere else in the world, and above all — stacked in crates marked with a sigil like a door drawn in a single unbroken line — the records of Vel Saren. Names. Thousands of names. The census rolls smuggled out during the burning by someone whose name had itself been lost, carried into the mist, and shelved here in the one place on earth where forgetting couldn't follow.

Because that was the thing about Ironhollow that Corren was only now, with riders at the ford, beginning to grasp. The magic that locked the valley out also locked memory *in*. Nothing written here faded. Nothing remembered here slipped. The valley held its contents the way amber holds an insect — perfectly, indifferently, forever.

And the wardens had come to break the amber.

"We take the Sigil crates first," Wren said, hauling one off the shelf; it weighed as much as a man. "Whatever else burns, those go out."

"There are nine of them—"

"Then we make nine trips, or we get clever. Help me with the handcart."

They got clever instead, because the world didn't give them time for either. The first sign was cold — not the honest cold of mountain air but a colorless cold, a subtraction of warmth rather than a lack of it, pouring down the stairwell like water finding its level. Corren felt the hairs on his arms lift and then lie flat, felt something in the room go quiet in a way rooms shouldn't. Above them, through the floor, came a sound like frost forming on glass, magnified a thousand times.

"They're touching the mist," he whispered. "Where they touch it, it doesn't part. It *dies*. It goes colorless and stiff, like smoke turned to dirty ice, and they walk through the corpse of it." He grabbed the handcart's handle. "We have minutes. Maybe fewer."

They loaded four crates — the door-sigil records, the key-cycles, the temple registers, and one crate whose label Corren read twice before his mind accepted it: *BRIGHTWATER, W. — TRIAL AND SENTENCE.* He reached for it without thinking and Wren caught his arm.

"Not that one."

"Wren—"

"*Not that one.*" And there it was again, that flinty clarity, but underneath it now Corren saw something rawer, something that looked almost like panic. "I know what's in it. Everyone knows what's in it. It stays."

Corren looked at him for one long second — at this man who had been exiled inside his own sanctuary, whose crime was a locked box even the valley's perfect memory apparently declined to open for him — and made a decision he didn't fully understand yet. He let the trial record slide to the back of the shelf, but as he did, he palmed the small brass inventory tag from its spine and closed it in his fist. If the archive burned, that tag might lead somewhere. He told himself it was scholarship. He wasn't sure he believed himself.

Then the stairwell filled with cold, and there was no more time for anything.

* * *

They came up into chaos.

A warden stood in the lamp-house doorway — just one, which somehow made it worse, the way one wolf at the sheepfold is worse than many, because many means the dogs woke. It wore the Court's grey like a second skin, ash-grey cloth over ash-grey mail, and where its boots rested the stone floor had gone pale and lifeless, bleached of grain and color both. Its lantern hung from one gauntleted hand, and the light inside it was the light of the pyres Corren had seen as a boy: white, heatless, hungry.

Its eyes were the worst. Hollow wasn't a metaphor. There was simply nothing in them — no fear, no curiosity, none of the small animal flicker that makes any face human. Two wells. Two doors left open onto nothing.

"Behind me," Wren breathed, and stepped forward, and Corren realized with a jolt that the exile was putting himself between the warden and the handcart. Between the warden and *him*. "This is lamp-work," Wren said louder, to the whole room, though the room held only them and the thing in the door. "Valley business. You have no—"

The warden raised its lantern.

Corren had seen what that light did. He'd watched it fall across a market square in Vareth Mole and leave the crowd standing, breathing, alive — and blank, faces smoothed like river stones, mothers looking at children they no longer knew how to name. He threw himself sideways into Wren, taking them both down behind the great lamp-housing, and the light swept over the space where they'd stood and struck the far wall, and the wall *forgot its paint*. Plaster went grey and bare and slightly wrong, the way a face looks when you can't recall it.

But the lamp-housing was iron and old flame, and the light broke around it like water around a keel.

And in the broken light, something happened that Corren would carry out of this valley if he carried nothing else. Wren — pinned beneath him, cheek to the cold iron, breath coming fast — lifted his head and *looked* at the warden, really looked, and his eyes changed. Not brightening. Deepening. Like weather gathering behind them, like the grey-green of a sky that has decided.

"That's mine you're burning," Wren said, very quietly, and the temperature in the lamp-house dropped, and every lamp in the room flared at once — not brighter but *harder*, flames standing up straight as spears — and the warden took one step backward.

One step. It recovered instantly, of course; hollow things don't stay frightened. But it had moved, and Corren had watched it move, and in that heartbeat he understood two things at once: that the prophecy had chosen correctly, and that he was more afraid of Wren Brightwater in this moment than he was of the warden.

Boots thundered below — valley men with iron bars and Maren Voss's voice cracking over them like a whip — and the warden, outnumbered and unhurried, withdrew the way fog withdraws, backward through its own dead mist, leaving the doorway bleached white as bone.

* * *

They got the cart out through the orchard paths while the valley's edge burned.

That was the only word for it. From the high terrace Corren could see the northern perimeter of Ironhollow, where the mist met the mountains, and along a quarter-mile of it the fog had gone rigid and colorless, a wall of frozen smoke shot through with slow veins of grey. It didn't glow. Fire that glows gives warmth. This fire drew warmth *out*, and the first row of apple trees along it stood bare and silver, their leaves fallen and forgotten on the ground, their fruit still hanging — grey now, weightless, husks.

"They'll widen it," Corren said. He was pushing the handcart; Wren walked beside it with a lamp-hook in his free hand, though they both knew the hook was a talisman, not a weapon. Behind them, four crates rattled. Behind them, a library burned or didn't, depending on how many brave people were still carrying books up cellar stairs. "The breach starts as a point and spreads like rot in fruit. By tomorrow they'll ride in anywhere along the north rim."

"Then tomorrow we're ready."

"We?" Corren huffed something that wanted to be a laugh and failed. "You've known me three days. Three days ago I walked out of a hole in the mist and told you a story about a prophecy, and now you're saying *we* like it means something."

Wren stopped the cart. The orchard was quiet around them — too quiet, the birds gone silent since the breach, the whole valley listening. He looked at Corren with those weather-gathering eyes, and when he spoke his voice was low and rough.

"I've spent nine years tending lamps for a valley that took my memories as rent and calls it mercy. Nine years watching the mist and wondering what was on the other side that everyone else was allowed to forget." He picked up one of the crates — the one labeled with the door-drawn-in-a-line — and set it in Corren's arms, close enough that Corren could feel the man's heartbeat through the wood, quick and stubborn. "Now the other side has come *here*, wearing the faces of what erased your country, and it wants to burn the last copy of a world. You're the only person in this valley who knows what that loss feels like from the inside." A muscle worked in his jaw. "So yes. *We*. Don't make me regret the pronoun."

For a moment Corren couldn't speak. The crate was heavy and the brass tag was a small hard secret in his fist and somewhere behind them a library was dying, and this stranger — this exiled, half-unmade stranger — had just handed him the last unburned history of Vel Saren like it was a loaf of bread, like trust were a simple thing.

"Come on," Corren managed. "There's a cellar under the mill. Stone all the way around, no timber. If the valley keeps its memories, we'll keep these ones ourselves."

They came down off the terrace as the second horn sounded, and behind them, faint and patient, the colorless fire ate another yard of mist.

It was full dark when they finished hiding the crates beneath the mill, and Wren lit the last lamp of the evening with hands that shook only a little, and Corren, watching him, finally opened his fist and looked at what he'd stolen.

Brass tag, stamped in the valley's unchanging script: *BRIGHTWATER, W. — TRIAL AND SENTENCE — SEALED BY ORDER OF M. VOSS.*

Sealed. Not lost. Sealed, by the elder who smiled at Wren like a mother and had signed the order of his exile.

Corren closed his hand around the tag until the edges bit, and said nothing, and followed Wren home through a valley whose mist was dying at its edges — carrying, in one pocket, the last library of a dead kingdom, and in the other, the first question of a living one.
