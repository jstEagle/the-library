## 6. The Prophecy Read Aloud

The lamp-room smelled of tallow and old iron, the way it had for nine years, the way it would long after Wren was gone from it — if anything in Ironhollow could be said to last. He had lit every lamp on the ridge path himself that evening, climbing the switchbacks with his brass lighting-rod in hand, touching flame to wick at each of the fourteen stations until the valley's spine glowed like a row of swallowed stars. It was the work he'd been given when he arrived, half-starved and name-shocked, and it was the work he still did, though by now he could have done it blind. Some nights he suspected he had.

Below him, Ironhollow settled into its evening hush. Lamplight pooled in the lanes like spilled honey. Somewhere near the tide gate, Corren was being walked through the customs of a valley he'd stumbled into out of a nightmare — shown where to sleep, given bread, asked gentle questions in the careful voices people used here, voices that had learned never to press too hard against anything, lest the mist press back.

Wren should have been down there too. Elder Maren had all but ordered him to rest before his date — *your first in nine years, Wren, you'll want your wits about you* — but instead he had climbed back up to the archive with the book under his arm, the one he'd found three nights ago wedged behind the lamp-rotation ledgers, its spine cracked and its boards furred with damp.

*The Vigil of Ironhollow: Being an Account of the Sigil and Its Keeping.*

He set it on the archive desk beneath the great brass lamp that hung over the cataloguing table, and he did not open it right away. He let himself have one more minute of not knowing.

That was the trouble with Ironhollow. The valley taught you patience whether you wanted it or not, because the valley itself was patient. Mist came down off the rim cliffs every dusk and sealed the passes. Memory came apart the same way — slowly, softly, so gently you never felt yourself losing hold of it. Nine years ago Wren Brightwater had arrived knowing there had been a world outside this valley, knowing only dimly what it held, and knowing nothing at all — nothing, not one face, not one reason — about why he had been sent here. The elders told him it was exile. They told him he had done something that required it. When he asked what, they looked at him with such genuine sorrow that he understood they were not withholding. They simply didn't know either.

The valley took payment for sanctuary. That was the phrase Maren used, once, early on, her hands busy with bandaging a cut on his palm he didn't remember earning. *Ironhollow keeps what matters safe, Wren. Sometimes that means keeping it safe from us.* He'd thought she meant the mist. He'd thought she meant the world beyond.

He opened the book.

The paper was Vel Saren work — he knew that much now, knew the watermark of the double mountain and the sun-disc pressed faintly into every third leaf, the same mark that appeared in half the oldest volumes of the archive. The kingdom of Vel Saren, which the valley's founding texts spoke of the way other books spoke of weather: as a fact, constant, unremarkable. A country that had been. The ink of the prophecy itself was brown-black, iron-gall gone to rust, and someone — the original keeper, whoever she had been — had written it out in a hand so steady it looked printed:

*In the hundredth year of the valley's forgetting, on the night the lamps burn low and the tide gate stands open, there shall come to Ironhollow a stranger out of the ash, walking through a door that is not a door, pursued by riders whose eyes have been emptied. Know him by the scar upon his jaw, cut leftward, and know the hour by his arrival: he comes on the eve of the storm-caller's choosing.*

Wren read it twice. His mouth had gone dry somewhere around *the scar upon his jaw*, because he had seen that scar tonight across a supper table, pale and leftward-slanting, catching lamplight whenever Corren laughed — and Corren had laughed, once or twice, nervously, between questions about whether the bread was always this good and whether the mist ever lifted.

*A stranger out of the ash.*

He turned the page. The handwriting changed here, larger, hurried, the pen pressing hard enough to emboss the next leaf:

*And the storm-caller shall be known thus: born outside the valley, exiled within it, struck by lightning and living. The valley will have taken from them the memory of their crime, for the door must be opened by hands that do not know what they are owed. When the last dragon-fire gutters — when Emberlain's heart burns down to its final coal — then the Sigil of Ironhollow will stand unsealed, and the storm-caller must choose: keep the valley shut forever over a dead thing, or open the door onto a country that no longer exists.*

Wren sat down. Not gracefully; his knees simply stopped negotiating with him, and the stool caught him.

*Struck by lightning and living.*

There was a story in the valley about him. Of course there was; valleys make stories the way ponds make reflections. They said the exile who came nine years ago had walked in through the western pass during a thunderstorm that shouldn't have been possible — storms didn't reach Ironhollow, the mist saw to that — and that lightning had come down the pass ahead of him like a herald, striking the boundary stones, splitting one of the old cedars, and that the boy walking behind it hadn't a mark on him except exhaustion. The elders had taken him in without discussion. Nobody discussed the exile. That was part of the story too.

He had always assumed the lightning was embroidery. Stories needed weather; weather gave them drama. But sitting here with a century-old book telling him otherwise — telling him the lightning was not decoration but *credential*, a signature on a contract he'd never signed —

*Exiled within it.*

Only one person in Ironhollow fit that phrase. Everyone else who lived here had come generations ago, whole families walking in before the mist rose, before whatever catastrophe had made the valley into a locked box. Their exile was generational, inherited, ambient. Wren's was singular. Wren had been sent. And the prophecy wanted a storm-caller whose crime had been taken from them — which meant the valley hadn't merely misplaced his memories. It had *collected* them. Payment for sanctuary. Maren's own words, rising up out of nine years' silence to meet him on the page.

He became aware, distantly, that his hands were shaking. He pressed them flat against the desk until the tremor ran up his forearms and stopped somewhere in his chest, which was where it had been living anyway.

The lamp above him hissed softly. Outside, the mist came down off the rim, right on schedule, folding the valley in like a hand closing over something precious — or something fragile. He'd never considered the difference before. Tonight the two meanings lay side by side in his mind like pages of the same book, and he couldn't stop looking at both of them.

*When the last dragon-fire gutters.*

Dragons were stories too. Better ones than his. Children in Ironhollow drew them with too many legs and breathed on their palms to make fire, and the festival songs had a verse about a scaled thing sleeping under the lake, and everyone sang it the way you sing any verse — without asking whether the lake had ever actually been disturbed. Wren tended the lamps. Lamps were real. Oil was real. Wick and glass and wind were real.

But Corren had come through a door that wasn't a door. Wren had watched the mist admit him — had stood at the tide gate that evening, having walked down early to calm his nerves, and seen the wall of grey part around the stranger like water around a stone, closing seamlessly behind him, and heard him say, white-faced, *I didn't knock, I swear I didn't knock, there was just a doorway standing in the ash and my sister's voice coming out of it.*

A man out of the ash, arriving on the appointed night, wearing the appointed scar. You could disbelieve a prophecy. What you could not do was disbelieve it *selectively*, keeping the parts that flattered you and discarding the parts that demanded things. And this part demanded: *Emberlain's heart burns down to its final coal.* Which meant there was a dragon under Lake Vessel, and it was dying, and everything — the mist, the sealing, the nine years of Wren's quiet, lamp-lit, carefully empty life — rested on a fire that was going out.

He heard boots on the stair and did not startle. He'd known, in the way you know weather, that it would be Corren. The footsteps paused at the top, hesitant, and then Corren came into the lamplight with his hands pushed deep in the pockets of a borrowed coat — Maren's son Tamir's coat, two sizes wrong — and looked around the archive with the expression of a man trying to memorize a room he expected to lose.

"The elder said you'd be up here." He gestured vaguely at the shelves. "She said you kept the— the records. The remembering." He pronounced the word carefully, like it might bruise. "Is that what this is? Everything the valley remembers?"

"Everything the valley lets itself remember." Wren closed the book — then, immediately, absurdly, opened it again, laying his palm flat on the page as if to hold the words down. "Corren. Sit down."

Corren sat. Up close, in honest lamplight rather than the flattering gold of the supper candles, he looked worse than he had at dinner — hollowed, unwashed in the way of someone who'd been running for longer than clothes could hide, and young. Younger than Wren, maybe. Twenty-three, twenty-four. The scar stood out livid on his jaw.

"I need to read you something," Wren said. "And I need you to tell me if it sounds like your life, because it sounds like mine, and I've been alone with it for an hour, and I don't recommend it."

So he read. He read the whole of it, from *the hundredth year of the valley's forgetting* to *a country that no longer exists*, his voice steadying as it went, finding the rhythm the dead keeper had built into the lines. Corren listened without moving. At *riders whose eyes have been emptied* he shut his eyes. At *know him by the scar upon his jaw* his hand drifted up toward his face and stopped halfway, as if touching it might confirm something he'd rather leave uncertain. At the end he was silent a long moment.

"There's more," he said finally. "Isn't there. You're holding the page like there's more."

"There's margin notes." Wren turned the book around. "The keeper wrote in the margins her whole life, it looks like. Corrections. Additions. This one's dated sixty years after the main text." He traced the line with one finger, though he no longer needed to look at it. He had already memorized it. He suspected he would be memorizing it involuntarily for years. "*The dragon does not guard the door against the world,*" he read aloud. "*She guards the world against what the Court would make of a door. Pray no one is ever cruel enough to ask her to choose.*"

"Cruel enough," Corren repeated. His voice had gone very quiet. "The Court doesn't need anyone to ask. They're already—" He stopped. Started again. "They burned my city. Not with fire — well, some fire. Mostly they burned it out of the record. There's a machine in the Ashen Court's citadel, we called it the Unwriting, and they feed it names. My sister's name went in four years ago and she walked out the other side a warden. They emptied her eyes, exactly like your book says, and put duty where everything else used to be." He laughed, a terrible small sound with nothing in it. "And I ran, and I ran through the one door in the world they can't follow me through, and I brought them to the last library they haven't burned. Because I'm the map now. Whatever's in my head — the roads, the cities, the people — I'm carrying the last copies of half a country, and I led them straight to the place where the rest of it lives."

"That's why the wardens want the archive."

"That's why they'll take it apart page by page if they get in." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious and frightened, all three at once, and Wren thought: *this is what I invited to dinner. This is what the mist handed me.* "I'm sorry. You should know that first, before anything else. Whatever happens here because of me — I'm sorry."

"It isn't because of you." Wren heard his own voice go strange. Flat. The voice he used for reading inventory. "It says so. Right here. *On the night the lamps burn low.* It was always tonight. You aren't the cause, Corren. You're the courier."

"And you're the—" Corren stopped himself, but the word was already in the room, hanging between them. *Storm-caller.*

"Me." Wren leaned back. The stool creaked. Above them the great lamp guttered slightly — oil low, he'd refill it in a minute, he'd refill it in a minute the way he'd refilled it a thousand times, the way the valley expected, the way the valley *required*, because the lamps were the valley's heartbeat and the heartbeat was his responsibility and had been since he was sixteen years old and knew nothing about himself except that he was owed punishment he couldn't name. "You want to hear the funny part? The prophecy needs a storm-caller whose crime has been forgotten. Whose crime the valley *took*. Do you understand what that means? It means I paid for my sanctuary with the exact piece of myself that would tell me who I am. I have spent nine years trying to reconstruct a person out of habits. I know I like my tea bitter. I know I hum when I trim wicks. I know I flinch at thunder, and until tonight I thought that was ordinary. Everyone flinches at thunder." He smiled, and felt how wrong the smile was on his face. "Except apparently I'm not flinching at it. Apparently it's *recognizing* me."

"Wren—"

"There's a version of me that did something so terrible the elders of a hidden valley said, *send him away, and while you're sending him, take the reason too.* And that version of me is gone. Not dead — dead would be simpler. Dead stays put. He's just *absent*, the way a tooth is absent, and my tongue keeps going back to the gap. Every day. Nine years. And the worst part—" His voice cracked and he hated it and kept going anyway, because if he stopped now he would never say it. "The worst part is I can't even grieve properly. Grief needs a shape. I don't have the shape. I have the hole where the shape was. I don't know if I was cruel. I don't know if I was desperate. I don't know if there are people out there who loved me, or people out there who buried someone because of me, and those are not the same life, Corren, those are opposite lives, and I got neither. I got *lamps*. I got oil and wicks and a routine, and everyone here is kind to me, genuinely kind, and none of them can tell me who I was because the valley ate it. The valley ate me and left the chores."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

Corren reached across the desk — slowly, telegraphing it, the way you move around something wounded — and laid his hand over Wren's where it gripped the edge of the prophecy's page. His palm was warm and calloused and shaking slightly too.

"My sister used to say the Court's cruelest trick wasn't taking memories," he said. "It was making the person carry the weight of them afterward, with nothing inside. She said you could see it in the wardens — they walk like people apologizing for existing, and they don't know why." His thumb moved once, briefly, over Wren's knuckles. "You don't walk like that. For what it's worth. You walk like a man carrying something heavy who decided a long time ago not to complain about it."

"That's not comfort, that's just accurate."

"It's both." Corren almost smiled. Then his gaze dropped to the page under Wren's hand, to the last line, the one Wren had saved for last without meaning to, the way you save the worst news: *then the Sigil of Ironhollow will stand unsealed, and the storm-caller must choose.*

"If the dragon's fire goes out," Corren said quietly, "and the door stands unsealed — what happens if nobody chooses? If the storm-caller just... tends lamps. Forever. Lets the door hang open onto nothing?"

Wren looked at him. Beyond him, through the archive's one small window, the mist lay against the glass like something listening.

"Then the valley finds out," he said, "what it's been keeping everyone safe *from*."

Outside, far off along the western rim, one of the fourteen ridge lamps flickered — not with wind, because there was no wind — and steadied, and Wren felt it go out through the soles of his feet like a skipped heartbeat, and knew, with a certainty that had nothing to do with prophecy, that the counting-down had already begun.
