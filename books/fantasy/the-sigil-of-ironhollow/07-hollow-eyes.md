## 7. Hollow Eyes

The lamp-room smelled of oil and old paper and, faintly, of the rain that had not yet fallen — the promise of weather that never came to Ironhollow. Corren stood at the narrow window with the crate of Sigil records between his feet like a child he was afraid to put down, watching the mist roll along the valley's rim.

It had been three days since the wardens burned their way through the outer veil. Three days of carrying books through the dark, of Wren's hands shaking as they loaded the archive into the cellar beneath the lighthouse-lamps, of listening for boots on gravel. The mist had healed over the wound the compass had cut — the valley always healed — but Corren could feel them out there still, patient as rot. Wardens did not give up. Wardens were not permitted to want anything else badly enough.

"They'll come at the lamps," Wren said behind him. He was trimming a wick with small silver shears, his movements precise, automatic, the ritual of nine years worn smooth as river stone. "The lamps are the valley's eyes. Blind them and the mist goes thin in patches. Maren thinks—"

"Maren thinks a great deal," Corren said, and heard the bitterness in his own voice before he could soften it. He hadn't forgiven her. Perhaps he would; perhaps that was what valleys did to you, made forgiveness feel like breathing. But not yet. Not while Sef might be out there in the ash-colored dark, hollow-eyed and obedient, hunting the last library of Vel Saren because someone had emptied everything else out of her and left only the task.

Wren set down the shears. "You said her name again."

"I didn't say anything."

"You said it anyway. You always do." Wren crossed the room and stood beside him at the window, close enough that their shoulders touched — a thing Corren had learned meant more here than it ever had outside, where touch was cheap and meaning was scarce. "Sef."

Hearing it aloud knocked something loose in Corren's chest. He had said the name so many times in the past days that it had begun to wear smooth too, the way grief wears a name until it fits in the mouth without cutting. But hearing *Wren* say it — hearing it exist inside this valley, this place of lamplight and safety — made it real again, sharp-edged, unbearable.

"She'd be twenty-six now," Corren said. "She liked eel pie and hated being cold and once punched a boy twice her size because he said our mother sang off-key. Our mother *did* sing off-key. That wasn't the point." His voice cracked on the last word and he let it. "The Court took her when she was nineteen. I watched them do it from under a cart. They read her a decree — some list of names, our street among them — and then a man in gray touched her forehead and she stopped being my sister and started being an instrument."

"And you've spent seven years looking for her."

"I've spent seven years running *from* them," Corren said. "Looking for her was just the shape the running took."

Below them, the first of the valley's lamps guttered.

Corren felt it rather than saw it — a wrongness in the air, like a held breath. The flame didn't go out. It went *thin*, its light draining toward gray, and through the window the mist beyond the harbor rocks seemed to lean inward, attentive.

"Wren."

"I see it." Wren was already moving, snatching the storm-shutter crank, but his hands had gone pale around the iron. "That's not how lamps fail. Lamps fail all at once or they drown in oil. They don't—" He stopped. Through the glass, down the crooked lane toward the tide gate, two more lights dimmed in sequence, like candles pinched by an unseen hand. "They don't die one by one in a line."

A line. A path. Something walking toward them through the valley's veins, drinking the light as it came.

Corren grabbed the crate. "Cellar?"

"No time." Wren's eyes flicked to the stair, to the door, to the single high window above the lamp gallery. "Roof. There's a walkway between the lamp towers — we can cross to the cliff stair if we're quick."

"We could also be trapped on a roof."

"Yes," Wren agreed, already climbing. "But we'd be trapped somewhere interesting."

Despite everything — despite the dying lamps and the ash-smell creeping up through the floorboards — Corren laughed, a short helpless sound, and followed him up.

* * *

The night above Ironhollow was not a night at all but a ceiling of mist lit from below by the surviving lamps, so that the whole valley seemed to float in a bowl of pearl. From the walkway between the towers, Corren could see the town laid out beneath them: slate roofs furred with moss, the harbor's black water, the little square where festival lanterns hung year-round because no one in Ironhollow had ever seen a reason to take them down.

And he could see her.

She came up the lane alone, which was wrong — wardens moved in pairs, in threes, a leash of gray coats and colder faces — and she came unhurried, the way water comes into a sinking ship. Her lantern burned white instead of gold. Where its light touched the cobblestones, color simply left them, the moss going bone-pale, the festival lanterns overhead fading like things seen through ice.

Corren knew her by her walk before he saw her face.

Seven years. Seven years of nightmares in which Sef appeared with a hundred different borrowed faces, because forgetting worked both ways and he had begun to lose the original. But the body remembered what the mind had failed to hold: the slight hitch in her left stride, the old break that had never quite healed, from the winter she fell through the millpond ice chasing a dog she refused to describe as stupid because, she said, dogs could hear her.

"Sef," he breathed.

Wren caught his arm. "Corren. Corren — that's a warden. Whatever she is to you, right now she is a warden, and wardens carry—"

"I know what they carry." Everyone did. The stories crossed borders faster than armies: the gray blades that didn't cut flesh but *took* from it, memory by memory, until the wounded forgot why they were bleeding and lay down in the road, contented, empty. "Stay here."

"That is the worst plan anyone has ever proposed to me."

"Then come," Corren said, "but don't stop me."

He went down the tower stair two steps at a time with the crate abandoned above him — let the records burn, let the whole archive burn, there was exactly one thing in this valley he could not replace — and out into the lane where the drained lamps stood like dead men waiting.

She stopped ten paces away and raised her lantern.

In its light her face resolved, and Corren's heart did the thing hearts do when hope and horror arrive in the same instant: it broke cleanly, almost gratefully, the way a bone breaks to be set. She was older. Thin. A scar ran from her ear to her collarbone that he didn't remember giving the world time to make. But it was her mouth, her stubborn mouth, and for one wild moment he thought — *they didn't take all of her, look, she's standing alone, she came alone, maybe some part of her walked seven years to find me—*

Then he saw her eyes.

They were not cruel. That was the terrible thing. They were not cruel, or angry, or even watchful. They were *empty* in the specific way a house is empty after the family has gone — swept, aired, ready for whoever comes next. Looking into them was like looking into a lamp with no flame: the shape of light without any of the warmth. Whatever lived behind those eyes now wore her face the way a uniform is worn.

"Corren Vale," she said. Her voice was level, pleasant, and utterly without self. Hearing your own name in your sister's voice should have been a homecoming. Instead it was the loneliest sound he had ever heard. "You are harboring materials designated for erasure under the Ninth Ashen Decree. You will surrender them. You will surrender yourself. Resistance will be recorded as grief."

"Recorded as grief," he repeated. "That's what you call it? When people fight to keep what they love?"

"It is what the decree calls it." She tilted her head slightly, and something flickered across her face — not expression, exactly. Weather. A cloud passing over a field where nothing grew. "You are grieving, Corren Vale. Grief is treatable."

He took a step forward. She didn't move, didn't tense, and somehow that was worse than a drawn blade.

"Do you know me?" he asked. "Not the name. Not the file. *Me.* Do you know who taught you to swim? Who held your hair back when you were sick? Do you know that you hate the cold, Sef, you *hate* it, and they've got you standing in a frozen valley in the middle of the night and you haven't even noticed?"

Something happened behind her eyes then. Corren would spend the rest of his life trying to describe it and never succeed — not a memory surfacing, nothing so kind. More like a fish rising under ice: a darkness passing below the surface, touching it from within, and the whole sheet trembling with the nearness of a living thing.

"The cold," she said.

"You hate it."

"I—" The lantern dipped, just slightly. Just a degree. "There is a lake. There was a lake. The ice was the color of—"

"*Green,*" Corren said fiercely, stepping closer. "The ice was green because the pines leaned over it. You fell through. I pulled you out. You bit my hand so hard I still have the scar, look—" He tore off his glove and thrust his palm into her lantern-light, the old crescent of teeth-marks pale against his skin. "Look at it, Sef. Look at my hand. You're the only person alive who gave me that scar and it doesn't count as one of theirs."

Her breath came out ragged. For a heartbeat — one full heartbeat — the emptiness in her eyes *warped*, the way heat warps air over a fire, and Corren saw something move behind it, vast and drowning and desperate.

"Corren." Barely a whisper. And in that whisper, buried so deep he nearly missed it, a girl's voice saying: "*finish the sentence.*"

"What sentence?"

But the moment closed like water over a stone. Her spine straightened; the lantern rose. When she spoke again her voice was the decree's voice, smooth and gray. "You will surrender the materials. You have until—" She paused, and her brow creased, and Corren understood with a lurch that she was consulting something inside herself the way one consults a ledger. "One hour. Return to the lamp-house. Remain there. At the end of one hour I will complete my duty."

"One hour," Corren said. "Why? Why give us an hour?"

Her head tilted again. That flicker of weather, that cloud over barren ground. "I don't know," she said, and there — there, for half a syllable — was wonder. Genuine, human, horrified wonder, a crack in the gray, a girl waking for one instant inside her own stolen life and seeing what had been done to her. Then it sealed over. "One hour, Corren Vale. Grief is treatable. Be treated."

She turned and walked away down the lane, and the lamps relit behind her one by one, gold flooding back into the world as though the valley itself exhaled. She did not look back. Wardens did not look back.

But halfway down the hill she stopped, just once, just for a moment, and stood very still with her face tipped up toward the mist — the way a person stands when they are trying to remember rain.

* * *

Wren found him sitting on the walkway stairs with his head in his hands, and sat down beside him without asking, which was one of the thousand quiet mercies Corren had already stopped taking for granted.

"One hour," Wren said.

"One hour." Corren laughed, and it came out wrong, wet and jagged. "Do you understand what that was? That wasn't mercy. Mercy means choosing. There's nothing in there left to choose with. Something *wore her* for a minute — something buried so deep the Court doesn't even know it's there — and it bought us sixty minutes and it doesn't know why."

"Or it knows exactly why." Wren's voice was soft. In the lamplight his eyes had that quality Corren had noticed the first night and had never since been able to unsee: the look of someone perpetually reading a page just past your shoulder. "The prophecy names a storm-caller and a stranger at the tide gate. It says nothing about sisters. But it does say—" he hesitated, "—it says the door opens for the broken key as well as the true one. I used to think that meant a person. Maybe it still does."

Corren lifted his head. Above them, the great lamp turned its slow golden wheel, sweeping light across the mist, across the lane where a gray figure still walked away into the dark, obedient, hollowed, carrying somewhere inside her a lake the color of green ice and a brother's bitten hand.

"An hour," he said again, standing, and the grief in him hardened into something with edges. "Then we'd better find out what she couldn't finish saying. Get the crate, Wren. We're going to read every page of that prophecy tonight — and this time, we start with whatever the Court was afraid enough of to leave a crack in her."

Far down the lane, Sef's white lantern paused at the valley's edge — and did not turn back.
