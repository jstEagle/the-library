## 8. The Dragon Beneath the Lake

The Sigil records did not want to be read.

Wren had discovered this over three nights of work in the lamp-room archive, the crate from the burned eastern storehouse open between them like a wound. The documents inside were old — older than anything else in Ironhollow — and they resisted him in small, maddening ways. Ink faded exactly where he needed it. Pages crumbled at their corners as though touched by a match. One sheet, when he held it to the lamp, showed only his own reflection, faint and warped, staring back at him from a century of varnish.

"They were hidden on purpose," Corren said on the third night, turning a fragment of vellum under his thumb. "Not just stored. *Hidden.* Someone wanted this knowledge hard to find."

"Someone wanted it found by the right person," Wren said. He didn't know why he was certain of that. He only knew the crate had practically fallen into their arms while the archive burned around them, and that felt less like luck and more like intention.

The records told a story in pieces, and Wren assembled it the way he assembled everything: patiently, lamplit, alone until Corren arrived and made solitude impossible.

Ironhollow had not always been Ironhollow. Before the mist, before the forgetting, it had been a sanctuary — a pocket of the world folded shut by its own inhabitants when the Ashen Court began burning names out of existence. The valley's founders had not fled with swords or armies. They had fled with a dragon.

*Emberlain,* the oldest document said, in a hand so careful each letter looked drawn rather than written. *Last of the fire of Vel Saren. She carries the door-key in her breast.*

Wren read that line perhaps forty times. He read it aloud once, quietly, and the lamp-flames all leaned toward him as if listening.

The key to the Sigil — the great seal that locked the valley away behind its curtain of mist — was not an object. It was not a word or a rune carved into stone. It was a living thing. A heart-fire. The founders of Ironhollow had asked a dying dragon to become their lock, and she had agreed, and for a hundred years her slow-burning flame had been the only reason the Ashen Court's wardens could not simply walk in.

And the records were very clear about one more thing: a heart-fire, however patient, however hoarded, does not burn forever.

*When the last light gutters,* the prophecy page said — the same page that had named tonight's date a century in advance — *the storm-caller must choose.*

Choose what? The record didn't say. But it did say where Emberlain slept, and it gave directions in the old way — not north or south, but by lamp-light and water and the fall of shadow at particular hours. Wren spent a full evening translating them, Corren hunched beside him offering guesses about dead kingdoms' measuring systems, and by midnight they had an answer that made them both sit back from the table.

The directions led to Lake Vaelle.

The lake where Wren had lived his entire adult life. The lake whose lighthouse-lamps he tended every single dusk.

* * *

They went down to the shore two mornings later, in the grey hour before the valley fully woke, because Wren could not bear to wait longer and Corren — who had slept badly since the breach at the mist-line — admitted he couldn't either.

The lake lay flat as poured glass. Mist came down to meet it, and where they met there was no seam, no horizon, just a soft dissolution of grey into grey, as though the world ended politely a few hundred yards out. Wren's lamps stood along the near shore on their iron posts, cold now in the daylight, looking suddenly like what they perhaps had always been: markers. Waypoints. A path lit every night for a hundred years by keepers who had forgotten why.

"The third lamp," Wren said. His voice sounded strange over the water, too loud and too small at once. "The record says stand at the third lamp when the shadow of the ridge touches the waterline."

He looked up. The eastern ridge's shadow crept down the far slope, long and blue-grey, crawling toward the shore with the unhurried patience of something that had done this ten thousand times and would do it ten thousand more.

Corren shifted beside him. In daylight, without the archive's candle-glow to soften him, he looked worn thin — hollows under his eyes, the scar on his jaw standing white against skin that hadn't seen enough sun in years of running. He kept glancing at the mist-line the way a man checks a door he knows is unlocked.

"If this is wrong," Corren said, "if we walk into that lake and there's nothing there but cold water—"

"Then we get wet."

"And if it's right?"

Wren watched the shadow slide across the shingle toward their feet. "Then I've been keeping a lighthouse over a sleeping god for nine years," he said, "and I don't know whether to laugh or apologize."

The shadow touched the waterline.

For a moment, nothing happened. The lake breathed against the stones. Somewhere inland a cart creaked, a dog barked, the ordinary sounds of a valley that still believed itself safe.

Then the water changed.

It began at the center — a light beneath the surface, deep down, the color of coals seen through a closed stove-door. It pulsed once. Twice. Slow, and getting slower, like a heartbeat winding down. The grey water took the glow and carried it outward in rings, and where the rings passed, fish rose stunned to the surface, and the mist overhead thinned into rags, and Wren felt something move through his chest that he had no name for — a recognition, vast and grieving, like hearing a song you'd forgotten you knew.

"It's answering," Corren whispered. "Wren. It knows we're here."

The lake floor opened.

There was no violence in it. The water simply drew aside, folding left and right like curtains of heavy silk, and a stairway of black stone descended from the exposed bed into the earth beneath — steps worn concave by feet long gone, slick with a century of silt, glowing faintly where the coal-light rose to touch them. Air moved up the stairwell, warm and smelling of ash and, impossibly, of summer.

Neither of them spoke. Wren took the first step, and Corren followed, and the lake closed over their heads with a sound like a held breath released.

But the water did not come down. It stood above them — walls of it, arched, trembling, shot through with the same ember-light — a tunnel through the lake itself, held open by whatever waited below. Small silver shapes swam past overhead. Daylight filtered down in wavering columns. And the stairs went on, and on, deeper than any temple Wren had ever imagined, until the light above failed and the light below took over entirely.

* * *

The drowned temple was not drowned at all. That was the first thing Wren understood, standing at the bottom of the stairs in a cavernous hall of black stone, dry air, and firelight. The founders had built it beneath the lake, sealed and secret, a vault disguised as a depth. Columns rose into darkness. Along the walls ran carvings worn nearly smooth — processions of figures, a kingdom's worth of faces, all turned toward the center of the room.

Toward her.

Emberlain filled the space the way a mountain fills a horizon. She lay coiled at the heart of the temple, vast beyond sense, her scales the dull red of banked furnaces, and around her body — threaded between the coils of her tail, cradled against the pale underside of her throat — burned a light that was not firelight at all.

It was smaller than Wren expected. That was the second thing he understood, and it broke something in him.

The heart-flame hung in the air just above her breast, a sphere maybe the size of a lantern, burning gold-white at its core. It should have been blinding. Instead it guttered. It flickered like a candle in a doorway, swelling and shrinking, swelling and shrinking, and with every flicker the carvings on the walls seemed to dim in sympathy, and the warm air of the temple went briefly cold.

"She's dying," Corren said. His voice cracked on the second word. He had stopped three columns away and would come no closer, and Wren realized he was shaking. "That's — Wren, that's the key? That little light?"

"That little light is the only reason your sister didn't walk in here last week," Wren said quietly. "That little light has been holding back the Ashen Court for a hundred years." He stepped forward, slowly, hands open at his sides though he couldn't have said why. "And it's almost out."

As he approached, Emberlain stirred.

It was not waking, exactly. It was the movement of a sleeper shifting under great weight — a ripple passing through coils each of which was thicker than the lighthouse, scales grinding softly against stone, the sound of it rolling through the temple like distant rockfall. Her head, when it emerged from the spiral of her own body, was ancient beyond description: horns worn to nubs, eyes filmed half-shut, nostrils wide and dark. She did not lift it. Lifting it, Wren understood, would cost her something she could not spare.

One eye opened. Found him. It was the color of the heart-flame, and it looked at him with an intelligence so old and so tired that Wren's throat closed.

*Storm-caller.* The voice did not come through the air. It arrived inside him, low as thunder heard through a mountain, patient as erosion. *You are late. Or early. The years have stopped meaning much.*

"You know me?" Wren managed.

*I knew your kind before your valley had a name. I felt your storm the year they sent you to us — a small wild grief of a storm, crying in the night. I have listened to your lamps go out and light again, nine hundred nights a year, for nine years.* The eye blinked, slowly, a shutter closing over a furnace. *You keep good flames, keeper.*

"I'm sorry," Wren said, and didn't know what he was apologizing for — the intrusion, the question, the whole weight of what they'd come to ask. He said it anyway. Behind him, Corren made a small sound, and Emberlain's gaze slid past Wren's shoulder, weighing the stranger, finding something there that made her exhale — a long sigh of hot ash-smelling air.

*You have come to ask me about the door.*

"Yes," Wren said.

*Then ask.*

He gathered himself. All the questions he'd carried down the stairs crowded together, and the one that came out first surprised him: "Does it hurt?"

Silence. The heart-flame guttered, steadied, guttered again. When she answered, her voice was softer, and somehow that was worse.

*Every hour, keeper. A flame is meant to give warmth and take fuel. I have given warmth for a century and taken none. There is no wood in this world shaped like a kingdom. I am burning my own memory of it to keep yours safe.* The great head lowered another inch toward the stone. *I remember Vel Saren less each winter. Its streets. Its songs. The color of its rain. I feed them to the flame so the flame can hold your door, and soon there will be nothing left of it but what your paper archives say, and then not even that, and then—*

She did not finish. The heart-flame dipped very low, and for three full seconds the temple went almost dark, and in those seconds Wren heard — or imagined he heard — the mist above the valley thinning, the wards straining, something on the far side of the world pressing its hollow-eyed face against the veil.

Then the flame rallied. Barely.

"How long?" Corren asked from the shadows. His voice was raw. "How long does she have?"

*Seasons. Perhaps weeks. The Court's compass-needles drink at the edges of my fire; every breach they cut costs me more than it costs them.* A plume of smoke curled from her nostrils and rose toward the lake-walls overhead, dissolving. *Your elders know this. Your elder most of all. Maren Voss climbed these stairs eleven years ago and asked me how to make the door stay shut forever, and I told her the truth: the only eternal lock is a willing sacrifice renewed, and I am the last willing thing in this valley.*

Wren's stomach turned. Eleven years ago. Two years after his exile. What had Maren asked, and what else had she arranged?

*You will need to choose soon, storm-caller.* The eye fixed on him again, and this time there was something new in it — something that might, in a younger creature, have been hope. *The prophecy you carry speaks of a choice, and you have not yet understood it, and I cannot tell it to you. The door's terms are not mine to speak. They are written where you will find them when you are ready to hate them.*

"Hate them," Wren repeated.

*All true doors cost,* Emberlain said. *Yours will cost you the thing you least wish to pay. That is not cruelty. That is how the founders built it, so that no tyrant could open it cheaply, and no coward either.* The coils shifted; the temple groaned; somewhere high above, the lake shivered. *But that conversation waits. Today you have only come to look. So look, keeper. Look well. Remember my face, because soon there will be no one alive who can describe it, and a thing forgotten twice is gone forever.*

Wren looked.

He looked until his eyes burned — at the ruined grandeur of her, the furnace-dim glow beneath her scales, the terrible smallness of the light she was spending herself to keep alive. He thought of every dusk of nine years, striking spark to wick along the shoreline, lighting lamps he'd believed were only lamps, never knowing he was tending the outermost candles of a dying hearth. He thought of the mist, and the wardens, and Sef's emptied eyes as Corren had described them, and a kingdom erased so thoroughly that even its dragon was being devoured as a side effect of saving it.

Awe sat in his chest like a stone. Underneath the awe, quieter and heavier, settled the other thing — the thing he'd felt gathering since the night the prophecy named him.

Responsibility. Not the abstract kind. The kind with teeth.

Because Emberlain was dying, and when she died, unless the storm-caller chose — unless *he* chose — the Sigil would fail, the mist would part, and the Court would walk into the last unburned library of Vel Saren and finish its work. And if he chose wrongly, the records suggested the cost might be worse than failure. Everything he loved sat within a half-day's walk of this temple, sleeping soundly, trusting the mist to hold.

The heart-flame flickered again, and this time it did not rally as quickly.

*Go now,* Emberlain murmured, already sinking back into herself, the great eye sliding shut. *Rest while the fire holds. And keeper — when you come again, bring the answer to a question you have not yet thought to ask me.*

"What question?"

But she was asleep, or something like it, her breathing slowing to the pace of geology, and the heart-flame burned on alone above her breast — small, stubborn, spending.

The lake parted for them on the way up as it had on the way down. Wren climbed the worn stairs with Corren's hand gripping his shoulder, both of them silent, and the walls of water closed overhead, and then they were standing on the grey shore in the flat morning light, soaked to the knees in spray, blinking at a valley that looked exactly as it always had and was not, could not be, the same place anymore.

Corren sank down onto the shingle. For a long moment he said nothing. Then: "She remembers the color of the rain." His voice was barely there. "My country's rain. She remembers it, and I don't, and she's *dying*, Wren, and the only thing keeping the door shut is—" He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "We're guarding a library with a candle. And the candle's going out."

Wren looked back at the lake. Beneath its still grey surface, faint as memory, the coal-glow pulsed once — slower than before.

"We need to go back to the records," he said. "All of them. Every page we saved." He was already moving up the path toward the lamps, toward the archive, toward the answer he was promised he would hate. "Because she said the door's terms are written where I'll find them when I'm ready. And Corren—"

He stopped. Turned. The mist hung close and quiet over the water, and beneath it something vast and generous was gutting itself out, hour by hour, waiting for him.

"I don't think I'm ready," Wren said. "I think we're out of time."
