## 9. Maren's Bargain

The council house smelled of tallow and old cedar, the way it had every evening of Wren's nine years in Ironhollow, and tonight that smell felt like a lie.

He had come without being summoned. He had told himself it was about the crate — the Sigil records they'd pulled from the burning archive, now locked behind the council chamber's iron-banded door — but his feet had carried him here on their own, up the crooked lane past Maren Voss's garden, where the rosemary grew in defiant rows against the mist. She had planted that rosemary the week he arrived, a boy of sixteen with empty hands and an emptier head, and she had said, *Rosemary for remembering,* and laughed at her own joke because he hadn't understood it.

He hadn't understood it. That was the thing. In nine years he had never once understood her jokes about memory.

Lamplight leaked under the council door. He knocked before he could talk himself out of it.

"Come in, Wren." Her voice, unhurried, as if she'd been waiting all evening. Perhaps she had. Perhaps she'd watched him circle the lane twice through her kitchen window, the way she used to watch him as a boy, pretending not to count his steps home.

* * *

The council chamber was empty except for her. The long table stood bare, the elders' chairs pushed in, the great seal of Ironhollow — a lamp inside a ring of stone — darkened with dust. Maren sat at its head with a single candle and a cup of tea going cold beside her elbow, her silver hair pinned up in the same severe knot she'd worn since he'd known her, her hands folded like a woman who had rehearsed this moment for a very long time.

"You read the prophecy," she said. Not a question.

"Some of it." He stayed by the door. "The part about tonight. About Corren."

"And the rest?"

"The rest is sealed." He hesitated. "It's written in cipher, or in a language I don't—"

"In a language you don't remember." She unfolded her hands. "Sit down, child."

*Child.* Nine years, and she had never stopped calling him that, and he had never minded, and now the word landed on him like a stone dropped down a well — falling and falling with no bottom to strike.

"I'll stand."

Something flickered across her face — hurt, quickly mastered, and beneath the hurt something older and wearier that looked almost like relief. As if part of her had hoped he would sit, and another part had hoped he wouldn't make this easy.

"The records you saved," she said. "You think the wardens want them because of the Sigil. Because of the door." She turned the cold teacup a quarter-turn, aligning its handle with the table's edge, a small compulsive neatness he had seen ten thousand times without ever seeing it. "They want them because Ironhollow is the last library of Vel Saren. Every name. Every record. Every proof that the kingdom existed at all. The Ashen Court has spent a hundred years making certain that nothing outside these mountains remembers it — no map, no chronicle, no gravestone with the right words carved on it. Forgetting is how they rule. A country that was never written down cannot rise again."

"I know. Corren told me."

"Corren told you what he knows." Her eyes came up, grey and steady and suddenly very tired. "But there are things in this valley that only I know, Wren. Things I have kept for thirty years. And one of them concerns a boy who came to me with his memories burned out of him like a field after fire, and asked me why everyone looked at him with pity, and I told him it was grief, and let him believe it."

The candle flame leaned though there was no draft. Wren became aware of his own heartbeat, slow and loud, the way it got before weather.

"What are you telling me?"

Maren rose. She moved to the chamber's back wall, where the shelves held the council's oldest ledgers, and her hand hovered over a slim volume bound in grey leather — not taking it down, only touching it, the way one touches a wound to confirm it still hurts.

"When you came to us," she said, "you were not sent by accident. You were not exiled *to* Ironhollow. Do you understand the distinction? The kingdom's courts sent criminals beyond the mist to die or vanish. But your exile was arranged differently. Arranged *here*. Arranged by me."

The room seemed to tilt very slightly, the way the lighthouse-lamp room tilted in high wind, and Wren gripped the back of the nearest chair.

"Arranged by—" He stopped. Started again. "You were the one who petitioned for my sanctuary. Everyone said so. Elder Voss argued before the council for three days that the boy deserved—"

"I did argue. Three days. I am very good at arguing for things I have already decided." She drew the grey ledger from the shelf at last and set it on the table between them, but did not open it. "I argued for your exile because your exile was required. Not by law, Wren. By the prophecy."

The word fell into the room and lay there.

"The valley takes payment for sanctuary," she went on, and her voice had gone quiet in the way it went quiet when she spoke at funerals, level and gentle and absolutely immovable. "Every soul that crosses the mist surrenders something. Most surrender a face they can't recall, a name from childhood, the color of a door. Small things. The mist is merciful in its way; it takes what we can spare. But you — you crossed the mist owing it everything. And I made certain the mist collected."

"You're saying—" His mouth had gone dry. "You're saying the valley didn't just take my memories. You chose which ones it took."

"The mist doesn't choose. It takes what is offered. And I offered." At last she opened the ledger, and turned pages until she found what she was looking for, and turned the ledger around so it faced him. "Read your own intake record. Read what was surrendered."

Wren looked down.

The handwriting was his own — a younger hand, rounder, unsteady, the letters of a sixteen-year-old writing fast because someone was telling him to write fast. *I, Wren Brightwater, being of sound mind, do willingly yield to the mist of Ironhollow the following:*

And then a list. Not a summary. An inventory, itemized with terrible care, each line a door closing:

*My mother's voice.*
*The road from our house to the sea.*
*Why I left.*
*What I did.*

His vision blurred. He read the lines again, and again, as if repetition might change them, might reveal some other meaning hiding underneath the ink.

"This isn't my handwriting," he said, though it was. "This isn't— I would remember signing—"

"No," Maren said softly. "That's rather the point."

She closed the ledger. Folded her hands atop it. And then she told him, in the voice she used for teaching children their letters, patient and precise and merciless in its gentleness, everything.

How she had found the prophecy forty years ago, young herself, newly raised to the council, cataloguing the archive the way he now tended the lamps. How the prophecy named a storm-caller, an exile within exile, who would come to the drowned temple when the last dragon-fire guttered, and open the Sigil. How she had believed, for thirty years, that this was salvation — that the door would open onto the restored kingdom, that Vel Saren would bloom again out of the ash, that Ironhollow would be rewarded for its faithfulness.

"And then I read further," she said. "Past the verses everyone quotes. Into the marginalia. The annotations of the archivists who hid these records when the Court came. They had *seen* what lies beyond the door, Wren. Or they had dreamed it, or divined it — it hardly matters. The Sigil does not open onto a living country. It opens onto the night the kingdom burned. A preserved echo. The dead, waiting. And the dead of Vel Saren are not — they are not *gentle*, child. Grief a hundred years deep does not stay grief. It curdles. The archivists wrote that whatever waits beyond the door wants to be remembered, and what wants to be remembered will take remembrance any way it can get it. Through us. Through the valley. Through every soul in Ironhollow who has spent nine years building a quiet life on ground the world has forgotten."

"So you decided to stop it."

"I decided to *delay* it." Her composure cracked, just once, just enough for him to see the woman beneath the elder — a woman who had spent four decades carrying a weight alone and calling it duty. "The prophecy cannot be prevented. I've tried. I've spent thirty years trying. The storm-caller comes whether or not I want him; the stranger arrives on the appointed night; the wardens follow him over the ridge. All of it moves, and I cannot stop any of it moving. But I could shape *who* the storm-caller was when he arrived." She met his eyes. "A storm-caller who remembers his crime would be consumed by guilt. Guilt makes people reckless. Guilt makes them open doors they shouldn't open, to prove something, to atone. So I took the guilt away. I took the crime away. I gave you nine years of peace, Wren. Nine years of lamps and festivals and a trade and friends, with no ghost at your shoulder. Tell me honestly — were those years worth nothing?"

And that was the cruelty of it. That was the knife turned just so. Because they had been worth everything. Because he loved this valley, loved its stubborn lamplit streets and its fog-muffled bells and the particular way rain never fell here and somehow nobody minded. Because Maren had been the closest thing to a mother the mist had left him, and every warm memory he owned had her somewhere in its frame.

"They weren't mine," he said quietly.

"Wren—"

"Nine years." His voice came out steadier than he expected, which frightened him more than shouting would have. "Nine years, and I wasn't a man living a life. I was a vessel you were keeping clean. You didn't give me peace, Maren. You gave yourself time. You kept me like a lamp in a storeroom — unlit, harmless, ready for the night you couldn't prevent. And you called it kindness."

"It *was* kindness!" For the first time in nine years, he heard her shout. The candle guttered; the teacup rattled against its saucer. "You don't know — you *cannot* know what you lost, because I made sure of it, and that is precisely why you may stand there and judge me! I knew your mother, boy. I sat with her the night before you were taken beyond the mist, and I listened to her beg, and I promised her — I *promised* her — that wherever you ended up, you would be safe, and whole, and free of the thing that broke you. And then I read the prophecy, and I understood that safety and wholeness were not mine to give, and I chose the only piece I could still control. I chose your forgetting. I would choose it again. God help me, I would choose it again tomorrow."

The silence afterward rang like a struck bell.

Wren stood very still. Somewhere outside, the valley's evening bells marked the hour — seven strokes, soft in the mist — and the sound passed through him without settling anywhere. He thought of the rosemary. *Rosemary for remembering.* Thirty years of jokes he'd never gotten, planted in rows along her garden path, and he had watered them.

"You took my mother's voice," he said. "Out of everything in the world, you handed the mist my own mother's voice."

"To spare you—"

"Don't." He raised a hand, and the air pressure in the room shifted — a faint, wrong stillness, the kind that precedes a squall, and Maren's eyes widened slightly, watching the first sign in nine years of what slept in him. "Don't tell me what it spared. You don't get to name the cost anymore. You spent thirty years deciding costs for other people."

Her shoulders dropped. All the elder went out of her at once, and what remained was an old woman in a cold room with a dead candle and a ledger full of sins she'd entered in her own careful hand. When she spoke again, it was barely above a whisper.

"Do you know what I prayed for, all these years? Not that the prophecy would fail. I knew better than that. I prayed that when the day came, you would hate me." She laughed, a dry broken sound. "Because hatred would keep you from the temple. Hatred would make you refuse. A storm-caller too angry to act buys the valley years — maybe a generation. Maybe long enough for Emberlain's fire to fail on its own and take the choice away entirely."

"But I don't hate you."

"No." She looked at him with an expression he had no name for — grief and love and terror braided into one. "You don't. That's what terrifies me. Because it means you might forgive me, and forgiving me means understanding why I did it, and understanding why I did it means believing me when I tell you that opening that door will drown us all in a hundred years of the dead's longing." She gripped the edge of the table. "Wren. Whatever the records say. Whatever Corren hopes. Whatever the dragon asks of you. The door must stay shut. Swear it. Here, tonight, with no one watching — swear it, and I will spend the rest of my life helping you live with what I took, and what I did, and you will never have to be the storm-caller at all."

The candle had gone out. Neither of them had noticed when.

In the dark, Wren heard his own breathing, and beneath it — far off, beyond the mist, beyond the ridge, down in the drowned temple where a dying dragon coiled around a key made of her own heart — he felt something answer. Not words. Just heat. Just patience. Just the vast, exhausted attention of a creature who had guarded a door for a hundred years and was so terribly tired.

He thought about swearing. He thought about how easy it would be — how Maren would pour fresh tea, and light a new candle, and they would sit together in the wreckage of the truth and begin the slow work of repair, and the valley would go on, and the door would stay shut, and someday he would die here among the lamps having never learned his mother's voice.

Then he thought of Sef, hollow-eyed on the ridge, holding her brother's face and knowing nothing of him.

He thought of a kingdom erased so thoroughly that even its ruins had been renamed.

He thought: *the dead want to be remembered.* And beneath the fear of that, quieter, undeniable: *they should be.*

"I can't swear that," Wren said.

Maren closed her eyes.

"Then you'll go to the temple," she said. "Tonight, while I can still stop you. Before the wardens reach the lake. Before—" She stopped. Drew a breath. And when she opened her eyes again, the elder was back, and the mother was gone behind it, and Wren saw — with a clarity that hurt worse than anything she had confessed — that she had already decided what she would have to do.

"Maren," he said carefully. "What will you do?"

She straightened the teacup. Aligned its handle with the table's edge. And smiled at him with terrible gentleness.

"Go home, Wren. Sleep. Tomorrow the council convenes, and you'll want your strength." She lifted the grey ledger from the table and held it against her chest like a shield, or like a child. "The rest is an old woman's business, and none of yours."

* * *

He walked home through lanes gone strange with fog, past windows glowing amber, past neighbors who nodded and wished him good evening and had no idea that the ground beneath their feet had shifted. The mist hung close tonight. Closer than usual. He could feel it pressing against the valley's edges the way a tongue presses a missing tooth — and somewhere out in that grey wall, faint as a heartbeat, something cold was testing the seams.

At his door, he stopped. Looked back once at the council house, where a second candle had finally been lit, and Maren's shadow moved against the window — pacing, stopping, pacing. Writing something, perhaps. Preparing something.

*An old woman's business, and none of yours.*

Wren stood in the fog a long moment, and the wind off the ridge carried the smell of cold ash, and he understood, with sinking certainty, that Maren Voss had not confessed to him tonight because she was sorry.

She had confessed because tomorrow, the valley would need to know exactly what kind of man its storm-caller was — and she meant to make sure the council heard it from her first.

He went inside, and packed a bag, and did not sleep.
