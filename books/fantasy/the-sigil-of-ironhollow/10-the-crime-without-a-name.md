## 10. The Crime Without a Name

The lamp-room smelled of oil and old paper and the faint mineral cold that clung to everything the mist touched. Corren had been in it for three hours now, cross-legged on the floor with his sleeves rolled to the elbow, sorting through a crate that smelled like the inside of a tomb.

They had saved one crate. One, out of two hundred, hauled down from the archive in the dark while ash drifted on the wind outside and Elder Voss's people screamed at each other in the square. Wren had chosen it without hesitation — *the Sigil records* — and Corren had not questioned him then. There had been no time for questions. There was time now, too much of it, holed up in the lighthouse while the valley's lamps burned low and the wardens' lanterns crawled along the ridgeline like slow yellow insects.

So Corren sorted papers.

Most of it was exactly what he'd expected: tide tables going back four hundred years, ledgers of lamp-oil, correspondence between keepers whose names had never left this valley and therefore had never needed to be burned. He handled each sheet with the care he'd learned in the scriptoriums of Vel Saren — though those were ash now, every vellum page of them, every inked line, erased so thoroughly that even the word *vellum* felt like contraband in his mouth.

That was what the Ashen Court did. It did not conquer countries; it *unwrote* them. Vel Saren had not fallen. Vel Saren had simply stopped having ever existed, and everyone who remembered otherwise woke up one morning with a hole where their history used to be — or didn't wake up at all, hollow-eyed and marching, carrying lanterns into the last corners of the world where forgetting hadn't finished its work.

Ironhollow was the last corner. Corren had walked through a door that should not have existed to get here, and he had brought the Court behind him like smoke follows fire.

His hands stilled on a bundle of documents tied with grey cord, and he made himself breathe before he lifted the next folder.

It was near the bottom of the crate, beneath the Sigil records proper — beneath diagrams of the drowned temple and genealogies of keepers and a hand-drawn map of the lake with depths marked in a careful, cramped script. The folder was plain. Unlabeled except for a case number: **I.H. 4471**. Confiscated record, sealed by order of the council. The cord around it was knotted by someone with strong, impatient fingers.

He almost put it aside. It wasn't Sigil business. It was none of his business.

But he had spent six years learning to read archives the way other men read faces, and there was something about the way this folder had been filed — not with the council's own papers, but buried here, in the keeper's archive, as if someone had wanted it lost rather than merely stored — that made his fingers refuse to let go.

He untied the cord.

Inside: a trial transcript, pages soft with age, dated sixteen years past. A deposition. Three witness statements. And on top, a single sheet in a formal hand:

*In re: WREN BRIGHTWATER, aged sixteen years, of the Lower Terraces. Charge: unlawful calling of weather resulting in death.*

Corren read the sentence twice. The lamplight guttered in its glass housing, and for a moment the letters swam.

*Unlawful calling of weather.*

He knew Wren Brightwater as the man who trimmed wicks with surgical patience. The man who had taken in a stranger because a book told him to, fed him stew, listened to an impossible story about burning kingdoms with more sympathy than skepticism. The man who, when the wardens came over the ridge, had grabbed Corren's arm and pulled him toward shelter without asking whether Corren was worth saving.

Corren turned the page.

*Deposition of MAREN VOSS, Elder of the Valley Council, taken the ninth day of the Harvest Month.* Her voice came off the page instantly recognizable — that measured warmth, the tone she used in council sessions, the voice that had offered Corren shelter a little too quickly. But here it was colder. Careful. Choosing words the way a surgeon chooses blades.

*I was present at the incident. I will state plainly what occurred. Three youths of the Upper Terraces — Tam Ostell, Bramm Ostell, and Devric Hale — went after dark to the house of SELA BRINDLE, aged fifteen, whom they accused of theft. Whatever the truth of the accusation, their intent was punishment, and their number and manner constituted an assault. Wren Brightwater was present at the house. Witnesses agree he ordered the three youths away repeatedly. Witnesses agree they advanced on him and the girl.*

*What followed is disputed. What is not disputed is that a storm arose in a cloudless sky, localized entirely to the Ostell farm road, lasting under four minutes. Lightning struck three times. All three youths died where they stood. No other property within a half-mile was touched. Not the girl's roof. Not the fence beside which the accused stood. Not the lantern in Wren Brightwater's own hand, which witnesses say did not so much as flicker.*

Corren set the page down on his knee.

His hands were shaking.

He thought of Wren's face when they'd talked by the tide gate — the particular stillness in him, that quality of a man standing very carefully inside a shape that fit him badly. Nine years in this valley and no family mentioned. Exiled, he'd said, though exiled *within* exile, a riddle Corren had been turning over for days without solving.

Now the riddle opened like a wound.

He kept reading. The witness statements contradicted each other in the way statements always do — except where they agreed, and where they agreed was damning. All four witnesses placed the three youths advancing first. All four heard Wren tell them to stop — once, twice, a third time. Two of the four stated explicitly that Sela Brindle had been struck by one of the youths before Wren called the storm; her wrist, her face. One witness, a ferryman named Odd Hale — kin to the dead, Corren noted, and wondered how much weight his statement had carried — claimed the lightning had come *from* Wren, out of his open hand, deliberate as a thrown stone.

And then the verdict.

*Found guilty of unlawful calling. Sentence: confiscation of all records pertaining to the incident; erasure of the incident from valley annals; removal of the subject's memories of the event and its cause, per the Rite of Sanctuary Payment; internal exile to the lamp service, term indefinite.*

Corren read it a third time because his mind refused, absolutely refused, to accept it the first two times.

They had punished *him*. Not the vigilantes — the dead could not be tried, fine, that was how these things always went — but the girl. Where was Sela Brindle in the verdict? Where was any charge against the three men who had gone by night to beat a fifteen-year-old? They were named only as *the deceased*, mourned in a closing paragraph that spoke of "tragedy visiting the terraces" as if the storm had come from nowhere, as if it had no cause, as if the boy who called it had simply been a natural disaster wearing a human face.

And the sentence. Not death. Not prison. Something the kingdom Corren came from would never have devised, because the kingdom Corren came from solved its problems with fire.

They had taken his memory of it. They had reached into a sixteen-year-old boy and scooped out the reason he was being punished, and then they had sent him to tend lamps forever, and he would spend his whole life knowing he was exiled without ever knowing why — a man serving a sentence for a crime that had been surgically removed from him, grieving a self he couldn't retrieve.

Corren became aware, distantly, that he was gripping the transcript hard enough to crease it. He made himself loosen his fingers, smooth the page flat against his knee.

*Fury* was too small a word for what moved through him then. It was fury with a blade's edge and a long reach — fury that wanted names and addresses and a reckoning. Tam Ostell. Bramm Ostell. Devric Hale. Dead, yes, and Corren found he could summon no grief for them, not even the polite counterfeit kind. Odd Hale, the lying kinsman. The council that sealed the record. The elders — *how many of them?* — who had voted to unmake a child rather than ask why a child had been forced to choose between a storm and watching a girl be beaten to death.

And Maren Voss. Deposing witness. Cold, precise, *present at the incident*. The woman who had welcomed Corren with open arms and then warned Wren privately that prophecy named "the exile who remembers." The woman Wren looked at the way sons look at mothers.

She had been there. She had watched a mob go after a girl, watched a boy call lightning to stop them, and then she had signed her name to making him forget he'd ever done it.

Corren sat with that for a long time. Outside, the mist pressed against the lamp-room windows, and somewhere far up-valley a lamp failed — he saw its glow die on the ceiling, one small constellation going out.

Then he heard footsteps on the stair, and the door opened, and Wren came in carrying two cups of chicory and a loaf wrapped in cloth, and Corren understood that he had perhaps ten seconds to decide what kind of person he was going to be.

He decided he was going to be honest. It was the only thing he had left that the Court couldn't take.

* * *

"I found something," Corren said.

Wren paused halfway to setting down the cups. He read Corren's face the way he read weather — Corren had noticed that about him, a keeper's instinct for pressure changes — and whatever he saw there made him set the bread aside slowly.

"You've gone pale," Wren said.

"Case number 4471." Corren didn't pick the folder up. He couldn't make himself touch it again while looking at Wren. "It was buried at the bottom of the crate. Under the Sigil records."

Something crossed Wren's face then — quick, involuntary, gone almost before it arrived. Recognition, maybe. Or dread finally catching up to a question he'd carried for nine years.

"That's not Sigil business," Wren said quietly.

"No."

"Wren—" Corren stopped. Started again. "There's no easy way to say this, so I'm going to say it badly and you're going to hate me for a while, and that's fair."

"Just say it."

"You killed three people." The words came out flat, stripped of anything, because Corren could not afford to dress them. "Sixteen years ago. You were sixteen. You called a storm — a real storm, out of a clear sky, lightning three times — and it killed Tam Ostell, Bramm Ostell, and Devric Hale on the Ostell farm road."

The silence that followed was enormous.

Wren had gone very still. Not the careful stillness Corren knew — this was different, deeper, the stillness of a man whose footing has just been removed from under him and who hasn't yet begun to fall. His eyes had fixed on a point somewhere past Corren's shoulder, on the fogged window, on nothing.

"The Ostell boys," he said at last. His voice sounded strange. Distant, like it was traveling a long way to arrive. "I know those names. Everyone knows those names. There's — there's a story. That they died in a freak storm. People don't talk about it much. It makes people uneasy."

"Yes," Corren said. "Because you were the storm."

"They died in a freak—"

"Four witnesses. All four say the three of them went to Sela Brindle's house at night to punish her for theft. All four say they attacked you when you stood in their way. Two of them say they'd already hit her. Once in the face." Corren heard his own voice rising and did nothing to stop it. "You told them to stop three times, Wren. Three times. And then the sky answered for you, and this council — your council, the people who raised you — tried you for murder, sealed the record, erased it from the annals, took your memories of the whole night as 'sanctuary payment,' and sentenced you to spend your life tending lamps for a crime you weren't even allowed to remember committing. They punished the *victim*. They took a boy who stepped in front of a mob to protect a girl and they unmade him."

He was on his feet now. He didn't remember standing. The transcript hung in his fist, and he held it out, arm rigid, the way a man holds evidence or a weapon.

Wren didn't take it.

He was looking at Corren now, and his face was doing something terrible — cycling through expressions too fast, disbelief and horror and a dawning, sick comprehension, like a man reading his own handwriting in a document he has no memory of writing.

"Sela Brindle," Wren said. "I know that name too. She — she left the valley. Years ago. Didn't she?" He frowned, reaching. "Or she — no. She married a ferryman's son and moved up to the north terraces. Why do I know two versions of that?"

"Because they gave you a replacement," Corren said, and the cruelty of it landed on both of them at once. "A patched memory. A story where nothing happened. That's what sanctuary payment means, isn't it? That's what they took from you — not just the night. They built you a life around a hole."

Wren sat down.

Not gracefully. His knees simply stopped agreeing to hold him, and he ended up on the bench by the cold lamp-housing with the bread untouched beside him and his hands hanging loose between his knees. For a long moment he stared at the floorboards.

"I don't remember any of it," he said. "I keep waiting for it to come back and it won't. It's not like forgetting. It's like — you know when a tooth is missing? Your tongue keeps going to the gap. My whole life has a gap and I've been running my tongue over it for nine years and telling myself it was nothing."

"Wren—"

"Three people." He said it as if testing the weight of it. "I killed three people." Then, sharper, with something flaring up through the numbness: "Did I mean to? Does it say? Did I—"

"It says you warned them three times," Corren said. "It says they were beating a fifteen-year-old girl. It says not one other thing on that road was touched — not a fence, not a lantern in your own hand. Whatever else you are, Wren, you were precise. You were a wall that fell on the right side."

"And the council decided that was worse than a beating." Wren laughed — a short, broken sound with no humor in it anywhere. "Three men go to hurt a child, and the crime the valley can't live with is the storm that stops them. Because the storm was mine. Because it came out of *me*, and they've been afraid of me ever since, and they dressed the fear up in procedure." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious and lost all at once. "Maren deposed. You said Maren deposed."

Corren hesitated. Then he nodded, because Wren deserved the whole weight of it, ugly as it was. "Present at the incident. Her testimony's the coldest thing in the file. And her signature is on the sentencing recommendation."

Wren closed his eyes.

When he spoke again, his voice had gone very quiet, very level — the voice of a man walking a ledge. "She's the closest thing I have to a mother. She taught me to trim wicks. She sat with me when the forgetting was fresh, when I used to wake up at night crying without knowing why, and she held my hand and told me the valley was my home and I was safe. *Safe.*" He opened his eyes. "Was she holding my hand, that whole time, knowing what she'd helped take out of me?"

"I don't know," Corren said. "But I know someone who does."

For a moment neither of them moved. The lamp above them hissed softly. Out on the ridge, through the streaming window-glass, a warden's lantern swung — patient, inevitable, a yellow eye that never blinked.

Wren stood. Slowly this time, deliberately, the way a man stands when he has decided something irreversible. He picked up the transcript from where it lay, read the verdict line once more with his jaw tight, and folded it closed.

"She owes me the truth," he said. "Not comfort. Not protection. The truth, and then I'll decide what she is to me." He looked at Corren, and beneath the devastation there was something new — something hard and clean, the first ember of a fire that had been banked for nine years. "You said the prophecy calls me the exile who remembers."

"It does."

"Then it's time I found out what I'm remembering."

He tucked the folder inside his coat, against his heart, like a coal carried to light something, and went out into the dark toward Elder Maren Voss's house.
