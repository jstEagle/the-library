## 11. Justice Outside the Law

The council hall of Ironhollow had not been full in Wren's lifetime. It was built for a hundred voices — tiered benches of grey riverstone curving around a floor of polished slate, a ceiling ribbed with whale-bone beams salvaged, so the story went, from ships that had sailed seas no one could name. For nine years he had passed its doors on festival nights and heard only the murmur of a dozen elders arguing about grain tariffs and lamp-oil allotments. Tonight every bench was occupied, and the people who could not fit stood along the walls and spilled out into the antechamber, and their breathing filled the hall like weather.

Word had traveled fast. It always did in a valley where memory was currency; a secret spent in one house was known in every house by morning. Corren had found the trial record at dusk. By full dark, half of Ironhollow had read it, or had it read to them, or had heard it shouted across a courtyard by someone who had only heard it shouted across a different courtyard. The truth moved through the valley like dye through water.

Wren stood at the center of the slate floor with the record in his hands. He had read it four times now, and each reading was worse than the last, because each time some small detail settled deeper — the date, the names of the three dead, the testimony of the vigilantes who had cornered him and Sable Fenwick behind the tannery sheds, the sentence handed down by this same council, in this same hall, to a boy of sixteen who had already lost everything a storm could take.

He had killed three people. That fact did not change. He had called lightning out of a clear evening sky because men with staves were beating his friend, and the lightning had gone where storms go, wild and wide and blind, and it had killed three people — two of them vigilantes, and one of them a weaver named Odell Crane who had only come running to see what the noise was. Wren had never learned that third name until tonight. The valley had taken it from him along with everything else.

And they had exiled *him*. Not the men with the staves. Not the ones whose testimony, written plainly in the record's neat clerk-hand, admitted they had gone to the Brightwater cottage intending to drive out the last family who remembered Vel Saren's name. The victim. The child. They had unmade his memories and put him in a lighthouse at the edge of the mist and told him it was mercy.

"Read it aloud." The voice came from the upper tiers — Tam Broadhurst, the miller, an old man with a voice like a grindstone. "Let everyone hear it in your own mouth, boy. No more whispers."

"I'm not a boy," Wren said, "and I've read it twice tonight. So has everyone else." His voice shook. He let it shake. He was done performing steadiness for these people. "But I'll say the part that matters, since apparently saying it is my job now. Sixteen years ago this council tried me for a crime committed against me first. You punished the person defending his friend, and you sent the attackers home to their suppers. And some of you—" he lifted the record, "—some of you signed the confiscation order for my family's land the same week."

The hall went very quiet. In that quiet, Wren looked up at the tiered benches and made himself do the thing he had dreaded all day: he looked at their faces, one by one, looking for knowledge.

He found it easily. Guilt has a texture. Most faces held only shock — genuine, sickened, the shock of people learning something unbearable all at once. But Elder Hesta Rill, who kept the seed-vaults, sat with her hands folded too neatly and her eyes fixed on the middle distance. Old Perrin Odo, who had always slipped Wren honey-cakes at the harvest festival, would not look up at all. And at the head of the hall, in the elder's chair beneath the carved sigil of the valley's founding, Maren Voss watched him with an expression he could not read at all — which frightened him more than guilt would have.

"You knew," Wren said. Not a question. He turned slowly, addressing the whole curve of benches. "Some of you knew. Maybe not all of it. Maybe you told yourselves it was necessary — the exile, the taking of my memories. The prophecy needed a storm-caller who had forgotten his crime, didn't it? Isn't that what she'll tell you?" He nodded toward Maren. "That it was all for Ironhollow. That the ends were holy."

"Wren." Maren rose. She wore her formal grey tonight, the wool robe with the silver clasp, and she stood the way she had stood over every council crisis of his nine years: like a wall. "Sit down, all of you, and listen—"

"No." The word came out of Wren before he knew he'd chosen it, and once it was out he understood that it was true, that he had been choosing it since the moment Corren pressed the brittle paper into his hands. "No, Maren. You don't get to manage this one. You don't get to stand there and be reasonable at us until we're all so tired we agree to whatever keeps the valley comfortable. I have been comfortable for nine years. I have been *so well cared for*. Do you know how that feels now that I know the price?"

His voice cracked on the last word, and he hated it, and he kept going anyway.

* * *

They talked for two hours. It went the way such things always go: in circles, in factions, in the slow grinding discovery that a community is not one heart but many, beating at cross purposes.

Hesta Rill finally spoke, and when she did it was not denial but accounting. "I knew the trial was unjust," she said, her dry voice carrying to every corner. "I voted for the exile anyway. Because the alternative — punishing the Hollis brothers and their mob, in a valley of ninety souls where everyone is someone's cousin — was a feud that would have eaten us alive. I chose the valley over justice. I've paid for it every day since, if that means anything."

"It doesn't," said a young woman near the back — Jena Marsh, the ferry-keeper's daughter. Her voice trembled but did not retreat. "It doesn't mean anything, because you didn't pay. *He* paid. You took his whole self and gave it to the mist and called it balance. My mother says the valley forgets the outside world and that's just how it is, that's just the water we swim in. But this wasn't the valley forgetting. This was *people* deciding what he was allowed to remember. That's not mist. That's us."

Perrin Odo stood then, ancient and bent, and confessed to the room that he had received the Brightwater pasture rights after the confiscation, and had farmed them for sixteen years, and had never once asked himself why a sixteen-year-old's family holding should pass to a man with sons of his own. He offered the land back with tears running down into his beard, and Jena Marsh said, not cruelly, that the land wasn't the point, and Perrin sat down looking like a man who had discovered the shape of a debt too large for any single payment.

And through it all, the second question kept surfacing, rising under the first like a drowned thing under ice: *the Sigil.*

Corren stood at the edge of the hall with the crate between his boots, and whenever the argument about Wren's trial began to exhaust itself, someone would turn to him and ask it again. What was beyond the mist, really. How bad the Ashen Court truly was. Whether opening the door meant bringing the war down on the only place left that had ever been safe.

"They erased a kingdom," Corren said, again, patiently, in his low traveler's voice. "Not conquered — *erased*. There are people walking around right now wearing hollow eyes because they once lived in Vel Saren and the Court couldn't tolerate even the memory. Your archive is the last place on earth where that country still exists. That's why the wardens are coming. Not for me. For the books."

"Then burn the books," someone called out — Aldous Pike, the smith, arms crossed like a closed gate. "Burn them and seal the Sigil and let Emberlain keep her fire. What's a dead country to us? We owe it nothing."

"We owe it *everything*," Wren said, and the force of his own voice surprised him. "Every lamp in this valley burns because of wards laid down by Vel Saren's mages. Every ward. The mist itself is their work. You want to spit on the grave of the people who built the roof over your head because acknowledging them might be inconvenient."

"And what does opening it cost?" Pike shot back. "You've seen the records as well as anyone, Brightwater. The door opens when the dragon dies. You want to walk out through a corpse. And then what — the Court comes through behind us? Wardens in the market square? Hollow-eyed riders collecting our children's memories to feed their Regent's little empire of forgetting? You'd trade a wound we can hide for a war we can't win."

"It's not hiding," Jena Marsh said hotly. "It's dying slowly and calling it safety."

"It's *surviving*, girl—"

"It's being a tomb," Wren said. "A beautiful tomb. With lamps."

Silence again. And in it, Wren felt the fracture happen — felt it not as metaphor but almost physically, the way ice makes a sound before it moves. He looked around the hall and saw the valley split cleanly down its old fault lines: the young and the restless and the guilty-conscienced on one side, Jena and Tam Broadhurst and the ferry families, wanting the door opened, wanting to face the world with their whole history in their arms; and on the other side the comfortable and the frightened and those with the most to lose, Pike and Hesta and a solid block of the older elders, wanting the Sigil sealed forever, wanting Emberlain's fire banked over the door like a stone over a well, wanting the world to stay a story told to children.

Two valleys, suddenly, occupying one set of walls.

Maren had not spoken since Wren silenced her. Now she came down from the elder's chair, slowly, one hand on the rail, and the hall hushed for her out of sixteen years of habit. She stopped one tier below the floor, so that for once she had to look up at him.

"I won't defend the trial," she said. "I signed the order. I took your memories with my own hands, and I will answer for it however this valley decides I must. But hear me, all of you, because I am about to say the thing none of you want said." Her gaze swept the hall. "I have read the Sigil records longer than any of you have been alive. The door opens when the last dragon-fire gutters out. Not when we choose. When it *gutters*. And what the records say waits on the other side—" her voice caught, actually caught, and Wren realized with a jolt that she was afraid, truly afraid, for the first time since he'd known her, "—what waits on the other side does not distinguish between those who open the door and those who merely live behind it. I wanted to spare you that. I wanted to spare *him* that. I was wrong about the price, and I will carry that wrongness into whatever comes. But do not mistake my fear for tyranny. Some of it is simply love, wearing armor."

Wren stood very still. Part of him — the part that had eaten her stew for nine years, that had brought her winter apples, that had let her braid the festival ribbons into his hair when his own hands were too cold — that part wanted desperately to fold into her warmth and be mothered and forgiven and finished. To let the valley decide nothing tonight, to sleep, to wake tomorrow in the soft grey routine of lamps and fog.

But another part, newer and harder and somehow more honest, looked at the trial record lying open on the slate floor and thought: *they loved me too. The ones who voted. They loved the valley, and the valley was easier to love than I was.*

"No," Wren said quietly. "Love doesn't get to decide alone anymore. That's the lesson. That's the whole lesson, Maren, and I had to lose myself to learn it. Whatever we do about the Sigil, we do it knowing everything — the trial, the theft of my memories, the land, all of it — and we do it *together*, or the valley that walks through that door isn't worth the walk."

He turned to face the benches.

"So vote," he said. "Tonight. All of you. Seal the door forever over a dying dragon's heart, or open it and walk into the world with your name in your mouths. But choose it awake. Choose it remembering. That's all I'm asking — the thing you never gave me."

* * *

The vote, when it came near midnight, was close enough to hurt. Fifty-three to forty-one. The door would be sought; the drowned temple would be entered; the Sigil would be answered, if it could be answered, honestly.

But forty-one people walked out of the hall into the fog without speaking to anyone, and Wren watched them go and understood that a number on a tally was not the same as a valley agreed. Pike paused at the doors, looked back at Wren with something that was not quite hatred — something more tired than hatred — and said only, "When the wardens come through, remember that we warned you," and was gone.

Corren appeared at Wren's shoulder as the hall emptied, carrying the crate, his face drawn in the lamplight. "You did it," he said.

"I broke it," Wren corrected. He looked around at the benches littered with abandoned shawls, the slate floor scuffed by a hundred departing feet, the great carved sigil overhead watching everything with its blank stone eye. "Nine years of belonging, and I broke it in one night."

Corren followed his gaze. "Or you found out where the cracks already were," he said. "Better to know. Better while there's still time to choose what holds."

Outside, the mist lay thick and silent over the rooftops, and somewhere far out in it — though perhaps Wren only imagined it — a lantern glowed that was not one of theirs. Above the lake, faint as a dying coal, the light of Emberlain's fire seemed dimmer than it had yesterday. Or maybe that was imagination too. Maybe everything was, now that he knew how much of his life had been curated for him.

He picked up the trial record, folded it along its worn creases, and put it inside his coat, over his heart, where he intended to keep it.

"Come on," he said to Corren. "We sail for the temple at dawn. And gods help us all — we're going to need every hour of it."

Far out across the black water, deep beneath the lake, something ancient shifted in its sleep, and its fire guttered once, like a candle feeling a draft.
