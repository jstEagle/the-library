## 12. The Guttering

The first sign was the birds.

Corren had learned, in his weeks beneath Ironhollow's mist, that the valley kept a small and stubborn population of them — wrens and wagtails, moorhens on the lake's grey margin — creatures that should never have survived in a place where the sky itself was a sealed lid. They lived anyway. They nested in the eaves of the lamp-towers and squabbled over crumbs outside Maren Voss's kitchen window with the particular insolence of animals who have never been told they are impossible.

That morning they were gone.

Every one of them. The eaves empty, the reeds silent, the whole valley holding its breath. Corren stood in the lane outside the archive-house with a crate of Sigil records balanced against his hip and felt the silence press against his ears like deep water.

Then he saw the mist move.

It did not thin all at once. It *peeled* — great slow curtains of white drawing back from the eastern ridgeline like bandages coming off a wound, revealing for the first time since his arrival the raw brown bones of the mountains beyond. And through those gaps, drifting down into the valley on a wind that smelled of cold iron and burnt paper, came ash.

It fell softly. That was the worst of it. It settled on the thatch and the cobbles and the still water of the tide gate like the first snow of a gentle winter, and it was grey, and it was warm, and Corren knew what it was because he had seen it fall before, over the smoking ruin of Vel Saren, when he was seven years old and small enough to be carried. It was the dust of burned things. Burned books. Burned houses. Burned people.

"It's starting," Wren said behind him.

Corren turned. Wren stood in the doorway of the archive-house, and he looked — not afraid, exactly. Resolved, the way a man looks when he has finally set down something heavy and picked up something heavier. His eyes went past Corren to the peeling mist, to the ash sifting down through it, and his jaw tightened around whatever he was not going to say.

"The dragon," Corren said. "Emberlain. Her fire's guttering."

"I can feel it." Wren pressed a hand flat against his own chest, as though feeling for a second heartbeat that wasn't his. "Everyone can, I think. The lamps—" He stopped. Down the lane, one of the valley's lighthouse-lamps — the tall brass ones that burned without oil or flame, fed by the same deep fire as the mist — flickered. Once. Twice. Then steadied again, but its light had gone from warm gold to a thin, watery yellow, the color of old paper held up to the sun.

They looked at each other, and there was no time left for the conversation they needed to have, so they had the one they could manage instead.

"The children go to the lamp-room at the harbor first," Corren said. "It's stone, it's high ground, and it has the strongest light left. Then the archive. We take the crate we saved, nothing else."

Wren nodded once. "I'll get the ferry families moving. Meet you there." He hesitated, one hand on the doorframe, and for a moment the mask slipped and Corren saw underneath it: a boy exiled at sixteen, watching the only home he could remember begin to die around him. "Corren. If this goes wrong—"

"It won't."

"You don't know that."

"No," Corren admitted. "But I know you'll do the right thing with whatever's left when it's over. I've watched you do it every day since I got here. Go."

* * *

The valley broke apart the way a plate breaks — all at once, and along lines no one had noticed were already there.

By midday the mist hung in torn rags along the ridges, and where it parted, the wardens came through. Corren saw the first of them from the harbor road: three riders on the high pasture path, sitting their horses with the terrible stillness of men who do not ride but are *carried*, their lanterns burning with that colorless, hungry light that ate warmth out of everything it touched. Where their lanterns passed, the ash on the grass stopped being grey and became white. Frost spread from their horses' hooves in patterns like dead ferns.

He did not stop to count them. He ran.

The lamp-room at the harbor was an old stone tower squatting on the breakwater, its lamp a crown of brass and glass above. The council had gathered the valley's children there — forty of them, maybe more, packed among the coiled ropes and stacked crates while their parents stood at the doors with pitchforks and boar-spears and faces gone pale and stubborn. Fishermen who had never raised a hand against anything larger than a tuna. Farmers who measured their lives in harvests. They would stand against wardens with those tools, and they would die, and everyone in the room knew it, and nobody left their post.

"Corren." Maren Voss found him at the foot of the tower stairs. Her hands were steady, which frightened him more than trembling would have. She pushed a bundle into his arms: two children, a boy of perhaps five and a girl asleep against his shoulder despite everything, her cheek hot with fever. "The lowland families won't make it here before dark. There's a woman, Tessa, broken leg, and her little ones are trapped in the mill house by the lower weir. The wardens are between us and them."

"How many?"

"Four riders. Maybe more behind them."

"And you're asking me."

"I'm asking the man who crossed a continent to warn us." Her voice dropped. "I'm asking someone who knows what we lose if the Court takes these children. Take Wren's storm if you must — no." She corrected herself, and something moved behind her eyes that might have been shame. "Take whoever will come. Go quickly."

* * *

They went quickly.

Wren came, of course, and Sef.

Sef came without being asked. Corren saw her standing at the edge of the lamplight as they slipped out the tower's landward door — hollow-eyed, ash-grey, her warden's lantern hanging dead at her belt because she had not lit it, because some splinter of the girl she had been had refused. When Corren caught her eye she said nothing. She simply fell into step beside him, spear reversed in her grip, and when he opened his mouth she shook her head once: *don't ask me why.*

He didn't. He understood better than anyone alive why. Some part of her knew him. Some part of her remembered rain.

The mill house sat in the fold of the valley below the lower weir, a low stone building hunched beside water that no longer turned its wheel. Ash drifted on the current like dirty foam. As they came down the slope Corren heard the wardens' horses on the far bank — the wet, wrong sound of their breathing — and pressed himself against the mill wall with Wren and Sef flanking him, and listened.

Two children inside, whimpering. A third, older, singing to them — a cracked, brave little lullaby. Tessa's voice, ragged with pain: *"Hush now, hush, the light will hold—"*

And then a lantern's colorless glow swept across the weir, and a warden's voice spoke from the far side of the water, flat as slate:

"The archive. Give up the records and the small ones may leave."

Corren closed his eyes. He had led them here. Every mile he had run toward this valley had been a mile the Court rode behind him, and now they stood at the door of the last unburned library in the world and made offers in voices like open graves.

"No," Wren called back. Quietly, but the water carried it. "You know we won't."

A pause. Then, from the darkness beyond the weir, more riders than four. Eight. Ten. Lanterns rising one by one like a constellation of cold stars.

"We need a miracle," Corren breathed.

"We need a distraction," Wren said. His hand was rising slowly toward the sky, fingers spreading, and Corren saw — really saw, for the first time — what the boy had been before the valley took his memory. Lightning lived under Wren Brightwater's skin. He could feel it waiting, coiled and patient as the dragon in the lake. "Get them out the back. All of them. I'll give you weather."

"Wren, your exile—"

"My exile is standing ten feet away about to kill children." His eyes never left the weir. "Go."

* * *

What happened next, Corren would remember for the rest of his life in fragments, the way survivors remember shipwrecks.

Sef went through the mill's back door first, spear leveled, and the look on her face was not the warden's blankness and not quite the sister he remembered — it was something raw and new, a person assembling herself out of wreckage in real time, fighting a war on two fronts: the enemy at the weir, and the emptiness the Court had poured into her skull. She did not know why she was protecting these strangers. He watched her not know. He watched her do it anyway.

He carried the smallest girl out himself. She weighed almost nothing. Behind him Tessa limped on a splinted leg with a boy on each arm, teeth gritted, refusing help twice and accepting it the third time. The older child — a girl of nine or ten with ash in her hair — took Corren's hand without being asked and matched his stride, because children always understand emergencies faster than adults do; it is adults who need things explained.

Behind them, the sky tore open.

There was no wind, at first. There was a sound like the world inhaling — a vast, pulling silence — and then the storm came down off the ridgeline like something released from nine years of chains. Rain fell in sheets, warm rain, *living* rain, the first true weather Ironhollow had ever known, and lightning walked the ridgeline in white pillars. Corren heard the wardens' horses scream. He heard lanterns shatter. He heard, threaded through the thunder, Wren's voice crying out words he couldn't distinguish — grief or rage or both braided together, a boy calling a storm with the same voice he'd used at sixteen, when the last time had cost him everything.

They reached the tree line. They reached the harbor path. The rain hammered down and the ash washed away in grey rivers and Corren ran with a sleeping child against his chest and a stranger's daughter gripping his hand, and beside him Sef fought — he heard it more than saw it, the crack of her spear, a warden's cry cut short — half-herself, hollow-eyed, defending people whose names she did not know for reasons she could not name, and Corren thought: *this is what the Court cannot burn. This. Whatever they pour out of us, this comes back.*

* * *

The lamps began to fail at dusk.

Not all at once. One by one, like a procession of funerals. From the harbor Corren watched the lights of Ironhollow go out across the valley — the lamp at the tide gate guttering to a bead of yellow and dying; the lamp on the north ridge flaring bright once, defiantly, and then vanishing; the great lamp above the council hall dimming to embers. Each failure let the darkness in a little deeper, and in the darkness the lanterns of the wardens multiplied, cold stars filling in the constellations the valley's death left behind.

Inside the lamp-room, the children slept in heaps among the rope coils, exhausted past fear. Tessa's leg had been splinted properly. The feverish girl's heat had broken. Small mercies, counted and hoarded like coins.

Wren sat on the stairs, soaked to the skin, hands shaking. Not from the storm. Corren lowered himself onto the step beside him and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the silence wasn't already saying better.

"She's almost gone," Wren said finally. "Emberlain. I can feel it — the way you'd feel a fire going out in the next room. Every lamp that dies is a piece of her heart-flame giving up." He stared at his palms. "When the last one goes, the mist goes. All of it. And there's nothing between the Court and this valley except us."

"Then we don't wait for the last one to go," Corren said. "The Sigil records. The drowned temple. If the prophecy is true—if the door opens when her fire gutters out—"

"If it opens at all." Wren laughed, and there was no humor in it, only exhaustion worn smooth. "Corren, the terms of that door. What it asks. You've read them."

"I've read them."

"Then you understand what I'm walking into."

Corren looked at him — at the storm-light still fading in his eyes, at the ash in his dark hair, at this man who had spent nine years paying for a crime he couldn't remember committing, and who had just called down the sky to save children he'd known less than a day. And he thought of the crate below them, the crate of records he had carried out of the archive with his own arms, and the thing buried in it he had not yet told Wren about. The trial record. The truth of the crime without a name.

*Tomorrow,* he promised himself. *He deserves to hear it from me, not from a page.*

"Yes," Corren said aloud. "I understand. So does Sef." Across the lamp-room, his sister stood watch at the arrow-slit window, spear in hand, staring out at the failing lights of the valley. Half-hollow still. But her knuckles were white on the shaft, and her jaw was set, and when the wind gusted through the slit she turned her face into it with her eyes closed — like a woman remembering, one thread at a time, what rain was.

"Soon," she said quietly, to no one. To the wind. To a country of rain and green hills that no longer existed except in the space between a brother's song and a dying dragon's flame. "Whatever's coming. Do it soon. I can't hold her much longer."

Below them, the harbor lamp guttered.

Above the drowned temple, out in the black water of the lake, something ancient shifted in its sleep, and the last light in the valley trembled like a candle cupped in dying hands.
