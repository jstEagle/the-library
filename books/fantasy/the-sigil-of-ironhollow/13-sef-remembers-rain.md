## 13. Sef Remembers Rain

The storm Wren had called still hung over the valley like a held breath, and beneath it the ash came down in slow grey spirals, settling on the lamp-room's shattered skylight until the glass looked furred with frost. Corren had dragged a workbench against the door and wedged it there with a coil of rope and two barrels of lamp oil he was afraid to think about too closely. Outside, somewhere down the ridge, warden lanterns burned their colorless light through the thinning mist, patient as moths.

Inside, his sister was dying by inches.

She sat on the floor with her back against the great brass housing of the valley's first lamp — the original flame, the one that had burned before Ironhollow had a name for itself, if the plaques were honest. She hadn't spoken since they'd come up from the burning archive road. Her hands lay open in her lap, palms up, as though she'd set down something heavy and couldn't remember what. Ash dusted her dark hair and made her look older than twenty-six, older than the girl who used to steal plums off the Saren estate walls and dare him to climb after her.

"Sef." He crouched in front of her, careful, the way you'd approach a bird with a broken wing that might still take your eye out. "Sef, it's me."

Her eyes came up. Hollow. That was the only word for them, and it was a terrible word because it was accurate — not empty, exactly, but hollowed out, like a gourd with everything scraped away except the shape of what had been inside. Duty lived in those eyes now, duty and the long cold patience of the Ashen Court, and beneath both, so faint he could almost pretend he'd imagined it, something that flinched when he said her name.

"You should have run," she said. Her voice sounded like paper left out in weather. "When I let you go at the wall. You should have run further."

"I know."

"The Court doesn't forget debts, Corren." A twitch at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, not quite the compulsion dragging her face back into neutrality. "One hour. I gave you an hour because—" Her jaw clenched. The tendons in her neck stood out like cables. "Because I—"

"Breathe," he said. "Just breathe. Whatever they put in you, fight it one breath at a time."

"It isn't *in* me, that's what you don't understand." The words came fast now, tumbling, as if she could feel herself going under and wanted to spend what remained of her saying true things. "It's a *hole* where things used to be. They don't fill you with anything. They just take everything out and leave the shape, and then they pour obedience into the shape, and obedience fits perfectly because it was made for the space." Her hands closed, opened. "I can feel the edges of what I lost. Like a tongue finding a missing tooth. There was — there was someone who sang badly. Off-key. On purpose, I think, to annoy me."

Corren's throat closed. He had sung to her every night of their childhood in Vel Saren — badly, deliberately, because she would throw pillows at him and laugh, and their mother would shout up the stairs about sleeping children, and none of them had known, none of them could possibly have known, that there would be a last night anyone sang at all.

"That was me," he managed. "I sang terribly. On purpose."

"I know." And then, barely audible: "*Kestrel* knows. I don't. Kestrel is a name I keep in my teeth like a stone. It means nothing to me and I will not put it down."

The lamp behind her guttered. In the wind coming through the broken skylight, the ancient flame leaned hard sideways, and for a moment the whole room went the color of old bone, and Corren thought — with the clarity of exhaustion — that this was what the end of the world actually looked like. Not fire. Not flood. Just light failing, room by room, while people stood around unable to remember why the light had mattered.

He sat down across from her, cross-legged, ash sifting between them.

"Then hold onto nothing," he said. "Let the Court keep its hole. I'll do the remembering for both of us tonight."

---

He started with the small things, because the small things were all he trusted himself not to break.

"Our house had a blue door. Not painted blue — stained, some dye Father got cheap off a caravan, and it faded unevenly so the top half was darker, like the door had been caught in a rainstorm that only reached its chin." He watched her face. Nothing moved in it, but nothing recoiled either, and that felt like progress. "You hated that door. You said we were the only family in the Street of Weavers whose house looked embarrassed."

Nothing. But her breathing had changed — slower, deeper, the way a person breathes when they stop bracing.

"There was a woman three doors down, Widow Ansel, who dyed wool in colors nobody else could get. You used to run errands for her and she paid you in scraps, and you sewed the scraps into a quilt that never matched anything, ever, and you loved it more than anything else you owned. When the Court took you, Mother burned it. She said she couldn't stand to look at it. I've hated her a little bit for that ever since, which is unfair, because grief makes everyone stupid."

Sef's lips parted. Closed. Her fingers had begun, very slowly, to move — tracing shapes on the floorboards, small repetitive motions like a child drawing in dirt.

"Keep talking," she whispered.

So he talked. About the festival of First Thaw, when the whole city walked down to the river and floated candles on little boats of woven reeds and the water carried them out past the bridge until the current bent and brought half of them back, tangled, and everyone laughed because the river always gave back what it could. About the baker on the corner who put too much honey in everything and lied about it cheerfully. About the day Sef, aged nine, had announced she was going to be a soldier and had drilled in the yard with a broom handle until their father sat down beside her and taught her how to hold it properly, because he believed children should be taken seriously even when they were wrong about everything.

"He wasn't wrong," Sef said suddenly, and her voice cracked on it. "About the holding properly. It's the only thing I remember how to do."

The compulsion surged. Corren saw it happen — saw her eyes go flat and distant, saw her spine straighten into parade rest, saw the hollow places fill with something cold and smooth as poured lead. Her hand drifted toward the short blade at her belt.

"*Sef.*"

"The wardens are converging on the temple," she said, in a voice like a clerk reading a ledger. "The storm-caller must be taken alive. The archive must burn. These are the orders."

"I'm not asking for orders. I'm asking about the quilt."

The blade stopped halfway free of its sheath. Her whole body trembled with the effort of standing still between two commands, and sweat beaded on her forehead despite the cold, and Corren understood with sick certainty that whatever the Court had done to her, it was fraying — that every memory he fed her was a crowbar working at a sealed door, and the door would either open or the frame would shatter, and he did not know which outcome he feared more.

"The quilt," she repeated. The blade slid home. She slumped forward, catching herself on her hands, shaking. "Red. And a green that was really more yellow. And one square of cloth I never identified, blue-grey, soft, worn nearly through. I slept under it until I was twelve and then pretended I was too old for it but kept it folded in my trunk anyway."

Corren laughed, or sobbed, or did some ruined thing in between. "That's right. That's exactly right."

"Why do I know that?" She looked up at him and her eyes were wet and still hollow and somehow both, and the sight of it was worse than anything the wardens had done all night. "Why does the *quilt* survive when I don't? Why can I tell you the exact weight of a blanket and not whether I loved my own brother?"

"Because they took the big things first," he said. "The names. The loves. The reasons. They leave the small stuff because it seems harmless." He wiped his face with his sleeve. "They were wrong about that."

---

The lamp flame sank lower. Down in the valley, another of Ironhollow's lights went out — he saw it through the skylight, a gold spark simply ceasing, and the darkness it left behind spread like ink in water. Three more followed before he finished counting to ten. The mist was failing. By morning there would be no mist at all, and the Ashen Court would walk into the valley the way a man walks into a room he owns.

And Wren was down at the drowned temple, walking into the water toward a dying dragon, carrying the whole impossible weight of a prophecy written a hundred years before anyone now living had been born. Carrying it alone, because Corren was here, hiding in a tower with a sister who might kill him before dawn.

He found himself humming before he decided to. An old tune, half-remembered, the kind that lives in the body instead of the mind — a skipping song, a street song, the kind children in Vel Saren sang while jumping the cracks in the pavement, each line ending on a note that demanded the next one. His voice was rough and it cracked on the high parts and he didn't fix any of it.

Sef went rigid.

"*Where does the river go,*" he sang, "*when the winter takes the stream —*" and stopped, because her lips were moving.

She wasn't looking at him. She was looking through the broken skylight, up past the failing lamps and the falling ash, at a sky she could not see. And she sang — barely, a thread of sound, wavering, uncertain of itself the way a leg is uncertain after long sleep:

"*— it goes where the rain came from, it goes where the swallows dream.*"

The song finished itself. Silence rushed back in. And Sef turned to him with an expression he had not seen on her face since before the Court took her — not hope, nothing so clean as hope, but *recognition*, the stunned animal look of someone waking in a familiar bed after years elsewhere.

"Rain," she said. "It rained there. Real rain, warm rain, you could smell the stones drinking it. Here it's only mist, mist isn't — mist is just fog pretending." Her hand rose to her own cheek, touched the tear track on it with apparent surprise, as if the crying belonged to someone else. "Corren. I remember rain. Why do I remember *rain*?"

"Because it's yours," he said. "They couldn't take all of it. Nobody has room to carry everything, even a Court."

"They're going to take it." Her gaze sharpened, and beneath the hollowness something desperate surfaced and swam. "I can feel it closing again. It comes like — like weather. Like pressure before a storm. Every time something surfaces, the hole pulls harder, trying to swallow it back down." She grabbed his wrist, grip fierce enough to hurt. "Listen to me. Listen. I don't know how long I have. An hour ago I tried to cut your throat and I remember wanting to, I remember it feeling *correct*, and I cannot live in a world where that feels correct. So you have to finish it. What the prophecy started. The Sigil, the door, the dragon — all of it, tonight, before the Court finishes arriving. Because if the door opens, everything they erased comes back. Doesn't it? That's what the records say. The country remembers itself and the forgetting stops working."

"Yes," Corren said. "That's what the records say."

"Then it's the only thing that saves me." She laughed, and the laugh broke in the middle. "Not the fighting. Not the wardens. The remembering. Do you understand? I don't need you to win a war, little brother. I need you to make it so that tomorrow morning, somewhere, someone says the name Vel Saren out loud and the sky doesn't fall."

Down in the valley, another lamp died. Then two together. The dark was climbing the ridgelines now, eating the mist's last pale ramparts, and through the silence Corren could hear — faint, rhythmic, wrong — the sound of many boots moving in unison along the lake shore.

"Wren's already at the temple," he said. "He went down an hour ago. If the terms are what the records claim, he's paying for the door right now with the only coin he has left."

"Then go to him." Sef pushed herself upright using the lamp housing, and stood, swaying, ash sliding off her shoulders. Her hand rested on her blade — not drawing it, just touching it, the way their father had taught her, properly, all those years ago in a yard full of sunlight. "Go now, while the compulsion sleeps. And Corren—" For a moment her face was entirely hers, every year of it, the stolen girl and the lost soldier and the sister, all of them crowded into one look. "When you get our country back — sing at the funeral. Badly. On purpose. Promise me."

"I promise."

She smiled. It was the worst smile he had ever seen, brave and breaking, a candle cupped in a hand already opening to let the wind take it. "Then run, storm-chaser. The rain's waiting on you."

And far below, at the edge of hearing, beneath the boots and the dying lamps and the settling ash, Corren heard the first low grumble of thunder roll up out of the drowned temple — and knew that whatever price the Sigil had asked, Wren Brightwater had begun to pay it.
