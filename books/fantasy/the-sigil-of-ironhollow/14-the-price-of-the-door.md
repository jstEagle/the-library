## 14. The Price of the Door

The drowned temple did not look like a temple. It looked like a wound that the valley had learned to live around — a bowl of black water sunk into the hillside east of the lamp-road, ringed by stones so old their inscriptions had worn to whispers. Mist lay on its surface the way it lay on everything in Ironhollow, but here the mist was thicker, almost reverent, as if it had been standing guard for a very long time and had forgotten how to do anything else.

Wren stood at the water's edge with his boots sinking into cold mud, the Sigil records open against his chest, and felt the valley hold its breath.

Behind him, Corren crouched over the last page they'd copied by lamplight in the archive's ruin, his fingers black with ink, his jaw scar catching the grey light. *Beneath the still water, the door beneath the door,* the record read. *She sleeps coiled around the key, and the key is her heart.*

Emberlain slept below. Wren could feel her now — not hear, not see, but feel — a slow heat under the cold, like a banked fire in a house where everyone has gone to bed. The whole valley rested on that heat. The mist, the lamps, the forgetting itself: all of it fed from a dying dragon's final stubborn flame.

"She knows we're here," Corren said quietly.

"I know."

"How?"

Wren didn't have an answer he liked. He only knew that the water had changed when they arrived, that the ripples moved wrong, that the cold coming off the surface had a listening quality to it. Nine years in Ironhollow taught a person to notice when the valley paid attention to them. Usually it happened only once or twice a year, on the festival nights, when the lamps burned brightest and the mist seemed almost fond. This was not fondness.

He waded in before he could talk himself out of it.

The cold was total. It climbed his legs like hands, then his waist, then his chest, and at some point between one step and the next the water stopped being water — or he stopped being in it — and he was walking down a stair that had no right to exist beneath a lake, dry stone under his feet, air in his lungs, light rising from somewhere far below like the memory of dawn.

Corren came through the surface behind him without even getting wet, which told Wren everything about whose country this stair belonged to. It wasn't the valley's.

* * *

The drowned temple was not drowned at all.

It stood in a vast hollow beneath the lake, whole and waiting, its pillars carved with dragons in flight, its ceiling lost in darkness above. And at its center, coiled around something Wren couldn't yet see, slept the last dragon of Vel Saren.

She was dying. There was no gentler way to say it, no softer shape to put the truth in. Her scales — bronze, once, Wren thought, bronze and gold, the color of the kingdom she had died defending — were dull now, flaking at the edges, going grey as ash. She was smaller than the carvings suggested her kind should be, shrunk by centuries of burning low instead of bright. Her ribs rose and fell with the patience of mountains. Every breath sent a faint shimmer through the air, and every shimmer made the mist above the lake thicken, and every thickening kept the Ashen Court one more day away from everything Wren loved.

Her eyes opened. They were the color of embers at the very end of a long night — mostly dark, with just enough light left in them to prove there had once been a blaze.

"Storm-caller," Emberlain said. Her voice was wind over ruins, wind over the burnt streets of a country Wren had never seen. "You took your time."

"You knew I was coming."

"I have known since before you were born." A plume of smoke curled from her nostrils, thin and grey. "That is the trouble with prophecy. It makes waiting bearable and hope unbearable, and it never tells you which is which."

Corren stopped at the edge of the pillar-shadow, keeping a respectful distance, though his eyes were wide and wet and fixed on her. Wren understood. Everyone Corren had ever loved had been taken apart by the Court; here was the living proof that the Court could be starved, that its power had a source and the source had a heartbeat, however faint.

"The Sigil," Wren said. His voice cracked on the word. "The records say you guard the key. That your fire keeps the door sealed."

"Sealed against them," Emberlain agreed. "And sealed against everyone else. A door locked against wolves is also locked against the starving." Her great head shifted, and Wren saw what she coiled around: a stone set into the temple floor, circular, wide as a wagon wheel, carved so densely with script that it looked solid black until the light of her breath touched it and the letters woke, moving slowly, like fish under ice.

The Sigil of Ironhollow. Not a wall. Not a ward. A door.

"It opens," Emberlain said, "for one who returns what the valley took."

Wren's mouth went dry. "What does that mean?"

"You are the exile who remembers. You have heard that much, I think." The dragon's ember-eyes held him without mercy. "Then you know the valley took something from you. Your crime, your trial, your reasons. Payment for sanctuary. That was the bargain Maren Voss struck with the mist nine years ago, and the mist has always collected honestly. It takes, and it keeps, and nothing it keeps is destroyed."

"Then it can be given back."

"No." The word landed heavy as a collapsed roof. "It cannot be returned to *you*. What the valley takes, it keeps forever — that is the covenant, older than me, older than the kingdom that wrote it. But a covenant has terms, and terms can be met." She drew breath, and the whole temple dimmed as if the air itself were being pulled toward her. "The Sigil opens for one who returns what the valley took. Not to himself. To the door. A life given over, whole and entire. Everything Ironhollow ever erased from you — and everything it gave you after — surrendered together."

Wren heard the words. He understood each one separately. Together they refused to make sense, the way a familiar room refuses to make sense in a dream.

"Everything," he repeated.

"Your memories of the world outside. The ones the valley took when you were sixteen." Emberlain's voice gentled, which somehow made it worse. "And your memories of the valley itself. The lamps. The festivals. Elder Voss. The friends you made. The man you—" She paused, and smoke curled thoughtfully. "The people you love. All of it. The Sigil does not distinguish between what was stolen and what was chosen. To it, a life is a life. It opens for the whole thing or not at all."

The silence afterward was enormous. Wren became aware of his own heartbeat, absurdly loud, counting out seconds in a place where time had been standing still for a century.

"And then?" he managed. "I walk through the door and I'm — empty? A stranger wearing my own face?"

"You walk through the door knowing nothing," Emberlain said. "Not why you fought. Not whom you fought for. Not what it cost. You will stand in a country that no longer exists, among dead who need to be remembered, and you will not remember them either — unless someone teaches you again, name by name, grief by grief." The dragon lowered her head, and her breath stirred his hair, warm as a hearth, terrible as a funeral pyre. "That is the price of the door, storm-caller. Not your blood. Not your magic. Your *self*. The Court built its empire on making people forget. The door out of its empire asks the same toll. The difference is only this: the Court forgets *for* you, and keeps what it takes. The door lets someone carry what you give up, out into the world, where it can be kept alive."

"Someone," Wren said. "Carried by someone."

"If such a person existed. If they were willing. If they understood what they would be holding." Emberlain's eyes closed briefly. "But I am an old creature and I will not lie to you even to spare you. There may be no such person. There may be no time to find them. The Court's wardens crossed the outer mist three days ago. My fire gutters. When it goes out, the door seals permanently — and so does every soul inside this valley, yours included, behind a lock the Ashen Court will spend a thousand years learning to pick."

* * *

He didn't remember climbing back up the stairs. He was simply suddenly at the surface, gasping in ordinary cold lake-water, Corren hauling him by the arms onto the stony shore, both of them soaked and shaking in the grey afternoon light while the mist closed overhead like a lid.

"Wren. Wren, look at me. What did she say?"

He looked. That was the problem. He looked at Corren's face — the scar on the jaw, the ink stains, the eyes that had walked through a door that shouldn't exist and hadn't stopped running since — and tried to imagine it blank. Tried to imagine meeting this man on the tide gate tonight, nine years ago, and feeling nothing. No recognition from the prophecy's illustration. No wonder. No fear shared. No hand pulling him out of cold water.

Tried to imagine Maren's kitchen and feeling nothing. The smell of cedar smoke and barley bread. Her hands, knotted and strong, wrapping his in hers the first winter and saying, *you're mine now, boy, whatever anyone says.* The woman who had unmade him and the woman who had remade him were the same woman, and if he gave up these memories he would never get to forgive her, because forgiveness requires remembering what was forgiven.

Tried to imagine the lamps. Two hundred years of lamps, lit every dusk by keepers who had inherited the work from their mothers, the little flames that were the valley's only weather, its only stars. He had tended them for nine years. They were the closest thing to a prayer he had.

"They want everything," he said.

Corren waited.

"The Sigil doesn't take my life. It takes my *having lived* it. Everything Ironhollow erased from me when I was exiled — my crime, my home before, all of that goes to the door. And everything since." He laughed, and hated the sound of it. "Corren, I wouldn't know why I was doing any of it. I'd stand in front of the ghost of a burned kingdom and I wouldn't know what I owed it. Someone would have to tell me I loved them. Do you understand? Someone would have to *tell me.*"

Corren went very still. Water dripped from his hair. Somewhere above them, distantly, a lamp on the ridge flickered — Wren felt it the way he felt a missed heartbeat — and neither of them said what that meant. The mist was thinning. The wardens were closer. There was no time, exactly as the dragon had promised, and less of it every hour.

"There might be another way," Corren started.

"Don't." Wren pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "Don't give me hope with holes in it. I've done the arithmetic already, Corren. There's me — what's left of me, the part that remembers being robbed and chooses to fight anyway. And there's everyone in that valley, and everyone your Court ever erased, and there's a dying dragon who has been holding a door shut for a hundred years out of duty to people who are all dead." He lowered his hands. "I know what the answer is. I've known since she said it. I just wanted — a minute. I wanted one minute of being allowed to grieve myself while I'm still me."

Corren sat down beside him in the mud. He didn't argue. He didn't offer comfort he couldn't deliver. He just sat, shoulder against Wren's shoulder, and the two of them watched the mist breathe over the black water, and Wren committed things to memory deliberately, furiously, the way a man counts coins he knows he's about to lose: the weight of Corren leaning against him. The sound of the lake. The grey-green smell of the valley in autumn. *This,* he told himself. *This is what you're buying with yourself. Hold it once, hard, before you pay.*

"Tonight," he said finally. "At moonrise. Before the fire fails."

"Wren—"

"Find me another way by moonrise," Wren said. "I'll wait until moonrise. After that, I go down and I pay."

Corren looked at him for a long moment, and something moved behind his eyes — something urgent and half-formed, a thought he was turning over like a stone looking for what lived underneath it.

"Wait here," Corren said, standing, wringing out his sleeves. "There's something in the crate. Something I saw and didn't understand until now. Give me until moonrise."

He ran. Wren watched him go up the lamp-road, small and fast against the greying sky, and felt the first cold drop of the truth settle into him: that whoever Corren was to him — whatever the last weeks had been building, word by word, danger by danger — in a few hours Wren Brightwater would look at this man and see a stranger.

Unless.

Unless Corren found the hole in the dragon's terms. Unless hope had fewer holes in it than Wren feared.

Above the lake, far off on the northern ridge, a second lamp guttered and died. Then a third.

The mist was coming apart like old cloth, and the price of the door was due at moonrise, and Wren sat alone at the water's edge memorizing the taste of the air, because he suspected — with a certainty that felt like prophecy's cold twin — that he would soon be asked to remember a night he was about to forget.
