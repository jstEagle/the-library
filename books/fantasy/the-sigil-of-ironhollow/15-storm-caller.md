## 15. Storm-Caller

The water of the drowned temple had gone still as glass, and Wren knew that was the worst sign it could give him.

For nine years the lake at Ironhollow's heart had breathed — a slow swell and fall against the sunken steps, like something vast turning in its sleep. The old folk called it the valley's pulse and hung their luck-charms on it without knowing what they were honoring. Now the surface lay flat and gray, reflecting the broken roof of the temple above the waterline, and every ripple that should have been there had gone out of the world.

*Emberlain is dying,* Wren thought, standing on the flooded threshold with Corren's lantern guttering beside him. *And when she goes, everything she held shut comes through.*

He could feel it already — not hear it, exactly; the mist never let sound travel far — but feel it, the way you feel a storm building behind mountains before the first wind arrives. A pressure against the far edge of things. The hollow-eyed riders were coming through the thinning veils of fog the way water comes through a failing dam: not all at once, but everywhere at once, patient and certain.

"Wren." Corren's voice came low across the water. He stood a few steps back along the causeway, the crate of Sigil records strapped to his back, Sef's hour-long mercy long since spent. "The lamps on the ridge road just went out. All of them. Maren's signal."

Wren closed his eyes. Nine years of lighting those lamps himself, climbing the lighthouse stairs each dusk with oil and wick and a small stubborn ritual that had been the only fixed point in an exile he couldn't remember earning. The lamps going dark was not a signal. It was a surrender of the skyline.

"She's coming down here," Wren said.

"To help us?"

"To buy us time." He opened his eyes. "There's a difference."

The temple rose around them as they waded inward — columns drowned to half their height, their carvings worn smooth by a century of water, though here and there the Sigil records had taught Wren what the stone once said. *Here stood Vel Saren.* *Here the door was sealed.* *Here the last fire keeps faith.* The words had been chiseled by people who believed the kingdom they named would last forever, and now the kingdom existed only in this room, in a crate of paper, in a dragon's failing heart.

At the center, where the water deepened to Wren's waist, the altar rose black and wet, and coiled upon it — half-submerged, vast as a hillside, her scales dulled from bronze to ash-gray — slept Emberlain.

She did not wake. But as Wren drew closer, her eye slid open beneath its lid, one sliver of ember-orange in all that grayness, and found him. There was no accusation in it. That was somehow worse than if there had been. Only recognition, ancient and tired, as if she had known for a hundred years that someone would eventually come down these steps, and had simply been waiting to learn who.

"I know," Wren said softly, uselessly, because there was nothing else to say yet. "I know. Hold on."

Above them, faint through the broken dome, the sky over Ironhollow began to change color. Not toward sunset. Toward nothing — a bleaching, a draining, the particular colorlessness Corren had described from the valley's edge when the wardens touched the mist. Ash falling upward instead of down.

"They're inside," Corren said. His hand found Wren's shoulder. "Whatever we're doing, we do it now."

* * *

They heard Maren Voss before they saw her.

It came up through the drowned colonnade like a struck bell — her voice, cracked and old and absolutely clear, pitched to carry across water the way only a woman who had spent forty years commanding council chambers could pitch it.

*"You will go no further."*

Then light — not lamp-light, not firelight, but the cold blue-white flare of wardens' lanterns, reflected off the ceiling of the temple in shivering sheets — and the sound of many boots on stone.

Wren hauled himself up onto the altar's flank, water streaming from his clothes, and looked back through the colonnade. They were coming along the causeway in a line: eight, ten, twelve figures in ash-colored coats, faces smooth and empty as eggs, their lanterns burning that wrong white flame. And before them, arms spread wide, standing between the wardens and the altar with nothing but her cane planted on the wet stone — Maren.

Behind her, to Wren's astonishment, stood others. Old Hollis the smith, who had never in nine years said a kind word to Wren. The twins Dara and Sen, barely twenty, who ran the ferry. Half a dozen more, elders and commoners both, the ones who had voted with him two nights past, the ones who had chosen to face the world honestly rather than seal it forever over Emberlain's corpse. They had come down into the temple armed with ferry-hooks and iron rods and, in Hollis's case, a hammer meant for horseshoes.

Against hollow-eyed wardens of the Ashen Court, it was nothing. Against them, it was everything anyone had.

"Maren!" Wren's voice broke across the water. "Get out of the way — get them out—"

She didn't turn. She was looking at the lead warden, and even from thirty feet Wren could see that she wasn't afraid, and that this frightened him more than anything else that night.

"I know what you are," Maren said to the warden. Her voice did not shake. "I've known since I was a girl, when my grandmother told me why the valley forgets. You are what comes after forgetting. You are the thing forgetting exists to keep out." She lifted her chin. "Well. I made mistakes in keeping this place safe. I unmade a boy to fit a prophecy. I thought safety was worth any price paid by someone else. But I'll tell you what I learned too late, creature—" and here her voice finally cracked, but only with fury, "—a life bought with stolen memory isn't a life. It's just a quieter version of what you do."

The warden tilted its head. When it spoke, its voice was the sound of paper burning — soft, sibilant, without anything behind it.

"The archive. The key. The storm-caller."

"Come and take them," Maren said.

What happened next happened fast, and Wren would spend the rest of his life — however much of it there was — replaying it in fragments, because the whole of it was unbearable.

Hollis went first, swinging his hammer at a warden's knee, and the warden caught the hammer's haft one-handed and simply *held*, and the twins' hooks flashed, and the blue-white lanterns swung, and where the cold flame touched people they folded up like cut marionettes — not dead, Corren had told him once, worse than dead, emptied — and Maren Voss raised her cane like a sword and struck the lead warden across the face with it, once, twice, a third time, shouting words Wren couldn't hear over the ringing in his own ears, and then—

—and then the lead warden took her by the throat, almost gently, the way you lift something fragile, and its lantern flared, and Maren's cane dropped into the black water with a sound very like a bell.

"No!" Wren was moving before he knew it, hauling himself along the altar, screaming her name into a temple that swallowed sound. "MAREN!"

Her eyes found his across the water. Whatever the warden's grip was doing to her, whatever cold white thing was pouring into her, for one moment longer her gaze stayed clear — stayed *hers* — and her mouth shaped words with the last strength she had.

Wren read them rather than heard them. He would always be grateful for that, and he would always be destroyed by it.

*Remembering isn't the same as keeping.*

Then the light took her, and Elder Maren Voss — who had signed his exile papers, who had smuggled honey-cakes to the lighthouse every midwinter for nine years, who had stolen his memories to save the valley and confessed it with tears on her hands — went slack in the warden's grip like a coat off a hook, and her eyes, when her head lolled, were beginning to go hollow.

Something in Wren's chest tore open.

* * *

Later — in the years after, when he told this part of the story to students in the rebuilt border town — he would struggle to describe what it felt like. He would say it was like a door opening. He would say it was like drowning. He would say it was like the moment a held breath finally breaks. None of it would be true, and all of it would be true, and the students would nod because there are some things language can only circle.

What it actually was, was nine years of exile ending all at once.

Because here was the truth the valley had never understood about Wren Brightwater, the truth the trial records had revealed and the prophecy had required and Maren's theft had buried: he had not lost his power when they sent him away. He had only lost permission. For nine years he had lived under a sentence he couldn't remember committing, in a valley where magic could not follow, tending lamps and mending nets and telling himself he was content, and underneath all of it, deeper than the stolen memories, older than the theft, the storm had waited. Storms are patient. Storms remember what people forget.

Maren's body sagged in the warden's grip. The wardens advanced along the causeway. Emberlain's ember eye watched him from the water, and the pressure against the edge of the world leaned in, and somewhere above the broken dome the last lamps of Ironhollow went out forever.

And Wren Brightwater stopped holding his breath.

It began in his hands. A prickling, then an ache, then a singing, as if every scar he'd earned hauling ropes and splitting wood had been a seam holding something in. The water around the altar began to move — not toward the wardens, not away from them, but *around*, in slow spirals, drawn up into shapes the lake had never made. Above the broken dome the clouds — the real clouds, the first clouds ever permitted over Ironhollow, because the mist was failing and the sky was coming in — began to turn.

Corren grabbed his arm. "Wren. Wren, your eyes—"

"Let go," Wren said, and his voice came out doubled, layered, his own voice and something vaster speaking through the same throat. "Let go, Corren."

Corren let go. And stepped back. And did not run.

The first thunder rolled not from the sky but from the lake itself, up out of the drowned temple, out of the bedrock, out of the bones of a kingdom everyone had tried to erase, and the wardens stopped. All of them, all at once, the way animals stop. Their smooth faces turned toward the altar, toward the boy standing on the dragon-tomb with water writhing up his arms in bright ropes, and for the first time in a hundred years of erasure, the hollow eyes looked at something they could not hollow.

"You came for a storm-caller," Wren said. The rain began then — the first rain in Ironhollow's history, fat and warm and salt-tasting, falling through the broken roof in silver shafts. "You were promised one. Here it is."

He raised his hands, and the storm answered.

Not gently. Not cleanly. This was grief given weather; this was nine years of exile and a mother's betrayal and an old woman's sacrifice and three names from a trial record he still couldn't read without shaking, all of it torn open and poured into the sky. Lightning walked the colonnade. The lake rose in a standing wave and broke over the causeway, tearing wardens from their feet, dousing their cold lanterns in ordinary water, and where the white flames died the wardens staggered as if strings had been cut. The wind came down through the broken dome in a spiral and stripped the ash-color from the air and filled the temple with the smell of rain on hot stone, a smell no one in this valley had ever smelled, a smell that belonged to a country that no longer existed except in a crate of paper and a dying dragon's heart and, now, in this.

The wardens fell back. Some fled. One — the leader, the one who had held Maren — stood its ground in the flooding causeway and lifted its lantern against the rain, and the rain simply put it out, drop by drop by drop, the way truth puts out a lie: slowly, and then completely.

When the last white light died, the warden looked down at its own empty hands with an expression Wren recognized. Confusion. The beginning of horror. The first flicker of a question — *what am I? what was I?* — rising in eyes that were, just possibly, starting to be eyes again.

Then the storm carried it away down the tunnel and out into the night.

* * *

Silence, afterward. Rain-silence — the soft percussion on stone, the drip from the broken dome, the lake settling back into its basin. Wren knelt on the altar, soaked and shaking, his hands pressed flat against the wet black rock, and the power drained out of him slowly, leaving him hollowed and scoured and more awake than he had ever been.

Corren reached him first. He didn't say anything — there was nothing to say — just crouched at the altar's edge with rain streaming off his jaw, close enough that Wren could see he was crying and pretending not to.

"She held them," Wren said. His voice was wrecked. "She knew what they'd do and she stood there anyway. Nine years I hated her for the exile, and then I forgave her, and then I hated her again for what she stole, and she—" His breath hitched. "She stood in front of me, Corren. She stood in front of me."

"I know."

"'Remembering isn't the same as keeping.'" Wren stared at the water where her cane had sunk. "What does that mean? What did she mean?"

But even as he asked, some part of him already knew — the part that had felt the Sigil's terms pressing against him in the records, the part that had read the price of the door and gone cold. Remembering wasn't the same as keeping. You could hold a thing in your mind your whole life and lose it anyway. You could give a thing away and have it kept for you, somewhere, by someone willing to carry it. Memory wasn't a possession. It was a trust.

A groan from the causeway. Hollis, alive, dragging himself upright with a ferry-hook for a crutch. Then Dara, coughing lake water. Two others stirring. Four who did not stir at all, and would not, and whose names Wren made himself kneel and learn, right there in the rain, because remembering wasn't the same as keeping, and he intended to do both.

And then the water at the center of the temple moved.

Not the storm's motion — something slower, deeper, vast. The lake swelled outward in rings from the altar, and Emberlain uncoiled by a single scale-length, her head rising dripping into the rain-light, and for the first time in a hundred years the dragon of Vel Saren looked fully upon the world she had been guarding it from.

Her voice, when it came, was not sound so much as heat — a dry crackle that arrived in the chest before the ears, like standing near a forge.

*Storm-caller.* The ember eye fixed on Wren, and dimmed, and steadied. *You are late. I have been dying since before your grandmother was born.*

"I'm sorry," Wren whispered. Rain ran down his face; he couldn't tell which drops were tears. "We came to free you. We came as fast as we could."

*Free me.* Something that was not quite a laugh moved the ruined cavern of her ribs. *Little caller. Come closer, then. Come and see what freedom costs, and what it buys — and tell me whether you have the courage to pay for a stranger's peace.*

Above them, through rain and wreckage and the first true clouds Ironhollow had ever known, dawn was still hours away. But below, in the drowned dark, the altar beneath Wren's knees had begun, very faintly, to glow — and the Sigil of Ironhollow was waking, and it wanted to talk about the price.
