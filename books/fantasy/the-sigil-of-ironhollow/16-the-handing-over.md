## 16. The Handing Over

The temple was drowning by inches.

Not with water — the lake had gone still as poured glass the moment Wren stepped onto the causeway — but with light. It drained out of everything. The drowned columns that had glowed with Emberlain's reflected fire now stood gray as bone. The carvings of the Sigil along the walls, which Corren had seen breathing with slow golden pulses on their first descent, had gone dark one ring at a time, like a city losing its districts to a rolling blackout. And in the center, where the altar rose from the shallow pool in a fan of carved stone petals, Wren stood alone, and the darkness was gathering around him like a tide coming in.

Corren ran.

He had run a great deal since the night he'd walked through a door that shouldn't exist — from wardens, from burning libraries, from his own guilt — but this run was different. His lungs burned with cold lake air and something worse than exertion. He had left Wren an hour ago with the Sigil's terms still ringing in both their ears, and he had spent that hour doing the only thing he knew how to do: reading.

*The door opens for one who returns what the valley took.*

Wren had understood it immediately. That was the cruelest part. He hadn't argued, hadn't raged, hadn't even looked surprised. He had simply nodded, the way a man nods when a debt collector names a sum he has always known he owed, and said, "Then it wants the rest of me."

*Return.* Not destroy. Corren had caught the word and refused to let go of it, the way you grip a rope over a drop. He had spent the hour in the saved crate with Sef's knife prying boards and his fingers bleeding on iron bands, digging through a hundred years of Vel Saren's dead bureaucracy for the difference between those two words.

He found Wren's shoulder and spun him around.

"Stop." His voice cracked on it. "Whatever you're about to do — stop. I need you to listen to me first."

Wren looked at him, and Corren felt his stomach drop. There was already a distance in his eyes. Not coldness — Wren could never be cold — but a kind of tidying up. A man putting his affairs in order. The lamplight of Ironhollow lived in Wren's face; Corren had thought so since the first night at the tide gate, that strange warm gold in a valley the sun barely touched. And that light was being packed away, shelf by shelf, before Corren's eyes.

"I have to do this," Wren said gently. "You heard it. The mist is failing. Emberlain has minutes, maybe less, and if the Sigil doesn't open—"

"The Sigil doesn't say *destroy*. Listen to me. *Listen.*" Corren grabbed his hands. They were shaking; he didn't care. "I went back through the crate. Every scrap. Trial records, sanctuary ledgers, the payment schedules — the valley took your memories as payment, Wren, and the Court's own law is written into the Sigil's foundation because the Sigil was built by the same hands that built the Court, before they went rotten. And the law says—" He had to stop and breathe. He had read the line eleven times. He had read it until the letters blurred, because it was the only thing standing between him and the end of every moment he had ever been happy.

"*Taken things may be carried,*" he said. "*A debt discharged is not a thing unmade. What is surrendered may be borne forth by another who departs.*"

Wren frowned. "That's not—"

"It's the third codicil of the founding compact. It's buried under four layers of property law because nobody ever used it, because nobody ever *wanted* to use it, because carrying someone's memories means carrying them *forever*, alone, with no way to give them back." Corren heard his own voice rising and couldn't stop it. "It was written for messengers. For people smuggling a country's memory out through enemy lines. It was never meant for—" He swallowed. "It was never meant for this."

"This is mercy," Wren said quietly. "Don't make it crueler than it has to be."

"It's not mercy. It's the Sigil taking the easiest shape of your grief and calling it a price." Corren pulled the page from inside his coat — he had copied it out in lamp-oil smoke on the back of a warden's requisition form, because there had been no paper and no time — and held it between them. "Look at it. *Carried forth by another who departs.* Someone has to leave anyway. Someone is leaving anyway." His voice failed him for a moment. He pushed through. "Me. I'm leaving anyway. I walked into this valley through a hole in the world and I never belonged here, and everyone in Ironhollow knows it, and the door is opening regardless. Let me carry them out."

Wren stared at the page. Then at him.

"You don't know what you're offering."

"No," Corren admitted. "I don't. Tell me."

And Wren told him.

He spoke the way he tended lamps, Corren realized — carefully, completely, giving each thing its full measure of light. He described the morning fog on the tide gate and how it tasted of iron and pine. He described Maren Voss teaching him to trim a wick so it wouldn't gutter, her hands over his, her voice saying *a flame fed badly dies angry.* He described the festival of the First Frost, when the whole valley walked the shore with candles and the lake held a thousand small fires and nobody spoke above a whisper, because the quiet was the point. He described nine years of ordinary evenings. Bread. Rain against windows that never quite sealed. The specific creak of the fourth stair in the lamp-room, which he had meant, someday, to fix, and never fixed, because some sounds were the sound of home.

"And you," Wren said, and stopped, and started again. "You. The tide gate. Your ridiculous boots full of lake water. The hour we sat on the breakwater and you told me about Vel Saren and I thought — I remember exactly what I thought. I thought, *oh. So that's what I've been waiting for.* Nine years I didn't know I was waiting, and then you came through a door that shouldn't exist and I knew." His eyes were bright and steady. "If I walk through that door without them, I won't know any of it. Not the lamps. Not Maren. Not why my chest hurts when I look at you. You'll be a stranger with a scar, and I'll be an exile with no crime, and none of it will have happened. That's the price. That's what you're volunteering to hold."

Corren had imagined this conversation on the run down the causeway. He had rehearsed arguments. He had not rehearsed for the way Wren would lay it all out plainly, like inventory, like a man showing you exactly what you were asking to steal.

"Yes," he said.

"You'd forget me too," Wren said. "That's the other half of it. If you carry my memories, you carry mine — but yours stay yours. You'd remember everything. Every evening on the breakwater. And I'd walk past you on the far side of that door and feel nothing."

"I know."

"That isn't love, Corren. That's just loss with extra steps."

Corren laughed — a broken, wet sound that surprised them both — and wiped his face with the back of his hand.

"Maybe. Or maybe it's the only kind I'm any good at." He stepped closer, close enough that he could see the lamplight still fighting in Wren's eyes, and made himself say the rest of it plainly, because Wren deserved plainness. "My sister is out there right now fighting beside people she doesn't remember loving, because the Court hollowed her out and filled the space with duty. I spent three years looking for her and when I found her she didn't know my name. Do you understand? I already live in the world you're afraid of. I've been living in it for years. I know exactly how much a person can survive remembering alone." His voice steadied. "And I also know this: somewhere in Vel Saren's ashes there are people waiting behind that door to be remembered, and the whole reason any of us are standing here is that somebody kept their names. Somebody carried them. That's the work. That's always been the work. Let me do it for you too."

Wren closed his eyes.

For a long moment there was no sound but the dying pulse of the Sigil, slower now, each dark ring deeper than the last. Above them, faint through the temple's open crown, came the thin whistle of ash falling like snow — Emberlain's fire, guttering, the mist failing, the wardens' lanterns surely crawling down through gaps in a sky that had been sealed for a century.

"If you're wrong," Wren said at last, "if the codicil fails, if the Sigil rejects it — I lose everything anyway, and you lose me, and we'll both have spent our last hour on a lawyer's footnote."

"Then I'll be wrong holding something," Corren said. "That's more than the Sigil was going to leave either of us."

Something moved through Wren's face — grief and wonder tangled past separating — and then he laughed too, short and helpless, and pressed his forehead against Corren's.

"Maren said something to me once," he murmured. "When I asked her why the valley takes memories instead of coin. She said, *because memory is the only wealth worth stealing, and the only gift worth giving.* I didn't understand her then."

"Do you now?"

"I think I'm beginning to." Wren pulled back and looked at him fully, and whatever he saw in Corren's face must have been answer enough, because he exhaled — a long breath, nine years long, maybe longer — and nodded once. "All right. Show me the words."

They knelt together at the altar's edge while the last golden ring above them flickered like a candle in wind. Wren placed his palms flat on the stone and began to speak the surrender aloud, each phrase of the old compact falling into place like stones in an archway — *I return what was taken; I discharge the debt; I name my bearer* — and with each phrase Corren felt the air change, felt something vast and patient turn its attention toward them from beneath the lake, from beneath the world.

*I name my bearer,* Wren said, and did not hesitate, did not falter, though Corren watched his hands tremble on the stone. *Corren Vale, who walks out carrying the name of Ironhollow. Let him carry me as well.*

The stone warmed under their hands. Light rose — not the draining gray of the failing Sigil but something older, softer, the color of lamplight through fog — and it gathered around Wren like a tide finally coming home, and Corren held his face in both hands and memorized him, furiously, desperately, the scar on his jaw and the storm-gray of his eyes and the exact way he looked at Corren as though Corren were the answer to a question he'd stopped asking years ago.

"It won't hurt," Wren whispered. The light was in his hair now, his shoulders, dissolving him gently from the edges inward. "That's the worst part. It feels like falling asleep."

"Then I'll be here when you wake up," Corren said. "Even if you don't know me. Even if you hate me for it. I'll be there."

Wren smiled — the tide-gate smile, the breakwater smile, the smile Corren would now be the sole keeper of in all the world.

"Then it's not loss," he said. "It's just a loan."

And the light took him.

Above them, deep beneath the lake, something immense stirred — a heartbeat of fire, one final flare, gold flooding the drowned temple from below as Emberlain gave the last of herself to the opening of the door — and the Sigil blazed awake in rings of white flame, and the water of the pool parted like a curtain, and where the altar had been there was now a doorway full of morning.

Morning. Real morning, sun and all, spilling through the stone into a valley that had forgotten the word.

Corren got his arm under Wren's shoulder and lifted him — lighter than he should have been, asleep and breathing, alive — and looked once back at the temple, at the dark water, at the place where a dragon's heart-flame had burned for a hundred years past death out of nothing but duty.

Then he turned toward the light, carrying two countries' worth of memory on his back, and walked through the door.
