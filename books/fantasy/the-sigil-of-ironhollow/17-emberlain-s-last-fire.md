## 17. Emberlain's Last Fire

The water of the drowned temple had gone still.

Wren noticed it first as a silence in his ears — the absence of the lapping that had accompanied every step since they'd descended the sunken stair. The flood that had swallowed this place centuries ago now lay flat as poured glass, and in it he could see the whole ruined ceiling reflected back at him: broken arches, the ribs of a roof that had once been painted with constellations, and above them all — impossibly, terribly — the dragon.

Emberlain hung in the flooded nave like a censer swinging on no chain. Her coils wrapped the central altar three times over, and her scales — Wren could not stop looking at them — were the color of embers at the very end of their burning: deep rust shading into ash-grey at the edges of each plate, as though she were being extinguished from the outside in. Where her body met the water, steam rose in thin veils. The temple smelled of hot stone and something older, something like the air before lightning.

*She's dying,* Corren had said, hours ago, when they'd first pried open the Sigil records' lead case by lamplight. *She's been dying for a hundred years.*

Wren stood at the altar's edge now, boots wet to the knee, the crate of rescued archives stacked behind him where they'd dragged it down the stair. The Sigil itself was here — of course it was here; the records had said so plainly enough once he'd learned to read the old script — carved into the altar's face in lines so deep his whole palm could have fit inside them. A door drawn flat. A lock drawn as a wound. And at its center, a hollow where flame was meant to go.

He had come down expecting a battle. He had rehearsed arguments on the stair: *I am the storm-caller, the prophecy named me, I have the right—*

The dragon opened one eye.

It was the size of a cart wheel and the color of a hearth going out, gold collapsing inward toward grey, and it found him across forty feet of still water without moving anything else about her. Not her head. Not her wings, folded tight against her flanks like a bird in winter. Only the eye, rolling with the slow, grinding patience of a glacier, until it settled on him the way a lamp settles on a moth.

"You are not what I expected," she said.

Her voice did not echo. That was the strangest thing. It filled the temple completely and left no reverberation behind, as if the words were too heavy to bounce.

Wren's mouth was dry. "You can speak."

"I could speak before your kingdom had a name." The eye blinked — a slow shuttering, like a house closing its windows one last time before the family leaves. "I expected a soldier. Or a thief. The Court always sends soldiers or thieves, or worse, true believers. Instead they send—" the great head shifted, just barely, and the water shivered in rings around her coils, "—a boy holding a box of paper."

"The records," Wren said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "From the archive. They told us how to wake you."

"Wake me." Something moved through the vast body — not quite laughter, but its skeleton. "Child, I have been awake since the burning. Do you know what it is to be awake for a hundred years underwater, holding a lock shut because everyone who might relieve you is ash? There is no word in your language for it. I have looked."

Corren came down the last steps behind him, and Wren heard the boy catch his breath — heard it catch again when the eye swiveled to take him in. But Emberlain's attention returned to Wren almost immediately, the way iron filings return to a lodestone.

"You are the storm-caller," she said. It was not a question.

"I don't know what I am," Wren admitted. "I know what a book says I am. I know an elder took my memories and told herself it was mercy. I know the wardens are coming through the mist right now, and the lamps are failing, and there are children up there—" his voice cracked, and he hated it, and he kept talking anyway, "—and the only door out of this valley is sealed with your heart, and I need it."

There. He'd said it. The ugly truth of why he'd climbed down into a dying creature's sickroom: not to free her, not even to meet her, but to spend her.

The silence that followed lasted long enough for Wren to count his own heartbeats. Twelve. Fifteen. Twenty.

"Yes," Emberlain said. "That is what they all needed. Every messenger the old kingdom sent me, in the years before the end. Every priest. Every king's son with his fine armor rusting on my threshold. *We need your fire.* No one ever asked what I needed. Why would they? I was not a person to them. I was a wall that breathed."

"I'm asking," Wren said.

"Are you?" The eye narrowed — and even dimmed, even guttering, it was an awful thing to be narrowed at, a look that made Wren understand how small the difference was between awe and terror. "Or are you asking the way beggars ask, knowing the answer you want?"

Wren thought of Maren. Of her hands on his face in the council hall, her voice breaking on *I did it to save you*, of the way love and theft had worn the same gloves in that room. He thought of the trial record Corren had found — *the accused called a storm; three dead; sentence: exile within exile* — and of the sixteen-year-old boy in those pages who had done a terrible necessary thing and been destroyed for it rather than thanked.

"I'm asking the way someone asks who has been on both sides of a debt," Wren said slowly. "I won't pretend I don't want your fire. I do. People I love will die without it. But wanting isn't the same as taking, and—" he stopped, because the next part hurt, and said it anyway, "—and Maren taught me that. The worst way. But she taught me."

The dragon was quiet a long moment. Steam curled off her shoulders in slow spirals, and somewhere far above, muffled by fathoms of stone and water and mist, Wren thought he heard a lamp fail — a faint bell-tone of glass giving up, carried down through the temple's bones.

"Lamps," Emberlain murmured. "I remember when this valley had no lamps. When it had no valley. I helped make the mist, you know. Did your books tell you that?" She didn't wait for an answer. "Vel Saren's last magisters came to me in the burning's final hour. *They are erasing us,* they said. *Not conquering. Erasing. When they finish, no one will ever have known we lived.* So we made Ironhollow — a pocket outside their power, a country with no borders because it had no existence. And I lay down at the only door and became the lock, because a seal must be made of something the enemy cannot burn, and nothing burns hotter than a promise."

"What was the promise?" Corren asked softly. He had drifted closer, along the altar's flank, close enough now that Wren could see the scar on his jaw working as he swallowed.

"To hold until someone worthy came to relieve me," Emberlain said. "A hundred years ago that seemed a small thing to promise. I was young. I thought the world would send someone soon."

"And no one came," Wren said.

"No one knew to come. That was the price of the hiding — the valley forgot the world, and the world forgot the valley, and I forgot—" the great chest expanded, contracted, a sound like a bellows full of gravel, "—I forgot whether I was keeping the door shut *for* Vel Saren or against everything else. Duty does that, after enough years. It eats the reason and leaves the shape."

Her head lowered. It took a long time — Wren realized with a jolt that she was weak, truly weak, that the movement he'd taken for majesty was effort — until her muzzle hung a few feet above the altar, above the Sigil's waiting hollow. This close he could see the individual scales around her nostrils, each one cracked like drought-earth, and beneath them a dull red glow that pulsed in time with her breathing. Her heart-flame. The key. Flickering like a candle in a doorway.

"So," Emberlain said. "Ask properly, storm-caller. Ask me what I need."

Wren's throat worked. "What do you need?"

"Sky." The word came out of her like something she'd been holding between her teeth for a century. "I have not seen sky since the burning. Water, stone, dark, and duty. I want to die under open sky. I want to feel weather that is not my own making. I want—" and here the ancient voice frayed, and Wren understood he was hearing the only moment of weakness she would allow herself, "—I want to stop being a wall and be a dragon again, for one hour, before the end. Is that so much? Is that so much to ask, for a hundred years of holding?"

"No," Wren said, and his eyes were burning. "No, it isn't."

Behind him, he heard Corren sit down on the wet stone, deliberately, the way you sit when your legs have stopped being trustworthy.

"But the door," Wren pressed, because some stubborn clerk in his head demanded the terms be read fully, "if I use your fire on the Sigil — if I spend it opening instead of sealing — the Court comes through. Everything the mist kept out—"

"The mist is already failing." Flatly. Factly. "You said your lamps are dying. Child, the lamps were never the ward. *I* was the ward. Every flame in this valley — every lamp, every hearth, every lantern — drew its steadiness from mine. When my fire goes, whether I die holding the door or flying under sky, the mist goes with it. The only question is what happens to my last hour." The eye fixed on him again, and now there was something in it that had not been there before, something bright and desperate and almost young. "Hoard me, and I gutters out here in the dark, and the Court walks through my corpse. Spend me, and I burn once, hugely, gloriously — and the door opens, and the world remembers Vel Saren existed, and I see the sky while it happens. There was never a choice, storm-caller. There was only ever the question of whether anyone would care enough to ask."

*Remembering isn't the same as keeping.* Maren's last words, bought with her life on the temple stairs, buying him these minutes. Wren carried them down into his chest and let them settle beside everything else the valley had given him and taken from him.

"Then I ask," Wren said. He stepped forward onto the altar itself, his boots ringing on ancient stone, until he stood directly beneath the hanging muzzle, small as an ant before a mountain, and did not feel small. "Emberlain, last fire of Vel Saren, keeper of the door: I'm asking you to give me your flame. Not to hoard. To ignite the Sigil. To open the way. And I promise you—" his voice shook and held, "—open sky. Weather not your own. An hour as a dragon and not a wall. And afterward, a world that knows your name, because I'll carry it out of here myself if I have to say it every day for the rest of my life."

Steam billowed. The red glow beneath her scales brightened — or perhaps it only gathered itself, the way a drowning man gathers himself for one last stroke.

"Say my name again," she whispered.

"Emberlain."

"Again."

"*Emberlain.*"

The dragon closed her eye. When she spoke, her voice had gone very quiet, and very clear.

"A hundred years," she said, "and that is the first time anyone has said it aloud. Names are fuel, little storm-caller. Did your books teach you that? Mine are nearly empty. Yours will do."

She uncoiled.

It was like watching a mountain decide to move. Scale by scale, ring by ring, she unwound from the altar, and the water that had lain still as glass all this time woke at last into waves, slapping the walls, climbing the steps, and Wren staggered and stayed upright through sheer refusal. The heart-flame rose with her — rose into her throat, he realized, traveling up through that vast body toward her jaws, gathering, condensing, becoming something small and unbearable, a coal the size of a fist that outshone every lamp in Ironhollow.

"Stand clear of the hollow," she rumbled. "And whatever happens — whatever you see when it opens — do not look away. That is the last thing I ask. Someone must witness. Witnessing is the whole point."

Wren backed to the altar's edge. Corren was on his feet beside him now, pale, gripping his arm hard enough to bruise, and Wren gripped back.

Emberlain reared against the flooded dark, wings spreading at last — torn, translucent, magnificent — and the temple filled with light like the inside of a struck bell.

"For Vel Saren," she said. "*For the ones erased. Witness me.*"

And she breathed her fire into the Sigil.

It did not explode. It *ignited* — the carved lines filling with flame the way veins fill with blood, the whole altar becoming a door becoming a dawn, and Wren threw his arm across his eyes and then, remembering, pulled it down again and forced himself to watch, because she had asked and watching was the whole point. Through the blaze he saw her turn — saw the enormous battered shape climb past the broken arches, past the drowned constellations, up the spiral of the temple's ruin, toward the stair, toward the surface, toward—

Toward sky.

The water parted before her. The stone groaned and gave. And as the Sigil roared open behind them and the first wind in a hundred years came through the door smelling of rain and distance, Wren heard — high above, faint and free and getting freer — a sound he had no name for and would spend the rest of his life learning to call joy.

"She's through," Corren whispered. His face was wet; Wren couldn't tell if it was spray. "Wren. She made it. She's *flying*—"

Above them, beyond the shattered crown of the temple, against a sky the mist had finally, mercifully released, a last ember rose, and burned, and did not go out.

Then the light of the opened Sigil washed over them both, and the door showed them what waited on the other side — and Wren forgot, entirely, how to breathe.
