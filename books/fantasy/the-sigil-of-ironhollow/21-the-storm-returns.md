## 21. The Storm Returns

The first thing Wren felt was the wind.

Not the mist-wind of Ironhollow, that soft breathless stirring that had no weather in it, no memory of rain or season — but real wind, cold and enormous, pouring down out of a sky he had never seen before and yet recognized instantly, the way one recognizes a face from a dream that has recurred so long it has become a country of its own.

He stood at the mouth of the Sigil with his hand still pressed against the stone, which was warm now, cooling slowly like an ember left in ash. Behind him the door stood open for the first time in a hundred years, and the light that spilled through it was not dragon-fire and not lamp-glow but ordinary gold morning sun, falling across the threshold as casually as if doors opened onto mornings every day.

And inside him — everything.

It came back not as a flood but as a tide, patient and total: nine years of lamps lit at dusk along the lake road; Maren's kitchen smelling of barley and burnt sugar; the festival of First Frost when the whole valley wore paper crowns and pretended, for one night, that they were kings of something larger than a bowl of mist. Corren's laugh, low and surprised, the night they'd first sat together on the tide gate and Corren had admitted he couldn't swim. The weight of Sef's head against his shoulder while she wept without knowing what she wept for. Emberlain's eye, vast and amber, closing at last beneath an open sky.

Maren's last words were there too, and they did not hurt less for being remembered. *Remembering isn't the same as keeping.* He turned them over now like a coin found in an old coat, testing their weight. She had been wrong about many things — she had unmade him, and he would carry that wound longer than any scar — but she had been right about this. The memories were back, and they did not weigh him down. They held him up.

Corren stood beside him. Of course he did. Wren could feel him the way one feels a hearth in a dark room — not looking, just present, warm at the edge of things.

"You're crying," Corren said softly.

"I know." Wren laughed, and the laugh broke halfway through and became something else, and Corren's arm came around his shoulders and let it become whatever it needed to be. "I remember everything," Wren said into his coat. "I remember you. I remember the first night, the prophecy, your ridiculous scar. I remember being afraid of you." He pulled back far enough to look at him. "I remember loving you."

Corren went very still. It was the first time either of them had said it aloud, though it had been sitting between them for chapters — for weeks, for a lifetime, for however long love takes to grow in a valley that forgets.

"Well," Corren managed, voice rough. "That's convenient. Because I've been carrying yours around all day like a sack of turnips, and I'd have felt rather foolish if you didn't."

Wren kissed him then, brief and salt-stung, under the open sky, in front of God knew how many refugees and one very smug ghost-country dissolving into morning behind them. Some things deserved witnesses.

* * *

They came out of Ironhollow all through that day.

Wren watched them from the ridge above the door, and he could not stop watching. They emerged blinking into sunlight like creatures raised in caves — because they were, because they had been, because none of them, not one soul in that valley, had ever seen weather. The children came first, as children do, running ahead of their parents' caution, and the smallest of them stopped dead on the hillside and looked up and up and up at the sky and began to shout words that tumbled over each other, *blue* and *big* and *where does it go*, and her mother caught her up and spun her around and was crying and laughing at once.

Old Tam Vetch, who had tended the eastern lamps for forty years and never once asked what lay beyond the mist, walked out into the grass and took off his hat and simply stood there, bareheaded, letting the wind move through his white hair like a benediction. A cluster of farmers knelt in the new green and pressed their palms flat to the earth as if taking its pulse. Two sisters from the weaving houses lay on their backs and watched clouds — actual clouds, moving, changing, becoming other shapes — and screamed with laughter every time one transformed.

Behind them, the valley itself was changing. Wren could see it from the ridge: the mist was lifting, drawing back off the lake and the terraced fields and the black shoulders of the fells like a sheet pulled from furniture long covered. The lighthouse-lamps along the shore still burned, small and stubborn, but they were only lamps now. They no longer held back anything. For the first time in a hundred years, Ironhollow was merely a place — beautiful, isolated, wounded — and not a secret.

"It's going to be strange," Corren said beside him. "For them. Living somewhere the sky can touch."

"They'll learn." Wren shaded his eyes. "We learned everything else the hard way. We can learn this gently."

Below them, the stream of people thickened. Word had spread through the valley overnight — the Sigil was open, the Court had fallen, the world outside was real and mostly kind and entirely unburned — and now they came in family groups and cart-trains, carrying what they could: seed-barrels, loom-weights, the archive crates that had survived the warden fire, old Tam's entire collection of lamp-wicks ("a man doesn't abandon his wicks"). They poured up the slope toward the pass and out into a country none of them had names for yet, and every single one of them paused, at least for a moment, to look up.

That was when Wren noticed the dust.

The summer had been dry here, wherever *here* was — the borderlands of a kingdom that had learned to live beside its own erasures. Weeks without rain, by the look of the hills, brown-gold and brittle, and the road south churned to powder by the carts of other refugees, other valleys, other forgotten places that had begun, since the Regent turned to ash, to remember themselves into existence. The children coughed. Old Tam sneezed violently into his recovered hat. Dust hung over the whole slow procession like a question.

Wren looked at it, and looked at the sky, and felt something stir in his chest that he had not felt in nine years.

Fear, first — old fear, sixteen-year-old fear, the taste of lightning and the sound of three names he would finally be allowed to grieve. Then, beneath the fear, something steadier. He had called storms in anger once, and it had cost everything. But anger was not the only thing storms could be made of. He understood that now in his bones, in the place where Emberlain's last warmth still lingered. Fire could burn or it could warm. Water could drown or it could carry. The power had never been the crime. What he had done with it, and why — those were the crimes, and those were forgiven, or would be, someday, by himself if no one else.

"Wren?" Corren's hand touched his arm. "You've got that look. The temple look. The 'I'm about to do something enormous and metaphysical' look."

"The sky is dry," Wren said.

"It is, yes. Very observant—"

"They've never felt rain." His voice came out low. "Any of them. Not one person coming out of that valley has ever felt rain fall on them. I have — I remember it, I remember my mother's garden and the smell of wet stone and—" He stopped, because his throat had closed. "Corren. I'm the storm-caller. It's the first thing the prophecy said. And all this time I thought it meant destruction."

Corren looked at him for a long moment. Then he smiled, that crooked smile with the scar pulling at it, and stepped back, and swept his arm toward the hillside full of blinking, wonder-struck, dusty people.

"Well then," he said. "Go on, storm-caller. Give them weather."

* * *

Wren walked down alone into the middle of the exodus.

People made way for him without quite knowing why — some recognized him as the one who'd opened the door, others simply felt the change in the air the way animals feel it before a tempest. He stopped on a flat shelf of grass where the road bent, where the crowd thinned enough that he could see the sky from horizon to horizon, and he closed his eyes.

He reached for the storm the way he had once, at sixteen, reached in panic and grief — but slowly this time. Gently. The way Maren used to lift sleeping children from festival benches. He found the moisture hanging invisible in the great warm currents above the hills, miles of it, rivers of it, waiting. He found the pressure and the cold sliding down off the high peaks. He did not seize any of it. He asked.

And the sky, which had never been asked anything by anyone in Ironhollow's hundred locked years, answered.

The wind arrived first — a soft, rising sigh through the brown grass, ruffling Tam's hair again, making the paper crown of some forgotten festival flutter on a little girl's head. People looked up. The clouds gathered with a slowness that was almost ceremonial, gray and silver stacking themselves over the pass, and the light below them went soft and pearled, and a hush fell over two hundred refugees who had never in their lives heard a hush quite like this one, the hush the whole world holds before rain.

Then the first drop hit the road with an audible *tick*.

Then another. Then ten. Then the sky simply *opened*, gentle and steady and warm, and rain fell on the people of Ironhollow for the first time in the history of their existence.

What happened next, Wren would spend the rest of his life failing to describe. The little girl in the paper crown shrieked with pure joy and spun with her face turned straight up into it. Farmers threw back their heads and let it run down their cheeks and could not seem to tell, afterward, which water on their faces had come from where. Old Tam Vetch stood in the downpour with his arms slightly away from his sides, palms open, laughing silently at the sky like a man greeting an old friend he'd only ever heard described. Children began to dance — actual dancing, stamping in the puddles that bloomed in the road's wheel-ruts, discovering mud, discovering splashing, discovering the extraordinary fact that water falls from *nowhere*, from *everything*, from the sky itself, endlessly, for free.

And the smell. Wren had forgotten — no, he had surrendered — the smell of rain on dry earth, that deep green mineral exhale, petrichor rising off the hillsides like the land itself waking up. The whole valley's worth of people breathed it in at once, and something in the collective chest of Ironhollow unlocked.

Wren stood in the middle of it, soaked through, holding the storm around them like cupped hands, and wept without shame.

This was what the power was *for*. Not walls. Not vengeance. Not even justice, exactly. This. A gentle rain over tired people. An arrival. Nine years ago a boy had torn the sky apart trying to save one friend, and the sky had punished everyone, and the valley had punished the boy, and the punishment had been forgetting — but the sky had never hated him. The sky had only ever been waiting for him to come home and ask nicely.

"Storm-caller," someone said near him — Tam Vetch, dripping, hatless, eyes bright. "Is it always like this? Out there?"

"No," Wren said, laughing through the rain. "Sometimes it's much bigger. Sometimes it's frightening. That's part of it too."

Tam considered this, water streaming off his beard. "Good," he said at last. "A gift you can't lose shouldn't count as one." And he shuffled off down the road into the rain, whistling something old.

Corren found him there a while later, when the storm had softened to a drifting silver mist and the procession had become a procession of drenched, delighted, mud-spattered pilgrims. He wrapped his coat around Wren's shoulders without comment, though the coat was nearly as wet as he was, and they walked together at the tail of the exodus, watching Ironhollow fall behind them into the clearing haze — the lake glinting, the terraces green, the lamps still burning small and faithful along the shore, the black doorway of the Sigil standing open on the mountainside like a pupil dilating toward the world.

"We'll need to send word ahead," Corren said, in the tone of a man building tomorrow out loud. "There are border towns, real ones, with councils and granaries and — apparently — weather of their own. We'll need maps. Teachers. Gods, we'll need teachers — half these people think the sun is a lamp someone forgot to trim."

"I'll teach," Wren said. "Lamps, and weather, and history. Our history. All of it, including the parts that shame us." He glanced sideways. "You'll teach swimming."

"Blackmail."

"Prophecy."

Ahead of them, at the crest of the pass, the last of the daylight caught the rain-mist and made a bridge of color across the road — red and gold and green, arched over the heads of the fleeing-and-emerging people of a valley that had never known its own name was worth carrying. The children saw it first and pointed, and the adults stopped, and for a moment the entire road stood still beneath the rainbow, silent, faces lifted, while the light of a world they were only beginning to meet broke apart above them into every color it had.

"There's your next book title," Corren murmured.

Wren squeezed his hand and said nothing, because some moments should not be narrated, only kept.

But as they crested the pass together — as Ironhollow sank behind them into evening and the open country rolled out ahead, vast, unmapped, waiting — Wren felt, for the first time since he was sixteen years old, that he was not walking out of exile or into exile or through exile at all.

He was walking *home*. It simply hadn't existed until today. And far to the south, past the hills and the border towns and the rebuilt roads, a town waited that had heard rumors already — riders arriving breathless, speaking of a fallen court, an opened door, a valley of strangers with rain in their hair and a name on their tongues — and in that town, someone had begun asking questions Wren did not yet know he would spend the next year answering.
