## 22. The Name We Carry Out

The bell over the schoolhouse door had a crack in it, and so it rang half a note flat, and so every morning for a year it had announced the day to Thornhollow in a voice that sounded, to Wren, like someone laughing through tears. He had come to love that cracked note more than any perfect sound he'd ever heard.

"Master Brightwater," said Pip Aldous from the doorway, breathless, one shoe on and one shoe in his hand, "Corren says the map's wrong again."

"The map is never wrong."

"It's *always* wrong." Pip held up the offending object — a child's drawing of the valley road, all furious crayon and confident geography — and pointed at a smudge where the river should have bent. "He drew Ironhollow twice."

Wren took the paper and studied it gravely, as befitted a matter of cartographic importance. "Well. He's not entirely wrong. There are two Ironhollows now. The one behind the door, and the one we carry out of it."

Pip considered this with the deep suspicion of an eight-year-old being handed philosophy instead of an answer. "That's not a river bend, though."

"No," Wren agreed, and handed back the map. "It isn't. Go tell him to draw the river properly, and then come inside before your other foot freezes."

The boy thundered off across the yard, and Wren stood a moment longer in the cold gold light of morning, looking down the long slope toward the road.

A year. It had been a year since he had walked out of a drowned temple carrying nothing but a name and someone else's memory of him. A year since Emberlain's last fire had gone up through the broken roof of the world like a lantern released from a string. A year since Maren Voss had died with her hand against a warden's blade and her eyes on Wren's face, saying what she said — *remembering isn't the same as keeping* — words he had turned over every night since, like a stone in the pocket, smooth now, warm now, but still heavier than it looked.

Thornhollow had been a border town once, back when borders meant something and Vel Saren's maps had shown a kingdom instead of a blank space politely pretending not to exist. The Court had let it rot rather than burn it — no glory in destroying a place no one remembered mattered. But people had crept back after the Regent fell. They always did. People were the one thing ash could not finish.

The schoolhouse had been a granary; you could still smell rye in the rafters if the day was warm. The houses along the lane had been rebuilt crooked, each family adding whatever they pleased — a carved lintel here, a dragon-shaped weathervane there, because dragons were fashionable now in the way that only things everyone had feared for a lifetime can be, once they are safely stories again. And at the crossroads below the town, where the old valley road began its climb into the hills toward a mist that no longer fell, someone had set a single grey stone.

It was not a large stone. It had been part of the Sigil's archway, Corren said, one of the keystone fragments that had crumbled free when the door opened for the last time, and Wren had carried it out himself without knowing why until he set it down at the roadside and understood: doors should leave something of themselves behind, or else the way through them stops being real.

The stone bore no inscription. It didn't need one. Travelers coming up from the south would stop their carts beside it, look up the road into the clean blue distance where the mountains opened onto a valley the whole world had learned existed again, and say it aloud, just to feel how the name sat in the mouth:

*Ironhollow.*

No fear in it anymore. Only the particular hush people gave to places that had survived being forgotten.

* * *

Corren was in the yard when Wren came down, kneeling in the frost-stiff grass with six children arranged around him in a ragged semicircle and a stick in his hand, drawing shapes in the dirt.

"Now," Corren was saying, in the patient tone he used when he was about to be lied to by small people, "what have I drawn?"

"A squashed egg," said Pip.

"It's the Sigil."

"The Sigil is a *squashed egg?*"

"The Sigil," Corren said with dignity, "is a spiral within a circle within a square, which, when drawn by a man whose hands are frozen, looks like a squashed egg. In its proper form it was three hundred feet tall and carved into the floor of a temple under a lake, and it opened a door between the living world and a country made of memory, and I watched it happen, so I'll thank you for some respect."

"You didn't open it," piped a girl named Sera. "Wren opened it. You carried the bag."

"I carried," Corren said gravely, "the most important bag in the history of bags."

Wren leaned against the fencepost and watched them and did not say anything at all, because this — this exact thing, frost and children and Corren defending the honor of a satchel full of books — was the thing he had almost lost, and he had learned in the losing what it cost to be careless with ordinary mornings.

There had been a moment, standing at the altar with the Sigil burning around him like a ring of fallen stars, when the terms of the door had been laid bare in his own bones. *Return what the valley took.* He had thought then that it meant only the memories of his exile, his crime, the storm — the years the valley had eaten as payment for sanctuary. He had been prepared to pay that. What the door had wanted instead was crueler and cleaner: everything. Every lamp he had tended. Every festival. Every winter evening in the archive with dust in his throat. Corren's laugh. Corren's scarred jaw. Corren, full stop. To open the door was to walk through it having forgotten why he had fought for it.

And he would have done it. That was the terrible arithmetic of that night — he would have done it gladly, and walked out into a world he couldn't remember wanting to save, and been saved anyway, hollow as any warden.

Then Corren had come running down the temple steps with a page torn from the Sigil records clutched in one fist and ash in his hair, shouting words Wren hadn't understood until they landed: *carried out, not destroyed — given to someone leaving anyway — let me be the one who remembers you.*

Wren had argued. Of course he had argued. He could still feel the shape of that argument in his chest, the desperate geometry of it: *you can't carry nine years of me, it will break you, you'll walk out of that door and I'll be a stranger—*

And Corren, ash-faced, terrified, absolutely certain: *"Then I'll spend the rest of my life introducing us."*

In the end it had happened the way the great things happen, which is to say quickly and quietly and with no one watching but the dying. Wren had spoken the giving aloud, hands on the warm stone of the arch, and felt nine years lift out of him like water from a cupped palm — and Corren had caught it, somehow, caught *him*, and the door had opened, and Emberlain had gone up into the sky one last time as a ribbon of fire that the whole valley saw and none of them ever managed to describe the same way twice.

And then — this was the part Wren still could not tell without his throat going tight — then Corren had kept his promise exactly backward. He had spent the first weeks outside the door reading those memories back to Wren every night like a man reciting scripture: *your lamp-room, your chapped hands, the date you were so nervous about, the way you looked when you first saw me in the tide gate and tried very hard not to look like you recognized my scar.* Piece by piece, night by night, he had handed Wren back to himself. Until there was nothing left in his keeping except the choosing to keep going.

"That's not fair," Pip was saying. "If Wren opened the door, why do you get to teach the Sigil?"

"Because," Corren said, standing and brushing off his knees and catching Wren's eye over the children's heads with the look that still undid him, a year on, the look that said *I know exactly who you are, I put you back together myself,* "somebody has to know where the important bag went."

* * *

They lit the lamp at dusk.

It was not a grand ritual. That had taken Wren most of the year to accept — that remembrance did not require incense or prostration or blood, only the small stubborn act of doing a thing again and again until the doing became the meaning itself. The lamp stood on a post outside the schoolhouse door, iron-framed, glass-paned, salvaged from the lamp-room of the old lighthouse-tower above the lake, and every evening when the light went blue over the mountains, one of them climbed the step and lit it.

Tonight it was Wren's turn. He struck the match and touched it to the wick and stood a moment with his hand cupped around the small flame, the way he had cupped a thousand flames in another life, in a valley where weather never came and lamps were the only sun the fog allowed.

"For Maren Voss," he said quietly. It was not a prayer. She would have hated a prayer. "Who unmade me trying to keep me safe, and spent her last hour putting the pieces back where she found them." He breathed. The flame leaned. "I forgive you. I think I forgave you the moment you stepped in front of that blade. But I'm saying it aloud anyway, because you taught me that remembering isn't the same as keeping — and neither is forgiving, unless you say it out loud where the dark can hear it."

The flame steadied. Somewhere down the lane a dog barked, and a cart creaked, and the ordinary world went on being ordinary, which was the whole miracle of it.

"For Emberlain," he said, "who kept a door sealed for a thousand years for a kingdom that forgot her, and asked one thing at the end — to die under open sky. You got your sky. I hope it was enough. I light this because you never got to see a sunrise, and this is the closest thing I have."

And last, always last, though she would have scolded him for the ordering:

"For Sef." His voice softened the way it always did. "Who stayed. Who woke up on a battlefield weeping with her brother's song stuck in her head like a splinter, and looked at the ruin of everything the Court had used her hands to do, and said — *I'll stay and fix it.* Who walks the valley road every week with supplies on her back and doesn't let anyone thank her, and who told me last month, with that look she gets, that if I put her name on anything sentimental she'd come down the mountain and reorganize my entire classroom."

He smiled at the flame. "So this lamp isn't for you, Sef. It's for the rain. You said you remembered rain, in a country that doesn't exist anymore. Well. It exists again. And it rains there now. Someone should light a lamp for that."

Down in the valley, past the keystone stone and the long climb of the road, the mists had thinned to ordinary mountain fog, and beyond the fog the refugee villages glimmered — new roofs, new fields, the slow stubborn work of ten thousand people learning to live in a place that had spent a century holding its breath. Sef was somewhere down there tonight, or up on the ridge, or asleep under a wagon with her sword under her arm and her brother's childhood song hummed low in her throat. Whole. Hollow-eyed no longer. Her eyes, these days, were full of everything.

* * *

Corren found him afterward on the schoolhouse steps, two cups of cider in hand, and sat down close enough that their shoulders touched, which after a year was still not close enough and probably never would be.

"Maren's day soon," he said.

"I know."

"The council wants a marker in the temple ruins. A proper one. Marble, inscriptions, the works." He snorted gently into his cup. "She'd haunt us."

"She'd reorganize our haunting schedule."

"She'd correct the spelling on her own memorial."

Wren laughed, and the laugh came easy, and that too was still new — the ease of it, the way grief had not left him but had stopped being a wall and become a window instead. He looked out over the town: lamplight spreading window to window down the crooked lane, smoke rising straight from chimneys into windless air, and above it all the first stars coming out over mountains that no longer hid anything.

"Can I ask you something?" Corren said, after a while. "You've never actually told me. That first night. Before any of it — before the prophecy, before the wardens, before I knew your name was written anywhere at all. You were waiting for a date. Nine years without one, and you'd finally worked up the nerve, and I came stumbling through your tide gate with hollow-eyed monsters on my heels." He turned, and the lamplight caught the old scar on his jaw, the one Wren had known before he knew him. "Do you ever wish I'd arrived on time?"

Wren considered the question honestly, because Corren deserved honesty and had earned it twice over — once by carrying his memories out of a dying world, and once by handing every one of them back.

"When I was sixteen," Wren said slowly, "the valley took my memories as payment, and I built a life out of what was left, and I told myself it was enough. It wasn't. It was a room with the windows painted shut. Then you arrived a day early and broke every window in the house, and I froze for a month, and grieved for a season, and then—" he gestured, taking in the lamp, the town, the stars, the whole improbable peace of it, "—then the air came in. So no. I don't wish you'd been on time." He knocked his shoulder against Corren's. "I wish you'd been later. I'd have liked one uninterrupted dinner first."

"We'll have it," Corren said. "Next year. Same date. No prophecies."

"There's always a prophecy."

"Then we'll ignore it. We've practice."

Below them, the keystone stone stood pale at the crossroads, and a late traveler was pausing beside it, hat in hand, reading the road signs — *Ironhollow, 2 days' walk; Thornhollow, lodgings; Vel Saren Memorial Road* — and Wren watched the traveler's lips move, shaping the names of places that a year ago had not existed anywhere but in one book in one archive in one forgotten valley.

The traveler looked up, found the schoolhouse lamp burning, and lifted a hand — the small salute strangers give to evidence that the world is inhabited — and walked on.

"They say it without fear now," Wren said.

"They say it," Corren corrected, "because you carried it out. Names don't survive on their own. Somebody has to hold them in their mouth all the way through the dark." He drained his cider and stood, and offered his hand, and Wren took it, and they went inside together, leaving the lamp burning in the window — for Maren, for Emberlain, for rain in a country reborn — a small flame in a large darkness, kept alive, as all the truest fires are, entirely on purpose.

Behind them, through the cracked glass, the light rang half a note flat against the frame, like laughter through tears, like a bell that had survived, like a valley's name spoken softly at dusk and found, at last, unafraid.
