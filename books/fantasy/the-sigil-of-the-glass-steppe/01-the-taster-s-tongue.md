## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The swans arrived at the banquet glazed in honey and bitterness, and Petra Voss tasted one before the queen's cousin did, because that was the whole of his life: to put his tongue where death might be and report back.

He stood at the sideboard behind the high table in the Hall of Amber Light, where a hundred candles burned in chandeliers of cut glass and the light came down like slow rain. Forty guests. Nine courses. Four tasters on duty tonight, though only two of them mattered — himself and old Bramwen, who was going deaf and missed things, which everyone knew and no one said aloud.

Petra lifted a sliver of the swan from the service plate with silver tweezers, set it on his tongue, and closed his eyes.

Duck fat. Honey burnt just short of bitter. A whisper of wormwood in the glaze — deliberate, the chef's signature, harmless. Salt from the eastern pans. And underneath all of it—

Petra opened his eyes.

Underneath all of it there had been something else. Something that did not belong to any kitchen in the capital.

He took a second taste, a larger one, chewing slowly while a footman watched him with the bored patience of a man paid to watch men eat. The flavor rose again, faint as breath on a winter window: cold iron. Wet stone. A grief so specific it had a *shape* — long, narrow, ending abruptly, the way a road ends when the bridge behind you has been cut.

Someone else's sorrow, folded into the sauce like a letter slipped into a pocket.

Not poison. Nothing in the Poisoner's Codex tasted like this. Arsenic sang sharp at the back of the throat; belladonna bloomed sweet then went to ash; the rare salts of the southern coast bit like frostbite. This was none of those. This was a memory, or the ghost of one, cooked into food the way saffron is cooked into rice — invisible, everywhere, unmistakable once you know its color.

"Master Voss?" The steward, Hollis, gray and patient. "The course waits."

"The swan is sound." He said it evenly, the way he always said it, and watched relief move down the line of servers like wind through wheat. "Note that the glaze carries wormwood. Some of the older guests may complain of it."

Hollis nodded and made his note, and the swans went out into the candlelight to be admired and dismantled, and Petra stepped back into the shadow of the sideboard and let out a breath he had been holding since the second taste.

No one had seen. No one ever saw. That was the discipline: taste everything, reveal nothing, keep your face as smooth as poured wax. In eleven years of service he had found poison four times — twice by flavor, once by smell, once because the chef's hands shook when he passed the dish and Petra trusted shaking hands over any palate. Four times. Three executions. One pardon, because the poisoner had been twelve years old and weeping, and the king had been feeling merciful that season.

And in eleven years, three times before tonight, he had tasted something that was not poison at all.

* * *

The Guild called it *reading*, though the word they used in the old tongue meant something closer to *swallowing the truth of*. Most people never developed the gift. Of those who did, most got only fragments — a mother who could taste her children's fevers coming a day early, a ferryman who spat into the river each morning and knew whether the crossing would kill anyone. The gift ran thin and strange through the blood of the gifted, like gold through river gravel.

Petra's vein ran richer than most. Food carried intention; intention carried memory. He could taste the cook's resentment of the under-steward she was sleeping with. He could taste that the baker's flour had been milled by a man thinking about drowning. Once, terribly, at a diplomatic dinner seven years ago, he had tasted an ambassador's soup and known — known with the certainty of his own name — that the man had ordered his own brother's execution and slept well after.

He had logged that one, too. In the other ledger. The night ledger.

Because that was the shape of Petra Voss's life: two ledgers, two names, two selves that touched only in the dark between them.

By day he was the Crown Taster, fourth of his rank, paid forty crowns a quarter and housed above the palace kitchens, respected in the small way useful men are respected. By night he walked down Copperlane Street to a door with no sign above it, gave a knock in threes and twos, and descended into the vaults of the Guild of Impossible Things, where he kept accounts that could not exist anywhere but paper made from saltmarsh reed and ink mixed with a dead woman's tears.

The Guild traded in bottled memories. That was what the street whispered and the broadsheets denied. What the street did not whisper, because the street did not know, was the breadth of the trade. Grief by the dram. First love, decanted. The taste of a homeland for exiles who could never go back. Courage sold by the swallow to men about to do brave and stupid things. Regret, purchased from the desperate and resold to the ambitious, because regret teaches faster than any tutor.

Every vial, every trade, every price — entered in Petra's hand, in his small exact script, because Corvane said his was the only hand in the guild that neither trembled nor embellished.

Corvane. His guildmaster. His employer, in the way that a hook is the employer of a fish.

Petra thought about Corvane now, standing in the candle-shadow while the orchestra played something in six-eight time and the queen's cousin laughed too loudly at the swan. He thought about the vault, and the ink, and the reed-paper, and the way Corvane watched him across the counting table some nights with an expression Petra had never managed to read — and Corvane was a man whose face Petra should have been able to read like a menu.

Loneliness has a flavor too. Petra knew it intimately: flat, faintly metallic, like water kept too long in a pewter jug. It was the flavor of every meal he ate alone, every banquet at which he stood apart, every entry he wrote in a ledger no one else would ever be permitted to read. He was fluent in the inner lives of everyone who fed him and known by no one.

He was thirty-one years old, and the fullest relationship of his life was with a poison ledger.

* * *

The fifth course was a sorbet of quince and white wine, served to cleanse the palate, and Petra tasted it and found nothing in it but fruit and honesty, and almost smiled.

It was during the seventh course — venison, roasted black over vine cuttings — that the thing in the swan came back to him and would not leave.

He had done his duty. He had tasted the venison, cleared it, watched it devoured. But his tongue kept returning to the ghost-flavor the way a tongue returns to a chipped tooth. Cold iron. Cut bridge. Grief with a shape.

Here was the problem, and he turned it over while the hall glittered around him: memories did not get into food by accident. To cook a memory into a dish, someone had to *have* the memory, and *give* it, deliberately, into the sauce — the way a cook might render grief like fat. Which meant somewhere in the palace kitchens today there was a person carrying a grief like that, and either they had sold it, or spilled it, or been made to pour it out.

Or — and here his mind went quiet and cold — someone had bought it. Bought a memory and hidden it in the queen's own banquet, in the one place in the capital guaranteed to pass through a gifted palate unremarked, because who tastes a royal dish and expects to find anything but poison?

Whoever it was had counted on the tasters being ordinary men. Had counted on Bramwen's deafness. Had counted, perhaps, on Petra.

That thought sat in him like a swallowed stone through the eighth and ninth courses, through the sweetmeats and the candied violets, through the toasts. He performed his duties flawlessly. He tasted, he cleared, he nodded to Hollis. Inside, he was already writing the entry.

* * *

The banquet ended near midnight. The guests drained away in carriages and sedan chairs, the candles were snuffed in ranks, and the great hall settled into its overnight silence smelling of honey and smoke and a hundred bodies' worth of perfume.

Petra did not go up to his rooms above the kitchens. He went down.

The servants' corridor behind the buttery was dark except for one lantern left burning for late workers, and past it, through a door the palace believed held pickles, a stair wound down into cellars the crown had forgotten it owned — cellars leased, sixty years ago, to a tenant whose lease renewed itself without paperwork, because the Guild of Impossible Things had written the original contract and the Guild wrote very good contracts indeed.

Three turns of the stair, a door of black oak, a knock in threes and twos.

"Late," said the doorwarden, a huge soft-spoken man named Osk who had no gift at all and was therefore, per guild doctrine, incorruptible. Osk looked at Petra's face and something in his own shifted. "Bad tasting?"

"No poisoning."

"But bad."

Petra didn't answer. Osk didn't press. They had an understanding built of years: the taster brought the palace's secrets down these stairs, and no one asked how he'd come by them, and in exchange the guild's protection reached up the stairs and covered him like a roof.

The scriptorium occupied the largest cellar, a long low room lined floor to ceiling with pigeonhole shelving, and in the pigeonholes sat the inventory: thousands upon thousands of small bottles, each sealed with wax the color of its contents, each labeled in a code of dots and dashes only the senior members could read. Lamplight moved over the glass ranks like water over a shoal of fish. Standing among them always made Petra feel as if he were inside a frozen ocean — every bottle a drop of somebody's life, suspended, waiting to be drunk by whoever could pay.

Corvane was at the master desk at the far end, as he was every night, a lean gray man in a coat the color of dust, doing sums. He did not look up when Petra entered. "You're late."

"The banquet ran long. The queen's cousin told stories."

"Stories are expensive." Corvane dipped his pen. "You have an entry?"

"One anomaly." Petra crossed the room, took a blank leaf of saltmarsh reed-paper, ground a little ink — the tears were already mixed in the pot; the guild bought grief cheap and used everything — and began to write in his small exact hand.

*Course five of nine. Swan in honey-wormwood glaze. Service kitchen, palace. Flavor-profile anomalous: not toxic. Foreign residue consistent with a transferred memory of grief. Notes: cold iron; wet stone; severance (bridge-cut). Strength suggests recent extraction, donor living, donor present in or near palace grounds. Logged for inquiry.*

Corvane read it upside down, which he was adept at, and for a moment his pen stopped moving.

"Grief," he said.

"In a sauce."

"You're certain it wasn't the cook's mood bleeding through? Kitchen work draws strong feeling. Sad cooks salt everything with themselves."

"This wasn't seasoning." Petra capped the ink. "This was an ingredient. Deliberate. Someone put a memory into a dish served to the royal table, and they did it knowing the tasters would taste it, and knowing that only one kind of taster would even notice."

Corvane laid down his pen at last. He had a way of becoming very still that Petra had learned to distrust more than any motion. "And what kind is that?"

"You know what kind."

"I know what you've told me," Corvane said mildly. "A sensitive palate. A family inheritance. Nothing that need concern the Hunt." The Gift-Hunt had not yet been proclaimed — that was rumor still, criers warming their throats in private — but rumors of purges travel ahead of purges the way thunder travels ahead of rain, and both of them had heard the weather coming. "Careful, Voss. Curiosity is a debt, and the guild collects its debts in the coin you least want to spend."

"Then consider this curiosity an asset." Petra kept his voice level, his eyes on the ledger. Eleven years of practice, keeping his face smooth as poured wax. "Someone smuggled a bottled memory into the queen's banquet. Either they're testing the guild's product—" he saw Corvane's eyelids lower a fraction, the only tell he would ever give, and filed it away— "or they're using the guild's methods without paying for them. Either way, I imagine you'd want to know who."

For a long moment the lamplight ticked against the glass ranks, and Corvane looked at him across the master desk with that unreadable expression, and Petra understood suddenly that he had just shown his guildmaster something — that his gift was sharper than he claimed, that he noticed tells, that he was dangerous in exactly the ways the guild valued and feared.

"Log the anomaly," Corvane said finally. "Then log it again in the secondary book, coded. If it recurs, bring me the dish itself. Not the palace authorities. Not the crown's witch-finders. Me."

"And if the trail leads somewhere you don't want it to lead?"

Corvane smiled. It was the worst smile Petra knew — warm as a banked fire, and just as likely to burn the house down. "Everything leads somewhere I want it to lead. That is what it means to keep the books."

* * *

Petra climbed the cellar stairs close to two in the morning and walked home through streets gone blue and empty, past shuttered stalls and a drunk singing to a cat, past the crier's post on Copperlane Corner where, in a few days or weeks, a man would stand and proclaim that the old blood must register or burn.

He did not know that yet. But he felt it, the way his tongue felt poison before his mind named it: a bitterness gathering at the back of the city's throat.

In his rooms above the kitchens he lit one candle, took out his private book — the third ledger, the one no one knew existed, not the crown and not Corvane — and added a line beneath years of lines.

*Tasted grief tonight that had no owner I could find. It was placed, not spilled. Whoever placed it knows about palates like mine.*

He sat for a while after that, listening to the palace breathe, feeling the loneliness come up in him like groundwater. Somewhere below, in the frozen ocean of bottles, a vial of someone's worst night sat waiting for a buyer. Somewhere above, a queen slept in a bed he had never guarded, eating nothing he had ever tasted.

He blew out the candle.

Tomorrow he would ask questions in the kitchens, carefully, sideways, the way a man tests ice. Tomorrow he would begin pulling a thread that ran through the queen's own supper and down into the dark of the guild's vaults.

He did not yet know that the thread was tied around his own neck.
