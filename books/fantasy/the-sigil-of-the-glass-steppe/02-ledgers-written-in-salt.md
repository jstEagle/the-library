## 2. Ledgers Written in Salt

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its vault beneath a chandler's shop on Candlewick Row, where the smell of tallow and hot wax masked the older, stranger scent that clung to everything the guild touched—that faint mineral sweetness, like rain on warm glass, which Petra had learned to stop noticing years ago and had never quite succeeded in forgetting.

He descended by lamplight, as he did three nights out of every seven, down a stair so narrow his shoulders brushed both walls. The steps were worn into shallow bowls by generations of clerks before him. At the bottom, iron hinges groaned, and then the vault opened around him like the inside of a throat: shelves upon shelves of bottled memory, ranked in their thousands, each vial stoppered with wax the color of dried blood, each one humming—if you pressed your ear close—with the small, patient sound of a life waiting to be remembered.

Petra did not press his ear to them anymore. He had done it once, in his first month, against Corvane's explicit instruction, and had spent two days afterward weeping at a stranger's funeral without knowing whose grief was leaking through the glass. He had learned since. The ledger desk sat apart from the shelves, in a pool of its own lamplight, and there he worked: quill, ink, salt, and paper.

The salt was the guild's oldest instrument. Every entry began with a pinch scattered across the blotter, because memory left residue even on transactions conducted entirely in glass, and the salt drank what the air carried—flavor-ghosts, intent-traces, the faint sourness of a bargain about to curdle. When an entry was disputed, you could taste the salt and know who had lied. It was the closest thing the guild had to a conscience, and Corvane trusted it more than any person.

Tonight's trade was already waiting for him in the intake room: a widow named Marta Ellin, sixty-one years of age, who had come up from the river district with her wedding day sealed in a vial no longer than her thumb.

Petra had watched the exchange through the grate above—the guild required a second witness to every sale, though the witness was not permitted to speak—and now he sat alone with the record of it.

*One (1) memory: wedding day, full sequence, seller's own. Condition: well-preserved, lightly wept-over.* He hesitated over the last phrase, then struck *lightly* and wrote *often*. Honesty in the ledgers was a discipline like any other; sloppy books were how clerks disappeared.

*Consideration received: fourteen silver crowns, bread money per seller's statement.*

Bread money. Fourteen crowns would buy bread for a year, if she ate nothing else, and she would eat other things, or less than nothing, before the year turned. He knew how these trades went. The guild called them *liquidations*. The sellers came in hollow-eyed with hunger or debt or grief, and they walked out lighter by one day, and for a while they could not say why certain mornings felt like standing in a room with the furniture rearranged. Their husbands' faces still fit their husbands' names, but the first kiss, the nervous toast, the dance where he'd stepped on her hem twice and laughed—all of it gone, sold for flour and lamp oil, and the widow would spend the rest of her life reaching for a warmth she couldn't name, the way a tongue returns to the socket of a lost tooth.

And the vial would sit on shelf nine, row twelve, waiting for some buyer with coin enough to rent another person's joy.

Petra wrote the entry out clean. His hand was steady. He had made himself steady through long practice, the way a taster makes his face flat over a poisoned cup. But the quill slowed when he reached the final line, the one the guild required on every memory-sale:

*Seller advised of irreversibility. Seller's understanding confirmed.*

Confirmed by whom? By the broker, who earned commission on the sale. By the seal, which cared nothing for her tears. Petra had watched Marta Ellin nod when they asked if she understood, and he had watched the nod come from somewhere shallow, somewhere already starving, and he had thought: *that is not understanding. That is surrender wearing understanding's coat.*

He dipped the quill again and added, in the margin, in his smallest hand—a habit the guild had never forbidden, perhaps because it had never occurred to Corvane that a clerk's conscience needed somewhere to live:

*Marta Ellin, river district. Husband: Joren, wheelwright, dead four winters. She kept the ribbon from her hair that day. Asked twice whether the buyer might return it someday, unsold. Told yes. This is not true. There are no returns.*

Then he sanded the page, blotted it, and sat looking at the lamplight through the amber bottle of ink until the flame doubled and swam.

That was when he heard the footsteps on the stair.

There was only one man who came down here unannounced. Petra straightened the ledger, squared the salt dish, and arranged his face into the expression Corvane liked best: attentive, competent, empty of anything worth reading.

Corvane descended into the light like something surfacing. He was not a large man—he had the dry, folded quality of paper kept too long in a drawer—but he filled a room the way a verdict fills a courtroom. His robes were grey wool, undyed, unadorned; the guild wore no colors, because colors could be remembered, and Corvane had opinions about being remembered. His eyes were pale as skimmed milk and moved the way an auditor's eyes move: everything weighed, everything entered, nothing forgiven.

"Voss." He said the name the way he said all names—as though confirming an entry. "You're late finishing."

"The widow's trade wanted care."

"Trades don't want care. Trades want accuracy." Corvane crossed the floor, his shoes making no sound on the stone, which Petra had never understood and had stopped trying to. "Show me."

Petra turned the ledger toward him. Corvane read the way he did everything—quickly, completely, and with the particular attention of a man checking for theft. His gaze reached the margin note, and stopped.

The silence lasted long enough for Petra to count four separate ways the evening could end badly.

"You annotate," Corvane said.

"I keep context. Context protects the guild. If a sale is ever contested—"

"Contested." Corvane lifted the salt dish, considered the residue in it—Petra saw his nostrils flare, that faint involuntary drawing-in of breath that meant he was tasting the air's leavings, though Corvane had no gift, none that anyone had proven—and set it down again. "Do you know what curiosity is, Voss?"

"A clerk's tool."

"Curiosity," Corvane said, "is a debt."

He drew out the chair across the desk and sat, folding himself down with those dry rustling sounds, and for a moment he simply looked at Petra with the milk-pale eyes, and Petra held still under it the way he held still under a magistrate's stare, knowing that movement read as guilt.

"Everything in this vault was once someone's debt," Corvane went on. "A hunger. A fear. A love too heavy to carry uphill anymore. They bring us their burdens and we weigh them fairly—weigh them *better* than fairly, better than any moneylender or priest has ever weighed anything—and we pay, and they leave free. That is the whole of the guild's morality, and it is sufficient. But curiosity—" he touched the ledger's margin with one dry fingertip, not quite touching the ink— "curiosity means you've begun keeping a second account. Debts owed *to you*. Questions you expect answered. And a man with two sets of books, Voss, is either a fraud or a founder."

"Which are you?" Petra asked, before he could stop himself.

Something moved behind Corvane's face—not anger, nothing so warm. Recalculation, maybe. The look of a man discovering an asset has more uses than catalogued.

"Ask me again in twenty years," he said, "if you're still curious. That will tell me which." He rose, and the chair whispered shut behind him. "Finish the entry. File the vial yourself—shelf nine. And Voss." He paused at the edge of the lamplight, half his face in shadow. "Widows cry easily and remember poorly. Don't let her grief season your judgment. Sentiment is also a debt, and I have never known anyone to pay it back."

His shoes made no sound on the stairs. The door closed. The lock turned—Corvane always locked it from outside, a courtesy he called safety and Petra called a cage with good manners.

Petra sat alone with the lamp and the ledgers and the low hum of ten thousand lives in glass.

After a while he took up the vial of Marta Ellin's wedding day to carry it to shelf nine, and this was a mistake, or a discipline, or both—he had told himself it was discipline, handling the stock, learning the stock, because a taster who feared the cup was useless—but when his fingers closed around the glass, the memory breathed against his palm the way warm bread breathes, and he tasted it before he could stop himself: candle smoke and rosewater, a boy's nervous laughter, the rough sweetness of honey-cake, and underneath it all, threaded through everything like wire through clay, a joy so specific and so total that Petra stood in the vault of the Guild of Impossible Things and grieved for a woman's happiness she no longer owned.

He shelved it carefully among its neighbors—first kisses, dying words, a soldier's last morning, a mother's voice singing—and he noticed, as he always noticed and never said, that shelf nine's neighbors had changed. A gap at row twelve where a vial had been sold last week. And beside the gap, a cluster of new acquisitions that did not belong: memories with intake seals from districts the guild didn't serve, dates that predated the current ledger, and one vial, unlabeled, wrapped in black cloth, that gave off no flavor at all.

No flavor. Everything had flavor. Grief, joy, terror, boredom—even the emptiest life left some trace on the air. A vial without flavor was either empty or warded, and the guild did not sell empty things.

Petra looked at it for a long moment. Curiosity is a debt, Corvane had said. He could hear it now in the man's dry voice, feel it settling onto him like fine ash.

He did not touch it. He finished the entry, banked the lamp, and climbed the narrow stair with the widow's rosewater still ghosting the back of his tongue, and he told himself the unease in his chest was only the dark, only the lock turning behind him, only the ordinary loneliness of a man with two lives and no one to share either one with.

But three streets from home, past the shuttered chandlery and the sleeping wine-shops, he passed a crier's post where tomorrow's proclamation was already pasted up in the moonlight, edges flapping, and he stopped—because the first word, in letters tall enough to read from a horse, was *BY ORDER OF THE CROWN*, and the second word was a word he had been dreading his whole hidden life.

*Register*, the proclamation said. Or else a word that began with fire.

* * *

He stood in the cold reading it twice, three times, committing it to memory the way he committed flavors—the exact phrasing, the seal at the bottom, the date of enforcement—and the rosewater grief in his mouth turned slowly to something metallic, something that tasted the way blood tastes before you know you're bleeding.

Anyone bearing the old blood must register with the crown within thirty days. Any bearer failing to register would be taken. The proclamation did not say *burned*. It said *taken*, which was worse, because *taken* left room for the imagination, and Petra's imagination had tasted enough of history to fill in the blanks with terrible fluency.

The old blood. The gift-bearers. Memory-workers, taste-readers, truth-tongues, dreamers of other people's dreams—the scattered remnant of the people who had fought the throne in the ancient war, whose gifts had been outlawed for three hundred years, who survived now only by hiding their natures in humble trades. Midwives and cooks and confessors. Food-tasters.

*Him.*

Petra looked up and down the empty street. Nothing moved but the paper and the moonlight. Somewhere a dog was barking at nothing, or at everything.

Thirty days. He thought of the ledger in the vault below, with his careful entries, his marginal confessions written in a clerk's smallest hand—*this is not true, there are no returns*—pages and pages of proof that he served the trade in stolen souls. He thought of the neighbor boy who could find lost things by smelling them. He thought of his mother, whose face lived now only in the one memory he guarded harder than any vial.

They would come with lists. Lists were how hunts began; lists were how hunts had always begun. And the guild kept the finest lists in the capital—every seller, every buyer, every clerk—and Corvane, who weighed everything, who entered everything, who forgave nothing, had just spent an evening studying Petra's handwriting in the margins.

*Curiosity is a debt,* Corvane had said. And debts, in the guild, were collected.

Petra pulled the proclamation off the post—it came away with a tearing sound far too loud in the empty street—and folded it into his coat, and walked home fast, and did not sleep. And when the grey hour came, he took out pen and ink and began, very quietly and very carefully, to plan which pages of the guild's perfect memory he could afford to burn—and which pages, when the time came, he would need to survive.
