## 3. The Proclamation

The bells woke the city before the criers did.

Petra had been awake anyway. He was always awake before dawn now, lying in his narrow room above the tallow-chandler's shop on Vetch Street, listening to the city breathe and trying to guess its mood by sound alone—the way he could guess a soup's mood by steam alone, or a man's mood by the salt on his upper lip when they shook hands. Lately the city breathed like a man with a fever: hot, shallow, waiting to sweat something out.

Then the bells. Not the carillon of Saint Ovelun that marked the hours, not the alarm-bells of the harbor gates. These were the deep bells in the citadel tower that had not rung since before Petra was born—his mother had told him about them once, her voice going strange and quiet, her hands stilling over whatever she was doing. *When those bells ring, Petya,* she'd said, *you count your spoons, lock your door, and be nobody.* She had said it as a recipe. Everything she taught him came as a recipe. That was how memory-workers talked, before the last purge took them.

He counted his spoons. He locked his door. And then, because he had never once in his life managed to be nobody, he went up to the roof.

The fog was still peeling off the rooftops when the first crier reached the fountain at the corner of Vetch and Candlemaker's Row. Petra heard him before he saw him—a raw, official voice, the kind of voice that had been trained out of all human warmth—and then the man emerged from the mist in the livery of the crown, a boy with a drum walking behind him, and unrolled a paper with a snap that carried across the square.

*By order of Her Majesty Queen Isolde the Second, and by the authority of the Office of Purity—*

Petra's stomach dropped through four floors of the building beneath him.

*—let it be known throughout the capital and its liberties that the Gift-Hunt is declared. All persons bearing the old blood, being possessed of unnatural faculties, arts of thought-reading, taste-divination, dream-walking, memory-craft, or any other working forbidden under the Edict of Ashes, shall present themselves for registration within seven days at the Hall of Registers.*

*Those who register shall be marked, listed, and bound to lawful employ.*

*Those who do not register, being discovered, shall be burned.*

The boy hit the drum twice. The crier moved on toward the grain market, and his voice went with him, and the words stayed where they were, hanging in the fog over Vetch Street like something spilled that would never wipe clean.

*Burned.* Not exiled. Not imprisoned. Burned. The old word, the mother-word, the word from the recipes. Petra stood on the cold lead of the roof with his hands flat against his thighs because if he didn't give them something to do they would shake, and he watched the square below begin to fill with people who had come out into the gray morning to hear their fate read to them like a menu.

* * *

He told himself he was going down for bread. He was very good at telling himself things; it was most of what kept the two halves of his life sewn together. Down the stairs, past the chandler's shutters, out into the street where the fog had thinned to a dirty lace and the smell of the city arrived all at once—coal smoke, horse dung, wet stone, and underneath it, faint as a signature at the bottom of a letter, fear. The whole street smelled faintly of fear this morning, the way a sickroom smells faintly of fever even when the patient smiles.

Marta Kessel was already at the baker's stall, arguing about the price of rye loaves the way she argued every morning, hands flying, gray hair escaping its pins. She lived two doors down from Petra in a room she shared with her grandson Tam, who was nine and could find anything you lost—keys, rings, a whole year of someone's life if you let him look too long. Marta called it *knack*. Everyone on Vetch Street called it knack. It was easier that way. Knack was a thing children had, like fast feet or good pitch, and it passed, and no one burned for it.

Tam was beside her, holding her basket, doing his trick for the baker's boy: eyes shut, face scrunched, pointing at the loose cobblestone where the baker's boy's lost copper sat hidden. The baker's boy dug it up laughing. Marta swatted Tam's shoulder without breaking off her argument. The whole little scene was so ordinary that Petra felt, absurdly, like weeping.

That was when the wagons came.

They came up Candlemaker's Row two abreast, plain carts with plain men in the livery of the Office of Purity—gray coats, gray hoods pushed back, iron collars stamped with a closed eye. No drums for these ones. They stopped at the mouth of Vetch Street and a sergeant unfolded the same paper the crier had held, and the market noise died the way water dies when it hits sand, spreading out thin and then gone.

They weren't there for Tam. Petra understood that quickly, horribly, watching the sergeant work down his list with a finger. They were there for names that had been given to them—by whom, Petra wondered, and felt the question go cold in his chest, because he knew exactly who gave names to the Office of Purity. Neighbors gave names. Debtors gave names. Guilds gave names.

"Oren Vasik," the sergeant read.

The wheelwright's apprentice. Twenty years old, maybe twenty-one. A big gentle boy who could put his hand on a broken axle and tell you which part dreamed of failing. *Knack*, everyone said. *Just knack.*

Oren wasn't at the market. His mother was. She came forward with her hands up, palms out, saying something Petra couldn't catch—saying it fast, the way you talk when you already know nothing you say will matter—and the gray men took her arms anyway, gently, the way you handle something you've been instructed not to bruise, because damaged property reflects poorly on the invoice.

"If he registers," she was saying, louder now, "he'll come in, he'll register, I swear it on the queen's name—he's at the shop, he sleeps at the shop—"

"Ma'am." The sergeant's voice had the trained warmth of a man who has said this many times and will say it again after lunch. "If he comes in within the seven days, all this will have been unnecessary."

"All this," Oren's mother said, looking down at herself, at the grip on her arms, "is already."

They took her anyway. Not to burn—she wasn't on the list—but as surety, the sergeant said, and the word went through the crowd like a fishhook through a net, catching everything. Surety. Hostage. The word made it real in a way *burned* hadn't. *Burned* was scripture, was history, was something that happened to other centuries. *Surety* was Tuesday.

The carts rolled away with Oren's mother standing between two gray men, very straight, not crying, and the market breathed back in, and the bread line re-formed, and Marta pulled Tam close and said, in a bright carrying voice meant for the whole street to hear, "Well. Good riddance to that sort. My Tam's got no more knack than a turnip."

Tam looked up at her. He was nine. He understood perfectly well what she was doing, and he did the cruelest, bravest thing a nine-year-old could do: he played along. "No knack," he said loudly. "None."

Petra bought his loaf. His hands did not shake. He had learned long ago, at banquets, in front of princes, with poison or its absence cooling on his tongue, how to keep his face a locked room. But inside the locked room, something was turning over furniture.

Because here was the arithmetic he could not stop doing, standing in the bread line with his heart going like a drummer-boy's: the Gift-Hunt did not ask whether your gift was great. It asked whether your gift existed. Taste-divination. The words had gone into him like a hook into soft flesh. There was a category with his name pre-written on it, and the crown had invented the category overnight, and somewhere in the palace there was a page with *Petra Voss, royal food-taster* on it already, or would be soon, because tasters were examined, tasters were tested, tasters were required to demonstrate—

No. They weren't. That was the point of him. The best taster was invisible; a taster anyone suspected was a taster whose verdicts started wars of doubt at every table. The Office of Purity wanted loud gifts, obvious gifts, the wheelwright's apprentice with his hand on the axle. Quiet gifts might pass. Might.

*Might* was not a word to build a life on. He had a double ledger in a vault under the Glasswrights' Guild with his own handwriting in it—not confessions, never quite confessions, but entries no honest clerk should know how to write. Entries describing trades of memory in terms of flavor. Entries only a gifted man could have composed.

Seven days. He had seven days to become nobody, and he had spent thirty years failing at it.

* * *

The guild vault smelled of cold glass and older secrets, as always—wax and vinegar and, beneath both, the faint sweetish ozone note of bottled memory, hundreds of vials sleeping in their racks like a choir holding one endless note. Petra had loved that smell once. Tonight it smelled to him like a list of charges.

Corvane was at the high desk with the day's intake, a lamp turned low, reading glasses perched on his long nose. His guildmaster was a narrow man built like a closing door—all edges and hinges, everything about him designed to shut. He did not look up when Petra came down the spiral stair.

"You've heard," Corvane said. It was not a question. News traveled faster than criers; news always traveled fastest among the people it intended to eat.

"The whole street's heard."

"And?" Corvane dipped his pen, wrote a figure, blotted it. "Is your palate disturbed? Shall I enter *disturbed* in the margin?"

"I want to know what the guild intends to do."

"Now you want to know what the guild intends." Corvane set the pen down with a small precise click and finally looked at him, and his eyes in the lamplight were the pale amber of weak tea. "For six years you have written my ledgers and asked me nothing, which is why you have lasted six years. Curiosity is a debt, Petra. Every question you ask, you owe me an answer to later. You are asking a large one tonight."

"The bottlers," Petra said. "The readers. Half the guild bears the old blood. What happens to them in seven days?"

"What happens to stock when the market shifts." Corvane rose and began moving along the rack wall, trailing one fingertip above the vials without touching them, the way a miser trails a finger above coin. "Registration is not death, despite the sermons. Registered gift is *documented* gift. Documented gift can be licensed, contracted, taxed." He smiled without warmth, a bookkeeper's smile, arithmetic wearing a face. "The Office of Purity thinks it is conducting a purge. In fact it is conducting an inventory. Scarcity, Petra. Do you know what scarcity does to the price of a bottled wedding day? To the price of a king's forgotten afternoon? By winter, half the peerage will pay us in land."

"You're going to hand them over." Petra heard his own voice go flat and strange. "You're going to register them yourself. Deliver them. Marked, listed, bound to lawful employ."

"I am going to keep the guild alive, which is the only duty I have ever sworn." Corvane turned fully now, and the lamplight made a thin gold edge along him, like the gilt on a ledger's spine. "The alternative is that the guild burns with everything in it, every vial, every trade, six hundred years of impossible things, gone in one cheerful afternoon of fire. You may prefer that, if martyrdom flatters you. I find it poor accounting."

It was monstrous. It was also, Petra admitted in the locked room behind his face, coherent. That was the horror of Corvane: he was never mad, never cruel for pleasure. He simply priced human beings correctly according to his own ledger, and his ledger had no column for anything but survival and profit. Somewhere below the vault, in the guild's true deep places, were vials that hummed. Somewhere above, in the palace, was a dying queen. And between those two facts stood this narrow man with tea-colored eyes, doing sums.

"Go home," Corvane said, returning to his desk. "Take the night's copy sheets with you. And Petra—" the pen paused mid-stroke, "—your entry for the widow Anselm. The wedding-day trade. You left a remark in the margin. I don't pay for remarks."

Petra's blood went slow and heavy. "I don't recall a remark."

"*She wept while she signed. Who are we, that we buy tears at bread-prices?*" Corvane recited it without inflection, from memory, which was worse than if he had read it. "Fourteen words of sentiment, entered in the guild's permanent record by its senior ledgerman. Sentiment is evidence, boy. Evidence is leverage. Leverage gets used—by me, if I must, and by others, if I'm careless." He held out his hand. "The sheet."

Petra gave him the sheet. Of course he did. And he climbed the spiral stair with the lamp-glow dying above him and Corvane's voice following, mild as milk:

"Curiosity is a debt, Petra. Mind what questions you can afford."

* * *

He did not go home.

He went home, in fairness—climbed the stairs, lit the candle, sat at the table with the copy sheets he was supposed to transcribe and return by morning. But the house around him had changed weight. Every board seemed to listen. Outside, somewhere on the Ring Boulevard, another crier was working his way through the districts, and each faint repetition of *shall be burned* arrived through the window glass like a pulse.

He spread the copy sheets and found the widow Anselm's trade among them, in his own careful clerk's hand: date, item, consideration, flavor-notes. *Warmth of June. Bread and beeswax. A man's laugh, unrecorded elsewhere.* Fourteen marginal words in his smaller, angrier script.

Who are we, that we buy tears at bread-prices?

He had written it in the vault, inked into permanence, because for one moment the grief of a stranger had gotten through his armor, and now the sentence sat in the guild's records like a splinter in a thumb—evidence, as Corvane said, of sentiment; and sentiment, in the capital of the Gift-Hunt, in the seventh day of grace, was one short step from evidence of the gift itself. No honest clerk described a memory's *flavor*. Only a palate like his could have written *bread and beeswax* about a bottled June. Corvane might not use it. Corvane might sell it. Corvane might simply lose it, carelessly, into a gray-coated sergeant's hands, the way one loses a key one no longer needs.

Or worse: the Hunt would not need Corvane at all. The Hunt had inventories. The Hunt had lists. And somewhere between the Office of Purity and the Glasswrights' Guild there were surely men who knew that the guild kept a ledgerman who tasted.

Petra looked at the fourteen words for a long time.

Then he took the copy sheet to the hearth, and fed it to the coals, and watched his own handwriting curl and blacken and lift away as ash. The widow Anselm's grief went up the chimney in a thread of smoke, unsold, unpriced, unrecorded—the only act of mercy he had performed in years, and even it was really just fear wearing mercy's coat. He understood that. He was not sentimental about himself. He had burned the sentence not to save her dignity but to save his neck, and the difference mattered, and he filed the knowledge away in the same locked room where he kept everything else.

But the fire did something else too. As the page went, Petra stood in his rented room with heat on his shins and looked at the flames and found that the shaking had stopped. Fear, he realized, came in two kinds: the kind that scattered a man, and the kind that gathered him. For thirty years he had been scattered—a taster who was secretly a reader, a servant who was secretly a ledgerman, a son of burned memory-workers pretending his blood ran ordinary and cool. The Hunt had just done him the service of making pretense expensive.

Seven days. In seven days the lists would reach the palace servants, and tasters would be questioned, and *taste-divination* would be read aloud in rooms where his name was known. He could run. The steppe roads were open, the glass flats east of the city swallowed travelers whole; men vanished out onto them every season. Or he could stay, in the belly of the thing, where a food-taster heard everything—who dined with whom, who feared which dish, whose cup was poured by whose hand—and a ledgerman saw everything, and a gifted man could taste a lie through three courses and a closed door.

The queen's household. The Office of Purity. Corvane's clean, monstrous arithmetic. Somewhere in the overlap of those circles was the answer to who had given the gray men their lists—and Petra had just begun, with the last of the page smoking on the coals, to suspect that the answer wore many collars and signed its work in several hands.

He banked the fire. He washed the ash from his fingers, thoroughly, a taster's habit—clean hands, clean palate, nothing carried over from the last dish to spoil the next.

Tomorrow he would taste for the queen's private table. He had done it a dozen times without thinking anything of it beyond the honor and the hazard.

Tonight, standing in the dark with the smell of burnt ink still in the room, he thought of her Majesty's kitchen summons, delivered that same afternoon by a footman who had seemed—Petra turned the memory over now, tasting it again, and found what he had missed—the footman had seemed *afraid*. Not of the queen. For her.

The summons had said: *come at the ninth hour. Her Majesty eats alone.*

And queens, Petra knew better than almost anyone, were the one diners in the world who could afford no poison but their own.
