## 4. What the Queen Cannot Remember

The summons came at dusk, carried by a page whose livery was too clean for the errand.

"Her Majesty requires her taster."

Not the kitchens. Not the banquet hall with its forty guards and forty dishes and its careful theater of pewter spoons and waiting breaths. *Privately.* In nine years of service, Petra Voss had tasted for the queen exactly twice, and both times had been ceremonies—performances staged before the full court, where the tasting was less a safeguard than a ritual, the crown's way of showing itself it still feared something enough to test for it. A private tasting was different. A private tasting meant someone close to the queen was afraid.

Or someone close to the queen was already poisoned.

He dressed carefully. Dark coat, no sigils—the Gift-Hunt criers were still working their way down the Processional, and even a taster's guild pin could draw eyes these days. He checked his ledger of poisons out of habit, though he knew every entry by heart: two hundred and eleven substances catalogued by taste, color, and aftereffect, from quicksilver's coppery slick to widow's-tears, which tasted of nothing at all until an hour later, when your blood learned to remember drowning.

None of them would explain what he'd found at last night's banquet.

The ghost of grief in the seventh course. He had turned it over all night like a coin of unknown minting. Someone else's sorrow, folded into the food the way a memory-worker folds intent into wine—and there should have been no one left in the capital with the skill to do it, not since the last purge burned the craft out of the old quarter twenty years ago. Except him. And except whoever had seasoned that soup.

*Curiosity is a debt,* Corvane had told him once, in the vault, over the widow's sold wedding day. Petra was beginning to suspect the debt was coming due.

* * *

The queen's private apartments occupied the eastern face of the palace, where the morning light came first and the evening light lingered longest—a vanity of architecture, the architects' small rebellion against mortality. Two guards flanked the door, and between them stood Mistress Halvard, the queen's lady-in-waiting, a woman built like a closed door herself.

"You will taste everything she takes tonight," Halvard said. "Everything. If you find anything amiss—" She stopped. Her jaw worked as though the next words were heavy cargo. "If you find anything at all, you will tell no one but me."

"And if what I find is not a poison?"

Something flickered across Halvard's face—too fast to name, gone before it finished arriving. "Then you'll be paid double to keep silent about that instead."

Petra followed her through a succession of rooms growing softer and smaller, silk replacing stone, until they reached a chamber lit by a single fire and a dozen candles that had been allowed to burn down past the point of prudence. The queen sat by the window in a plain grey gown, hands folded in her lap, watching the glass steppe glow on the horizon like a second sunset that refused to die.

Queen Isolde Vane. Forty-one years old. Twenty-two years on the throne. The woman whose face was stamped on every coin Petra had ever stolen or spent.

She looked up at him with the polite blankness of a stranger being introduced at a wedding.

"This is my taster?" she said to Halvard. "He seems very young."

"Your Majesty, he has served the crown for nine years."

"Has he." The queen studied him, and Petra felt the strange vertigo of being weighed by a gaze that held nothing behind it—or rather, held something else behind it, something that wasn't hers. "Come closer, then. Let me see your face."

He approached and knelt as protocol demanded. Up close, he could see what the distance had hidden: the fine tremor in her hands, the skin stretched too smoothly over cheekbones that had grown prominent the way old women's do, the eyes a shade too wide, drinking in the room as if the room might leave if unwatched.

And beneath it all—the smell. Not a poison smell. Petra's gift worked through the tongue, but the tongue was only the gate; beyond it lay a country where flavors became other things, where bitterness could be a lie and sweetness a confession, and the air around the queen carried a flavor he had no ledger-entry for. Old smoke. Iron. A cold so deep it burned. It clung to her like perfume worn so long its wearer could no longer detect it.

There was a splinter in her. Something lodged under the surface of her self, foreign and festering, and her own memories were pulling away from it the way water pulls away from ice.

"Tonight," Halvard said briskly, "she will take broth, bread, sweetened milk, and a cordial. Nothing from the common kitchens. Everything prepared under my eye."

"I understand, my lady."

The meal began. Petra tasted each dish in sequence, the little theater of spoon and pause and swallow, and found them clean—clean of poison, anyway. But the broth was made with rosemary, and when his tongue touched it, his gift flared open without permission, the way it always did with strong herbs, and for half a heartbeat he was standing in a burning orchard holding someone's hand, and the hand belonged to—

He set the spoon down. Carefully. As if it might go off.

"Is it acceptable?" Halvard asked.

"It's rosemary," Petra said. "I'm fond of rosemary. Forgive me a moment of sentiment."

It was a good lie. He was getting better at those; the Guild of Impossible Things rewarded fluency in falsehood the way other guilds rewarded carpentry. But the queen was watching him with that too-wide gaze, and when he met it, she tilted her head like a bird considering whether something was food or threat.

"Say that again," she said softly. "About the rosemary."

"That I'm fond of it, Majesty?"

"No." She leaned forward. "Before. You said—I heard you say—" Her brow creased, and for a moment the splinter seemed to shift under her surface, and the air went cold and smoky again, iron and burning orchards. "You sounded like him."

"Like who, Your Majesty?"

"My brother," the queen said. "Tomas."

The name dropped into the room and kept falling. Halvard had gone very still by the door. Petra understood, from the quality of that stillness, that Tomas was a name no one had spoken aloud in this chamber for years.

"Your Majesty," Halvard began carefully, "Prince Tomas died when you were—"

"When we were children, yes, I know, everyone tells me, everyone is very certain." The queen's voice stayed pleasant, but her hands had begun to tremble in earnest now, plucking at her grey skirt. "But he was here. Last week he was here, sitting where this young man sits, complaining about the rosemary, because Tomas hated rosemary, said it tasted like a medicine chest, and I laughed and told him a king shouldn't complain about his food, and he said—" her voice caught, snagged on something invisible— "he said, *one day you'll give me anything I ask, Isolde, and you won't even remember asking.*"

Silence. The fire popped. On the horizon, the glass steppe held its light like a held breath.

"Majesty," Petra said gently, "I've never met Prince Tomas."

"Of course not." She blinked, and the strangeness receded, banked like a covered flame. "Forgive me. I confuse... lately I confuse things. Halvard tells me it's the season. A woman's melancholy." She smiled, and it was the smile from the coins, flawless and empty. "Well. Taster. Tell me honestly—is my food safe?"

Petra considered the question the way he considered all questions now: as an entry in one of his ledgers. The visible ledger said: *broth, bread, milk, cordial—no poison detected.* The hidden ledger said: *there is something inside this woman that is eating her alive, and it tastes of war.*

"Your food is safe, Majesty," he said. "That I can swear."

"Good." She nodded, satisfied, and looked back toward the window, toward the steppe. Then, without turning: "Do you know what I miss most, taster? Everyone assumes it's my youth, or my husband, or my beauty. No. What I miss is the beginning of things. My coronation—I can't find it. I know it happened; there are portraits. But when I reach for it, it's like reaching into a drawer that's been emptied and refilled with someone else's linens. I ruled through the famine year. Did I? There are documents. I don't remember signing them. My hand remembers the weight of the pen and nothing else." She laughed lightly. "Listen to me. Melancholy indeed."

Halvard stepped forward. "Majesty, the taster has duties—"

"Yes, yes. Everyone has duties. That's the whole of government, isn't it? Duties wearing each other's coats." The queen waved a hand, dismissing him, and then—just as he reached the door—said, without looking around, in a voice suddenly young and lost:

"He complained about the rosemary. You did too. Same voice. Isn't that strange?"

Petra bowed and left, and did not let his hands shake until the door was shut behind him.

* * *

Halvard walked him out personally, which was itself unprecedented, through the soft rooms and the hard ones, until they stood in a corridor lit by a single lamp and empty of ears.

"How long," Petra asked, "has she been forgetting?"

Halvard's face performed neutrality the way a bad actor performs a king. "The queen is tired. She carries the weight of—"

"I've seen palsy. I've seen the wasting sickness. I've seen grief." Petra kept his voice level. "This isn't any of them. She isn't losing her mind, my lady. She's losing her *reign*. Specific years, specific events, peeled away in order. Someone has been—"

He stopped himself. *Someone has been harvesting her.* He couldn't finish the sentence aloud, because finishing it meant admitting he knew such a thing could be done, and knowing such a thing marked him as gifted, and gifted meant register-or-burn.

But Halvard was watching him with an expression that had stopped pretending. "Someone has been *what*, taster? Finish the sentence. I've served her for thirty years. I have watched her misplace a decade like a housekeeper misplacing keys, and every physician in the capital has prescribed rest and tonics and prayers, and none of them—" her composure cracked, just once, a hairline fracture— "none of them can tell me why my queen looks at me some mornings like I'm the ghost."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere below, the palace kitchens were banking their fires for the night, and the smell of tomorrow's bread rose faintly through the floors.

"If I answer you," Petra said slowly, "you may wish you hadn't asked. And you may wish I hadn't answered. There are people—there is a Hunt, my lady, and it doesn't distinguish between the sickness and the cure."

"I have buried two kings and raised a third's widow onto the throne," Halvard said. "I stopped wishing things hadn't happened somewhere around the second funeral. Finish your sentence."

So he did. Quietly. About memories that could be taken and bottled and sold. About a splinter lodged in the queen that her own life was rejecting, grain by grain, year by year. About the flavor of it—smoke, iron, cold—that he had caught on her skin the moment he knelt.

He expected disbelief. He got worse: recognition. Halvard closed her eyes briefly, the way one does upon hearing a diagnosis already suspected.

"The physicians called it the fading," she said. "A court illness. Courtly melancholy." A short, bitter laugh. "You're telling me my queen has been robbed. In her own house. For years. Under my nose."

"I'm telling you she's still being robbed. Whatever it is, it's still lodged in her. Still feeding. Tonight, when she spoke of her brother—" Petra chose the words with a taster's care— "it moved. Like a fish under ice. Whatever took her memories left something behind in the hole, and it isn't hers, and it isn't sleeping."

Halvard straightened, and thirty years of palace survival reassembled themselves around her like armor. "Who could do such a thing?"

That was the question, wasn't it. Petra thought of Corvane's vault beneath the counting-house, the rows of stoppered glass, the widow's wedding day filed away like a debt instrument. He thought of the soup at last night's banquet, seasoned with a dead man's grief. He thought of a queen who had forgotten her own coronation, in a palace where every door required a password and every password could be bought.

"I don't know yet," he lied, because the truth—*my own guildmaster, possibly; certainly my own guild*—was a confession that would burn them both. "But I intend to find out. Give me access to the kitchens. All of them. Whoever is doing this has to pass something through the palace's food and drink—it's the only road into her that never closes. And roads," he added, "leave tracks. Even for someone with my kind of tongue."

"Your kind of tongue," Halvard repeated flatly. "Yes. I wondered when you'd get around to mentioning that."

"My lady—"

"Spare me the disclaimers. I don't care what you are. Do you understand? The Hunt can burn every gifted soul from here to the steppe and it won't warm my queen's grave one degree sooner, because right now the only person in this palace who can diagnose her is standing in front of me apologizing for his own mouth." She fixed him with a stare that would have curdled milk. "Find what's poisoning her, taster. Use whatever tongue you have. And Petra Voss—if you betray her, I will not report you to the Hunt. I'll handle you myself."

It was, he reflected later, the most honest offer of alliance he'd received in years.

* * *

He walked home through streets that smelled of rain and woodsmoke and fear. The Gift-Hunt notices had multiplied on every post and archway overnight, the ink still wet in places: REGISTER OR BURN. Below the crier's square, someone had scrawled a reply in charcoal—*we remember what you forget*—and someone else had scrubbed at it, leaving only ghosts of letters.

Ghosts of letters. Ghosts of grief in banquet soup. A ghost of a brother in a dying queen's mouth.

In his rooms, Petra lit one candle and opened his private ledger—the true one, the one written in a shorthand only he could read—and recorded the day.

*The queen carries a foreign memory. It feeds. It wears the shape of the dead. The palace is compromised at a depth I cannot measure, because the road into her runs through the kitchens, and I cannot search the kitchens alone.*

He paused, quill hovering.

*I know where bottled memories are bought and sold. I keep the books for the men who sell them. Which means either the guild is innocent and someone stole from us—*

The candle flickered.

*—or Corvane sold her the knife and kept the receipt.*

Below that, he wrote the flavor. The taster's discipline: record everything, trust nothing, forget nothing. *Smoke. Iron. Cold like a struck bell. And underneath, older than all of it: a war.*

He was blotting the page when he heard it—faint, from the street below, cutting through the rain. A sound he'd catalogued long ago in another ledger entirely, the one where he kept the guild's trades.

Glass. Many pieces of it, wrapped in wool, being carried carefully through the night. Bottles. Someone moving inventory.

Petra blew out the candle and went to the window. Three figures in oilskins moved along the far side of the street, burdened and bent, heading not toward the guild counting-house but away from it—toward the river gates, toward the wealthier quarters, toward the palace hill.

Deliveries. Someone in the palace had placed an order.

And the queen was forgetting, course by course, reign by reign, while something wore her brother's voice and waited inside her like a tenant who intended to inherit.

Petra took down his coat. Tomorrow he would walk into those kitchens and read every cup, every pot, every plate, and follow the flavor to whichever traitor was feeding off the crown of Vane.

He just didn't yet know that the trail would split in two.
