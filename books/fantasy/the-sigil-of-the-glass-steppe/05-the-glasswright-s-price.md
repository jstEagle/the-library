## 5. The Glasswright's Price

The summons came the way all of Corvane's summons came: a plain card slipped beneath Petra's door, bearing no name, no seal, only a single pressed flower of salt-glass that bloomed violet when it touched lamplight. Anyone else would have taken it for a curiosity. Petra knew it for what it was—a key with teeth on both ends. It opened the guild vault at the hour when honest men were asleep, and it reminded him that the door could just as easily close behind him.

He dressed in the dark. He did not wake the landlady. He did not look at the ledger page he had burned three nights ago, its ash still ghosting the grate like a bruise on the hearthstone.

Outside, Vellum Row lay under fog thick as milk. The Gift-Hunt had been running nine days now, and the city wore it the way a body wears a fever—flushed in some quarters, cold and clammy in others. From somewhere near Tanner's Bridge came the flat knock of a crier's staff and the recitation Petra could now recite himself, word for word, the way condemned men learn their own sentences: *All who bear the old blood shall present themselves for registration within thirty days. Those who conceal their gift shall be burned. Those who name the concealed shall be paid.*

*Paid.* As if loyalty were a trade good. Though perhaps, he thought grimly, pulling his collar up, that was precisely how Corvane would put it.

The guildhouse stood at the end of Vellum Row where the street gave up pretending to be respectable. To passersby it was a glasswright's workshop of modest prosperity—kilns in back, finished panes stacked in the window, a sign depicting a blowpipe crossed with a pair of shears. The windows were real glass, flawless and clear, which should have been the first warning to anyone who thought about it. A firm that made glass this perfect had nothing left to sell but the impossible.

Petra set the salt-flower against the door and felt the lock taste it, so to speak—the mechanism reading the violet bloom the way his own tongue read a broth. Inside, the shop smelled of hot sand and vinegar, and beyond it, down the stair that creaked on every fourth step because Corvane refused to have it mended (*a man who forgets where his floor is unsafe forgets where his enemies are*), the vault waited with its lamp already lit.

Corvane was waiting too. He sat at the great recording desk with his coat off and his sleeves rolled, a big man gone soft at the edges the way a candle goes soft—not melting, exactly, but losing the crispness of his own outline. His hands, though, were still a craftsman's hands: broad, scarred at the knuckles from old kiln work, steady as a scale's arm. He was writing. Of course he was writing. Corvane wrote the way other men breathed.

"You burned a page," he said without looking up.

Petra stopped on the last step. "I archived it."

"You burned it." Corvane finished his line, blotted it, and set down his pen with the care of a man laying down a knife he might need again soon. "Entry four hundred and eleven. A taster's fee, logged as a tasting, for a client whose name you recorded as *discretion*. You fed the fire rather than the ledger. In eleven years, Petra, you have never once broken an entry." He looked up then. His eyes were the pale gray of glass before it takes color. "So either you've begun to steal from me, or something frightened you badly enough to make arithmetic feel dangerous. Which was it?"

"The Hunt frightens everyone."

"The Hunt frightens the guilty and the stupid, and it has never once frightened you." Corvane rose, joints cracking, and crossed to the shelf where the night's work waited—a rack of vials in padded slots, each one stoppered with wax stamped by the sigil Petra knew better than his own signature: a cup and a closed hand. "Sit. We're going to do an audit."

"I'm your taster," Petra said. "Not your clerk."

"You are my palate," Corvane said, "which is a far more valuable instrument than any clerk, and tonight I intend to use it properly. Sit down, boy. This will go easier if you don't make me explain it twice."

There were two stools at the tasting bench. Petra took one because taking the other would mean leaving, and leaving meant the conversation would happen later, on Corvane's terms instead of now, on these. He sat. The bench between them was scarred wood worn pale in two places—one where Corvane rested his elbows, one newer, shallower hollow that Petra had spent eleven years pretending wasn't shaped exactly like his own forearms.

Corvane brought out a tray. On it, six glasses, each holding a measure of some pale liquid, each covered with a square of clean linen. Beneath the linen, Petra's gift stirred like a dog hearing its name.

"Six samples," Corvane said. "From our reserve stock. I want dates. Provenance. Seller, if you can find the seller's flavor in them—you usually can; people leave more of themselves in what they sell than they think. And I want—" he paused, and something moved behind his face like a fish under ice, "—particular attention to number four."

*Number four.*

Petra lifted the first glass, drew back the linen, and tasted. A fingertip's worth on the tongue, held, read, spat into the brass cuspidor. Old grief, well-preserved. A woman's memory of a funeral—rain on black wool, the specific loneliness of being the only one weeping. He gave the date within a season, the seller's age, her habit of chewing fennel seeds. Corvane noted it all without comment.

The second was joy, cheap and commercial—a wedding dance bought and sold a dozen times over until the memory had gone thin as broth made from a bone boiled twice. The third was terror, recent, raw; Petra's stomach tightened around it and he made himself finish the reading anyway. A man fleeing across rooftops. The Hunt again. Business was good.

Then the fourth glass.

He knew before the linen came away. He knew from the weight of it in his hand—from the way the liquid sat in the glass with a faint luminescence, like moonlight kept in a jar—and most of all he knew from the hum, the faint sub-audible vibration he felt not in his ears but in his teeth, in the roots of his tongue, the way you feel a struck bell through a table.

Because he had tasted this before. Ten days ago, in a banquet hall full of candles and lies, in a dish of quail that no poison ledger could name: a splinter of someone else's past lodged in the food like a shard of mirror in a loaf. Grief with a crown on it. A memory wearing ermine.

He tasted it now, formally, professionally, and let his face do nothing at all. It was harder than anything he'd ever done. The memory poured into him the way water finds a crack: a throne room hung in black, a field of dead men stretching to a horizon that smoked, a young woman's voice cracking on an order she swore she would never give, and underneath it all, vast and patient, the pressure of a choice so heavy it had bent a reign around itself—

He spat. Wiped his mouth. Set the glass down with a small, precise click.

"Royal vintage," he said. "Palace-grade preservation work. Whoever bottled it used the old method—salt-wax, triple-seal. It's been in storage a long time." He met Corvane's gray eyes. "It's hers. It's the queen's."

The silence went on long enough that Petra heard the lamp flame working at its wick.

"Say the rest," Corvane said quietly.

"You sold it." Petra heard his own voice come out flat and strange, as if someone else were using his mouth. "Years ago. Before I ever kept your books. The queen's memory—the one that's killing her right now, the one that has her forgetting her own coronation, calling chambermaids by her dead brother's name—that's been sitting in your vault earning interest while she rots from the inside out."

"Yes," Corvane said.

No excuse. No ornament. Just *yes*, laid on the bench between them like a coin.

Petra stood up. His legs worked, which surprised him. "How long?"

"Twenty-two years. She sold it herself—or her spymaster did, on her instruction, though the paperwork named neither. A reigning monarch cannot be seen selling pieces of herself, can she? Bad for the coinage." Corvane spoke in the tone he used for inventory: unhurried, exact, faintly bored. "She wanted the war forgotten. Not remembered and resisted—forgotten, root and branch, because remembering it tempted her. There are things locked beneath the Glass Steppe, Petra, things the old kings buried with oaths and blood, and the memory of where those locks are and how they open—that was what she carried, and carrying it was eating her alive even then. So she amputated. We bottled. Standard transaction, honestly conducted, fairly priced. She signed nothing because queens don't sign, but her gold was real and her intent was clear, and I'll stand before any tribunal in this kingdom and say the Guild of Impossible Things acted as a faithful broker."

"A broker." Petra laughed, and hated the sound. "You're telling me the guild was *hired*."

"Of course we were hired. What did you think we were? Idealists?" For the first time Corvane smiled, and it was the worst thing Petra had seen all year, because there was no cruelty in it at all—it was simply the smile of a man confirming a price. "We are neutral, boy. That's the whole architecture of the trade. Buyer and seller, bottle and seal, no questions asked in either direction. The widow sells her wedding day, the miser buys it, and we take our commission and keep our mouths shut forever. Neutrality isn't a virtue. It's a load-bearing wall. Take it out and everything above it comes down."

"And yet," Petra said, "you're about to ask me a question."

Corvane's smile faded. He reached beneath the tray and produced a document—real paper, official stock, the kind with the crown's watermark woven through it—and laid it beside the four glasses.

"Three nights ago, a register was delivered to the Hunt with certain names on it. Bottlers. Runners. Two of my collectors. One of them was Master Ellery Voss, no relation to you, a good man who taught you to triple-seal. They took him this morning." He said it the way he recorded deaths in the ledger: necessary, regretted, entered. "Someone inside the palace is buying memories, Petra. Someone with access to the queen's household, because whatever is poisoning her is being administered in her own kitchens, in her own cups, in her own food—and it is being done with *our merchandise*. Her stolen war-memory, or a distillation of it. Fed back to her in fragments. Do you understand what that means?"

Petra understood. He had tasted the queen's fading mind ten days ago and told himself it was illness, or curse, or the natural rot of a long reign. But a memory didn't fade like that on its own—not one stored whole in a vault. It faded when pieces of it were being *fed back*, wrong-sized and unsealed, each fragment scraping through her mind like a key forced into the wrong lock.

"Someone is returning it to her," he said slowly. "In doses. Broken."

"Or weaponizing the return. Either way, the buyer holds our product and the queen is dying of it, and when she dies with the old blood's fingerprints on the murder, this kingdom burns every gifted man, woman, and child from the coast to the Steppe. The Hunt won't need registers anymore. It'll need mass graves." Corvane leaned forward, and the lamp threw his shadow huge across the shelves of sleeping memories. "Which brings me to the question."

"No," Petra said.

"You haven't heard it."

"I don't need to. You're going to ask me to taste my way through the palace and find your buyer. And you're going to offer me a reason I can't refuse."

Corvane slid the document across the bench. Petra looked down at it and felt the floor tilt, very slightly, the way it does on a ship.

His name. His true name, the one not written in any guild book. *Petra Voss, taster to the royal household, bearer of the old blood.* Dated today. Witnessed. Sealed for delivery to the Office of the Hunt—tomorrow, unless.

"Neutrality," Petra said. His mouth had gone dry. "You were just explaining about the load-bearing wall."

"This isn't a betrayal of neutrality. This *is* neutrality. The buyer broke faith with us—used our goods to kill our best customer and hand the kingdom to the pyres. A neutral party defends the trade. The trade is defended by finding that buyer before the Hunt finds him, or before he kills her, whichever comes first." Corvane straightened his sleeves with two precise tugs. "Find him. Taste your way to him. You walked through that banquet hall and clocked a foreign memory in a single dish while performing ignorance for forty courtiers—you are the finest instrument I have ever owned, and I say that with genuine affection. Find the buyer, and this paper goes into the stove. Fail, and tomorrow morning a register-clerk reads your mother's surname aloud in a courtroom, and we both know how quickly a trial goes once the pyre-men smell smoke."

"My mother," Petra said, "was none of your business."

"Everything in my ledgers is my business. That is what a ledger *is*." Corvane picked up his pen again, dismissing him with the gesture, then paused. "One more thing. The buyer is inside the court—which means whoever they are, they eat at that table, drink from those cellars, walk those halls daily. You'll be tasting for them eventually whether you hunt them or not. Better you find them on purpose."

Petra stood in the doorway a moment, the sealed paper burning in his hand like a coal.

"You said she sold it to stop a war," he said. "And now it's going to start one anyway. Doesn't that bother you? Even as arithmetic?"

Corvane considered the question with the seriousness he gave all questions of accounting.

"What bothers me," he said, "is unsold inventory in a burning market. Goodnight, Petra. The palace kitchens open before dawn."

* * *

The fog had thickened while he was below. It swallowed Vellum Row whole, and Petra walked through it clutching the paper, feeling the city breathe around him—the shuttered houses, the watchmen's lanterns bobbing like drowned stars, the distant orange glow that might have been dawn or might have been another pyre.

Twenty-two years. The whole architecture of his life had been built on a wall he'd believed in: the guild as refuge, the guild as neutral ground, the one place in a hunting kingdom where the gifted could be useful instead of hunted. Usefulness as sanctuary. He'd told himself that story so long it had worn smooth as river stone.

And underneath it, all along, Corvane had been keeping the queen's missing years in a wax-stopped vial, renting out her agony by the dram, waiting—no. Not waiting. Corvane didn't wait. Corvane *positioned*.

Petra stopped at the corner of his street and looked back toward the river, toward the palace rising above the mist in its tiers of lit windows. Somewhere in that glittering honeycomb was a person who bought broken pieces of a dying queen's mind and fed them to her like medicine or like poison—he couldn't yet know which, and the not-knowing sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

Tomorrow he would walk into those kitchens with his gift sheathed and his eyes open, and he would taste every cup, every spoon, every breath of steam until he found the flavor that matched a vault in Vellum Row.

And if the buyer turned out to be someone powerful enough to burn the guild's evidence along with the gifted—if finding them meant dying of knowing—well.

His mother had died of knowing less.

Petra folded the death warrant into his coat, over his heart, where he could feel it with every step, and went home to sleep badly and wake early, the way instruments do.
