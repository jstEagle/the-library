## 6. A Cup of Someone Else

The queen's stolen memory tasted of smoke and honey and a bell rung until it cracked.

I carried that flavor out of her chambers like a coal cupped in bare hands, careful not to let it touch anything, careful not to let it grow. By the time I reached the servants' stair my mouth had watered itself raw. Grief does that to me. Not my grief—hers, or the piece of her that someone had pried loose and sold, the way you'd pry a gem from its setting and leave the crown gold hollowed and light.

Three days I had been tasting shadows since that private audience. Every dish that crossed my tongue at the long banquet table, every sauce and broth sent up as tribute to the royal palate, I read twice: once for poison, once for *her*. Somewhere in this palace was the vessel that had held the queen's memory before it was poured into her like arsenic in wine. Memory leaves a signature the way a ring leaves a mark in wax. You cannot pour something out of one person and into another without the middleman—the bottle, the cup, the throat—keeping a trace.

Corvane's orders sat in me like a swallowed stone. *Find the buyer. Or I give your name to the Hunt.* He had said it without raising his voice, in the vault among the shelves of other people's yesterdays, and then he had smiled the way an accountant smiles when the columns balance.

So I went down. Down past the state kitchens with their brass and their shouting, down to where the palace kept its real appetite: the under-kitchens, the sculleries, the long stone corridor they called the Throat because everything the palace consumed passed through it first.

* * *

Heat hit me at the top of the steps like a wall with opinions. Forty fires, a hundred bodies, steam rising off cauldrons in grey banners. Under-cooks moved through it with the grim choreography of soldiers who had long ago stopped expecting relief. Knives kept time. A boy turned a spit with his whole body, arms and hips together, eyes fixed on nothing.

And underneath all of it—under the onions, the dripping fat, the char and the lye—I began to taste.

I worked the way I always did: slowly, unremarkably, a taster with an idle hour, lifting lids, dipping a clean spoon here and there, nodding as though cataloguing salt. To anyone watching I was a fussy court functionary doing his tedious duty. Inside, I was reading.

The kitchen's flavors were ordinary and loud. A stockpot that had been simmering since before dawn, thick with the cook's own boredom. Bread with pride in it—real pride, the baker's only luxury. A fish sauce carrying a flicker of resentment so sharp it nearly made me sneeze; someone had been cheated at cards last night and salted their anger into the anchovies. Small lives, legibly written. I had spent thirty years learning that every hand that touches food leaves a little of what it is carrying, and most people carry very little worth stealing.

Then I caught the thread.

It came off a copper warming-pan near the far wall, faint as breath on a mirror. Smoke. Honey. The cracked-bell note beneath, high and wrong, the taste of something ancient being forced into a shape too small for it. My knees actually softened. I gripped the pan's handle and let the flavor pour up my arm.

*Her.* The queen's memory had been in this pan—or in something that had traveled through it—and recently. Hours old. The residue of a poured-out memory fades fast; by tomorrow it would be gone entirely, and Corvane knew that too, which was why he'd given me three days instead of three weeks. The trail wasn't just warm. It was dying.

I followed it.

Through the warming-room, where the thread thickened. Past the butler's pantry, where it split around a shelf of flagons like a stream around stones and rejoined. Into the small stone chamber where the drinking vessels were polished—row upon row of goblets hanging from pegs, cups in oiled leather sleeves, the palace's silver throat waiting to be filled.

There, the thread stopped behaving like a stream and started behaving like a rope.

It ran to two places.

Two cups.

The first sat apart from the others in a velvet-lined case reserved for the Chancellor's service: a tall silver-gilt cup stamped with Aldery's arms, the rearing hart over crossed keys. I lifted it with both hands the way a priest lifts a relic and put my tongue to the rim, quick, professional, the gesture any taster might make checking for tarnish or soap.

Smoke. Honey. The cracked bell.

Not residue this time. Residue fades. This was *soaked in*—the flavor had sunk into the metal the way wine sinks into unglazed clay, which meant contact, which meant the memory had been drunk from this cup, or held against these lips, more than once. Lord Chancellor Aldery had not merely bought a bottled war. He had been *tasting* it. Sipping the queen's stolen night the way a miser takes out his gold to count it.

I set the cup down with hands that wanted to shake and made myself breathe. One buyer. Clean. Simple. I could walk out of the Throat, walk to Corvane, say the name, and be free of him—

Except the rope had two strands.

The second cup hung on a plain peg among forty identical ones, third row from the door, a workman's pewter mug with a dented lip. Nothing about it said *mine*, nothing said *look at me*. But when I lifted it the flavor came off it so strong I staggered back a half-step and knocked my elbow against the shelving.

Smoke. Honey. The cracked bell—and under it, something the Queen's cup hadn't carried at all. A second signature braided through the first, thin and cold, like wire through wax. Another memory. Older. Not hers.

Two buyers. Two cups. And the second one belonged to no lord at all.

* * *

"You're holding my mug."

The voice came from behind me and to the left, pitched low, pitched exactly the way you pitch a voice when you don't want the room to turn around. I turned with the mug still in my hand, which was, I realized immediately, the single worst thing I could possibly be holding.

She stood by the flour bins with a knife still in her fist and a rabbit half-dressed on the block in front of her, because apparently she intended to finish the conversation and the rabbit simultaneously. Small, dark-haired, forearms bare to the elbow and dusted white, with the kind of eyes that do arithmetic while everyone else is still counting on fingers. An undercook's apron, an undercook's station, and absolutely no undercook's expression on her face.

"I—" I looked at the mug. It was somehow worse in her presence than it had been alone. "Apologies. I was—"

"Tasting." She said it flatly, not a question, and my stomach dropped through four floors of palace. Then she nodded at the case across the room, at the Chancellor's gilt cup gleaming in its velvet. "You touched that one first. Held it longer. But you flinched at mine." The knife went back to the rabbit, one economical stroke. "Why would a royal taster flinch at a scullery mug?"

Every instinct I owned told me to lie, and every instinct I owned was also screaming that she would hear the lie the way I heard resentment in fish sauce. There are people who can't be lied to with words because they've learned to read the other ledger—hands, breath, the pause before speech. She was one. I knew it the way you know weather coming.

"Because it shouldn't smell like that," I said, and gave him a truth small enough to pass. "Neither should his."

Something shifted behind her eyes—not softening, exactly. Recalibration. "Smell," she repeated. "That's a strange word for what you were doing with your tongue on the rim."

"A taster checks for taint."

"On a dry mug." She wiped her hands slowly, deliberately, never taking her eyes off me. "On two mugs. In a polish-room. Where nothing is ever cooked." She stepped closer, lowered her voice further, and now there was something else in it, something that made the hair on my neck rise: fear, banked and managed, the same flavor I'd tasted in my own mouth for weeks. "I know what the Hunt pays informants. Before you decide what I am, know that I've already decided to watch you, and that I'm very good at watching."

"Sefa!" bellowed a voice from the main kitchen. "The venison won't joint itself!"

"In a moment," she called back, without turning her head, which told me more about her standing in the Throat than her apron did. Then, to me, quiet again: "You haven't given me a name."

"Petra Voss. Royal food-taster." I set the mug back on its peg with exaggerated care, as if it were evidence, which it was. "And I didn't come looking for your mug. I came looking for what's on it."

"And what's on it?"

I hesitated. Here was the fork in my own road, as real as the two cups. Tell her nothing, walk away, deliver Aldery's name to Corvane, live small and safe and watched forever. Or—

Or trust a stranger with a knife because her fear smelled honest.

"The queen is forgetting her own reign," I said. "Piece by piece. Someone sold her a memory that doesn't fit her, and it's lodged in her like a splinter, and she is dying of it by inches. What's soaked into your mug is part of what was taken out of her." I watched her face do several things quickly and settle on none of them. "Someone in this kitchen has been close to that memory. Recently. More than once."

The rabbit sat forgotten. Sefa glanced—quickly, expertly, the glance of a woman who has practiced glances—at the doorway, at the scullery boy, at the steam. When she spoke again her voice had changed registers entirely: not hostile now, not warm either. Professional. The voice of someone opening a second ledger.

"Third row," she said. "That mug belongs to whoever washes up on the late shift, and the late shift changes weekly. But the Chancellor's man collects his master's cup personally every evening after the council sits, and he's begun letting it sit in the warming-pan while he gossips." Her jaw tightened. "A warming-pan that also holds the night-drink for—" She stopped herself. Recalculated. Started again from a different angle, giving me a piece she could afford to lose. "There's a servant. Quiet one. Been in the palace eight years, invisible as furniture. Lately she stands outside the council chamber when it sits, holding that mug, waiting to carry it back down. And lately, Master Voss, the Lord Chancellor has taken to meeting her on the back stairs."

Two cups. Two buyers. Or—one buyer, and one courier. Or one courier serving two masters. The rope hadn't split; it had been *braided*, and I'd been too busy reeling to notice.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked.

"Because you flinched." She picked up the knife again, and for the first time something almost like warmth crossed her face—brief, wry, gone as fast as it came, a match struck in wind. "Because whatever you tasted in that mug scared you, and you didn't hide it well, and people who hide things badly are usually the only honest ones left in this building." The knife fell. "And because if the queen dies, the Gift-Hunt stops being a purge and becomes a festival. I have old blood, Master Voss. Same as half the Throat. We boil water for the people burning us."

The words hung between us in the steam. I thought of the neighbor dragged from his doorway, of the page I'd burned out of the guild ledger, of Corvane's smile over balanced columns. I thought: *she just handed me her life in a sentence, wrapped in a complaint about venison.*

"Sefa!" roared the kitchen again.

"*Coming!*" She drove the point of the knife into the block and left it standing there, quivering, a message to the room. Then she leaned in, close enough that I could smell the flour and the rosemary and, beneath it, the clean iron scent of someone deciding something dangerous. "The Chancellor meets her tonight. Back stairs, past the wine cellar, after the second bell. If you want to follow that flavor to its mouth, you'll want company that knows which stairs creak." A beat. "And I'll want to know why a royal taster reads mugs with his tongue like a man reading letters. That price isn't negotiable."

"It's a long story," I said.

"I boil bones for eleven hours at a stretch." For one unguarded moment she smiled, and it rearranged her whole face into someone I hadn't met yet. "I've got time."

She walked back into the heat and noise and took up her station as though she'd never left it, and I stood alone in the polish-room between two cups—one gilt, one dented—both steeped in the same stolen night.

Second bell. Back stairs. And somewhere above, a faction that wanted the queen's war-memory loosed like a hound, so they'd have an excuse to burn every tongue like mine.

I had until dark to decide how much of myself to spend.

From the kitchen, Sefa's voice rang out over the clatter—light, easy, aimed at no one in particular:

"Master Voss! If you're staying, make yourself useful. The taster can tell me if the stock's off."

It wasn't the stock. It was never going to be the stock. But I rolled up my sleeves, took the ladle she handed me, and tasted.
