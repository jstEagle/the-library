## 7. The Second Buyer

Sefa had worked the palace kitchens for eleven years, and in that time she had learned that every person who passed through them was a kind of dish. Some were plain fare—honest, predictable, gone from your memory by morning. Some were rich sauces that hid rot. And some, very rarely, were like a spice she couldn't name: something that made her want to keep tasting long after sense told her to put down the spoon.

The royal food-taster was the second kind.

She'd watched him for three days now without meaning to. It wasn't his face, though the face was fine enough—lean, dark-eyed, with the careful stillness of a man used to being watched. It was what he *did*. When Petra Voss tasted, he didn't sip and nod like the tasters before him. He listened. She'd seen him hold a spoon of the venison glaze against his lower lip for a full ten seconds, eyes half-closed, as though the sauce were whispering to him. Then he'd set it down with a small frown, taste it again, and frown deeper.

Nobody frowned at Master Odell's venison glaze. The glaze was famous.

So on the third evening, when he came through her scullery door asking—in that soft, courteous way of his—to see where the Chancellor's wine was decanted, Sefa decided she was done watching.

"The Chancellor's wine comes through here same as everyone's," she said, not looking up from the pile of onions she was reducing to ribbons. "Bottles arrive sealed from the cellars. I break the seal, I pour, I cork, I send it up. You've watched me do it twice already."

"I have." He stood just inside the doorway, hands folded behind him, making no move to come closer. That too she noted; most men with questions walked right into a woman's kitchen like they owned the floor under their boots. "And I noticed the second decanting took you longer than the first."

"Because I dropped the stopper."

"You didn't."

The knife paused against the board. Onions could hide a lot of things—their smell drowned subtler scents—but Sefa had spent a decade in rooms where the powerful came to eat, and she knew what it meant when someone noticed what you'd tried not to show them.

She set the knife down and turned. "Say what you're actually asking, taster."

Petra glanced at the doorway. A scullery boy rattled past with a load of trenchers, oblivious. Only when the footsteps faded did he speak, low enough that even she had to lean toward him.

"On the night of the Chancellor's last private dinner, two cups left this kitchen carrying the same flavor. Not the same *wine*—the same flavor underneath the wine. Something that doesn't come from any vineyard or still in this city. I've tasted it once before, in a dish served to the queen." His eyes held hers, dark and steady. "You know what it is. You flinched when I said the word *decanted* because you thought I meant more than wine."

The kitchen was warm around them—banked ovens, copper pots, the fat sweetness of tomorrow's pastries proofing under cloth. Sefa felt none of its warmth. Her palms had gone cold against her apron.

"I don't know what you're talking about," she said.

"No," he agreed quietly. "But you know *who*."

* * *

She threw him out. Politely, in the way kitchens are polite—a wooden spoon pointed at the door, a promise to speak to the steward if he came back. He went without argument, which somehow made it worse. Men who argued were simple. Men who apologized and left you alone with your own thoughts were dangerous.

That night she didn't sleep. She sat at her narrow bed in the servants' attic with a candle guttering, and beneath her mattress, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the thing that had kept her awake for two months now: forty-one pages copied in her own small, neat hand. Palace accounts. Grain ledgers. Wine inventories. Disbursement records bearing signatures that shouldn't have been on them.

She had started copying for coin. Everyone in the palace sold something—gossip, favors, silence—and a sharp-eyed undercook who could read figures was worth paying. But somewhere along the weeks, the copying had stopped feeling like coin-work and started feeling like standing on a ledge in the dark, listening to something breathe below her.

Because the accounts didn't add up, and they didn't add up in a *pattern*. Certain sums moved through lines marked only as *special provisions, ceremonial use*. Ceremonial use of what? No feast calendar matched them. And every one of those entries carried, eventually, a connection to Lord Chancellor Aldery—or to the men Aldery dined with privately, the ones who never appeared at court functions but whose names surfaced in the margins of things: a master of petitions, a captain of the inner gate, a guildsman whose name Sefa had heard only once, spoken by a clerk with a nervous cough.

Corvane. Of the Glasswrights' Guild.

Sefa had grown up on stories about the Glasswrights, the way children grow up on stories about wolves. They bottled memories, the stories said. They bought your grief, your joy, your wedding day, your worst sin—sealed them in glass and paid you in bread or blood or bargains. Old wives' tales, mostly. Except that the queen had been fading before the whole court's eyes like a portrait left in rain, forgetting ministers' names, signing orders twice, mistaking a food-taster for her dead brother—and except that the taster who stood in her kitchen tonight had looked at her like a man who already knew the shape of the answer he wanted.

At some point past midnight, Sefa made the mistake every sensible person makes exactly once: she decided to find out how much danger she was actually in, instead of simply assuming all of it.

* * *

She found him the next morning, or engineered finding him—she was honest enough with herself not to pretend otherwise—at the sink-room where the palace's water was tested. He was alone, lifting a ladle of the morning's first draw, and when she shut the door behind her he didn't startle. He just set down the ladle and waited, patient as a held breath.

"I'll tell you what I know," Sefa said, "and then you're going to tell me why a royal taster cares about kitchen bookkeeping, and then we're both going to forget this conversation happened. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"Don't agree so fast. You haven't heard my price."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, but the place one would live. "Name it."

"When this is over—if there is an over—you tell me the truth. All of it. Whatever you are, whatever you can do, whatever it is you taste when you hold a spoon against your lip for ten seconds. I've spent eleven years feeding liars. I'd like to be fed the truth once, by someone who owes it to me."

For a moment he said nothing, and she watched him weigh it—actually weigh it, visibly, like a man counting out coins he couldn't spare. Then he nodded once. "Done."

So she told him. Not everything—she kept the pages themselves hidden, and she kept the name of the person who paid her, because that name was the last wall between herself and the open air. But she gave him the shape of it: the special-provisions entries, the sums that flowed toward Aldery's circle and then vanished, the single mention of Corvane, the dates. And here she watched him go very still, because the dates aligned—she saw it land on his face before she understood it herself—with something he knew.

"The queen began failing fourteen months ago," he said slowly. "The court physicians called it a wasting melancholy. The first *special provision* entry is fifteen months old."

"So whatever they bought, they bought before she broke," Sefa said. "You don't buy a remedy in advance unless—" She stopped. The thought arrived whole, cold as well-water.

Unless you were the ones who made her break.

"That's not the worst of it," Petra said. He crossed to the door, checked the passage, came back. When he spoke again his voice had dropped to almost nothing. "What I tasted in the queen's food, and again in the Chancellor's cups—it isn't poison. Poisons have flavors I know the way you know the weight of your own knife. This is older. It's *someone*. A memory, carried in a substance the Glasswrights distill. Someone has been feeding pieces of a stolen memory into the palace—into the queen's food, and into the Chancellor's circle—to keep the flavor circulating. And the memory itself..." He hesitated, and she realized he was deciding how much to trust her with, and hated him for it, and admired him for it, both at once. "It's a war. An old one. The kind of war that ended badly enough that people still burn candles against its return."

Sefa leaned back against the stone basin. The water dripped somewhere behind her, patient as a clock. "Aldery wants a war?"

"Aldery wants an *excuse*." Petra's jaw tightened. "There's a proclamation coming—I've seen drafts pass through the tasting office. Registration for anyone bearing the old blood. Penalties for concealment." He said *penalties* the way other men said *pyres*. "Right now, the Gift-Hunt is the crown's fear talking. But if a faction could produce proof that the gifted are dangerous—that one of them did this to the queen, stole her mind, maybe loosed an old weapon—they wouldn't need to ask for the purge anymore. The city would demand it. Every gifted soul in the capital, registered or burned, and the crowds lighting the fires themselves."

"And the proof," Sefa said, following it through, her stomach turning as the pieces seated themselves, "is sitting in a bottle somewhere. A stolen memory of a war. Which means whoever holds the bottle doesn't need to release anything. They just need to let everyone *taste* it. One demonstration. One cup raised in the wrong hall, and—"

"—and the Hunt stops being a policy and becomes a festival." Petra finished it flatly. "Two buyers. That's what I found in the kitchen. Two cups carrying the same flavor, poured the same night. One trail leads to Aldery's table. The other splits off—goes somewhere else in the palace. There's a second buyer, and I don't yet know whose hand holds the second cup."

"And you think I might."

"I think you copy accounts for someone who knows things about Aldery's money that Aldery would kill to bury." His voice stayed gentle, which somehow made it worse. "I'm not asking for the name. But ask yourself, Sefa—whoever pays you, do they want the truth, or do they want leverage? Because those aren't always the same purchase."

She should have been angry. Instead she felt the floor tilt, gently, the way it does when you realize the room you trusted has been leaning all along. Two months of copying, of telling herself she was a hired pen and nothing more—and now here was a stranger with a dangerous mouth showing her that her little ledger of sins might be one thread in a design big enough to hang a whole people.

"I don't sell to monsters," she said, and heard how thin it sounded.

"I believe you." He said it simply, without the oily reassurance she'd have expected from any courtier, and that undid her more than an argument would have. "Which is why I'm going to leave you with something dangerous, and you can decide what to do with it. If your employer asks you, in the next few days, to copy anything touching the Queen's Household accounts—anything at all—that's not bookkeeping. That's someone mapping how far the queen's sickness reaches, and who profits from it. Watch for it."

"And if it comes?"

"Then we'll both know whose side your employer is on." He moved to the door, then paused, hand on the latch. For a heartbeat the careful courtier-mask slipped, and she saw the man beneath it—tired, watchful, carrying something heavy in the space behind his ribs. "Thank you. For telling me what you told me. Most people in this palace would have sold me to the Hunt for less."

"Most people in this palace haven't tasted their own cooking," Sefa said. "We know exactly what goes into things."

This time the smile came all the way, brief and real, and she hated how much warmth three seconds of it could leave behind in a cold room. Then he was gone, and she stood alone among the dripping basins with her heart beating too fast and her mind running in circles around a single, stubborn thought:

*He knew I'd help him before I did.*

* * *

Three days later, the request came.

Her employer's messenger—a gray-faced clerk named Hask who smelled of ink and fear—appeared at the kitchen's back stair with the usual sealed instruction. Sefa broke it open expecting the routine: shipping manifests, cellar tallies, another slice of Aldery's hidden money laid bare for her anonymous pen.

Instead she read the page twice, then a third time, while Hask shifted his weight and studied the ceiling.

*Copy the Queen's Household disbursements for the past eighteen months. All entries. Include the apothecary's bills.*

Include the apothecary's bills. Whoever paid her wanted to trace what the queen had been given, when, and by whom—which meant they either feared the same answer Petra feared, or they *were* the answer, checking their own stitching. She couldn't tell which. That was the thing about working blind for unseen masters: every kindness and every cruelty arrived looking identical on paper.

Hask cleared his throat. "Well? The usual fee stands. Double, for the volume."

"Tell your principal the undercook needs four days," Sefa said. "Household records live in the west annex, and the annex mistress counts her keys nightly. I don't rush and I don't get caught."

Hask nodded and shuffled away, and Sefa stood in the stairwell holding the instruction, feeling the ledge under her feet crumble another inch.

Four days. Enough time to make the copies—she would, because refusing was how people vanished, and because a double game required playing both sides until the moment came to choose. Four days, also, to decide whether the man with the dangerous mouth deserved the truth she was sitting on. Forty-one pages in her own handwriting. A name she hadn't spoken.

That evening she found him again, in the corridor outside the tasting office, and pressed a folded scrap of paper into his hand without breaking stride—just two cooks passing in the traffic of the palace, nothing to see. He read it later; she knew because the next morning a reply appeared inside the flour bin, written in pencil, in a small precise hand:

*I understand. Keep copying. Give them exactly what they asked for. And when the second buyer's cup turns up—I think I know whose table it belongs to now—we will need every page you have. Trust me a little longer. — P.*

Trust. Sefa turned the word over like a coin of doubtful metal. Eleven years in the palace had taught her that trust was the most expensive item on any menu, and the one most often counterfeited.

But she thought of the queen fading in her tower like a candle drowning in its own wax. She thought of the proclamation drafts, of neighbors who'd bear the old blood, of pyres lit by cheerful crowds. She thought of a man who had promised her the truth as payment—had treated it as a *debt*, as though honesty were something owed rather than extracted—and had smiled at her like she was a person and not a purse.

And she thought, with a sensation she refused to examine too closely, of the way he had said *thank you*, as if her help were a gift and not a transaction.

"All right, taster," she murmured to the empty kitchen, folding his note away beside the forty-one pages that could hang them both. "A little longer. But when this breaks—and it will break—you'd better be standing where I can see you."

Outside, the bells rang the change of watch, and somewhere above the kitchens, in the high rooms where the queen forgot and forgot and forgot, the second cup waited to be filled.
