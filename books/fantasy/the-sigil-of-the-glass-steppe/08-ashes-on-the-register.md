## 8. Ashes on the Register

The bells began at dawn, and by the third hour of the morning the smoke had a taste.

I stood in the doorway of the guildhouse on Vintner's Row and let it settle on my tongue the way I let everything settle: patiently, professionally, with the ledger of my own reactions held carefully shut. Woodsmoke first—cheap pine, pitch, the resin they used to seal the register ledgers. Under that, wool burning. Under that, hair. And under that, faint as a signature at the bottom of a page, something I knew too well to mistake: the sour-silver tang of bottled memory cooking in its glass.

They were burning the stock along with the man.

"Petra." Corvane's voice behind me, dry as a turned page. "You're blocking the light."

I stepped aside. He came to stand beside me at the threshold, hands clasped behind his back, and watched the crowd gather at the far end of the row with the mild interest of a man watching rain approach his neighbor's field.

Down the street, between the shuttered bottling-houses and the memory-brokers' shops with their painted signs—a weeping eye, a cup split in half—the king's men had erected the platform. It had gone up overnight. That was the thing about the Hunt that no one told you: it didn't arrive like a storm. It arrived like furniture. One morning there was simply a scaffold where the fish-cart used to park, and everyone pretended it had always been there.

"They took Hesper last night," Corvane said.

"I heard."

"She refused the register. Three times offered, three times refused." He said it the way he read entries aloud during audit—flat, complete, without emphasis. "She spat in Registrar Quill's face, which was unwise. Spitting is a documented act. Everything is a document now, if you're careless enough to make one."

Hesper Marun. She had bottled my mother's laugh for me once, years ago, when I still had money and didn't know yet what I'd pay for it later. A small vial, amber glass, the laugh inside it warm as fresh bread. I had listened to it—I had *tasted* it, one drop on the tongue, because that was how I listened—until the flavor faded from repetition, and then I had kept the empty bottle on my shelf like other men keep saints' bones.

Now her stock was feeding the fire beneath her feet, and the whole street could taste what she had spent thirty years preserving: first kisses, dead children's voices, the exact color of a summer that would never come again. Grief and joy both went up together, indistinguishable, sweetish and acrid, like sugar thrown into a lamp flame.

"You should go inside," Corvane said. "There's work."

"There's a woman burning in front of our shop."

"There's a woman *outside* our shop," he corrected gently. "Inside, there are four hundred entries that need reconciling before the auditors come, and every one of them is a life someone chose to sell. Hesper chose this too. Write it down however you need to, but write it accurately."

He went back in. The door closed behind him with its soft, expensive click.

* * *

I did not go inside. I am not sure I have ever fully forgiven myself for what I did instead, which was nothing—which was stand there and watch, and taste, and remember.

The crowd at the end of the row had grown. They came out of the tenements with their collars up, out of the counting-houses with their ledgers under their arms, and they arranged themselves the way crowds arrange themselves now, in a wide ring, leaving room for the soldiers, leaving room for the fire. Nobody pushed. Nobody shouted. The Gift-Hunt had taught the capital a new posture in six weeks: heads down, hands visible, mouths shut around anything that might be testimony later.

A priest of the Unblinking Sun climbed the platform steps with the register book under his arm. I could see the gilt edges of the pages even from the guildhouse door. Every name written there was a name that would live. Every blank space was a pyre. It was the simplest contract ever drafted, and the guild, I thought bitterly, would have admired its economy.

They brought Hesper out at midmorning.

She was smaller than I remembered, or the platform made her small, or the ropes did. Her bottler's apron was gone; they'd burned it separately, I suppose, because it bore the sigil of her trade—a vial cradled in two open palms—and the sigil itself was now evidence. An officer read the charge. I couldn't hear the words over the distance and the wind, but I knew them. Everyone on Vintner's Row knew them. *Bearing the old blood. Concealing the gift. Refusal of lawful registration.*

Then they gave her the book one last time.

I saw her look at it. Even at that distance, even through smoke-stung eyes, I saw her look at it the way a taster looks at an unfamiliar dish—one long, measuring, unflinching assessment. And then she shook her head, and she said something, one sentence, that made the priest step back as though she'd struck him.

Later, a baker's boy who'd been closer swore she said: *"You can't register what you never agreed to carry."*

The fire caught quickly. Stocked wood, oiled, prepared—someone had done their accounting well. And here is the thing I will carry to whatever grave I'm owed, the thing that curdles in me even now: because her inventory burned with her, the whole street tasted her death as a symphony. Thirty years of bottled lives released at once into the heat, and every person standing in that ring received, unasked, an involuntary communion. A wedding. A funeral. A child learning to walk. A soldier's last letter home. All of it rising off the pyre in flavors no palate could refuse, soaking into the tongues of five hundred witnesses who had come to watch a punishment and left carrying pieces of a stranger's soul.

Some of them wept and didn't know why. Some laughed and looked horrified at themselves. One man dropped to his knees and began confessing sins to a stranger, loudly, in detail, because a memory not his own had surfaced in him like a fish breaking water and he could not tell anymore whose guilt it was.

That, I understood suddenly, was the point. That was why they burned the stock publicly. Not to destroy it—to *distribute* it. To make every witness complicit, contaminated, unable to claim afterward that the old blood was something foreign to them. You have tasted the gift now, the fire said to the city. It is in your mouth. Deny your neighbors and you deny your own tongue.

Or perhaps I gave the crown too much credit, and it was simply cruelty wearing procedure as a coat. In those weeks I believed both things at once, which is a kind of madness the gifted learn early.

Hesper herself made almost no sound. What sound reached us was mostly the glass—vials cracking in sequence as the heat found them, each one a small clear note, a struck bell, a scale running up past hearing. She had organized her vault beautifully, everyone said so. Even at the end, her life's work rang in order.

When it was over, the crowd dispersed the way water finds a drain, fast and silent and relieved to be going anywhere else. The soldiers raked the coals. Two clerks in the Sun's colors began sorting the ash into buckets—salvageable residue, I learned later, sold back to licensed brokers at three coppers the ounce. The crown had found a way to tax even this.

I stood in the doorway until my legs ached, and then I went inside and did what I was told.

* * *

The reconciliation room sat behind the vault door, windowless, lit by lamps that burned memory-tallow and gave off a faint smell of other people's summers. Four hundred entries. I worked through them with pen and blotter while the ash-men's carts rattled past outside, and Corvane moved among the shelves behind me, checking seals, making his small notes in his small precise hand.

At entry two hundred and eleven I stopped.

It was a purchase record, dated nine days ago. Buyer: *registered broker, name withheld per standing arrangement.* Item: *one lot, assorted domestic memories, forty-one units, provenance: Vintner's Row.* Provenance: Vintner's Row. Nine days ago. Before the proclamation had even reached our district.

Corvane bought Hesper's inventory before the Hunt came for her.

No—not before. That would have been mere opportunism, and I might have swallowed it, the way I'd swallowed everything else, telling myself the guild survived by buying what grief put on the market. But the date mattered. Nine days ago, the Gift-Hunt existed only in rumor, in the criers' rehearsals, in the private briefings that ministers gave to men like my guildmaster. Ordinary brokers were still selling ordinary lots at ordinary prices. Only someone who *knew*—who knew the purge schedule, who knew which houses would be seized and which owners would burn rather than sign—could have known that Hesper's forty-one units would be worth their weight in war-gold within a fortnight.

I turned the page. Entry two hundred and fourteen: another lot, another house on another street the Hunt hadn't reached yet. Entry two hundred and thirty: a bottler in Tanner's Court whose name I recognized from the rumor-lists. Lot after lot after lot, all purchased in the same narrow window, all dated ahead of the flames.

"Corvane," I said. My voice came out steady, which surprised me. "Who told you about the purge?"

The lamps hissed. Somewhere in the vault a pressure-seal ticked as a bottle settled in its rack.

"Sit down, Petra," he said pleasantly, "and reconcile your column."

"The entries are dated before the proclamations. You bought Hesper's stock nine days before they took her. You bought Tanner's Court before Tanner's Court knew it was dying." I stood, and my chair scraped, and the sound seemed very loud in that sealed room. "You didn't survive the Hunt, Corvane. You *scheduled* it."

He set down his pen with great care, aligning it parallel to the ledger's edge, and looked at me across twenty years of shared work. There was no anger in his face. That was the worst of it. There was nothing in his face at all but the patience of a man explaining arithmetic to a slow student.

"Sit," he said again, "and I will teach you the only lesson this profession has ever taught anyone."

I sat.

"When the Hunt was a rumor," he said, "a bottle of a farmwife's wedding cost eight silver. Today, with her village's registry burned and every drop of that region's memory confiscated as evidence, the same bottle—if you can find one—costs forty. When the purges reach the coast provinces next month, coastal memories triple. And when"—he laced his fingers—"when the Hunt reaches the court itself, when certain high-born memories enter the market through channels only we control, prices will stop being prices at all. They will become terms. Conditions. Leverage." He smiled slightly. "Every purge in history has created scarcity, Petra. Scarcity creates value. The gifted have spent four hundred years being hunted for free. I have simply arranged for us to be hunted *profitably*."

"Hesper is ash."

"Yes. Tragic. She refused to be an asset, and assets that refuse become examples, and examples accelerate demand." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow more terrible than cruelty. "You think I wanted her burned? I wanted her *inventory*. The fire was the crown's contribution, not mine. I merely knew its date."

"Because you bought it. From Aldery's people—from whoever drafts the schedules." I was leaning forward now, my knuckles white on the table edge, tasting my own fury like copper at the back of the throat. "You traded the names of every bottler on this street for advance notice. You sold them Hesper. You sold them the list, Corvane. We keep ledgers of what people sell away. Who keeps ours?"

"No one," he said, "which is precisely why the position is valuable."

He came around the table then, and stood over me, and for a moment the lamplight caught the fine scar at his temple that I'd never asked about, and I thought: *there is a story in that scar, and he sold it long ago, and whatever it bought him, he's been paying interest ever since.*

"You are angry," he observed. "Good. Anger is attention with the safety off. Now attend. The Hunt will reach this guild eventually—my name is on enough registers, my blood is old enough, and the ministers I bought notice from will sell me to save themselves the moment it profits them. I have planned for that day since before you were born. There is a place beneath the glass flats east of the city where the true vault lies, and in that vault is inventory that no fire will touch and no minister can price. When the time comes, I will need a taster. Someone whose palate can verify provenance without opening a seal. Someone discreet, meticulous, and—today's display suggests—sufficiently motivated."

"And if I refuse? If I take what I know to the registrars?"

He considered it honestly, which I hated him for.

"Then tomorrow you'll be entered in the Hunt's book," he said, "because I wrote your name in mine the day you first tasted a client's grief and lied to me about it. You were eleven. You've been gifted since before you were employable, Petra, and every year since, I have chosen not to spend you." He straightened his cuffs. "Consider whether that was mercy or investment. Then finish your column. The auditors come Thursday."

He left me alone with four hundred entries and the taste of smoke still lodged behind my teeth.

* * *

I finished the column. Of course I finished the column. That is the trap of the double ledger—it trains you until your hands move even when your heart has stopped believing in the work.

But somewhere around entry three hundred, my hand slowed, and I found myself writing in the margin, in the tiny coded shorthand only I could read, a note beside Hesper Marun's final inventory line. Not sentiment. Data. The date her stock was purchased against the date her house was scheduled. The pattern, repeated across eleven entries. The arithmetic of betrayal laid out in figures no auditor could dispute and no registrar could ignore.

If I could prove the dates, I could prove the bargain between the guild and the Hunt. If I could prove the bargain, I could break it—and Corvane with it. And if the trail ran where I suspected, upward through the brokers into the palace itself, then the man who had bought the queen's stolen memory and the man who had scheduled Hesper's pyre might turn out to share a desk.

I blotted the margin dry and closed the ledger.

Outside, the ash-carts were making their last rounds, and through the shutters I could hear the clerks calling prices for what they'd salvaged. Three coppers the ounce. Somewhere beyond the rooftops, the glass flats lay pale under the afternoon sun, and beneath them, if Corvane spoke truth, waited a vault full of things too valuable to burn.

He had invited me to see it.

And I found, examining myself as carefully as I examined any dish, that beneath the grief and the cold new hatred, one other flavor remained—small, sharp, unmistakable.

Curiosity.

Which Corvane had once warned me was a debt.

I was beginning to understand who intended to collect it.
