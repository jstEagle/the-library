## 9. The Vault Beneath the Steppe

We left the city before the third bell of morning, when the fog still owned the streets and the Gift-Hunt's criers had not yet found their voices.

Corvane drove the cart himself. That was the first strange thing. In eleven years I had never seen my guildmaster touch a horse's reins; he was a man who believed that hands were for signing, sealing, and taking. Yet there he sat in a plain wool coat with his hood up, guiding two gray mules through the Salt Gate as if he had been born between their ears.

"You'll want to eat nothing today," he said, without looking at me. "Not even water. Your palate must be a clean page."

"Where are we going?"

"To church," he said, and almost smiled.

The glass steppe began three miles beyond the walls. Everyone in Vhalen knows it—the great flats where, in the age before the crown, some cataclysm had melted a whole province into a sheet of pale green glass that ran to the horizon like a frozen sea. Farmers would not plow near it. Shepherds said their dogs refused to drink from the pools at its edge. The city had grown up beside it the way a scar grows around a wound, and everyone agreed on one rule: do not walk far out onto the Steppe, because men go out and the Steppe keeps them.

I had never been more than a stone's throw onto it. Now Corvane turned the cart straight out into the middle of all that shining silence, and kept going.

The sound was the worst part at first. Glass carries the wheels' noise for miles—a low ringing under everything, as if we were driving across the skin of an enormous bell. The sun climbed. There was nowhere to hide from it, no tree, no wall, no shadow but our own. I sat with my hands folded like a good clerk and watched the city shrink behind us to a smudge of smoke, and I understood, finally, why no one had ever found the guild's true vault: because no one sane would look for a door in the middle of nowhere, under a floor that rang when you touched it.

"Master," I said carefully, "the Hunt has registers now. If anyone sees us—"

"No one sees anything on the Steppe," Corvane said. "That is its virtue. Light goes out here and never comes back. It goes *down*." He flicked the reins. "You have kept my ledgers eleven years, Petra. You have recorded the sale of weddings, of first kisses, of a man's mother's voice, sold to him by his own brother. You have never once asked what happens to a memory after we bottle it. Where it lives. What it costs to keep it sweet."

"I assumed a cellar."

"A cellar." He laughed once, softly. "Yes. A cellar."

* * *

Near midday we came upon the marker.

It stood alone on the flats: a pillar of the same green glass, perhaps twice a man's height, carved long ago into the shape of a cup held in an open hand. Weather had worn the fingers smooth. At its base someone—ages ago—had set a ring of small stones, each one dark with old fire.

"The Sigil," I said. Every child in Vhalen knew the shape; it was chalked on doors against bad luck, stamped on old coins no longer minted. A cup and an open hand. *What is poured may be received.*

"The sigil is older than the crown, older than the guild, older than any name we use for the gift," Corvane said. He stepped down from the cart and walked to the pillar and laid his palm flat against the glass hand. "And it is not a monument, boy. It is a lock."

He pressed. Nothing happened—except that the ringing under the Steppe changed. I felt it in my teeth before I heard it, a deep note shifting to another, like a bell being turned by a giant's careful fingers. Ten paces behind the pillar, the glass floor opened.

Not cracked—opened. A circle of the Steppe swung down and inward on some hinge older than hinges, and beneath our feet yawned a stairway of the same green glass descending into light. Not torchlight. The light came up from below, pale and sourceless, the color of moonlight trapped in ice.

"Eleven years," Corvane said, watching my face with something like pleasure. "You never asked, and so you never guessed. Curiosity is a debt, Petra. But today—" he gestured down the stair, courteous as a host at his own front door, "—today the debt comes due."

I went first. I told myself afterward that he ordered me to. He did not. I went first because if there was a monster at the bottom, I wanted to meet it before it met him.

The stairs went down farther than they should have. I counted ninety steps and lost the count somewhere past two hundred, and all the while the light grew, until I stepped off the last stair into the vault, and stopped breathing.

It was a cavern the size of the cathedral square, walled and roofed in glass so pure it seemed the Steppe itself had been hollowed like a fruit. And every wall, every shelf, every rack from floor to unseen ceiling, was full of vials.

Tens of thousands of them. Hundreds of thousands. They hung in wire cradles, lay in velvet trays, stood in ranks on shelves that receded into distances no cellar could contain—and each one glowed faintly with the color of what it held. Rose-gold for wedding days. Gray-blue for griefs. One whole aisle burned a sullen orange that made my tongue curl behind my teeth just to look at it, and I knew without asking that those were angers, wars, last words spoken in hatred. The light of them all mixed in the air above us into a soft aurora, so that the vault glowed like the inside of a lantern, like the belly of some sleeping god.

"There are more below," Corvane said mildly, behind me. "Three more floors. This is only what the guild holds *now*. The rest is debt."

"What do you mean, debt?"

"A memory cannot be destroyed, Petra. You know this—or you would know it if your mother had lived to teach you." I did not let my face move, but he saw anyway; he always saw. "Burned, drowned, buried—the vessel dies, and the memory slips loose, and it goes *somewhere*. Into the land. Into the blood of children not yet born. We call those the wild ones. Half the gift-blood in Vhalen is a memory that escaped a fire two hundred years ago and took root." He walked down the central aisle, and the vials brightened faintly as he passed, leaning toward him—I swear it, the way wheat leans after sun. "So we keep them. Contained. Sweet. For sale. Every vial in this room is a thing that cannot die, waiting patiently to be lived again by someone else. Do you understand what we are standing in?"

I looked at the endless glowing racks, and my mouth had gone dry as salt.

"We are standing in every stolen hour of the last four centuries," I said.

"*Stored*," Corvane corrected. "Stolen is such a loud word. Purchased. Most of them begged us to take them. Grief is heavy; we lighten pockets." He turned, and his face in the aurora-light looked younger and crueller than it ever did above ground. "Come. You did not come all this way for inventory."

* * *

He led me to the center of the vault, where the other aisles converged on a single plinth of black stone.

On it, alone, in a cradle of silver wire, stood one vial.

It was smaller than the others—no longer than my thumb—and it did not glow. It *burned*. White light, hard and steady, like a star caught in glass, so that I had to narrow my eyes against it. And it hummed.

I heard it before I was within twenty paces: a single sustained note, high and clear and perfectly steady, like a wet finger drawn round the rim of a crystal bowl, like a struck bell that had decided never to fall silent. The note went through my chest. My bones picked it up and rang along. Around us, I realized slowly, the entire vault resonated with it—every one of the hundred thousand bottled hours humming in sympathy, a choir holding one endless chord, and at the center of the chord, this.

"The queen's memory," I said. My voice came out wrong, flattened by the hum.

"Taken eleven years ago," Corvane said, "from the Queen Amaris herself, in her own bedchamber, by a taster she trusted." He watched me. "You will want to know which taster. You will want to ask how it was done, whether she screamed, whether she signed a receipt. Save your questions. Look at it. Tell me what you taste from here. That is what I brought you for."

I approached the plinth the way you approach a horse that has never known kindness. The hum rose as I came near—not louder, but *closer*, filling the space behind my eyes. Three steps away, my gift woke on its own, unasked, the way a dog stands up when its master reaches for the leash.

I tasted it without touching anything. That had never happened to me before. The air itself carried it: iron and snow, smoke and honey, burnt cedar, the copper tang of blood swallowed rather than spilled, and underneath everything a sweetness so enormous it was nearly rotten—the sweetness of something loved beyond all measure and lost beyond all repair.

And then I touched the plinth. Only the black stone, only for balance, only a fingertip—

The vault vanished.

* * *

Night. A plain of grass to the horizon, bent flat by wind, and above it a sky torn open.

I stood on a hillside among ten thousand people, and none of them could see me, because I was not there; I was inside the memory, wearing its eyes, borrowing its grief. Torches below in the valley—no, not torches, *armies*, two rivers of fire flowing toward each other across the dark. And above them the sky was breaking like ice on a spring river, great panes of it peeling back to show something behind the stars that moved and was hungry, and the gifted stood on the hilltops with their hands raised, holding the sky shut by main force, dying of it one by one. I could taste what they tasted. The working filled my mouth: it tasted of every bitter herb and every sweet one boiled together, of a mother's milk gone to vinegar, of the last breath of the world's best day.

And beside me—beside the memory's owner—a young woman in armor too big for her, her braid singed at the ends, her hands shaking, saying in a voice I knew, though I had only ever heard it aged and frayed:

*"If I let go, they win. If I hold, I burn the valley. Either way it ends tonight, and either way it is my doing."*

Queen Amaris, thirty years younger than the woman fading in the palace now, deciding the end of the ancient war with her palms full of fire. I felt her choose. I felt her pour the whole night—all of it, the armies, the broken sky, the names of the dead, the taste of the working itself—into herself, because someone had to carry it or it would slip into the soil and grow again like a weed of war, and she would not let that happen. She swallowed the war the way I swallow poison for kings.

She meant to carry it to her grave.

But memories do not sit quietly in a living mind. Hers gnawed. Hers *leaked*. Eleven years ago it had begun to leak so badly that she had done the second unthinkable thing: she had paid a trusted tongue to cut it out of her and seal it in glass. And the cutting was killing her anyway, slow as frost, because you cannot amputate a decade of your own soul and stay whole.

The vision released me like a hand opening.

I was on my knees on the glass floor, both hands gripping the black plinth, and the vial was humming directly into my skull, and tears were freezing on my face in the vault's cold, and Corvane was crouched beside me with an expression I had never seen on him before.

Reverence.

"Now you understand," he said quietly. "That is not a queen's memory in that vial, Petra. That is the *end of the war*. That is the proof of what the gifted did to save the throne that now burns them. Held in the right hands, shown to the right crowd, it is worth more than every other vial in this room together. It can make the crown kneel. Or—" his voice dropped, silk over stone, "—it can be drunk by someone strong enough to hold it. And then that person does not testify. That person does not bargain." His fingers closed gently over my wrist, prying my hand from the stone. "That person simply *knows*, the way the old ones knew. Imagine walking into Aldery's next dinner with the last night of the ancient war sitting on your tongue."

I got my feet under me. The vault swayed. Somewhere above us, miles of glass rang with the bell-note, patient as tide.

"Why show me this?" I managed.

"Because in a few weeks I will ask you to do the hardest tasting of your life, and you will need to know what is at stake." He smiled, and it was almost kind, which frightened me more than cruelty would have. "Go home, Petra. Eat nothing. Think about what you tasted. And think about whose side of the ledger you want to be written on when the Hunt arrives at our door—because it is coming, boy. I have read the arithmetic. Fires always come. The only question is who owns the matches."

I climbed the two hundred stairs on legs that did not feel like mine. Behind me the chord went on singing, one white note at the heart of a hundred thousand stored souls, and I carried its taste out into the daylight like a coal carried in cupped hands, already burning through.

Because I had lied to him, there on the glass floor. I had not only glimpsed the war's last night.

In the moment the vision opened, the memory had tasted *me* back—and somewhere in that vast and grieving dark, something old had stirred, recognized a fellow vessel, and begun, very softly, to call my name.
