## 10. Terms of Surrender

The note came folded inside a delivery of lard, which was the first sign that whoever wrote it knew kitchens.

Sefa found it at the bottom of the cask when she was rendering fat for the morning's pastry work, a slip of paper greasy at one corner, the writing fine and dry as an accountant's hand. *The old stairwell. Midnight. Come alone. Bring what you copied. Your employer wishes to discuss terms.*

She had been copying accounts for three months now—palace provisioning ledgers, wine inventories, the private disbursements of the Lord Chancellor's household—slipping pages out under her apron and returning them by morning with copies in her own careful hand. The arrangement had begun with a note tucked in a flour sack and a purse of silver that appeared where her tips usually went. She had told herself it was simple: someone in the palace wanted eyes in the kitchens, and she wanted coin enough to buy her mother's house back from the tax collectors. Everyone sold something. In this city, even bread had a price written in someone else's blood.

But midnight meetings were not simple. Midnight meetings were how people disappeared.

She finished the lard anyway. A cook who abandoned her station left ripples, and ripples got noticed, and Sefa Vane had survived nineteen years in this palace by being too useful to question and too dull to remember. She rendered the fat until it ran clear as glass, she proofed the doughs, she salted the stock pots, and all the while the note sat against her breastbone like a coal she couldn't spit out.

At half past eleven she climbed to the old stairwell—the one behind the tapestry stores, built for servants of a court two queens dead, now used only by rats and people who didn't want to be seen. She brought the copies. She also brought the boning knife from her own kit, strapped flat against her forearm beneath her sleeve, because her mother had raised her on one proverb above all others: *trust is a dish you taste before you swallow.*

* * *

He was already there.

She had imagined her employer a dozen ways—a guild man with soft hands, a disgraced clerk, some merchant's agent grown greedy—and every imagining fell away the moment she saw the grey coat and the chain of office glinting faintly at his throat in the light of the single lamp he'd set on the step.

Lord Chancellor Aldery. Second only to the crown. The man whose accounts she'd been copying.

"You're punctual," he said. "I value that."

"My lord." She did not bow. Bowing was for people who wanted something visible from each other, and right now she wanted nothing on record.

"Sit." He indicated the step below him without looking at it. He was examining her instead, the way she'd seen buyers examine fish at the morning market—checking the gills, the eyes, the firmness of the flesh. "Do you know why I chose you?"

"Because I can count," she said, "and because nobody looks at an undercook."

"The second reason, mostly." Something moved at the corner of his mouth that might have been approval. "You have a gift, Mistress Vane. Not the old blood—" and here he said the words carefully, tasting them, the way one tastes a suspect vintage— "but a plainer gift. You see what's in front of you. Most of the palace has learned to look through everything. You look *at*. That's rarer than magic, these days. And considerably more survivable."

Sefa kept her face still. Her heart was doing arithmetic of its own. *He knows I've been copying him. He let me.* Which meant either she'd been a leash he held from the start, or she was a knife he was only now deciding how to hold. Neither possibility made her warmer toward him.

"What do you want, my lord?"

"Straight to terms. Good." He drew a paper from inside his coat and unfolded it across his knee, smoothing it with both hands like a man laying out a map of territory he already owned. "In four days I host a dinner. Small, private, significant guests. At that dinner, certain matters will come to a head regarding a taster in Her Majesty's service—one Petra Voss."

Her stomach dropped so cleanly she felt dizzy. She thought of the kitchens, three nights past: Petra moving along the cup racks with that strange, listening attention, fingertips hovering over glazed surfaces, nostrils flaring like a man reading smoke. She thought of the way he'd caught her watching him and hadn't looked away, hadn't performed innocence, had simply *nodded*, as if they'd agreed on something neither of them could say aloud.

"What about him?" she asked, and hated how much interest leaked through the words.

"He is gifted." Aldery said it flatly, no drama, the way a man says *the roof leaks*. "The Hunt requires testimony. Sworn, specific, from a credible witness within palace service. Someone who observed the taster behaving in ways no ordinary palate could explain." He turned the paper around and held it out. "Your deposition. Already drafted. You need only read it, swear it, and sign."

She took it because refusing to take it would tell him more than reading it would. The lamp was close enough that the words swam up clear:

*I, Sefa Vane, undercook in the royal household, do testify that I have on multiple occasions observed the food-taster Petra Voss handling vessels, cups, and instruments in a manner consistent with the forbidden gift, including but not limited to...*

It went on. Dates she recognized. Nights she had indeed watched him do things she couldn't account for. Whoever drafted this had been watching her watch him. The realization crawled up her spine on small cold feet.

"And in exchange?" she said.

"Immunity." Aldery leaned back against the stone wall, folding his arms, comfortable as a landlord at rent day. "Full protection under the Hunt's registry. Your name entered as crown witness, exempt from seizure, exempt from questioning. Your mother's debts—" he watched her flinch, and she understood that he had known about the house all along, that every fact of her life had been inventory before she ever was— "discharged. A position. Head of provisioning, if you want it; you'd be good at it. Or a quiet stipend and a cottage somewhere the criers never reach. Your choice. All of it yours, for one signature."

The stairwell was very quiet. Somewhere below, water dripped from a pipe joint with the patience of a thing that had been dripping since before either of them was born.

Sefa read the deposition again. It was well made. That was the obscene part—it was *well made*, every detail true or true-adjacent, assembled by someone who understood that the best lies are load-bearing walls of truth. She thought about her mother's house with its crooked chimney, the debt ledger in the tax office with her family name written down it like a sentence. She thought about the registry lists nailed to the church doors, names in black ink, and the smoke that came from the far side of the river some mornings, thin and wrong-smelling, that everyone pretended was tanneries.

And she thought about Petra Voss standing in her kitchen doorway two nights ago, holding a stolen cup, saying, *Someone in this palace is drinking the queen.* Saying it like a confession. Like a hand extended in the dark.

"If he's guilty of what your paper says," she said slowly, "why do you need me? You're the Lord Chancellor. You could hang him with a nod."

"I could arrest him," Aldery corrected. "Arresting is easy. Convicting a royal officer requires procedure, and procedure requires witnesses, and witnesses require credibility. An undercook who saw what she saw and tells the truth about it—that is worth more than ten lords' opinions. The Hunt needs the city to believe the rot goes all the way to the king's own table. You are how they'll believe it."

*The rot goes all the way to the table.* She turned the phrase over and found it double-edged. Because she had seen what Petra saw—or near enough. She had seen the queen at the autumn feast, that beautiful hollowed-out face, asking after a brother dead fifteen years. She had seen the accounts she copied: sums moving from the Chancellery to a name that appeared nowhere else, *the Glasswrights' Guild*, month after month, like a wound that wouldn't close. If anyone was pouring poison into the palace, she strongly suspected the man offering her immunity had signed for the delivery.

"I need time," she said.

Aldery's expression didn't change, which was itself a kind of answer. "How much?"

"Two days."

"One. The dinner is in four; the deposition must be sworn before it. You'll bring the signed page to my secretary by tomorrow midnight, same place." He rose, unhurried, and retrieved his lamp. For a moment the light threw his shadow huge and wavering up the stairwell wall, a giant with a small precise head. Then he paused, and looked down at her, and his voice softened into something almost kind, which frightened her worse than anything else he'd done.

"Mistress Vane. Consider carefully what he is. Whatever kindness you think you've seen in the taster—he is what the Hunt exists for. A man who can drink a secret out of your cup and walk away knowing everything. Would you trust such a creature with your life? With your mother's?"

"No," Sefa said honestly. "I'd make sure he owed me first."

For the first time, Aldery laughed—a short, genuine bark of surprise. "There it is. The plain gift." He started up the stairs, then stopped. "One day, Mistress Vane. After that, my offer stands only for those who stood with me early."

His footsteps faded. The lamp-glow faded. She sat alone in the dark with the unsigned deposition in her lap and the boning knife against her forearm and the drip, drip, drip of the pipe keeping time like a patient little heart.

* * *

She didn't go straight back. She took the long way, through the scullery passage and out the yard door into the herb garden, because she needed sky, even a smudged orange smear of it above the palace roofs, even air that smelled of cold chives and ash from the Hunt's burnings across the river.

She laid it out the way she laid out a service: course by course.

Sign the page. Be safe. Her mother's house returned, the debt burned, a title with weight to it—*Head of Provisioning*, said aloud in her own voice, tasting of warm bread and permanence. And underneath it, the cost, itemized: Petra Voss on a pyre or a scaffold, and every person like him—because she had understood by now that he was not the only one, that the world was full of people whose tongues and hands carried the old blood—lined up behind him, one signature at a time. Hers would be the first name on that particular menu. Chefs knew what it meant to be first course. Everything after was easier to swallow.

Refuse. Stay clean. And then what? Aldery didn't need her specifically; she was convenient, not essential. He'd find another witness, another angle, and she'd be a loose end with knowledge in her head and no protection at all. Loose ends in this palace had a way of ending up in the river with their pockets full of stones.

Or—

Or there was a third course, and she'd been turning it over since the night Petra first showed her the cup. *Two cups,* he'd said. *The trail splits.* He believed someone besides the Chancellor was buying pieces of the queen's mind from the Glasswrights, and he believed Aldery's faction meant to loose whatever was in those bottles like a hound at a hunt—to give the throne its war back, and use the panic to burn every gifted soul in the capital at once. She had copied the accounts. She knew the payments were real. She had never known what they bought, and now she suspected, and the suspicion sat in her gut like spoiled cream.

If she signed, she fed that hound. If she refused, she fed herself to it.

Unless she did neither. Unless she stalled him, kept the immunity dangled in front of her like bait on a hook she had no intention of swallowing, and used the time to put the whole plot in front of Petra—and whoever else he trusted—and let them decide what to do with it. Copies. She had copies. She had *always* kept copies; that was the first rule of surviving powerful men, that you never surrendered your only evidence of their sins. Aldery thought he was buying a witness. He was, in fact, negotiating with his own archive.

It was a good plan. It was a plan with teeth. She recited it twice in the dark garden and felt her pulse slow.

Then she thought about what happened to plans with teeth when they met men with armies, and her pulse climbed again, and she stood there in the cold until her fingers went numb, wanting with a nakedness that embarrassed her to simply be safe, to be small, to be paid, to stop being the sort of person midnights happened to.

When she finally came back inside, the kitchen was empty except for one figure sitting on the flour sacks near the banked ovens, elbows on knees, waiting. Petra looked up, and in the ember-light his eyes went straight past her face to the paper still clutched in her fist—as if he could smell the ink, as if he could taste whose hand had written it.

"Aldery," he said quietly. Not a question.

Sefa stood in the doorway between the cold yard and the warm kitchen, holding the deposition that named her friend's death, and heard herself say:

"We need to talk about your dinner invitation. Because I've just been told I'm the main course—and I think you are too."
