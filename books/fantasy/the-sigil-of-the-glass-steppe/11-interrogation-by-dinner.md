## 11. Interrogation by Dinner

The invitation arrived at dawn, delivered by a boy in the Chancellor's colors who did not wait for a reply. Heavy card, gilt-edged, smelling faintly of something Petra could not name until he held it to his tongue's memory: *vetiver and iron*. Ink brewed with a trace of truthroot. Even the summons was bait.

*Lord Aldery requests the pleasure of the Queen's taster at supper. Seven o'clock. Dress simply.*

Dress simply. As though simplicity were not its own performance.

Petra turned the card over twice, then set it on his table beside the cold remains of his breakfast and let himself feel the fear properly for exactly sixty seconds — he counted them by the drip of the eave outside his window — before folding it away into the small locked room behind his ribs where he kept everything that could kill him.

Aldery knew. Not everything, perhaps. But enough to set a table like a snare, and now he wanted Petra to walk into it wearing his best manners.

There was no refusing. A man who declined the Lord Chancellor's supper might as well send a confession written in ash. And there was no warning Sefa — or rather, there was no *safe* way to warn her; every note between them was a noose waiting for the wrong pair of hands. He had settled instead for a single line slipped into her flour sack that morning: *If I dine poorly tonight, feed yourself well tomorrow.* She would understand. She understood everything, which frightened him almost as much as Aldery did.

He dressed simply. He sharpened nothing. He practiced ignorance in the mirror until his face forgot how to know things.

* * *

The Chancellor's residence stood on Vellum Row among the other great houses of the court faction, all pale stone and narrow windows, built like faces turned politely away from the suffering in the lower streets. Inside it was warm in the way of banked fires and closed doors, and smelled — Petra catalogued it automatically, the way other men counted exits — of beeswax, roasted quail, dried orange peel, and beneath it all, so faint a lesser nose would have missed it entirely, *silverleaf and black horehound*. The base note of confession-drafts.

Every dish in that house had been brewed to open a gifted palate like a lock.

"Master Voss." Aldery received him in the antechamber personally, which was itself a message. The Lord Chancellor was a spare, silvered man with kind eyes that had never once been kind, and he took Petra's hand in both of his own as if greeting a son. "You honor me. I confess I've long wanted to meet the famous tongue of the palace. They say you can taste a lie in the butter."

"They flatter me, my lord. I can only taste what's *in* the butter."

Aldery laughed — a warm, genuine-sounding sound, which meant it had been rehearsed. "Modest, too. Come. We eat early in this house; old habits from the border campaigns." He steered Petra forward with a hand light between his shoulder blades, and Petra felt the touch the way a deer feels a shadow crossing the grass.

Seven guests at table. He counted them by threat. Two guild-adjacent merchants whose perfumes carried the waxy ghost of bottling-seal. A magistrate who had signed three burn-orders that month and ate with the appetite of a man untroubled by smoke. A woman in dove-gray whom nobody introduced and everybody deferred to, who watched Petra the way falconers watch falcons. And at Aldery's right hand, a place laid for one more guest who never arrived — an empty chair that sat through the whole evening like a held breath.

Petra understood the empty chair within the first course. It was his seat. The one they'd have given him if they'd decided already what he was. Its emptiness said: *this is still negotiable.*

The first course arrived under a silver dome: a clear soup, amber-gold, floating slivers of preserved lemon.

"Barley water and citron," said the footman, "with honeyflower."

Honeyflower. Petra knew it under four other names, two of them illegal. Steeped correctly, it did nothing to an ordinary tongue. In a gifted mouth it loosened the palate's shutters — made flavors *loud*, made the buried signatures in food rise up shouting. Drink it and every trap in the following courses would find him wide open.

He lifted the spoon, let the broth catch the candlelight, and performed the small theater of a cautious gourmet: a sip, a pause, a thoughtful tilt of the head. "Lovely," he murmured. "Though I'd swear there's a thread of saffron the cook isn't owning to."

There was no saffron. But the lie did its work: it reframed the soup's strange brightness as a spice dispute, a matter of kitchen pride, and gave the table something safe to argue about. The dove-gray woman's gaze flickered — recalculating — while the magistrate launched into a story about a cook he'd once had flogged.

Petra drank half the soup and let the rest cool. Half was courtesy. All of it was suicide.

* * *

The second course was quail, glazed in sour cherry, and it was honest food — deliberately, conspicuously honest. He tasted nothing hidden in it at all, and understood that too. Aldery was teaching him the rhythm. *Here we are generous. Here we are not.* A cat letting the mouse believe in open doors.

Conversation circled the Gift-Hunt with the delicate cruelty of a knife being sharpened at the far end of a room.

"—register, of course," the magistrate was saying. "That's all anyone asks. Registration is mercy. My grandfather kept a register of wolves on his land and no one called him cruel for it."

"And yet," said one of the wax-scented merchants mildly, "a wolf registered is still a wolf."

"Ah, but a wolf that registers has agreed to be *counted*." Aldery smiled around the table. "Counting is civilization. Uncounted things are where danger lives. Wouldn't you agree, Master Voss? You count for a living, I'm told."

"I keep ledgers, my lord. Numbers mostly. Numbers don't bite."

"No," Aldery said softly. "But the people behind them do."

Under the tablecloth, Petra's hand rested flat and still against his knee. Above it, he smiled the smile of a man who found accounting amusing.

The third course came in small covered dishes, one per guest, which was unusual — potted hare, dark and gamey, served with black bread. The moment the dome lifted, the smell reached him and his blood went to ice water and stayed there.

*Grief-root.* Not the harmless ornamental variety — the mountain strain, the one that cost more per ounce than the quail had in its life. Brewed into food, it did one thing: it resonated with the gift. To an ordinary diner, merely an exceptionally rich potted hare. To a tongue like Petra's, it rang against any foreign flavor lodged in the eater's own body and *sang* it back out. Eat this, and if he had ever swallowed so much as a drop of bottled memory, the residue would surface in his sweat, his breath, his next words. The whole table would taste his guilt on the air.

They weren't testing whether he was gifted. They were testing whether he'd *fed*.

He looked down at the potted hare and thought, absurdly, of Sefa's stew — of the night she'd fed him without knowing what she was feeding, and how his own childhood had risen out of the bowl like steam. If grief-root met that buried past here, at this table, in front of these people—

No. That memory was his mother's recipe, his mother's kitchen, sealed deep under years of deliberate forgetting. Grief-root rang out *foreign* flavors. What was native to him, buried in his own marrow, it could only warm, not reveal.

Probably.

He ate it anyway. Refusal was confession. He took the black bread first — bread blunted everything, another taster's trick — then the hare in small, appreciative bites, narrating his enjoyment aloud in the flat vocabulary of a professional. "Excellent depth. Juniper in the curing, I think. And the meat hung properly — a week at least?"

"A fortnight," said the cook's messenger proudly, and the table relaxed into culinary chatter, and Petra chewed and swallowed and waited to see if he would die of his own history.

What rose in him was manageable: a warmth behind the breastbone, the smell of woodsmoke that existed nowhere in the room, the ghost-pressure of a small hand in his. His mother's kitchen, rising like sediment in wine. He kept his face pleasant and his breathing even and thought, over and over, *I am a man eating dinner. I am a man eating dinner,* until the warmth sank again.

The dove-gray woman was watching him. Not the way the others watched — not calculating, but *listening*, head slightly tilted, as if she could hear the sediment moving.

He filed her away as the most dangerous person in the room after the host.

* * *

Between courses, Aldery dismissed the servants — dismissed them *all*, even the footmen, with a wave that seemed casual and was surgical — and the doors were shut by a single guard who then stood with his back to them, facing outward.

"Now," Aldery said pleasantly, refilling Petra's wine himself, "we may speak freely."

The wine was clean. Petra checked it twice and found nothing but good vintage, which told him this was the part where Aldery wanted him sober.

"You've served the crown for eleven years," Aldery said. "Tasted for two queens. There's no man in this city whose discretion I trust more." He sat back, cradling his own glass. "Which is why I'm troubled by rumors."

"Rumors are the cheapest commodity in the capital, my lord. I'd have thought you'd cornered that market years ago."

Someone at the table inhaled; the magistrate hid it badly. Aldery's smile didn't move a hair. "Indeed. And yet some commodities I take seriously. Bottled memory, for instance. Do you know much about it, Master Voss?"

"Only what any man knows. That it exists. That it's forbidden."

"Forbidden things flow best in this city. Did you know—" and here Aldery leaned forward, voice dropping into the register of a confessor, "—that the Queen's malady is not a malady at all? That somewhere in this city sits a vial containing nine years of her reign, sold by the very guild your ledgers serve?"

The silence had texture. Petra felt it press against his eardrums. Around him the guests had gone still in the particular way of people who already knew the script and were watching to see if he knew his lines.

"I keep accounts for a trade guild, my lord. Salt, sugar, glassware. If they sell sins on the side, they don't write them down where I can audit."

"Don't they?" Aldery reached into his coat and produced a single sheet of paper — not a ledger page, but a *copy* of one, and Petra's stomach dropped through the floor because he knew that hand. Knew it the way he knew his own reflection. It was Corvane's handwriting, or something close enough to hang a man.

One line, transcribed in careful ink: *Item: one sovereign recollection, royal vintage, nine years' decant. Buyer retained. Taster's fee pending.*

*Taster's fee pending.* As if Petra had priced it. As if Petra had brokered it.

"That's a forgery," he said, and was proud of how bored he made it sound. "Anyone who's seen my figures could copy my style. My sevens have a crossbar. That one doesn't."

Aldery turned the paper toward the light, examining it like a jeweler. "Perhaps. Forgeries cut both ways in this town — I'm sure you know that better than most. But here is what interests me, Master Voss." He set the paper down between them, squared to the table's edge, precise as everything else about him. "I don't need it to be genuine. I only need it to be *plausible*. The Hunt needs convictions, and convictions need stories, and stories—" he spread his hands, "—stories are just memories written down. You, of all people, should appreciate the craft."

There it was. No more pretense than a knife wrapped in a napkin. *Cooperate, and the paper stays fiction. Resist, and it becomes testimony.*

"What would cooperation look like?" Petra asked quietly. Better to make Aldery say it. Everything said at this table was being remembered by seven witnesses, and he intended for every word to be a stone in someone else's shoe someday.

"Simple. The guild has grown greedy. It sells to factions inside this court — dangerous factions, men who want the old war back because war is profitable and purges are tidy. I am trying to save this crown from those men, Master Voss, whatever the street-poets say of me. Help me map their purchases. Names, dates, buyers. Your ledgers, read carefully." Aldery's kind eyes held his. "Do that, and this paper goes in my fire. And no one ever asks why the Queen's taster can taste a lie in the butter."

*And if I refuse,* Petra did not say, *you hang me with ink I didn't write, and the true buyers walk free, and the queen dies unfed.*

"I'll need time," he said. "The ledgers are coded."

"I'd expect nothing less from a man of your quality." Aldery raised his glass. "A week."

Around the table the tension broke like weather clearing; servants returned; dessert appeared — plain candied pears, honest as bread, the meal's final lesson: *see how reasonable we can be.* Petra ate one pear and tasted nothing but sugar and his own pulse.

As the guests rose, the dove-gray woman passed close beside him. Close enough that only he could hear, close enough that her words rode on a breath scented with the same grief-root she'd eaten without flinching.

"Your sevens have a crossbar," she murmured. "I checked."

Then she was gone through the door ahead of him, and Petra stood alone a moment in the candlelight, cold to the bone in a warm room, doing arithmetic.

Aldery had a forged ledger page. Which meant someone had shown Aldery the original — or someone had *made* the original available. Corvane's hand, or Corvane's price. Either way, his guildmaster had already begun selling him, the way the guild sold everything: to whoever paid, whichever side won.

A week. He had a week to produce either treason or treachery, and both roads ended with him in someone's pocket.

He walked home through streets gone quiet under curfew lanterns, taking the long way, doubling back twice, and when at last he climbed the stair to the only door in the city he trusted, his hands were shaking for the first time all night.

Sefa opened it before he knocked. She looked at his face and stepped aside to let him in, and behind her the room was lit and warm and smelling of onions and thyme — a stew already on the fire, as if she'd known, as if she always knew, and Petra crossed her threshold thinking: *I cannot carry this alone anymore.*

"I dined poorly," he said.

Her eyes went over him once, cataloguing damage the way he catalogued flavors. "Then sit down," she said, "and tell me who tried to poison you — and what they used."
