## 12. The Taste of Home

The rooms above the undercooks' quarters smelled of rendered fat and river-stone, and Petra Voss had never in his life felt safer anywhere.

That was the thing he could not stop noticing—the wrongness of it. He had spent three days as a fugitive from two powers, sleeping in a bed too short for him, listening to Sefa's boots on the stairs at odd hours, and instead of fear he kept finding this absurd, animal contentment, like a dog that had finally been let inside. His body did not understand what his mind knew: that Corvane's collectors had eyes in every market stall, that Aldery's clerks were combing the palace rolls for a food-taster who had vanished between one dinner and the next, that the Hunt burned people on the salt flats now rather than waiting for pyres to be built.

His body just noticed that the bread here was warm.

"You're staring at my ceiling again," Sefa said from the doorway. She had flour on her forearms to the elbow and a smudge of ash—real ash, hearth-ash, not the other kind—along her jaw. "There's nothing up there but a crack I've been meaning to fix since winter."

"I'm memorizing it," Petra said. "In case I have to describe these rooms to someone someday."

"Describe them how?"

He considered the question honestly, because she deserved honesty even when he was lying about everything else. "Warm," he said. "Small. The kind of place where a person keeps their good spoon wrapped in cloth because they only use it once a year."

Sefa looked at him for a long moment, her dark eyes doing that thing they did—sorting him, filing him, deciding which drawer he belonged in. "You're a strange man, Petra Voss."

"I'm a taster. We're all strange. You spend your life imagining the worst thing that could happen next, and eventually you can't turn it off."

"No." She set down the bundle she was carrying—a covered pot, dented at the lip, older than either of them. "You spend your life tasting what other people put into things. There's a difference." She lifted the lid, and steam rose up between them, thick with marrow and thyme. "Eat. You've been living on my scraps and stolen plums for two days, and you look like a ghost that owes money."

It was a simple stew. That was all it was supposed to be. Barley and neck bones, an onion gone soft in the bottom of the pot, thyme from a bunch she'd bought off the herb-seller with the crooked cart because he gave undercooks a fair price. It was the kind of food that existed below the notice of poisoners and guildmasters and witch-hunters, the food of people who had never been worth poisoning.

Petra took the first spoonful the way he took every first spoonful, out of habit older than thought: he let it sit on his tongue and listened.

And the past opened like a door he had nailed shut twenty years ago.

* * *

It came in layers, the way memory always did when it arrived uninvited.

First: barley. Barley cooked in a pot with a cracked glaze, in a kitchen so small the stove and the table shared a wall. A woman humming—not a song he could name, a made-up song, the hummed nonsense mothers invent when they are tired and happy anyway. Rain on a roof that leaked over the far corner, into a bucket they'd positioned years ago and simply stopped discussing.

Then: her hands. Brown hands, quick hands, hands that moved through the air when she talked as though she were shaping something invisible—which she was, though he hadn't known it then. Hands that smelled faintly of vinegar and cloves, because clove-water was what she used to rinse the taste of other people's lives out of her mouth.

*Mama.*

The word rose in him with a physical force, a fist behind the breastbone, and Petra set down the spoon very carefully because his hand had begun to shake.

He had built walls around this. He understood that now, seeing them from the inside for the first time—he had walled it off so thoroughly and so young that he had come to believe the room behind the wall was empty. He remembered being an orphan raised by a wool merchant's widow in the river districts. He remembered it *specifically*, with details, the way you remember anything you've told often enough. What he had never let himself remember was that the story had seams. That the widow's face wouldn't hold still when he tried to recall it. That there was a gap in his childhood shaped exactly like a woman who hummed while she stirred.

She had been a memory-worker. Not a bottler—something older, rarer, one of the last practitioners of the oral craft, the gift-bearers who carried other people's griefs in their own bodies so the grieving could sleep. She'd take a widow's mourning into her mouth like a draught and hold it, sometimes for months, until the widow was strong enough to take it back or until it faded safe enough to bury. Petra used to watch her at night, standing at the window, swaying slightly, her lips moving with words that weren't hers, carrying some stranger's dead through her own dreams.

He remembered asking her once—it surfaced now with terrible clarity, the smell of rain and wet wool—*Mama, does it hurt?*

And she had crouched down to his height, this small kitchen, the cracked pot, the leaking roof, and said: *Everything true hurts a little, little fish. But it hurts less than a lie. Remember that. If you ever have to choose, choose the truth that hurts.*

They had come for her in autumn. He remembered the boots on the stairs now—why had he ever forgotten the boots?—and the way she'd pushed him into the flour chest and held the lid down from outside, and through the crack he had seen the fire-lighters' badges, the gray-and-orange of the last purge, and heard her say, in a voice completely without fear: *I carry no one's memory but my own tonight. Check my mouth if you don't believe me.*

They checked. And they found thirty years of strangers' sorrows layered in her like rings in a tree, and they called it hoarding, and they called her a vessel of corruption, and the last thing Petra saw through the crack in the chest was her looking directly at the flour chest—at *him*—as they led her out, and the last thing she mouthed was not goodbye.

It was *choose the truth that hurts.*

He had been seven years old. The widow had paid him in stories: *your mother died of fever, your mother was nobody, your mother left no traces.* And he had swallowed the story whole because seven-year-olds will swallow anything to survive, and the swallowing itself had become the wall, and behind the wall his mother hummed in a kitchen that no longer existed, stirring barley in a pot with a cracked glaze—

"—Petra."

Sefa's voice. Her hand on his wrist. The stew going cold in front of him, one tear sitting on the back of his hand like a coin he hadn't earned.

He hadn't heard himself crying. That frightened him more than anything else. A taster who couldn't hear his own body.

* * *

He told her all of it.

Not all at once, and not gracefully. He started with the stew—"what did you put in this"—because deflection was the first language he'd ever learned, and she cut through it with the flat pragmatism he was coming to rely on more than food: "Barley, bones, onion, thyme, salt. Nothing. It's *nothing*, it's the cheapest pot in the city. Whatever you tasted, it wasn't in the pot."

"No," he agreed. "It was in me."

So he told her. About his mother, and the flour chest, and the gray-and-orange badges. About how the orphanage story was stitched together by a well-meaning widow who probably believed she was saving him, and how he had lived inside the stitching for twenty-five years. About the day the gift had announced itself—fourteen years old, tasting for the merchant's table, and a wine that tasted of someone else's wedding, and how he'd run to the privy and been sick and then sat on the floor and laughed until he cried, because his mother's curse had waited inside him like a seed in frozen ground.

About the Guild. About Corvane finding him before the Hunt did, the way Corvane always found them, offering ledgers instead of fire. About the double life, the double books—the honest ledger for the crown, the true ledger in salt-ink for the vault. About the widow who'd sold her wedding day for bread money, and how he still saw her sometimes at the fish market, smiling at strangers with a face that didn't remember why it had ever been happy.

Sefa listened the way she cooked: steadily, without wasted motion, giving the whole of her attention to the task. When he faltered she refilled his bowl. When he stopped altogether she waited, and the waiting was somehow worse and better than questions.

When he reached the queen—the fading queen, the splinter of foreign memory lodged in her, Corvane selling her own stolen self back into her house like a knife wrapped in a gift—her hands went still on the table.

"The chancellor," she said quietly. "Aldery's faction wants that memory loose. A war-memory. Proof the gifted are dangerous. Proof the purge should be total." She exhaled slowly. "I've been copying his accounts for eight months, Petra. I knew pieces of it. I never had the shape of it."

"And now?"

"Now I have the shape of it." She met his eyes. "Why are you telling me all this? Truly. You could have kept the childhood. Everyone keeps the childhood. You could have given me the guild and the plot and kept your mother behind whatever wall she lived behind."

Petra looked at the stew. At the cheap pot with its dented lid. At the good spoon wrapped in cloth on the shelf, which he had apparently identified correctly, and which suddenly mattered to him in a way he couldn't justify.

"Because you fed me the truth," he said. "Your cooking does that. Did you know that? Everything you make tastes like exactly what it is. No performance. No second ledger." He laughed, and it came out broken. "Do you know what I do all day? Every flavor is a sentence someone is trying to get past me. Every dish is a document with clauses hidden in the sauce. I haven't eaten an honest meal in—I don't know how long. Years. Maybe since I was seven years old and my mother put a bowl in front of me and there was nothing in it but barley and love."

Sefa was quiet for a moment. Then, in the tone she used for correcting apprentices: "Your mother sounds like she was smarter than the whole Guild."

"She was." His throat closed. "She carried other people's griefs for a living, Sefa. She held strangers' dead in her mouth so they could sleep at night. And they burned her for it in a public square, and her son grew up and went to work keeping books for the men who inherited her trade and turned it into a marketplace." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "Corvane calls us vessels. Assets. He says we're safer inside the Guild than outside it, and the worst part—the part I can't forgive—is that he's right. Outside the Guild I'm just meat for the pyre. Inside it I'm meat with a salary."

"And what are you here?"

He lowered his hands. She was watching him with an expression he had no ledger-entry for—not pity, not the sharp sorting look, something warmer and more dangerous than either.

"Here," he said, "I think I might be a person."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere below, a cart passed, and the floorboards carried its rumble up through the soles of their feet like a slow heartbeat.

"Sefa." His voice did something unfamiliar—dropped its armor mid-word, the way a man sets down a shield knowing he may not pick it up again. "Tomorrow or the day after, Corvane finds me, or Aldery finds me, or the Hunt finds both of us, and none of those meetings end well. I've done the arithmetic; I always do the arithmetic. The odds that I see another spring are poor. So I'm not going to spend whatever time I have being careful about this."

"About what?" she asked, though her eyes said she knew.

"About the fact that I love you." The words came out steadier than anything he'd said all evening, and that steadiness surprised him most of all. "Not the way I love things I understand. I understand flavors, ledgers, poisons. I don't understand you—you keep choosing courage when cowardice would cost you less, and it makes no sense to me, and I can't stop watching you do it. I love you the way your stew tastes. Like exactly what it is. And if I die on the glass flats next week, or burn on a register, or drink something that erases me down to the last syllable—I wanted one person to have heard it plainly, from my own mouth, with nothing hidden in the sauce."

For a long moment Sefa said nothing. Then she reached across the table and took his hand—turned it palm-up, examined it like a cook checking a blade—and said:

"You taste everything before you swallow it. That's your whole life. Tasting first." She closed his fingers over hers. "So taste this."

She leaned across the cold stew and kissed him, and Petra Voss—who could read intent in a grain of salt, who had never in twenty-five years taken a single mouthful on faith—closed his eyes and swallowed without tasting at all.

* * *

Later, in the dark, with her breath slowing against his shoulder and the lamp burned down to a blue bead, Petra lay awake doing the thing he could not stop doing: tasting forward.

Because there had been something else in that first spoonful of stew, underneath his mother's kitchen, underneath the grief. A thread. Faint as a hair in broth, but his palate had been trained by masters, and it did not miss threads.

Clove-water. Vinegar and cloves.

His mother had rinsed her mouth with it after carrying strangers' memories. And somewhere in the palace—on a cup, in a vial, threaded through the queen's fading days—the stolen war-memory carried the same faint signature. Not coincidence. Craft. Whoever had bottled the queen's memory had learned the old oral techniques, the pre-Guild techniques, the ones that were supposed to have burned out entirely in the last purge.

Techniques his mother had known.

Which meant either the Guild's founders had kept her lineage's secrets alive in their vaults—or someone who had stood in that small kitchen, in that rain, had survived besides him.

Petra stared at the ceiling crack and, for the first time since he was seven years old, let himself remember his mother's face without flinching.

Then he got up quietly, found Sefa's scrap-paper by the banked stove, and began to write down everything he could recall about the men in gray-and-orange. Because tomorrow he would need a list of names, and names, unlike memories, could be hunted.
