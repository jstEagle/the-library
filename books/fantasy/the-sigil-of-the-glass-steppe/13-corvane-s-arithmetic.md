## 13. Corvane's Arithmetic

He was waiting for me in my own rooms.

I knew it before I opened the door—knew it by the smell. Corvane carried his wealth the way other men carried knives: discreetly, close to the body. Beneath the leather-and-ink scent of him there was always a trace of ambergris and cold iron, the perfume of the vault, of vials sealed in wax that had once sealed lips. It came through the gap beneath my door like a rumor.

I had left Sefa's rooms before dawn, moving through streets still blue with night, taking the long way twice, doubling back past the tanneries where the Gift-Hunt's new registers hung nailed to posts like flayed skins. I had told myself no one followed. I had almost believed it.

But Corvane did not follow people. He simply arrived where their debts were kept.

The door was unlocked. Of course it was. Locks were for men who feared theft; Corvane feared nothing in this city because everything in this city already belonged to him on paper, in salt, in memory pressed between glass.

He sat at my table—the small table where I ate my meals alone, where I kept my honest ledger, the one I showed the crown's assessors—and he was reading it. My double-entry life laid open under his finger. The false columns. The true ones. He had found both without looking for either.

"Your handwriting has improved," he said, not raising his eyes. "When you came to me you wrote like a frightened clerk. Now you write like a man who knows what each word costs." He turned a page. "This entry here. *One grief, unclassified, detected at banquet, logged pending.* You've started keeping a third ledger, Petra. One even I didn't know about."

"Master Corvane." I stood in my own doorway like a stranger seeking admittance. "You're in my home."

"I own your home." He said it mildly, the way he said everything, as though cruelty were merely an accounting term. "As I own your contract, your lodging stipend, and the debt against your mother's name that brought you to me when you were fourteen years old and starving and so very proud of your tongue." He finally looked up. His eyes were the color of the vault's darkness—not black, but deep, deep glass. "Sit down, Petra. Let us do arithmetic together."

I sat. It was not courage. It was arithmetic too: the door behind him, the window beside him, the knife in the drawer at his right hand. I counted these the way he counted assets, and I waited.

* * *

"You went to the kitchens," he said. "You traced the queen's memory through the service corridors. You found Aldery's cup." He recited it like entries in a book he'd written long before I'd lived it. "And then you found Sefa Amari, undercook, third-year, wages garnished for her brother's register fines. And you—" here he paused, and something moved across his face that in another man might have been called tenderness, though on Corvane it looked more like a ledger noting an unexpected expense, "—you fell in love with her. Or near enough. Near enough is how wars start."

"How do you know all this?"

"I know everything that passes through my guild. Every bottle, every bargain, every whisper sold by one servant to another." He folded his hands. "You think the Hunt burns its way through this city blind? Petra. Who do you think sold them the registers' first draft?"

The words went into me like a fishhook. "You gave the Hunt its lists."

"I gave the Hunt its *shape*. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole of what I am about to teach you." He rose, walked to my window, and looked out over the rooftops toward the palace, its glass spires catching the morning like blades catching light. "Do you know what happens to a gift-bearer who registers?"

"They're catalogued. Collared. Put to work."

"They're put to work," he agreed, "until they are used up. And those who don't register?" He turned back to me. "Burned. You watched them burn the bottler Ovesse on Tuesday. You stood in that crowd and you wept, and you thought: *this is madness, this is the throne devouring its own hands.* You thought it was fear. Fear of us."

"Isn't it?"

"No." Corvane smiled, and it was the worst thing I had ever seen him do, worse than any threat, because it was patient. "It is opportunity. Someone at court wanted the gifted purged—or wanted the *threat* of purge, which is more useful—and they needed a market panic to justify it. A purge empties the vaults of competitors. Independent memory-workers, hedge-tasters, village cunning-folk with a drop of old blood—all of them seized or burned or scattered, their stocks confiscated, their clients terrified. And into that vacuum walks one institution large enough, discreet enough, indispensable enough to hold every bottle, every bargain, every secret in the capital." He spread his hands, presenting the city itself. "Guess which one."

"The Guild of Impossible Things."

"The Glasswrights' Guild," he corrected softly. "We changed our name the last time, too. Names are cheap. Survival is expensive. That is the first lesson."

"And Ovesse?" My voice cracked on the bottler's name. Old Ovesse, who had taught me to seal wax without bubbles, who hummed while he worked, who would not sign the register because he said his gift was a prayer and prayers could not be taxed. "Was his death also survival?"

Corvane did not flinch. That was the terrible thing about him; I have seen men flinch at their own cruelty, and he never did. "Ovesse refused three contracts over ten years and preached refusal to others. His death bought compliance from two hundred workers who watched. Yes, Petra. His death was survival. Not his. Mine. The guild's. Yours, though you haven't the stomach yet to see it."

"I see it fine." I stood up. My chair scraped back and the sound was very loud. "You built the fire. You sold the wood. And now you sit in the smoke and call yourself our shepherd."

"I call myself your *arithmetic*." He remained seated, which was its own kind of contempt. "Sit down, boy. I'm about to offer you the only mercy anyone will offer the gifted in your lifetime, and you'll want to be sitting."

* * *

He took something from inside his coat and set it on the table between us.

Not a vial. A document. Heavy paper, sealed with the guild's sigil—the closed hand—and countersigned in a second seal I did not recognize until I looked closer and felt the floor tilt: the privy seal of the Lord Chancellor's office. Aldery.

"Partnership," Corvane said. "Full partner. Vault access. A seat at the table where the guild decides which memories live and which are poured out onto sand. A share of the reserves—real shares, Petra, not the fiction we feed the journeymen. And protection." He tapped the second seal. "Aldery's faction believes the gifted can be bargained with. They are wrong, but they are wrong in ways that keep us alive for now. This document makes you untouchable by the Hunt. Registered not as property but as *asset of the crown*, which is the highest rank of property there is."

"Why me?"

"Because you have a palate that reads intent, which is rarer than reading memory, and more dangerous, and more useful. Because you've been keeping my ledgers for eleven years and know where every body is buried, metaphorically—" a thin smile, "—and otherwise. And because you found Aldery's cup in a single morning, which means you can find the *second* buyer, the one whose signature split from the Chancellor's. The faction within the faction. The ones who want the queen's war-memory not locked away, not traded, but *opened*."

The room seemed to narrow around that word. Opened. I thought of the vial beneath the steppe, humming like a struck bell, and of what I had glimpsed when I touched it: the last night of the ancient war, the sky full of burning names, the gifted and the throne destroying each other over a century of stolen remembering. The queen had cut that memory out of herself like a tumor. Somewhere in the palace, men wanted it planted back in her like a seed.

"And if I refuse?" I asked, though I already knew. I asked anyway, because a man should hear his own sentence spoken.

"Then tomorrow morning a page arrives at the Hunt's office bearing your name, your mother's name, and a description of your gift detailed enough that even a half-blind witchfinder will believe it." Corvane said it without heat, without apology, the way he recorded interest owed. "You'll have perhaps a day's head start. Take Sefa if you like; her testimony is already compromised; she copied accounts for Aldery and never asked whose. Two fugitives cross the steppe. Collectors find them. The steppe is very flat, Petra. There is nowhere on it to hide, and the glass carries sound for miles."

I looked at the document. I looked at his hands—clean, ringed, unhurried, hands that had signed Ovesse's death as easily as a delivery note. And I understood, finally, the shape of the trap, which was not a trap at all but a door held open, and that was what made it monstrous. Corvane did not need to force me. Force creates martyrs. He offered ladders.

"Say it plainly," I said. "What does partnership cost?"

"Loyalty. Discretion. And one task." He leaned forward, and for a moment the merchant's mask slipped and I saw the thing underneath, the true Corvane, the one who had survived the last purge by being more useful to the burners than the burned. "The war-memory stays in the vault. Sealed. It is the single most valuable object in existence—leverage over the crown, leverage over the factions, proof that the gifted's greatest sin and the throne's greatest terror are the same event, held in glass, held by *us*. Whoever controls that vial controls whether this kingdom remembers why it fears us. We will never open it. We will never sell it entire. We will sell *access* to the fear of it, year after year, generation after generation. That is not corruption, Petra. That is peace. Ugly peace, priced peace—but the only kind this world has ever honored."

"And the queen dies of it. Fades a little more each month. Forgets her reign, her dead, herself. You'd let her die holding your leverage."

"She stole the memory from herself," Corvane said. "Whatever kills her is her own knife, turned inward. I merely declined to return what was legally purchased." He paused, and added, with what he clearly believed was generosity: "Though a partner with vault access might, in time, argue for a partial restitution. Fragments. Enough to slow the fading. Enough to buy the crown's gratitude. Gratitude, properly administered, is worth more than gold—it renews."

There it was. Mercy as merchandise. He had built an entire cathedral out of the idea that salvation should carry interest, and he was offering me a pew near the front.

I thought of Sefa's kitchen. The stew she'd made me, plain barley and root vegetables and a rind of salt pork, nothing in it but honesty—and how my own buried childhood had come up through the taste like water through chalk: my mother's hands, my mother's voice, my mother climbing onto a pyre in the last purge with her gift unnamed and her spine straight. She had died rather than be an asset. I had spent my life since then being exactly that, and telling myself it was survival, and Corvane had just handed me the same lie with better upholstery and called it partnership.

"You genuinely believe this," I said quietly. "That's the horror of it. You're not wicked. You're *arithmetic*."

"Wickedness is a luxury of the powerful," Corvane said. "We are the powerful now, or we will be, if you have the sense your tongue suggests. Sentiment is how our people die, Petra. I have watched sentiment burn for thirty years. I am done watching." He pushed the document across the table until it touched my hand. "Sign. Tonight. And by week's end we will identify the second buyer together, and the guild will be beyond reach forever."

I picked up the pen.

I let him watch me pick up the pen. I dipped it. I let the ink gather at the nib, fat and black, and I felt the whole architecture of my life lean toward that signature—eleven years of ledgers, my mother's debt, the warmth of Sefa's kitchen, Ovesse's ash on the wind, the queen mistaking me for her dead brother and gripping my wrist with fingers like a bird's, saying *you came back, you always come back.*

Then I set the pen down without signing.

"Not tonight," I said. "Tonight I taste for the palace. A partner keeps his obligations."

Something flickered in Corvane's eyes—suspicion, or its cousin, patience. "Tomorrow, then."

"Tomorrow," I agreed, and I hated how smoothly the word came, hated that lying to him had become as natural as breathing, hated most of all that some cold cellar of my soul had already begun weighing his offer and finding it *heavy*—not false, never false, that was his genius—heavy with the weight of everything true in it.

He rose, buttoned his coat, and paused at the door.

"One more entry for your third ledger," he said. "The second buyer—the signature that split from Aldery's. I know whose it is. I have known since winter. When you sign, I'll tell you." He smiled again, that patient, terrible smile. "Curiosity is a debt, Petra. I told you that years ago. Interest accrues daily."

And he was gone, leaving the unsigned contract on my table and the smell of ambergris and cold iron in my rooms like frost that would not melt.

* * *

I did not go to the palace.

I went to the window and watched him cross the courtyard below, unhurried, nodding to my neighbors as though he owned them too, because he did. Then I took the contract to the stove and held its corner over the flame until the privy seal curled and blackened, and the guild's closed hand burned last of all, the wax bubbling like a small screaming mouth.

The paper was treated against fire—I knew it would be; the guild wrote nothing it couldn't protect—but treated paper burns slowly, and I stood there the whole time it took, watching the seals die, memorizing the terms I was refusing. Partnership. Asset of the crown. Access to the vial. *Fragments, enough to slow the fading.* That phrase lodged in me like a splinter of its own.

Enough to slow the fading. He had dangled the queen's life as a perk.

By the time the last of it was ash, my decision had made itself in the place below decision, the place where taste lives before words. Tomorrow I would tell Corvane yes—yes, and mean the shape of the word and none of its substance, buying days, buying access, buying my way down into the vault one lie at a time. And somewhere in that descent, I would find what I actually needed: not leverage, not partnership, not survival.

The queen herself. Before her fading finished. Before Corvane's arithmetic closed over the last unpriced thing in this kingdom—a woman who had cut her own memory out to save the world from her, and was dying of the mercy.

I brushed the ash from my hands. Outside, the bells of the Hunt's chapel began tolling the hour, and far off, past the city walls, the glass flats caught the afternoon sun and threw it back blinding white, all the way to the vault beneath the steppe where a small vial hummed like a struck bell, waiting for someone to decide what remembering was worth.

Tomorrow I would say yes.

God forgive me, I was already rehearsing how to say it.
