## 14. The Queen's Brother

The summons came at moonrise, carried by a girl in palace gray who would not meet Petra's eyes and would not say who had sent her. Only: *The Queen asks for the taster. Alone. Come now, and come quietly.*

Petra had tasted for the queen four times since that first private audience—the small suppers she took in the western tower with no court, no ceremony, just a single candle and a servant who wept when she thought no one watched. Each time, the foreign memory lodged in the queen had grown louder to his tongue. At first it had been a whisper of smoke at the back of a spoonful of honeyed pear. By the third supper it was a drumbeat under the broth. Now he could taste it before the food even touched his lips—iron and ash and something older than either, the flavor of a night that had ended a world.

He went alone. He told himself it was because refusing was more dangerous than obeying.

The tower stair was narrow and unlit except for lamps set in niches every seventh step, each flame burning behind glass tinted the green of deep water. His footsteps sounded wrong on the stone—too loud, too much like a countdown. On the landing outside her door, he paused and pressed two fingers to his own tongue, an old habit, checking himself as a man checks a blade's edge. Still his. Still only his griefs there, and Sefa's name folded into them like salt into dough.

*Come now,* he thought, *and come quietly,* and opened the door.

* * *

She was standing at the window when he entered, and for a moment he did not recognize her.

He knew the queen the way everyone in the capital knew her—from coins and proclamations and the distant gold figure at coronation processions, always veiled against the sun. He had known the dying woman of the private suppers: vague, drifting, calling servants by the names of dead friends, sometimes by the names of trees. But the woman at the window stood straight as a drawn bow, silver-haired and sharp-eyed, wearing a plain wool robe with no crown anywhere in the room, and she turned to look at him with the full weight of a reign behind her gaze.

"Taster," she said. "Close the door. There is a bolt. Use it."

He used it. The click of iron was very loud.

"Do you know why I summoned you?" She did not wait for an answer. "No. Of course not. You've been tasting my food and tasting me, young man, and you think I don't know. You think a woman can be emptied out piece by piece and never notice what leaks through the cracks." She crossed the room with a stride that belonged to a general, and stopped close enough that he could see her eyes were not the gray of the coin-portrait but a pale amber, almost gold, almost the color of the glass flats at dawn. "You have your mother's gift. Don't flinch—I've known since the second supper, when you tasted the pear and went white as whey. Only a gifted palate reads what isn't on the plate."

"Your Majesty—" His voice cracked. He hated that it cracked. "I have registered nothing. I have told no one—"

"I am not here to burn you." Something moved across her face—tiredness, vast and old, surfacing through the lucidity like a drowned thing through still water. "I am here because I am running out of hours, and you are the only soul in this city who might understand what I am about to show you. Sit down, taster. This will take most of my hour, and I intend to spend none of it lying."

* * *

There was a table in the corner beneath a cloth of undyed linen. She pulled the cloth away, and Petra's stomach dropped, because beneath it lay a meal laid out for one: bread, hard cheese, a pear, a cup of dark wine—and beside the cup, a vial of guild glass, stoppered with wax stamped with the sigil he knew better than his own heartbeat. The Glasswrights' mark. The impossible knot.

"You recognize it," the queen said. It was not a question. "You would. You keep their books."

His mouth went dry. "I don't know what you—"

"Petra Voss." She said his name the way Corvane said figures in a ledger: precisely, without mercy. "Apprentice taster at fourteen, journeyman at nineteen, keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things' true ledgers since you were twenty-three. Do you imagine the crown does not watch the guild? Do you imagine the crown has not *always* watched the guild?" She sat, and gestured to the chair opposite, and when he did not move she said, gently, "Sit. Please. I have so little time to be kind in."

He sat.

"For thirty years," she said, "I have kept a memory in a bottle. Not stolen from me—no one took it. I gave it up myself, willingly, paid for the extraction with three years of the treasury's gold and swore the glasswright who did the work to silence on pain of everything he loved. He kept his oath. His guildmaster did not." Her hand rested on the table near the vial but did not touch it, the way a hand rests near a sleeping snake. "Corvane sold it. Years ago. And it has been passed from buyer to buyer ever since, like a coin that burns every pocket it enters, until someone finally decided the safest place to hide it was inside the one person no thief would dare rob."

"You," Petra whispered. "Someone put it back in you."

"The Lord Chancellor's faction found a bottler willing to break the wax and pour it into my wine. They believed it would make me mad enough to be replaced—or desperate enough to sign whatever they put in front of me. Instead—" the ghost of a smile, bitter as alum, "—instead they gave me back the worst night of my life, one sip at a time, and I began to forget everything else around it. My reign. My husband's face. The name of my brother." She looked at him then, fully, and Petra felt the floor tilt beneath him, because he understood a half-second before she said it. "You have his hands. Did anyone ever tell you that? You have Maren's hands exactly."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere below, a dog barked once and fell silent, ashamed.

"Maren Voss," the queen said softly. "My brother. Your grandfather, though perhaps your mother never told you the whole of it. He was a memory-worker, as your mother was, as you are. And on the last night of the Gift War, forty years ago, he stood where you are sitting and asked me to do a terrible thing, and I refused, and men died for my refusal, and in the end I did the terrible thing anyway and called it wisdom." She drew a breath. "I am going to tell you what happened that night. All of it. Because you are about to be asked to choose between my wish and another's, and you cannot choose honestly unless you know."

* * *

She told him, and the candle burned lower, and Petra forgot to breathe in the places where breathing mattered least.

There had been weapons. Not swords—the old war had long outgrown swords—but workings, great slow engines of the gift built into the land itself, seeded into the glass flats during the centuries when the gift-bearers and the throne had still pretended to be one people. Memory-engines. A working that could reach into ten thousand minds at once and *unmake what they remembered being*, leaving armies that woke as strangers to their own names. The gifted had built them in desperation; the throne had nearly used them; and on the final night, with both sides broken and bleeding across the steppe, the last of the engines had been armed and keyed to a single royal voice.

Her voice. She had been young then, newly crowned, and the keying had been done to the bloodline itself, so that any heir who spoke the words of release could wake the engines.

"The war was over," she said. "We had signed. We had buried. And then the hawks on my own council came to me with proof—real or forged, I still cannot tell you which—that the gifted were rebuilding. They knelt in this room and asked me to speak the words. One working, they said. One night's work, and the gift would be scoured from the bloodline forever, and there would never be another purge, another pyre, another register. Peace, purchased all at once." Her hands, folded on the table, had gone white-knuckled. "And I wanted to say yes. Gods forgive me, I wanted it. My husband was three months dead. The pyres were still smoking. And the words were *right there*, taster. They live in the blood. You have no idea what it is to feel a weapon waiting behind your teeth."

"But you didn't speak them."

"No. Because Maren came to me that night—your grandfather, a boy of twenty-two, still smelling of the hospital where he'd spent the war pulling memories out of shattered soldiers so they could sleep—and he didn't argue. He didn't plead. He poured me a cup of wine and asked me to taste it, and what I tasted was *him*: his memory of the last field hospital, the ones the engines had already touched, the men who woke not knowing their mothers' faces and screamed for weeks because some animal part of them knew something beloved had been taken and couldn't say what." Her composure slipped then, one hairline crack, and Petra saw the young queen beneath the old one, drowning. "He let me taste what I was about to do. That is all the argument he made. And I could not unsay the words I hadn't spoken yet, so instead I did the only other thing in my power. I cut the night out of myself. The whole of it—the engines, the council, the temptation, the words themselves—everything that made up my knowledge of how to wake the weapons. If I did not remember the words, I could not speak them. No heir after me could learn them from me. I paid a glasswright to take it, and I lived thirty years with a hole in me shaped like the end of the world."

"And the hole is killing you."

"A hole that size doesn't close." She said it simply, the way one reports weather. "A mind grows scar tissue around a wound like that, or it bleeds. Mine has bled for thirty years, slowly. Now the memory is back inside me, raw and unstitched, fighting the scar, and I am forgetting everything else to make room for it. In a month I will be nothing but that night. In two, I will be nothing at all." She leaned forward. "But hear the rest, taster, because this is the part that matters."

She reached out and, at last, touched the vial. It did not hum for her. It hummed only for the gifted; Petra could feel its note through the table, faint and awful, like a struck bell heard through a wall.

"If that memory is poured back into me whole—as your guildmaster intends, as Aldery intends—it will not only kill me faster. It will make me *whole*, and a whole queen remembers the words. The council's hawks are counting on it. They will hold the cup to my lips themselves if they must, and I will be too far gone to refuse, and I will stand on the balcony of this tower and speak the release, and the glass flats will open their mouths, and every gifted soul in these kingdoms will forget their own names by morning. Then the pyres will need no registers at all. There will be no one left to burn who remembers being anything else." Her golden eyes held his. "That is the trap your guildmaster has been building all these years. Not leverage. Not a market. An *ending*. Sold piecemeal to buyers who each thought they were purchasing advantage, assembled at last into a knife pointed at my own throat and at yours."

Petra's mind raced ahead of him, traitorous and quick. "Then I return it differently. Carefully. Piece by piece—I've read how the old extractions worked, I can reverse the pour, give it back slow enough that the scar holds, slow enough that the words never—"

"No."

The word fell like a door closing.

"Your Majesty, if I destroy it, the hole stays. You die anyway, just—"

"Slower. Yes. Perhaps. Perhaps not; the physicians disagree and I have stopped listening to them." She withdrew her hand from the vial. "But listen to what you're proposing, boy. To return it safely, you must hold the whole of it in yourself first—you must drink the end of the world and carry it across this city in your own skull, past Aldery's men and Corvane's collectors, while it eats your childhood to make room. I know what the gift costs. My brother paid it. I will not spend a Voss twice." For the first time her voice shook. "Maren gave his memories to save strangers. Your mother gave hers to the fire rather than let the guild have them. I will not add you to that arithmetic. Destroy the vial. Let the words die in the glass. Whatever happens to me happens to a woman who has already lived past every year she expected."

"And if the hawks force the pour before I can?"

"Then I will bite through my own tongue before I swallow," she said, "and I would thank you not to make it necessary."

* * *

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The candle guttered, righted itself. Petra looked at the vial—at the impossible knot stamped in the wax—and felt the full weight of the choice settle onto him like a coat of mail.

Two queens' wishes, he thought. The queen who had cut the night out of herself to save the gifted, asking him now to finish the work, to let the words die forever even if it cost her the rest of her reign, her mind, her life. And somewhere across the city, Corvane with his cold bookkeeping, offering partnership, offering survival-as-asset, offering the same memory as leverage and calling it mercy. And between those two wishes, one vial, humming like a struck bell, and one taster whose palate could read every grief in the capital but somehow not his own future.

"There is a third thing," he said slowly. "Not return, not destroy. The memory could be given back to the *land*. The engines were keyed to your blood—but the workings themselves live in the flats. If the memory were returned to what it belongs to—"

"It would arm them," she snapped. Then stopped. Then, quieter: "Wouldn't it."

"Maybe. Maybe not. I don't know. I'm not a glasswright. But I know someone who copied palace accounts for a buyer she won't name, and I know a vault beneath the steppe where the containment rig stands, and I know that Corvane expects me to choose between his arithmetic and yours by week's end." He met her eyes. "Give me until then, Your Majesty. Don't ask me to promise destruction. Ask me to promise I won't let the words be spoken. Those I can swear on my mother's ashes."

The queen studied him for a long moment, and something in her face shifted—not softening, exactly. Recognition. The look of a woman seeing, across forty years, a pair of hands she had grieved.

"Maren argued with me for six hours," she said at last. "You bargain in one. The blood improves." She rose, and took the vial from the table, and held it out to him, and the bell-note of it sang up his arm and settled behind his teeth like a word waiting to be spoken. "One hour is all the lucidity I can buy, and mine is nearly spent. Take it. Hide it where even Corvane's collectors won't think to look. And taster—" her voice was fraying now at the edges, the fog rising in her again, visible as a tide, "—when you decide, decide as a man who has tasted what the engines do, and not as a man who wants to live. It is the only advice I have left in me."

"Your Majesty—"

But the golden light was draining out of her eyes even as he watched, the straight spine softening, the general folding back into the fading woman. She blinked at him, and tilted her head, and smiled with gentle, terrible confusion.

"Oh," she said. "You have Maren's hands. Has anyone told you that? You have Maren's hands exactly. Is he coming tonight? He promised he'd come. He always comes, on the bad nights."

Petra took the vial with steady fingers, though nothing in him was steady, and bowed over it like a man bowing over a grave.

"He's coming, Majesty," he heard himself say. "Rest now. I'll mind the door."

He let himself out past the green-glass lamps, down the counting stairs, past the sleeping guards, out into the cold air of the palace gardens with the end of the world singing softly against his palm. And it was only there, in the shadow of the hedge, that he saw the light burning in a ground-floor window of the chancellery—and the silhouette within, pacing, waiting, awake at this same dead hour—and understood that somewhere between the tower and the gate, someone had marked his visit, and the hour he had just bought for the queen had already begun to run out.

Behind him, faint through the stone, the vial hummed. Ahead, in the dark, a bell began to ring the alarm for the Hunt's next sweep.

He ran.
