## 15. Sefa's Testimony

The summons came at the hour when the kitchens went quiet, between the last of the supper service and the first of the night's baking—a silence Sefa had learned to love, and now learned to dread.

A page in Aldery's livery stood in the doorway of her narrow room above the scullery, holding a folded card of grey vellum. He didn't announce himself. He didn't need to. The livery said everything: silver thread on black, the Chancellor's own colors, worn by no one who ever carried good news.

"Lord Aldery requests your presence," the page said. "Tonight. His study."

Sefa wiped her hands on her apron, a habit, though her hands had been dry for an hour already. She'd known this was coming since the morning she'd seen the second copy of the palace accounts go missing from her lockbox—missing, not stolen, because nothing in Aldery's household was ever stolen, only *retrieved*. She'd known since she found the lockbox's hasp re-hung with a craftsman's precision, since she'd counted her copied pages twice and come up one short. One page. The page that mattered: the disbursement record showing funds moved from the Chancellor's discretionary purse to a Glasswright bottler three years ago, the same season the queen had begun to fade.

They hadn't taken all of it. They'd taken exactly enough to tell her they could.

"I'll come now," she said.

The page hesitated. "He said to say—" and here the boy's voice dropped, embarrassed by his own errand, "—he said to say you may bring your coat. It will be cold where you're going."

*Where you're going.* Not *the walk is cold.* She heard the difference. She'd grown up hearing the difference, in a house where her mother read fortunes in the dregs of tea cups and taught her daughter to listen for the sentence under the sentence.

She put on her coat.

* * *

Aldery's study smelled of cedar oil and old paper, and underneath both, faint as a stain, of glass. Sefa had never been inside before. She'd served at his table a hundred times without ever seeing past the dining room—the Chancellor ate in state, publicly, every course witnessed, which was precisely why Petra's discovery had mattered so much. A man who lived his life in public kept his sins somewhere else.

Now she saw where.

The study was modest by a great lord's standards, almost deliberately so. A desk, two chairs, shelves of ledgers bound in sober leather. A fire burned low. On the desk, arranged with the neatness of a man who believed order was a form of virtue, lay three things: her missing page. A writ of immunity, signed and sealed. And a pair of iron manacles, small ones, wrist-sized, polished bright.

He gestured her to the far chair, the one that faced the fire, and took the other himself.

"Sefa Undara," he said, reading her name from a card rather than from memory. "Undercook, fourth rank. Nine years' service. No family listed. No guild affiliations." He looked up, and his eyes were pale and patient, the eyes of a man who had never once needed to raise his voice. "And yet for the past several months, copies of the crown's internal accounts have been leaving this palace in the lining of a flour delivery girl's satchel."

The fire popped. Sefa kept her hands still in her lap, though every nerve in them wanted to move.

"I don't know what Your Lordship means."

"No," Aldery agreed mildly. "You wouldn't. That's what I like about you, actually. You're careful. You don't lie elaborately; you simply decline to confirm. It's a rare discipline in a servant." He turned the missing page toward her, face down, like a player refusing to show his hand even to himself. "Here is how tonight will proceed. You will answer some questions. Depending on your answers, you will leave this room either carrying that—" he touched the writ, "—or wearing those. The manacles are Gift-Hunt issue. I borrowed them. I want you to look at them while we talk."

She looked at them. Bright iron. Someone had oiled them recently. Recently enough that the oil smell was part of what she'd taken for glass.

"First question. Who gave you access to the sealed accounts?"

"The accounts aren't sealed to kitchen staff," she said. "I reconcile provisions against expenditure. That's my work. Anyone in the comptroller's office would tell you—"

"Second question." He let the interruption sit there, unhurried, a stone placed on a pile. "Who is Petra Voss to you?"

The name landed in her chest like a swallowed coal. She thought of him in her rooms three nights ago, his hands wrapped around a bowl of barley stew, his face undone by the taste of it—by his own childhood rising up out of the broth—and the way he'd said *you*, afterward, like the word was new to his mouth. She thought: whatever I say in the next ten minutes decides whether he burns.

"Nothing," she said. "He tastes the queen's food. I cook some of it. We are colleagues."

"Mm." Aldery rose, crossed to the window, stood with his back half-turned. Outside, the city lay dark except for the watch-fires, and beyond the walls, faintly visible on clear nights like this one, the glass steppe caught the moonlight and threw it back flat and white, like the eye of something sleeping. "Let me tell you a story, Sefa Undara, and you can tell me if it sounds familiar.

"Three years ago, a certain faction within this court—which I will neither confirm nor deny includes myself—acquired, through intermediaries, a bottled memory of considerable value. The memory of a war. The last night of it. What the old gifted kings did with their weapons, and what it cost to stop them. This faction intended to keep that memory as insurance. Leverage. There are people in this city, right now, who carry the old blood and hide it, and this court has been too soft on them for too long. A queen who remembers what the gifted did would understand that. Would act."

He turned back to her.

"But the memory was never used, because the buyer grew frightened of it, or covetous of it—I've never established which—and instead of unleashing it where intended, poured it into the queen herself, where it has been killing her by inches ever since. A beautiful irony, isn't it? The weapon meant to harden the throne against the gifted instead hollows out the woman sitting on it."

"You're confessing," Sefa said quietly. "To treason."

Aldery smiled. It was the worst thing she'd seen all night, because it was gentle. "I'm establishing context. Here is the third question, and it is the only one that matters. The royal taster, Voss—he is gifted. He reads intent through taste. I have evidence. I have witnesses. And in a matter of days, when the queen dies or fades past recall, this faction intends to move: declare regency, purge the gift-bearers from the capital root and branch, and burn the Guild of Impossible Things to its foundations—for optics, you understand; we'll need someone visibly punished. All of that proceeds regardless of anything you say tonight.

"The only variable is you." He came back to the desk and laid his hand flat on the writ. "Testify that you saw Voss use the gift. Testify that he recruited you, that he coerced you into copying accounts for the guild, that you feared him. Do that, and this writ makes you untouchable. New name if you want it. Passage anywhere in the nine provinces. You're young. You're clever. Don't spend your life paying for a man who would not cross the street to save you."

"He would, though," Sefa said.

It was out before she could weigh it, and she watched Aldery register it—not with triumph, exactly, but with the quiet satisfaction of a fisherman feeling the line go taut.

"So there is something between you."

"There's barley stew," Sefa said. "That's all Your Lordship has proven."

For a long moment the only sound was the fire. Then Aldery sat, folded his hands, and became, impossibly, even more reasonable.

"I want you to understand your position clearly, because I respect you and I dislike waste. If you refuse to testify, you will be arrested tomorrow on charges of sedition and theft of crown documents. The documents themselves guarantee conviction. Under interrogation—and the Hunt's interrogators have methods that do not require the victim to be gifted—you will testify anyway. Everyone does. The only difference is what condition you're in when you do, and whether your testimony is worth anything afterward. Whereas if you speak freely, tonight, of your own accord—" he tapped the writ once, "—then everything after is theater. A brief trial. A tearful confession. Pardon. Freedom."

"And Petra?"

"Petra Voss is a dead man walking either way. Your testimony changes nothing for him. It only changes something for you." Aldery's voice softened by a degree, and somehow that degree was crueler than any threat. "You cannot save him, child. But you can save yourself. In my experience, when people refuse that trade, it's rarely because they hope to rescue the other person. It's vanity. They'd rather be martyrs than survivors. Martyrdom is just pride with better lighting."

Sefa looked at the manacles again. At the writ. At her own page lying face-down on the desk like a corpse covered with a sheet.

Her mother had told fortunes in tea leaves, and the one thing she'd said over and over, the lesson beneath all the lessons, was this: *every reading is a choice. The leaves don't tell you what will happen. They show you what you're willing to see.*

Aldery wanted her willing to see survival. He'd built the whole room to make survival the only legible shape.

So she made herself see something else.

"Your Lordship is right about one thing," Sefa said. "I did copy the accounts. Months of them. Every disbursement, every transfer, every payment routed through shell merchants and false provision orders—including the payments to the Glasswright bottler, including the sum that matches, to the copper, the price Corvane's guild charges for a high-order memory extraction. I know exactly what this faction bought, and when, and out of whose purse."

Aldery's patience flickered—the first true expression she'd seen on him. "Careful."

"And I gave copies to someone safe," she went on, and her heart was hammering but her voice came out level, almost conversational, the voice she used calling timings across a hot kitchen. "Not hidden in a flour satchel. Not in this palace at all. So burning me gets you nothing, and arresting me gets you a trial—at which I will be asked, under oath, before witnesses, what I found in the crown's accounts. And I will answer. Fully. With dates."

The silence stretched. Somewhere in the corridor a clock chimed the third hour.

"You would hang," Aldery said at last, "to inconvenience me."

"No." Sefa stood. Her legs felt strange, light, as if her body had already left ahead of her. "I'd hang to make sure the truth comes out of my mouth and no one else's. You taught me that tonight, actually. You said testimony under interrogation isn't worth anything. You're right. So here's mine freely given: Lord Chancellor Aldery conspired to purchase and deploy a weaponized memory against the reigning queen of this realm. I have the paper trail. I have the names. And I am going to walk out of this room, and every step I take outside your door, you have to ask yourself whether today is the day I give them to someone who'll use them."

She picked up the writ. He didn't stop her—couldn't, quite, and they both knew it; the arithmetic had shifted too far for force. She held it over the candle flame on his desk and watched the seal blister and blacken.

"I don't want immunity bought with Petra's blood," she said. "Keep the manacles. I have a feeling I'll be seeing them anyway."

Then she walked out, past the page in the corridor who stared at her like a boy watching someone survive a fall, down the stairs, out into the freezing dark, and she did not begin to shake until she reached the well at the corner of the kitchen yard, where she stopped, gripped the icy iron rim, and shook.

* * *

Petra opened the door before she finished knocking.

He must have been listening for her—had been, maybe, every night since the dinner at Aldery's table, since the trap-courses and the performed ignorance and the walk home together through streets gone silent with fear. He looked at her face and stepped aside to let her in, and only then, in the warmth of her single room with its banked stove and its row of spice jars on the sill, did the shaking come back, worse, until she was sitting on her own bed with her teeth chattering and his coat around her shoulders because hers had been soaked through by the walk.

"They offered me a writ," she said. "Immunity, new name, passage out. All I had to do was swear I saw you use the gift."

"What did you tell them?"

"I told them I'd testify." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "Just not for them. Petra, I burned the writ in front of him. I threatened a Lord Chancellor of the realm in his own study. I told him I had copies of everything, and—" her voice caught, steadied. "That part wasn't even true. Not all of it. I have most of it. The disbursements, the bottler's payment, the routing through the provision houses. But I never got the final link. The document that ties the money to the memory itself. Without that, it's corruption, not treason, and corruption he can survive."

Petra crouched in front of her, and she watched him decide not to touch her, and then touch her anyway—one hand closing over both of hers, warm, deliberate, as if signing something.

"Corvane has the final link," he said quietly. "The guild keeps counterpart records. Every sale logged twice—once in the vault ledger, once in the vial's own seal-mark. Corvane showed me the vault, Sefa. Beneath the steppe. The queen's memory is still there. I've *touched it*." His jaw tightened. "He thinks he owns me. He thinks I'll drink it and become his weapon against the crown, or die refusing. Either way the guild wins."

"Then we take the records."

"We?" 

"You were going to say *I*," Sefa said. "I watched you do it just now. You do it constantly. You carry things alone and call it protection, and it's the most insulting thing anyone's ever done to me." She pulled her hands free—not to push him away, but to reach past him, to the loose brick behind the spice jars where she kept what mattered, and draw out the oilcloth bundle she'd prepared weeks ago, in the nights after she'd understood how deep she was in. She unwrapped it on the blanket between them: copied pages, months of them, each dated and initialed in her own cramped hand, plus two keys, a route map of the guild quarter drawn from memory, and a small tin of salt.

"Provision girls get everywhere," she said. "Even across the steppe, if the provision is desperate enough. You're not going to that vault alone, Petra Voss. And if you try, I'll follow you, and I'll be angrier about it."

He looked at the spread of papers, then at her, and something in his face broke open—the wary competence finally cracking to show what was under it. "Do you understand what you've done tonight? Aldery won't forget. When this ends—whatever way it ends—you're marked. Publicly. Permanently. There's no version of this where you go back to your kitchen."

"I know." She found she was smiling, and couldn't help it, and didn't particularly want to. "I've been marked since the day I started copying his accounts. Tonight I just made it official." She took his hand again, properly this time. "There's no version where I stand in that kitchen stirring his soup and wait to hear whether you died on the flats. That's not a life. That's a slow testimony against myself. I'd rather be punishable with you than safe without you."

Outside, a bell began to ring—not the hour bell but the watch-bell, urgent, arrhythmic, and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, voices. Many voices. Moving toward the river district.

Petra went to the window, eased the shutter open a finger's width, and went very still.

"Fires," he said. "On the guild street. Sefa—the Hunt is moving tonight. Not in days. *Tonight.*" He turned from the window, and the firelight from the stove caught his eyes, and she saw the fear in them transmute, in real time, into the thing she'd chosen instead of safety. "Corvane will cut losses. He'll burn his own ledgers and vanish into the steppe, and the queen's memory goes with him, and everyone we've burned ourselves for becomes ash with no proof."

"Then we go now," Sefa said, already pulling on her coat, already reaching for the bundle, "before the whole city is smoke."

They went out together into the ringing dark, toward the fires.
