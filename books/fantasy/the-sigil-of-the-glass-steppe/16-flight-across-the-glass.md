## 16. Flight Across the Glass

We left through the tannery gate at the hour when even beggars sleep, because it was the only hour in which neither the Hunt nor the Guild could be trusted to be elsewhere.

Sefa carried the accounts wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, strapped down like a wound she was afraid to look at. I carried two things: the queen's ring, pressed into my palm by a woman who had one clear hour left in her and had spent it asking me to destroy rather than restore, and a skin of water that tasted of copper pipes and old fear. Between us we carried almost nothing else. Fugitives learn quickly that everything weighs.

The city ended at the Salt Gate, and beyond the Salt Gate began the glass steppe — forty miles of fused sand where some ancient catastrophe had melted the earth into a single pane, polished by centuries of wind until it mirrored the sky so faithfully that travelers spoke of walking on clouds and meant nothing poetic by it. The guild's true vault lay beneath those flats, out where no magistrate's writ reached and no honest person had reason to go.

Corvane would know we were coming. Corvane had known before we did; I was certain of it. He had let us run the way a man lets wine breathe.

But knowing a thing and stopping it are different trades, and for once I intended to beat him to his own ledger.

* * *

By dawn the city was a smudge behind us, and the world had turned upside down.

That is the only way I can describe the glass steppe to someone who has not crossed it: you walk beneath your own feet. The clouds passed under our boots as often as over our heads. The sun rose twice — once in the east above the horizon and once in the west below it, its reflection climbing toward us like a second, colder dawn. Sefa kept her eyes fixed on my shoulders and admitted, around midmorning, that looking down made her feel like a fly on a window.

"Then don't look down," I said.

"Easy for you." She said it lightly, but there was an edge under it. "You taste the whole world anyway."

I laughed, and the laugh came out strange in all that open air, with nowhere to hide. The steppe swallowed sound the way it swallowed everything else — gave it back flat and far away, as if someone else had laughed, somewhere else, a long time ago.

We walked on the old road markers, brass studs set into the glass every half-mile by whoever had needed to cross this place before the guild claimed it. They were warm from the sun and they hummed faintly if you touched them, and I tried not to think about why anything buried under forty miles of mirror would hum.

It was at the third marker that I first tasted pursuit.

Not smelled — tasted. The wind off the steppe came to me carrying a flavor I knew too well: cold iron and burnt sugar, the signature of guild collectors, men who had been drinking bottled nerve for years until it seeped into their sweat. Three of them, maybe four, a mile back and closing. I spat, though there was nothing in my mouth to spit.

"How far?" Sefa asked. She had learned to read my face the way I read flavors — quickly, and with growing dread.

"An hour. Maybe less. They're not hurrying." I looked west, where the horizon bent around the vault's location like light around something dense. "They don't need to hurry. This is their country."

"Whose country?" she demanded. "You told me the vault was a secret."

"It is a secret the way a knife in the dark is a secret. It works until it doesn't." I took her arm and turned us off the marker line, out onto open glass where our reflections ran beside us like drowned twins. "There are fissures out here. Places where the pane cracked in the old wars and healed wrong. If we can reach the Lattice —"

"The what?"

"You'll know it when you see it. Everyone does. It's the one place on this steppe where the glass remembers being angry."

She didn't ask more questions. That was one of the thousand things I loved about her and did not yet have the courage to say: Sefa asked exactly as many questions as survival required, and not one more.

* * *

They caught sight of us at noon.

I heard it first — the particular ring of boots on glass carrying across the flats, multiplied by the mirror into the sound of a regiment. When I turned, there they were: four figures walking in perfect interval, dark coats drinking the sunlight, and above each of them their reflections hanging inverted in the sky like hanged men.

"Run," I said, and we ran.

Running on glass is its own education. Every footfall rings. Every shadow is doubled. There is no cover, no rise, no fold of land — the steppe is honest in the way of executioners. We ran anyway, lungs burning, the vault's distant bend pulling us west, and behind us the collectors did not run. They walked. Fast, unhurried, the walk of men being paid by the day and certain of the outcome.

The first bolt came without warning — a crossbow quarrel that struck the glass ten feet ahead of us and shattered into a spray of glittering shrapnel that sang as it flew. The second took the heel off Sefa's boot. She staggered, caught herself, swore in kitchen dialect with a fluency that would have made me smile on any other day.

"They're herding us!" I shouted. "West — they want us at the fissures —"

"Why?"

"Because at the fissures, they only have to guard the doors!"

The Lattice rose out of the mirror ahead of us like a fever dream: a forest of broken glass, acres of shards thrust up from the steppe at every angle, some thin as needles and tall as gallows, all of them ringing in the wind with a sound like a thousand struck bells gone sour. It was where the old war's final weapon had struck the earth and failed, and the glass had never forgiven it. Light went into the Lattice and came out wrong — fractured into colors that hurt, colors with no names, colors I could *taste* from a quarter-mile away, bitter and electric.

And somewhere beneath it, down shafts the guild had cut with tools that shouldn't exist, sat the vial that hummed like a struck bell. The queen's stolen night. The war's last word.

We dove into the shards as the third bolt sang past my ear.

* * *

Inside the Lattice the world became a hall of knives.

Every shard threw our images back at us in fragments — Sefa's eyes here, my hands there, a hundred partial selves fleeing between the pillars. The collectors' boots rang outside, patient, circling. I pulled Sefa deep into the thicket, following a memory I hadn't known I carried: my mother's voice, years dead, saying *the glass remembers paths, Petra, walk where it sings low.*

Where it sings low. I put my palm against the shards as we moved, tasting each one's vibration, reading the steppe the only way a gifted tongue can read anything. Most of the glass shrieked. But here and there a pillar hummed a dull, reluctant note, and along that hidden chord we threaded deeper, while outside the circle tightened.

Then a bolt came through the Lattice itself, threading the gaps with impossible precision, and I understood that at least one of them was gifted too. A finder. Corvane always kept finders.

"Sefa — down—"

She didn't go down. She went *up*, onto her toes, into the path of the second bolt, and I watched it happen the way you watch a cup tip — in terrible slow detail. It took her high in the chest, below the collarbone, punching through the shoulder of her coat, and she made a sound I will hear for the rest of my life however long or short the rest of it is, and she fell against me, and her blood was on my hands and on the glass, and the glass drank it greedily, every drop, ringing softly as it fed.

"No," I said. It wasn't a word. It was just the shape my mouth made around the end of the world.

"I'm fine." Her voice was white and thin. "I'm fine, it's — shoulder, it's the shoulder, keep *moving*, you idiot—"

I got my arm around her and we moved. Behind us the collectors entered the Lattice, and the whole forest of glass began to scream with their passage, and I did the arithmetic I had sworn never to do: I could not outrun four hunters carrying a wounded woman. I could not hide; the finder would taste us out. I could not fight; I had never held anything sharper than a paring knife with intent.

But I could taste.

I stopped. Set Sefa against a great leaning shard as gently as I could. Tore my sleeve, packed the wound — the bolt had gone through, thank whatever gods listen to liars, clean through above the lung — and then I stood up straight in the open aisle between the pillars and I waited.

He came around the corner of glass expecting a cowering fugitive and found a food-taster standing with his head tilted, like a man sampling a vintage.

"You're the finder," I said. "Iron and burnt sugar. You've been drinking bottled steadiness for six years, maybe seven. Old stock. It's turning bitter in you — you can't sleep, can you? You wake at the third bell every night with your heart going like a drum."

His crossbow wavered. Gifted men are never more frightened than when someone reads them aloud.

"And you," I went on, stepping closer, letting my voice carry the way flavor carries, filling the whole ringing corridor of glass, "your handler cuts your rations when you miss. I can taste the hunger on you. Corvane pays in product and punishes in withdrawal, and you've told yourself it's loyalty because the alternative is admitting you're owned. Well. Look at the flats, friend. Look at who walks free and who walks in circles."

The other three were converging; I heard them. So did he. And in that heartbeat of hesitation — that single human heartbeat — I grabbed the nearest humming shard and snapped it.

The Lattice screamed.

All of it, every pillar for acres, a chord of pure outrage rolling outward through the glass and through the boots of every man standing on it, a sound that went through the teeth and the bones and the bottled steadiness in the collectors' veins and shook it apart. Two of them dropped. The finder clapped his hands over his ears and fired wide, his bolt shattering a pillar into a bloom of glitter. The fourth simply turned and ran, and I did not blame him; I wanted to run too, and it was *my* plan.

I gathered Sefa up. She weighed nothing and everything.

"That," she breathed against my neck, "was the stupidest — bravest —"

"Hush. Save the insults. I'll want them later."

* * *

We came down off the glass as the sun died, descending a spiral stair cut into the steppe's underside, into air that tasted of stone and patience. The vault door stood ahead of us — plain bronze, unguarded, unbothered, exactly as arrogant as its owner.

Sefa was gray-lipped and shivering by then, propped against the wall with my coat balled under her wound, refusing with her whole stubborn body to be dying. I knelt and pressed my brow to hers, and for a moment neither of us said anything, because everything worth saying was too large for the space left in the day.

"The accounts," I said. "If I don't come back up—"

"You're coming back up." She gripped my wrist with her good hand, hard enough to leave marks I hoped to keep. "Petra Voss, you listen to me. Whatever's down there, whatever he offers you — remember that you are not a ledger entry. You are not a price. Come back up, and we will burn every book he owns."

I kissed her then, salt and blood and kitchen smoke, the best thing I had ever tasted, and I let myself believe — one full, reckless breath — that I would taste it again.

Then I opened the bronze door, and the humming swelled up from the dark below like a struck bell that had been waiting forty miles and twenty years for someone to answer it.

And Corvane's voice came floating up the stairwell, warm as a host greeting guests:

"Punctual. I do admire that in an asset."
