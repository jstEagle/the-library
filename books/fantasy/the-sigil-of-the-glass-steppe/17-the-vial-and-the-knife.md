## 17. The Vial and the Knife

The vault beneath the glass steppe was colder than any winter Petra had ever known, and it breathed.

That was the only word for it. The air moved in long slow cycles, in and out, as though the whole chamber were the interior of some vast sleeping animal, and every breath carried a taste he could not stop cataloguing even now, even with Sefa's blood drying on his sleeve: ozone and old iron, tallow smoke and burnt hair, and beneath it all something sweet and wrong, like honey left too long in a copper pot. The taste of memory held too many years past its decanting.

Corvane's lamps lit the vault in tiers. Below them, shelves of glass descended into the dark in concentric rings, thousands upon thousands of vials racked in salt-crusted wooden cradles, each one labeled in the guild's cipher. Petra had kept ledgers for this place for eleven years. He had recorded trades here that would have curdled a bishop's blood. And still he had never stood at the center of it, at the heart of the rings, where a single pedestal of black glass rose from the floor like a tooth.

On the pedestal sat a containment rig — a cage of silver wire and blown-glass prisms, guild-work older than the city above — and inside it, a vial no longer than his thumb.

It hummed.

He had heard it from thirty paces. He could hear it through his teeth, through the bones of his skull, a tone like a struck bell that never finished ringing. The queen's stolen memory. Thirty years of her life bottled and sold and traded back and forth across the capital until it came to rest here, in the dark, under a mile of glass flats that rang faintly with their own buried light when you walked on them.

"Beautiful, isn't she," Corvane said.

He stood on the far side of the pedestal, and for once he did not look like a bookkeeper. He had shed the ledger-room cardigan somewhere between the city and the steppe; now he wore a long coat the color of wet slate, and his hands — those patient, ink-stained hands that had taught Petra to write in cipher when he was nineteen — rested openly at his sides. Behind him, filling the stairwell mouth, four collectors stood in a row. They did not carry knives. They carried hooks, long-shafted, glass-tipped, the tools used to draw memory out of the unwilling. Petra had inventoried them often enough to know exactly how they worked.

Sefa's hand found his in the dark. Her palm was slick with blood — her own, from the shard that had opened her forearm half a mile back, when a collector's hook had grazed her instead of him. She had not made a sound. She had not stopped running. He pressed her fingers once, briefly, and let go. There wasn't time for what he wanted to say, and there never had been.

"You killed Tamas," Petra said. "You burned the bottling house. You gave Aldery's faction everything they needed to start the Hunt."

"I sold nothing I didn't already own," Corvane said mildly. "And Tamas refused to register, which was his choice, made loudly, at the wrong moment." He turned the lamp so its light fell full on the vial, and the humming deepened, and every vial in the outer rings shivered in its cradle like a congregation leaning toward a sermon. "Do you know what this is worth, Petra? Not in coin. In years. This is the last night of the War of Gifts, held in suspension. This is the moment the gifted broke the world and the throne broke the gifted. Every faction in the capital has bled for a look at it. Aldery wants to release it and watch the old terror justify the new pyres. The queen—" he smiled, thin as a ledger line, "—the queen wants it destroyed, though I doubt she told you that herself. You've been speaking to her, haven't you? One hour of lucidity. Sentimental of her."

Petra said nothing. His tongue lay flat and careful against his teeth, the way it did at state banquets when the dish in front of him might kill a king.

"Here is what I want," Corvane went on. "I want neither war nor ash. I want leverage. A gift-bearer who can hold this memory — drink it whole and keep it, keep *it*, not be kept by it — becomes the most valuable person alive. The crown cannot burn us if we hold its oldest sin behind our teeth. The factions cannot purge us without losing the proof that purges are lies. Drink it, Petra. Wield it. Be our vessel, our voice, our—"

"Our hostage," Sefa said.

Corvane looked at her the way he looked at a misfiled entry. "The cook speaks."

"The cook reads accounts," Sefa said. Her voice was steady, but Petra heard the tremor under it, the note of someone standing upright through pure refusal to fall. "The cook knows what your collectors pay per vial. The cook knows you've been selling the same widow's wedding day three times over because dead men don't audit. And the cook knows," she stepped forward, putting herself half between Petra and the hooks, "that whatever you're offering him, you're offering it the way you offered Tamas a place in the guild forever."

"Tamas is in the guild forever," Corvane said. "Ash keeps."

The words went into Petra like a knife going into a joint — precisely, expertly, finding the space between bones. Grief curdling into hatred, he had thought, weeks ago, standing over the register with its burned page. But hatred was too clean a taste for what filled his mouth now. This was something more like the flavor of the vault itself: sweet gone wrong, honey in copper, love for a man who had raised him rotting slowly into something he would have to spit out or swallow forever.

"And if I refuse?" Petra asked.

"Then we take it from you the way we take everything," Corvane said simply. "The hooks work faster than you'd think. And then Sefa watches, because grief makes the extraction cleaner — the gift runs deeper when the vessel has something left to lose. I'm sorry about that part. I did try to arrange your education without attachments."

*Curiosity is a debt,* Corvane had told him, ten years ago, over the widow's wedding day. *Everything you learn costs something, boy. Make sure you're the one who sets the price.*

Petra set the price now.

"Yes," he said.

Sefa's head turned. He felt her stare like heat on his cheek, and he did not meet it. He couldn't. If he met it, his face would tell her everything, and her face would tell Corvane.

"Say it plainly," Corvane said. Suspicion lived in the man like marrow lives in bone; it had kept him alive through three purges. "Say you'll drink it, and say why."

Petra made himself look at the vial. Made himself want it — that was the trick of it, wanting it visibly enough to read true, and the terrible thing was how little acting it took. The memory called to him. It had been calling since chapter and verse of his childhood, since the first time he tasted another person's grief in a spoonful of soup and understood what he was. Thirty years of the queen. The last night of the war. Power held behind the teeth, exactly as promised.

"I'll drink it," he said, "because the Hunt won't stop until someone holds something worse than fire. You taught me that. Assets, not people — that was your arithmetic. Fine. Make me the asset."

Something eased in Corvane's shoulders. It was small, fractional, the loosening of a man who had just closed a trade he'd been afraid to lose — and it was enough. It was all Petra needed.

"And her?"

"She stays," Petra said. "She's mine. My witness, my keeper, my—" he let the pause do the work, let Corvane fill it with the contemptuous word he chose, "*liability*. If I go into the bottle, someone has to pull me out. You don't trust your collectors with that."

"No," Corvane agreed. "I don't." He gestured, and two of the collectors unhooked a chain from the rig's base and stepped back from the pedestal. "Come forward, then. Both hands. The rig requires a bearer to open it — that's the design, that's the safety. Two hands on the frame, and the prisms will yield to a living palate. Touch it with anything else and it seals for a century."

*I know,* Petra almost said. *I wrote the inventory entry.*

He crossed the ring of shelving. Each step rang on the glass floor, a small struck bell answering the great one, and the hum climbed in his skull until his back teeth ached. Sefa stayed where she was — she had to, there was a collector behind her now, courteous as an usher — but her eyes were on him, and in them he saw what he had seen in her kitchen doorway the first night: a woman who noticed him noticing, and had decided, against all sense, to notice anyway.

*I'm sorry,* he thought at her, with everything he had, the way he had once tasted a message straight into a dying friend's last cup of wine. *Watch my hands.*

He laid both palms on the containment rig.

The silver wire was warm. That was the first shock — after all that cold, warmth, body-temperature, blood-temperature, as if the rig itself were alive and waiting to be held. The prisms caught the lamplight and threw it in fragments across his knuckles. Inside, the vial turned slowly, though nothing stirred the air, and its hum passed up through the glass into his fingerbones and settled behind his sternum like a second heartbeat.

*Two hands on the frame.* True. What Corvane had not said — what perhaps only the guild's dead founders knew, and one clerk who had spent eleven years reading inventories instead of skimming them — was that the frame was tuned to a living palate. Tuned to *taste*. The prisms yielded to what they recognized.

And Petra Voss had been tasting this memory's signature for weeks. Through the queen's soup, through Aldery's cup, through the trail he had followed across the palace like a scent-hound. The vial knew him. The vial had been singing to him in a key only his tongue could hear.

He let his palate open. Let it reach, gentle as a thief, through the wire and the glass, and touch the memory inside.

The rig shivered.

"Oh," Corvane said softly, and the softness was worse than shouting, because it meant he understood. "Oh, you clever, stupid boy—"

Petra squeezed the frame and pulled apart.

The prisms did not shatter so much as *open*, petals of blown glass unfolding along seams that had waited a hundred years for a hand that spoke their language, and the vial rose free into the air between his palms — and then the world cracked.

Not the vial. The vial held. It was everything else that broke: the hum climbing past hearing into feeling, the racks in the outer rings shivering so hard that vials began to leap their cradles and burst on the glass floor in bright blooms of other people's lives, a wedding day here, a drowned sailor's last sunset there, decades of bottled selves flooding the cold air with flavors that hit Petra's open palate all at once —

— and the queen's memory, freed of its cage, poured upward like smoke finding the chimney, like water finding the crack, like everything in the world finding the one vessel shaped to receive it.

Him.

"*No!*" Sefa's voice, raw, tearing across the vault.

He saw her lunging past the collector. He saw Corvane reaching out with both hands, not to catch the vial but to catch *him*, to seize the vessel before it filled — and he saw, with the strange clarity of the final instant, that he had perhaps three seconds to choose, and that the choosing had already happened somewhere below thought, in the same instinct that had made him a taster: *when the dish is poisoned, you swallow first, so that no one else dies of it.*

His hands closed around the vial.

The glass was hot now, furnace-hot, and it did not burn him, because it was already becoming him, because the boundary between vessel and contents is a polite fiction that only holds while nobody drinks — and he drank. Not by choice. By gravity. The memory fell into him the way the sea falls into a broken harbor wall, and the last thing he registered with his fading surface-mind, his small remaining self, was Sefa's face an arm's length away, screaming his name, and Corvane's hand closing on empty air.

Then Petra Voss went under.

* * *

He stood on a field under a sky the color of a bruise, and the grass was glass.

It stretched to every horizon, mile upon mile of it, ringing faintly under a wind that had no source, and everywhere the glass held shapes — figures frozen mid-stride, mid-scream, mid-reach — soldiers and gift-bearers and horses and children, all of them transmuted in a single instant, all of them still there, still waiting, thirty years deep. He knew this place. He had walked its edges above ground all his life. The glass steppe was not a landscape. It was a wound, and he was standing inside the moment it was made.

Above him the sky tore, and the queen — young then, dark-haired, her face streaked with ash and something darker — raised her hands, and the tearing answered her.

*This is what I stole from myself,* said a voice that was hers and also his own, coming from everywhere, from the hum in his teeth. *This is the night I put in a bottle and sold to strangers because I could not carry it. Watch. You have to watch, if you're going to hold it.*

And Petra, who had spent his whole life tasting what others could not bear to swallow, opened himself and began, sip by sip by endless sip, to drink down a war.
