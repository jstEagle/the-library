## 18. The War Inside

The vial broke and the world poured in.

Not as light. Not as fire. It came as taste—the first and oldest of his senses—and Petra Voss drowned in flavor the way other men drowned in water.

Salt first. Then iron. Then something beneath both, older than either: a bitterness like scorched bone that he recognized the way a child recognizes its own name. He had tasted ten thousand dishes, a thousand poisons, a hundred bottled memories in Corvane's vault, and none of them had prepared him for this—memory at full strength, undiluted by glass, unmeasured by ledger, a whole life with no walls around it.

He tried to hold on to himself the way he had learned to hold a spoonful of soup on his tongue without swallowing. *Taste it. Name it. Do not become it.*

But there was no spoon. There was no tongue. There was no Petra.

There was only the steppe, three hundred years ago, and the sky full of wings.

* * *

They came down through the smoke like a judgment, the gift-bearers of the old blood, and the ground where they landed turned to glass.

He was not watching it. That was the horror of it. He was *in* it—a soldier's body, young and strong, hands blistered from a spear-shaft, standing in the shield-line at the edge of what would one day be called the Glass Steppe because of what happened here tonight. The sky above was black with the shapes of the gifted rising on wind they had pulled out of the air itself, and each one that descended left a scar of fused sand behind them, and the scars were spreading, joining, becoming a mirror that reflected a burning world.

*This is the memory,* Petra told himself, clinging to the thought like a man clinging to a spar in open water. *The queen's memory. The last night of the war. You are a guest in it. A taster. You taste. You do not swallow.*

He tasted anyway. He could not help it. His gift worked even here, even now: grief like over-steeped tea, rage like raw spirit, terror like copper coins held too long in the mouth. And threaded through all of it, faint but unmistakable, the flavor he had caught at the banquet a lifetime ago—someone else's grief, aged in a royal throat for twenty years.

A horn sounded. The shield-line braced.

And the queen came.

Not this queen—not Elira, gray-eyed and fading, who mistook a food-taster for her dead brother. The first queen. The one the histories called the Unforgiving, the one whose name the guild still wrote in ledgers with a small mark beside it meaning *do not trade*. She walked out ahead of her army with her hands empty and her sleeves rolled back, and every gifted eye in the sky turned toward her, and the burning world seemed to hold its breath.

Petra knew, distantly, what the histories said. The war ended here. The accord was signed here. The registration laws, the Hunts, the burnings—all of it traced back to this night, this field, this woman.

The histories did not say she was beautiful. The histories did not say she wept while she did it.

"Hold," the soldier's mouth said—Petra's borrowed mouth, someone else's voice. "Whatever you see, hold the line."

What he saw was the queen raising her hands, and the air between them folding like paper.

* * *

She did not fight them. That was the sin of it. That was the thing no ledger recorded, no crier proclaimed, no history dared print: she did not defeat the gift-bearers. She *unmade their gift*.

Petra felt it happen from the inside, because the soldier whose body he wore was gifted—one of the low-blooded, a finder of lost things, nothing more, a man who could taste truth in water. When the queen folded the air, the fold went through him like winter going through a wet coat. Something that had lived in him since birth simply... stopped. A sense gone dark. A room in his soul locked and shuttered, and the key melted.

All across the field, hundreds of voices rose at once—not screams of pain, worse, screams of *absence*, the howl of people suddenly missing a limb they'd had since the womb. Above, the winged shapes fell. Some died hitting the glass. Some lay breathing and blind and hollow, staring at hands that no longer answered them.

And the queen stood in the center of the silence she had made, and Petra—inside the soldier, inside the memory, unable to look away—tasted what she tasted.

Triumph. Relief. And underneath both, already curdling, the flavor that would define a dynasty:

*Guilt.*

Because she had not stopped there. Standing amid the hollowed survivors, she had felt their gifts pour loose into the air—homeless, masterless, looking for vessels—and she had done the thing that made her son's son's granddaughter build vaults of glass and fill them with bottled lives.

She had drunk.

Petra watched her do it. Watched her close her eyes and take the fallen gifts into herself—all of them, or near enough—because she could not bear to leave them lying around like unattended knives, because she trusted no one else to carry them, because power hoarded in fear looks exactly like power hoarded in greed and no one will ever believe the difference. He watched the gifts go into her, dozens of lives' worth of impossible things, and he watched her stagger under the weight, and he heard her say, to no one, to everyone, to the ash coming down like snow:

"No one should have this. Least of all me."

*But you took it anyway,* Petra thought, and the thought was not entirely his own—it had the soldier's bitterness in it, the soldier's loss, three centuries of inherited grievance. *You took it, and your heirs kept it, and my mother burned for the crime of being what you unmade.*

The field began to blur. The memory was moving faster now, years sliding past like cards shuffled by an impatient hand—the treaty signed on glass, the first registers written, the first pyres lit, the guild founded in a cellar by a man whose descendants would one day include Corvane, the queen's burden passed down like a crown that ate its wearers, until at last a gray-eyed girl named Elira sat on the throne with a splinter of ancient fury lodged behind her eyes and began, quietly, terribly, to forget.

*There.* Petra found the seam. The place where the young queen—Elira, not yet crowned, terrified, holding the old weapons her grandmother's guilt had preserved—had done what her ancestor did, but smaller, and saner, and just as fatal. She had cut the war-memory out of herself. Not destroyed it. *Stored* it. Because destroying it felt like lying, and keeping it felt like dying, and so she had chosen the third road, the road of ledgers and vials, the road that killed you slowly instead of quickly.

She had sold it to the only people who dealt in such things.

She had sold it to Corvane.

And Corvane had held it for twenty years, waiting, weighing, doing arithmetic with a kingdom's oldest wound—until the arithmetic came due tonight in a vault beneath the steppe, in the hands of a food-taster who had smashed the containment rig rather than let anyone wield it, and the freed memory had poured toward the nearest vessel like water finding a crack.

*Toward me.*

* * *

He surfaced, gasping, into a darkness that took him a moment to understand.

He was kneeling on cold stone. The vault. The broken rig glittered around him like spilled sugar. Somewhere far above, muffled by thirty feet of glass and earth, Sefa was screaming his name.

He tried to answer. What came out was not his voice.

"*Hold the line.*"

He clamped his jaw shut. His heart slammed against his ribs like something trying to escape a locked room. He could feel it now, feel *all of it*—the war coiled in his chest, vast and furious, pressing outward against the walls of him, testing them, learning them. It had been a guest in the queen for twenty years and in the vial for longer. In him it intended to be a tenant forever, and it was already renovating.

Because that was how memory took a vessel. Not by force. By *displacement*. Every new thing required room, and the room had to come from somewhere, and the somewhere was him.

He felt the first wall come down before he understood which wall it was.

His mother's face—he reached for it and found it smooth, featureless, a portrait painted over in fresh white plaster. He knew he had a mother. He knew she had mattered. The knowing was intact; the image was gone, dissolved, drunk down to make space for a burning steppe and a falling sky.

*No.* He dug his nails into his palms. *No. I get to choose what I lose.*

He built the room back. Forced it. Took his mother's face out of the memory's grip and set it in place piece by piece like a man rebuilding a shattered cup—dark hair, flour on her wrists, the way she hummed when she stirred. He could hold it if he held it *hard*. But holding it meant spending himself, strength draining out through his clenched fists into the dark, and the memory was patient, the memory had waited three hundred years, and it could wait out one tired man in a cold cellar.

So he bargained. That was what he knew how to do. Ledgers. Terms. Trade.

*You want room?* he said to it, to the war, to the queen's ancient guilt sloshing in his chest like ballast in a hull. *Fine. Take the taste of my seventh birthday cake—I never liked marzipan anyway. Take Master Corvane's face when he taught me to write in salt. Take the smell of the guild vault. Take every entry I ever copied, every widow's wedding day, every bargain I logged in a book no one should read. Take the parts of me that belonged to the guild. Take them all. They were never mine to keep.*

It took them. Gladly. Greedily. He felt whole years of his life go soft and slide away like wax off a candle, and he let them go, sip by sip, the way he'd once swallowed poison for kings: deliberately, professionally, counting the cost and paying it.

But when the memory reached further—when it slid its weight against the wall where Sefa lived, the undercook's sharp eyes, her laugh like a lid rattling on a boiling pot, the stew in her rooms that had tasted like home, the word *love* that he had finally said aloud in the shadow of the Hunt—

He refused.

The refusal was not graceful. It was not brave. It was the ugliest, most animal thing he had ever done inside his own skull: he threw himself bodily against the memory's advance, wrapped his whole self around that single bright room, and *held*. The way the shield-line had held, three hundred years ago, while the sky fell. The way the first queen had held her guilt until it killed her slowly. The way the second queen had held her secret until it hollowed her into mistaking strangers for brothers.

Everyone in this story held something until it cost them everything. Very well. Let him be in the story too.

"You can have the guild," he said aloud, and his own voice was his again, barely. "You can have my childhood. You can have every meal I ever tasted before this year. But her—" His breath tore. "Her, you don't touch. That's the term. That's the price. Take it or drown with me."

The memory considered him.

He felt it consider him—an attention vast and old turning its regard upon one small stubborn vessel, weighing, the way he weighed dishes, the way Corvane weighed lives. And then, slowly, with something that might in a kinder century have been respect, it withdrew. Settled. Coiled itself down in the deepest chamber of him and went quiet, a furnace banked to embers, a war waiting for orders.

It would not stay quiet. He understood that. Nothing this size stayed contained out of courtesy. Every hour it would test the walls again. Every hour he would pay again. He was not a victor; he was a vault, and vaults existed to be eventually opened.

But he was still Petra Voss. He checked. Name, mother, the burnt-hummingbird taste of the queen's grief at the banquet, the widow's wedding day he had logged and never forgotten, the stew, the laugh, the love. All present. All paid for. Held.

Above him, faintly, boots on stairs. More than one pair. Voices—Sefa's, sharp with terror, and another voice answering hers, cold and familiar, and Petra's blood went from ember to ice.

Corvane had followed them down.

Petra got one hand against the wall and hauled himself upright, the war shifting heavily inside him like ballast in a listing ship, and began—to his own astonishment—to laugh, low and ragged, because he finally saw the shape of the endgame, the final entry in the final ledger, the trade that had been waiting for him all along.

The memory could not stay in him. The queen needed it back. And between him and the throne stood the one man who had spent twenty years betting that someone would carry it out of this vault alive.

Footsteps in the dark. A lamp flaring.

"Ah," said Corvane, from the top of the stair, and his voice was warm as a merchant closing a sale. "There you are, Petra. And look at you." A pause, savoring it. "*Full.*"
