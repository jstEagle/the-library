## 19. What Petra Voss Chose

Sefa had always believed that the worst night of a person's life would announce itself—thunder, bells, screaming. Instead it came as silence: a silence so complete she could hear the glass flats ticking as they cooled beneath the moon, like a great cooling pie, like the whole world settling into its crust.

She knelt beside Petra in the shadow of the vault's shattered doorway and pressed her palms against the wound in his side, and the blood came away warm and too fast, and he was smiling at her.

"Stop that," she said. "Stop smiling."

"It worked," he said. His voice sounded scraped thin, like a knife drawn across stone one time too many. "The rig. It broke the way I needed it to break."

"You're bleeding into my hands, you absolute fool. That's not *working*."

But even as she said it, she felt it—the change in the air. The freed memory rose off the broken containment rig in threads of pale gold, drifting up through the vault's throat toward the distant pinprick of stars above the steppe, and it was not dissipating. It was *waiting*. Hovering. A swarm of light with nowhere to pour.

And then it found him.

Sefa watched it happen and could do nothing. The gold threads turned in the air like fish sensing current, and then they came down—not violently, not all at once, but with the terrible gentleness of snow falling into open water—and sank into Petra through his skin, through his eyes, through the corner of his mouth where blood had dried. He arched once. Then he went still.

"Petra." She shook him. "Petra, what did you do?"

His eyes opened. For one heartbeat they were his—brown, wry, always half-laughing at something she hadn't heard yet. Then the color shifted, deepened, took on an amber cast like lamplight through old honey, and when he spoke again the voice was still his voice but the cadence was wrong, older, weighted.

"She wanted to burn them," he said—or someone said, using his mouth. "The weapons of the first age. She wanted to burn them all, and her sister begged her to wait, and waiting is how wars are lost—"

"*Petra.*"

He blinked. The amber receded, like a tide pulling back from shore. "I'm here," he said. "I think I'm here. Sefa—I need you to listen very carefully, because there isn't much of me left to explain things twice."

* * *

They came out of the vault as the sky went grey at its eastern edge, and the glass steppe caught the first light and threw it back at them in blades. Petra walked leaning on her shoulder, and every fifty paces he stopped and shuddered, and she learned to tell the difference between his shudders: some were pain, and some were the war moving through him, whole centuries of it passing behind his eyes like weather.

At the third stop he gripped her wrist hard enough to bruise.

"My mother's name," he said. "Tell me my mother's name."

"Maren," Sefa said. "Maren Voss. You told me in your rooms, over the stew. You said she sang while she cooked and she burned the onions on purpose because she liked them bitter."

"Bitter on purpose." He nodded slowly, holding the words like a man holding water in cupped hands. "Yes. Bitter on purpose." He started walking again. Fifty paces later: "What street did we live on?"

"Petra—"

"What *street*, Sefa."

"I don't know. You never told me the street."

He stood very still in the silver light, and she watched him search for it and watched him fail, and then she watched him decide not to show her that he'd failed, which was somehow worse.

"The palace first," he said instead. "The queen. Everything else can fall away after that. The queen has to come first, or none of this meant anything."

"And after the queen?"

He looked at her then, fully, and she saw that he already knew—he had done the arithmetic in the vault, while the memory poured into him, and he had chosen anyway—and what she saw in his face made her want to sit down on the cold glass and never stand up again.

"After the queen," he said gently, "we'll see what's left. Walk. We're losing the dark."

* * *

The city gates were chaos. The Gift-Hunt's bonfires had gone out one by one through the night—Sefa could see the smoke still standing over the districts, straight black columns in the windless dawn—and in their place, rumor ran ahead of them through the gate crowd like spilled water: the Queen wakes, the Queen speaks, the Chancellor is fled, the Chancellor is dead, the Chancellor was never Chancellor at all but a Glasswright in fine clothes.

No one looked twice at two ragged figures limping in from the steppe. Everyone was looking at the palace.

They entered by the kitchens—of course they did; it was the only door Sefa had ever trusted—and the kitchen was empty except for the scullery boy asleep in the flour bin, and the ovens banked and breathing, and everything exactly as she had left it a lifetime ago, four days ago, before Aldery had put his ringed hand on her shoulder and offered her immunity in exchange for a taster's name.

From the kitchens, the servants' stair. From the stair, the long gallery hung with portraits of kings who all shared one jaw. And in the queen's antechamber, armed men who should have arrested them and instead fell to their knees—because the word had run through the guard faster than any crier: *the taster, the taster has come back, the taster carries the queen's missing years.*

"They're saying it like a prayer," Sefa whispered.

"They're saying it because Aldery's faction spread the other story first," Petra murmured. "*The gifted stole the queen's mind.* It was the whole design, remember? Steal the memory, let her fade, blame the gift-bearers, justify the fire." His mouth twisted. "So we bring the truth back ourselves. Before they can finish telling the lie."

A chamberlain met them at the queen's door—a grey, exhausted man whose hands shook as he opened it—and inside, the bedchamber smelled of lavender and candle-wax and sickness, and the queen lay small against white pillows, her skin the color of the ash Sefa had swept out of the Hunt's braziers.

Her eyes opened when they crossed the threshold. They were clear eyes. Lucid, for whatever hour the lucidity lasted.

"You," the queen said. Her voice was dry paper. "The taster. The one who tastes what isn't there."

"Your Majesty." Petra knelt at the bedside, and Sefa saw him sway and steady himself against the frame. "I've brought back something of yours."

"Something of mine." A ghost of a smile. "You say that like a man returning a borrowed coat. Do you know what it is? Do you know what you're carrying?"

"Yes."

"Then you know I asked you to destroy it." Her hand came up from the coverlet, trembling, and found his wrist. "Burn it. Let me die whole rather than live as the woman who ended the world. That was my wish. My only wish, in the last of my sanity."

"And I am going to disobey it," Petra said quietly. "Forgive me, Majesty. But there are two queens in this room tonight. There's the queen who stole her own memory to keep herself from unleashing the old weapons—and she was right, and she was brave, and I will honor her. But there's also the queen who made that choice alone, carrying it alone, letting it eat her alive rather than trust anyone with the weight. And that queen—" his voice caught, and Sefa realized with a jolt that the catch wasn't entirely performance, that somewhere inside him a woman named Maren had sung while she cooked and he could no longer find the song— "that queen taught everyone who served her that duty means drowning quietly. I don't accept that lesson anymore. Neither should you."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, a bell began to ring—the dawn bell, or an alarm, impossible to tell.

"If you pour it back," the queen said at last, "I may not be strong enough to refuse it. You understand that. You'd be handing a starving woman a feast and asking her not to eat."

"That's why I won't just pour it." Petra turned, and found Sefa's eyes across the candlelight. "That's why she's here."

* * *

They arranged it like a ritual because rituals hold shape when everything else is dissolving. The queen propped upright against her pillows. Sefa at the window with the shutters thrown wide so the rising sun laid a bar of gold across the floor between bed and door—an escape route of light, the queen called it, half-smiling, *in case I try to run*. Two guards inside the door with orders, given in the queen's own cracked voice, to restrain her if her own body moved against her will.

And Petra kneeling at the bedside with a plain ceramic bowl—no silver, no guild-glass, nothing enchanted, nothing that could grip the memory and keep it—filled with water from the kitchen well. Sefa had drawn it herself. She didn't know why that mattered. It mattered.

"How does it work?" she whispered, before he began. "The giving back?"

"He'll name each piece as it returns," the queen answered for him, watching Petra with an expression Sefa couldn't parse—fear and longing braided together so tightly they'd become one thing. "That's the old craft. Naming. You can't force a memory into someone—but if you name it truly, you offer it, and they can take it or refuse it. Piece by piece. Choice by choice." Her eyes closed. "My sister used to do this for me, at the end of the war. Name the dead so I could grieve them instead of avenging them. She's been gone three hundred years and no one has named anything for me since."

Petra dipped his fingers in the well water. And began.

Sefa had expected theater—incantation, sigils, the guild's bottle-craft made grand. Instead it was the quietest thing she had ever witnessed. He touched the wet fingertips to the queen's lips, and spoke.

"This is the last night of the war," he said. "This is the sound of the glass plains burning. This is your sister's voice, saying *wait*. Take it or refuse it."

For a moment nothing. Then the queen's lips moved under his fingers, barely: "*Take.*"

And Sefa saw it go into her—a shiver across the queen's shoulders, a deepening of breath, a single tear tracking down into the pillow. And she saw what it cost him, because he flinched as if the leaving of it hurt, and when he drew his hand back his fingertips had gone faintly translucent at the edges, like paper held too near a flame.

"Petra—" she started.

"Don't count yet," he said, without turning around. His voice was steady. Too steady. "There are many pieces. Don't count."

Piece by piece. The siege of the Amber Gate. The names of the nine generals who had begged her to strike first. The old weapons sleeping beneath the steppe, and their waking-words, and the exact weight of the decision not to speak them. Each one named, each one offered, each one taken—and with each one the queen grew more solid, more present, her eyes sharpening from fog to frost to fire, and with each one Petra grew less. Sefa watched his hair lose its color at the temples in real time. Watched him forget, mid-sentence, the word for *saddle*, and reach for it, and fail, and move on. Watched the amber light behind his eyes flare brighter as his own brown drowned beneath it, drop by drop, a cup filling as another empties.

Halfway through, the queen suddenly seized his hand.

"Stop," she gasped. "Stop—you're pouring yourself out with it, boy, I can feel it, you're giving me pieces of *you*—"

"It doesn't matter what I give," Petra said. Sefa heard the lie in it, plain as a cracked bell.

"It matters to me," the queen said. And then, softer, in the voice of a woman remembering: "Maren Voss. Your mother carried memories for the resistance, in the last purge. She held other people's griefs so they wouldn't have to. And it emptied her, piece by piece, year by year, until there wasn't enough left to recognize her son." Her grip tightened. "I knew her, child. I knew her. Is that why you never registered? Is that why you hid in my kitchens tasting poison?"

Something moved across Petra's face—grief reaching for a shape and finding the shape gone. "I... yes," he said slowly. "I believe so. I believe that's why." And Sefa had to turn to the window and press her forehead against the cold shutter-latch, because he had just lost his own mother a second time, in front of witnesses, and thanked the queen for the loss.

"Continue," the queen said hoarsely. "But hear me, taster. When it's done—if there's anything left of you—we will talk about what this crown owes."

* * *

It took until midmorning. The sun climbed the gallery windows and crawled across the floor, and Sefa stood watch at the door the whole time, waving away servants, turning back a panicked chamberlain, lying fluently to a captain of the Hunt who arrived demanding entry and left pale-faced at what he glimpsed through the opening: his sovereign, sitting upright, eyes blazing, ordering him in a voice like a drawn blade to bring her the Privy Council *now*.

The last piece came near noon.

"This is the choice itself," Petra whispered. His voice was nearly gone now, a thread of sound, and the amber filled his eyes completely, and Sefa could see that behind it he was holding on by fingernails, by habit, by the last stubborn syllable of his own name. "This is the moment at the end of the war, when you stood before the sleeping weapons with the waking-word on your tongue, and your sister's hand on your arm, and you chose. This is why you chose. Take it or refuse it."

And the queen—whole now, tall even lying down, a presence that filled the room the way a storm fills a valley—took a long, slow breath, and said:

"*Refuse.*"

The word rang in the chamber like a struck bell. And the amber light that had been pouring into her stopped, gathered, and settled—not swallowed, but *accepted*, taken in the way a nation accepts a hard law, knowingly, with open eyes. She exhaled. The translucence faded from her skin. Color flooded back into her face like ink into water.

She was, Sefa thought, the most frightening person she had ever seen, and she had seen Corvane do his bookkeeping over a burning man.

The queen turned her head on the pillow and looked at Petra—at what remained of him.

"Taster," she said. "What is your name?"

Silence.

Sefa's heart stopped. Then—

"Petra," he said at last, wondering, like a man finding a coin in an old coat. "Petra. Someone—someone kept it for me. I can feel where it was kept." His unfocused gaze drifted around the room and snagged, briefly, blindly, on Sefa, and slid past. "Someone kept it safe. I don't remember who."

Sefa did not cry. She had decided this in advance, on the steppe, walking through the cold: whatever happened, she would not cry in front of the crown. She bit the inside of her cheek until she tasted copper—her own taste, her own memory, hers to keep—and she stepped forward and took Petra's arm as the queen's physicians finally burst through the door in a flood of grey robes and raised voices, and the world outside erupted: bells ringing properly now, cheering in the streets, someone shouting that Lord Chancellor Aldery had been taken at the river gate trying to flee with two trunks of guild-vials and a forged abdication, that his faction was folding like wet paper, that the Hunt was dissolved, dissolved, *dissolved*—

Triumph everywhere. Triumph like sunlight.

And Petra Voss stood in the middle of it, leaning on a stranger's arm, smiling politely at everyone, having no idea why the woman holding him was gripping so hard.

* * *

By evening, the palace had reorganized itself around its returned queen the way iron filings leap around a magnet. Warrants signed in her restored hand. Guild assets seized. Corvane's name—given freely by Sefa, along with the copied accounts, along with everything—entered into the record of a tribunal that would convene within the week.

They gave Petra a room in the guest wing. Of course they did; the queen insisted, the hero of the restoration, the taster who carried back the years. Servants brought him broth. He ate it and thanked them graciously and asked them, each time, very politely, who had sent it, and whether they might remind him of their names.

Sefa stayed. No one ordered her to. No one would have dared order her anywhere.

She sat with him as the lamps were lit, and he studied her across the table with those strange amber-flecked eyes—his own eyes now, mostly, though the color had never fully washed back—and said:

"You've been crying."

"No, I haven't."

"All day. Off and on. Your eyes have the particular redness of—" he paused, frowning at himself, at the gap where knowledge used to be. "Of someone who... knows about crying. I appear to know things without knowing where I know them from. It's very strange. Like finding furniture in a house you don't remember owning." He turned his cup slowly on the table. "You kept my name. In the queen's chamber. I felt it being kept. Was it you?"

"Yes," Sefa said.

"Will you tell me..." He hesitated, and she watched him weigh the question and decide to ask it anyway, which was, she thought, the most Petra thing she had ever seen him do, and the cruelest thing about this whole ruinous day: that the gesture survived when everything that had made it his had burned away. "Will you tell me who you were to me?"

Sefa looked at him for a long time. Outside, the city celebrated. Inside, the broth went cold between them.

"Not tonight," she said finally. "Tonight you're half-dead and I'm half-mad, and if I tell you our story now you'll only be hearing a stranger's tale. Tomorrow I'll start again. From the beginning. With food." She managed something that was almost a smile. "You always said you remembered people best through what they fed you. I'm hoping some of that survived."

"Did I say that?" He smiled—warm, puzzled, utterly lost. "It sounds like something I'd say."

"It is," Sefa said, and stood, and gathered the tray, and at the door she stopped, because on the sill, wedged under the lamp, lay a folded note in a hand she recognized with a lurch—small, precise, unhurried, the handwriting of a man who balanced ledgers in blood and called it mercy.

She opened it with cold fingers.

*Well done, both of you,* it read. *The market for memory has never been healthier. Give my regards to the queen.—C.*

Beyond the window, far out past the city walls, the glass steppe caught the last of the sunset and held it, gleaming, all the way to the horizon—empty, luminous, and patient as a ledger awaiting its next entry.
