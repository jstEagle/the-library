## 20. The Empty Ledger

They gave the Hall of Registers a new floor for the trial.

I noticed it before anything else—the way my boots stuck faintly to fresh pitch, the smell of pine resin and hot iron still rising from the seams where the old boards had been pried up. The old boards had been scorched. I did not need my gift to know why. Every taster in the city could have read that room's history from its smoke: the names read aloud, the pyres built between the benches, the ash swept into the gutters of the Vellum Quarter when the fires were done.

Now they had laid clean wood over it, as if a floor could forget what a palate cannot.

I stood at the witness table with my hands flat on the rail, because if I did not give them something to do they would shake. Behind me, filling half the hall, sat the gifted who had come out of hiding to be counted—bottlers and charm-cutters, a hedge-witch from Tanner's Row whose hands still bore rope-burns from her arrest, three children with the old blood in their eyes who kept looking at the queen's empty chair as though it might bite. Sefa sat among them, not beside me. She had chosen that seat herself, deliberately, so no one could say the woman who testified against Lord Chancellor Aldery's faction had been swayed by the man she loved.

*The man she loved.* The phrase sat strangely on me now, like a coat cut for someone taller. I knew it was true the way one knows an entry in a ledger one did not write—accurate, attested, foreign to the hand.

The queen was present after all. Not enthroned but seated at the prosecutors' bench, wrapped in grey wool instead of white ermine, and whole. That was the miracle the whole city whispered about and no edict confirmed: Queen Isolde, who had been fading for two years like ink left in rain, had woken three weeks ago lucid, furious, and thin as a candle-end, and had spent every waking hour since dismantling the faction that had fed on her forgetting. Aldery sat below her in irons, and he did not look at her once. Guilt has a flavor. His was cold mutton and wet stone, and it filled the hall whenever the doors opened.

"Petra Voss," the Recorder called. "Royal food-taster. Keeper—for the Guild of Glasswrights—of ledgers entered in evidence. You swear your testimony is true?"

"I swear it is tasted." A murmur went through the benches. "Everything I will tell this court, I hold in my mouth. No poison ledger can name what I found in the queen's cup, because it was never poison. It was memory. Bottled, sold, and poured by degrees into our sovereign until it ate her reign away from the inside."

I told them. All of it, in order, because order was the only mercy I had left to give: how Corvane had bottled the queen's stolen war-memory years ago; how he had sold it not once but twice—to Aldery's faction, who wanted it released to justify a purge, and to the queen herself, who had come to him in secret, before any of us knew, begging to buy back the thing she had taken from her own head. How the guild had played both sides of the same vial. How the Hunt's registers had been drafted in the guild's own vault, in Corvane's own hand, each name a debt to be collected or a rival to be burned.

When I said that last part, the hall went quiet in a particular way—not the silence of shock but of recognition. Half the people in those benches had wondered, in their worst hours, whether the lists that sent the collectors to their doors had been written by someone who knew them.

"The ledgers," the Recorder said. They brought them forward then, all nine volumes, carried on a bier like relics. Salt-bound, glass-edged, every trade recorded in Corvane's beautiful hand: the widow's wedding day for bread money; a soldier's courage for a pardon; a mother's face, sold twice, because the first buyer had worn it out. I had written the fair copies of most of them myself. My own script looked back at me across the courtroom, neat and damning, and I understood that I would be reading it for the rest of my life.

"Petra Voss," the queen said.

It was the first time she had spoken directly to me since the night on the palace steps, when Sefa had held me upright and the queen had wept over the taste of her own returned years. Her voice filled the hall without effort. That, too, was a kind of recovery—two months ago she could not have raised it above a whisper.

"You drank what was mine. You carried it back to me knowing the cost." She paused, and I watched her choose her words the way a fencer chooses a line of attack. "This court cannot restore what you paid. But it can decide what the payment purchased. Hear me: there will be no more pyres. The Register of the Gifted will remain—but under crown seal, administered by oath-takers drawn *from* the registered, not against them. Registration is protection. Anyone who burns a registered soul answers to me personally."

She let that land. Somewhere behind me a woman began to cry, quietly, the way people cry when they have been holding it for a very long time.

"And the Guild of Glasswrights?" the Recorder asked carefully. "Its charter—"

"Is dissolved. Its assets seized. Its master—" The queen stopped. I felt the whole hall lean toward the gap in her sentence.

"Its master," I said, because someone had to, "is gone."

* * *

He had been gone eleven days by then, and I could tell them exactly which eleven days, because I had reconstructed it the way I reconstructed everything now—from traces, from residue, from what things tasted like after.

The night the collectors came for the vault beneath the steppe, Corvane had already stripped it. Not the memories themselves—he had left those, hundreds of vials sleeping in their salt-cradles, because to carry them would have been to carry evidence. What he took was smaller and worth more: the client rolls. Nine years of buyers. Names, sums, flavors requested. Every noble, minister, and merchant in the capital who had ever purchased a stolen hour, a borrowed talent, a rival's shame. He had taken the one book that could have bought his own head a pardon, and instead of spending it—

He spent it on the road. We found the first pages fluttering in the glass grass twenty miles east, torn small, scattered like seed. He was feeding himself to the steppe as he fled, leaving nothing behind that anyone could subpoena, becoming progressively less a man and more a rumor with a ledger's handwriting.

Sefa and I had reached the vault ahead of the collectors by four hours—four hours bought with her blood on the mirror-flats and a wound in her side that the healers said should have killed her—and we had stood in that humming dark among the sleeping vials, and I had known, with the certainty of the palate, that he was already gone. The air in the vault had tasted of struck bell and absence. Of a door closed gently from the other side.

At trial, they made me say it plainly. "You are telling this court that Corvane, master of the Guild of Impossible Things, architect of the purge, seller of the queen's mind—has escaped justice entirely?"

"I am telling you what I tasted in the vault," I said. "Corvane was never in the business of being caught. He was in the business of being necessary. Remove him, and you discover how much of the market he *was*." I looked at the queen. I owed her that much honesty and no less. "Your Majesty dissolved a guild. You did not dissolve the appetite. Somewhere beyond the horizon, someone is already bottling."

Aldery laughed. It was the only time during the whole proceeding. One short, ugly bark from the dock, and the marshals silenced him, and the hall pretended it hadn't heard, and everyone had heard.

* * *

The verdicts came at dusk.

Aldery and four others of his faction: death, commuted in two cases to the salt mines, because the queen had learned something in her years of fading about the weight of taking. The collectors who had lit the pyres on the guild's street: registered, disarmed, and set to rebuilding what they had burned, under supervision of the very survivors they'd made. The hedge-witch from Tanner's Row was appointed first oath-taker of the new register. When they handed her the seal she held it like a live coal, and then, deliberately, like a tool.

And for me: nothing. That was the verdict. *No charge.* The Recorder read it in a bored voice, as though clearing a clerical matter, and perhaps to him it was—I had broken guild law, not crown law, and the guild no longer legally existed, and the queen had made it plain in a private audience that the man who carried her memory home through the fire of his own self would not be tried for the ledgers he'd kept under orders and threat. Justice, I had learned, rarely arrives as triumph. It arrives as paperwork, correctly filed, and you stand in a drafty hall while a bored clerk says *no charge*, and somewhere outside a baker is burning the morning's loaves and the smell drifts through the windows and reminds you, absurdly, that you are hungry, that the world continues, that the dead stay dead and the guilty mostly get away.

Afterward, in the colonnade, the gifted came to me one by one. Not to thank me—that would have been simpler. To *taste*. An old bottler took my hand and pressed his thumb to my pulse, the old greeting, the one from before the purges, and said, "Empty cup," which is what they say to honor a sacrifice. One by one they did it, dozens of them, until my arm ached and my eyes stung, because every one of them had lost someone to the ledgers, to the Hunt, to the long quiet years, and I was the nearest thing the age had produced to a witness that survival was possible.

Sefa waited until the last of them had gone. She leaned against the column beside me, arms crossed, watching the light die over the rooftops.

"No charge," she said.

"No charge."

"They'll write ballads about you anyway. Wrong ones." She glanced sideways. "In most of them I'll be very beautiful and do nothing."

"In the ones I'd write," I said, "you'd be insufferable and save my life twice."

Her mouth twitched. Then, quieter: "Do you remember the stew?"

I looked at her. This was the ritual we had fallen into, she asking, me bracing, both of us knowing the answer. "No."

"Do you remember that you loved someone?"

"Yes." The word came easily, which was somehow worse. "It's the truest thing left in me. It has no shape. It's just—a warmth with a direction, and the direction is you."

She nodded slowly, accepting it the way she accepted everything now: as weather, as work, as a thing to be cooked with rather than cursed. "Then tomorrow we start again. Lesson forty-one. I'll make the stew with the caraway. You'll tell me what it tastes like, and I'll tell you what it meant." She pushed off the column. "That's how we'll fill the empty ledger, Petra. Entry by entry. Our hand this time."

She walked ahead of me toward the gates, toward the rooms above the kitchen where I now slept badly and woke grateful, and I followed, and the taste of the evening was bread-smoke and resin and the ghost of a wound healing, and underneath it all, faint but unmistakable, from far off past the eastern walls where the glass flats began—

Salt. And struck bell. And the particular dry mineral tang of a page being written by a hand I knew.

Somewhere out under the first stars, Corvane had found a new vessel.
