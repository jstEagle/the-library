## 21. A Stranger at My Own Table

The first thing I learned about the woman in my kitchen was that she salted before she tasted.

I stood in the doorway of my own rooms—my rooms, the lease signed in my hand, the coat on its hook shaped to my shoulders—and watched her work. Small and quick, sleeves rolled to the elbow, a scar along her forearm like a stitch of white thread. She moved through my kitchen the way water moves through a channel it has cut itself: without asking permission, without apology.

She caught me watching. She didn't startle. She'd had weeks, I think, to grow used to the look on my face—the look of a man greeting a stranger who knows his name.

"Salt first," she said, holding up the pinch between two fingers. "Because you always said a cook who waits for the taster is a coward."

"Did I say that?"

"You say it every morning." Her voice was steady, but her knuckles whitened around the salt jar, and I understood with a sick, swimming clarity that she was performing steadiness for my benefit, the way I once performed ignorance over Aldery's dinner table. "You've been saying it for three years. You just don't remember saying it today."

I did not remember her at all.

That is the sentence I have had to learn to carry. Not *I forgot*—forgetting implies something misplaced, retrievable, a name on the tip of the tongue waiting for the right association to shake it loose. No. There is simply an absence where she should be, smooth-walled, like a tooth pulled long ago. I know facts about her as one knows facts from a report: her name is Sefa; she was an undercook in the palace kitchens; she copied accounts; she testified against Lord Chancellor Aldery's faction and burned her own safety to do it; she saved my life on the glass steppe with her body between me and a collector's knife. The queen herself thanked her by name in the Hall of Accord.

But when she says *Petra*, some stranger inside me answers, and he does not know why his chest tightens.

What remains is not memory. What remains is weather. A pressure behind the ribs whenever she laughs. The certainty—I can call it nothing else—that I once loved someone, and that the shape of the hole she left fits her exactly. Draining the queen's stolen war back into her cost a vessel, and I was the nearest vessel, and the memory took its rent in the only coin I had: three years of her.

She stayed anyway.

* * *

We rebuilt ourselves in the only language I have left.

It was her idea, though she delivered it like a surrender. "You can't read your own past," she said, on the second morning, setting down a bowl of barley stew, "but you can taste anything else. So we'll do it backward. I'll cook what we ate. You tell me what you find."

The stew was simple—barley, root vegetables, a bone of smoked mutton, marjoram. I put the spoon in my mouth and the room went away.

Not the way the war-memory had taken me, all claws and fire. This was gentler. A small kitchen with a crooked window. Rain. Two people arguing about whether marjoram belongs in barley stew, and the argument itself worn smooth with repetition, beloved, a ritual disguised as quarrel. Her voice, younger by some seasons. My own laugh, answering hers.

I came back to myself with tears on my face and no idea whose grief they were.

"Well?" she asked. Her hands were folded very tightly on the table.

"There's a window," I said slowly, "that leans left. And you cheat at cards. Or you used to. I don't know how I know that." I looked at the stew as if it might explain itself. "Sefa. The flavor remembers you. It's only me it won't tell."

Something crossed her face—not hope exactly, hope would have been crueler. Recognition, maybe. The look of someone finding a footprint where they'd buried a body.

"The window leans because the frame rotted and neither of us could afford the joiner," she said. "And you let me win at cards so I'd keep playing. Which means you were bad at letting me win, because I noticed by the third game."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry." She reached across the table and stopped, her hand hovering above mine, asking a question her mouth wouldn't. "Be accurate. That's what you'd want. You told me once—" and here her voice finally cracked, just a hairline fracture, quickly mortared over, "—you told me that a ledger that flatters its keeper is worse than none at all."

So we kept the ledger.

A real one, this time, bound in grey cloth, ruled in my own careful hand. *Barley stew, marjoram: window leaning left, rain, card games, she cheats, I let her.* *Honey-cakes on fast-days: bought from the stall by the Tanner's Gate, the seller with the gold tooth, you always overpaid and called it investment.* *Burnt sugar: the night after the vault, you made it wrong on purpose because I liked it burnt and you never admitted why.*

That last entry stopped my pen. "Why *did* you like it burnt?"

"Because my mother burned everything," Sefa said, not looking up from the onions she was slicing. "Sugar, bread, rice. She said a kitchen that smelled of burning was a kitchen where someone was home. After she died I couldn't eat anything properly cooked for a year." She set down the knife. "You figured that out before I ever said it. That's the kind of man you are. Were. Are." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "Tenses are the worst part. Nobody warns you about the tenses."

I wrote it all down. Every word. Because here is the thing I learned in those first weeks, the thing that keeps me writing still: my gift survived. Whatever the war-memory devoured, it left the tongue intact. I cannot remember loving her, but I can taste the truth of her, and the truth of her is everywhere—in the stews, in the honey-cakes, in the way she salts before tasting—and each dish is a page, and I am a taster, and reading pages is the one thing I have never forgotten how to do.

Love, it turns out, leaves residue. Like smoke in wool. Like iron in water. You can lose the fire and keep the stain.

* * *

Not every meal was gentle. Some flavors bit.

There was a night in late autumn when she made eel pie—river eel, butter, a crust latticed the way palace cooks lattice it for feast days—and I was halfway through the first slice when the taste seized me by the throat.

A corridor under the palace. Torch-smoke and panic. My hand gripping a wrist. Her voice saying, low and furious: *"You drink it and there may be nothing left of you to pour back out. Do you understand? Nothing."* And my answer—I heard my own answer, felt my own mouth shape it—*"Then remember the ledgers are under the loose stone in the pantry. Someone should get the truth out, even if it isn't me."*

And beneath that, threaded through the pastry like a wire through wax: fear. Mine. Terror, even—but terror braided with something harder. Purpose. I had known what it might cost. I had walked into that vault with my eyes open.

I set down the fork. My hands were shaking badly enough to rattle the plate.

"That night," I managed. "Before the vault. We fought."

"We argued," she corrected softly. "You wanted to send me away with the ledgers. I wanted—" She stopped. Took a breath the way one takes a blow. "I wanted to come with you. So I did. You couldn't stop me. You tried locking the pantry and I climbed out the washroom window and waited for you at the Tanner's Gate with two horses and a face like thunder."

"And then I was shot off my horse on the flats and you put yourself between me and the collector."

"Ribs. Three of them." She touched her side absently, an old habit, I could see—an old habit from a life I couldn't see. "You held the bandage on the whole ride to the vault. Kept talking to me so I'd stay awake. You talked about—" a wet, broken laugh escaped her— "you talked about opening a tavern. After. A little place near the river, no guild connections, where nobody ever had to sell a memory for bread."

"Do you still want that?"

"I stopped wanting it when they carried you up the palace stairs half-dead." She looked at me across the ruined eel pie, and her eyes were dry in the way that means the crying happens later, alone, where the other person can't be burdened by it. "I wanted you awake more than I wanted anything. And now you're awake. So." She picked up her fork. "Eat your pie. Tell me what else is in it."

I ate. I told her. Corridor, torchlight, her fury, my purpose, the tavern, the loose stone. She added corrections in the margins of my ledger the way a scholar annotates scripture: *not thunder—rain, actually, it rained that whole week.* *The horses were borrowed from Ostrec the farrier, we never paid him, add him to the list of debts.*

The list of debts. That became its own page. People we owed. People who owed us. The widow from Chapter Two of our story—the one whose wedding day Corvane had bottled for bread money—had been found, Sefa told me, and compensated from the guild assets the crown seized, though no coin could give her back the day she'd sold. She had wept on Sefa's shoulder at the trial and Sefa had held her until the bailiff cleared the room.

"How do I know that?" I asked. "How do I know her face when you describe her? I never met her."

"You met everyone," Sefa said. "You remembered them for them. Half the city walked around with their dead kept alive in your mouth." She smiled, and it hurt to look at, the way beautiful things hurt when they're slightly out of focus. "You used to hate it. Being everyone's reliquary. But you never once refused."

* * *

Winter came down off the hills and closed the roads, and the meals grew longer and slower, the way meals do when they have become the whole architecture of a life.

By then the ledger had filled one book and started a second. I knew our story the way a cartographer knows a coast he has never sailed: every inlet charted, every reef marked, and yet the water itself—what it felt like to be in it—forever beyond the map. I knew that we had danced once, badly, in this very room, to a fiddler's tune drifting up from the street. I knew it because the entry existed and because when she hummed that tune while kneading bread, something in my body turned toward her like a plant toward light, obedient to a season it no longer remembered.

One night I found her asleep at the table over the open ledger, her cheek on the page recording the burnt sugar, her breath fogging the ink. I covered her with my coat and sat down across from her and did something I had not yet dared: I tasted the air of the room itself. Old habit, older than the theft of my memory—the palate reaching out unbidden for the emotional sediment of a place.

And the room answered.

It was faint, ghost-faint, the way a hearth holds heat hours after the fire dies. But it was there. Years of us. Two bodies sharing a small warm space, the accumulated steam of a hundred cooked meals, the mineral trace of shared salt. Grief in it, yes—hers, freshly renewed daily, poor woman—and loneliness, mine, and underneath both, dense as a vein of ore, something patient and enormous that my tongue had no honest name for except the one word I could not bring myself to write:

*Love. Residue of love. Occupant unknown.*

Occupant unknown. I almost laughed aloud, there in the dark, and woke her. Even my own gift filed me under *stranger*.

But here is what I wrote instead, that night, by candle stub, in the grey ledger, in the hand that is the last unbroken thing about me: *The room remembers us together. Therefore we were together. Therefore the love was real. Therefore it can be built again, because I am building it already, meal by meal, entry by entry, whether or not I feel it yet. Feeling is a flavor. Flavors return.*

She woke at dawn and found the entry. She read it twice. Then she got up, salted the pot before she tasted it, and said, "Today we do the plum dumplings. The ones from the harvest festival. You cried at the harvest festival."

"I did not."

"You absolutely did. Right into the dumplings. Best ones anyone ever ate." She was smiling as she rolled the dough, and for the first time since I'd woken to a stranger in my kitchen, the smile reached all the way to her eyes. "Ledger says so. Ledger doesn't lie."

* * *

The knock came at the door on a grey morning in the first thaw, and everything tender we had built tilted on its hinge.

Two visitors. The first was a page from the palace, snowmelt dripping off his boots, carrying a letter sealed with the new sigil—the cup and the open hand—which the queen had decreed should mark all correspondence between the throne and the registered gifted. The letter was brief. The queen requested my presence. There was matter concerning the glass flats, and a trade reported at the edge of the steppe: bottled memories changing hands again, sold to buyers whose names the informants would not speak, priced in coin no guild ledger had ever recorded.

Corvane. Alive, unhanged, and trading, somewhere past the horizon where justice had failed to follow him.

The second visitor was no page. He waited at the foot of the stairs, lean and grey-eyed, travel-worn, with the flat stare of a man who has crossed the steppe alone in winter. He looked at me the way men look at a door they intend to open.

"Taster Voss," he said. "My name is Ostrec. I shoe horses near the Tanner's Gate. I'm told you owe me for two ponies." A pause, deliberate as a card turned face-up. "I've also come about the flats. Because whoever is selling memories out there—" and his gaze flicked upward, past my shoulder, to where Sefa had gone very still in the doorway with a wooden spoon still in her hand, "—is buying from someone on this side of the wall. And I think that someone has been in this kitchen."

The spoon trembled once in her grip, and stopped.

I looked at Sefa. She looked at me. And in the silence between us, I reached for the only sense I trusted, the sense that had survived the theft of everything else—and tasted, hanging faint in the thaw-air of my own doorway, a flavor I knew from the ledgers, from the trial testimony, from a thousand entries in her handwriting:

Fear. And guilt. And underneath them both, unmistakable as burnt sugar, the taste of someone keeping a secret.
