## 22. The Sigil of the Glass Steppe

The salt flats at dawn looked like the sky had fallen and refused to get up.

Petra stood at the edge of the glass steppe with a wagon-load of ledgers behind him and a brazier's worth of oil soaking into their covers, and for a long time he did nothing at all. He simply let the light come. It came the way it always did out here—sideways, blinding, thrown up off the flats in sheets until the whole world was white on white and a man could believe he had been erased already and simply hadn't noticed.

The ledgers were heavier than they should have been. He had counted them twice on the road from the city: forty-one volumes spanning ninety years of the Guild of Impossible Things, each page written in his own hand or in hands he had learned to read like flavors. Every trade. Every widow who sold her wedding day. Every soldier who sold the war he'd survived because surviving it was worse than selling it. Every bottled grief with a buyer's mark and a price in coin or favor or silence.

He had carried them out here himself. He could have ordered it done—the queen would have sent soldiers, carts, an honor guard if he'd asked—but some things you do with your own back or not at all.

"Petra."

Her voice. He turned.

Sefa came across the flats with two clay cups wrapped in cloth against the cold, walking carefully the way people learn to walk on glass—not because it breaks, but because it remembers every footstep. She had told him once that she'd grown up three streets from where he had, that they might have passed each other at the well a hundred times before either of them could walk without being watched. He had no memory of this. He had no memory of her at all, not truly—not her laugh in the dark of the vault tunnel, not her blood on the mirror-flats when she threw herself between him and a collector's blade, not the night she confessed she had copied palace accounts for a man she despised because fear is its own kind of employer.

What he had was a notebook. Forty pages, in her handwriting, titled at the top in her blunt, unornamented script: *For Petra, who paid for us both. Read one page a day. Do not rush.*

He had read them all twice now. He knew the shape of what he'd lost the way a man knows the shape of a room by the dust left where furniture stood.

"You're supposed to be asleep," he said. "You were up half the night arguing with the registrar's clerks."

"They were wrong about clause nine." She handed him a cup. It was chicory and honey, no milk—the way he apparently took it, according to page twelve. "I'm always right about clause nine. Drink. You look like something the tide forgot."

He drank. The bitterness of it sat on his tongue and, as everything did now, opened a door. Chicory meant the winter he'd spent sick at eight years old; honey meant his mother's kitchen, though the kitchen itself was gone, burned down to flavor alone. His gift remained. That was the joke at the center of everything—that the Hunt had taken his name for it, the guild had taken his past, and still the tongue went on tasting, faithful as a dog at a grave.

*You kept the ledger of yourself,* Corvane had told him once, in that voice like a key turning. *You only thought you were keeping mine.*

* * *

They built the pyre together.

It was not a grand thing. The flats gave nothing to burn—no wood, no brush, only salt and the glass beneath it—so they stacked the ledgers themselves into a tower, forty-one volumes lashed with cord, and soaked them with lamp-oil brought out in jugs over three days. Sefa worked beside him in silence, tearing the wax seals off the spines where the guild's sigil was stamped: a closed fist gripping a vial.

*Closed fist,* Petra thought. *Of course. We stamped it on everything and never once asked what the hand was holding, or from whom.*

When it was built he knelt at the base of the stack and opened the last volume to the final page, because there was one entry left unwritten, and he had promised himself he would write it before the fire took everything else.

He wrote it now, in the cold, with a pen that shivered in his fingers:

*Entry the last. The Guild of Impossible Things traded in memory for ninety years and called it commerce. Its master believed the gifted could survive only as assets, and nearly made the world agree. This ledger records his arithmetic. Let the fire record the remainder.*

He signed it. Then, after a moment, he added a second line:

*Also recorded: Sefa of the underkitchens, who chose the harder right over the easier safe, and lost nothing but my name, which I am told I will spend the rest of my life earning back.*

He showed it to her before closing the cover. She read it standing very straight, chin up, the way she did when she didn't trust her face to hold still.

"That's not how I'd have phrased it," she said.

"How would you have?"

"Louder," she said, and her voice cracked exactly where he'd known it would, and he reached up and gripped her hand, and neither of them said anything else for a while, because some entries are better left spoken only once.

Then he struck the flint, and the Guild of Impossible Things began to burn.

* * *

Fire on salt is a strange sacrament.

The flames ran up the stacked volumes and the heat bent the air above the flats, and the light of the burning came back off the glass in every direction at once, so that the fire seemed to be happening everywhere—underfoot, overhead, on the horizon in all four quarters—as though the steppe itself had caught the flame and was showing it to the sky. The ledgers curled and blackened. Ink boiled off the pages in little dark blooms. Ninety years of other people's stolen mornings went up in smoke that smelled, Petra thought, like a library after a flood: paper and salt and something faintly sweet underneath, like the ghost of a wedding cake.

People came to watch. He hadn't invited anyone; word had simply traveled, as word does when a thing has waited long enough to be done. First a handful, then dozens—the widow from Salt Row whose wedding day he had once entered into Book Three, older now, her new husband's hand around hers. A bottler named Ives who had survived the burning of the guild street by hiding in a cistern for two days. Two of the registered gifted, wearing the new sigil on armbands of undyed wool. A clerk from the queen's own office, taking no notes, only watching, because the queen had asked her to see it with her own eyes and carry the seeing home.

No speeches. Petra had decided against speeches. Speeches were Corvane's currency—he had watched the old man spend them for years, buying crowds the way he bought memories, cheap at the source and dear at resale.

But when the last ledger collapsed into embers, Petra stepped forward onto the bare glass, and the crowd quieted on its own.

"I was a taster," he said. His voice carried strangely on the flats; the salt gave it back doubled. "I was taught that my gift was for finding poison. It was. But poison isn't always in the cup. Sometimes the poison is the price. Sometimes it's the ledger. Sometimes—" and here he paused, because the next words had cost him forty pages of notebook to learn, "—sometimes it's the belief that people are safer as property than as neighbors."

He drew the knife.

It was a plain thing, the kind sold in any market stall—Sefa had chosen it deliberately, he suspected, from page nineteen: *he distrusts ceremony; give him tools, not relics.* He knelt on the glass and began to cut.

The sigil took him most of an hour. He carved it large—a man's height across, deep enough to hold shadow—and he carved it slowly, because he wanted the work of it in his hands even if his mind could not keep what it was working toward. A cup, first: simple, open-topped, the kind any kitchen held a hundred of. And then beside it, touching its rim, an open hand—fingers spread, palm up, empty. Not giving. Not taking. Just open.

A cup and an open hand. What one offers freely, the other receives freely. No fist. No vial. No seal.

When it was done, the crowd came forward one by one and pressed their palms to the cut edges of the sigil, and the salt settled into the grooves and made the lines shine white against the dark glass, and somewhere in the middle of all those hands Petra felt Sefa's find his, and he held it without knowing why it steadied him more than the others, and then remembering—he did know why. Page seven. Page thirteen. Page twenty-two, which ended, *He will not remember this day. Write it anyway.*

He remembered it now. He would write it again tonight. That was the bargain they had made, the two of them: not restoration but reconstruction, love rebuilt the way a wall is rebuilt—one course of stone at a time, mortar mixed fresh each morning, patient as masons who know the wall will outlast them.

The widow from Salt Row wept openly. The clerk from the queen's office pressed her palm to the glass last of all, and when she lifted it she bowed to Petra, low, the bow owed to an equal, and he understood that the accord was real now—not parchment and seal, but salt and skin. The queen had signed the Registration Act in the spring. Registered gifted bore the cup-and-hand on their doors. Aldery's faction had broken apart like wet bread when the accounts Sefa stole went before the Assembly, and Aldery himself had taken the quiet exile offered to men whose crimes were great but whose testimony was still useful. Justice incomplete. The queen had said it plainly: *We cannot hang our way to peace. We can only build faster than they burn.* Petra had tasted the truth in her voice, and it had been bitter and clean, like chicory.

* * *

By midday the fire was ash, and the wind off the steppe had begun its slow work, lifting the grey flakes in spirals and carrying them out across the flats in long ribbons, northward, toward the city, so that it seemed for a while as though the ashes were traveling home—to the guild street, to the vault, to all the places the ledgers had lived. Petra watched until the last of it went.

That was when he saw the rider.

Far off—at the very limit where the flats' white blur surrendered to the horizon's haze—a single figure on horseback, moving east along the salt road. Too far to make out anything but motion. But Petra's eyes had been trained for twenty years to notice what didn't belong, and something about the figure's set of shoulders, the unhurried economy of its seat, snagged on him like a fishbone.

Then the rider stopped. Turned. And though the distance made certainty impossible, Petra felt—with the same deep tongue he tasted memories with, the sense beneath the senses that no purge had ever reached—felt the man look directly at him across five miles of salt, and smile.

Corvane.

They had found the guildmaster's chamber empty the night the vault fell. Empty except for one item: a single blank vial, stoppered, sitting on the desk beside a fresh sheet of ledger paper bearing a single line in Corvane's immaculate hand—*Inventory transfers with the trader. New market, same goods. You'll understand eventually, boy. Memory always finds new vessels.* They had hunted him through the spring. Warrants, rewards, patrols on the salt roads. Nothing. A man who had spent his life teaching other people to forget had known better than anyone how to be forgotten himself.

And now here he was at the edge of seeing, riding east with the sun at his back, and Petra understood the message perfectly, because it was the oldest message there was: *I am not finished. Neither is the trade. Burn your books; the market needs no books. Burn the sigils; the thirst remains.*

Sefa followed his gaze. "Do you want me to fetch the patrol?" she asked quietly. "They're an hour out."

Petra considered it. Considered the warrants, the reward, the satisfaction of watching collectors ride down that white road and bring the old man back in chains.

"No," he said at last. "Let him go. Not mercy." He said it before she could ask, because page three of her notebook said he always had to say it before she could ask. "Arithmetic. If we take him, he becomes a martyr with disciples. If we let him run, he becomes a rumor—and rumors starve, given enough honest trade to feed on instead. Let the cup-and-hand outlast the closed fist. That's the only vengeance that works. Outlasting."

She studied his face for a moment, then nodded, and took his arm, and they stood together and watched until the rider crested the far rise and was gone, swallowed by the shimmer where the salt became the sky.

"He'll be back," Sefa said. "Men like that always come back."

"Yes," Petra agreed. "But he'll come back to a different world. That matters more than whether he comes."

* * *

They stayed until evening.

There was no reason to, and every reason. The crowd had dispersed hours ago; the clerk had ridden back to report; the ash had finished its journey north. But the flats at dusk were the most honest place Petra knew—the hour when the glass gave back not the sun's glare but its warmth, amber and rose and fading violet, so that walking on the steppe felt like walking inside the memory of the day just ended. Fitting ground, he thought, for what they had buried here.

He knelt again at the edge of the carved sigil and laid his palm flat against the glass. The stone had kept the sun's heat. Beneath it, deeper than heat, he felt—or imagined he felt, and had stopped caring about the distinction—the hum of the old vault far below the flats, empty now, its racks shattered, its vials returned or destroyed, the great bell-sound of the queen's stolen war finally silent. Somewhere under all that salt lay the scar of everything the guild had been.

Above it, in the cooling air, stood what came next.

"A toast," Sefa said, and produced, from nowhere, a flask.

He raised an eyebrow. "Is that—"

"Cheap wine from the Salt Road carters' stall. The kind you complained about for six years, according to page fifteen." She poured a measure into the cup and pressed it into his hands, and her eyes were bright and fierce and patient, all at once, the way they always were. "To the ledger keeper. Who kept everyone's accounts but his own, and is learning."

"To the undercook," he answered, "who copied a corrupt chancellor's accounts by candlelight for two years out of sheer stubborn decency, and got a novel out of it."

"It's not a novel. It's forty pages."

"It's the best book I've ever read," he said, and meant it more than he could explain, and saw that she knew, and that knowing was enough.

He drank. Cheap wine: vinegar-bright, careless, alive. And beneath it, faint as a signature in an old book, the taste of the two of them—of this year, of notebooks and rebuilt walls, of a woman teaching a man his own heart one meal at a time. His gift held it gently. He would carve it into himself if he had to. He had, after all, learned the craft from masters.

Below the horizon, in whatever city or camp or crossroads market Corvane Dunmore's old master had already begun to haunt, someone was surely pouring a drink of their own tonight—sealing a vial, naming a price, telling some frightened soul that forgetting was for sale and memory could always find a new vessel.

Let them, Petra thought. The thirst was old; it would outlive them all. But so would the open hand.

He set down the empty cup on the glass beside the sigil—left it there, deliberately, an offering to whoever passed this way next, a cup with nothing hidden in it—and took Sefa's hand, and together they walked west, toward the road, toward the city, toward the lamplit kitchen where tomorrow's page was waiting to be written.

Behind them the salt wind moved over the carved lines, filling the cup and the open hand with white, and the flats held the shapes the way the steppe held everything: patiently, completely, and without ever quite letting go.
