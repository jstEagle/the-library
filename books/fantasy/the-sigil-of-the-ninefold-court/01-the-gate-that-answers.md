## 1. The Gate That Answers

The mountain had been quiet so long that Rosalind Villalobos had begun to mistake the silence for peace.

They walked the patrol at dusk, as they had walked it every seventh evening for forty years, boots finding the same worn hollows in the shale path, lantern swinging the same slow arc against the dark. The path to the Thornfell gate was not a road. Roads invited travelers, and travelers asked questions, and questions were the one thing Rosalind could not afford. So there was only a path — a goat-track, really, threading up through scree and wind-bent pine, invisible unless you already knew it was there.

Rosalind knew it was there the way they knew their own heartbeat: by never once having been allowed to forget it.

The air changed a hundred paces from the seal. It always did. Cold rolled down from the heights like water finding its level, carrying the mineral smell of deep stone and something older beneath it — a sweetness gone sour, like honey left in a dead man's pantry. Forty years ago that smell had nearly driven Rosalind back down the mountain with their hair white at twenty-three years old. Now it was simply the smell of home, which said more about home than Rosalind cared to examine.

The gate itself was nothing to look at. That was the point of it.

A slab of grey stone set into the mountainside, taller than two men and wider than a wagon, unmarked except for a hairline crack that ran from crown to base in a pattern no mason had cut. To a shepherd stumbling onto it, it would read as natural — one more face of rock among ten thousand. But Rosalind saw what lived under the surface: the wards, laid down in layers deeper than language, older than the kingdom that now claimed this valley on its maps. Nine seals nested within nine seals. The Thornfell gate did not bar a door. It barred a throat.

Rosalind set their palm flat against the stone, as they did at the start of every patrol, and spoke the keeper's words into the grain of it — low, tuneless, half-swallowed by habit.

*Stone sleep. Stone keep. Stone remember me.*

The mountain, as always, said nothing back.

Except tonight it didn't.

Tonight, as the last syllable left their mouth, Rosalind felt the stone *warm*. Not sun-warmth — the gate's north face hadn't seen direct light since before Rosalind's grandmother drew breath. This was warmth from within, a slow animal heat rising through the rock like blood returning to a limb long asleep. And then, threaded thin through the granite, faint as a voice heard through a wall:

*—keep her, keep her, oh old ones below, she is small and I am poor, take anything but her, I'll give you anything but—*

Rosalind snatched their hand back as though the stone had bitten them.

The prayer went on. A woman's voice, cracked and exhausted, worn smooth in places from saying it over and over until the words had lost their edges. It came up through the mountain the way water comes up through chalk — everywhere at once, seeping. And beneath it, deeper than the voice, Rosalind felt something else move. The wards. Nine layers of silence that had held without a tremor for four decades shivered, each one waking in turn like a sleeper disturbed — a ripple passing down through the dark of the stone, down and down, toward whatever lay coiled at the bottom of all that patience.

"No," Rosalind said aloud. Their voice sounded absurdly small against the mountainside. "No, you don't."

But the stone was no longer listening to them. Or rather — it was listening to someone else first.

Rosalind stood before the gate for a long while, long enough for the lantern oil to burn low, long enough for the cold to work through their coat into the old break in their shoulder that ached when winter thought about coming. They pressed their palm to the rock again. The prayer had thinned to nothing. The wards had settled — but not fully, not the way a house settles, which is honest. This was the way a held breath settles. Something waiting to be exhaled.

*Mountain-madness,* they told themselves, walking back down in the blue dark. *Forty years alone with a secret will do that. You heard your own thoughts bounce off the stone. You're an old fool talking to rocks.*

It was a comfortable lie. Rosalind had survived this long by being very good at comfortable lies, and they wrapped this one around themselves like a second coat.

It almost worked. Right up until they reached the bottom of the track and saw the lights moving in the smugglers' camp below.

* * *

There were eleven souls wintering at Thornfell's foot that year, camped in the fold of the hill where the stream bent — smugglers, mostly, and the families smugglers accumulate the way ships accumulate barnacles. Rosalind tolerated them because tolerating them was safer than noticing them. They used the hidden roads Rosalind kept; they paid in salt and silence; and once a season their leader, Wren, climbed the goat-track to trade maps and gossip and sit by Rosalind's fire eating stew with the particular appetite of someone who had grown up hungry and never entirely believed the hunger was over.

Wren had arrived at Rosalind's door twenty-six years ago, an infant in a fish crate, and stayed long enough to learn every road Rosalind knew. Then Wren had done the thing foundlings do — grown, and left, and come back, and left again — until leaving and returning had become its own kind of devotion. Rosalind told themselves this was fine. Rosalind told themselves many things.

The camp was lit wrong tonight. Too many torches. Torches meant searching, and searching meant panic, and panic near Thornfell meant the safe invisible life Rosalind had built was cracking along some seam they couldn't yet see.

They broke into a walk that was nearly a run.

They found the mother first. She sat by a guttering fire wrapped in a blanket she wasn't warm in, rocking slightly, lips still moving — and Rosalind's stomach dropped through the floor of the world, because they knew those lips' shapes. Even exhausted and half-swallowed, even carried up through forty feet of living rock, they knew the shapes of the words.

*Keep her, keep her, oh old ones below.*

"You prayed," Rosalind said. It came out harsher than intended. The woman looked up, eyes swollen, uncomprehending. "To the old ones. Under the mountain. You prayed to them."

"A hedge-priest." Her voice was scraped raw. "Passing through, weeks back. He said — he said the old gods still listened under Thornfell, if you were desperate enough to mean it. My girl's sick. Fever since the frost came. Nothing works. So I meant it." She laughed, a terrible small sound. "I meant it so hard. And now she's gone anyway. Out of her blankets, out of the tent, no tracks past the stream —"

"How old?"

"Seven."

Rosalind closed their eyes. Behind their eyelids they saw the wards shivering, nine layers deep, and felt again that slow animal warmth rising through the stone like something turning over in its sleep after forty years of dreaming of exactly this: an open door, a desperate voice, a soul offered up in perfect desperation. Old bargains needed only old words. And the old gods below had never once, in all their long hunger, been picky about whether the offering understood what she was giving.

"Show me where you last saw her."

They searched until moonrise. Rosalind walked the stream bank twice, reading the ground the way Wren read maps, and found what the others missed: a single line of small bare footprints heading *uphill*. Toward the track. Toward the seal. The prints were steady, unhurried, spaced like a child walking in her sleep — or like a child being *called*, gently, patiently, the way you call something you have already caught.

At the bend below the goat-track the prints simply stopped, as if the girl had stepped out of the world.

Rosalind stood in the freezing dark with torchlight scattering behind them and felt the truth settle over their shoulders like snow: the gate had answered a prayer for the first time in forty years, and it had answered *hungry*. The wards would need tending. The seal would need checking, word by word, layer by layer. And somewhere between here and tithe-night — six weeks off, at the dark of the winter moon — Rosalind was going to have to go down into the archive beneath the shrine and look at records they had spent four decades not looking at, because prayers woke memories, and memories woke debts, and debts, in Rosalind's long experience, always came due with interest.

Behind them, the mother was weeping. Ahead, the mountain breathed slow and warm and patient.

And down the slope, climbing fast with a storm-lantern held high and fear written plain across a face Rosalind had known since it was toothless — Wren was coming home early, and asking, even at fifty paces, in a voice that cracked on the second word:

"What's happened? Whose child is missing?"
