## 2. Wren's Debt

The brand woke on a Tuesday, which was the kind of ordinary cruelty the mountain specialized in.

Wren had crossed the Thornfell gate a thousand times before—slipping through the old stone arch with salt-bags on their back and Rosalind's maps folded into the hollows of memory where no customs man could search. The gate had never cared for them. It was a door, and doors did not care; they only opened or didn't, and Wren had spent twenty-six years learning every way to make this one open. The third stone from the left hummed when you pressed your palm to it. The wardline ran shallow along the eastern jamb, thin as an old woman's wrist. There was a gap in the wards' attention at dusk, a blind eye lasting perhaps forty breaths, and Wren could thread it in thirty.

But on that Tuesday, coming through at first light with frost still silvering the bracken, the brand on their left shoulder blade lit up like a coal slipped down their collar.

They dropped the salt. They went to one knee in the wet grass, breath punched out of them, and clawed at their own shirt like a man trying to put out a fire that was inside his skin. The pain was not burning, exactly. It was *recognition*—hot and specific and horribly attentive, the feeling of being seen by something that had been waiting a long time to see you.

When it faded—when Wren lay gasping in the bracken with dew soaking through to their chest—they pulled the shirt off over their head and twisted around to look.

They had known about the mark all their life, the way you know about a scar you can't remember earning. A small dark shape below the left shoulder blade, no bigger than a plum, smooth as if drawn in ink beneath the skin. As a child they'd asked Rosalind about it once, and once only. *Foundling's mark,* Rosalind had said, not looking up from the ledgers. *Some midwives press a sigil into the skin so the child can be claimed back if the mother changes her mind.* Then Rosalind had turned the page of the ledger very deliberately, and Wren, who had learned early which questions closed doors, had never asked again.

It looked different now. The edges had sharpened overnight—or perhaps Wren simply saw it clearly for the first time, the way you see a face in a crowd you've been walking past for years. It was not a plum-smudge. It was nine interlocking lines, wound tight as a knot of sleeping snakes, and at its center a single point that seemed, when Wren stared too long, to be staring back.

*Claimed back.* The words sat cold in their stomach.

They picked up the salt bags and went home and said nothing, because saying nothing was the family trade.

* * *

For six days Wren tested it, quietly, the way they tested everything—a lock picked twice before trusting it, a road walked both directions before mapping it.

Monday: through the gate at noon, carrying nothing but a canteen. The brand flared the moment they passed under the arch, a hot wire drawn across the shoulder blade, and cooled as soon as they were three strides beyond the wardline. Not distance, then. Threshold.

Wednesday: through at dusk, using the blind-eye gap, moving slow as honey so the wards wouldn't feel the seam of them passing. The brand flared anyway—softer, a banked ember rather than a struck match, but unmistakable. The wards weren't seeing them. Whatever marked them was announcing itself regardless, some part of the gate recognizing kin even when the gate itself was fooled.

Thursday they tried the shrine road instead, the long way around the mountain's shoulder where the stones were just stones and the only wards were the ones Rosalind maintained out of habit rather than need. Nothing. They stood in the cold wind above the valley with their heart hammering and felt almost giddy with relief. *It's the gate,* they told themselves. *It's only the gate. Some old thing in the stone doesn't like my blood, that's all. Mountains are full of old things.*

That was the story they carried home. They told it to themselves again while mending nets, again while counting coin, again lying awake in the loft above the drying shed listening to the wind worry the eaves. *Only the gate. Only an old thing.*

But stories, Wren knew better than anyone, are things smugglers build to move dangerous goods past careful eyes. And this story was moving something dangerous indeed—something with teeth—that they were trying to get past the one person who would know what it meant.

Rosalind would know. Rosalind knew everything about the mountain; it was less a talent than a condition, the way other people knew their own hands. Forty years of patrols, forty years of maps and ledgers and quiet, patient watching. If anyone alive understood what nine interlocked lines meant pressed beneath a foundling's skin—

—and there the thought always snagged, caught on a splinter too deep to pull free. Because if Rosalind knew what the mark meant, then why had Rosalind called it a *foundling's mark*? And why, in twenty-six years, had they never once looked at it straight?

Wren remembered that ledger-page turning now with terrible clarity. The deliberate slowness of it. People turn pages quickly to escape them. You turn them slowly when you're buying time to arrange your face.

They hated themselves for the thought. They buried it deep. They kept crossing the gate, because the work required it, and each time the brand burned, and each time they told the story a little thinner, a little more frayed, until it was less a story than a rag over a hole in the floor that they were very carefully not looking down.

* * *

On the seventh day, going up through the high passes with a load of untaxed wool, Wren found the hedge-priest dying in the lee of a cairn.

He was a small man folded smaller by age, wrapped in robes gone grey as the granite, and he was singing—not loudly, more a humming in the chest, the way old men hum when the mind has wandered off ahead of the body. But the tune stopped Wren dead between one step and the next, because it was the tune the mountain made. The low subterranean note you heard through bedrock in the deep ways, the sound Rosalind had always dismissed as water in flooded galleries. This man had it exactly, note for note, and he was singing it to the cairn as though serenading a door.

"Old man." Wren knelt, setting down the wool. "You're a long way from anywhere."

"Everywhere is a long way from here," the priest agreed placidly. His eyes were milk-pale but found Wren's face without trouble. "You're the one who crosses."

"I cross lots of things."

"The gate. The seal. You cross it weekly, and weekly it knows you." He smiled with few brown teeth. "I felt it wake. First prayer through the stone in forty years, and then—" he tapped his own shoulder, mirroring the place, "—a bell rang somewhere under the world. A claim-mark answering its master's house. I came to see whose it was."

The cold went through Wren like meltwater through a boot. "You're mad."

"Probably." He said it cheerfully. "Madness is mostly just hearing clearly in a room full of people pretending not to. Show me your shoulder, child."

Every sensible instinct said walk away. Leave the old man his cairn and his humming, take the wool down to Garrowby, collect the coin, tell no one anything. That was the trade. That was survival.

Wren pulled off their coat and shirt and turned their back to him.

The priest was silent a long moment. When he spoke again the cheer was gone out of his voice, and what remained was worse—gentleness, the tone people use at bedsides. "Ninefold knot," he murmured. "Sealed sovereign's signet. Oh, you poor creature. Who sold you?"

"Nobody sold me. I'm a foundling. Rosalind Villalobos took me in off a smuggler's boat when I was a week old."

"Mm," said the priest, in the voice of a man looking at a bill of sale. "And did the Villalobos ever serve a great house, before they took to keeping gates?"

The question landed like a hook behind the breastbone. Wren turned. "How could you possibly—"

"It's my work to know the debts of this mountain. Old work. Nearly finished work, thank the gods." He settled back against the cairn, and up close Wren could see how far gone he was—the grey lips, the shallowness of breath, death already sitting patient as a customs officer. "Listen, child, because I haven't much song left in me. That mark is not a foundling's mark. It's a claim-mark of the Court below—the Ninefold Court, the sealed ones, the takers-of-tithe. Your soul was signed away before you drew your first breath, and the contract sleeps until the mark wakes. And marks wake when the Court stirs. And the Court, these last weeks—" he laid his hand flat on the stone, on the humming stone, "—is stirring."

"You're wrong." Wren heard their own voice go flat and hard, the voice they used at knife-point negotiations. "There's no Court. There's stories to keep children from the mines. Rosalind says—"

"Rosalind says many things," the priest said mildly. "Forty years of saying them. Ask yourself why a keeper of a dead gate needs forty years of vigilance, if nothing is behind it." He coughed, wet and long, and waved off Wren's hand. "Tithe-night comes in six weeks. On tithe-night the contracts come due, and the Court collects what it holds. Unless the contract is voided, or the debt is paid, or—" another smile, terrible in its kindness, "—the holder of the debt chooses mercy. Do you understand me? Someone owns you, child. Find out who. Find out why they waited."

The wind came down off the pass and moved the wool bales and made the bracken hiss like whispering. Wren sat in it, shirtless in the cold, one hand pressed flat over their own shoulder blade as if they could hold the mark shut, keep it from speaking, from singing back to whatever was humming below.

"I don't believe you," they said at last. It came out small.

"I know." The priest patted the cairn fondly. "Belief is heavy cargo. No one carries it until they must. But you'll want to talk to your keeper, yes? Ask Rosalind what happened the year you were born. Ask about the Aldermarchs. Ask—" and here his breath caught, and the next words came out on the last of his strength, urgent as a shoved-open door, "—ask what Rosalind *witnessed*."

Then he was only breathing, and after a while not even that, and the mountain's low note went on underneath everything, patient as interest accruing.

Wren dressed slowly. Shouldered the wool. Stood a moment looking down the long switchback trail toward the valley, toward the smoke of home, toward the small figure—even now, probably—walking the gate's patrol with a lantern and a ledger and forty years of careful silence.

*Ask what Rosalind witnessed.*

"No," Wren said aloud, to the priest, to the mountain, to the knot of snakes beneath their shoulder that pulsed faintly warm against their palm. "Whatever it is, she was protecting me. She's always been protecting me. That's the whole of it. That's the only story there is."

The mountain, naturally, said nothing. It had already answered, seven days running, every time Wren crossed the line.

They went down toward home with the wool on their back and dread riding shotgun in their chest, rehearsing the question they were terrified to ask—and knowing, with a smuggler's certainty about hidden compartments, that Rosalind was already asking it too.
