## 3. What the Mountain Owes

The shrine had not been swept in three days, and Rosalind noticed.

It was a small thing—a scatter of pine needles across the threshold, a film of dust on the offering-stone where forty years of habit had kept the surface clean as a held breath—but small things were how mountains spoke to those who listened. Rosalind had spent their whole life listening. They knelt at the door with the broom they kept behind the loose stone and worked in silence while the evening light went amber through the shrine's single window, and they told themselves the neglect meant nothing. That the villagers were busy with the lambing. That Wren had been away too long on the high roads and no one else remembered whose duty this was.

That the wards beneath the floor had been humming for two nights like a struck bell that would not finish ringing.

Rosalind set the broom down. Looked once over both shoulders—the habit of a lifetime, though there was no one to see—and lifted the trapdoor hidden under the altar's shadow.

The stairs went down farther than any cellar had a right to. Forty-one steps, each one worn concave by feet older than the village, older than the Aldermarch name, older perhaps than the mountain itself. Rosalind counted them as always, lips moving without sound, because counting was a kind of prayer and because the alternative was to feel how deep the dark went. At the bottom, cold air rose to meet them, smelling of wet slate and tallow and something faintly sweet, like fruit rotting in a sealed room.

The archive was not grand. That had always been its protection. No vault door, no guardian—only a low room hewn from living rock, shelves of lead-edged cedar, and documents wrapped in oilcloth against the damp. The lords of the Ninefold Court below kept their treasures behind gates of singing bronze; the keepers above had learned long ago that the best hiding place for a dangerous truth was inside something boring enough to be forgotten.

Rosalind lit the lamp on its iron hook. The flame steadied, leaned toward the back wall, and steadied again—as if it too were nervous.

"I know," Rosalind murmured to it. "I know."

Forty years of keeper's records lined the shelves. Tithes paid, wards renewed, the small bribes and larger silences by which Thornfell gate had stayed shut since the last true keeper died. Rosalind had written most of them. Rosalind had inherited all of them. And somewhere among them—Rosalind had known this for forty years the way a body knows a wound it never looks at—was the record of a sale.

They did not go to it directly. They straightened ledgers that needed no straightening. They rewrapped three bundles whose cloth had come loose. They stood a long while before the shelf where the oldest contracts slept, and in that standing still, guilt rose in them the way drowned things rise: slowly at first, a pale shape far below the surface, then nearer, then breaking the water all at once, gasping and undeniable.

Wren had crossed the gate four days ago and come back white as milk, saying the stone had *sung* at them. Saying there was a mark on their shoulder burning like a coal. Rosalind had looked at that mark—the nine-pointed sigil pressed into the skin like a brand from a smithy of gods—and had lied. Had said, *old smuggler's ink, nothing more.* Had watched the child they'd raised believe them, because Wren had always believed them, and had felt something in their own chest crack along an old seam.

Now Rosalind unwrapped the bundle at the back of the oldest shelf.

The contract was vellum, fine as skin, and written in ink that had not faded so much as *settled*, the letters sunk into the page like stones into riverbed. Two columns. The left in the court-hand of the Aldermarch stewards, which Rosalind knew better than their own heartbeat, having copied it for eleven years. The right in a script that moved—slowly, like things moving under ice—if you looked away and looked back.

*Be it known,* the left column began, *that the house of Aldermarch, in satisfaction of debts incurred and honors owed, conveys unto the Ninefold Court one soul entire: issue of the foundling line, born under the sign of the gate, marked at birth by the Court's own seal.*

Below it, a date. Below that, the steward's signature—Lord Cassian Aldermarch himself, the flourish of his final letter trailing off the page like a man hurrying away from what he'd done.

And below *that*, in the right-hand script, the terms:

*Collection to be made upon the tithe-night following the gate's first hearing. The seal shall wake; the claim shall sound; the debt shall be gathered. Six weeks hence.*

Rosalind read it three times. Their hands did not shake until the third reading, and then they shook so badly the vellum whispered like wings.

*Six weeks.*

The prayer had come through the stone four days ago. The wards had shivered awake. Rosalind had called it mountain-madness, had called it imagination, had called it everything except what it was: the gate answering after forty years of silence, because the Court below had heard it too, and had begun to count the days.

And Wren—that sharp-eyed, soft-hearted stray Rosalind had pulled out of a snowdrift when they were both younger and stupider, who had grown up memorizing Rosalind's maps the way other children memorized lullabies—had been sold before they could walk. Sold by the family Rosalind had served. Sold with Rosalind's own lord's hand signing the paper, and Rosalind—

Rosalind closed their eyes.

They had been twenty-three. They remembered the day of the sale with the terrible clarity of a memory that has been avoided for decades and therefore never wore smooth. The steward's office, smelling of beeswax and fear. Lord Cassian at his desk, grey-faced, the Court's emissary standing in the corner where the candlelight refused to reach. A squalling infant brought in on a servant's hip, red-faced and furious at the world already, as if it knew.

*It's only a formality,* Cassian had said. To Rosalind, or to himself. *The Court collects slowly. It may be a hundred years.*

*A hundred years* —and Rosalind, young and ambitious and afraid of losing the only position that had ever been offered to someone with their name and no family, had said nothing. Had watched the emissary press its thumb—its grey, wrong-jointed thumb—to the infant's shoulder, and had said *nothing*, and had taken the promotion that came in spring.

Then the plague came, and the Aldermarch line thinned to nothing, and the foundling was abandoned in the chaos, and Rosalind—who by then had fled north with whatever secrets they could carry—had found the child again, or been found, depending on how you believed such things worked. And had raised them. And had told themselves every winter that the debt was old, the Court dormant, the seal holding, and that love could sit comfortably on top of a lie if you built the floor thick enough.

Guilt is patient. Guilt had waited forty years, and now it stood in the lamplight wearing a child's face.

* * *

Rosalind did not remember deciding to burn it. They simply became aware, some minutes into the dark, that they were kneeling at the archive's iron brazier with the contract in both hands and the lamp's flame already licking at the lower edge.

The vellum resisted. Of course it did—it was a legal instrument of the Ninefold Court, and such things do not die easily. The fire crawled across it grudgingly, and where it burned, the writing screamed. Not loudly. In the voice of dry leaves, of pages turning in an empty room, of a name being read out in a hall very far below the ground. The right-hand script fought hardest, letters writhing off the page like worms on a hook, and one phrase burned last of all, blackening syllable by syllable while Rosalind held on and did not let go:

*THE COURT ALREADY KNOWS.*

The ash drifted up. It should have fallen; ash falls. But it rose instead, spiraling toward the ceiling crack where the mountain breathed, and for a moment it hung there in the shape—Rosalind would swear this later, swearing it to no one—of nine points. A sigil. An acknowledgment. A receipt.

Then it was gone, sucked up into the stone, and the archive was only an archive again, and Rosalind was only an old keeper kneeling in front of an empty brazier with singed fingers and a heart going like a drum at a hanging.

Burning the copy changed nothing. They had known that before they struck the flame. The original lay in the Court's vault below, behind gates of singing bronze, and a copy destroyed above ground was worth precisely as much as a word unspoken. But the hands wanted to do *something*, and burning was the only honest motion available, and so the hands had burned.

What mattered—what mattered with the weight of six weeks falling on them like a closing door—was the rest of it. Tithe-night. Collection. The soul of Wren Villalobos-Thackeray, claimed at birth, due to be gathered like a sheaf at harvest, unless someone went down the old roads and took it back.

Rosalind sat on the cold floor of the archive for a long time. The lamp burned low. Above them, muffled through forty-one steps and a trapdoor and a floor of packed earth, they could hear the shrine creaking in the night wind, and beneath even that—so faint it might have been blood in their own ears—the wards were still humming.

Not humming anymore, they realized. Singing. Quietly, steadily, in a key no human throat could hold: the sound of a gate remembering what it was for.

"Six weeks," Rosalind said aloud, and their voice cracked on the second word like river ice in spring.

There were roads. There were always roads—Rosalind knew nine ways into the deep places that no map recorded, because Rosalind had drawn the maps that didn't record them. There was Brannic, who owed them a life-debt he'd never named and drank to forget. There was Sefa, who could unravel a ward the way other people unpicked a knot, and little Pip, who could open anything with a lock on it. And there was Wren, who had to know. Who deserved to know, though the knowing might cost Rosalind the only thing they had left: the way those dark eyes looked at them, trusting, believing, *home*.

Truth or loyalty. The mountain had finally asked its question, and it wanted an answer in six weeks or less.

Rosalind climbed the stairs. Counted them. Did not look back at the empty brazier.

But at the top, with one hand on the trapdoor, they felt it—the faintest tremor through the wood, through the stone, through the soles of their boots. Far below, past the sealed gate, past the sleeping courts, something vast had turned over in its slumber. Something had read the ashes. Something knew that the keeper above had finally, after forty years, begun to move.

The wards sang a little louder.

Rosalind blew out the lamp, closed the shrine behind them, and walked down into the village under the rising moon to find Wren—before the mountain came looking for what it was owed.
