## 4. A Family of Strays

The safehouse above the Salt Lantern was the kind of place Wren had learned to love in pieces: a room with a crooked chimney, a floor that remembered every footstep, and a window that looked out on Thornfell's lower terraces where the mountain smoke rose thin and blue into the dusk. It smelled of tallow, wet wool, and Sefa's incense—the cheap kind, resin-and-ash, that she burned when she was working a debt or hiding from one.

Wren stood at the window with their hand pressed flat against their shoulder, against the brand beneath three layers of shirt.

It had not stopped burning since the hedge-priest died. Not hot, exactly—hot they could have borne—but *awake*, the way a limb is awake when it's been slept on wrong, full of pins and slow lightning. They had scrubbed at it in the canal water until Brannic hauled them out by the collar like a drowned cat. They had let Sefa look at it, which was worse, because Sefa had gone very quiet and then said only, *I know this sigilwork. I've seen it in ledgers.* And Sefa did not say things like that unless the thing she'd seen was bad.

Now the others were coming.

Wren heard Pip first—of course they did, you always heard Pip first—a scramble on the drainpipe, a soft thump on the roof, then the particular creak of the third stair that meant someone light was pretending to be heavier than they were so as not to startle anyone. Then boots on the lane: Brannic's heavy tread, unhurried, the tread of a man who had once been paid to be somewhere before everyone else and now took his time about everything out of spite for his former self. Last came Sefa, her footsteps nearly silent, her presence arriving like weather.

They filed in. Brannic filled the doorway the way he always did, half in shadow, gray at the temples, the old scar across his knuckles white in the lamplight. Pip dropped onto the windowsill and hung there upside down by their knees, thirteen years old and boneless as rope, watching Wren with eyes too old for the face. Sefa settled cross-legged on the floorboards and began, without being asked, to lay out her reading-cloth: coins, knucklebones, a sliver of grave-glass.

"Sit," Wren said, and their voice came out strange. "Or don't. But listen."

Brannic sat. He sat the way he did everything—like a wall deciding to be a chair—and waited.

* * *

"I'm going to tell you something," Wren said, "and when I'm done, some of you are going to want to leave. That's allowed. I mean it. There's bread in the tin and coin in the jar for whoever goes, and no one will think less—" They stopped. Swallowed. Started again, because lying to these three was like lying to mirrors. "No. Someone will think less. I'll think less. But you'll be alive, and that matters more than my opinion of you."

Pip righted themselves slowly on the sill.

"The brand on my shoulder," Wren said, and pulled up their shirt, and turned, and let the lamplight find it.

They had never shown it to all three at once. It was an ugly thing even now, even understood: a nine-pointed sigil seared into the skin below the shoulder blade, its lines fine as engraving, older than any scar Wren had earned honestly. As they turned, it pulsed—once, gently, like a heart remembering its work—and Pip made a small sound and Brannic went still in a way that made the whole room go still around him.

"Aldermarch sold me." Wren kept their voice level. Kept it flat as a ledger line. "Before I could walk. Before I had a name anyone wrote down. The family Rosalind used to serve—they held the contracts for the Court under Thornfell, back when the Court still collected. My soul went into their books the day I was born. Price of a debt. And the Court's woken up, and tithe-night is six weeks off, and on tithe-night they come to collect what's owed."

The silence afterward had texture. It had weight. It sat on Wren's chest like a cat that did not love them.

"So," Wren said. "That's the truth. I wanted you to hear it from me and not from the wards, not from rumor, not from Rosalind's face. I'm going down after it. Into the deep ways, past the gate, all the way to the vault if I have to. And I'm asking—not ordering, asking—if any of you will come."

* * *

Brannic spoke first, which surprised no one; Brannic always spoke first when silence hurt him.

"No," he said.

The word fell into the room like a dropped anvil.

"No?" Pip said.

"No." Brannic leaned forward, elbows on knees, and looked at the floor as though it were a battlefield map. "You're asking me to walk into a court of gods with a knight who ran. You know what I am, girl. Boy. *Wren.* You know what I did at Coldermarch. I held a gate once already in my life. One gate, one night, one order to hold—and I turned and ran and eleven people died behind me because I wasn't there." His voice didn't break. It did something worse; it went quiet. "Every door I've stood in since, I've been waiting for it to be that door again. And you want me standing at yours."

"Yes," Wren said.

"You should want literally anyone else."

"There is no one else. There's you. There's the crew. There's the family." Wren crossed the room and stood in front of him, close enough to see the tremor he thought he was hiding. "Brannic. I'm not asking you to be the knight you were. I'm asking you to hold a door for people you love. That's all any knight ever actually does. The rest is embroidery."

Brannic looked up at them. For a long moment nothing moved in him at all, and Wren thought: *there it is, the wall, he'll stay behind it.* But then something shifted—the smallest collapse, a single stone rolling free—and he said, gruffly, "Someone has to carry the lantern. Your arms are too skinny for lantern-work," and that was yes, and everyone knew it was yes, and no one said so, because saying so would have broken him like glass.

* * *

Sefa did not refuse. Sefa negotiated.

She laid her cards on the floor between them—three coins, two bones, the grave-glass—and said, "Understand what you're asking. The Court doesn't deal in coin. It deals in *value*. Names, memories, oaths, first loves, the taste of your mother's cooking. Everything you carry down there is currency, and everything you spend is gone, and you don't get receipts." She turned a knucklebone over with one finger. "I'm a debt-witch, Wren. Do you know how I got that title? I bought it. With what, do you think?"

Nobody answered.

"With the memory of my sister's laugh," Sefa said. "I know I had a sister. I know I loved her. I know she laughed, because the ledger says so—it says *payment received: one (1) laughter, sister's, genuine*. I read the entry every year on her name-day. That's all I have of her. An entry." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "So no, I won't refuse. Refusing is for people who haven't already paid worse. But I'm telling you the price going in, so that when we're in the dark and something asks you for the sound of your own name, you understand that it *will collect*, and it does not care what you'll miss."

"Sefa—" Wren started.

"Don't thank me." She gathered up her cards with sharp, precise movements. "Thanking is also currency. Save it for something you can afford."

But later, when the lamps were low, Wren saw her looking at them—at Wren specifically, at the brand, at the shape of them against the window—with an expression they couldn't read, an expression like someone counting coins they hadn't decided to spend yet.

* * *

Pip refused last, and loudest, and least convincingly.

"This is stupid," they announced from the windowsill, upside down again, arms folded. "This is the stupidest plan in the history of plans, and I've *heard* plans, I live in a city of plans. You want to rob *gods*. Gods don't even have pockets. What are we robbing? Where's the vault? Who's the mark? This isn't a job, it's a funeral with extra steps."

"Pip—"

"I'm *thirteen*." Their voice cracked on it, and they hated that it cracked, and they talked faster to cover the crack. "I'm a lockchild. I open doors. Rich people's doors, merchant doors, temple doors—doors that lead to *money*. Doors where the worst thing behind them is a dog or a bored guard. You're describing a door with a god behind it, and I'm supposed to pick it? With what? Feelings?"

"With your hands," Wren said quietly. "Same as always."

Pip unfolded from the sill and landed on their feet, and for a moment they just stood there, small and furious and scared in the lamplight, and Wren watched them try to find the shape of their own refusal and fail to hold it.

"My nan used to say," Pip muttered finally, staring at the floor, "that nobody comes for foundlings. Nobody comes down the well, nobody opens the cage, you just—you make yourself small and useful and maybe somebody keeps you." They kicked at a floorboard. "You came for me. Off the Aldermarch barge, in the rain, when I bit you."

"You drew blood. It was impressive."

"And now you want me to come for you, and that's not *fair*—" Pip's voice broke properly this time, and they wiped their face angrily with their sleeve, and Brannic, wordlessly, held out a handkerchief the size of a tablecloth. "Fine. FINE. I'll pick your stupid god-door. But if I die, you have to haunt whoever taught you planning skills, because it clearly wasn't anybody good."

"That's a binding oath," Sefa said. "Witnessed."

"*Shut up*, Sefa."

* * *

Later—much later, when Brannic had banked the fire and Sefa had wrapped herself in her shawl like a moth and Pip had fallen asleep sitting up, mid-argument, and slid sideways onto a cushion—Rosalind arrived.

Wren heard them on the stairs, that careful weight, and opened the door before the knock came. Rosalind stood in the hallway with snow in their hair and forty years of secrets in their face, and for a moment neither of them spoke.

"They said yes," Wren whispered. "All of them. Idiots. All of them said yes."

Rosalind's mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling. "Of course they did. Strays always say yes. It's the whole trouble with strays." They stepped inside, dripping, and looked around the lamplit room—at the sleeping child, the silent witch, the disgraced knight keeping watch by habit even in sleep—and something in their expression softened into something almost like grief. "When I was young, I thought family was a thing you were given. Contracts and bloodlines. Ledgers." A pause. "I've since learned it's a thing you build, and the building never stops, and it costs more than anything on any Court ledger."

Wren leaned against the doorframe, shoulder throbbing its slow awake-throb. "Then it's settled. We go together."

"It's settled," Rosalind said. But their eyes went to the fire, to the dark beyond the window, to the mountain looming over all of it, and their voice, when it came again, was quieter than the snow. "There's a road I haven't walked in forty years. It starts behind the shrine, under the roots of the old yew. Tomorrow at dawn, I'll show you why they call it the Weeping Stair."

Outside, far up the black flank of Thornfell, something ancient turned over in its sleep—and every ward on the gate sang one low, clear note, like a bell rung by a finger of frost.
