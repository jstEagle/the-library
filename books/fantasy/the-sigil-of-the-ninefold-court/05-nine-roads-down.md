## 5. Nine Roads Down

The first smuggler's road into the deep ways opened behind a waterfall that no longer fell.

That was the first sign the mountain was waking. In forty years of keeping the Thornfell gate, I had known the falls to freeze, to thin to a whisper in drought years, but never—never—to simply stop, hanging in the air like a curtain of glass someone had forgotten to take down. The water stood suspended in a hundred silver threads, and behind it, dry as a held breath, waited a door of black stone that I had last seen when my hair was brown.

"Is it supposed to do that?" Pip whispered, pressing one small finger against a droplet that hung unmoving an inch from their nose.

"No." I said it too quickly. "The old roads answer to the mountain's moods. The mountain is in a mood."

Sefa made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been a prayer. She'd been doing more praying since we left the shrine, her debt-witch fingers worrying the knotted cord at her wrist like a rosary of everything she owed. Wren stood closest to me, as they always did on the threshold of anything dangerous, their shoulder—the branded shoulder—turned deliberately away, as if they could keep the mark from noticing where we were going.

"It knows we're here," Brannic said. He said everything flatly, the way men speak who have learned not to hope out loud. His hand rested on the pommel of a sword he hadn't drawn in six years, not since the disgrace, but he carried it anyway, and I understood why. Some weights you carry because putting them down would mean admitting you were carrying them at all.

"The road doesn't know," I told them, and heard the lie in my own voice before it finished leaving my mouth. The road knew. The mountain knew. Something beneath the mountain, older than the Ninefold Court itself, had felt our boots on its skin the moment we stepped off the pilgrim track. "Stay close. Answer honestly if you're asked a question. And whatever you see—whatever you *hear*—do not stop walking unless I tell you."

"And if you don't tell us?" Pip asked.

I looked at each of them: the lockchild with chalk dust still under their fingernails; Sefa with her knots; Brannic with his unworn armor of silence; Wren, who had every one of my maps memorized and still walked half a step behind me, as though the maps might change if they blinked. My family. Built out of strays and secrets, and now marching straight into the dark to steal back one of their souls.

"If I don't tell you," I said, "then run."

* * *

The road began gently, the way all lies begin.

It was wide enough for two abreast, floored in smooth river-stone, lit by veins of pale lichen that glowed like drowned moonlight. I had walked it twice in my youth, in service to the Aldermarch family, when I was still the kind of person who believed contracts could be honorable. It had changed. The walls had grown ribs—arches of living rock that flexed almost imperceptibly, like the flanks of something breathing in its sleep.

We made good time for three hours. Long enough for Pip to start a game of counting stalactites, long enough for Sefa to teach them a debt-witch's counting song to keep the rhythm of our steps, long enough for me to almost forget what these roads did to travelers.

Then the lichen dimmed, and the first trial found us.

It came as a voice—not from ahead or behind, but from inside the stone itself, from everywhere, patient as erosion. It did not speak words so much as it *asked*, and what it asked was different for each of us. I knew this because I watched it happen.

Pip went first. The lockchild stopped mid-step, eyes going glassy and far away, and their mouth moved around words too quiet to catch. Whatever the mountain pulled from Pip's oldest hiding place, it took gently; after a minute the child shivered, wiped their nose, and kept walking, looking smaller and somehow steadier, like a lock finally turned to its true setting.

Sefa lasted longer against hers. I saw her grip her knot-cord until the skin of her knuckles went white, saw her lips form *no, no, no* in three languages. But the mountain does not argue. It waits. And after a long while Sefa bowed her head, and tears cut clean lines through the grime on her face, and she whispered something that ended with *"—and that's why I never went home,"* and then she was walking again, hollowed and lighter, the way people are lighter after vomiting.

Wren I watched hardest. But whatever the mountain asked of them, Wren answered with their jaw set and their chin up, quick and defiant, and passed through it in less than a minute. The brand on their shoulder glowed through their shirt like a coal wrapped in linen, and I wondered—if the Court had claimed their soul before they drew their first breath, what lie could the mountain possibly find in them? They hadn't lived long enough, back then, to bury anything.

Maybe that was the mercy of it. Maybe that was the horror.

Brannic went last.

* * *

The knight stopped walking so suddenly that Pip nearly collided with his back. The lichen-light died altogether then, all along the corridor, and in the darkness I heard the mountain ask him something in a voice like a door opening very slowly.

"No," Brannic said.

Stone ground against stone. Ahead of us, the corridor narrowed—I felt it rather than saw it, the way the air pressure changed, the way the breathing rock contracted.

"Brannic," I said quietly. "Answer it."

"I've answered enough questions in my life."

The walls tightened. Somewhere above us, pebbles began to fall like fat rain. The mountain wasn't angry—it didn't have anger any more than a scale has malice—but it had weight, and weight has opinions, and right now its opinion was that we would go no further until the knight set down what he was carrying.

I moved through the blackness to his side. I couldn't see him, but I could hear him breathing, short and controlled, the breathing of a man holding a door shut with his body.

"Whatever it asked," I said, "the truth won't kill you. I promise you that much."

"You can't promise that."

"I've walked this road twice. No one dies of honesty down here. They die of *carrying*."

Silence. Then, barely audible, in a voice stripped of all its flatness, all its armor: "You don't know what I did."

"I know you're a disgraced knight who follows a smuggler and a witch and a child into the roots of a mountain for a soul that isn't even yours. Whatever else there is, I doubt it weighs more than that."

A sound escaped him then—half laugh, half sob—and I think it surprised him more than it did me. And then Brannic spoke to the darkness, and the darkness carried it to all of us:

"There was a bridge. At Caelmoor. We were ordered to hold it so the villagers could cross, and we held it, and then the order came to burn it with the last forty people still on it, because the enemy was close behind them, and burning it would slow the enemy down." His voice cracked like ice on a pond. "I gave the order to light the pitch. Not my commander. Me. Because my commander's hand was shaking and mine wasn't, and I thought—that was the thought, I swear it on anything you want—that being the one whose hands don't shake was the same as being the one who's right."

The mountain exhaled. I felt it in my teeth.

"They call me a deserter," Brannic said, quieter now. "That's the official word. That's the easy word. I wish to God I'd only deserted."

For a moment nothing moved. Then the lichen-kindling caught all at once, veins of pale light flooding the corridor, and the walls drew back, and the road lay open before us, wider than it had been, as though the mountain had made room.

Brannic stood swaying. Pip reached up without a word and took his gauntleted hand, and the big man let them, which told me more about what he'd just survived than his whole confession had.

"That's why you never wear the sword," Wren said softly.

"That's why I always do." He looked at me. "Keeper. You said no one dies of honesty."

"I said they die of carrying. You've put some of it down. There'll be room for the rest later." I turned up the road before my own expression could betray me, because I could feel the mountain turning its attention my way, patient as erosion, and I had forty years of lies stacked up like cordwood, and I did not intend to feed it a single one before I absolutely had to.

* * *

We camped that night in a chamber shaped like a cupped hand, where a spring rose out of bare rock, hot enough to fog the air, sweet enough to drink. Sefa bound Brannic's rope-burned palms. Pip slept curled against the knight's breastplate like a cat against a warm stove. Wren sat beside me at the chamber's mouth, watching the lichen-veins pulse slow and rhythmic in the walls.

"It's a heartbeat," Wren said. "The whole road. Did you notice?"

"I noticed."

"We're walking down something's throat, Roz." They used the name only family used. "And you keep saying *stay close, answer honest, don't stop walking*. Like you've done this before. Like you know exactly how far it is to the Court, and what it costs, and you're rationing the telling of it." They weren't accusing. That was the worst part. Wren never accused; they simply observed, the way they'd learned to read my maps, line by line, until nothing on them was hidden. "What haven't you told us?"

The spring murmured. Somewhere below us—far below, past nine roads' worth of stone—the Court was counting down to tithe-night, and I was carrying a contract with Wren's name on it burned into my memory deeper than any brand, and a second secret underneath that one, heavier, older, the one I had promised myself I would confess on my own terms or die unconfessed.

"Not yet," I said. "Soon. Before tithe-night, I swear it. But not while we're standing inside something that's listening."

Wren studied me for a long moment. Then they nodded slowly and leaned their head briefly against my shoulder, the way they had when they were small and the night sounds frightened them.

"When we're out of earshot of gods, then," they said. "You owe me that."

The spring murmured agreement, or maybe it was the mountain. And far down the dark throat of the road, faint as a bell rung underwater, something below began to sing—a market-song, many-voiced, calling buyers and sellers alike toward a bazaar where the only coin worth carrying was the pieces of yourself you could bear to lose.
