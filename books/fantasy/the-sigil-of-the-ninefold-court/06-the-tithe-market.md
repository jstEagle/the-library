## 6. The Tithe-Market

The way down to the Tithe-Market smelled of cold iron and old honey.

Sefa had walked debt-roads all her life—had knelt in counting-houses while clerks weighed her mother's sins against her own small name—but she had never descended a stair like this one. It spiraled inside the throat of the mountain itself, and each step rang faintly underfoot, a note too low for ears and felt instead in the teeth. Brannic went first with his shield slung across his back like a coffin lid. Pip rode in Wren's arms, half-asleep, fingers twitching through the shapes of locks even in dreams. Rosalind came last with a lantern hooded in green glass, and the light it threw was the color of deep water, of things that had learned patience at the bottom of lakes.

"Rules," Rosalind said, and their voice had the flat calm Sefa had come to recognize as the sound of fear held very still. "You don't give your true name to anyone. You don't laugh at what's for sale—you'll see things that were stolen from people you'd weep for. And whatever you trade, trade knowing you won't get it back. Nothing here is lent. Everything here is kept."

"And if someone tries to buy from us without asking?" Brannic rumbled.

"Then they're not merchants," Rosalind said. "Run."

The stair opened without warning into vastness.

Sefa's breath left her in a slow ribbon of white. The market filled a cavern so wide its ceiling was only a rumor, hung with lanterns of every color except green—the keepers' color, Rosalind had said, forbidden below—and beneath those lights sprawled stalls of black wood and bone-white canvas, hundreds of them, tiered down into the dark like the seats of an amphitheatre where the spectacle was memory itself. The crowd moved between them in murmuring rivers: deepfolk with lamp-eyes, veiled women whose hems trailed frost, things Sefa could not name and made herself not study. Above it all, woven through the air like smoke, rose a sound—not music, not quite. A thousand voices humming fragments. Every soul in the market, she realized, was quietly humming something they had sold or hoped to buy. The whole cavern was a choir of missing pieces.

"Oh," Pip breathed, waking in Wren's arms. "Oh, I hate how much I love this."

* * *

They found the passage-broker three tiers down, in a stall draped with chains of copper bells that never quite rang. The broker was a narrow man with a ledger bound in gray leather and eyes the yellow of tallow candles. He did not look up when they arrived.

"Nine Roads Down ends here," he said. "Beyond my booth is the Silent Stair, and the Silent Stair goes nowhere unless I say otherwise. Passage for five to the Court's outer vault. What have you?"

Sefa stepped forward before anyone else could, because this was her country, this arithmetic of loss. "Passage first," she said. "Price second."

The broker's gaze lifted, and it snagged on her the way a fishhook snags on wool. "A debt-witch. How rare you've become. You know what I deal in."

"Memories," Sefa said. "Names. Years. Firsts." She kept her voice level as a scale. "I know your rates better than you think, broker. A common memory—a face from childhood, a meal, a rainstorm—buys a day's passage. Something load-bearing costs more."

"Load-bearing." The broker smiled with too many teeth. "I do enjoy professionals."

What followed was an hour of the most careful conversation Sefa had ever conducted. Brannic paid first, jaw set, giving up the taste of the victory feast he'd earned the day he won his spurs—the taste only, the rest of the day remaining—and when he finished he stood blinking as though the world had a hole in it shaped like bread and wine. Pip traded the memory of their mother's lullaby and wept about it afterward in a way that was almost cheerful, singing a nonsense tune into the gap to fill it. Wren—

Wren frightened Sefa most. Wren bargained like someone who had already decided everything they owned was forfeit, offering a scarred knuckle's worth of years, a stolen afternoon, the smell of a particular harbor, each piece surrendered with a shrug that said *it wasn't doing me any good anyway*. As if they had been preparing their whole life to be spent. Sefa wanted to shake them. She wanted instead, terribly, to outbid the world for them, and she had nothing to bid with but the thing she was already hiding behind her ribs.

The broker turned last to Rosalind, and the yellow eyes narrowed. "Keeper," he said softly. "Forty years of gate. They say your memory is a fortress with no doors. What would you pay?"

"Nothing today," Rosalind said, and the green lantern swung once, deliberately. "But I know what lies past the Silent Stair, broker, and I know what the Court keeps in its outer vault besides souls. When this is done, I'll bring you the ward-names of the tithe-doors. Freely given."

The broker went very still. Then he laughed, a dry rustle like paper turning. "The Court will unmake you for less."

"The Court intends to unmake us regardless. I prefer to be useful first." He drew a key of black iron from his sleeve and laid it on the ledger. "Silent Stair. Five passages. Don't speak on it. The stair remembers voices."

* * *

They had an hour before the stair's next turning, and the crew scattered into the market like coins from a cut purse—Brannic to watch the exits, Rosalind to question a lantern-seller in a language that sounded like water over stones, Pip vanishing instantly toward a stall that sold, impossibly, the sensation of falling.

Which left Sefa alone with the memory-mongers.

She told herself she was gathering intelligence. That was even partly true. She drifted along the memory-row with a witch's practiced stillness, reading stalls the way other folk read faces: *first snowfalls, three coppers; the name of a dead brother, sealed in wax, price negotiable; twenty years of a marriage, the good years only, buyer must take both grief and joy together.* In one booth a woman sat selling her own laughter, coin by coin, and laughed beautifully at nothing between customers. In another, a child no older than Pip hawked bottled courage to soldiers going down to the Court's procession.

And there—tier seven, a modest stall run by a crone with riverweed braided into her hair—Sefa saw it displayed in a shallow dish of glass:

*A memory of Wren.*

Not Wren themselves. A memory *of* them: a moment, held in amber light above the dish, visible to any buyer who leaned close. Sefa leaned close and her heart stopped and then began again wrong.

It was the night Wren had first called the crew family. Not in words—Wren had never said it in words—but on the roof of the Gilded Rat, passing around a bottle of plum brandy too expensive for any of them, and Wren had looked at the four of them laughing in the smoky dark and worn an expression Sefa had carried privately in her chest ever since like a coal banked in ash: *belonging, astonished.* The memory shimmered with it. Anyone could see it. Anyone could buy it.

"How?" Sefa managed.

The crone shrugged. "Folk shed memories here without knowing. The market sweeps. Swept pieces go to whoever found them." Her eyes were shrewd and not unkind. "You want it back, girl? Rare, that. Most folk sell. Almost nobody buys."

Sefa should have walked away. She was a debt-witch; she knew better than anyone alive that a price unpaid becomes a chain. But the memory of Wren sat in its dish looking lost, and Sefa thought of Pip singing nonsense into the hole where a lullaby used to be, and she understood suddenly, with terrible clarity, that she could not walk down the Silent Stair carrying this. Some debts you pay before they're asked.

"What's your rate," she said, "for something load-bearing?"

* * *

She chose carefully. That was the discipline of her order: never pay with what you can't spare, and never let the payer choose blindly. She laid her memories out on the crone's counter like a hand of cards—the taste of her grandmother's bitter tea, the first spell she ever cast alone, the exact blue of a sky over a village that no longer existed—and the crone weighed each with two fingers and shook her head.

"Not enough. Not for that one." A nod at the dish. "That memory is dense. Loved-in. See how the light sits heavy in it?"

Sefa looked at the amber glow and knew it was true. She had one more card, and she had known since the stairway which card it was, and she hated herself with a clean, efficient hatred as she reached for it.

There was a morning, three years ago. Wren feverish after a job gone wrong, delirious in the loft of the Gilded Rat, and Sefa sitting up all night drawing the fever down thread by thread, and somewhere before dawn Wren had surfaced just enough to say—clearly, lucidly, eyes open and fixed on hers—"If I die, don't let them bury me facing the mountain. I want to go out looking at the road home." And then, softer: "You're my home, Seff. All of you. Don't forget I said it."

Sefa had built half her life since on that sentence. It was load-bearing. It was the keystone.

"I'll pay with a memory of Wren," she told the crone, and her voice did not shake, because she was a professional. "One of mine. My choosing."

The crone studied her a long moment. "You understand the shape of what you're buying back. If you return it to yourself, you'll carry it twice—heavier than carrying it once. If you free it—" she gestured at the amber, "—it goes back to the market. Or to the person it's *about*, if they've the sense to claim it."

"Sefa!" Wren's voice, from three tiers up, bright and impatient and utterly unaware. "Stair's turning! Seff!"

"Free it," Sefa said quickly. "Toward the person it's about. Someday. However that happens."

The transaction took eleven seconds. The crone pressed her thumb to Sefa's brow, and something slid out of her—quietly, without pain, the way a splinter leaves skin you'd forgotten held one—and for a heartbeat Sefa stood in her own skull feeling a roominess she didn't trust, a smooth wall where a door had been. She knew a memory of Wren had been there. She could feel the absence the way you feel a missing tooth with your tongue: a fact, an outline, an ache with no content. *Something about a morning,* she thought. *Something important. Something I paid too much for.*

She picked up the dish-memory of Wren's belonging, astonished face glowing in its amber shell, and slipped it into the inner pocket of her coat, against her heart, where the weight of it sat exactly like guilt.

"You didn't have to," the crone said, almost gently. "Whatever it was."

"Yes, I did," Sefa said. "That's rather the point of me."

She climbed the three tiers with her face arranged into professional calm, and when Wren fell into step beside her—warm, alive, complaining about the price of bottled courage—she smiled and answered and did not tell them that somewhere in her coat they were traveling in amber, bought back from the dark at a cost Sefa could no longer remember paying. The coal was gone from her chest. She noticed the coldness of it only in the way you notice weather.

Above the Silent Stair's mouth, Rosalind waited with the green lantern, and the keeper's expression stopped Sefa mid-step. Rosalind was staring down into the market with a look Sefa had seen once before on a drowning woman: recognition.

"That lantern-seller," Rosalind said quietly, to no one, to everyone. "He knew my name. My *whole* name." Their knuckles whitened on the lantern's handle. "The Court isn't waiting for tithe-night to learn we're coming. It already sent invitations."

Below them, far down in the humming dark, every bell in the passage-broker's booth began to ring at once.
