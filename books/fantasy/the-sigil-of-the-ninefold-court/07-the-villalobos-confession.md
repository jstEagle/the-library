## 7. The Villalobos Confession

The fire had burned down to a red heart by the time Wren came to relieve the watch.

They always did it this way on the long descents—one of them waking while the other slept, trading the dark between them like a debt neither would name. Rosalind heard Wren's boots on the scree before they saw the shape of them: that particular light step, heel never quite landing, learned in childhood from a woman who had spent forty years teaching foundlings how to be unheard. Their own lessons. Coming back to them now like knives returning to a whetstone.

"You should sleep," Wren said, settling onto the flat stone across the fire. Above them the mountain's throat went up and up into blackness; below, the road they'd taken that morning coiled away into the deep ways, where Brannic's confession still hung in the air like smoke no wind could move. "You look like something the archive spat out."

"The archive did spit me out." Rosalind fed a twist of dry moss to the coals. The light jumped, caught the lines of Wren's face—the jaw they'd first seen smeared with goat's milk, the eyes that had gone from milk-blue to storm-grey over nineteen years. "Wren. I need to tell you something, and I need you to let me finish it."

Wren's hand stilled where it had been loosening their bootlace. In the firelight Rosalind watched every wall go up at once, brick by brick, the way walls do in people who learned young that news arriving after midnight was never good.

"That sounds like a confession," Wren said.

"It is."

* * *

Rosalind had rehearsed it all day—on the road, at the water stop where Sefa filled the skins in silence, at the moment when Pip asked why the old gods' trials smelled like tallow and Rosalind hadn't answered. They had composed sentences in their head, orderly as ledger entries, each one leading cleanly to the next. But sitting here with Wren's face lit orange and waiting, every sentence burned away and left only the thing itself, raw and shapeless, like meat pulled too soon from the fire.

"Forty-two years ago," Rosalind began, "I served the Aldermarch house. Not as a servant. As a keeper-in-training. My mother had been keeper of the Thornfell gate before me, and her mother before her, and when I was seventeen they took me into the under-shrine for the first time and showed me what the family kept there." A breath. "Contracts. Souls. Debts owed to the Court beneath us, signed in blood older than any kingdom you've ever smuggled through."

"I know about the contracts," Wren said quietly. "Sefa told us about the tithe-market. I know what the Court collects."

"You know *what*. Not who." Rosalind turned their hands toward the fire. Old hands now. Keeper's hands, scarred from wards and winters. "There was a midwife in Aldermarch village, the winter of the great fever. Families who couldn't feed another mouth would come to her, and she would bring them to the house, and the house would pay their debts and take the child's soul as surety—a claim-mark laid at birth, collected on the child's thirtieth year or upon death, whichever the Court preferred. It was called the Mercy Ledger. Everyone called it mercy."

Wren's bootlace hung forgotten. "I was born in that village."

"Yes."

"And my mother—"

"Couldn't feed you. The fever had taken your father and two brothers already. She came to the house in the ninth month of winter, carrying you wrapped in a blanket that had been dyed twice to hide the holes." Rosalind made themselves keep going, made themselves walk into it the way they'd walked into the archive weeks ago, knowing what waited at the bottom of the stairs. "I was in the under-shrine that night. Copying ward-sigils. I saw her sign. I saw the mark laid on your shoulder—I heard you cry when it burned, though you were only hours old. And I heard Lord Aldermarch say—" Here the memory did what it always did, rising like a drowned thing breaking surface, bloated and unmistakable. "He said, *a strong-lunged one. The Court pays well for those.*"

The fire cracked. Somewhere far above, water dripped in the mountain's throat, patient as a clock.

"So you knew," Wren said. "From the beginning. From the goat shed, from the maps, from all of it—you knew what I was carrying on my shoulder."

"I knew," Rosalind said.

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"Because telling you would have killed you."

"Don't." The word came out sharp enough to draw blood. "Don't dress it up as protection. You've been protecting yourself for forty years, Rosalind. I've seen how you move through the world—invisible, unremarkable, a keeper nobody remembers meeting. That's not caution. That's craft."

"It is both." Rosalind leaned forward, elbows on knees, and let the heat of the coals press against their face like a hand. "Listen to me. Hear all of it before you decide what I am. Because if I had spoken—if I had gone to your mother, or to anyone outside the house—the Aldermarchs would have known within a day. Keepers were bound by oath and by ward; our tongues were not entirely our own, but our silences could be bought, and our betrayals were punished in the currency we loved most." They looked up. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? If I had broken that silence, the contract wouldn't have been voided. It would have been *accelerated*. Collection isn't only on tithe-night, Wren. Collection can be summoned. A keeper who betrayed the house would have been dismissed, yes—but first they would have been made an example of, and the example they'd have chosen would have been the child whose soul was already in their ledger."

Wren had gone very still. Stillness was their weapon; Rosalind had taught them that too, had watched them use it on customs men and cutpurses and once, memorably, on a bear. But this was the first time Rosalind had seen it turned on them, and it felt like standing at the edge of their own gate, looking down.

"You're saying you stayed silent to keep me alive," Wren said.

"I'm saying I stayed silent because the alternative was watching them take you at four years old instead of thirty. Or at nine. Every year I kept the secret was another year you were breathing above ground, learning maps, learning lockwork, learning how to be free in every way except the one that mattered." Rosalind's voice cracked and they hated it, hated the crack, hated the wet burning behind their eyes. "And then the years stacked up. One year became ten became twenty, and every year it got harder to say, because the longer I waited the more it looked like I'd chosen my position over your truth. Which is exactly what I did. I'm not asking you to forgive that, Wren. I'm asking you to know it. You deserve to have walked into the deep ways knowing everything, and I stole that from you along with everyone else."

"How long?" Wren's voice was flat, which was worse than shouting. Shouting meant feeling. Flatness meant arithmetic. "How long have you known about the brand? About the collection date?"

"Since the archive. Since the night I burned the copy."

"No." Wren shook their head slowly. "No. Before that. Before the priest, before the gate started answering prayers—did you know when you took me in? When you pulled me out of that village and carried me up the Thornfell road? Did you know what you were carrying?"

Rosalind closed their eyes. There was no honest answer but the true one, and the true one was a blade either way.

"I knew," they said. "I took you anyway. That's the part I can't make clean, Wren. I took you because I couldn't live in that house one more season knowing what they did in the under-shrine, and taking you was the only rebellion I could manage without dying. I stole a soul the Court owned and raised it as my own kin and told myself that made up for the silence. Some nights it even worked."

The silence that followed was long enough to hold a funeral.

When Wren finally moved, it was to stand—to rise off the stone in one smooth motion, gathering nothing, needing nothing, the way they left rooms they didn't intend to reenter. "Brannic confessed his oldest lie on that road today," they said, "and nearly died for it, and came out the other side lighter. Do you know why? Because he told us *before* the mountain made him. Because he chose the truth instead of being cornered into it." They looked down at Rosalind, and in the firelight their eyes were the grey of the moment before snow. "You had six weeks, Rosalind. Six weeks of roads and fires just like this one. Six weeks of me trusting you with my life on every mile. And you chose to wait until the mountain forced your hand, until I stood at the edge of the vault where my own soul is kept, before you gave me the one thing I needed to survive it: the truth, early enough to be useful."

"Wren—"

"I need to walk."

They walked. Rosalind listened to the light steps fade up the trail toward the watch-point, heel never landing, unheard even by someone listening for exactly that sound—and then they were alone with the fire, and the drowned thing in their chest sank back down into the dark, leaving only the knowledge of what it had cost to raise it.

Sefa appeared at some point, silent as debt, and sat at the edge of the light without speaking. She didn't ask. Debt-witches knew the weight of a paid-out truth better than anyone.

"She'll come back," Sefa said at last.

"I know she'll come back. That's not what frightens me." Rosalind fed the last of the moss to the coals and watched it flare and die. "What frightens me is what happens if she comes back and forgives me before tithe-night. Because forgiveness given too easily is just another kind of silence. And I've had forty years of silence. I'd rather have her fury than her mercy. Fury means she believes she has time. Mercy means she's already saying goodbye."

Far above, faint through leagues of stone, the gate shivered again—a low harmonic hum, the wards singing in their sleep. The old gods were awake, and they were counting.

Six days to tithe-night now. Five, if the mountain's moods shortened the roads.

Rosalind sat the rest of the watch alone, and did not sleep, and did not pray, because they were no longer certain which of the nine sovereigns would answer—or what price they'd quote.
