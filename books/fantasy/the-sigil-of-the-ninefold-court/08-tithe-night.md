## 8. Tithe-Night

**WREN**

The mountain opened its mouth at moonrise.

Wren had imagined the Court's doors a hundred ways—iron, bone, a seam of light—but never this: simply stone becoming air. One moment the cliff face above the drowned lake was solid black rock veined with silver; the next, it breathed outward, and a corridor of pale lamplight unfolded like a throat swallowing them whole.

Below, in the flooded basin that no map had ever named, the procession gathered. Claimed souls walked two by two down a causeway of white stone, and Wren's stomach turned at the sight of them—not corpses, not ghosts, but people gone *smooth*, their faces wiped to a pleasant blankness, dressed in burial-fine clothes stitched with names that were no longer theirs. Some had been dead for generations. Some, Wren realized with a cold twist of the gut, might have been taken living.

"Your brand," Sefa murmured beside him. She'd painted her eyelids with ash-sigils, and beneath them her eyes looked bruised and ancient. "Is it singing?"

It was. A low, sweet note under his skin, rising toward the doors like a dog hearing its master's whistle. Wren pressed his palm hard against his shoulder until the burn dulled.

"It knows where I'm going," he said. "That's the problem."

They moved with the procession—Sefa had woven them a glamour of smoothness, borrowed blankness draped over their living faces—and the strangest part was how easy it was. How *welcoming*. The causeway stones were warm through his boots. The lamplight smelled of beeswax and myrrh and something older underneath, something like the inside of a grave that had been kept very clean.

Brannic walked at his left, armor muted with soot, one hand resting on the pommel of a sword he was not supposed to draw unless everything failed. Pip scurried at the rear, small enough to pass for a child-claim, eyes enormous in the dark.

At the doors stood nine thrones in shadow, empty for now, and between them a steward made entirely of folded grey cloth, tall as three men, who counted the passing souls with a sound like pages turning.

Wren did not look up. He had memorized Rosalind's maps of the deep roads, every turn of them, and Rosalind's last instruction rang in his skull: *The vault is below the ninth throne. Everything else is theater.*

He kept walking. The doors took him in.

* * *

**PIP**

Pip had picked locks since they were four years old, but they had never picked one that *breathed*.

The vault door sat in a hollow beneath the ninth throne, exactly where Rosalind's maps promised, and it was not iron or wood but a slab of grey stone set with a lock whose tumblers were, unmistakably, alive—nine slender fingers of bone, curled inward like sleeping grubs. When Pip laid their picks against them, the fingers stirred.

*Hush,* Pip told them silently, the way they'd hush the locks of rich men's houses. *Hush now. I'm gentle.*

The bone fingers tasted the picks. Somewhere far off, water dripped once, like a clock striking.

Above, muffled through stone, Brannic's voice boomed—a distraction pitched perfectly, the bellow of a lost knight demanding audience with the sovereigns about a debt unpaid. Guards of folded cloth turned toward the sound. Pip worked.

First tumbler: the finger uncurled, pliant, tasting salt from Pip's fingertips and finding there the memory of a hundred opened doors. It approved. Second tumbler. Third. Sweat ran into Pip's eyes. Fourth—

A hand closed over their shoulder, and Pip nearly bit it before recognizing Sefa's grip, her other hand already moving in the quick sigil-language: *Ward on the inside. I take it. You keep picking.*

She knelt, bit her thumb, and began drawing on the stone with blood that glowed faintly gold, unraveling something invisible thread by invisible thread, her lips moving without sound. Pip went back to the lock.

Fifth. Sixth. Each bone finger gave way easier than the last, and that ease frightened Pip more than resistance would have. Locks fought when they wanted you stopped. This one seemed to be *making room*.

Seventh finger. Eighth.

The ninth would not move.

It curled tighter, and through their picks Pip felt it pulse—not like a mechanism, but like a heartbeat—and understood with terrible clarity that this last finger wasn't part of the lock at all. It was listening. Reporting.

*Let me be wrong,* Pip prayed, to whatever gods lived down here, the old ones, the waking ones. *Just this once, let me be wrong.*

They wrenched the ninth finger straight.

The vault door swung open onto darkness, and somewhere high above, every lamp in the Ninefold Court went out at once.

* * *

**BRANNIC**

Brannic had deserted one army in his life, and he had sworn an oath on his mother's grave never to run from anything again. So when the lamps died and the Court woke around him like a beast opening its eyes, he planted his feet in the great antechamber, drew his sword at last, and became the door.

They came at him out of the dark—stewards of folded cloth, unfolding as they came, spreading into shapes with too many angles and no faces. His blade sang through the first, and it came apart like cut linen, but the pieces kept moving, crawling back toward each other across the floor.

"I am Brannic of the Marchwall," he roared, giving them his name because names were all he had left to give, "and this threshold is held."

The second steward hit him like a landslide. He went down, rolled, came up bleeding from the ear, and cut it again, and again it reassembled. Behind him, down the passage, he could hear Pip's feet drumming away toward the vault, hear Sefa chanting in the old tongue, and he thought—absurdly, in the space between parries—of the day he'd come to Thornfell with nothing but shame and a sword nobody would let him carry, and Rosalind had looked him over once and said, *You'll do. Strays hold better than blood.*

*Strays hold.*

The third and fourth stewards came together. He braced against a pillar, gave ground inch by inch, buying seconds the way he'd once bought nothing in his life—generously, wastefully, all of it spent in one place.

Somewhere above, vast and slow, a bell began to toll. Tithe-night proper had begun, which meant the sovereigns were coming to their thrones, which meant the clock on everything was now running faster than any clock Brannic had ever raced.

A cloth edge opened his cheek to the bone. He laughed—actually laughed, blood in his teeth—and held.

* * *

**SEFA**

The ward was the most beautiful thing Sefa had ever hated.

It wrapped the vault interior in nine concentric layers, each one a different color if you had eyes to see it, each one singing a different note of the same chord. She had trained twenty years to unravel wards of this complexity. She had expected to need hours.

Instead, she needed only to listen, because the ward was not built—it was *grown*, and grown things could be persuaded. She unpicked the first layer with a whispered promise. The second with a half-truth. The third layer she paid for outright, feeding it a year of her own luck, feeling the world go subtly grayer around her.

By the fifth layer her hands shook. By the sixth, blood ran freely from her bitten thumb, spelling counter-sigils on the air.

And then she reached the seventh layer, and found it was not a defense at all.

It was a *record*. The ward remembered every soul that passed the vault threshold, and it showed her, unbidden, a flicker of images: a woman's hands signing a contract decades ago, Aldermarch rings on every finger; a keeper standing witness in grey robes, face turned away; an infant's cry echoing off contract-stone.

Wren's beginning. Sold and witnessed.

Sefa's breath caught. She saw the witness turn—the young face beneath the grey hood, younger than she'd ever known them, jaw tight with something swallowed—and she understood, suddenly and completely, what Rosalind had confessed on the night watch, and why Wren still hadn't spoken to them except in clipped necessities.

*Later,* she told herself, and unraveled the seventh layer, and the eighth.

The ninth layer she could not unravel. It was keyed to a name.

So Sefa did the only thing left. She spoke the name aloud—"Rosalind Villalobos"—and the ninth layer parted like a courtier bowing, admitting them all, and she knew in her marrow that speaking it had been heard by far more than a ward.

* * *

**WREN**

Inside the vault there were no shelves, no chests, no treasure. There were *niches*, thousands of them, honeycombing walls that rose beyond sight, and in each niche burned a small flame of a color that had no name above ground. Souls, banked like coals. Wren walked the aisles with his brand screaming joy against his shoulder, following the pull of it deeper, deeper, past flames that flickered as he passed, past niches labeled in script older than kingdoms.

He found his own niche at the vault's heart.

His soul looked like a candleflame the color of autumn, and it *reached* for him when he leaned close, straining against its niche like a child at a window. Wren put both hands against the warm stone and felt, for the first time in his life, wholly present in his own body—as though some part of him had been leaning on a crutch since birth and only now stood straight.

"There you are," he whispered.

Behind him, the vault door boomed shut.

He turned. The folded-cloth stewards filled the doorway, and above them, descending slowly through the dark as though the air itself were stairs, came nine shapes.

They were not monstrous. That was the horror of them. They looked like nine aspects of one regal figure—nine ages of the same sovereign, from a child crowned too early to a crone crowned too long—and when the eldest of them spoke, its voice was kind, which was worse than cruelty.

"Wren of Thornfell," it said. "Claimed at birth, due this night. And your companions." Its gaze moved past him, taking in Sefa's bloodied hands, Pip pressed against the wall, Brannic limping in last, sword still drawn. "Thieves, witches, deserters, lockchildren. A family of strays." Something like amusement touched the ancient voice. "How very like *her*."

Wren's blood went cold. "Like who?"

"You know who." The eldest sovereign smiled. "You have been raised by her. Guarded by her. Lied to by her for twenty years." It drifted closer, and the flames in the niches bowed toward it like wheat. "But we wonder, Wren of Thornfell, whether you know what she is. Not a smuggler. Not a keeper merely."

Its eight siblings spoke as one, soft as snowfall:

"Rosalind Villalobos. Witness to the sale. Sworn keeper of our gate. Debtor of the deepest kind."

The eldest leaned down, close enough that Wren felt its breath, cold and patient, smelling of incense and old paper.

"And here she comes now—to pay."

Far behind, down the long throat of the entrance hall, footsteps sounded on the white causeway. Unhurried. Familiar. Wren knew that walk the way he knew his own heartbeat, even now, even furious, even betrayed.

Rosalind was coming into the Court of their own free will, and the ninefold sovereigns turned to greet her like old friends settling an old account.
