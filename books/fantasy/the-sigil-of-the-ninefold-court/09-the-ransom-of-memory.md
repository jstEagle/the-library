## 9. The Ransom of Memory

The doors of the Ninefold Court were open, and Rosalind Villalobos stood in the throat of them.

They had imagined this moment a hundred times across forty years—usually at three in the morning, when the wards hummed low and the mountain breathed against their back. In those imaginings, the Court was always distant, always theoretical, a debt owed by someone else. Now the theory had teeth. The hall before them was vast as a swallowed cathedral, its ceiling lost in dark where pale shapes drifted like fish under ice. Nine thrones rose from the far end, carved from the mountain's own bone, and on them sat the sovereigns.

Not gods, exactly. Not anymore. Something older than the word, wearing shapes like half-remembered faces. One had Rosalind's grandmother's eyes. Another wore the smile of the first alderman of Aldermarch, dead two centuries. The Court dressed itself in whatever it had eaten.

"Villalobos," said the centermost sovereign, and its voice came from everywhere, from the stone underfoot and the marrow in Rosalind's bones. "Keeper of Thornfell. Forty years you kept our door. Did you think we would not know your name?"

Rosalind's mouth was dry as old paper. Behind them, Brannic held the vault door with his shoulder and his sword both, his breath sawing; Pip crouched over the lockwork, fingers flying; Sefa stood with her hands raised and unraveling, threads of borrowed magic smoking off her knuckles. And Wren—

Wren was beside the contract-shrine, the claim-mark on their shoulder blazing through their shirt like a coal, one hand outstretched toward Rosalind, one toward the small glass vessel on the shrine's altar where a soul sat coiled and glowing soft as a firefly in a jar.

Their soul. Wren's soul, sold at birth, due for collection on tithe-night, six weeks of running and bleeding and grieving all come down to this: twelve feet of black marble between the crew and the jar.

"Take another step," the sovereign said gently, "and the knight dies. Touch the vessel, smuggler, and we take what is ours along with your friends' memories for the trouble. We are not cruel. We are only exact."

The procession of claimed souls waited behind Rosalind in the entryway, hundreds of hollow-eyed figures who had sold themselves or been sold, shuffling forward one by one into the dark. The tithe was being counted. There was no way through that crowd that was not made of blood.

Rosalind stepped forward anyway.

* * *

"You know why I'm here," Rosalind said.

"We know many things." The sovereign with the grandmother-eyes leaned forward on its throne of bone. Up close, its face was wrong in the way of dreams—beautiful and slightly melted, features sliding like wax near a flame. "We know the keeper burned the copy in your little archive. We know the hedge-priest spoke before he died. We know the child you raised has walked nine roads to stand where they stand. But tell us, Villalobos—why *are* you here? You could have let the tithe fall. You have let a hundred tithes fall. What makes this soul different?"

And there it was. The question Rosalind had been avoiding since a prayer moved through stone and cracked their careful life open like an egg.

Because Wren was theirs—not by blood, not by contract, but by every meal cooked and every fever watched and every night spent teaching a foundling the shape of the hidden roads so they'd never be trapped anywhere, ever. Because family wasn't something the Court could invoice.

But saying that aloud would be like handing them a knife.

"They're mine," Rosalind said instead. "Raised under my roof. Fed from my table. By the old laws—the ones older than your contracts—that makes them kin."

"The old laws." Something like amusement rippled through all nine thrones at once, a sound like wind through a canyon. "You invoke the old laws. Keeper, we *are* the old laws."

"Then honor them."

Silence, vast and mineral. The drifting shapes overhead stilled. Somewhere behind Rosalind, Sefa whispered a word that made the air taste of iron.

The centermost sovereign rose. It was tall as a doorway, and when it descended the steps of its throne, the marble did not ring beneath it—it *remembered* ringing, and played back the memory.

"There is a payment," it said slowly, "that we value above souls. Above names. Above firstborn sons and lastborn daughters and the vows of dying kings." It stopped three paces away, close enough that Rosalind could smell it—cold stone, river water, the inside of sealed rooms. "Souls are common. Every creature has one. But a keeper's memory—an *unbroken* keeper's memory, forty years of every ward, every seal, every hidden road, every name written in the gate's deep ledger—" Its smile widened, and the grandmother's eyes gleamed. "That is a country no one else has ever held entire. That is worth more than any single soul. Even this one."

Rosalind's heart did not stop, though they wished it would. They had known. Some part of them had known since the archive, since they read the contract and understood that burning paper meant nothing to a thing that kept accounts in flesh. The Court did not want Wren's soul because it was precious. It wanted it because it was *owed*—and things that were owed could be traded, if you brought something to the table that made the arithmetic embarrassing.

Forty years. Every secret. Every road. Everything they were.

"I offer it," Rosalind said. "All of it. Unbroken. In exchange, the contract is voided. Not transferred. Not deferred. Voided. Wren walks out of here owning their own soul, and none of you—or anything that serves you—ever holds a claim on them again. Swear it on your own names, which I know you cannot lie upon."

The sovereigns went very still.

It was Sefa who broke it—a sharp intake of breath behind Rosalind, the sound of a debt-witch understanding the shape of a bargain before anyone else. "Rosalind. *Rosalind.* That's not a payment, that's—keeper's memory isn't a purse you dip into. It's the loadstone. Take it and everything anchored to it goes with it. You won't just lose the roads. You'll lose—"

"I know what I'll lose," Rosalind said quietly.

They didn't look at Wren. If they looked at Wren, they would not be able to do it. Forty years of discipline held their spine straight and their voice level, and they spent all of it staring at the sovereign's melting, beautiful face.

"Swear it," Rosalind said. "On your nine names."

* * *

The Court conferred without speaking, which was worse than argument—nine sovereigns turning their heads in perfect unison, considering, the way a jury of stones might consider a moth. Overhead, the pale shapes resumed their slow circling.

Then, together, in one voice layered nine ways: "Voided. Sworn on the Nine Names. The soul of the foundling called Wren is unclaimed, unclaimable, forever. And the keeper called Rosalind Villalobos pays in full."

"No." Wren's voice cracked across the hall like a whip. "No—*no*, you don't get to—you don't get to *decide*—"

"Wren—"

"Don't." Wren was crossing the marble now, past the line the sovereigns had drawn, and the guards of shadow that should have stopped them parted—perhaps because the contract was already voiding, perhaps because even the Court wanted to watch. Their face was wrecked, furious, wet. "You don't get to buy me. Not them, not *you*, not anyone. I'm not worth—I'm a smuggler, I'm a stray, I was never supposed to cost anybody anything—"

"You were never supposed to *cost* anything?" Rosalind finally turned, and the sight of Wren's face hit harder than any ward ever had. "Child, you were the best thing I ever stole."

Wren made a sound like something breaking.

"It's done," Rosalind said softly. "Hold onto Brannic. This part might be rough."

The sovereign raised one long hand, and from its palm unfolded a ribbon of light, silver-white, humming with the sound of every locked door Rosalind had ever opened. It reached toward Rosalind's brow.

The last thing Rosalind thought, deliberately, furiously, was: *the third turn past the split rock, Wren, mind the loose stone, count your steps, count your steps, I taught you, you remember, keep it, keep it—*

Then the light went in.

* * *

There was no pain. That was the cruelest part.

There was simply a great quieting, like snow falling on a field, covering each distinct thing until the field was only white. Forty years of the gate's secrets poured out of Rosalind in a silver stream—the wards singing their true names, the ledger of the deep roads, the passwords worn smooth as river stones—and behind the secrets, helplessly tangled, went everything the secrets had touched. The shrine. The patrol routes. The nights.

A child crying in a basket at the gate, forty years ago. Gone.

Teaching small hands to pick a lock with a hairpin. Gone.

A name, spoken ten thousand times across four decades, in anger and laughter and grief and love. A name that had meant *home*. Gone, gone, gone—snow falling, field going white, white, wh—

The light withdrew. The sovereign closed its empty hand.

Rosalind Villalobos stood alone in the throat of the great doors, blinking at the vast dark hall as though seeing it for the first time—which, in every way that mattered, they were. On the altar, the glass vessel sat open and empty. A young woman with a brand-scarred shoulder stood nearby, clutching her own soul to her chest, sobbing as though her ribs were breaking, staring at Rosalind with an expression of desperate, terrible hope.

Rosalind looked at her politely, the way one looks at a stranger.

"I'm sorry," Rosalind said. "Have we met?"

Behind her, a disgraced knight lowered his sword and bowed his head. A debt-witch pressed her fist to her mouth. A small lockchild began, very quietly, to cry.

The stranger—the sobbing one—took a step forward, then another, holding her soul like a lantern.

"Yeah," she said, voice wrecked but steady, wiping her face with the back of her hand. "Yeah. We've met. My name's Wren. And I've got stories."

She took Rosalind's hand, and did not let go.
