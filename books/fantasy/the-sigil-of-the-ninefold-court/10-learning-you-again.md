## 10. Learning You Again

The gate above ground was nothing like the gate below.

Wren had spent their whole life thinking of Thornfell as a wound in the mountain—a seam of black stone and older iron, humming with wards that bit at the fingers if you touched them wrong. But the shrine that stood over it in the spring light was small and almost gentle: a circle of standing stones furred with moss, a slate roof patched with lichen, wind coming down off the high passes carrying the smell of pine resin and thaw. The Court was sealed now, properly sealed, its ninefold hunger turned inward on itself for another age, and the old gods had gone back to sleep beneath the roots of the world like dogs settling by a fire.

Someone still had to watch them sleep.

So they stayed. All four of them—five, Wren corrected themself, though the word caught some mornings like a splinter too deep to draw. Brannic had raised a proper guardhouse out of the shrine's old foundations, honest timber and honest nails, because he said a knight who had run from one door ought to stand at another until the debt evened out. Sefa kept her ledger open on the table by the window, trading small charms to pilgrims who came up the mountain now that word had spread that the gods answered again. Pip had grown half a head taller since autumn and had claimed the roof as sovereign territory, from which they issued proclamations about supper and dropped things on people's heads with great ceremony.

And Rosalind Villalobos walked the gate every morning at first light, exactly as they had for forty years, and did not know Wren's face.

Wren stood at the window of the guardhouse with a chipped mug of barley tea and watched them come up the path from the stones—grey-streaked braid, patched coat, the same deliberate stride that had once meant safety to a smuggled child in the dark. Rosalind paused at the gate itself, laid one palm flat against the central stone the way they always did, listening. Whatever they heard made them nod, satisfied, and then they turned toward the guardhouse, and Wren braced, as they braced every morning, for the look.

Polite. Courteous. The look you gave a stranger who was doing a job well.

"Morning," Rosalind said, stamping frost off their boots on the threshold stone. "Pip's been in the flour again. I found handprints on the rafters."

"Those are Pip's *art*," Wren said. "You can't just go around finding it."

"Mm." Rosalind hung their coat on its peg—third peg, always third peg—and reached for the tea Wren had already poured without being asked. They took it with a nod of thanks, courteous as a toll-keeper. "The eastern ward's singing flat. I'll want to re-cut the sigil before the meltwater gets into the channel."

"I could come with you."

"If you like." A pause. Rosalind looked at them then, really looked, and Wren watched the arithmetic happen behind those grey eyes—the same eyes, the exact same eyes, searching Wren's face for whatever it was they were supposed to find there and not finding it. "You're very good at the channels," Rosalind said at last, carefully, the way one handled a borrowed tool. "Sefa says you know the deep roads better than anyone she's met."

"I had a good teacher."

"Did you?" Something flickered—interest, or the ghost of it. "I'd like to hear about them sometime. My own maps only go so far."

*Your own maps.* Wren set their mug down before their hands could do anything foolish. Every road Rosalind had ever cut through that mountain lived in Wren's memory like scripture—thirty-one passages, nine false doors, the trick of the leaning stone at the fourth crossing. Wren had memorized them as a child lying awake in the loft above the shrine, tracing them on the ceiling with one finger. And Rosalind stood here asking about them like a stranger asking directions.

"Yeah," Wren said. "Someday."

Rosalind nodded and went out into the cold with their chisels, and Wren stood at the window and breathed until the thing in their chest settled back down into its familiar shape. Grief with no body to bury. That was what Sefa called it. Grief where the person was still walking around, making tea jokes, re-cutting sigils. Forty years of keeper's memory, traded to the Ninefold Court like a purse of coins—and Wren was not in any of it. Not the foundling pulled from a smuggler's crate. Not the fevered winter when Rosalind hadn't slept for six nights. Not a single one of the ten thousand ordinary mornings.

But here was the thing Wren had decided, somewhere in the dark weeks after tithe-night, alone on the ridge with the wind trying to talk them out of it:

A family was made twice. Once by choice. And once again after, if you were stubborn enough, by devotion. By showing up. By telling the story until the story grew roots in someone new.

They had rebuilt harder things than this. They had rebuilt themselves out of nothing but a brand and a name nobody wanted.

* * *

In the evenings Wren told stories.

It had started accidentally, three days after they'd all come down from the mountain's heart, hollow-eyed and smelling of vault-dust. Rosalind had asked—not rudely, never rudely—why a stranger would stay at a gate shrine in the middle of nowhere, and Wren had opened their mouth to say *because I owe you everything*, and found there was nothing behind the words that Rosalind could reach. So instead they'd said, "Let me tell you about the worst smuggler who ever worked these roads," and Rosalind, who apparently could no more resist a good story than the mountain could resist gravity, had sat down.

Now it was ritual. Supper eaten, dishes done, Pip bribed into bed with threats of extra watch-duty, and Wren would take the chair by the hearth while Rosalind mended nets or sharpened tools or copied out ward-notes, and Wren would talk.

"So there's this smuggler," Wren said, on an evening in late spring, rain hammering the slate roof like applause. "Works the Thornfell roads. Best there is—knows all thirty-one passages, knows which stones lean and which ones lie. But she's got this rule, right? Never carry anything with a heartbeat."

"Why's that?"

"Because once, years ago, she broke it." Wren fed the fire a split of pine. "Crate comes through the fourth crossing, heavy, nailed shut, and she can hear something breathing inside. Contract says deliver it sealed. She sits with that crate for half the night, knife in her hand, and every time she reaches to pry the lid, she hears the breathing change—gets quicker, like whatever's in there is scared of her."

Rosalind's hands had gone still on the whetstone.

"And finally she opens it," Wren went on, quieter. "And it's a kid. Maybe two years old. Brand on the shoulder like somebody's stamped her for postage. And the smuggler looks at this kid, and she thinks: everyone in my life has been cargo. Everyone's been something carried and delivered and paid for. And she decides—" Wren's voice snagged; they pushed through it, "—she decides this one is going to be different. This one is going to be *kin*. So she burns the contract, and she carries the kid up out of the dark herself, and she raises her in a shrine full of mossy old rocks, and the kid grows up learning every map the smuggler ever drew, memorizing every road, because that's how the kid figures out how to say thank you. Roads are love, in her family. Roads are the only language anybody's fluent in."

The fire popped. Rain ran silver down the black glass of the window.

"That smuggler," Rosalind said slowly, "sounds reckless. Burning a contract with the Court."

"She was reckless. It got worse. Later somebody sold the kid's soul anyway—before the smuggler ever found her—and the smuggler had to go down under the mountain with a crew of strays to steal her back. Nearly lost everything. Lost—" Wren turned the mug in their hands. "Lost something big. Paid the whole ransom herself."

"What did she pay?"

"Her memories. Of the kid. All of them." Wren looked up and found Rosalind watching them across the firelight, expression unreadable, whetstone forgotten in their lap. "That's the ending so far. She walks around knowing every road in that mountain and not one single morning with the kid in it. And the kid stays anyway. Keeps telling her stories. Because the kid figures—if the memories are gone, we'll grow new ones. Plant 'em right next to the empty space and water them daily."

Rosalind was quiet a long moment. When they spoke, their voice was rougher than usual. "Does it work? In the story."

"Dunno yet. Story's not finished."

"No," Rosalind said softly. "I suppose it isn't."

They went back to the whetstone. But that night, Wren saw the light in Rosalind's room burn past midnight, and in the morning there was a fresh page in the keeper's logbook, in that precise archival hand: *Notes toward a story, told by W. Details unverified but emotionally consistent.*

Emotionally consistent. Wren laughed until they had to sit down on the woodpile.

* * *

Summer came up the mountain in stages—first the crocuses punching through the last snow, then the green flood, then Pip discovering swimming holes and becoming insufferable about it. Brannic taught Rosalind to spar in the yard behind the guardhouse, and Rosalind turned out to be better than their thin arms suggested, all economy and dirty tricks learned from forty years of dealing with things that didn't fight fair. Sefa started teaching Rosalind debt-magic, carefully, one small binding at a time, and Rosalind learned it the way they learned everything: completely, silently, and then forever.

And the empty space beside Wren began, very slowly, to fill.

Not with the old memories—that door stayed shut; the Court kept its payments. But with new ones. Rosalind learned that Wren took their tea bitter and their eggs burnt. Learned that Wren hummed off-key when working locks and perfectly when they thought no one listened. Learned that Wren went quiet at the fourth crossing of the deep roads, and never asked why, just stood beside them in the dark until they were ready to walk on—which was, Wren thought, a better answer than most people ever managed in a whole lifetime of remembering.

One evening in high summer, they sat together on the ridge above the shrine, watching the sun go down over the lowlands where Wren had once run cargo. Rosalind had brought two cups of tea without being asked, and handed Wren theirs first, and Wren realized with a jolt that they knew how Wren took it. Bitter. No honey. When had that happened? Some Tuesday, probably. Some completely ordinary Tuesday that neither of them would remember as anything special, except that Wren would remember it, and hoard it, and tell it back someday.

"You never finished your story," Rosalind said. "About the smuggler and the kid."

"Haven't gotten to the end."

"What happens at the end? In your version."

Wren watched the light drain gold out of the valley. "I think," they said, "the kid keeps telling stories. And the smuggler keeps listening. And one day—years from maybe—they're walking some road together, and the smuggler stops, and looks at the kid, and doesn't remember anything new at all. Doesn't get one second of the old life back. But she says the kid's name like she's known it forever anyway. Like the name grew back on its own. And that's the ending. Not getting the past back. Just—" they shrugged, throat tight— "choosing each other twice. Once blind, once with your eyes open."

Rosalind looked at them for a long moment. The wind moved the grey streaks loose from the braid.

"Wren," Rosalind said.

Just the name. Nothing else. Said plainly, the way you'd set something precious down on a table between you.

And Wren—who had rehearsed this moment for months, who had imagined it a hundred ways and prepared to survive all of them—found that none of the preparation mattered at all. Their eyes burned. "Yeah," they managed. "That's me."

"I know," Rosalind said quietly. "I'm starting to."

Below them the shrine stones caught the last light, and beneath them, far beneath, the old gods slept on, and the gate held, and the tea went cold in their hands while the valley filled up with evening. Somewhere down the mountain road, dust rose where no cart should be at this hour—a rider, moving fast, flying colors Wren didn't recognize from any house in the lowlands.

Brannic's horn sounded from the guardhouse, one long note of alarm.

Wren set down the cup and stood, and Rosalind stood with them, and without a word they started down the slope together—keeper and storyteller, kin chosen twice, walking into whatever came next the way they'd learned to walk everything now: side by side, into the dark, with the whole story still ahead of them.
