## 1. The Ledger of Small Impossibilities

The cargo arrived on a Tuesday, which Casper Amberdale would later think was the first wrong thing about it. Impossible things, in her experience, preferred to arrive at dusk, or in fog, or during the small hours when the guild's corridors were empty and the lamplight made everything look like an omen. They did not arrive at half past ten on a bright Tuesday morning with a bill of lading and a driver who wanted his receipt signed.

"Fourteen crates," said the driver, a broad man with salt crusted white in the seams of his coat, though the nearest coast was forty miles east. "Manifest says perishable. Manifest lies."

"They usually do," Casper said, and signed.

She watched the crates come down the loading ramp into the Receiving Hall, and she felt them before she saw them — that was the second wrong thing, though she didn't yet know to call it that. A pressure against the eardrums, like standing too near a waterfall. A smell that wasn't quite brine and wasn't quite cold iron. The crates were ordinary pine, stamped with the shipping house's mark, but as they passed her she noticed that the wood was damp. All fourteen of them. Damp on a dry morning, in a dry hall, as if they had recently been somewhere very wet and very deep.

Casper had been cataloguing for the Guild of Impossible Things for eleven years, and in eleven years she had developed what Vess called *the touch* — not magic, exactly, nothing so grand, just a cartographer's sensitivity to edges. To the places where one thing stopped being itself and became something else. She could feel the seam of a disguised door through three feet of stone. She could tell when a map lied, not because of errors but because of a certain tension in the ink, a held breath. And now, standing among her fourteen damp crates, she felt that every single one of them was holding its breath.

She fetched the pry bar.

Inside the first crate, packed in straw gone grey and soft, were bottles. Dozens of them, each wrapped individually in oilcloth, each about the size of a wine bottle but heavier-looking, blown from glass so dark green it was nearly black. The stoppers were wax over cork over lead, triple-sealed, and stamped into the lead of every one was a sigil she didn't recognize: a circle broken by a wavy line, like a horizon seen through a keyhole.

She unwrapped one, turned it in the light, and understood immediately why the manifest had lied.

There was a tide inside it.

Not water. Not a miniature sea, not some clever glassblower's joke. A *tide* — the motion of one, the rhythm of it, contained. The bottle held darkness and movement, a slow swell that rose along the curved glass and fell away again, rose and fell, patient as breathing. When she tilted it, the darkness inside didn't slosh the way water would. It leaned, deliberate, the way the sea leans toward a shore it means to have. And faintly, so faintly she thought at first it was the blood in her own ears, she heard it: a long, low withdrawal-and-return, the sound a beach hears in its sleep.

"Bottled tides," she said aloud, to no one, because saying things aloud helped her think, and also because the Receiving Hall was otherwise empty and there are some words you need another mouth to hear, even your own.

She counted. Sixty-one bottles across fourteen crates. She set up her cataloguing station at the long oak table by the north windows, where the light came in clean off the inner courtyard, and she began to work the way she'd been taught to work: systematically, skeptically, with both hands and full attention.

The Guild of Impossible Things occupied a converted customs house on Maren Street, four storeys of grey granite that had spent two centuries learning how to look boring. To the street, it was a society of antiquarians and surveyors, harmless obsessives who measured old roads and argued about obsolete units. This was not entirely false. But behind the public reading rooms and the handsome engraved maps sold for civic purposes, the guild kept its true work: the cataloguing, containment, and occasional correction of things that should not exist. Bottled storms. Seeds that grew backwards. A mirror, in Vault Nine, that reflected a room nobody had ever stood in. The guild existed because the world occasionally produced loose threads, and someone had to keep track of them before the whole garment unravelled.

Someone. Casper smiled a little as she wrote the date at the head of a fresh ledger page. She was that someone, this week, this shipment. Eleven years ago she had been a starving apprentice copyist taken in off the street by a woman with silver hair and a voice like dry paper, and now she was Senior Cataloguer of the Third Archive, and the guild was the only shape her life had ever held. It fit her the way a good map fits a coastline: precisely, because it had been drawn around her for a long time.

*Entry 4471,* she wrote. *Consignment of sixty-one sealed vessels, contents classified provisionally as "tidal essence, contained." Provenance: Halcrow & Sons Shipping, acting for an unnamed consignor. Destination per manifest: the Guild itself.*

She paused over that last line. The manifest named the guild as the destination, which meant the order had come from within — from the Upper Archives, from Corvane's office, most likely, though the Guildmaster's seal was oddly absent. Purchases of this scale went through channels. Channels left paper trails. There was no requisition form on file; she checked twice, once in the physical cabinet and once in the copying room's index, and found nothing. Fourteen crates of impossible ocean, ordered by nobody, arriving on a Tuesday.

Third wrong thing. She was starting to keep count without meaning to.

She examined the bottles more closely as she logged them. Each was labelled in a fine, cramped hand — not written on the glass but somehow *in* it, the letters floating a hair beneath the surface like fish under ice. The labels bore numbers only. No names, no ports of origin. *Tide of the Unnumbered Shore, 3rd hour of ebb.* *Tide of the Long Withdrawal.* *Tide That Returns Before It Leaves.* She copied them all out, and the act of copying them made her fingertips ache, the way her eyes ached when she read maps of places that resisted being mapped. Whatever these tides were, they had been taken from somewhere. Somewhere with shores enough to number its hours of ebb.

And one bottle — the forty-fourth, dark even among the dark ones — had a label she couldn't finish reading. The script was the same cramped hand, but the name slid. She would fix her eyes on it and get as far as *Tide of* and then find herself thinking about lunch, or the loose hinge on the courtyard door, or the fact that she ought to re-ink her good pen, and when she looked back the label would be waiting, patient, unread. Three times she tried. On the third attempt she got a little further — *Tide of the S—* — and then her attention simply poured off the words like water off waxed canvas.

"Hm," said Casper Amberdale, and reached for her loupe.

Under magnification the label was worse, or better, depending on how you liked your mornings. The letters weren't floating under the glass. They were moving — drifting with the tide inside, rising and falling in slow circulation, and every time a letter sank below the visible curve of the interior it ceased, briefly, to be legible at all. Not hidden. Absent. As if the word were being written and unwritten continuously, a coastline redrawn with every wave.

She knew, in a professional and entirely detached way, that she ought to flag the bottle for Vault assessment. Category Seven at minimum, possibly Eight, given the resistance to observation. She even wrote the note. Then she sat looking at it for a while, elbows on the table, chin in her hands, and found that the unease in her chest wasn't professional at all.

It was personal. Which made no sense. She had never seen a bottled tide before. She had grown up landlocked, orphaned, map-mad, a girl who thought the sea was a beautiful idea other people got to have. The sea meant nothing to her except as lines on paper.

So why did bottle forty-four feel less like a curiosity and more like a letter she'd sent herself and forgotten?

* * *

Vess found her at the end of the afternoon, which is to say Vess appeared beside her without having crossed the room, which was simply how Vess moved, and had been for as long as Casper had known her. Silver-haired, spare, dressed always in the same slate-grey coat with ink stains on the right cuff where she dragged her sleeve. Her eyes were pale grey too, the colour of a page left blank on purpose.

"You've been frowning at that crate since noon," Vess said. "I could see you from the gallery. You have a very eloquent back."

"It's a consignment of bottled tides," Casper said. "Sixty-one units. Unrequisitioned, unmanifested beyond a lying bill of lading, and one of them won't let me read its label." She turned bottle forty-four so her mentor could see it. "Also I don't like it. Professionally speaking."

Vess picked up the bottle. She held it the way she held everything, with a lightness that belied her age, turning it once against the window light. For a moment — a brief, specific moment that Casper would return to many times in the weeks to come — the older woman's face did something complicated and then put itself away, like a ledger closed mid-entry.

"Tidal phenomena," Vess said. "Unusual. Where did it come from?"

"That's rather my question. No requisition, no consignor. Someone upstairs ordered this and didn't sign their name."

"The upstairs of this building signs fewer names than you'd think," Vess said mildly. "Half of what we do runs better without provenance. Provenance invites questions, and questions invite visitors, and visitors—" she set the bottle down with a soft clink, "—are how incidents happen. Log it as received, flag the difficult one for the vault, and let Corvane's office sort the paperwork. That's the whole of your job today, and you're doing the part of it that isn't yours."

"You didn't even look at the label."

"I looked at the label." Something in the way she said it made Casper look up sharply, but Vess's face was already smooth again, dry paper over blank pages. "It's a tide, Casper. Tides resist being read. That's practically their defining feature. You're finding mysteries because it's autumn and the light's going and you always get restless when the light goes. Eat something. Sleep. Tomorrow it'll be six dozen bottles of seawater and a story to tell at dinner."

It was a good answer. It was a warm answer, delivered in the voice that had talked a frightened fifteen-year-old into eating soup in a strange kitchen eleven years ago, and it settled over Casper like the coat Vess used to drape on her shoulders. That was the thing about Vess: she made doubt feel like weather. Passing through, nothing to be done, here's tea.

But Casper had learned cartography from this woman too, and cartography taught you that the comforting explanation and the accurate one were frequently different documents.

"Vess," she said, "who else has handled this shipment?"

"No one. It came straight to receiving." A pause, small as a dropped stitch. "Straight to *you*, I mean."

"I know it came straight to me. I was here." Casper turned the ledger toward her mentor, open to the entry she'd begun. "I mean before me. Who decided the guild needed sixty-one bottles of tide? Because whoever it was paid for them, and payment leaves records, and the records—" she tapped the ledger's spine, and the shelf behind her, and the whole grey building around them both, "—are in this house somewhere. Everything's in this house somewhere. That's the entire point of us."

For the first time all afternoon, Vess looked genuinely tired. "Some records aren't kept, Casper. Some records are *unkept*. Deliberately. By people with good reasons."

"What kind of good reason does anyone have for unkeeping a record?"

"The kind you'll understand someday," Vess said, "or thank us for never making you," and then she smiled — the real smile, the crooked one, the one that had raised Casper as much as any rule or lesson — and said dinner was at eight and Casper was expected, and left the way she'd arrived, without crossing the intervening room.

* * *

Casper did not go to dinner at eight.

She told herself she'd lost track of time, which was even partly true. The catalogue had needed finishing; sixty-one entries, each bottle weighed, measured, described, its label transcribed where transcription was possible. She worked by lamplight as the courtyard windows went blue and then black, and the Receiving Hall filled with the particular silence of the guild after hours — the tick of the great clock in the stairwell, the settling of old stone, the distant footsteps of the night wardens making their rounds two floors up.

At entry 4415 she stopped pretending.

Bottle forty-four sat apart from its fellows, on a square of velvet she'd requisitioned from the supplies cabinet without logging that either. Under the lamp its darkness moved with the same patient swell, rise and fall, lean and withdraw. She had transcribed fifty-nine labels successfully. Bottle nine had smudged; bottle thirty had been mislabelled by the original hand and she'd noted the discrepancy. Fifty-nine out of sixty-one accounted for.

One bottle unreadable. One ledger, downstairs in the Third Archive, that she'd never been able to finish reading either — the tall folio bound in cracked blue leather, *Register of Consignments, Early Period*, whose pages seventy through eighty-three resisted her the same way this label did. Not locked. Not damaged. Simply *sliding*, attention pouring off the vellum like water off wax. She'd assumed for eleven years that the pages were spoiled, that some old spill of solvent or salt had eaten the ink, and she'd never once asked anyone about it because everyone else seemed able to consult it freely and she'd been embarrassed, privately, ashamed of her one blind spot in a house full of things she could read.

She had never tried reading those pages by lamplight, alone, at night, with a bottle of unreadable tide sitting next to her like a tuning fork.

Casper was many things — careful, thorough, loyal to the marrow, her mentor's best student — but she had never once in her life been able to leave a blank space unexamined. It was, Vess said, her defining virtue and her eventual doom, though she'd said it laughing.

She took the ledger down from its shelf. Blue leather, cracked spine, the smell of dust and old salt. She carried it up to the Receiving Hall and laid it open on the table beside bottle forty-four, and the moment the two were within a hand's breadth of each other, the tide inside the glass leaned — visibly, deliberately, the way a dog leans toward a familiar hand — and the pages of the ledger stopped sliding.

Pages seventy through eighty-three opened themselves under her fingers as if they had been waiting eleven years to do exactly this.

The writing was cramped and fine and utterly, horribly familiar. She knew that hand. She had imitated that hand for years in her apprenticeship, practised its peculiar hooked capitals until hers were indistinguishable from it. It was *her* handwriting. And the entries it recorded were dated forty years before Casper Amberdale was born.

She read the first line three times. The lamp flickered. In its glass prison, the tide rose and fell, rose and fell, keeping time with her heartbeat, and far above the guild's grey roof, in a sky she could not see from where she stood, the first stars of evening waited to begin going out.

*Consignment received, this date, per agreement. The seal holds. The memory is paid. — A. Amberdale, Cartographer, Guild of Impossible Things.*

Outside the window, the night came on like deep water.
