## 2. A Map with No Coast

The commission arrived the way impossible things always did at the Guild: on ordinary paper, in an envelope that smelled faintly of nothing at all.

Casper Amberdale turned it over twice before opening it, standing in the delivery vestibule where the morning light came down through glass tinted the color of weak tea. The Guild of Impossible Things occupied a building that was larger inside than out — not dramatically so, not in a way that announced itself, but by exactly the amount needed to house its contradictions. There were staircases here that only worked on Tuesdays. There was a door to a room that existed solely so that something could be lost in it. Casper had mapped all of it in her first year, and had since learned to stop asking questions about the extra corridor on the third floor, which appeared only when someone was crying.

She slit the envelope with her cartographer's knife and read the commission twice.

*To the Guild's finest hand: chart the coastline of Vellichor Reach. Payment negotiable. Urgency considerable.*

Below the words, someone had added, in a different ink, almost apologetic: *If it still exists when you arrive.*

"Vellichor Reach," she said aloud, tasting it. The name meant nothing to her, which was itself unusual. Casper prided herself on knowing every place that had ever been written down — not because she'd memorized them, but because names stuck to her like burrs. She remembered coastlines the way other people remembered faces. She could draw the drowned harbors of Meridian from memory, though she'd never seen them; she could sketch the salt-roads of the Ashwind steppe without reference. But Vellichor Reach slid off her mind like water off oiled canvas. She would think she had it, and then it was gone, the way a dream is gone the moment you reach for its logic.

She went to find Vess.

Her mentor was in the map vaults, as always, hunched over the great reading table with a loupe screwed into one eye, repairing a chart of a city that had burned down two centuries ago and now existed only because the Guild insisted on remembering it. Vess Ishikawa was small and dry as an autumn leaf and possessed the particular patience of someone who had spent fifty years arguing with paper.

"Vellichor Reach," Casper said, laying the commission on the table.

Vess did not look up immediately. That was the first strange thing. Her mentor's hands — steady enough to redraw a coastline freehand within a hair's tolerance — paused over the vellum for the length of half a breath before resuming their work.

"There's no such place," Vess said.

"That's what the Guild is paying me to determine."

"No." Vess set down her pen at last and looked up, and her face had arranged itself into the careful blankness she wore when she was deciding how much to tell you. "I mean there is no such place *now*. It isn't simply uncharted, Casper. It is unexisting. Do you understand the difference?"

Casper understood more than she wanted to. "It keeps going away."

"It keeps having gone away." Vess pulled off the loupe and rubbed the bridge of her nose. "Three survey teams have been sent. The first came back with charts of open ocean and no memory of why they'd sailed. The second came back with nothing at all — we assume they're dead, though we can't even mourn them properly, because we can't remember what we'd be mourning. The third—"

"Let me guess. Never came back."

"Came back," Vess said quietly, "but wrong. Older. By years. And insisting they'd never left." She tapped the commission with one blunt finger. "You are the fourth attempt. Corvane requested you specifically. Your instruments are the newest we have. And your memory—" she hesitated, "—is unusually stubborn. You hold onto places the way other cartographers hold onto tools. If anyone can pin that coastline down long enough to draw it, it's you."

There was something underneath the compliment. Casper had worked with Vess for eleven years, ever since the older woman had found her — a feral, orphaned girl of fifteen with a head full of maps nobody had taught her — sleeping in the Guild's chart room. She knew every weather of her mentor's silences. This one was the silence of a woman choosing not to say something, and it sat between them like a third person at the table.

"You've heard the name before," Casper said.

"I've heard a great many names." Vess gathered her pens with brisk finality. "Go prepare. You sail on the morning tide. And Casper—" For a moment she seemed about to add something, some caution or confession. Then it passed behind her eyes and was gone, filed away in whatever locked drawer Vess kept for truths too heavy to hand to a younger woman. "Take notes of everything. Especially the things that don't make sense. Especially those."

* * *

The ship was called the *Patient Question*, which Casper considered either very honest or deeply ominous, and she sailed east for six days into waters that grew progressively less interested in being anywhere.

It began subtly. On the second day, the gulls vanished — not flew away, but stopped having ever been present, so that she found herself remembering their cries while looking at an empty sky. On the third day, the navigator's charts began to disagree with each other, each showing a slightly different sea, as though the ocean couldn't settle on its own shape. By the fifth day, the crew had started avoiding eye contact, and the cook served a stew that everyone agreed tasted like forgetting.

On the sixth day, Casper stood at the bow with her field instruments and watched the horizon refuse to commit.

It wasn't fog. Fog has texture, weight, a way of moving. What lay ahead was more like an unfinished sentence — a grayness that was not empty but *pending*, the visual equivalent of a word on the tip of the tongue. Her instruments behaved strangely: the compass needle spun slowly, thoughtfully, like a man trying to recall a name; the depth line came back dry.

Dry. She measured it three times, because a sounding line coming up dry from the middle of an ocean was the kind of impossibility the Guild would pay good money for, and then she made herself laugh, because the alternative was screaming.

"We should turn back," said Captain Ferro, a broad woman whose face had the weathered honesty of driftwood. "I've run this route for twenty years. I don't like waters that argue with my charts."

"The commission says urgency considerable."

"The commission also says *if it still exists*." Ferro spat over the rail. "My instinct says whatever's ahead ate three crews, and I've grown attached to mine."

"And mine says it didn't eat them." Casper surprised herself with the certainty in her own voice. "It *misplaced* them. There's a difference. Things eaten leave bones, Captain. Things misplaced can be found." She didn't know how she knew this. The knowing rose in her like tide-water, cold and absolute, and she decided — as she often did — to trust it and examine it later. "Give me until dawn tomorrow. If I haven't charted anything worth the risk, we turn home."

Ferro studied her for a long moment. "Guild folk," she muttered, but rang for the anchor.

That night, Casper did not sleep. She sat in her cramped cabin with the lamp turned low and reviewed her notes, and somewhere past midnight — in the hour the old sailors called the *hollow watch*, when the world is thinnest — she felt the ship pass through something.

There was no sound. No shudder. Just a sensation, brief and total, like walking through a curtain of cold water, or like the moment between hearing your name called and turning around. Every hair on her arms stood up. The lamp flame leaned sideways without wind.

Then the grayness outside the porthole resolved into stars.

Not night sky — there was still daylight coming through, thin and sourceless — but stars anyway, hanging in the pale like pins in cloth, and beneath them, unmistakably, a coastline.

* * *

She was on deck before the watch bell finished ringing.

Vellichor Reach unrolled before them in the gray light, and it was beautiful in the way that abandoned things are beautiful — a long sweep of chalk-white cliffs stitched with green, a harbor town climbing the hillside in terraces, slate roofs, a lighthouse at the point with its lamp dark. Fishing boats bobbed at moorings. Smoke rose from chimneys. Everything was perfectly, precisely *there*, and everything was perfectly silent.

No gulls. No voices. No bells from the harbor chapel, though Casper could see the chapel clearly. No dogs. A town of perhaps two thousand souls, and not one sound but the water against the hull.

"It's a ghost town," whispered the mate.

"It's not dead." Casper raised her spyglass and swept the terraces. In a window on the third row, a curtain moved. In a garden below it, laundry hung on lines — wet laundry, she noted, still dripping. Somewhere in that town, people were living lives at a volume just below audibility. Or the town was living them *for* them. She couldn't decide which possibility unsettled her more.

"Lower the boat," she said. "I go alone. If I'm not back by noon, sail home and tell the Guild what you saw."

Ferro opened her mouth, closed it, and nodded. Wise captains, Casper had learned, do not argue with cartographers who have that look.

The harbor water was clear as glass and cold as guilt. She rowed herself in past fishing boats whose nets hung full of fish that did not struggle, and stepped onto stone steps worn concave by generations of feet, and put her boot down on the soil of a place that, according to every map in the world, did not exist.

The silence accepted her. That was the only word for it. The town did not resist her presence; it simply folded her in, like a page closing over a pressed flower.

She walked the main street. Doors stood ajar. Inside one house, a meal was laid on the table, bread half-sliced, a knife resting mid-stroke. In another, a child's toys lay scattered across the floor in patterns that suggested play interrupted or play ongoing — she genuinely could not tell which, and the not-telling made her chest ache. Everything here was in the present tense. Nothing was ruined. Nothing was abandoned. It was simply *paused*, a held breath of a town, waiting for someone to exhale.

And everywhere, on every surface, there were maps.

Chalked on walls. Inked on tabletops. Scratched into doorframes. Crude ones, elegant ones, children's drawings of the harbor with the sun wearing sunglasses. As if the entire population had been seized by a compulsion to record their own geography — as if they had known, somehow, that they were in danger of being forgotten, and had covered their town in self-portraits of place, hoping some future stranger would come and read them.

Casper knelt beside the largest one, spread across the flagstones of the square: a magnificent chalk rendering of the whole coastline, cliffs and coves and the road inland, annotated in a flowing, confident hand.

She knew that hand.

Not recognized — *knew*, the way you know your own reflection, the way your fingers know the weight of your favorite pen. The descenders looped just so. The *e*'s carried a slight forward lean. The numbers were formed with that little horizontal stroke she'd been teased about her whole career, because it made her sevens look like continental divides.

It was her handwriting.

Her heart performed a slow, deliberate somersault and settled back into rhythm, beating hard. She told herself: coincidence. Hands resemble one another. She told herself: some ancestor, some namesake, some trick of training — the Guild teaches a standard script, after all. But the Guild's standard script was nothing like this, and neither was anyone else's, and she knew it, and the knowing rose in her again, that cold tide of certainty that had carried her across the unmapped water.

With fingers that trembled only slightly, she traced the annotation at the map's edge, where the cartographer had signed and dated their work, as the Guild required:

*Surveyed and drawn by C. Amberdale, Cartographer of the Second Expedition.*
*In the year 4021.*

Forty years before her birth.

She sat down on the flagstones rather abruptly, in the middle of a town that did not exist, next to a signature that was hers and could not be. Around her the silent houses kept their counsel. The wet laundry dripped. The half-cut bread waited for a hand that might return, or might have been waiting forty years, or both.

*Especially the things that don't make sense,* Vess had said. *Especially those.*

So she took out her notebook — her real notebook, the one bound in gray leather that she trusted with everything — and she wrote it all down: the dry sounding-line, the stars in the day sky, the town paused mid-breath, the maps on every surface, and the signature. She drew the coastline with meticulous care, cliff by cliff, cove by cove, and beneath her own drawing she copied the impossible date exactly as it stood.

When she finished, she noticed something she had missed. At the corner of the great chalk map, half smudged, half erased — deliberately, she thought, by a hand in a hurry — there was a symbol she had seen only once before in her life: pressed into the skin of her own left shoulder blade, birthmark-shaped, sigil-shaped, in the form of a wave breaking over a star.

Beneath it, in that same familiar-unfamiliar hand, four words survived the erasure:

*THE SEAL IS FAILING.*

Far out at sea, without warning, the stars in the pale daytime sky went out. All of them. All at once. Like candles pinched by a single unseen breath.

Behind her, in the silent town, for the first time since her arrival, a bell began to ring.
