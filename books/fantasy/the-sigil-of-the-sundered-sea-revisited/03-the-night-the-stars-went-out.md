## 3. The Night the Stars Went Out

The wards began to fail at the eleventh hour, though no one in the guild would learn this until much later, and by then the failing had a different name.

Casper Amberdale was awake because she could not sleep — a common enough condition for a cartographer with an unfinished chart pinned above her desk — and so she became, without meaning to and without knowing what it meant, one of the first people in the world to notice that the stars were going out.

She noticed it the way she noticed everything: as a problem of measurement.

Her window faced east over the guild's inner courtyard, where the old compass rose was set into the flagstones in brass and lapis, worn smooth by two centuries of apprentices pacing out their bearings. Beyond it, past the slate roofs of the Scriptorium wing, the sky stretched black and enormous, and Casper had been using it idly, half-dreaming, as a reference — checking her memory of the constellations against the chart of the Ninefold Reach she was supposed to deliver to Master Ellery by morning. She had drawn the Reach's eastern coast from the survey journals, but the journals disagreed with each other on where the coast bent northward, and so she had turned to the oldest authority any cartographer trusted: the stars. Sailors steered by them; therefore coasts could be triangulated from them; therefore if she fixed the positions of the Lantern and the Three Sisters precisely enough—

The Lantern was gone.

Not clouded over. Not dimmed behind weather. Gone, the way a word is gone when you look at it too long and forget how to read it. Casper leaned closer to the glass, breath fogging it, and wiped the pane with her sleeve. The patch of sky where the Lantern hung — that steady amber point every navigator in the known world had used for four hundred years — was simply empty, a hole in the fabric of the night, and around the hole the other stars seemed fainter, as though they had drawn back from it.

"The Sisters," she said aloud, to hear something ordinary in the room. Her voice sounded wrong, flattened, as if the walls had stopped listening.

She found the Sisters. Or rather, she found where they should have been, and then she watched — her heart beginning its slow, heavy climb up into her throat — as the middle Sister winked out. Not flickered. *Winked out*, like a candle pinched between wet fingers, and the darkness where it had been did not feel like absence. It felt like removal. Like something reaching up with great care and plucking the lights down one by one.

Casper had grown up in the Guild of Impossible Things. She had cataloged bottled tides and maps that bled and doors that opened onto yesterday. She knew, better than most, that the world's edges were not as solid as other people believed. But there was a difference between impossibility contained — labeled, shelved, entered in a ledger in her own careful hand — and impossibility loose in the sky, unmaking the oldest lights there were.

She threw open her door and ran.

* * *

The corridor outside her rooms was full of people already, and none of them were looking at the sky.

That was the first strange thing, and later Casper would understand it was the most important one. The guild's night staff stood along the walls holding lamps, and the apprentices from the dormitory wing crowded the stairwell in their nightclothes, and everyone's faces were turned inward, toward the center of the building, toward the deep vaults below the Map Room where the border wards were anchored. They were listening. The whole guild was leaning, like a ship, toward some sound beneath the floor.

"Can you feel that?" whispered Toma, one of the second-year apprentices, catching at Casper's sleeve as she passed. He was gray-faced. "In your teeth? It's like—"

Like standing on a bridge as something vast passes underneath. Casper felt it in her molars, in the bones of her fingers, a low subsonic shudder that was not quite sound. The lamp flames were all burning perfectly still. Not a single one flickered. That was somehow worse than if they'd all gone mad.

"The wards," she said. "Something's happening to the wards."

"No." It was Master Ondrel, the Keeper of Seals, emerging from the vault stairs with a ring of iron keys in his fist and an expression Casper had never seen on him before. Ondrel was the calmest man she knew; he had once re-cataloged the Room of Weeping Instruments during a fire without raising his voice. Now his hands were shaking so badly the keys chattered against each other. "No, child, it isn't something happening *to* them. They're being *called*. Do you understand me? Something is calling them home."

And then, all together, in the same silent instant, the stars went out.

Casper saw it happen. She would see it for the rest of her life, in the moment before sleep and sometimes inside her dreams: she was standing at the tall window at the end of the corridor, and the whole guild was behind her holding its breath, and the sky — which had been losing its stars one by one, patiently, like a ledger being erased line by line — emptied all at once. Every star. Every constellation. The whole glittering host that had hung above the world since before the guild kept records, gone between one heartbeat and the next, leaving a black so complete and so total that the night itself seemed to have been deleted, and the world left standing in a room with the door closed.

No one screamed. That was the terrible part. Two hundred people looked up at a starless sky for the first time in the history of the guild, and nobody screamed, because there was nothing in the human voice equal to it. There was only the silence — a true silence, deeper than the one before, because now even the subsonic trembling under the floor had stopped — and then somewhere below them, muffled through stone and earth, a single bell tolled once.

It was not a guild bell. None of the bells of the guildhouse rang that night again. This bell came from far away, or from very deep, and it rang with the slow patience of something that had waited a long time to begin counting.

"The doors," said Master Ondrel, and now he did raise his voice, and it cracked doing it. "Seal the doors. Wake the Guildmaster. Seal *everything*."

* * *

They sealed the guild the way a body seals a wound.

Casper worked beside the senior cartographers for three hours without stopping, because work was the only prayer she knew. They locked the Map Room and chained the archive gates. They drew salt lines across every threshold and set the ward-stones humming in their niches. In the Hall of Charts, where the guild's master map of the known world hung framed in silvered wood, Casper helped Ondrel bar the shutters over the great windows — and it was while her arms were raised, hooking the last shutter-iron, that she saw the map.

Every coastline on it had changed.

Not moved — changed. The ink of the frame's reflection lay across the map like a veil, and through that veil Casper could see that the seas had darkened. All of them. The Sunlit Sea, the Glasswater, the cold grey reaches of the north — every ocean on the master map had gone the color of deep water at night, the color of water with nothing above it to reflect, and along every coast, faint but unmistakable, a new line was surfacing through the parchment as though rising from beneath, the way a drowned thing rises.

She did not know what the line said. It was not in any alphabet she knew. But her eyes — cartographer's eyes, trained to read a coast at a glance — told her it was a coastline. A shore belonging to no sea on the map, running beneath all of them, patient as truth.

"Casper." Vess's voice, low, at her shoulder.

Casper turned. Her mentor stood in the doorway of the Hall of Charts holding a case.

It was a ledger case — she knew it at once, had carried a dozen like it — but this one was older than anything in the guild's collection. Black leather gone soft and cracked as riverbed clay, brass corners green with age, and across the front a lock she had never seen the like of: not keyhole nor combination nor sigil-lock, but a small round window of cloudy glass, and behind the glass, slowly turning, a curl of something dark suspended in liquid. Ink, she thought. A lock made of living ink, deciding who it would open for.

"What is it?" Casper asked.

Vess did not answer immediately. Around them the guild thundered with the business of sealing — boots on stairs, orders called, the groan of the great outer doors taking their bars — but within arm's reach of them there was a pocket of quiet, as though the case refused to let the noise near it. Vess looked at the case, and then at Casper, and Casper saw something cross her mentor's face that frightened her more than the empty sky had: guilt. Old, worn-smooth guilt, the kind that has been carried so long it has become part of the hand that holds it.

"Forty years I've kept my promise to a dead woman," Vess said quietly. "Tonight the debt comes due early."

"Vess, you're not making sense. What is this? Whose ledger—"

"Carry it." Vess pressed the case into Casper's hands. It was heavier than it should have been, the way grief is heavier than it should be, and warm, warmer than the room, as though something inside it were alive and slow. "Do not open it. Do not show it to Corvane. Do not let anyone take it from you — and they will try, tonight, and some of them will be people you love. Whatever happens next, whatever you see, whatever you are told about what we are, what we've done—" Vess's voice caught, and she gripped Casper's wrist hard, hard enough to bruise, her eyes bright and terrified and fierce. "You carry it. Promise me."

The single distant bell tolled a second time.

Above the guild, in the black and starless dark, Casper heard — or thought she heard, or perhaps the case heard for her — the sound of water. Very far away. Very deep. Rising.

"I promise," Casper said.

Vess nodded once, released her, and walked back into the noise and lamplight of the guild, leaving Casper standing alone in the Hall of Charts with a locked case warming in her arms like a sleeping animal — and above the barred shutters, unseen, the master map's hidden coastline drawing itself a little closer to the surface of the world.

The third bell began to toll.
