## 4. What the Wards Were For

The Great Hall of the Guild of Impossible Things had been built to hold wonder, and it did so the way a fist holds a bird.

Casper had always loved it — the vaulted ceiling painted with constellations no astronomer would claim, the long tables scarred by two centuries of compass points inked into the wood, the great orrery at the hall's heart that turned through orbits of moons belonging to seas that did not exist. She had taken her oath beneath that orrery at nineteen, her hand still smelling of graphite, Vess's palm warm between her shoulder blades. *We chart what others refuse to see,* she had sworn. It had felt like being handed the world.

Tonight the orrery was dark. Every lamp in the hall burned low, trimmed to a somber amber, and the members of the Guild sat in rows like mourners who had agreed in advance not to weep. Three hundred cartographers, ledger-keepers, ward-scribes, and surveyors, and the silence among them was the worst thing Casper had heard since the stars went out — worse even than that soundless hour when the whole sky above the harbor simply stopped, as if someone had blown out a candle the size of heaven.

She sat near the back with the locked case across her knees. Vess had pressed it into her hands three nights ago with only those two words — *carry it* — and Casper had carried it since: to bed, to breakfast, to the privy, to the narrow window of her room where she'd watched the starless dark and tried to remember what the sky used to look like. The case was iron-banded oak, old enough that its corners had gone soft and round, and it hummed. Not audibly. She felt it in her teeth, in the bones behind her ears, the way one feels an approaching storm. Whatever slept inside it did not wish to be opened.

Vess sat beside her, hands folded, face composed into the particular stillness she wore for funerals and Guild ceremonies. She had not looked at Casper once. That, too, was a kind of speech.

At the front of the hall, beneath the painted constellations that now mocked them, Guildmaster Corvane rose from his chair.

He was not a large man, but he occupied space the way an anchor occupies water. He wore his guildmaster's chain of office — links of silver, each stamped with the sigil of a different impossible thing: the bottled tide, the self-folding road, the map that maps itself. Casper had catalogued that chain once, early in her apprenticeship, entry four hundred and eleven in her first ledger. She remembered the weight of each link in her palms. She had thought then that the sigils were decorative. A history worn as jewelry.

Corvane let the silence stretch until it hurt.

"Three nights ago," he said, and his voice carried without effort, pitched low enough that everyone leaned in, "the border wards fell. All of them, in a single hour, without warning, without failure of craft. You have seen what followed. You have seen the sky." He paused. Somewhere in the hall a quill rolled off a table and no one moved to catch it. "I will not lie to you tonight. The Guild has earned better than comfort. Tonight you will have truth."

Casper's heart performed a strange maneuver — hope and dread arriving together, indistinguishable.

"The wards were not raised against smugglers," Corvane continued. "Not against the empire's surveyors, not against the things that live in the gaps between realms, though they kept those out too, incidentally, the way a wall keeps out both wolves and weather. The wards were raised against the sea."

Murmurs moved through the rows like wind through wheat. Casper felt Vess go rigid beside her.

"Not our sea," said Corvane. "Not any sea you have sailed. There is a realm — I will call it a realm because our language has no better word — that lies adjacent to everything and appears on nothing. We call it the Sundered Sea. It is called sundered because it was. Once, it touched every coast. Once, it appeared on every map. And then, four hundred years ago, the founders of this Guild made certain that it never would again."

The murmurs died. In their place came the particular silence of people listening to something they had always half-known and never dared say aloud. Casper knew that silence intimately; she had heard it in the archives, around certain ledger pages, whenever a name came up too close to the erased entries. She had told herself it was superstition. Guild folk were superstitious the way sailors were, full of rituals that kept the work honest.

But the ledgers. Gods, the ledgers. Pages that resisted being read. Entries whose ink slid off comprehension like water off oiled cloth. She had catalogued a cargo of bottled tides a week ago — actual tides, folded into glass — and when she'd gone back to check the manifest against older shipments of the same, the older manifests had been *smooth*. Not torn out. Smoothed over, as if the paper itself had healed, and healing, she understood now, was a thing paper should not do.

"Four hundred years ago," Corvane said, "there was a god in that sea."

The words dropped into the hall like a stone into a well, and everyone waited for the splash.

"It was not the god your grandmothers warned you about," he went on. "It was older than warning. It held the Sundered Sea together — every current, every coast of it, every island that rose and fell according to its dreaming. And the sea leaned on it, and the god bore the weight, as gods do, until the founders of this Guild decided the arrangement could be improved upon." His voice flattened. "I will not pretend to understand fully why. The records are... incomplete. Deliberately. What I can tell you is this: the founders sealed the god beneath a sigil, and the sigil required payment, and the payment was memory. Not the founders' own. They spent a memory that was never theirs to spend, and the seal has held ever since, anchored —" here his voice faltered, just slightly, the first crack in the anchor-voice — "anchored in the sky."

Casper looked up before she could stop herself. At the ceiling. At the painted constellations.

*Anchored in the stars.* The thought arrived whole, unwelcome, wearing her own handwriting somehow, though she had never written it down. The wards fell and the stars went out on the same night. She had noticed the coincidence — how could anyone not? — but she had filed it under *symptom*, not *cause*. Now the filing system in her chest overturned entirely.

Beside her, Vess's breathing had gone shallow and careful, the rhythm of someone counting.

"The stars were anchors," Corvane said, as if answering her directly, and Casper's skin prickled. "Each star in our sky held a thread of the sigil. This is why the Guild has always kept its own almanacs. Why we have never relied on imperial charts. For four hundred years, the founders' successors — us, all of us, every member past and present — have tended those anchors. Quietly. Without ceremony. Because a seal is strongest when no one remembers it is a seal."

He spread his hands.

"Three nights ago, the anchors failed. Whether the seal itself has cracked, whether something pulled the threads loose from the other side, we do not yet know. What we know is this: the Sundered Sea is pressing against the edges of the world again. The wards were its dam, and the dam is broken, and the water it held back is not water." He paused, and when he spoke again his voice was quieter, which somehow made it worse. "You have all worked with erased pages. You know what erasure costs. Understand that the Sundered Sea is an erasure given weight. Everything it touches, it unmakes from record first. A ship does not sink in the Sundered Sea. Its name leaves every ledger, its crew leaves every memory, and then, and only then, does it go down. By the time the drowning happens, there is no one left to drown."

The hall was very still. Someone near the front began quietly to pray, and the sound of it was obscene, and no one told him to stop.

"We are sealing the doors," Corvane said. "All of them. No member leaves the Hall without sanction. The lower archives are closed pending review —" and here Casper saw, in the third row, a woman named Halloway exchange a glance with the man beside her, a glance with edges on it, sharp and quick, and Casper's stomach turned over with a premonition she could not name — "and I ask each of you to do what the Guild has always done. Keep your ledgers. Chart what you can. Trust the records over your recollections, for your recollections may not remain your own."

He bowed his head. "Questions will be answered as far as answers exist. The Guild endures. We have endured impossibility for four hundred years." A breath. "Dismissed to your sections."

* * *

No one dismissed.

They rose, but they did not leave; they clustered instead, in knots of three and five, speaking in the hushed urgent registers of people rearranging their understanding of the floor beneath them. Casper stood with the locked case hugged to her chest and watched the hall become a tide pool of whispered conferences, and she felt — absurdly, shamefully — like a stranger at her own family's wake.

Because that was the thing. That was the thing sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone.

*The ledgers know more than the guild admits.*

She had known it for weeks, hadn't she? Since the page that resisted reading. Since the manifest that had healed. She had brought her unease to Vess and been patted down like a fire being smothered — *old ledgers decay strangely, child, don't fret* — and she had accepted it because accepting Vess was easier than doubting her. But now Corvane stood beneath a ceiling of fake stars telling three hundred people that the real ones had been load-bearing, and Casper did the arithmetic she had been avoiding:

If the founders had stolen a memory to power the seal — a memory that was never theirs — then the erasures in the ledgers weren't damage. They weren't decay. They were *payments*. Ongoing ones. The Guild had been spending memory for four hundred years, quietly, line by line, route by route, person by person, and every smoothed-over page in the archive was a receipt.

Her hand found the case on reflex. It hummed against her sternum.

"Casper." Vess's voice, close, low. Her mentor's hand settled over hers on the case's lid — warm, familiar, trembling almost imperceptibly. "Come with me. Not now — later. After second bell. There are things you should—"

"You knew." It came out flat, and Casper was startled by her own voice, by the lack of question in it. "About the stars. About the sea. You knew before tonight."

Vess's eyes, gray and kind and old, finally met hers. Something moved behind them — grief, yes, but also a weary sort of relief, the look of someone setting down a load she had carried so long her shoulders had grown around it.

"I have known longer than you have been alive," Vess said softly. "And I have hated every year of knowing, if that helps. It won't. But it's true."

"Why wasn't I told?"

"Because telling is spending." Vess's gaze flicked, briefly, to the case. To Casper's hands on it. Something in her face closed like a door. "Every secret shared is a debt incurred, and this Guild's debts are not paid in coin. Please, Casper. Second bell. The old map room. Come alone, and bring the case, and don't read anything on the way."

Then she was moving away through the crowd, small and straight-backed, and Casper stood alone in the amber lamplight with three hundred murmuring colleagues and a stone in her chest and a humming box in her arms, and the thought arrived with terrible clarity, the way truths do when they've finished circling:

*The ledgers aren't hiding the truth from me. The ledgers are trying to tell me. They've been trying for weeks.*

And beneath it, quieter, colder, a second thought she didn't want, the kind that surfaces at 3 a.m. and stares:

*Why me? Why give the case to me?*

Around her the Guild — her family, the only one she had — murmured about wards and anchors and a god beneath a sigil, and above them all the painted constellations glittered on the ceiling, false and beautiful and bright, the last stars left in anyone's sky.

Second bell was four hours away.

Casper held the case tighter, felt it hum against her heartbeat like something impatient, and began, very quietly, to count the hours.
