## 5. The Tide-Worshippers

The lower archives burned with a strange, tidy fire.

That was the first thing Casper understood as she ran, the second thing after the sound of the door slamming behind her and the taste of smoke settling on her tongue: the flames were orderly. They moved through the shelves in ranks, consuming the ledgers row by row without leaping, without spreading to the stone, without so much as licking at the iron brackets that held the lamps. Someone had taught the fire manners. Someone had given it a list.

She had been two floors down when it began, elbow-deep in the cartographic annex, pulling survey folios for a route she barely remembered commissioning. The alarm had not been a bell. It had been a silence — a sudden, total absence of the guild's usual hum of scratching pens and murmuring clerks — followed by Corvane's voice in her ear, delivered through one of the speaking-tubes that laced the building like veins.

*"Lower archives are closed. All personnel withdraw. This is not a request, Amberdale."*

Then the speaking-tubes had gone quiet too, all of them at once, as if the guild itself had stopped breathing. And then the smell of smoke, sweet and mineral, like salt drying on hot stone, and Casper had understood that whatever was being purged was not merely paper.

She climbed now through the service stairs, the case Vess had given her strapped tight against her ribs beneath her coat. The stairs were full of people going the other way — archivists, junior clerks, an ink-master Casper had known for eleven years, all of them moving with the blank, obedient calm of sleepwalkers. No one wept for the archives. No one even looked back. That frightened her more than the fire did. She caught the ink-master's sleeve as he passed.

"Berrow. What's burning?"

He looked at her hand on his arm as though it were something he'd found on the street. Then his eyes focused, briefly, and there was a flicker in them — fear, real fear, quickly smothered. "Nothing that matters," he said. "Nothing that ever mattered."

"That's not how archives work."

"Go home, Casper." He said her name gently, the way you speak to someone standing at the edge of a cliff. "Some nights the guild has to choose what survives. Don't make yourself one of the things it has to choose about." He pulled free and continued up the stairs, and did not look back either.

Casper stood alone on the landing, listening to the tidy fire eat its way upward through the floors below, and made the decision that would unravel everything: she went *down*.

Not back into the annex — that way was flame — but along the old drainage gallery, a relic of the guildhall's first century, a brick throat that once carried away the wash-water from the map-washing rooms. Every apprentice learned it existed and every master pretended they hadn't. The gallery ran out beneath the guild quarter toward the canal district, and where it met the outer wall there was a grate, and where the grate had rusted there was a gap wide enough for a woman who had spent her childhood wriggling through the undercrofts of a dozen harbors.

The air changed as she crawled. Behind her, smoke. Ahead of her, water-smell — but wrong. Not canal water, not river water. This was deep water, cold water, water that had never seen a sky. It came up through the bricks like breath through lungs, and with it came a sound she felt in her teeth before she heard it with her ears: a long, rhythmic pressure, like something vast turning over in its sleep.

The tide. But there was no sea within forty miles of the guildhall. There never had been. She had mapped the coastline herself, twice.

Casper emerged through the grate into an alley behind the chandlers' row, and the night was black in a way nights had not been black before last week. No stars. The moon a rumor behind cloud. The city's lamps seemed thinner than they should have been, their light pooling close to the wicks as if afraid to reach further. Above the rooftops, where the constellations should have hung — the Cartographer's Quadrant, the Drowned Crown, all the shapes she had charted by since she could hold a stylus — there was nothing. A ceiling of unmarked dark.

She had grown up under those stars. Whoever had erased them had taken a piece of her childhood along with the light.

She got three streets before they found her.

They came out of the dark between lamp-posts the way fish rise to bait — smoothly, without splash. Four figures in oilskin cloaks the color of wet slate, hoods back, faces calm. Not enforcers; she knew the guild's grey-and-brass at a glance. These wore no colors at all, only a single mark at the collar: a spiral, drawn inward, like a wave deciding to become a whirlpool. Their leader was a woman with a scarred jaw and eyes that held the flat patience of deep water.

"Casper Amberdale," the woman said.

Casper's hand went to the knife at her belt. It was a mapmaker's knife, good for cutting vellum, less good for anything else, and everyone present knew it. "You have me at a disadvantage."

"We've been looking for you since the stars went out." The woman tilted her head. "The guild is purging its archives tonight. By morning, everything that points to the Sundered Sea will be ash. Including, I suspect, anyone who has been reading things she shouldn't." Her gaze dropped, deliberate and slow, to Casper's left wrist, where the sleeve had ridden up in her crawl through the gallery.

Where the birthmark sat.

Casper had lived with the mark her whole life — a whorl of pale skin on the inside of her wrist, spiraling tight, the shape of a sigil or a shell. The guild physicians had called it a common naevus. Her mother — the mother whose face she could no longer quite summon — had called it her *little compass*. She had never thought much about it until this month, when she'd noticed the first thing: that in the ledgers of the old founders, in the margins of maps dated a century apart, the same spiral appeared, drawn over and over, always in the same hand.

Now the woman looked at it the way a jeweler looks at a stone she already knows is real.

"You belong to the sea," the woman said simply. "Come with us, and no fire in this city will touch you."

"I don't belong to anyone," Casper said. "And I'm not interested in cults."

Something moved through the four faces — not anger. Amusement, faint and knowing, like light shifting on the seafloor. "A cult," the leader repeated, tasting the word. "Yes. That is what the guild would call us. The guild calls everything it cannot file a cult." She stepped aside, gesturing down the alley toward the canal. "We aren't asking you to believe anything tonight, cartographer. We're asking whether you'd rather be ash in the morning or a guest till dawn. Those are the only offers on the table."

Behind Casper, faintly, she heard boots — the measured, synchronized tread of guild enforcers sweeping the district, checking alley mouths. Grey-and-brass. Coming this way.

She ran. Not toward them. Toward the water.

* * *

They took her down through the canal district by ways she would have sworn were impossible — a flooded culvert that surfaced inside a warehouse, a warehouse whose cellar stairs descended past the level of the foundations into air that smelled of kelp and old stone. Twice she tried to fix their route in her head for later mapping, and twice she lost it, the turns sliding out of memory like water through fingers. Whatever craft these people practiced, it included making themselves unmappable. The realization chilled her almost as much as the guild's fire had. She knew only one other thing in the world that resisted being charted.

At the bottom of the last stair was a door of green bronze, crusted with verdigris, worked with waves. Beyond it: the drowned cathedral.

She had heard of it the way one hears of ghosts — a sunken chapel from the old harbor district, drowned two centuries ago when the canals were cut, officially demolished, unofficially forgotten. It was not forgotten. It rose around her now, vault upon vault, its nave filled to knee-height with black, perfectly still water that reflected nothing — not the candles floating on little wooden rafts, not the vaulted ceiling, not the dozens of robed figures standing waist-deep in silence among the pillars. The water lay like a sheet of polished obsidian, holding the candlelight on its surface and giving none of it back.

And everywhere, carved into pillar and pew and flagstone, the spiral. Inward-turning. Over and over. The mark on her wrist, multiplied ten thousand times.

"They say the cathedral drowned itself," said a voice beside her, "rather than stand in a city that had agreed to forget."

Casper turned. The man who had spoken stood a pace off, and she understood immediately why the others had parted for him. He was younger than she expected — no older than forty — with dark hair going grey at the temples and a face built for kindness that had clearly been tested hard and often. He wore the same undyed wool as his people, but at his throat the spiral was rendered in what looked disturbingly like pearl. His eyes were the color of the water in the nave, which is to say they gave back more than they took.

"Sable," he said. "Priest of the Drowned God. And you are standing in my church, Casper Amberdale, dripping canal water on two hundred years of patient prayer."

"Your church drowned," Casper said. "I'm just standing in the wreck."

Sable smiled, and the smile was warm, and she hated herself a little for how much warmth she found in it. "So it did. Come. Walk with me. You may keep your knife; you may keep your skepticism. I ask only your attention, and I'll pay for it honestly — with truth, which is more than your guild has ever paid anyone."

He walked her along the raised rim of the nave, above the still water, while the congregation watched without staring. He pointed as he talked, casually, the way she might point out landmarks on a familiar coast.

"Your guild tells you the Sundered Sea is a fable. A realm that keeps failing to exist. They commissioned you yourself to map it, didn't they? And the realm declined to hold still for you." He glanced sideways, reading her flinch exactly right. "It declines because it is grieving. Something was taken from it — taken and buried beneath a seal, and the seal was paid for with a theft. The wards your masters maintain are not protection, Casper. They are a lid on a pot. And the pot has been boiling for a very long time."

"The wards fell," Casper said. "Last week. The stars went out."

"Yes." For a moment the warmth in his face went utterly still, and beneath it she saw something enormous move, the way a shadow moves under ice. "The stars went out because the anchors failed. Because the debt came due. Your founders borrowed against a memory that was never theirs, and the note has come to collection." He turned to face her fully, and took her left wrist in both hands — gently, asking permission with his grip before he had it, and she let him, and hated that she let him.

He turned her arm palm-up and laid the wrist bare to the candlelight. The whorled mark stood pale against her skin.

"Do you know what this is?" he asked softly.

"A birthmark."

"It is a countersignature." His thumb traced the spiral without touching it. "When the founders sealed our god, the seal demanded a price: a memory, freely surrendered, of the one person who could undo it. So they found someone whose memory could be taken — someone young, someone unguarded — and they took her entire first life. Her name. Her family. Her coast. Everything, down to the ground she stood on. And they wrote the theft into her flesh as a signature, so that the seal would know its own hostage." He released her wrist. "You have been walking around for twenty-six years wearing the receipt for the greatest crime your guild ever committed."

The cathedral was very quiet. The water did not ripple. Somewhere far above, muffled by stone and city, Casper could almost hear the tidy fire still eating its way through the lower archives, burning the evidence of a crime she was apparently the living document of.

"That's a story," she managed. "A good one. You tell it to everyone you fish out of alleys?"

"I tell it to people with that mark," Sable said. "There are none others alive. Which is rather the point." He smiled again, gentler now. "Ask your mentor. Ask Vess what happened to the founder named Amberdale. Ask her why your own handwriting appears on maps older than your grandmother. Ask her what the guild does with memories it borrows." He stepped back, giving her space, giving her the choice, and the choice sat between them like a third person. "We want the same thing you want, cartographer. The truth, restored. The names, returned. The difference is that we are willing to drown for it, and your guild is willing to burn for the opposite."

"And if I walk out of here and report all of you?"

"Then you do," Sable said simply. "The water gives and the water takes. But you won't. Not yet. Because you looked at that mark on your wrist just now not like a stranger's brand, but like a word in a language you almost remember. And I know that feeling. It is the loneliest feeling in the world." He inclined his head toward the stair. "Our door stands open, Casper Amberdale. Tonight and always. The tide remembers what the land forgets."

She climbed the stair. At the top, in the dark of the warehouse cellar, she paused and looked back down at the green bronze door, at the faint glow rising from the drowned nave below.

Her wrist ached. Or perhaps it sang. She could no longer tell the difference.

She should go to Vess. She should demand answers, confront, accuse. That was the sensible sequence, and she had always been a sensible woman.

But as she stood in the dark with the guild's fire at her back and the sea's promise below, one question rose in her louder than all the rest, and it was not *is it true* — some part of her had already stopped asking that — it was:

*If they stole my whole first life, what else did they steal?*

And behind her, from the depths of the drowned cathedral, faint as breath against glass, she heard the water begin — very softly — to sing.
