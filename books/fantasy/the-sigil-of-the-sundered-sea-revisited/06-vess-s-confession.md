## 6. Vess's Confession

Vess had counted the bells of the Guild of Impossible Things for forty years, and she knew their language the way other people knew their mothers' voices.

The ninth bell rang wrong that night.

Not broken — she would have noticed a broken bell; she had personally rehung three of them after the Quake of the Salt Year, and she loved them each with the particular devotion of someone who has sweated over a thing. No, this was subtler. The ninth bell rang and the sound went *out* instead of *through*, swallowed at the edge of the courtyard as if the night air had grown a throat. She stood at her window with her hands pressed flat against the cold glass and listened to her guild eat its own echo, and she understood — with the calm, terrible clarity that comes to people who have been waiting for something for decades — that it had begun.

Below her, in the courtyard, Casper Amberdale stood in the falling ash-light with the locked ledger case hugged against her chest like a child carrying a kitten through a storm.

*Carry it,* Vess had told her. Two words. Forty years of cowardice compressed into two words, because she had not been able to say what she actually meant: *carry it because I am too old and too frightened and too guilty, carry it because you are the only one of us who can, carry it because I have known since the day I pulled you out of the harbor wrack that this night would come and I spent every one of those days hoping I would die first.*

Vess dressed quickly. Wool over wool, the good boots, the key on its cord around her neck where it had lain against her sternum so long she sometimes forgot it was there and sometimes could think of nothing else. She took the lamp from her desk — the old lamp, whale-oil and brass, older than the guild itself by anyone's honest accounting — and she went down into the dark to find the girl she had raised on borrowed time.

* * *

The lower corridors were empty in a way corridors should never be empty.

Guild halls were living things. Even at midnight they breathed — apprentices sneaking down for smuggled wine, archivists who slept among their shelves rather than climb back to their cells, the endless soft industry of people who had made a religion of cataloging what ought not exist. Tonight the halls were silent as a held breath. The wards had fallen an hour past midnight, all of them at once, along the whole border, and the stars had gone out with them — not dimmed, not clouded, *gone*, plucked from the sky like stitches from a hem — and every soul in the building had retreated behind sealed doors to pretend, collectively and with great dignity, that none of it was happening.

That was the guild way. Vess had taught it herself, in her gentler years: when the impossible comes to the door, you do not open it; you write it down, you lock the page away, and you pour yourself a cup of tea. It had seemed like wisdom once. Tonight it felt like the practiced stillness of prey.

She found Casper in the map room.

Of course she did. The girl had been found in the map room at six years old, asleep beneath the great chart of the Northern Reaches, ink on her cheek, and she had been found in the map room at every crisis since. Now she sat at the long table with lamps lit all around her — four, five, six of them, more light than the room needed, which told Vess everything about how the dark felt tonight — and before her lay the locked case, unopened, and beside the case, spread flat, the page she had shown Vess a week ago. The page that resisted being read. The page whose ink slid off the eye like water off oiled leather.

Casper looked up. Her face in the lamplight was pale but steady, and Vess felt the old ache move through her chest, the one that was equal parts love and dread, because the girl had her father's stubborn jaw and her mother's way of looking at you as though lies were simply maps drawn badly.

"You're awake," Casper said. "I half hoped no one was."

"I don't sleep well when the stars go out."

"Nobody does. Half the apprentices are sitting up telling stories about eclipses." Casper's mouth did something that wanted to be a smile and didn't get there. "They'll run out of eclipse stories by morning. Then they'll start asking questions we can't answer."

"We can answer them," Vess said. "We've always been able to answer them. That was rather the problem."

She said it quietly, and she watched the words land — watched Casper go very still in the way she went still when a chart showed a current where no current should be, that cartographer's stillness, the whole body becoming an instrument tuned to a single reading.

"Vess," Casper said carefully. "What did you give me?"

Vess set the lamp down on the table. Her hand was not quite steady, and she hated that; she had imagined this conversation a thousand times across four decades and in none of those imaginings did her hand shake. In most of them she was braver than this. In several of them she was already dead, which had begun to seem like the easier version.

"The case is warded," she said instead. "It will open for you and no one else. It was made that way forty years ago, before—" She stopped. Started again. "Before you were born. Before the second time you were born."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight and grain, like sand shifting underfoot.

"Second time," Casper repeated.

Vess pulled out the chair opposite her and sat, because her knees had informed her that standing was no longer among the evening's options. The lamp between them threw their two shadows up the wall behind Casper, tall and wavering, and Vess thought absurdly that they looked like the painted figures on the old sea-charts, the wind-blowers and wave-makers, the little gods cartographers used to draw in the corners of the unknown.

"I am going to tell you a story," Vess said. "And then you are going to hate me, and I want you to hear the whole story before you do, because the hating will be earned but the story deserves better than to be interrupted."

"Tell me."

* * *

"When the guild was founded," Vess began, "there were eleven of us. Eleven cartographers who had all, independently, charted things that could not be charted — a river that flowed uphill through a village that didn't remember being flooded, a forest that appeared on no two maps in the same place, a road you could walk only while singing. We found each other the way such people always find each other. Slowly, and by accident, and with a great deal of arguing about projection methods.

"And we decided that the world deserved better than to have its impossibilities scattered and forgotten. So we built the guild. A house for the things the world couldn't hold. And for thirty years it was good, Casper. It was the best work any of us ever did. We mapped the Whispering Steppes. We proved the existence of the Second Moon and then had the wisdom to lose our notes about it. We were happy."

Casper hadn't moved. "You said eleven."

"I'm getting there." Vess wrapped her hands around the lamp's base, needing something warm to hold. "There was a twelfth member. Not one of the founders — she came later, or perhaps earlier; time behaved oddly around her. She was a cartographer of the sea. Not *a* sea. The sea underneath all seas, the deep water that every ocean is only a puddle above. She called it the Sundered Sea, and she said — she swore — that it had once been part of the world. Whole coastlines. Cities. A nation with songs and laws and a name that everyone alive had forgotten, because it hadn't merely sunk below the water. It had been erased. Taken out of memory itself, root and branch, so completely that even the word for it survived only as a feeling — the feeling you get looking out at the horizon and grieving for no reason."

"That feeling," Casper said slowly, "has a name in the old ledgers. *Thalassalgia.* The homesickness for water you've never seen."

"Yes." Vess closed her eyes. "Yes, exactly that."

"And the twelfth member —"

"She was the last one who remembered. That was why she'd survived, I think, why she'd been spared when everything else was taken. Someone needed to remember enough to grieve. Or perhaps it was cruelty, leaving one witness. I never learned which." Vess opened her eyes and made herself look at Casper, made herself hold the look, because she owed the girl at least that much courage. "What she remembered was not only a drowned country. It was what had drowned it. There was a power in that deep water, Casper. A god — though 'god' is a small word for it, and we used it carelessly, the way people use words for things they fear. It was the keeper of that coast. Its guardian. And it had been bound — imprisoned beneath its own homeland — by people who wanted the sea's roads for trade, who found a guardian inconvenient, who bound it and then, when binding wasn't enough, erased everything it had ever protected so that no one would ever come asking questions. No one would ever come looking for what was buried. You cannot free what no one remembers exists."

Casper's hand had drifted to the locked case without her seeming to notice. "The erasures in the ledgers."

"The same craft. The same hand, maybe, or hands taught by the same teacher. Memory can be spent like coin, Casper. That's the secret at the bottom of everything we do. Every map we keep, every impossible thing we file away — somewhere, someone paid for the forgetting. Usually it's small. A name here, an afternoon there. The founders learned the craft from the people who erased the Sundered Sea, and they thought — gods forgive us, we *thought* — that if the tool existed anyway, better it should belong to people with scruples."

"And the god?"

"It broke its chains." Vess heard her own voice go thin. "Of course it did. It had nothing left but rage, and rage is a lever that moves anything. It rose, and the seas rose with it, and the twelve of us — eleven founders and the last remembering woman — understood that the world as it stood would not survive its grief. So we did what cartographers do. We drew a line. A sigil, vast as a coastline, anchored in the sky itself — every star in the heavens became a pin holding the seal shut. And the seal required payment. Binding magic always does. The price was memory."

Whose memory. Vess watched Casper arrive at the question before she asked it — watched her lips form it and then stop, because some part of her had already read the answer in the margins of a map dated forty years before her birth, in handwriting identical to her own.

"A memory that was never ours to spend," Casper whispered.

"The woman who remembered the Sundered Sea — she refused to pay. She said she was the last witness, that her memory was the country's grave-marker, and she would not spend it, and she was right, and it didn't matter. The others paid in pieces. Corvane's predecessors gave up their childhoods, their loves, whole decades. But the seal demanded a keystone. One complete life, freely given — or given anyway; the sigil was never particular about *freely*. A life with the right shape. A cartographer's life. A life that had touched the sea." Vess's voice failed her for a moment, and she let it, because the pause was true and the truth deserved its silence. "There was one among us whose life fit. She was young. She was brilliant. She had been born on a ship, in sight of water, and every line of her future bent seaward. And she was — we told ourselves this, afterward, to survive it — she was willing."

"No," Casper said. Not a question. A refusal, quiet and absolute, the refusal of someone standing at the edge of a cliff they have already stepped off.

"She walked into the sigil's center and she was taken out of the world. Not killed. *Erased.* Every record, every recollection, every footprint. Her parents forgot her face. The sea forgot her ships. And the seal held, because the debt was paid, and the stars burned on as anchors, and the guild wrote the whole affair into a single ledger and then locked the ledger and then — because even locked pages can be found — we scrubbed ourselves. All of us. We cut the memory out of our own heads, because Corvane's predecessor argued, and rightly, that a secret kept by people who remember it is not a secret; it is a wound that talks."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere far above, the tenth bell tried to ring and produced only a breath of sound, like the building sighing in its sleep.

"So the founders' bargain," Casper said, and her voice was frighteningly level, "is that a god rots under a stolen seal, bought with a stolen life, guarded by people who stole their own memories of the theft."

"Yes."

"And the stars going out—"

"The seal is failing." Vess laid her palm flat on the table, on the wood worn smooth by forty years of her elbows, as if the familiar grain could anchor her. "A seal is a promise, Casper, and promises are paid in installments whether you agree to them or not. The founders' payments are spent. The anchors are burning down like candles. And the debt — the original debt, the life that was taken — it hasn't been discharged. It's been *deferred*. Debts of that size don't vanish. They accrue. They wait. And they come due." She finally said it. Forty years, and it took less air than a whisper. "The debt comes due in a life of the same shape as the one we took. A cartographer. Born of the sea. Marked by the sigil."

Slowly, Casper raised her left hand and turned it over, and there in the lamplight, faint as a watermark, the birthmark stood up along the inside of her wrist: the interlocking lines that every apprentice assumed were a stylized compass rose, that Vess had kissed goodnight ten thousand times and never once allowed herself to truly see.

"This isn't a birthmark," Casper said.

"No."

"It's a signature. It's the founder's mark — the one in the margins of the map that shouldn't exist." Her voice cracked at last, just once, a hairline fracture running through all that steadiness. "Vess. Whose handwriting is in that margin? Whose life paid for the seal? What was her name?"

And there it was. The question Vess had dreaded for four decades, the question she had rehearsed answers to in a hundred sleepless dawns, and now that it had arrived she discovered that the honest answer was also the cruelest one and also the only one she had left.

"We erased her name," Vess said. "We were thorough. That was the whole horror of it — we were *thorough*. I have spent forty years trying to recall it and there is only a hole where it lived, a hole shaped exactly like a person." She reached across the table and covered Casper's marked wrist with her hand, gently, the way you touch something you're afraid will break or burn. "But I know the shape of the hole, child. I raised you inside it. I chose your name myself — *Amberdale* — because it surfaced from the hole one night like a bubble from deep water, and I was too much of a coward to wonder where it came from. When I pulled you from the harbor wreck at six years old, with no family and no history and ink already under your fingernails, I told the guild you were a foundling. I told *myself* you were a foundling. But the sea does not deliver children to doorsteps by accident, and neither does fate. You are not near that missing woman, Casper. As near as I can bear to say it, and nearer than I have ever said aloud—"

"You think I'm her," Casper said. "Returned."

"I think the debt doesn't care what you are. I think the sigil knows its own signature. I think the stars went out because something under the water has finally recognized the shape of what was taken from it, walking around in the world again with a new name and my old lies stitched into its pockets." Vess's thumb moved over the mark, back and forth, a gesture from a decade of bedtime comfort performed now on the wrist of a grown woman who might be the reason the sky was empty. "I have lied to you since the day I found you. Every kindness I gave you was built on top of that lie, and the terrible thing, the unforgivable thing, is that the kindnesses were real. I would do all of it again. That is what I have learned tonight, saying this out loud: I am not sorry I loved you. I am only sorry I made you grow up inside a cage and called it a home."

For a long moment Casper said nothing. The lamps burned. The shadows of the two women climbed the wall and trembled there like figures on a chart of an unmapped coast.

Then Casper turned her hand over — slowly, deliberately — until her fingers laced through Vess's, grip firm, and Vess felt something in her chest crack open that she had soldered shut forty years ago.

"The case," Casper said. "It opens for me."

"It was keyed to her. To the life that was taken. I have carried the knowledge of it for forty years waiting for the night it mattered." Vess swallowed. "Open it, and you'll find her ledger. The real one. Everything the founders wrote before they made themselves forget — including how the seal works, including what's failing, including—" She stopped, because the next words were the ones she had promised herself she would say and now found lodged like a fishbone. "Including a list. Of names the woman wrote herself, before the end. People she loved. A family. And Casper — the names are gone. Scrubbed off the page by her own erasure. Except one. One name survived the scrubbing, and I have never understood why, and I have been afraid of it for forty years, and it is *Amberdale*. It is your name. Written in her hand. On the last legible line."

Casper's breath caught audibly.

Above them, faint through stone and timber, the eleventh bell attempted its stroke and made no sound at all.

"The bells are dying," Vess whispered. "One by one, oldest first. Whatever is rising under the Sundered Sea is counting down, child, and I no longer believe we have until dawn."
