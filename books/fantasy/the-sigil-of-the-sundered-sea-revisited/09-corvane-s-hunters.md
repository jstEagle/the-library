## 9. Corvane's Hunters

The drowned cathedral's bells rang without sound.

Casper felt them anyway — a pressure behind her sternum, a rhythm like a second heartbeat that did not belong to her. She had been in the nave for the better part of an hour, standing beneath arches furred with pale coral, watching Sable light candles that burned blue-green and gave off the smell of low tide and old wood. The tide-worshippers moved around her with a reverence she had not expected. They did not touch her. They did not ask questions. They simply made room, the way one makes room for weather.

"You keep looking at the doors," Sable said, coming to stand beside her. He held two cups of something hot and brackish — kelp tea, she guessed, though it smelled sweeter than anything the surface grew. "There are no guards at them. There never have been."

"That's what worries me." She took the cup because refusing felt like a small cruelty, and she had been cruel enough lately, in her own head, to everyone who had ever lied to her. "Your people sing to a god under a seal your prophecy says is failing. Mine send hunters after anyone who reads the wrong ledger page. And I'm standing in the middle with a stolen case of erased entries and no idea which side of the door I'm on."

"Is there a door?" Sable asked mildly.

She looked at him. Firelight from the blue-green candles moved across his face and made him look older than he was, or perhaps younger — it was hard to say with him. He had the kind of face that settled into whatever expression you brought to it.

"There's always a door," Casper said. "That's the first thing the guild teaches. *Every map is a negotiation with a door.*"

"And the second thing?"

"*Never draw a coastline you haven't walked.*" She almost smiled. It hurt to almost smile; it was becoming a habit. "Why do you know our catechisms, Sable? Your priests quote Corvane's own founding lectures back at me like scripture. Where does a tide-cult learn the Guild of Impossible Things by heart?"

Sable turned his cup slowly in his hands. For a moment he said nothing, and in that silence Casper heard the cathedral breathing — the slow ingress of water somewhere below, the drip and settle of a building that had been drowning gracefully for centuries.

"Because we were the Guild once," he said. "Or part of it. Before the Sundering, before the seal, there were cartographers who thought a god deserved charting rather than caging. They were scrubbed out too. We kept their pages. That's all worship is, Casper. Keeping pages someone else tried to burn."

The words landed harder than he could have known — or perhaps he knew exactly, and that was the trouble with Sable. Everything he said landed where it was aimed.

Before she could answer, the sound came.

Not loud. That was the horror of it. A single knock at the great doors of the drowned cathedral — three knocks, evenly spaced, patient as a tax collector — and every candle in the nave guttered at once, as if the flame itself had flinched.

Sable's cup stopped turning.

"They found us," Casper whispered.

"Yes," Sable said. And then, so quietly she nearly missed it: "Good."

* * *

The word went through her like a splinter working toward the bone.

"Good?" She set down the tea untouched. "Sable. What did you do?"

He was already moving, unhurried, lifting a hanging lantern from its bracket, and he would not look at her. Around the nave his congregation had gone still — not frightened, she realized with a coldness spreading through her chest, but *ready*. They were folding into positions along the pillars, drawing hoods up, moving crates aside from a rear passage as though this had been rehearsed. As though this had been the plan all along.

"Sable." She caught his sleeve. Under her fingers his arm might have been carved from driftwood for all the give it had. "Look at me. What did you do?"

He looked at her then, and his eyes were kind, and that was the worst thing about it.

"I let them come," he said.

The cathedral seemed to tilt. Or perhaps that was only the tide, rising through channels beneath the floor, responding to something — to the seal far below weakening another degree, or to the truth arriving in her body ahead of her understanding.

"Why," she managed. Her voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice. "Tell me why."

"Because they will not negotiate with us, Casper. We are heretics and drowners. But you—" he touched the sigil-marked birthmark at her wrist, gently, the way one touches a wound that belongs to someone else, "—you are Amberdale. Founder's blood, founder's hand, founder's name. Corvane's hunters cannot kill you and they cannot leave you. You are the one piece on the board neither side can afford to lose. Which makes you the one piece worth taking."

"You're using me."

"I'm *saving* you," he said, and there was such terrible sincerity in it that she believed he half-believed it. "When they breach those doors they take you back to the guild, yes — but they also confirm you're alive, unharmed, *ours*. Every hour the guild believes we hold their lost cartographer, every rumor, every ransom note, buys my people time to reach the sigil before the debt comes due. You are not a hostage, Casper. You are a lantern. You make us visible."

"A hostage is exactly what I am," she said. "You've just given it a nicer name. You want to know the difference between you and the guild? There isn't one. You both just erase different things. The guild erases pages. You erase *people* — you look at me and see a name, a mark, a use. Nobody in this whole salted world has looked at me once and seen Casper."

The knock came again. Three more. Closer together now. Behind the doors, she could hear the soft many-footed sound of enforcers taking position — the guild's hunters, quiet as falling snow, thorough as rot.

Sable's jaw tightened. For one moment something else crossed his face — regret, or grief, or the memory of being a person himself — and then the priest reassembled over it like water closing over a stone.

"The back passage leads down," he said quickly, low. "To the undercroft, and from there to the deep ways. My people will hold the doors—"

"Your people will *die* holding the doors," Casper said. "For a plan I wasn't consulted on. For a god I haven't agreed to free. You didn't save me, Sable. You spent me. And you didn't even have the decency to lie well enough that I wouldn't notice."

The doors boomed.

Not knocked this time — struck, with something heavy, with intent. Salt water shivered in the font. One of the hooded figures near the doors began a low chant, and others took it up, and the sound was beautiful and useless, a hymn thrown against a battering ram.

Sable grabbed her arm. "Casper—"

"Don't." She pulled free. "Don't touch me. Don't pray over me. Don't tell me the sea chose me. I'm done being chosen. Everyone keeps choosing for me — the guild chose before I was born, you chose last night, and I am so tired of being a page in other people's ledgers."

The doors split down the middle with a crack like ice going out in spring.

Through the gap came gray light and gray shapes — enforcers in the guild's slate-gray oilskins, faces masked with waxed cloth, moving in the practiced silence of people who had done this in a hundred archives. Their leader carried a hooked pole wound with silver wire, and when the light caught the wire Casper saw the fine etched script running along it: containment glyphs, guild-made, guild-licensed, guild-blessed.

They were here for a person. They had come prepared to take one.

"Casper Amberdale," the leader said. The voice was muffled, sexless, almost gentle. "By order of Guildmaster Corvane. Come home."

Home. The word was a hook baited with everything she'd lost. Vess's kitchen. The smell of ink and lamp oil. The long table where she'd learned to draw coastlines. All of it real, all of it loved, all of it built on a theft so large it had a sea inside it.

Behind her, Sable shouted something — a command, or an apology, she couldn't tell and no longer cared. His people broke from cover, chanting, hurling bottles of bottled tide that burst against the enforcers' shields in blooms of captured ocean, waves the size of a fist crashing and dying on waxed canvas. It was brave and futile and it bought seconds, nothing more.

Casper ran.

Not toward the enforcers. Not toward Sable. Toward the dark.

The back passage Sable had described was there, just past the shattered reliquary — a stair going down into blackness that breathed cold and wet. She had no torch, no instrument, nothing but the locked ledger case strapped to her back and the birthmark burning at her wrist like a brand remembering its origin. Behind her she heard Sable scream her name — actually scream it, *Casper*, raw and human — and heard the wet thud of bodies, and the chant collapsing into coughing, and the leader's muffled voice calling, patient even now: *"Miss Amberdale. There is nowhere to go that is not on a map."*

She smiled, wild and breathless, in the dark.

"That," she said to no one, to everyone, to the god below and the founders above and the girl she used to be before everyone started spending her, "*is exactly where you're wrong.*"

The stairs ended. Beyond them lay the space between realms — the unmapped dark she had glimpsed only once, in the margins of a map forty years older than herself. No walls. No floor she trusted. Only distance the way dreams have distance, and cold, and a silence so complete she could hear her own heart negotiating with it.

Somewhere behind her, the drowned cathedral fell silent. Somewhere above, Sable was kneeling in the wreckage of his own prophecy, and the guild's hunters were reporting failure, and both of them — priest and guildmaster alike — were learning what Casper Amberdale had just learned:

That she belonged to no one.

That the dark between realms had been waiting for her longer than either of them had been alive.

And that somewhere out in that vast and listening blackness, a lighthouse was burning — though no map had ever marked it, and no light should have been possible where all the stars had gone out.

She felt it before she saw it: a pull, faint and certain, like a thread tied somewhere behind her ribs.

She stepped off the edge of the mapped world, and followed it down.
