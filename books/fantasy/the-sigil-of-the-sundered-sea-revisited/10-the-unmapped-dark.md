## 10. The Unmapped Dark

The dark between realms was not empty. That was the first thing Casper learned, and it took her a long time to learn it, because emptiness would have been kinder.

She had fled through the breach Sable's ritual had torn — or the enforcers' blades had widened; she still could not say which — and for a while there had been falling, or something like falling, the sensation of it without the direction. Then the sensation stopped too, and she was simply *here*: suspended in a darkness that pressed against her eyes like water that refused to be water. No floor. No sky. Her lantern burned with a small stubborn flame that cast light perhaps three feet in any direction, and beyond those three feet the dark did not end so much as *decline to continue*.

Her instruments were useless. She checked them anyway, because checking instruments was what she did when the world went wrong, and because the ritual of it steadied her hands. The astrolabe spun lazily, its rings finding no purchase on anything. The compass needle — guild-forged, attuned not to north but to the nearest mapped coastline — swept in slow, drunken circles, as though drunk was the only honest state left available to it. The sextant gave her an angle of elevation to a horizon that did not exist, which meant it gave her nothing at all.

"Right," she said aloud, and her voice fell dead a foot from her lips, absorbed rather than echoed. "Right. Think."

But thinking was the problem, wasn't it. Because every tool of her trade assumed a world that agreed to be measured, and this place made no such agreement. She thought of Vess's old lesson — *a map is a promise you make to a place* — and felt the words turn bitter in her chest. Vess, who had lied to her. Corvane, who had sent hunters. Sable, who had smiled at her with such warm conviction right up until the moment his people closed around her like a net and she understood she had been cargo all along, a relic to be delivered to the sea.

Everyone wanted something from her. Everyone had always wanted something from her. And here, in the dark where no one could reach her, the wanting finally stopped, and what remained was just Casper Amberdale: twenty-six years old (or forty years older than that, if the handwriting in the margins was hers, and she was increasingly afraid that it was), carrying a locked case full of erased truths through a geography that refused to have any.

The case was still slung across her back. Small mercies.

She began to walk, because standing still felt too much like surrender, and after some unmeasurable time — minutes, hours, she genuinely could not tell — the dark changed under her feet. Not into ground, exactly. Into *suggestion* of ground: a texture like cobbles remembered rather than laid, each stone slightly wrong, as if drawn by someone describing cobblestones from a letter they'd once received.

She knelt and touched one. It was warm. Cobblestones should not be warm.

And then she knew where she was — knew it with a lurch, before any evidence arrived — because these were the cobbles of Tanner's Row in Lower Saltmarsh, where she had lived until she was nine, and she had fallen and skinned both knees on this exact stretch when she was six, chasing a paper boat down the gutter after a rainstorm, and her mother had —

Her mother had —

The thought hit a wall. Not a wall of forgetting, exactly. A wall of *unbeing*, smooth and total, like pressing her palm against the surface of deep water. She knew there had been a mother. She knew there had been a knee, two knees, blood and rain and a paper boat going soft and sinking in the gutter while she cried. But the face above her, the hands that had cleaned the scrapes, the voice — gone. Not hidden. *Gone*, the way the ledger pages were gone, the way whole coastlines were gone from maps that showed open ocean where cities used to be.

The cobbles flickered. For half a heartbeat the street around her sharpened — rain-smell, gull-cries, the orange smear of lamplight through wet air — and then it guttered out like her lantern almost had, and the dark came back, and the cobbles under her hands were only almost-cobbles again.

Casper sat back on her heels, heart hammering.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, that's how it works."

The dark responded to memory. Not to instruments. Not to will, or at least not to will alone. To *memory* — and it responded the way a mirror responds, faithfully but inverted, giving her back not what she remembered but what she remembered *well enough to believe*. The Tanner's Row cobbles had been warm because she remembered them warm from summer days, even though the day of the paper boat had been cold rain. The dark didn't care about accuracy. It cared about conviction.

Which meant, she realized, with a feeling like cold fingers closing around her spine, that the dark also cared about doubt.

She stood up carefully, the way one stands up on river ice.

* * *

She tested it the way she tested everything: methodically, taking notes by lantern-light in the back of her field journal, her cartographer's hand steady even when the rest of her was not.

*Item:* She pictured the guild's map room in exhaustive detail — the long oak tables scarred by a century of compass-points, the smell of ink and beeswax, the tall windows with their leaded panes that turned sunlight green-gold, the great chart of the Northern Reaches hung from iron rods. She held it in her mind the way she held a pen, firmly, and counted her breaths.

The dark obliged. Slowly, reluctantly, like a cat deciding to trust, the space around her filled: tables, windows, chart. The light through the panes was green-gold and sourceless, since there was no sun to come through them, but her mind supplied the sun anyway and the dark accepted the amendment. She walked to the table and put her hand on the oak and felt the grain, felt the old burn-mark near the third chair where an apprentice named Doss had once dropped a candle, and she laughed — actually laughed, alone in the void — because it *worked*, it worked perfectly, she had built a room out of remembering.

Then she thought, unavoidably, of Vess standing at that table. Vess's hands smoothing a chart flat. Vess saying, *Casper, love, don't worry your head over blank pages.*

The room shuddered. The windows went grey. The burn-mark faded under her fingers like breath off glass, and for a horrible moment the whole map room seemed to lean toward her and ask, silently, *are you sure? are you sure you want to keep any of this?* — because every memory of the guild now carried Vess in it, and every memory of Vess now carried the lie, and the lie made the memory less solid, and the less solid memory made the room less real, and Casper stood in her own remembered sanctuary watching it dissolve because she could no longer remember it *innocently*.

"That's not fair," she whispered. The room finished dissolving. The dark took it back without malice, the way the tide takes back a sandcastle.

She wrote in her journal, hand shaking now:

*The dark builds from belief. Grief is a solvent here. Doubt is a solvent here. I must carry only memories I can bear to hold completely, or I will unmake my own ground.*

It was the loneliest sentence she had ever written, and she had once catalogued the personal effects of a cartographer eaten by his own atlas.

* * *

She learned the rules the way she learned everything else about impossible things: by breaking most of them first.

Rule the first: *carry few memories, but carry them whole.* She found that small, complete memories held far better than grand, complicated ones. The exact weight of her drafting pen — three ounces, brass, the nib worn to a slight leftward bias by her hand. The taste of the salt-bread the dockside bakeries sold, dense and faintly sweet. The sound the guild's front doors made, that particular iron groan that every member learned to sleep through. These she could hold entire, without contradiction, without grief clinging to their edges, and wherever she walked with them held firmly in mind, the dark furnished her passage: a dock, a bakery street, the guild steps rising out of nothing under her boots.

Rule the second: *never remember anything you aren't sure happened.* This one cost her. Twice she tried to build herself a childhood — the cottage in Lower Saltmarsh, the paper boat, the mother — and twice the dark gave her something worse than nothing: a half-built mockery, walls without interiors, a kitchen with a table set for one, and a shape moving at the edge of the lamplight that might have been a woman and might have been a hole in the world wearing a woman's outline. Both times Casper ran, and both times the dark let her run, and she suspected — with the professional instinct for a trap that had kept her alive through seven commissioned surveys — that it could have stopped her whenever it liked.

Because that was rule the third, the rule she hated most: *the dark is not passive.* It furnished what she remembered. But it also, occasionally, furnished things she didn't. Once, walking a corridor of remembered guild-stone toward nowhere, she passed a doorway that was not in any building she knew — tall, arched, crusted with salt — and from behind it came the sound of waves, patient and vast, and beneath the waves a voice singing in a language she did not know and yet somehow understood, the way you understand a lullaby sung to you before you could speak. She did not stop. She walked faster. The singing followed her for what felt like a mile and then, gently, let her go.

Once, she found a footprint in the almost-ground ahead of her. Boot-shaped, recent, filled with a thin film of water that reflected stars.

There were no stars. There was no sky. There was no one else here.

She looked at the print for a long time. Then she stepped around it, very carefully, the way you step around a sleeping animal, and walked on.

* * *

Time behaved strangely, and Casper — who had spent her career arguing with time zones, tidal calendars, and the Guild of Impossible Things' notoriously optimistic delivery schedules — decided early that fighting it would only spend strength she needed. She slept when the lantern dimmed, ate hardtack from her satchel in careful rationed portions, drank from her waterskin and worried quietly about what one drank when the water ran out in a realm with no rain.

In the sleeps, she dreamed of the sea. Not the Sundered Sea — she had never seen it, no one alive had seen it, that was the entire point — but the ordinary gray-green sea of Saltmarsh, rolling under an ordinary sky. In the dreams she stood on the harbor wall and the water spoke to her in her own voice, a child's voice, high and clear, saying things she woke unable to hold onto, phrases dissolving like sugar in warm water. She woke each time with the wordless conviction that the dreams were not dreams at all, that something in the dark was talking to her through the only channel she couldn't lock, and that it was being — this was the strangest part, the part she turned over and over — *gentle*. Patient. Almost tender. Like someone reading over a sleeper's shoulder, very slowly, careful not to wake them.

On the third waking — or the thirtieth; her journal entries had begun drifting between *Day One* and *Day One Again* — she made a decision that frightened her more than the hunters or the tide-priests or the dark itself.

If the dark built from memory, and her instruments were dead, and everyone who had ever offered to guide her had betrayed her — then the only navigation she had left was herself. Her own past. Her own true record. Which meant that to cross this place, she had to stop flinching from the thing that had been eating at her since the ledger page that refused to be read.

She had to find out what she remembered. All of it. Even the parts with walls built across them.

She sat down on the guild-steps she had remembered into being, opened the field journal to a fresh page, and drew a single line down the middle. On the left she wrote: *What I know.* On the right: *What I know I don't.*

The left column filled quickly and comfortingly. Cartography. The guild. Vess's lessons. Seven commissioned surveys. The bottled tides, the ledger, the night the stars went out. Sable's smile. The enforcers' blades. Corvane's calm, terrible reasonableness.

The right column stayed nearly empty for a long time. Then, slowly, she made herself write:

*I don't know my mother's face.*
*I don't know my father at all — I don't even know if there was one.*
*I don't know why a guild that takes apprentices at ten took me at eleven, with no family petition, no fee, no record.*
*I don't know why Vess chose me.*
*I don't know what my birthmark is.* (She pushed back her sleeve. The sigil-shaped mark sat on her forearm, dark as dried ink, the same mark that made Sable fall to his knees and call her *daughter of the drowned*.)
*I don't know who taught me to draw the symbol I've been doodling in margins since I was a child, that I have never seen in any guild text, that matches the seal on the locked case.*

She stared at that last line for a long time. Then she turned to the back of the journal, where for twenty years she had idly sketched the same looping figure in moments of boredom or worry — a design she had always assumed was her own invention, some cartographer's fidget made habitual. She compared it, line by line, with the sigil stamped into the leather of the case on her back.

Identical. To the last curve. Drawn by a child who had never been taught it, repeated for twenty years by a woman who never questioned it, matching a seal whose makers had all vanished from history.

"I'm not carrying the key," Casper said to the dark, and her voice sounded very small and very clear. "I *am* the key. Aren't I. I've been the key my whole life, and everyone who ever looked at me knew it except me."

The dark said nothing. But the almost-cobbles beneath her hands grew warm, and somewhere — not near, not far, in the way of things in this place — she heard water. Real water, moving with intent, filling a space the way the sea fills a broken hull: not rushing, because it did not need to rush. It simply arrived, everywhere at once, inevitable as arithmetic.

And light. Far off — or close; distance was a courtesy this place extended and withdrew — a light swung slowly through the blackness. Not the white of her lantern. Not the green-gold of remembered windows. A cold beam, sweeping, rhythmic, turning and turning with the metronome patience of something that had been keeping its vigil for a very long time.

A lighthouse. Out here. In the dark between realms, where there was no coast to warn ships away from and no ships to warn — unless the coast it guarded was not made of land, and the ships it guided were not made of wood.

Casper got up. Her legs ached; she noticed she had no idea how long she'd been sitting. She looked at the sweeping light and felt something she hadn't felt since the stars went out: not hope exactly, but its rough draft. The instinct of her whole life, the thing the guild had honed in her and the dark could not dissolve — *when you see a light where no light should be, you go to it, because either it's a beacon or it's a lure, and either way it knows something you don't.*

She marked the spot in her journal — a small circle, and beside it, because she was who she was even at the edge of unbeing, a neat notation: *Uncharted light. Bearing unknown. Source unknown. Investigating.*

Then she picked up the case, squared it on her shoulders, and walked toward the turning light, and the dark parted before her memory of lighthouses — white tower, spiral stair, the smell of lamp-oil and brine — like a book opening to a page it had been waiting, patiently, for someone to read.

Halfway there, without deciding to, she began to hum. An old tune. She didn't know its name, or where she'd learned it, or why the singing voice in her dreams sang it back to her in a child's voice, note for note, like an answer.

The light swung toward her. And held.
