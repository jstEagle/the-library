## 12. Why the Stars Went Out

The lighthouse did not burn.

That was the first thing Amberdale had to explain, and the hardest, because Casper kept looking at the lamp room overhead with an expectation she could not shake — the old animal faith that light comes from fire, that a beacon is a thing you kindle. The great lens above them turned and turned in its slow bath of oil, and what it threw out across the unmapped dark was not flame but *recollection*: a pale gold radiance that moved like memory moves, unevenly, catching on some things and sliding off others.

"You're staring at it the way I stared at it," Amberdale said. "Four hundred years ago, give or take the years that stopped counting." She poured tea from a dented copper kettle — real tea, hoarded from a world that still had harbors — and her hands shook only a little. "Sit down, cartographer. What I have to tell you is best taken seated."

Casper sat on the keeper's bench beneath a window whose glass was thick with salt-frost, though there was no cold here, or no weather, or nothing that deserved either word. Beyond the pane, the dark pressed close — not empty dark but *populated* dark, full of half-real geographies drifting past like icebergs: the shoulder of a mountain that remembered being climbed; a river running upward through air it had once been promised; a coastline so faint it seemed drawn in breath on glass.

"The stars went out," Casper said. It was not a question. She had said it perhaps twenty times since they'd come up from the gallery of anchors, since she'd seen what hung beneath the lighthouse instead of a foundation. "Every one of them. In one hour."

"In one hour, yes." Amberdale settled into the chair opposite, and for a moment the lamplight found the ruin of her face — the way age and erasure had worn her like water wears a stair, hollowing the cheeks, smoothing the eyes until they were more tide-pool than person. "But you are asking the wrong question. Everyone asks why they went *out*. No one ever asks what they were doing *on*."

* * *

She rose, joints cracking like ship's timber, and crossed to the great chart table that dominated the keeper's room. It was covered in vellum so old it had gone the color of ivory, and upon it was drawn — in ink that still gleamed wet after four centuries — a map of the night sky.

Not a star chart. Amberdale watched Casper realize this, watched the young woman's fingers hover over the constellations and flinch away. These were not the friendly figures of navigators, the Bear and the Swan and the Archer. Each star was drawn at the center of its own concentric rings, and each ring was inscribed with writing so small it could only be read with a magnifier, and the writing was names.

Thousands of names. Tens of thousands.

"Anchors," Amberdale said. "That is the word the founders used, and it is a good word, because an anchor does three things. It holds. It connects. And it can be *raised*." She touched one star on the chart, a bright one near the center, and her finger left no mark. "When we sealed the Sundered Sea — when your guild's founders, my colleagues, my friends, sealed it — we needed something to hold the sigil against the weight of what it imprisoned. A god does not sit quietly under a stone. The pressure of it, the *want* of it, would crack any seal made only of ink and intention inside a generation.

"So we reached up. The stars were already holding the sky together; they were accustomed to bearing loads no one thanked them for. We ran the sigil's lines up through them — every anchor-point of the seal tied to a star, and every star tied to the others, so the whole net of heaven became our cage's frame." Her voice did something complicated on the word *cage*, caught between pride and nausea. "It worked. Of course it worked. We were very clever, and we were very frightened, and those two things together can build almost anything.

"But here is what none of us understood, sitting in our warm chapter-house drinking our clever wine. A star is not a stone. A star *burns*. It burns by spending itself, by pouring its own substance into the dark. We had asked the sky to hold our prison forever, and the sky agreed — but the sky keeps accounts too. Every year the anchors burned a little lower. Every century the sigil's lines grew thin as spider silk."

"And the guild knew," Casper said. Her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of someone walking on ice over deep water. "The guild knew, and—"

"And the guild did what guilds do." Amberdale met her eyes. "It found a cheaper material."

She opened a drawer in the chart table. Inside, nested in rotted velvet, lay a bottle.

It was small — no longer than a hand — and made of green glass gone cloudy with age, stoppered with wax stamped by a sigil Casper would know anywhere, because she catalogued fifty of them a week. The Guild of Impossible Things. Inside the bottle, something moved. Something vast moved inside it, the way an ocean moves inside a teacup when you hold it to your ear: swell and drag and the slow grinding of a tide that has somewhere to be and is being prevented.

"Bottled tides," Amberdale said. "You logged a cargo of them, didn't you? I felt it when you arrived — felt the ledger open. Chapter one of your acquaintance with all this, though you didn't know it yet. Tell me what the manifest said."

Casper's mouth was dry. "Salvage. Recovered from the wreck of the *Kindly Current*, lost sixty years ago. Bottled tides, provenance unknown, pending cataloguing."

"Provenance unknown." Amberdale laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as old salt. "The provenance is a warehouse under your own guildhouse. There is a floor below the lower archives that does not appear on any plan, where the bottled tides are racked in their thousands like wine in a cellar. And do you know what they are *for*, cartographer? Do you know why your guild has spent four hundred years buying tides from drowning sailors and desperate coastal witches, bottling them, racking them, cataloguing them as curiosities?"

The lamp overhead turned. Its light passed across Casper's face and Amberdale saw understanding arrive there slowly, the way dawn arrives over a drowned coast — not all at once, but feature by feature, each one worse than the last.

"No," Casper whispered.

"When a star burns low enough," Amberdale said, gently, because the gentleness was the cruelest thing available, "the sigil's line anchored to it begins to fail. And when a line begins to fail, the guild sends out its quiet people with their quiet bottles. They find the failing star's reflection — every anchor-star has one, a place where the sigil touches the world — and they pour a bottled tide into the wound. Sea-stuff, held and compressed, burning in the star's place. It holds. For a while. A bottled tide burns fast and dirty and it does not hold the way a star holds, because a star holds *everything at once* and a bottle only holds what fits inside the glass.

"So they replace one star. Then another. Then another. Slowly, over centuries, so slowly that no single guildmaster ever has to feel the whole weight of it — each of them inheriting a little lie and adding a little to it, telling themselves they'll find a true solution soon, that the next generation will be braver than they were." She spread her hands over the chart, over the constellation-rings crowded with names. "Look closely. How many of these stars do you think are still burning?"

Casper looked. And now that she knew to look, she saw it: the map was a graveyard pretending to be a sky. Star after star was drawn with its rings faded, its names gone grey, replaced by a small stamped notation in the margin — a bottle-mark, the guild's own inventory glyph, neat as a clerk's initials.

"How many?" Casper asked again.

"On the night the wards fell? Fewer than a hundred true stars remained. Out of ten thousand." Amberdale let that stand in the room like a body. "Your guild did not lose the sky in an hour, cartographer. Your guild *sold* it, an ounce at a time, over four hundred years, and called the loss maintenance."

* * *

Casper stood at the salt-frosted window for a long time. Amberdale let her stand. There were things a person had to hold alone before they could be handed anything else, and Amberdale had learned patience the hard way — four hundred years of it, alone in a lighthouse that did not burn, tending a chart no one would read.

"I catalogued them," Casper said finally. Her voice came out sanded smooth, emptied. "Bottled tides. I wrote *pending cataloguing* in the ledger and I felt — I remember feeling — that they were beautiful. Little oceans. Little impossibilities. I wanted to open one."

"They are beautiful," Amberdale said. "That is how it works. That is how all of it has always worked. Nothing stolen from the sea looks like theft. It looks like wonder, neatly stoppered." She came to stand beside her — beside herself, in a sense; the thought still turned her stomach, the girl's profile against the frosted glass carrying an echo of a face Amberdale had seen in mirrors before the mirrors started lying. "I bought them too, at the end. I signed the first requisition. Did you know that? The founders' bargain took a memory as payment — one memory, freely given, to power the original seal. But the *replacements* — the guild decided memories were too expensive to waste on maintenance. Tides were cheap. Tides could be purchased from people who didn't know what they were selling.

"We told ourselves the god wouldn't notice the difference. That a cage of borrowed sea was as good as a cage of heaven." She shook her head. "The god noticed. Oh, the god noticed. You do not feed a prisoner substitute bread for four hundred years without teaching it something about hunger. Every bottled tide we fed into the failing anchors was sea-stuff taken *from* the Sundered Sea's reach — the guild was literally feeding the god's own waters back to it as fuel, compressed and starving. It learned our tricks. It learned our ledgers. And when the last hundred true stars guttered—" 

"—the wards fell," Casper finished. "All of them. In one hour."

"In one hour. Because they were never a hundred separate wards, child. They were one ward, wearing a hundred masks. The guild thought it had redundancy. It had a single rope, frayed in ten thousand places, and it cut the frays one at a time and called each cut a success." Amberdale turned from the window. Above them the false lamp wheeled its memory-light across the dark, and far off, at the edge of seeing, something answered it — a long pale shimmer, like the underside of a wave seen from beneath. Closer tonight than it had been yesterday. Everything was closer tonight than it had been yesterday.

"There is one more thing," Amberdale said. "And then you must decide what to do, because I am done deciding. Four hundred years is past enough."

Casper turned to face her fully. Up close, in the turning light, the resemblance between them was no longer deniable — the same stubborn set to the jaw, the same cartographer's squint, as if the years between them had simply been folded away like a chart along its creases.

"The bottled tides in your guildhouse cellar," Amberdale said. "The ones you catalogued. The ones marked *pending*."

"What about them?"

"They are not waiting to be catalogued, Casper. They are waiting to be *spent*. Corvane has called a vote — you'll see it when you go back, and you must go back — a vote to renew the seal the old way. The founders' way. One memory, freely given, to re-anchor the last hundred stars and buy another thousand years." Amberdale's hands closed around each other, knuckles white, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "He will tell the guild it must be a member's memory. He will tell them it should be a cartographer's. And there is exactly one cartographer alive who carries the founder's name in her blood and the founder's mark on her skin, and everyone in that hall knows it, including him."

The lamp turned. The dark outside leaned closer, patient as tide.

"He means to spend you," Amberdale said, "the same way the guild spent the stars. An ounce at a time, and call it maintenance. Go home, cartographer. Before they come looking for what's left of me — and they will, because I am the last loose page in their ledger."

Casper was already reaching for the locked case at her feet.
