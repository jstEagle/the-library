## 13. Return to the Guild

The guildhall looked smaller from outside.

Casper had walked away from it twice in her life — once as a girl, when she'd run to the harbor to watch ships and been dragged back by Vess's gentle hand, and once, three nights ago, when she'd fled through the lower archives with Sable's hand around her wrist and enforcers shouting behind them. Both times she had looked back. This time she made herself not look back at all, because the building was doing something wrong.

It was breathing.

Not visibly. Not in any way a sane person would swear to under oath. But the guildhall of the Guild of Impossible Things had stood on Amber Row for two hundred years, and Casper had drawn its every elevation, its every hidden stair, and she knew — with the certainty of someone who had spent her life learning how places lied — that its stones were rising and falling by a hair's breadth, slow and tidal, like the chest of something sleeping very deep below.

*Below.* The word sat differently now.

She stopped at the edge of the square and let herself feel it: the pull backward, the old gravity of home, warm windows and lamplight and the smell of Vess's ink-and-lavender workroom drifting from somewhere memory insisted was real. Against that pull, she held up what she carried.

The locked ledger case rode against her spine, wrapped in oilcloth, light as a lie. Inside were pages that should not exist: entries describing trade routes no one remembered sailing, names no one could pronounce without weeping, and one page — one terrible, patient page — describing a cartographer named Amberdale who had vanished sealing a sigil beneath the sea, in handwriting Casper knew better than her own reflection.

And beside the case, tucked into her coat where Sable had pressed it, a folded scrap bearing the tide-priests' mark: *truce*, written in salt-dried ink, guaranteed by a man who had sold her to the enforcers and then, inexplicably, warned her away from his own trap.

Two families. Two sets of hands, both open, both holding knives.

"Neither of them is clean," she said aloud, to the empty square, and found that saying it steadied her the way a fixed north star does. "Fine. Then neither am I."

She crossed the square.

The doors opened before she knocked. That was Corvane's way — he always knew who stood outside his walls, though whether that was guildcraft or something older now living in the foundations, Casper no longer cared to guess. The entrance hall was lit but empty, which was itself an answer; at this hour the hall should have been full of clerks arguing over manifests, apprentices chasing loose quills across the flagstones. Instead every door gaped dark, and the silence had the specific texture of many people pretending very hard not to exist.

"They're all here," Casper said to the tall figure descending the main stair. "Aren't they."

Guildmaster Aldous Corvane reached the bottom step and folded his hands. He was a long, spare man, gray at the temples, with the patient face of someone who had spent forty years telling difficult truths gently — or, she thought now, telling gentle lies carefully. His eyes went first to the case on her back.

"You brought it back." Something moved through his voice — relief, or grief wearing relief's clothing. "I confess I didn't believe you would. When you ran with the tide-cultists, I told the council you'd return. Vess argued otherwise." A pause. "Vess argued you'd never forgive us."

"Vess argued correctly."

"I know." He said it simply, without defense, and that simplicity frightened her more than any denial would have. "Come upstairs, Casper. There are things you deserve to hear from me rather than from a drowned priest or a dead woman's handwriting."

"She isn't dead."

Corvane's composure flickered — a candle in a draft. "No," he admitted. "I don't suppose she is. Not entirely. Come."

* * *

The map room occupied the guildhall's crown, a round chamber walled floor-to-ceiling in charts, and Casper had loved it since childhood — had believed, in the uncomplicated way children believe, that a room containing the whole world must be a good room. She saw it differently now. Every chart on those walls had gaps. Coastlines that trailed off into decorative fog. Seas labeled only with the cartographer's honest shrug: *here be nothing, for nothing is permitted.*

They had hung the world with curtains and called it cartography.

Corvane poured two cups of tea from the pot that always waited on the sideboard, because ritual was armor and he wore his well. He set hers down within reach but did not insist. Outside the high windows, the starless sky pressed black against the glass, and Casper watched him notice her noticing.

"The wards fell four nights ago," she said. "Every star, all at once. And the guild locked its doors and told its members to wait. So I'm going to ask plainly, Guildmaster, and I'd take it as a courtesy if you answered the same way." She set the ledger case on the table between them and unlatched it. "What did the wards hold back?"

He looked at the case a long moment. "You've read it."

"Enough."

"Then you know the wards held back the Sundered Sea." He said the name without flinching, and she understood he had said it perhaps a thousand times alone in this room, rehearsing for an audience that never came. "A realm erased from every chart we keep. Not lost — erased. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole of our shame."

"And the stars."

"Were anchors." He turned to the window, to that obscene unbroken darkness. "Twelve hundred of them, each one pinned to a point of the sigil. Each one a nail in a lid. When they went out, it wasn't catastrophe, Casper. It was arithmetic. The founders spent more than they could afford, and the debt has been compounding ever since."

Casper pulled the oldest folio from the case and laid it open between them: the page with her own name in her own impossible hand. "Then tell me about her. Amberdale. Tell me why her handwriting matches mine stroke for stroke. Tell me why the ledgers remember a founder the members have never heard of. Tell me—" and here her voice finally cracked, and she hated it, hated the crack, "—tell me what the guild took from me before I was old enough to know I'd lost anything."

Corvane sat down slowly, like a man setting down something heavy he'd been carrying so long his arms had forgotten other shapes.

"You want me to say we were monsters," he said. "That would be cleaner. You could hate us cleanly and walk out cleanly and burn us down cleanly, and the world would divide neatly into the guilty and the saved. But I'm going to tell you the truth instead, and the truth is that we were afraid, and afraid men do arithmetic too."

He told her.

How the founders — six of them, though the ledgers now confessed only five — had found the thing beneath the sea not sleeping but *waiting*: a god, yes, but a guardian first, keeper of a drowned homeland whose name had already begun to slip from the world's tongue. How it had raged at some theft the founders would not name even then, even among themselves, and how its rage had shaken coastlines. How the six had faced a choice written in drowning villages and broken harbors: seal it, or let it grieve.

"And so we built the sigil," Corvane said. "Twelve hundred stars for nails. And the mortar of a sigil like ours is memory — that's the oldest law of making, older than the guild. Something must be forgotten for something to be bound. The founders voted. Five agreed to each give a memory of their choosing." His eyes came up, level and terrible. "Amberdale refused to choose. So the sea chose for her. It took everything — her childhood, her name's weight, her first life entire. What remained walked back up out of the water, half a person, and finished the sealing anyway, because someone had to, and then she went down to keep the light, and the guild wrote her out of its own books so her sacrifice wouldn't ache at us every day at breakfast."

"And me?"

Corvane was quiet for a long time. Beyond the window, far off across the rooftops, something vast shifted in the dark with a sound like a cathedral clearing its throat.

"When the seal began to fail," he said at last, "the guild needed a vessel. A bloodline that could carry the sigil-mark and pass unnoticed — a child raised inside these walls, loved, kept close, so that if the debt came due, the payment would be... at hand." He met her stare without apology, which she would later realize was the most honest cruelty in the room. "We didn't erase your past to steal from you, Casper. We erased it because the alternative was that you'd grow up knowing you were collateral, and no child should carry that."

"No child should carry *that*," Casper repeated. "So instead I carry a hole. I grew up reaching for a shape in my own chest that wasn't there. You call that mercy?"

"I call it the least drowning available." He rose and went to the window, silhouetted against the absolute dark. "You've been out there. You've seen what's rising. Do you know what happens if the sigil fails entirely? Not the god's rage — I'll grant you, after reading those pages, that the rage may even be righteous. I mean the *sea*. The Sundered Sea is not water, child. It is everything that was ever deliberately forgotten, given depth. Every name scrubbed from a ledger. Every route struck from a chart. Every person the world agreed, quietly, comfortably, to stop remembering. If that realm pours back into the world unreduced—"

"It comes back *as* forgetting."

"It drowns the world in its own discarded past. Yes." He turned. "Some truths drown worlds, Casper. That is not a proverb. It is a surveyor's finding. I have read the soundings. The founders weren't villains; they were the last adults in a room full of rising water, and they made the choice nobody wanted, and then they spent two hundred years paying for it in secret so that you could grow up drawing maps of a world that stayed put." His voice softened, and that softness was worse than anger. "You think we're your enemy. We are your family. Families lie to each other exactly as much as they must and no less, and anyone who tells you different has never loved anything that could break."

Casper stood very still.

The terrible thing — the thing she would write down later, in a different book, in a different life — was that she *understood*. That was Corvane's genius and the guild's poison both: they never lied crudely. Every concealment was load-bearing. Every secret held up some portion of the sky. Standing in that room, hearing the logic laid out with such care, she felt the old belonging close around her like warm water, and she had to look at the open ledger, at her own erased name, to remember which way was up.

"If it's mercy," she said quietly, "why does it require guards? Why purge the lower archives? Why hunt a cartographer carrying your own records?" She tapped the page. "Mercy doesn't post sentries, Guildmaster. Mercy doesn't need the ledgers locked. You didn't hide the truth to protect the world. You hid it to protect the *decision* — so no one would ever get to make it again except you."

Something like respect moved through his exhaustion. "Perhaps. And if the vote goes against renewal tomorrow, and the sea takes the coast, will you comfort yourself with the purity of having been right?"

"There won't be a renewal vote."

"Casper—"

"Because you can't renew it." She pulled the second folio from the case — the pages she'd deciphered in the lighthouse's borrowed light, cross-checked against the bottled tides in the guild's own manifests. "The stars are gone, Corvane. All twelve hundred. And do you know why? Look at your own shipping logs. Bottled tides. Forty years of bottled tides, catalogued as cargo, sold as curiosities — and each one a star's anchor, siphoned, decanted, *spent*. The guild hasn't been maintaining the seal. It's been liquidating it. Slowly enough that no one noticed. Enough small withdrawals and the account empties, and no one feels poorer until the night the lights go out." Her hand shook, and she let it. "You didn't inherit a hard choice. You inherited an embezzlement, and you've been managing it like an estate."

For the first time, Corvane looked old.

He sat back down. He looked at the manifest figures she'd spread before him, and she watched him check them — actually check them, line by line, the habit of forty years — and watched the confirmation arrive in his face like water finding its level.

"Thorne," he said at last. Names were confessions, and he knew it. "My predecessor's predecessor. He began the transfers. Called it *reallocation*. By the time I understood, the stars were half-spent and stopping it meant the failure came all at once instead of gradually. So I chose gradual. I chose the lie that buys time, because the truth bought nothing but panic." He laughed, short and hollow. "You see? Afraid men do arithmetic. I told you we weren't monsters. I never claimed we were good."

Outside, the pressure against the windows deepened. Casper felt it in her teeth, in the sigil-mark beneath her hair — a faint cold pulse, like a second heartbeat keeping time with something far below and getting closer.

"Tomorrow the guild votes," Corvane said. "On renewal. On what it costs." He looked at her then with an expression she couldn't parse — guilt, or hope, or the place where they blur. "You should know that the renewal rite requires a memory freely given. A large one. And there is exactly one person in this room whose memories are keyed to the sigil."

The cold pulse beneath her hair beat again, patient as tide.

"You're asking me to vote on erasing myself."

"I'm asking you to come to the vote," Corvane said. "That's more than any of us gave Amberdale. It isn't justice. Justice isn't on the table anymore — it drowned forty years ago with Thorne's first withdrawal. All that's left is the choice, and I find I can't make it alone anymore." He closed the folio gently, the way one closes a face. "Will you stay? Under truce, under my word, until the guild decides what it is?"

Casper looked at the walls of curtained maps, at the tea going cold between them, at the man who had signed her childhood into a ledger and called it shelter.

Her family. Her captors. The same people, and no clean line anywhere between.

"Yes," she heard herself say. "I'll stay."

She felt the words land somewhere far below the guildhall's foundations — felt them *received* — and the pulse beneath her hair slowed, almost thoughtful, almost pleased.

Above the round chamber, through stone and timber and two hundred years of careful lies, the starless sky leaned closer, waiting to learn what the guild would choose to forget next.
