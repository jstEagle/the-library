## 14. The Vote

The Hall of Precedents had not been used for a vote in eleven years.

Casper knew this because she had catalogued the room herself, two winters ago, running her fingers along the dust-veined ledgers stacked in its alcoves and noting, in her neat cartographer's hand, that the chamber was *disused — ceremonial only*. She had written that without irony. The Guild of Impossible Things kept a hall for deciding impossible things, and then declined to use it, which was perhaps the most honest thing about them.

Now every bench was full. Now the great orrery above the dais turned with all its spheres, brass planets grinding through their slow orbits, and beneath it sat the Guildmaster's chair, and in the chair sat Corvane, and Corvane was looking at her.

Not at the assembly. At her. The way a man looks at a coastline he is deciding whether to keep on the map.

She sat where Vess had put her — third bench from the front, aisle seat, close enough to the doors that a quick exit remained possible and far enough from them that everyone would see if she took it. Vess sat beside her, spine straight as a surveyor's rule, hands folded over the locked ledger case that no longer held anything but its own history. Casper had emptied it in the night, page by page, and copied what mattered into her satchel. Some part of her expected Vess to be angry about that. Vess had only said, quietly, *bring everything you can carry that is true*, and that had been worse than anger.

The assembly murmured like a tide going out. Casper counted faces out of habit — forty-one voting members, though three seats stood empty and one of those, she now understood, would never be filled again. Master Ellery of the Lower Archives had been found at dawn in the flooded stacks, drowned in six inches of standing water that should not have been there, his lungs full of salt. The guild had called it an accident. The guild was very good at calling things accidents.

"Bring the chamber to order," Corvane said, and the murmur died.

He rose. He was not a tall man, but the chair made him seem carved rather than seated, and when he spoke his voice carried the particular calm of someone who has rehearsed catastrophe until it fits inside his mouth.

"Members of the Guild. You know why we are here. Three nights ago the border wards fell. Two nights ago the stars went out. Yesterday we learned what the stars were." He let the silence do its work; it did it well. "They were anchors. Points of the sigil that holds the Sundered Sea beneath the world. And they are gone because for sixty years this guild has been selling them."

Casper watched the benches absorb this. Some members had known — she could tell by the way they didn't flinch, by the stillness of the old cartographers in the back rows who had signed off on cargo manifests of bottled tide for decades and told themselves stories about decorative purposes. Others flinched visibly, hands rising to mouths, a woman in the second row whose quill snapped in her grip.

"The trade was necessary," Corvane continued. "I will not pretend otherwise, and I will not apologize for it, because apology is a luxury purchased by people who have never had to choose between a lie and a drowning. The sea beneath the sea wants up. It has always wanted up. Our founders bound it, and the binding cost more than they could pay, and so the debt has been coming due ever since — slowly, in installments, disguised as commerce. We sold pieces of the seal to keep the whole of it intact. That is the arithmetic of mercy. It is ugly arithmetic. But it held."

*It held,* Casper thought, *until it didn't.* Her hand drifted to her collarbone, to the mark beneath her shirt — the sigil-shaped birthmark Sable had kissed like a relic, the mark Amberdale had traced with shaking fingers in the lighthouse and called *the last piece of the lock*. She thought of the god's voice wearing her childhood timbre, saying *you were the payment*, and she pressed her palm flat against her sternum as if to hold something in.

"For sixty years," Corvane said, "the installments were paid in memories that belonged to no one living. Erased entries. Scrubbed routes. Names lifted from ledgers before anyone could grieve them. But the sea is patient and the debt compounds, and the installments have run dry. There is nothing left to spend that is not already spent." He paused, and his eyes came back to her, and stayed. "Almost nothing."

The chamber went very quiet. Quiet the way the night had been quiet when the stars went out — not silence but *held* sound, the whole room breathing shallowly around a single point.

"There remains one unspent memory in this guild's keeping," Corvane said. "One. It belongs to Cartographer Casper Amberdale. It is the memory of her first life — the life the founders borrowed to complete the seal, the life returned to her incomplete, the life whose absence has been eating her from the inside since the day she was found on our steps with a map folded in her swaddling clothes." His voice did not soften, but it slowed, the way a man slows when crossing ice. "To renew the sigil — truly renew it, not patch it, not sell another star-shaped hole in the sky — requires a willing memory of sufficient weight. Hers is sufficient. It may be the last sufficient thing in the world."

"And if she refuses?" someone called from the back. Old Master Quillon, his voice like gravel poured slowly.

Corvane did not look at him. "Then we are here to ask her not to refuse. And if asking fails—" the smallest pause, calibrated, terrible— "then we are here to decide what we owe her, and what we owe the world, when those debts fall due in different currencies."

Vess's hand found Casper's under the bench. Her grip was cold. Casper gripped back and thought: *this is the room where my family decides whether to unmake me, and they have dressed it in procedure so it will feel like weather instead of murder.*

* * *

They gave her the floor first. Of course they did. Procedure demanded the subject speak before she was spoken about, and Casper understood, walking to the well of the chamber, that this courtesy was also a cage — whatever she said would become the record against which their choice was measured.

She stood beneath the orrery. Above her, the brass worlds turned without light to steer by, and she thought of the new stars she had seen in visions and in Amberdale's fading eyes — *mapped, not anchored* — and she made herself stand straight.

"My name is Casper Amberdale," she began, and stopped, because the name itself was the wound. "That name belonged to someone else first. A woman who drew the Sundered Sea's coast before it was erased. Who sealed your god — our god, I don't know how else to say it — and paid for the sealing with everything she was, and was given a second life as a foundling with a hole where her beginning should be. I am what grew in that hole. I am the shape the absence made."

She heard her own voice steady, and was grateful to it.

"I know what you're considering. I've read the ledgers. I've read the precedents — I catalogued this room, remember, I know exactly which precedent you'll cite, the Rite of Necessary Forgetting, 214th session, when the guild erased a harbor master's grief to keep a lighthouse lit. I know you will call it mercy. Some of you even believe that." She looked at Corvane. "You believe it. I watched you believe it in your office while you told me some truths drown worlds. And maybe you're right. Maybe the truth does drown worlds. But here is what I learned in the unmapped dark, Guildmaster, and I learned it the hard way, alone, with no instruments and no name: *the dark responds to what you remember.* Forget enough, and there is nothing left to hold the world up with. Your seal is failing not because the god is strong but because you have forgotten too much. Every erased entry was a rope cut. You are proposing to cut the last one and call it rigging."

Murmurs. Somewhere a bench creaked.

"I won't beg," she said. "Begging is for people asking for something they might not deserve. What I'm asking for is simpler and stranger: remember me. All of me. Even the parts that hurt. Especially those. If the sigil must be renewed, find another way — find *my* way. I've seen it work. Acknowledgment holds better than erasure. A named thing can be reasoned with. An unnamed thing can only be feared, and fear is the most expensive material in the world, and you have been paying in it for sixty years."

She stepped back. "That's what I say. Now you say yours. And whatever you decide—" her voice caught, just once, and she hated it, and let it— "whatever you decide, I want it minuted. Written down. In ink that cannot be erased. Because I have read your ledgers, and I know what this guild does to entries it regrets."

She walked back to her bench. Vess did not take her hand this time; Vess put her arm around her shoulders, briefly, formally, the way one braces a comrade before artillery, and then folded her hands again, because the vote was coming and sentiment was a luxury neither of them could afford to be seen purchasing.

* * *

The debate lasted four hours.

Casper listened to all of it. She had thought she would stop listening — that self-preservation would build its little wall and she would retreat behind it into the cartographer's trance, measuring distances, counting exits. Instead she listened the way she had once listened to the sea beneath the cathedral: wholly, helplessly, hearing her own name taken apart and reassembled by forty voices.

Master Quillon argued for the erasure. He was kind about it, which made it worse. "I have known the girl since she was nine years old drawing coastlines in the margins of other people's charts," he said, and Casper's chest tightened, because she *hadn't remembered that*, and here was proof of how much else she'd lost. "And because I have known her, I can tell you: she is already half-unmade. The hole in her is killing her by inches — I've seen her wake screaming at nothing, I've seen her lose hours. To spend what remains of her first life might heal her even as it saves us. The forgetting would be a mercy twice over." He believed it. That was the horror of the guild, Casper thought. Not cruelty. Conviction, worn smooth as river stone.

A younger cartographer — Merren Oduya, who had once asked Casper to teach her the contour-hatching style and whom Casper had privately marked as the future of the craft — stood and argued against. "We are the Guild of Impossible Things," Merren said, voice cracking on the title. "Not the guild of convenient ones. If we erase her, we prove every word the tide-worshippers say about us. We prove the seal was never a covenant, only a theft with good bookkeeping. I say we find another way, even if the finding kills us, because a guild that saves the world by devouring its own daughter has already lost the world it's saving."

An old hydrographer Casper didn't know spoke of duty. A ledger-keeper spoke of precedent. Someone proposed a compromise — a partial erasure, a tithe of memory, ten years of her first life offered up like a tithe of grain — and Casper felt that proposal land in her body like a hook, because it was clever, it was *kind-looking*, and it would leave her gutted in a new configuration, missing things she couldn't name because they'd be gone. She raised her hand to speak against it and Corvane, without looking at her, said, "The subject has spoken. The floor belongs to the members now." And that, too, she noted. That, too, went in the invisible ledger she was keeping behind her eyes.

Through all of it, Vess sat beside her and said nothing. Casper kept waiting for her to speak — Vess, who had confessed everything, who had carried the guilt of the founders' bargain like a stone in her chest for thirty years, who loved her, who had said so in the dark of the archive with tears freezing on her lashes. Vess had the standing. Vess had the moral authority. One speech from Vess about what the stolen memory had cost, about watching Casper grow up hollow and loving her anyway, about the sin of spending what was never yours to spend — one speech, and the vote would tilt.

Vess kept her hands folded and her mouth shut.

When the division bell rang, Casper finally turned to her. "Why aren't you fighting?"

Vess looked at her with an expression Casper had no chart for — grief and discipline fused into something almost serene. "Because if I speak for you, they will count my love as bias and discount it. And if I speak against you—" her jaw worked— "if I speak against you and they listen, I could not live in the same guildhall as my own voice. So I am doing the hardest thing I know how to do, Casper. I am trusting them. I am sitting here, in the room, letting my family decide, because that is what family *is* — the people whose choices about you you cannot escape and must survive." She squeezed Casper's wrist once, hard. "Whatever they choose, I will be here after. That is my speech. It isn't minuted. It doesn't need to be."

* * *

The vote was by white stone and black.

Each member came forward to the dais and dropped a stone into the brazen urn, and the guild had done this for four hundred years precisely so that no one would know how anyone voted — anonymity as armor, cowardice dressed as fairness. Casper watched the stones fall and could read nothing in the sound. White stones clicked high and bright. Black stones landed with a duller note, or perhaps she imagined the difference, or perhaps she was listening so hard her ears invented distinctions just to keep from screaming.

Forty-one members. Thirty-eight present. Nineteen stones needed for a majority either way.

She counted. She was a cartographer; counting was the one prayer she knew.

At twenty-two stones the tally stood thirteen black, nine white, and Casper's stomach had dropped through the floor of the world. At thirty it was eighteen black, twelve white. One more black stone and it was finished — her remaining memory, the fragment of her first life, the last true thing she owned, spent like a bottle of tide on a cargo manifest. She thought of the god in the drowned cathedral speaking in her childhood voice, and thought: *soon I won't know whose voice that was.* She thought of Amberdale in the lighthouse, half-gone, and thought: *that is the shore I'm being marched toward.* She did not cry. She held her body very still, the way one holds still on thin ice, and waited for the thirty-first stone.

It was white.

She nearly laughed. One stone. The difference between a person and an entry was one white pebble in a brazen urn.

The rest came slowly. Fourteen white. Fifteen. Sixteen. Seventeen. Eighteen. Nineteen — majority spared, and the room's breath changed, and still the line came forward, member after member, each stone a small verdict, and the final tally when the herald read it out was:

Twenty white. Eighteen black.

Two stones. The width of her survival was two pebbles.

Casper sat very still while Corvane rose to announce the result, and the announcement was a masterpiece of institutional language: *"The guild declines, by narrow margin, to exercise the Rite of Necessary Forgetting upon Cartographer Amberdale. The question of the seal's renewal is remanded to committee. The guild expresses its... gratitude... to Cartographer Amberdale for her testimony."*

Gratitude. As if she had given a lecture. As if she hadn't just stood naked-souled on a stage while her family debated whether to eat her heart to keep the house warm.

The chamber emptied in the shuffling, avoiding-eyes way crowds leave funerals. Members passed her bench and some touched her shoulder — Merren Oduya squeezed it hard and said nothing, which Casper understood perfectly. Old Quillon paused beside her, opened his mouth, closed it, and finally said only, "I'm glad, girl. I want you to know I'm glad." And Casper believed him, and it cost her something to believe him, and she decided the cost was worth paying, because a guild that could not forgive its own near-betrayals would soon have no members left to betray.

Corvane came last. He stopped at the bench and looked down at her, and for a moment the Guildmaster's mask slipped and she saw underneath it a tired man in his fifties who had inherited a debt he never contracted and had spent his whole tenure paying it in other people's currency.

"You think today was a defeat," he said quietly.

"I think today was a coin flip," Casper said. "I think my existence hung on two stones, and next month it might hang on none, because the sea isn't waiting for your committees."

"Perhaps." He straightened. "But understand what happened here, Amberdale. Forty people were asked to save the world at the price of one soul, and they said no. Narrowly, messily, with cowardice and confusion in the mix — but they said no. Sixty years ago this guild wouldn't have needed a vote. Today it needed one, and barely got it, and that *barely* is the first honest thing this institution has done in my lifetime." He almost smiled. "Hollow victories are still victories. Sometimes they're the only kind on offer. Learn to live in them — you'll be living in a great many."

He walked away, and Casper sat in the emptying hall beneath the blind turning orrery, and let herself feel it: relief and rage and love and grief, all at once, none of them clean. They had spared her. Her family had spared her. By two stones. Out of fear and fondness and procedure and principle, in proportions she would never be able to measure, and she would spend the rest of her life wondering which stone was which.

Vess touched her elbow. "Come. You haven't eaten since yesterday, and martyrs make poor cartographers on empty stomachs."

They walked out together through corridors that smelled of salt and lamp oil, past windows showing a starless sky, and Casper was so wrung out, so hollowed by relief, that she almost missed it.

Almost missed the boy.

He was waiting in the shadow of the porter's arch — a tide-worshipper's runner, one of Sable's, a child of maybe twelve with salt-crusted boots and eyes too old for his face. He shouldn't have gotten past the wards. The wards were down, Casper reminded herself. Everything was down. The world's doors were all unlocked now.

The boy pressed a scrap of oilskin into her hand and was gone before Vess turned around, vanished into the city's dark like water into sand.

Casper opened the oilskin with numb fingers. Inside, in a hand she recognized — that confident, sweeping script she had seen signing the drowned cathedral's guest-ledger — were five words:

*They come at moonrise. Run.*

And beneath the words, a sketch. Quick, urgent, drawn by someone who could draw: the guild's lower harbor, and upon it, moving through the black water where stars should have been reflected and were not, shapes. Many shapes. Boats without lanterns, packed bow to stern, and at their prows a sigil she knew intimately, because she wore it on her collarbone and had redrawn it in a flooding cathedral with her own bleeding hand.

Sable had warned her. Sable — who had lied to her, who had used her, who had let the hunters come — had spent whatever remained of his credibility, maybe his safety, maybe his life, on five words and a map.

Moonrise was in two hours.

"Casper?" Vess said. "What is it?"

Casper looked up at the starless sky, at the horizon where the sea breathed against the dark, and folded the oilskin into her coat with hands that had finally stopped shaking.

"Get the ledgers," she said. "All of them. The true ones, the copies, everything — get them out of the building. The tide is coming to the guild tonight, Vess, and it isn't coming to negotiate."
