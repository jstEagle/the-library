## 1. Charms for the Godless

The luck-knots sold best after the third bell, when the honest work was done and the dishonest work hadn't started yet.

Felix had learned that in their first month in Vhalen, back when they still flinched at every lantern being lit and every boot on cobblestone. Three years later they knew the night market's rhythms the way a sailor knows a rope: which stall-keepers would look away, which constables took coin and which took pride, which corners held shadow deep enough to hide a small act of kindness dressed up as commerce.

"Two coppers," Felix said, turning the knot over in their palm so the lamplight caught the braided cord. "Tie it to your bedframe. Not the headboard — the foot. Luck runs uphill."

The dockworker — Halvard, third mate off the *Kestrel's Pride*, hands like knotted rope themselves — squinted at it. "That's it? No words? No... you know." He waggled his fingers mysteriously.

"No words." Felix smiled the smile they'd practiced until it sat naturally on their face, easy and slightly apologetic, the smile of a craftsman selling trinkets. "If I could put words in a knot, do you think I'd be charging two coppers?"

Halvard laughed, dug the coins from his vest, and walked away holding the luck-knot like it was already working. And maybe it was. That was the thing Felix had learned to live with, the small hypocrisy they carried like a stone behind the breastbone: the knots *did* work. Not because of the braid. The braid was nothing — harbor-cord, three strands, the kind any child could learn. But Felix spoke over each one as they tied it, a whisper, less than a whisper, breath shaped into something older than Vhalen's walls, and the knot remembered.

Luck wasn't a gift anyone could give. But you could nudge it. You could stand at the shoulder of a man's bad day and lean.

Felix pocketed the two coppers and felt the guilt arrive on schedule, punctual as tide. It wasn't the taking of money. Two coppers for a dockworker's peace of mind was fair trade by any honest ledger. It was the concealment. Every coin that crossed their palm bought another day of pretending the gift didn't exist, and every day of pretending was a small betrayal of everyone who'd died for having the same gift and no talent for hiding it.

*You survived,* their mother had said, pressing the sword into their hands the last night. *Surviving is not a sin.* She'd been wrong about that, or right about it, depending on the hour. At this hour — fourth bell now, market thinning, gulls arguing over fish-guts near the quay — it felt like both.

They packed the remaining knots into the canvas roll. Nine left. Six luck-knots, two calm-knots for sailors' wives who couldn't sleep in storm season, and one fever-knot that Felix had made badly and couldn't bring themself to destroy, because the fever-knots were the closest thing to real work they did anymore, and doing real work badly felt like a kind of honesty.

"You've got a fever-knot."

Felix went still.

The voice came from under the awning of the chandlery next door, from the gap between a barrel of pitch and a stack of ratline. Small voice. Young. Felix hadn't heard an approach, and nobody approached Felix without being heard — three years of listening for hunters' boots had tuned their ears finer than any charm.

They turned slowly, keeping the motion lazy, unthreatening, the motion of a tired vendor closing up shop.

The child stepped out of the gap. A girl, maybe nine or ten, all elbows and sharp collarbones, with hair cut short in the uneven way of someone who'd done it herself with a fishing knife and a mirror she didn't trust. Her eyes were gray-green, sea-colored, and they were fixed on Felix's canvas roll with an attention that made the skin crawl along Felix's arms.

Not the attention of a child wanting sweets or coins. The attention of someone reading.

"I don't sell fever-knots," Felix said. "Bad for business. People come back complaining they got better too fast and want refunds."

"It's the third one from the bottom," the girl said. "You wrapped it in blue cloth because the others are in white and you don't want anyone asking why one's different." She tilted her head. "It's humming."

The market noise seemed to recede, leaving Felix alone in a small clear space with the girl and the sound of their own heartbeat.

"Humming," Felix repeated.

"Not out loud." She frowned, frustrated, hunting for the word. "Like before thunder. When the air gets thick and your teeth feel wrong. Your knots feel like that. All of them, but the fever one most." Her eyes finally lifted from the canvas roll to Felix's face, and what Felix saw there wasn't fear or greed or the flat assessing stare of a hunter's informant. It was recognition. Bare, unguarded, relieved recognition, like a drowning swimmer sighting a light on a cliff. "I thought I was the only one."

Felix's mouth was dry. *I thought I was the only one.* Six words, and every one of them a knife, because Felix had thought exactly the same thing for twenty years, and then for three years had made sure to keep thinking it, had built a whole life on the foundation of never hearing anyone say it aloud again.

"You're mistaken," Felix said gently. "Knots hum if you braid them wet. Old sailor's trick. Go home, little one. Where are your parents?"

"Brannick says my parents are a question I shouldn't waste time on." The girl said it without self-pity, the way you'd report the weather. "He's not my father. He just feeds me. Odile says he's terrible at it, feeding people, because he cooks everything until it's grey." She stepped closer, and Felix caught the smell of tar and salt and underneath it something else, something faint and strange, like ozone before a storm at sea. "I'm Nia."

"Nia." Felix did not offer a name in return. Names were currency here, and Felix's was counterfeit anyway — Marchetti, borrowed off a dead family whose gravestones were far away and whose living kin were fewer every year. "Nia, listen—"

"You speak to them," Nia said. "The knots. You think you're quiet but you're not quiet, you're *quiet-like-a-whisper*, and whispers carry if the air's right, and tonight the air's right because the wind's coming off the water and—" She stopped herself, visibly reining in, and something about the effort of it, a ten-year-old learning to cage her own mouth, cracked Felix open in a place they'd sealed long ago.

"Nia." Felix crouched, bringing their eyes level with hers, and let the vendor's smile fall away entirely. What remained underneath was tired, and they let her see it. "What you're describing — whatever you think you hear — is dangerous to talk about. Not pretend-dangerous. Real dangerous. There are people in this city who would pay silver for a child who talks like you, and worse than silver for whoever taught her. Do you understand?"

Something flickered across her face. Not fear. Calculation, quick and practiced, and it frightened Felix more than fear would have.

"They came to the harbor last month," Nia said quietly. "Salt-and-iron masks. Brannick moved me behind the net-drying racks and stood in front of me and told me a story about his grandmother until they went past." Her chin lifted. "So yes. I understand. Do *you* understand that I'm not going to stop hearing things just because hearing is illegal? Because I tried that. I tried it for a year. It doesn't work. It's like trying to stop hearing your own name."

Felix had no answer to that. There was no answer to that; there was only survival, and survival was a house with no windows, and Felix had lived in it so long they'd forgotten what daylight cost.

"Where does Brannick moor?" Felix asked instead.

"Why?"

"So I know which pier to avoid."

Nia grinned, sudden and crooked, and for a moment she looked exactly like a child. "Pier Nine. The *Salt Lark*. But you'll come anyway. Brannick always says people say they won't come and then they come, because nobody can stand us for long but everybody keeps coming back." She shrugged, wise and weary in a way no child should be weary. "He says we're like fleas."

"Fleas."

"Comfortable fleas." She backed toward the alley between the chandlery and the ropewalk, walking backward so she could keep watching Felix, and Felix knew — with the same certainty that hummed in the fever-knot wrapped in its blue cloth — that this child would remember their face, would find them again, would not be deterred by anything as reasonable as danger. "Third bell tomorrow. Brannick makes fish stew on market nights. It's grey. Bring your own bread."

Then she was gone, swallowed by the alley's dark, and the night market went on around Felix — a cartwheel squeaking, a woman haggling over lamp oil, the gulls still screaming their ancient grievances — as though the world hadn't just tilted three degrees sideways.

* * *

Felix didn't go straight home.

Home was a rented room above a chandler's on Cordwainer Street, one window, one lock, one false panel behind the loose brick where the sword slept wrapped in oilcloth. Instead Felix walked the long way, along the seawall, letting the wind off the Sundered Sea push against them like a hand testing their weight.

The sea was calm tonight. It was almost always calm now, and that was its own wrongness — old-timers said the Sundered Sea had earned its name in the Breaking, when the coast drowned and the mountain rose out of the flood, and they said the calm was just the sea holding its breath. Felix didn't think much about the sea. Thinking about the sea meant thinking about the mountain, and thinking about the mountain meant thinking about the court beneath it, and that was a door Felix kept shut and barred and painted over, the way you'd treat a room where something had once died.

At the seawall's end they stopped and unwrapped the fever-knot from its blue cloth and held it up to the moonlight.

It hummed. Of course it hummed. Everything Felix touched with intent hummed; the gift ran through their voice like a seam of ore through plain rock, and every whispered word over every knot mined it a little. The fever-knot was badly made — the braid crossed wrong at the second pass — but the word inside it was true, and true words had weight regardless of the vessel.

Somewhere below, waves worried the pilings. Somewhere above, the mountain stood black against the stars, and beneath the mountain, if half the smugglers' tales were true, something old kept the sea in check by means nobody discussed in daylight.

And somewhere between those two facts, a ten-year-old girl with sea-colored eyes had looked at Felix's canvas roll and seen through three years of careful disguise in under a minute.

*I thought I was the only one.*

Felix closed their hand around the knot. The word inside it pressed back, warm, patient, waiting to be spent on some stranger's sickbed.

"That's how it starts," they said aloud, to the wind, to no one. "That's how it always starts. First they hear you. Then they follow you home."

But even as they said it, Felix was already calculating: Pier Nine, third bell, the *Salt Lark*. Grey stew. Bring bread. They knew it even as they denied it, the way they knew the knots worked, the way they knew the gift was coiled in their throat like a sleeping animal that dreamed of open country.

They rewrapped the fever-knot, shouldered the canvas roll, and turned back toward Cordwainer Street.

Behind them, out past the seawall, the Sundered Sea lay smooth as poured glass, and if you watched it long enough — Felix didn't watch, but if you did — you might have sworn it was listening too.
