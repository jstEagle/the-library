## 2. The Proclamation

The criers came at dawn, and Felix knew before they opened their mouths.

Not because of anything they said — the words were the usual crown boilerplate, rolled out in the flat cadence of men paid by the proclamation. It was the bells. Vhalen rang its harbor bells for storms, for fires, for royal deaths. They were ringing now for none of those things, a slow triple-strike from the Saltspire that made the gulls lift off the breakwater all at once, like a sheet shaken into the sky.

Felix stood in the doorway of their stall with a half-packed crate of luck-knots in their arms and listened to a man in crown blue read the Purification Edict to a square that hadn't finished waking.

*"By order of His Majesty's Grace, be it known: all persons harboring gifts of the old blood — workings, bindings, speakings, charms beyond honest craft — shall present themselves for registration within nine days. Those registered shall be assessed. Those unregistered shall be taken."*

Taken. Not seized, not detained, not brought to justice. *Taken*, the way you took a tithe, a tooth, a child.

The crowd was thin at that hour — fishwives, a few dockers waiting on the tide shift, an old man selling boiled chestnuts who had heard a hundred proclamations and expected to hear a hundred more. Nobody shouted. That was the worst of it. In the ballads, edicts were met with fury or defiance. Here it landed like weather: people glanced up, calculated what it cost them personally, and went back to work.

A woman beside Felix nudged her companion. "Old blood," she said. "That's the mountain-folk, isn't it? Upcountry hedge-witches."

"Probably," her companion said, and bought a chestnut.

Felix's hands were very steady around the crate, which took effort, because everything under their skin was ringing louder than the bells. *Workings, bindings, speakings.* Three categories. Felix Marchetti was two of them, if anyone ever thought to listen past the harmless surface of their voice. The luck-knots were nothing — thread and knotwork any grandmother could tie, powered by nothing but suggestion and good salesmanship. But the voice. The voice that could unknot a grief, talk a locked door into forgetting it was locked, hold a bleeding wound quiet while a surgeon did his work — that was the old blood, straight through, and there was no registration that ended anywhere but a cellar under the Saltspire with salt-iron on your wrists.

Nine days.

Felix carried the crate back inside and sat down on the packing bench and let themselves, for exactly sixty heartbeats, be afraid.

Then they got to work.

* * *

Burning charm stock is harder than it sounds. Not physically — paper and thread burn fine, even damp harbor air notwithstanding. It's the arithmetic that grinds. Every luck-knot was coin already earned and not yet collected. Every warding ribbon, every traveler's blessing written on oiled cloth, every one of the little knotted promises Felix had spent three years building into a livelihood that looked like nothing — each was a small debt owed to someone who'd paid in copper and trust, and burning them felt less like caution than like theft.

Felix burned them anyway, in the iron brazier behind the stall, feeding the fire in careful handfuls so the smoke stayed thin and gray and unremarkable. A neighbor leaned over the fence and asked if they were clearing out dead stock.

"Moving to cheaper thread," Felix said, and their voice did what voices do when you lie well: settled into the listener like a warm hand, smoothed the question away before it fully formed. The neighbor nodded, satisfied, and went back to his nets, and Felix hated it a little — how easy, how clean, how much like the gift instead of just lying.

By midmorning the brazier held ash and brass pins and the smell of scorched wool, and Felix had a decision left that couldn't be burned.

They closed the stall early, which drew more attention than the smoke had, and walked the long way through the Tanners' Stair to the harbor district, taking corners slowly, cataloguing without meaning to: the crown-blue coat buying sausage at Merla's stand; the pair of clerks nailing a fresh copy of the edict to the customs house door, its wax seal fat and red as a wound; the way the harbor itself seemed to have gone slightly quieter, boats pushing off earlier, conversations dropping as officials passed. Fear arriving not as torches and screaming but as paperwork. As lists. As a clerk with a brush writing down names while everyone watched him write and said nothing.

Felix climbed the outside stair to the smuggler house on Rope Lane — three floors of sagging timber that Brannick had won in a card game and Odile had turned into the only place in Vhalen where Felix slept whole nights — and found the household already arguing.

"—not hiding, Odile, *moving*. There's a difference, and the difference is whether we're alive in autumn." Brannick stood by the cold hearth with his arms crossed, which he only did when he'd lost the argument twice already and was preparing to lose it a third time. He filled the room the way he filled a doorway, gray-bearded, scarred across one forearm from a winch cable, a man built entirely of practical decisions. "There's a coaster out of Serevan takes passengers Thursday. No questions, cash fare."

"And then what?" Odile didn't look up from the ledger she was auditing at the kitchen table, but her pen had stopped moving, which meant she'd been listening the entire time. "We run to Serevan with no cargo, no contacts, and a purse full of coin we can't replace, and we wait for the edict to get bored? Edicts don't get bored, Brannick. They get administrators."

"Better administrators than hunters."

"Will you two—" Nia, cross-legged on the windowsill with her knees drawn up, thirteen years old and furious about it, "—will you both stop deciding where I'm going to be living like I'm furniture?"

The room adjusted around her the way it always did. Nia had come to them four years ago, delivered by a storm and a fishing crew that claimed they'd pulled her out of open water thirty miles offshore, dry as a cat, with no memory of how she'd gotten there and a gift she didn't bother hiding because nobody had taught her to be afraid yet. She dreamed true — small things, mostly, lost earrings and coming weather — and lately, Felix had noticed, larger ones she kept behind her teeth. She had Odile's stubbornness without Odile's armor and Brannick's temper without his mass, and she was, though no one had ever said it aloud in so many words, the reason the house existed at all rather than being four separate people renting rooms.

"Nobody's deciding anything," Odile said, and set down the pen, and finally looked at Brannick, and something passed between the two of them that Felix had learned to read over three years: they'd had this fight before, quietly, and reached a truce that Nia wasn't supposed to know was fragile.

Odile turned to Felix. "You've heard it, then. The crier."

"I heard it. I also watched the customs house take delivery of forty blank registers this morning, and I counted six crown-blue coats between here and the Stair, which is six more than I've seen in a year." Felix sat down across from Odile. "They're not announcing this for the upcountry hedge-witches, Odile. You don't send clerks to the mountains. Clerks are for cities. Cities are where the gifted actually live."

"You're certain that's what it means."

It wasn't quite a question. Odile Voss ran contraband, forged manifests, and bribed three harbormasters, and she had never once asked Felix directly what Felix was. It was a courtesy Felix had understood, eventually, as love: she didn't want to know, because knowing would make her complicit in a way that smuggling never had, and because Felix deserved a home where their silence was respected instead of interrogated.

Today the courtesy had to end.

"I'm certain," Felix said, "that the edict says *speakings*. And I'm certain that registration isn't a list, it's a net. And I'm certain that whatever happens next, this house is on it somewhere, because Nia pulls rain out of the air when she has nightmares and the harbor watch has eyes, even stupid ones."

Nia went very still on the windowsill. "You knew I still do that?"

"It rained indoors last winter, Nia. In the pantry. For an hour."

"That was one time."

"It was memorable."

Brannick laughed — one short bark, cut off fast, the laugh of a man remembering the room's business. "So we run," he said. "Thursday's boat. All four of us. Odile, tell me you'll at least look at the manifest."

And Odile closed the ledger, and squared it, and put both hands flat on top of it like a woman taking an oath on a holy book, and Felix felt the answer arrive before she spoke it, the way you feel a wave before it breaks.

"No," Odile said.

"*Odile.*"

"This city raised me. Off these docks, out of these lanes — my mother worked the rope walks until her hands gave out, and every coin I've ever moved moved through this harbor first. If I run, I confirm what the edict says. That we're something to be flushed out. That the right response to fear is to vanish." Her jaw was set in the particular way that had once gotten her banned from three trading houses before she turned twenty-five. "I'm not vanishing. I'm not teaching Nia that the answer to a crown is a rowboat."

"The answer to a crown," Brannick said, "is staying alive."

"The answer to a crown," Odile said, "is making it sorry it asked."

The silence after that had weight. Nia had stopped breathing, Felix realized; the girl was watching Odile with an expression Felix recognized because Felix wore it too sometimes in private — the look of someone measuring themselves against another person and finding out how much growing they had left.

It was Felix who broke it, quietly.

"There's a middle road," they said. "We don't run, and we don't sit in the open either. The house goes dark. No shipments aboveboard for nine days, nothing that puts our names near paper. Nia stops practicing —"

"I don't *practice*, it's not —"

"—Nia goes quiet," Felix repeated, gently, "and I clear out everything that ties this house to anything uncrownable. We become the most boring household in Vhalen. Boring survives edicts. Boring has survived everything since the invention of clerks."

Odile studied Felix across the table for a long moment, and Felix watched her do the thing she did — weigh risk against pride against the people in the room, running sums faster than most people count change.

"Boring," she said at last. "Fine. Boring until the ninth day. Then we reassess." She pointed the pen at Brannick. "And you keep the Thursday boat's name anyway. Just in case reassessment goes badly."

"Just in case," Brannick agreed, and some of the iron went out of his shoulders, and Nia exhaled, and the house resettled around its decision like a ship adjusting ballast.

Later — after Brannick had gone down to warn the warehouse crews, after Odile had disappeared into her office to start unpicking three years of paper trails, after the light through the kitchen window had gone amber and then gray — Felix found Nia on the roof.

She sat on the ridge tiles above the kitchen with her chin on her knees, looking out at the harbor, where the lamps of the customs house burned steady and official. Felix climbed up and sat beside her, close enough that shoulders touched, far enough to leave her room to not-talk if she wanted it. They sat in the quiet for a while. From up here Vhalen looked almost kind — lamplit lanes, masts swaying, woodsmoke going up soft and gray from ten thousand ordinary hearths.

"They're going to come here," Nia said. Not a question. Dreamers knew things in the wrong tense.

"Somebody might," Felix said. "Someday. Maybe not. Maybe the clerk's ink dries up first."

"I dreamed about the mountain again."

Felix's stomach dropped through the roof tiles, down through the kitchen, into the mud of Rope Lane. They kept their voice level with an effort that would have shown, if anyone but a dreamer were listening. "Oh?"

"The court under it. The one with the water sounds." Nia's eyes stayed fixed on the harbor. "It's been every night this week. Someone down there knows my name, Felix. He says it like he's reading it off a list."

The wind came off the sea and smelled of salt and, underneath, faintly, of the smoke from a brazier where three years of careful, harmless work was now ash. Nine days, said the edict. Registration, assessment, *taken*.

"We'll talk about the dream tomorrow," Felix said. "Tonight you're just a girl on a roof getting cold."

Nia nodded. But as they climbed back down through the skylight, she paused on the ladder, silhouetted against the last of the light, and said, in the matter-of-fact tone she used for weather:

"Felix? The list he's reading off. I think it's already been sold."

Down in the harbor district, unnoticed by anyone on the roof, a crown courier nailed the day's final posting to the customs house door, checked his seals, and walked on into the dark.
