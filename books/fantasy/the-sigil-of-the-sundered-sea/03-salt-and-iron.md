## 3. Salt and Iron

The knock came at the wrong hour, in the wrong rhythm, and Brannick knew before he was fully awake that it was over.

Three years of running a safehouse teaches you the grammar of doors. A friend knocks twice and waits. A customer knocks soft and names a price. The watch knocks loud and announces themselves. But this — this was four strikes, even as a metronome, spaced like a man counting his own heartbeat. That was salt-iron discipline. That was the Purification arriving at your door with its boots already wet from the harbor.

He rolled out of bed without a sound, which for a man his size was its own small miracle, and put his ear to the floorboards. Above him, the house held its breath. Odile's room: silent. Nia's loft: silent. Felix's corner by the kitchen, where they slept sitting up more often than not: silent, but Brannick had learned to read Felix's silences the way sailors read weather, and this one felt like a drawn blade.

Four strikes again.

"Household of the Verrin warehouse," said a voice through the door, flat as slate. "By edict of the Crown. Open for enumeration."

*Enumeration.* Brannick almost laughed. They'd found a word for it that sounded like tax season. Counting souls the way you counted barrels of salted herring, and if a soul came up gifted, well — crates got shipped too, didn't they.

He pulled on his boots. He took the iron poker from the hearth because it was there, and because a man should hold something when the world comes to take everything else. Then he went down the stairs he'd built himself, past the shelves where Felix's luck-knots sat in their innocent rows, and stopped with his hand on the bar of the alley door.

The back way was clear. He'd checked it at moonset, like every night for three years. Odile could have Nia down the coal chute and over the tanner's wall in ninety seconds if she didn't argue. If she didn't argue.

Odile was already on the stairs.

She'd come down barefoot and silent, hair loose, and she was looking at him with the expression she wore when she was about to do something brave and stupid, which in Odile's case was most expressions worth having. Behind her, at the top of the stairs, Nia's face floated pale in the dark — awake, of course she was awake, the child slept like a cat with one ear always turned to the sea.

"No," Brannick said quietly.

"You don't get to say no to me in my own house."

"It's not your house tonight." He kept his voice low, level, the voice he used for talking men off ledges. "Listen. Four knocks, even count. That's a sweep team, six at least, maybe eight. They'll have the front covered and the roof watched. You go out the coal chute with Nia, you go left along the tannery wall, and you don't stop until you're under the fish-market awnings."

"And you?"

"I'm going to open the door," Brannick said. "And I'm going to be very large, and very slow, and very boring. I'm going to talk about leases and rent receipts until their eyes glaze. I've been doing it to harbor inspectors for twenty years. These mask-fellows are just inspectors with worse manners."

"Brannick—"

"Odile." He said her name once, gently, the way you set something heavy down. "You raised half the orphans on this coast and fed the other half out of that kitchen. Nia needs someone who knows which rooftops don't creak. That's not me. That's you. Go."

Above them, at the top of the stairs, Nia spoke for the first time. Her voice was small and steady and it went through Brannick like a fishhook.

"Come after us," she said. "Not soon. After."

"I will," he lied, or told the truth, he honestly couldn't tell which, and then Odile kissed his cheek — quick, fierce, tasting of salt — and took the girl's hand, and they were gone down into the dark of the coal chute, and the house was just a house again, full of nothing but furniture and one man and an iron poker.

Brannick set the poker down. It would only make them rougher.

Then he lifted the bar, and opened the door.

* * *

They came in like tide through a broken hull.

Eight of them, in masks of grey-green metal that looked cast rather than forged, pitted like old ship's bronze, each one different in its corrosion — and Brannick understood, with the cold clarity of a man watching his own ship go down slowly enough to study the leak, that the masks were made of salt-iron, the stuff dredged up off the Sundered Sea's drowned wrecks, and that they were pitted because the sea itself hated them. Gifted metal. The crown hunted magic with magic and called it purification.

Their leader carried a ledger chained to his wrist.

"Name," the leader said.

"Brannick Voss. I hold the lease on this property and three others on Tanners Row. If this is about the customs duties—"

"Occupants."

"Myself. My housekeeper is away visiting her sister in—"

"Search it."

They moved through the house like men who had done this a hundred times and had stopped pretending it was anything but theft. They turned up Felix's charm shelf inside a minute. One of them gathered the luck-knots into a canvas sack with the tenderness of a man collecting evidence, which was exactly what they were now. Another went up the stairs, and Brannick heard him find the loft, and heard the pause, and heard him call down:

"Bunk's still warm, sir. Small sleeper. Child-sized."

The leader looked at Brannick. Brannick looked back, and gave him twenty years of harbor boredom, and thought about coal chutes and tannery walls and a girl with one ear turned to the sea.

"Where is the child, Master Voss?"

"What child? This is a warehouse household. Children come through here all hours — dockworkers' brats, we feed them, it keeps them out of trouble—"

"The ledger says a gifted minor registered to this address. Registered under the name—" he consulted the chained book, and here his flat voice did something terrible, something almost pleased, "—of *Nia*."

Brannick's heart did not stop. He'd have sworn later that it sped up instead, hammering, because somewhere out past the tanner's wall a small person was moving fast and quiet and alive, and every second he stood here being boring was a second bought with interest.

"There are forty Nias on this coast," he said. "It's a common name among the—"

"Bind him."

They took his arms behind his back with cord that bit, salt-iron thread woven through hemp, and Brannick noted distantly that it stung where it touched skin, and wondered what that meant about a man who'd spent his whole life within smell of the sea. The leader flipped to a fresh page in the chained ledger.

"Brannick Voss. Obstruction. Harboring. Enumeration deferred pending transport." He looked up, and behind the corroded eye-holes of the mask there was nothing at all, just shadow and lamplight. "You'll be taken to the registry barge. If the household presents itself for registration within nine days, penalties may be—"

That was when the lamp went out.

Not blew out. Went out, all at once, like a hand closing around the flame, and in the sudden dark Brannick heard — felt — a word spoken in a voice he knew better than his own mother's, though he'd never heard it used like this. It wasn't loud. It didn't need to be. It landed in the room the way an anchor lands in shallow water, and every hunter in the house staggered as if the floor had tilted.

Someone said, from the doorway, in a voice like a blade being drawn very slowly:

"*Stand away from him.*"

* * *

Felix Marchetti had been on the roof across the alley since before midnight.

They had watched the hunters arrive. They had watched Brannick open the door like a fool, like a saint, like exactly the man he was, and they had done what they had trained themselves to do for three years, which was nothing. That was the oath. Not *never fight* — anyone who said a swordspeaker never fought had never met one — but *never draw the sword for show*. Never spend the gift on witnesses. Never let the name in the blade be seen speaking, because a seen swordspeaker was a dead one, and worse, a caught one, and worse than that, a *used* one. The crown had ways of making a caught swordspeaker useful. Felix had watched it happen, once, from a distance they still dreamed about.

So they lay flat on the cold shingles with their sword wrapped in sacking beside them and they counted hunters — eight in, two at the mouth of the alley, one on the far corner with a lantern — and they recited the terms of their own survival like a rosary. *Don't draw. Don't speak. Don't look at the door. Let Brannick be large and slow and boring. Let the law be satisfied with paperwork.*

Then they heard the leader read Nia's name out of a chained ledger, and heard him say *registered*, and something in Felix's chest that had been carefully folded away for three years came unfolded all at once, because *registered* meant *sold*, meant somebody had signed papers on a seven-year-old before the ink on the edict was dry, meant this was never a hunt at all but a delivery route, and Felix Marchetti discovered that there is a kind of fear that does not freeze you but instead makes the world very, very simple.

The world, right then, contained twelve hunters and one door and a man tied to a chair who had once carried Felix, feverish and nameless, three miles through a storm because leaving people on beaches was not something his hands knew how to do.

The rules were clear. The rules had kept them alive.

Felix picked up the sword, unwrapped the sacking, and drew it.

The name came up the blade like heat through a poker — their true name, the one they had spoken into the steel at nineteen years old in exchange for the power to make words into weights, and it rose singing, and Felix put their mouth close to the metal and gave it one word to carry.

"*Out.*"

Every flame in the house died.

They crossed the alley between one heartbeat and the next — the gift made their body light as a spoken promise — and came through the door into a darkness full of stumbling, masked men, and said the second word straight down the blade so it struck the floorboards and rang outward:

"*Kneel.*"

Six of them went down hard, joints buckling, salt-iron masks cracking against wood. Two stayed upright through sheer discipline, blades out, swinging blind toward the sound of Felix's breathing, and Felix stepped inside the first swing and broke the man's wrist with the flat and put him face-down with a knee, and was turning for the second when the lamplight from the alley mouth caught the edge of things and showed them the room.

Showed them Brannick, bound to a chair, eyes wide.

Showed them the leader, backed against the far wall, ledger clutched to his chest like a breastplate — upright, unbound, untouched, because Felix's words had needed weight to move men and this one had anchored himself somehow, or simply been too far, or simply mattered less. He wasn't reaching for a weapon. He was reaching for a whistle, and his voice, when it came, was no longer flat at all. It was delighted.

"Swordspeaker," he breathed. "A live one. Oh, the registry will pay for you—"

Felix had perhaps four seconds before the whistle, before the corner-watchman's lantern swung this way, before the night filled with boots.

Four seconds, and Brannick in the chair saying, ragged, "*Go, you idiot, go—*" and the leader's lungs filling for the blast—

Felix did the arithmetic they would spend every remaining day of this story trying to undo, and chose the only sum that saved everyone in the room except themselves.

They spoke one final word down the blade — "*Sleep*" — and laid the leader out mid-breath, whistle unblown, and then they were out the back and over the tannery wall and gone into Vhalen's labyrinth with the whole city's darkness swallowing them, and the last thing they heard behind them was not pursuit at all but silence, and the silence was worse.

Because they had drawn the sword. For witnesses. For a cause. In front of a hunter who had seen, who had *known*, whose ledger now held the single most valuable entry on the entire coast.

And because they had left Brannick bound in that chair, and the hunters would wake, and the whistle would blow, and the sweep teams would come back in numbers that no word, however weighted, could kneel.

Felix ran. Rooftop to rooftop, wrapped sword tight against their spine, blood singing with three years of unused power finally spent, and beneath the singing was a colder note, steady as a keel: *the point of no return is only ever visible from the other side of it.*

Behind them, faint over the water, a whistle began to blow.

And ahead, under the fish-market awnings, a small figure peeled away from the shadows where Odile had tried to make her stay hidden — because Nia, cat-eared and sleepless, had heard every word of the fight, and had heard something else besides, something in the tone of Felix's voice through the walls, something that made her turn not toward safety but toward the sound of the one person in Vhalen whose gift sang in the same key as hers.

She was waiting at the foot of the wall when Felix dropped down, and she looked up at them with eyes that held no fear at all, and she said:

"They're going to take him to the barge. I dreamed it. I dream everything they do now, and I need to tell you what else I saw."
