## 4. What Nia Knew

Nia had stopped sleeping the way other people slept.

She'd learned to do it in pieces, the way you eat stolen bread — quick, watchful, ready to swallow and run. She'd sleep two hours, wake, check the bolt on the door, sleep again. Brannick called it her "cat habit." Odile said it was because she was twelve and twelve-year-olds were all half-feral. Felix never said anything, but Felix always knew when she'd been up, because Felix noticed everything and pretended not to, which was its own kind of kindness.

So Nia was awake at the hour the dreams came, sitting cross-legged on the pallet in the loft above the charm-shop-that-wasn't, watching rain slide down the window like it was trying to escape too.

The dream had started nine days ago.

She hadn't told anyone. That was the first betrayal — hers, not theirs. Nine days of waking with salt drying on her lips and the taste of deep water in her throat, and she'd said nothing, because saying it out loud would make it real, and if it wasn't real then it couldn't take her.

But tonight it had said her name. Out loud. In a voice like a bell rung underwater, patient as tide, and there was no pretending after that.

*Nia,* it had said. *Nia of the Saltglass House.*

Nobody called it that anymore. The Saltglass House had been Odile's grandmother's smuggling crew, three generations back, before the name went quiet and the family went into honest-ish work running charms and contraband out of a harbor district nobody inspected twice. Nia had found the name scratched inside a drawer when she was eight, and Odile had told her the story once, by candlelight, the way you tell a child about dead kings — something that happened long ago and far away and could never reach you here.

The drowned king knew it anyway.

*Come see me, little anchor,* he'd said, and smiled, and his smile was kind the way deep water is kind when you've already stopped struggling. *You were sold fair. I keep what is bought.*

Nia pulled her knees up to her chest.

Sold fair.

* * *

Morning came gray and smelling of kelp. Downstairs, the shop was doing its morning pantomime: Felix at the counter laying out the harmless stock — luck-knots, traveler's wards, a jar of "blessed" salt that was just salt — while Odile counted coin and Brannick pretended to sweep a floor that didn't need sweeping. The criers had come through yesterday with the Purification Edict, and the whole street wore the sound of it like a bruise. *All gifted souls to present for registration.* Registration. As if they were dogs.

Felix looked up when Nia's foot hit the third stair. They always knew. "You're pale."

"I'm always pale."

"Paler."

Odile set down the ledger. Brannick leaned the broom against the wall, which from him was the equivalent of drawing a weapon. Three faces turned toward her, and Nia felt the strange vertigo of being loved by people who were very bad at saying so and very good at showing it — and understood that what she was about to say would change the shape of every day after this one.

"There's something I should have told you," she said. "Nine days ago."

She told them standing up, because sitting down would have made her small and this needed her to be as big as she could manage.

"I've been dreaming of a court under the mountain. The one from the old stories — the Sundered Sea, the drowned king. It's real. He's real. And he knows my name." Her voice cracked on the next part and she pushed through it, the way you push through cold water. "He calls me *little anchor*. He says—" She made herself look at each of them. "He says I was sold fair. He says he keeps what's bought."

The silence that followed had weight, like the pause between lightning and thunder.

"Sold," Odile repeated. Her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice she used with customs men. "Sold how. Sold to whom."

"I don't know. But Odile — the edict was signed four days ago. The dreams started nine days ago." Nia watched the arithmetic land on their faces one at a time, watched Felix get there first because Felix always did. "Whatever's hunting us, it started before the law did. The law isn't the hunter. It's just the net."

Brannick swore, long and low and creative. Odile was already moving, pulling ledgers, checking dates on shipments, on buyers, on everyone who'd been through the shop in the last season — and Nia saw it happen behind her eyes, the terrible recalculation of every friendly face into a possible seller of children.

It was Felix who came around the counter and knelt in front of her, slow, the way you approach a spooked animal. Up close their eyes were the color of shallow water over sand, and for the first time Nia wondered what color they'd been before whatever oath had taken everything else from them.

"Little anchor," Felix said quietly. "That's a seal-word. Do you know what an anchor does?"

"Keeps a boat still."

"Keeps something *held*." Felix's hand hovered near her shoulder without landing, asking permission even now. "There are stories — bad ones — about what the mountain does with gifted souls. What it needs them for." A muscle worked in their jaw. "We're going to find out exactly who sold you, and we're going to un-sell you. I swear it."

And Nia, who had spent nine days alone with a king's voice in her head, finally let herself cry — not because she was scared, though she was, but because someone had just said *we*, and meant it, and meant *you* most of all.

* * *

They held council that night with the shutters barred and a ward humming — a real one, Felix's, the first Nia had ever felt at full strength, like standing inside a struck bell.

"It has to be a broker," Odile said. She'd been pacing for an hour; the floorboards knew her route by now. "Bonds don't sell themselves. There's paperwork. There's always paperwork — the crown doesn't sneeze without a form in triplicate." She slammed a ledger shut. "Someone registered Nia's bond with the court beneath the mountain, and someone used the edict to make the collection legal."

"The harbor brokers," Brannick rumbled. "Half of them deal in worse than souls. The Tanneries crowd especially."

"Then tomorrow we visit the Tanneries." Odile's jaw was set. But Nia, watching from the stairs, saw the fear underneath it — saw that Odile, who feared nothing with a face, was terrified of something without one: the possibility that someone she'd trusted, drunk with, traded with, had priced her daughter's soul and shaken hands on it.

Felix caught Nia watching and beckoned her over. "How does he talk to you? In the dreams. Does he ask things?"

"He tells things." Nia hugged her arms. "Mostly he's lonely. That's the worst part. He sounds like—" She hunted for it. "Like somebody who's been holding a door shut for so long he forgot why, but he can't let go because he's scared of what comes through. And he keeps saying the sea is coming, and only the anchors hold it, and anchors are *given* or they're *taken*."

Felix went very still.

"Felix?"

"That's an oath-formula," they said softly. "Old liturgy. Where did you hear those words?"

"In the dream. He sings them sometimes. Like a lullaby." Nia frowned. "Why? What does it mean?"

But Felix wasn't answering. Felix was looking at the middle distance with the expression of someone reading a letter from a ghost, and whatever they saw there made them older by years in a single breath.

Outside, far off toward the harbor, a bell began to toll — not the tide-bell, not the temple bell, but the sharp triple-strike of the witch-hunters' muster. Then another bell. Then another, ringing district by district toward them like footsteps.

Odile blew out the candle.

"They're not waiting for registration," she said into the dark. "Everyone up. Brannick, the back stairs. Nia, stay behind me."

And in the last grey light before the shuttered dark, Nia heard the drowned king's voice one more time, gentle and terrible, closer than it had ever been:

*I have sent for you, little anchor. Come willingly, and no one else needs to burn.*
