## 5. The Broker's Ledger

The Tanneries smelled the way they always did — like something dying slowly and being paid for it. Felix had spent three years avoiding the district for exactly that reason: too many sharp noses, too little light, and a population of people who had learned to survive by selling things nobody should sell.

Tonight it suited them fine.

Odile walked half a step ahead, which was her way of saying she'd already decided how this would go and was merely letting Felix attend. She wore her good coat, the one with the brass buttons she polished when she wanted to look respectable to someone she intended to ruin.

"He keeps rooms above the rendering shed," she said. "Corvo Vess. Calls himself a registrar of unusual inheritances."

"Unusual inheritances." Felix turned the phrase over like a bad coin.

"That's what he puts on his door. What he actually does is match gifted children to buyers who want a soul on a leash. He's been doing it since before the edict gave him cover. The edict just made him legal."

Felix's hand found the hilt of their sword without being asked. They made themselves let go of it. *Not yet. Not here.* Drawing steel in the Tanneries over words alone was how you ended up in a harbor grave, and Odile needed them thinking, not bleeding.

"How do you know him?"

"I don't. Brannick did." Odile said the name flatly, the way you set down something heavy so your hands are free. "Brannick kept lists. Ships, cargo, contacts. Corvo Vess was under a heading I never asked about until two nights ago."

Two nights ago. Before the raid. Which meant Brannick had known about brokers in Vhalen and hadn't told Felix — or hadn't thought it mattered, because none of his people were for sale. Because Nia wasn't a thing you sold. Felix filed that wound away to bleed later.

The stairs above the rendering shed were narrow and greased with years of tallow smoke. A sign at the top read CORVO VESS, REGISTRAR, in letters that had once been gilt. Under it, smaller: *Inquiries by appointment.*

Odile knocked twice, then put her boot through the door instead.

* * *

Corvo Vess was smaller than his reputation required. A soft man, fiftyish, with ink-stained fingers and the particular calm of someone who had bought his safety in advance from everyone who might come for him. He didn't reach for anything when the door splintered. He simply folded his hands on his desk, atop a ledger bound in grey leather, and looked at them the way a clerk looks at customers who've arrived after closing.

"Gentlefolk," he said. "I assume this is an inquiry."

Odile crossed the room and set both palms flat on the desk. "You registered a bond three weeks ago. A girl. Twelve years old. Brown skin, crooked front tooth, answers to Nia even when she pretends not to."

Something moved behind Vess's eyes — not guilt; Felix had learned what guilt looked like during the purges, and this wasn't it. This was inventory being counted.

"Registrations are confidential," Vess said.

"So is the inside of a rendering vat," Odile said pleasantly. "Though I hear the smell carries."

Felix stayed by the door. That was the arrangement they'd made on the walk over: Odile talked, Felix loomed. It was a role Felix had never expected to be good at, but three years of hiding a gift coiled in their voice had taught them a great deal about standing still while something dangerous waited underneath. They let a little of it rise now — not the oath-voice, nothing that could be named as magic, just the weight behind it. The sense of a held breath. Vess felt it. His hands tightened on each other.

"You're making an error," he said. "Whatever you think I took from you—"

"Her name is Nia," Felix said.

It wasn't the oath-voice. But it was close enough to its border that the candle flames leaned, and Vess stopped talking.

Odile pulled the ledger out from under his hands before he remembered to grab for it. She opened it on the desk and there it all was, in a beautiful clerk's hand, columns and columns of it: names, ages, districts, prices, and in a final column, destinations. Most entries said *market* or *private*. Some said *crown*. And near the back, dated nineteen days ago — before the edict, before the criers, before any of it —

*Nia. 12. Harborside. Gift: unclassified, strong. Sold. Destination: The Court Beneath. Bond registered. Reclaimable until renewal.*

"Reclaimable until renewal," Odile read aloud. "What renewal?"

Vess had gone the color of tallow himself. "You can't— those pages are sealed by contract—"

"The next tide-moon," Felix said quietly. "That's when, isn't it."

They knew the tide-moons the way every child of the coast knew them: the sea ran strange for three nights, higher than it had any right to, and the old people locked their doors and said the mountain was drinking. Nobody said what it was drinking. Nobody had ever needed to say it aloud where Felix could hear.

"Nineteen days," Vess said. And then, because he was a clerk to the marrow and couldn't bear an imprecise record: "Eighteen, if we're counting from moonrise tonight."

* * *

They got the rest out of him in pieces, and the pieces were worse than the whole.

The court beneath the mountain bought souls the way other courts bought grain — against shortage. The seal that held back the Sundered Sea needed feeding, and the drowned king's stewards paid in crown gold through intermediaries so deniable that even the witch-hunters' own quartermasters might not know what they were carrying. The edict, Vess explained, with the detached pride of a man describing infrastructure, had simply streamlined supply. Registration created inventory. Hunters created delivery.

"You're telling me the crown is farming its own people," Odile said.

"I'm telling you the crown is *fulfilling obligations*," Vess corrected, and had the good sense to flinch when Felix's chair creaked forward. "I move product. I don't ask what the product is for. Asking questions is how registrars stop being registrars."

"What happens at renewal?" Felix asked. "To the ones delivered."

"They go into the seal." Vess said it the way you'd say *they go into the ground.* "The bonded souls. That's the harvest. That's the whole architecture of it. A bond registered to the court can't be broken by anyone else — but it can be reclaimed by kin or claimant up until the rite renews the seal and fixes everything in place. Permanently." He wet his lips. "After renewal, reclaiming isn't a matter of law anymore. There's no law down there. There's only what holds the sea back."

"And if the bond isn't reclaimed?"

Vess looked at Felix with something almost like pity, which was the most honest thing he'd done all night. "Then the girl goes into the seal at the tide-moon, and the coast stays dry, and everyone who isn't her sleeps soundly. That's the bargain the city has had for three hundred years. You've all been living on it. You just never saw the ledger."

Odile closed the ledger with a snap that echoed off the low ceiling. When Felix glanced at her, expecting fury, they found something colder and more frightening instead. Her face had gone still the way water goes still before it freezes.

"Nineteen days," she said. "How do we reclaim a bond?"

"You don't," Vess said. "You can't storm the court. The gate-rites alone—"

"I didn't ask if it was possible. I asked how."

And there it was again, that flicker behind Vess's eyes — and Felix finally identified it, and hated it. It was recalculation. The soft man was weighing whether these two were worth more alive as clients than dead as complications, and finding the arithmetic interesting.

"There's a form," Vess said slowly. "There's always a form. A claim of kinship or wardship, presented to the court's steward before the renewal rite. The steward honors valid claims — it's in the old compacts, older than the crown, older than the edict. The court keeps its bargains to the letter. It has to. The letter is all that's holding the sea." He paused. "But a claimant must appear at the mountain before the tide-moon. In person. Through the sea-gate. With proof of the bond's terms and standing to challenge them."

"Proof," Odile said. "Meaning?"

"Meaning the original entry. My entry." Vess touched the grey ledger with one ink-stained finger, and Felix understood, with a lurch, why the man hadn't screamed for help when the door came apart. Why he'd sat so calmly with his hands folded over the very book that condemned him.

He'd been waiting to sell it to them.

* * *

They stood in the street outside afterward, in the reek of the vats, and Odile counted coins into Vess's soft palm — more coin than the ledger was worth as paper, less than it was worth as Nia's life, and all three of them knew exactly what kind of transaction that was. Vess counted it again, because men like him always did, then produced a certified copy with seals already affixed, because men like him had prepared that too.

"A pleasure doing business," he said, meaning none of it.

"If Nia dies in that seal," Odile told him, "I'm going to come back here, and I'm going to take my time."

"Registrars are protected persons under the edict," Vess said.

"The edict has eighteen days left in it, far as I care. Spend them well."

They walked. Neither spoke until the rendering sheds fell behind and the street opened toward the harbor, where the lamps came down to the water and the black sea breathed against the pilings like something asleep and dreaming badly.

Felix stopped at the rail above the fish-market steps. Below, the tide was already climbing — early, restless, licking at stones it usually ignored. Eighteen days from moonrise, Vess had said. The old women would have known without a ledger.

"Before the edict," Felix said. "He sold her before the edict was signed. Nineteen days ago. The hunters raided our house four days after the proclamation." They heard their own voice go flat and careful, the way it did when the alternative was shaking. "The hunt was never hunting, Odile. It was *delivery.*"

"I know."

"Brannick. The others taken in the raid. Were they inventory too? Or just the noise that covered one girl being carried off?" Felix's hands were fists on the rail. Somewhere below, a night-fisher cursed the high water. "Three years I kept my head down. Three years I sold luck-knots and swallowed my own voice and told myself staying small was keeping us safe. And the whole time there was a man in the Tanneries with a column for her name and a price at the bottom of it."

"Felix."

"She watched me work. That first night in the market, she watched me tie a luck-knot, and she *knew*, she always knew, and I told her to run along home—" Felix stopped. Breathed. The sea breathed with them, huge and patient, and for one vertiginous moment Felix understood what the drowned king must feel, holding all that back, all that hunger, night after night for three hundred years.

No. Not yet. Pity for the monster could wait. Nia couldn't.

Odile put her hand over Felix's fist on the rail. Her grip was iron wrapped in silk, the way she did everything.

"Here's what we're not going to do," she said. "We're not going to drown in the what-ifs. We're not going to panic, because panic spends days we don't have. We have a copy of the bond. We have a claim process. We have eighteen days and a sea-gate." Her thumb moved once across Felix's knuckles, the only tenderness she allowed in the open. "What we don't have is a crew, a way past the gate-rites, or the first idea what we're walking into under that mountain. So tomorrow we start buying all three. Tonight we go home, and you tell me everything you know about swordspeakers and oaths and whatever it is you're not saying whenever someone mentions the drowned king. Because I saw your face when he said *seal*, Felix Marchetti. Or whatever your name is."

"It's Marchetti," Felix said automatically, and then heard it — the lie so worn it fit like their own skin. Their true name hadn't been theirs in eleven years. It lived in the blade at their hip, spoken into the steel by their own young voice, and only a swordspeaker's death or a swordspeaker's breaking would ever give it back.

"Not tonight," Felix said. "But soon. I promise you that much. If I'm asking this family to sail into the dark for a child, the family deserves to know what I am."

"We're not sailing into the dark for *a* child," Odile said. "We're sailing into the dark for Nia. Say it right or don't say it."

"Nia," Felix said. And the name came out of them carrying weight, the way names did when a speaker meant them, and down in the black water something vast shifted its attention — briefly, distantly, like a sleeper turning toward a light left burning in a window.

Neither of them noticed. But the sea did.

"Eighteen days," Odile said. "Starting now, we spend every one like it's the last coin in Vhalen."

She turned toward home, coat buttons catching the lamplight, already composing lists — Odile built armies the way other people built breakfast, out of whatever was lying around and a refusal to accept the recipe. Felix lingered a moment at the rail, looking east along the coast to where the mountain stood against the stars: a black absence shaped like a molar, roots sunk deep, and somewhere beneath it a king grown old around a duty he no longer remembered how to lay down.

*Hold,* the sea seemed to say, in the voice it used inside dreams. *Or break. Either way, come.*

Felix pushed off the rail and followed Odile into the lamplight, cold resolve settling over the panic like a plate over armor — not gone, just fitted for war.

Eighteen days. One stolen child. One forged copy, one corrupt registrar, one impossible gate.

And, somewhere in the wreckage of Vhalen's outlawed and desperate, a crew that didn't exist yet.
