## 6. A Crew of the Outlawed

Odile had buried three husbands and one smuggling empire, and she'd learned the same lesson from each funeral: grief is a luxury you schedule after the work is done.

Brannick was two days taken. Nia was sold. Felix was somewhere on the rooftops not sleeping, and the harbor district had gone quiet in the particular way quiet goes when everyone is listening for boots.

So Odile did what she always did. She made a list.

She sat at the kitchen table with a stub of charcoal and a sheet of paper that had once wrapped salt fish, and she wrote down every person in Vhalen who owed her, feared her, loved her, or hated her enough to be useful. The list was short. It got shorter as she crossed off names — dead, fled, registered, taken.

Four names survived the cull.

She looked at them a long time. Then she blew out the candle, because daylight was coming and daylight was for other people's work.

* * *

Sefa lived above a chandlery on the Tidewalk, in a room with one window and forty jars of seawater arranged by moon-phase. She opened her door before Odile knocked, which told Odile two things: she'd seen her coming, and she'd already decided.

"You're going under the mountain," Sefa said.

"Good morning to you too."

"I read the water." Sefa stepped back to let her in. She was sixty if she was a day, brown as old rope, with hands that shook unless they were touching salt. "The tide's been wrong for nine days. Pulling north instead of east. Something under the cliffs is drinking." She looked at Odile over the rims of her spectacles. "You want me to tell you what I see in it, or do you want to pretend this is a social call?"

Odile sat on the only chair that wasn't stacked with jars. "Nia's bond is registered to the court beneath Grayharrow. We have until the tide-moon to get her out."

"Sixteen days now."

"Fifteen and a half. Thank you."

Sefa lifted a jar off the shelf — the third from the left, its water clouded with something that wasn't sediment — and held it up to the window. In the murk, Odile could swear the water moved against the light, pulling toward the mountain the way iron pulls to a lodestone.

"The last time the pull ran this hard," Sefa said, "I was nineteen. They took my sister." She set the jar down gently, the way you set down something breakable or beloved. "The court called itself merciful then too. Registered bonds. Fair compensation. My mother signed because the alternative was the mask-men." Her shaking hands went still around the jar's neck. "What's your plan?"

"Break in. Get her out."

"That's not a plan. That's a wish with a deadline."

"It's what I have."

Sefa was quiet for a moment. Then she began pulling jars down off the shelves, wrapping them in oilcloth with quick, practiced motions, packing them into a canvas satchel like a woman who'd had this bag packed for forty years and was only waiting for permission.

"Tide-reading is a registered gift," she said. "You understand that. The edict has my name on a list already; I've just never been home when they knocked." She cinched the satchel closed. "If I come with you, I don't come back from it. Either the mountain takes me or the crown does. There's no version where I grow old in that chandlery."

"Sefa—"

"Don't apologize. I've been waiting fifteen years for someone to give me a door out of this room." She slung the satchel across her shoulder and looked at Odile with eyes the gray-green of deep water. "But hear me, Odile Marsh. I'm not doing this for the girl. I'm doing it for my sister, whose name was Wren, and whom nobody came for. If we find her name in those ledgers under the mountain, we burn them. All of them. Every bond, every contract. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"Then let's go ruin somebody's harvest."

* * *

The twins could be found, as always, wherever locks were being discussed.

Odile found them in the back room of a locksmith's shop on Copper Lane — legitimately employed, which was new, and visibly bored, which was eternal. Ivo was picking the shop's own display case for practice while Rell timed him with a stolen innkeeper's watch. Neither looked up when she entered.

"Forty-one seconds," Rell said.

"Forty," said Ivo.

"Thirty-nine and a half if you hadn't bragged."

"You're timing me with a broken watch, Rell."

"It's not broken. It's *honest*." Rell pocketed the watch and finally looked up, and her face did the thing it always did when she saw Odile: a flicker of guilt, quickly buried. "Oh no."

"Oh yes," Odile said.

"We didn't touch your warehouse."

"You robbed my warehouse twice before you were sixteen. This isn't about the warehouse." Odile leaned against the doorframe and let the silence do some work. The twins were twenty-two now, all elbows and identical smirks, but she'd known them since they were eight-year-old chimney-thieves sleeping in her rafters, and she knew exactly which lever moved them. It wasn't money. It had never been money. "It's about Nia."

Rell's smirk died. Ivo set down his picks.

"She used to alphabetize our stolen letters," Ivo said quietly. "When she stayed with us that winter. By recipient. Including the ones we hadn't opened yet."

"She'd sit there for hours," Rell said. "Judging us."

"Judging us correctly," said Ivo.

"So here's the job." Odile laid it out plain, because the twins respected plainness: a mountain, a gate, a court full of things that ate souls, sixteen days, no legal exit. "I need lock-wrights who can open doors that were built to be opened by gods. I can pay you in danger and a probable death."

"Sold," said Ivo instantly.

"Ivo." Rell caught his sleeve. "The gate-rites. Everyone knows about the gate-rites. You have to answer questions, truthfully, and the mountain knows when you lie." She looked at her brother, and Odile saw something pass between them — the old arithmetic, the twin-math they'd done since childhood, dividing everything by two. "We've never lied to each other. Not once. Can we promise a mountain the same?"

Ivo considered this with uncharacteristic seriousness. "We can try."

"Trying gets alarms rung."

"Then we won't lie." He grinned, sudden and bright. "We'll just be very careful with the truth. There's a difference."

"There really isn't," said Rell. But she was already reaching for her tool-roll, the one with the picks filed from whalebone that couldn't be scryed, and Odile knew she'd won before either twin said another word.

"One condition," Rell added, shrugging into her coat. "Nobody splits us up. Whatever happens in there. Whatever bargain anybody offers. The twins stay twins."

Odile thought of soul-bonds and collateral and the shape of the price she might have to pay, and felt the first cold finger of foreknowledge touch her spine.

"Agreed," she said, and hated how easily the word came.

* * *

Maren Voss was harder.

Not because she was hiding — disgraced witch-hunters didn't hide in Vhalen, they drank, loudly and publicly, at the Drowned Rat on Anchor Street, daring the world to make something of it. Odile found her at the end of the bar at midday, three cups deep and stone sober behind the eyes, wearing her old coat with the insignia torn off but the shape of it still ghosted in the wool.

"You've got nerve," Maren said without turning. "Coming to sit next to me. Half this city would cross the street."

"Half this city is registered or running. They've got other worries."

"Hm." Maren turned. Up close she was younger than her reputation — thirty-five maybe, with a hunter's flat calm that alcohol hadn't managed to drown yet. "You're the smuggler. Pemberton. The one whose crew got harvested."

"Taken. And one sold."

"Same thing, different paperwork." Maren signaled the barkeep for another cup, then changed her mind and waved him off. That small war, Odile noted, she was losing daily. "What do you want? Because women like you don't visit women like me for the conversation."

"I need someone who knows the mountain's protocols. Gate-rites. Guard rotations. How the seal-renewal works."

Maren laughed — a short, ugly sound with nothing in it. "You need a traitor."

"I need a guide."

"Same thing, different paperwork." She turned her cup in slow circles. "Ask me why I was disgraced, Pemberton. Go on. Everyone wants to know and nobody asks."

"Why?"

"Because I asked a question." Maren's voice went flat and careful, a voice walking on ice. "Three years ago I delivered a bonded soul to Grayharrow. Standard procurement. And on the road, the crate sang. Three days, Pemberton. A child's voice, singing through pine boards, and my partner told me it was a trick the gifted use, and the protocols said the cargo doesn't feel anything once the bond takes, and I believed it because believing was easier." She stopped turning the cup. "At the gate, the mountain asked me the ritual questions and I answered them truthfully, because that's the rite, and one of the questions is *do you bring this gift freely*, and I said yes, and the gate *knew*. Gates like that know. It opened anyway. It opens anyway. That's the part that broke me — not that I lied, but that the mountain didn't care whether I lied. It just wanted delivery."

The bar noise seemed to recede around them.

"So I filed a protest. Wrote it up proper, through channels. Six weeks later my commission was revoked for 'conduct unbecoming,' my partner got promoted, and the crate —" Maren's jaw worked. "The singing stopped. I tell myself that means the child arrived safe and settled. I have to tell myself that. It's the only version where I'm still a person."

Odile let the silence sit. Then, quietly: "Come with us."

"To the mountain." Maren laughed again, worse than before. "Into the place I fed. With the people it feeds on. You're mad."

"Probably. But you know the gate-rites because you performed them. You know the rotations because you stood them. And you know what's in those crates because you carried one." Odile stood, dropping coins on the bar. "I'm not offering absolution, Voss. I'm offering labor. Come carry something heavier this time."

Maren stared at her cup for a long time. When she looked up, her eyes were dry and terrible and awake.

"If we get inside," she said, "and I find out what happened to the child in my crate — I need to know. Even if it's bad. Especially if it's bad."

"Done."

"And Pemberton." She rose, steadier than she had any right to be. "Your swordspeaker friend. Felix. The oath-bound one." Something moved behind the hunter-calm — fear, Odile realized, old and specific. "There's a standing protocol about swordspeakers. Priority capture. I know what the masks are instructed to do with them." She pulled her old coat tight. "If your Felix walks into that mountain, they'll know within an hour. You should plan for that."

"I know," Odile said. "We're planning for it."

They walked out together into the gray afternoon, and Odile counted her crew on her fingers like coins: a tide-reader with a dead sister, two twins with one rule, a hunter with a crate on her conscience. Plus Felix on the rooftops, and Brannick in whatever cell they kept him in, and Nia beneath the stone.

Five recruits. Fifteen days. One mountain.

It was the worst crew she'd ever assembled and the only one she'd ever needed.

They were crossing Anchor Street when Sefa stopped abruptly, satchel swinging, and put one bare hand flat against the cobblestones as though taking a pulse from the ground.

"Odile," she said. "The tide just changed again. It's pulling harder." She looked up, and the gray-green eyes were wide. "Whatever's under that mountain just started counting faster. We've lost time we haven't spent yet."

Behind them, out over the Sundered Sea, the horizon darkened a shade too early for evening — and far off, faint as a memory, Odile heard the first toll of a bell that had no business ringing.

The mountain knew they were coming.

Or it simply didn't care.
