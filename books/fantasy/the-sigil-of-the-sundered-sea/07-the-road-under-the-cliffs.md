## 7. The Road Under the Cliffs

The drowned coast road began where the mapmakers gave up.

Felix had walked it once, years ago, in the other direction — fleeing Vhalen with a stolen name and a sword that whispered to them at night. They'd sworn never to come back. Now they were walking it again, south toward the mountain, with a crew of five and a child who kept asking questions.

"Is it true the road floods at high tide?" Nia asked. She was walking backward, which Odile had forbidden twice, which meant she did it constantly.

"Parts of it," Felix said.

"Have you ever seen the water take someone?"

"Nia." Odile's voice from the front of the line, flat as a deck plank. "Walk forward."

Nia turned around, but her eyes came back to Felix like gulls to a fishing boat. She'd been doing that since they left the Tanneries. Watching. Waiting for something Felix couldn't name yet.

The road itself was a marvel nobody claimed credit for. It had been cut into the cliff face a hundred feet above the Sundered Sea, wide enough for two carts or six wary people, and every few miles it dipped through a gap where the ocean had won an argument with the stone. In those gaps the water ran green-black and deep, and you could see things moving down there that had no business being shaped the way they were.

Sefa walked at the rear, reading the tide-lines off their fingers like a rosary. "High water in four hours," they said, unprompted, the way they said everything. "We make the waystation before then or we swim."

"We'll make it," Odile said.

Nobody argued. Odile had been different since the Tanneries — quieter, harder, the softness she wore in Vhalen packed away somewhere for later. She walked like a woman carrying a ledger only she could read, and every entry was a debt.

Maren walked alone, which everyone pretended not to notice. The disgraced hunter kept her hood up against a sun that wasn't shining, and when Nia drifted too close, Maren found reasons to be elsewhere. Felix understood. Guilt has a smell. Some people can't stand their own.

* * *

They stopped at noon in the shadow of a sea-arch, and Felix taught Nia her first ward.

It wasn't much. A circle scratched in the dirt with a stick, three words spoken low — Felix's own invention, stripped of anything the old texts would recognize, because the old texts got people burned. The ward wouldn't stop a blade or a hunter. It would stop a listening ear. That was all. That was everything, these days.

"Again," Felix said.

Nia spoke the words. The circle flickered and died.

"You're pushing. Don't push. A ward isn't a wall, it's a held breath. You speak it like you're telling a secret."

"To who?"

"To yourself. To the part of you that already knows how."

Nia frowned at the dead circle, then closed her eyes, and this time when she spoke the three words they came out small and true, and the circle lit up like a coin at the bottom of a well — a soft gold shimmer, there and holding.

Felix felt something twist in their chest. Pride, maybe. Or fear wearing pride's coat. Because the girl had power, real power, the kind the crown called gifted and the court beneath the mountain called harvest, and every lesson Felix taught her made her brighter and more visible and more valuable to the things that wanted her.

"That's good," Felix said. "That's very good. Now let it go gently. Wards die hard if you strangle them."

Nia released the ward and looked up, and her face did the thing it did sometimes — went old, went knowing, went eleven going forty. "You're scared of teaching me."

"I'm careful about it."

"That's not what I asked."

Felix held her gaze. Somewhere behind them, Sefa was showing Ivo and Rell how to read a tide-line, and Odile stood watch on the arch, and the sea breathed below them all like something sleeping badly.

"I'm scared of what happens after," Felix said. "Teaching is easy. Teaching is just handing someone a lamp. What scares me is that the world we're walking into charges money for light."

Nia considered this with the seriousness she gave everything. Then: "You never charge me."

"No," Felix said. "I don't."

* * *

The waystation was a hollowed sea-cave the smugglers had used for generations — dry stone above the tide-line, old fire rings, walls carved with initials going back further than anyone could count. Odile found her own mark near the entrance, cut at fourteen with a fish-gutting knife, and touched it once like greeting an old friend.

They ate salt cod and hard bread. Rell told a story about a lock in Corvane that had taken her nine hours and cost her two fingers' worth of frostbite; Ivo corrected three details and added a fourth. Even Maren laughed, once, briefly, like a door opening and shutting.

And then the fire burned low, and Nia came and sat beside Felix on the cave's seaward lip, where you could hear the water working at the cliffs below, patient as a debt collector.

"Felix," she said. "What's a swordspeaker?"

Felix went still.

She'd heard the word — of course she'd heard it. Maren knew it. The broker in the Tanneries had spat it. And Nia missed nothing, never had, from the first night in the night market when she'd watched a luck-knot change hands and seen too much.

"It's an old word," Felix said carefully.

"I know it's an old word. Maren said it like it was a bad one. She said there used to be orders of them, before the Purification, and now there's—" Nia counted on her fingers— "basically you."

"There might be others. Hiding."

"Were you in an order?"

"Nia—"

"You said you don't charge me," she said. "That means you owe me truth instead. That's fair trade. Brannick taught me that."

Brannick. The name landed in Felix's chest like a hook. Three days now since the alley door, since the salt-iron masks, since Felix had broken a three-year rule and killed a man on a rooftop while Brannick was dragged away in chains. Every mile of this road was a payment on that debt.

"All right," Felix said. "Fair trade."

The sea worked at the cliffs. Felix looked at it for a long time.

"A swordspeaker doesn't cast spells," they said finally. "Not the way you're learning wards. We speak oaths, and the oaths become real. Bind a promise into iron and the iron keeps it. Speak protection over a doorway and the doorway holds. The old orders built bridges with vows and sank fleets with curses. Our whole art was making words so heavy the world had to carry them."

"That sounds wonderful."

"It sounds wonderful," Felix agreed. "Now. Do you know what an oath costs? Not the little ones. Not luck-knots and door-wards. A true oath. An ordaining."

Nia shook her head.

"Everything you are," Felix said. "When a swordspeaker takes the great oath — the one that binds your life to your blade — you speak your name into the steel. Your whole name. The one your mother used. The one that makes you *you*. And the sword takes it, and keeps it, and gives it back to no one. After that, you can't say it. You can't remember it right. Other people forget it too, slowly, like water wearing down a stone. You spend the rest of your life as a person with a hole where the most important word used to be."

Nia was quiet. Below them, the tide turned, and the sound of the sea changed pitch.

"Your name," she said. "You gave yours away?"

"Three years ago. In a cellar under the Temple district, with a blade across my knees and a war coming that I thought my oath could stop." Felix's hand had gone, without their noticing, to the hilt at their hip — the plain sword, the one that never left them, the one that hummed sometimes at the edge of hearing. "The war didn't stop. The order died anyway. And I woke up one morning in Vhalen and realized I couldn't recall my mother's voice saying my name, because there was nothing for her voice to say."

"What do people call you?"

"Whatever I tell them. Felix is — Felix is a name I bought off a dead man in a harbor town. It fits like borrowed boots."

Nia looked at the sword. Felix watched her look at it, and saw her arrive at the question before she asked it.

"Is your name still in there? Right now?"

"Yes."

"And you can't get it back?"

"Not without breaking the oath. And breaking an ordaining oath kills the speaker. That's the design. That's why the old orders lasted — you didn't swear lightly when swearing was forever."

Nia absorbed this. Then she did the thing that Felix would remember for the rest of their life, though they didn't know it yet: she reached out and put her small hand flat on the scabbard, right over the place where a stolen name slept in steel.

"Hello," she said to the sword. Politely. The way you'd greet a stranger guarding a door.

The sword hummed.

Not imagination — Felix felt it through their hip, a vibration like a struck bell heard through a wall, and Nia's eyes went wide, and for a moment neither of them breathed.

"It likes you," Felix said, and their voice came out rougher than they intended.

"Why?"

"Because you're not afraid of it. Most people are afraid of it. I'm afraid of it, half the time." Felix covered her hand with theirs, lifted it gently away. "Don't wake it again without asking. Names sleep badly."

* * *

Odile appeared at the mouth of the cave an hour past midnight, when the others slept and Felix sat watch with the sword across their knees.

"You told her," Odile said. Not a question.

"She asked."

"She's eleven."

"She's harvested," Felix said. "Or marked for it. There's a difference, and it's smaller than either of us wants." They fed a stick to the fire. "She deserves to know what she's walking toward. What I am. What any of it costs."

Odile crouched by the fire. In its light her face was all planes and shadows, the harbor-queen mask gone, and underneath it Felix could see the woman who had raised three stray children in a city that ate strays.

"When we get her back from this," Odile said slowly, "when this is done — she'll need more than a smuggler house. She'll need someone who knows what's inside her." She looked up. "I'm not asking you to promise anything. I'm telling you what I see."

"And what do you see?"

"A family missing a piece. Has been for three years. We just didn't know the piece was a sword."

Felix laughed — quietly, so as not to wake the others, but really laughed, for the first time since the alley door. Odile almost smiled, which from Odile was a coronation.

Then Sefa stirred at the cave's far end, and sat up, and said in their flat tide-reader's voice:

"The water's wrong."

Everyone was awake in seconds. Sefa stood at the seaward lip, fingers spread, reading air that had gone strange and heavy. Below the cliffs, where the drowned road dipped through its gaps, the sea had gone glass-still — no swell, no breath, a sheet of black silk stretched to the horizon.

"Tide's four hours out," Sefa said. "This isn't tide. Something down there is holding its breath."

And out of that stillness, faint and far and unmistakably directed — not drifting, *aimed* — came a sound none of them had ever heard and all of them understood.

Singing.

A single voice, rising from beneath the black water, singing a name.

Nia's.
