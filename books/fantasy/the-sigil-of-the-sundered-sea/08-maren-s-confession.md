## 8. Maren's Confession

The singing lasted until dawn.

Not the whole night — it came in phrases, patient as a fisherman hauling line, and each time it stopped, the silence it left behind was worse than the sound. Maren didn't sleep. She sat with her back against the cliff wall, her stolen hunter's cloak around her shoulders, and listened to a voice she recognized.

That was the thing no one else knew yet. She recognized it.

She had spent eleven years in the salt-iron mask, and in eleven years she had learned every sound the crown's machinery made: the creak of transport wagons, the rattle of manacle chains, the particular wet cough of people who had been crying for days. She knew the sounds of the taken. She knew, too, the sounds they made before — the bargaining, the praying, the flat quiet of those who had already understood there would be no rescue.

But once — once, only once — she had heard one sing.

* * *

She waited until the fire was down to coals and Sefa had finished explaining, for the fourth time, that whatever was below the water was not calling so much as *confirming*. Like a broker checking a ledger against the goods.

"It knows her name because someone told it," Sefa said. "Names travel on bonded water. That's how the seal hears."

"Then we move faster," Odile said. She hadn't sat down all night. She stood at the edge of the campfire light with her arms crossed, looking out at the black silk sea like it owed her money. "We're four days from the mountain. We cut it to three. We—"

"No." Maren heard her own voice come out of her, low and rough, and was surprised by it. Everyone turned. "You can't outrun this. You don't even know what you're running toward. The gate alone will kill you."

"You've said." Odile's voice went flat. "Nine questions, answered true, gifts masked. We have Sefa for the masking. What we don't have is anyone who's actually stood in front of that gate and lived."

Maren looked at the coals.

"I have," she said.

The silence that followed had weight. Felix, who had been sitting apart with their sword across their knees the way they always did — never drawn, never shown, just held, like a conversation they were refusing to finish — went very still.

"The gate-rites," Maren said. "The nine questions. The order of asking. Which answers the gate will accept and which ones it pretends to accept and then drowns you for. I know them because I performed them. Eleven deliveries through the sea-gate. I opened the way myself."

Ivo was on his feet first. Rell caught his arm, but he shook her off. "Deliveries," he said. "Of what?"

Maren met his eyes. He was twenty, maybe, with burn-scarred knuckles and a lock-wright's clever hands, and he deserved the truth delivered clean, without cushioning, because everyone in this crew had earned worse.

"Gifted souls," she said. "Crated. Sedated when I could get away with it, which was rarely. I carried them from the collection points to the mountain's gate, performed the rites, and handed them to the court's stewards. Eleven times. I was good at it. They gave me commendations."

Rell made a small sound, quickly swallowed.

"And the singing?" Nia asked.

Nia. Who should have been asleep hours ago, wrapped in Odile's spare coat, and who instead sat cross-legged by the fire with her chin on her knees, watching Maren with those too-old eyes. The child the whole crew was walking into a drowned mountain to save. The child whose name was being sung up through black water.

Maren's throat worked. She had rehearsed this confession on the road, in the dark, in the privy at the last way-station, and every rehearsal had collapsed at exactly this point.

"There was a boy," she said. "My seventh delivery. From the Tanneries district. Gifted — he could make plants grow, that's all, he could coax green things out of dead soil. His family sold him for six crowns and a pardon for an unpaid debt. Six crowns." Her laugh came out wrong, cracked in the middle. "I put him in the crate at Vhalen. Standard procedure. Ventilation slats, sedative dose, three days' transit along the drowned road to the gate."

"He sang," Nia said. Not a question.

"For three days," Maren said. "The sedative barely touched him — some gifts burn it off, we never learned which. So he was awake in there, in the dark, and he sang. Folk songs, mostly. His mother's songs. And on the third day, when we reached the gate, he'd gone quiet, and I performed the rites, and I handed him over, and—" Her hands had curled into fists without her noticing. She made herself open them. "And I took my pay. And I did four more."

Odile said, quietly, "Why are you telling us this now?"

Because the singing outside had been a boy's voice, Maren wanted to say. Because I lay awake all night matching pitch. Because every delivery I made taught me another turn of the gate-rites, and knowledge bought that cheaply has to be spent on something or it rots the hand that holds it.

What she said was: "Because you're going to walk into that gate blind otherwise, and blind is how they want you."

Ivo spat into the coals. "So that's your play. Confess, look sorry, keep us alive long enough to use you. Clever. Hunter-clever."

"Ivo," Odile started.

"No." He stepped forward, and Rell let him, because Rell was watching Maren with an expression Maren had seen before — on the faces of families at collection points, right before they stopped seeing her as a person. "Eleven crates. You counted them yourself. Did you count the singing? Did you lie awake counting?"

"Yes," Maren said. "Every night since. That's not absolution. It's arithmetic."

"Arithmetic." Ivo laughed, ugly and short. "You want to know what I think? I think the crown doesn't lose its hunters. Hunters stop being hunters. They run, they hide, they find smuggler crews desperate enough not to ask questions. And then, when the crew gets close enough to the prize, the hunter remembers where her loyalties—"

"That's enough." Felix's voice.

It wasn't loud. Felix almost never raised it; the whole market knew the rumor that the charm-seller Marchetti had never once been heard to shout. But it landed in the clearing like a dropped anchor, and Ivo stopped mid-word, breathing hard.

Felix rose. They crossed to the fire slowly, sword still across their back, and stood between Maren and the others — not shielding her, exactly. Standing with her, which was somehow worse, because it put Maren's guilt in the same light as everyone else's faces.

"Maren saved my life twice before any of you met me," Felix said. "In Vhalen, during the first sweep after the edict, when Brannick was already in irons. She walked past two of her own kind to do it. She could have reported our safehouse then. She didn't."

"She could be reporting it now," Ivo muttered.

"With what signal?" Sefa asked mildly, from the seaward lip. They hadn't turned around. "I've been reading the air since we left the cliffs' shadow. No relay-gifts active, no scrying residue, nothing. If she's a traitor, she's a very quiet one." A pause. "Also, she snores. Traitors usually manage better discipline."

Despite everything, Rell snorted.

"She also knows the gate," Felix said, "which none of the rest of us do. And she came to us knowing what it would cost her to say any of this." Felix turned to Maren, and their eyes — steady, unreadable, carrying three years of hidden weight — held hers. "Tell them the rest. All of it. The part you've been circling."

Maren's stomach dropped. She hadn't told Felix everything — she'd thought she'd buried the worst of it deep enough that even a swordspeaker's instincts wouldn't dig it up. But Felix had spent a lifetime hearing the truth under words, and apparently hers was no different from anyone else's.

"There's a reason they gave me the deliveries," she said. "Eleven of them, to the same hunter. It's not rotation. Rotation keeps anyone from learning too much. They gave me the route because of my gift."

The word fell into the firelight and nobody breathed.

"I'm registered null," Maren said. "That's why they trusted me near the crates — nulls don't hear bonded souls, the brokers said. The bond-magic slides off us. It's why the masks work on us, why the crates don't sing to us." She looked down at her hands. "Except I'm not null. I hear them fine. Always have. I just learned very young that saying so gets people taken. So I lied on my registration papers, and the crown's best lie detector is a bureaucrat with a checklist, and I passed."

"So the court thinks you're deaf," Sefa said slowly, turning from the water at last, "and you've been standing next to their cargo hearing every word."

"Every word," Maren said. "Eleven crates. Do you know what they say in there? Not prayers. Not begging, not after the first day. They talk about home. Recipes. Brothers. One woman described her garden to me for six hours through three inches of oak, because she said if someone remembered it, maybe it kept existing somewhere." Her voice frayed. She pulled it straight. "And the boy sang, and I stood outside his crate for three days pretending to hear nothing, and on the third day I memorized his song, and I have hummed it to myself every night for four years so it wouldn't be lost. That's my confession. That's all of it. There isn't more, and there isn't less."

Nobody spoke. The fire ticked. Out past the cliffs, faint as memory, the sea began its phrase again — *Ni-a, Ni-a* — and stopped.

It was Nia who moved. She got up, crossed the campsite, and sat down beside Maren, close enough that their shoulders touched.

"What was the boy's name?" she asked.

Maren blinked. Of all the questions — the accusations, the arithmetic, the verdicts she'd braced for — this one undid her. "Tomas," she managed. "His name was Tomas."

"Tomas," Nia repeated solemnly, as if filing it somewhere important. Then she looked up at the rest of the crew, and the too-old eyes were blazing now. "You're all doing sums about whether to trust her. I've got a simpler sum. She's the only person here who's been inside and come back out. She's the only person who knows the door. And she just told us the worst thing about herself in front of strangers, which is more than most people ever do." She shrugged, small and fierce. "Also, she's coming, because if she doesn't, who's going to teach us which question makes the gate drown you?"

A shaky breath went around the circle. Odile uncrossed her arms for the first time all night.

"Conditions," Odile said. "You carry no messages out. You touch none of the court's people without telling us first. And if there is anything — anything — you're still holding back, it comes out now, tonight, because after tomorrow there's no time for second confessions."

Maren searched herself honestly, the way she'd searched the crates, listening for what hid beneath the floorboards. One thing remained. Small. Terrible in its smallness.

"One thing," she said. "The ninth question. The gate asks it last, and everyone assumes it's a formality. *Whom do you serve?* Everyone answers with the crown's formula and passes." She met Felix's eyes. "But the gate weighs the answer. It always has. Every delivery, I answered with the formula and felt the gate... hesitate. Just a moment. Like something behind the stone was disappointed." She paused. "I think the gate wants a true answer. I think it's been waiting centuries for someone to give one. And I don't know what happens when it finally does — whether the mountain opens like a flower or swallows us whole."

Sefa had gone pale, which for a tide-reader was roughly equivalent to screaming.

"Well," Ivo said at last, rubbing his scarred knuckles, "at least we'll die honestly."

"Not the rallying speech I'd have picked," Rell murmured, "but it'll do."

Odile looked around the circle — at the twins, at Sefa, at Nia leaning against Maren's shoulder as if she belonged there, at Felix silent by the fire with their hand resting, just barely, on the hilt they never drew.

"We sleep in shifts," she said. "We move in two hours. Three days to the mountain." Her jaw tightened. "And when we reach that gate, we tell it the truth. Whatever it costs."

Out beyond the cliffs, the black water held perfectly, terribly still — and then the singing came again, closer now, no longer aimed at Nia alone.

It had learned all their names.
