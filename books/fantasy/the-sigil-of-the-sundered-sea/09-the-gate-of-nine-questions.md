## 9. The Gate of Nine Questions

The sea-gate was older than the mountain it was cut into.

Sefa had read tides her whole life — read them in foam-lines and gull-flight, in the way kelp leaned before a swell arrived. She could tell you what the water would do three days from now off the Vhalen breakwater, could feel a storm gathering two hundred leagues out as a pressure behind her eyes. But she had never read anything like the water at the drowned coast's end.

It didn't move.

It lay against the cliffs like poured glass, black-green and utterly still, holding the reflection of the tide-moon so precisely that Sefa felt she was looking down at a second moon underwater rather than up at the real one. No waves. No current. The Sundered Sea here wasn't sleeping; it was *waiting*, the way a dog waits at a door, and everything in her gift told her that when the tide-moon reached its fullness in nine days, that stillness would break all along the coast at once.

"Say something," Odile said quietly beside her. "You've been staring for half an hour."

"I'm listening," Sefa said. "There's a difference."

"And?"

Sefa pulled her coat tighter. The wind coming off the motionless water was cold in a way that had nothing to do with weather. "And the sea is counting."

Maren, standing behind them with her hood back and her hunter's mask hanging from her belt like a confession, made a small sound in her throat. "Counting what?"

"Days." Sefa turned away from the water because looking at it too long made her teeth ache. "Nine of them. It knows the schedule better than we do."

Behind them, the rest of the crew came down the last stretch of cliff road: Felix with their sword wrapped in sailcloth across their back, Nia walking close enough to touch Felix's elbow without actually touching it — she'd been doing that since the nursery dreams started getting worse — and Brannick, freed three days ago from the hunters' holding barge by means Odile refused to describe in front of Nia, moving stiffly but moving, which was more than any of them had dared hope on the night he was taken.

Ivo and Rell brought up the rear, carrying the lock-wright kit between them like pallbearers. Rell hadn't spoken much since Maren's confession around the fire two nights past. She kept looking at Maren the way you look at a door you're not sure is locked.

"There," Felix said.

The gate was set into the cliff face itself, forty feet above the still water, reachable by stairs carved into the rock — nine flights, Sefa counted automatically, nine landings, and at each landing a lamp burned with no oil reservoir she could see. The flame was the wrong color. Not gold, not blue. The green-black of deep water under moonlight, burning without flickering in the wind.

At the top, the gate itself: black stone veined with white, tall as a ship's mast, and across its face nine rings of carved script, each ring turning slowly, independently, like the rings of some vast orrery. The sound they made was almost below hearing — a grinding hum Sefa felt in her sternum rather than heard with her ears.

She'd read about this. Every smuggler had heard rumors of the mountain's sea-gate, and most of the rumors were nonsense. But Maren's protocols, extracted over six days of bad sleep and worse questions, had been specific: *the Gate of Nine Questions. Nine truths asked of those who would enter. Answer false and the mountain answers instead.*

"Positions," Odile said, and the crew arranged itself without being told twice, because whatever else they were — smugglers, liars, thieves, a disgraced witch-hunter — they were people who understood that some doors only open once.

Sefa took her place at the center. That had been argued about for two nights. The gate-rites required a speaker, someone to answer for the group, and the answerer had to be gifted — the gate tasted truth through the gift, Maren had said, not through the tongue. A mundane could lie to it all day. But every gifted soul in their crew was a liability: Felix's oath-voice, Nia's bond, Brannick's salt-sense, Odile's luck that bent around her like current around a stone. If the gate looked at any of them directly, it would see exactly what the crown wanted registered.

Which left Sefa, whose gift was the quietest of them. Tide-reading left no residue. No marks, no signatures, nothing the gate could taste unless she let it rise.

Her job was twofold: answer the nine questions truthfully — because lies rang against this stone like cracked bells, Maren had sworn it — and hold the others' gifts beneath the surface while she did it, keeping the whole crew's true nature submerged the way she kept her own.

"You can refuse," Felix said quietly, coming to stand beside her. They unwrapped the sword from its sailcloth, considered it, and wrapped it again. "We find another way in."

"There is no other way in. You know that. Maren knows that. The mountain has one mouth and we're standing in front of it." Sefa flexed her fingers. They were already cold, and the cold wasn't from the wind. "Ask me tomorrow if I regret it. Tonight, ask me if I'm ready."

Felix looked at her for a long moment. Under the green light of the nearest lamp, their face was hard to read — three years of selling harmless charms will teach a person to make their face unreadable — but their voice, when it came, was the voice Sefa had learned to trust on the drowned road, low and even and honest.

"When the ninth question comes," Felix said, "and it will be the worst one — it always is, Maren said so — remember that the truth doesn't have to be complete. It just has to be true."

"That's either very wise or very lawyerly."

"Both. I was almost disbarred from two temples." And there — the ghost of the smile that made Felix Marchetti, last of the oath-bound swordspeakers, seem briefly like a person and not a weapon wearing a person's coat.

Then the gate noticed them.

* * *

It happened the way weather happens — all at once, and everywhere. The nine rings of script stopped turning. The hum dropped to silence so complete that Sefa heard her own heartbeat stumble. And from somewhere inside the mountain, deeper than any mechanism, came a voice.

Not a sound. Sefa had spent her life translating the sea's noises into meaning, and she knew instinctively that this was not noise. This was language arriving fully formed, bypassing the ear entirely, pressing itself against the inside of her skull like a hand pressed to glass.

**WHO SEEKS THE COURT BENEATH?**

The first question. Maren had warned them it would come like this — not asked, but *installed*. And she'd warned them of the rule that mattered more than any other: the question must be answered aloud, by the speaker, and the answer must be true.

Sefa lifted her chin. Beside her, unseen, she felt the crew's gifts — she'd been holding them under for an hour now, a gentle downward pressure like keeping beach balls submerged in a bath — and she felt them strain toward the surface as the gate's attention swept over all of them, broad and slow, like light through water.

"We seek the court beneath," she said. Her own voice sounded thin and far away after the gate's voice. "As guests. Under guest-right, by the old rites."

The words hung in the air. Then, one by one, the outermost ring of script began to turn again, faster than before.

**WHY DO YOU COME?**

"For trade," Sefa said, and it was true — Odile had insisted on this, had spent an entire evening crafting a cover story built entirely out of true things. Smugglers seeking the drowned court's salt-glass and bottled light, goods worth a fortune on the surface market, offered honestly under guest-right. Every word verifiable. Every word a wall to hide behind.

Second ring turning. Third ring.

**WHAT DO YOU CARRY?**

Here Sefa felt the first real strain. Because the truthful answer was *we carry weapons, stolen protocols, forged papers, a bonded child the court already owns half of,* and the survivable answer was also, technically, true: "Tools of our trade. Goods for exchange. Ourselves."

The gate paused. Sefa felt it pause — felt its attention contract around her like a fist closing around a fish, testing, squeezing. She held her gift down. Held Felix's oath-voice down. Held Nia's bond down, that faint tidal pull the girl carried like a second heartbeat, the thing that had gotten her sold in the first place —

Nia gasped.

It was small. Barely audible. But in the gate's silence it might as well have been a scream, and Sefa felt the girl's bond surge upward against her hold like a hooked thing, drawn by the gate's attention, wanting — *wanting* — to answer.

Sefa clamped down. Sweat broke cold across her spine. Somewhere behind her, Felix shifted their weight forward half an inch, and Odile's hand found Nia's shoulder and stayed there.

**WHO SPEAKS FOR YOU?**

"Sefa of the Vhalen harbor," she said, steadier than she felt. "Tide-reader. Speaker for this crew."

Fourth ring. Fifth. The lamps along the stairway were brightening, one flight at a time, and Sefa realized with distant horror that the mountain was lighting its own way for them — welcoming them, or reeling them in, and the difference mattered less with each passing minute.

**HAVE YOU COME TO TAKE?**

"No." True. They'd come to take Nia back, but *take* implied theft, and Nia was theirs by every law except the ones written here. "What is ours we carry out. Nothing more."

**HAVE YOU COME TO GIVE?**

This one stopped her.

Because the honest answer was yes — wasn't it? Hadn't they all, in some private accounting, already priced themselves against Nia's freedom? Felix most of all; Sefa had watched them do the arithmetic on the drowned road, watched them decide, silently, that they were the coin. And the gate would taste that truth whether Sefa spoke it or not.

"Yes," Sefa said finally. "Though not what you think we've come to give."

The sixth ring turned. The seventh. The mountain's attention was heavier now, and Sefa could feel her hold on the others' gifts fraying at the edges — Brannick's salt-sense prickling up like gooseflesh, Odile's luck swirling, and Nia's bond beating against her restraint steady as a second pulse.

Three questions left. Maren had said the last three were the ones that unmade people. She hadn't said why. Now, feeling the gate lean closer, Sefa understood: the last three questions weren't tests. They were invitations. The mountain wanted to know them — truly know them — and it would use whatever they gave it.

**WHAT DO YOU FEAR?**

"Drowning," Sefa said, before she could stop herself. The word came out raw. She hadn't meant to say that. She'd prepared *nothing, we fear nothing,* knowing it would ring false, knowing she needed a truth that was small and human and deflecting — but the gate had reached into her and pulled the word up from the bottom like a fisherman hauling a net.

The eighth ring turned. One left. Sefa could feel the crew behind her holding their breath, could feel Felix's hand hovering near hers, not touching, anchoring.

**AND WHAT LIE WILL YOU TELL TO PASS THIS GATE?**

* * *

Later — much later, in the years after, when the story got told and retold in the reopened smuggler house until it wore smooth as sea glass — everyone agreed the ninth question was the trap. Everyone agreed the gate had been designed, centuries ago by whoever built it, to catch exactly the kind of people who would try to enter it: people with something to hide, offering truths like coins, hoping the weight of honesty would buy passage.

But in the moment, on the stairway before the turning rings, there was only silence, and the question hanging in the air like a blade, and Sefa's mind going white and empty.

Because the truthful answer was *none.* And the moment she said *none*, the gate would look. Really look. Past her held-down gifts, past the cover story, straight down into the crew's buried nature — and it would find a swordspeaker's oath-voice, a bonded child, a deserter hunter, a smuggler's impossible luck, and four other assorted crimes against the Purification Edict, and it would sing them all to the court beneath, and the drowned king would know exactly what had come knocking at his door.

And the lying answer — *this one, I'll tell you the lie I plan to tell* — was also a lie, because admitting a planned lie was itself a deception, and the gate would ring against it like a cracked bell, and the alarm would toll, and—

Rell moved.

Later, no one could quite reconstruct the decision. Not Odile, not Ivo, not even Rell herself, though she tried, afterward, in the flat careful voice of someone examining a scar. What the record shows is this: while Sefa stood frozen before the ninth question, Rell — lock-wright, twin, nineteen years old, the younger of the two by eleven minutes and the quieter by a lifetime — stepped forward out of the line, past Sefa's shoulder, and said, clearly:

"The lie is that we're traders."

Silence.

"We're not here for salt-glass," Rell went on, and her voice didn't shake, which Sefa would remember forever, because everything else about the next thirty seconds shook. "We're here for a person. We came to take her back."

The ninth ring stopped.

For one heartbeat, two, the world was perfectly still — the sea below, the rings above, Sefa's heart in her chest — and then the gate spoke, and it was not the gate anymore. It was the mountain. It was the alarm, and the alarm was a bell the size of a cathedral tolling once, deep and slow, and the sound came up through the stone under their feet and out through the still black water, rolling across the Sundered Sea in a visible ring, a ripple where no ripple should be.

Every lamp on the stairway flared green-white. The gate's nine rings spun wildly, blurring, and the carved script began to shed light like a molting bird sheds feathers, and beneath their feet Sefa felt the mountain *wake* — a vast slow shifting, like something enormous turning over in bed, deciding whether to be curious or hungry.

"Rell—" Ivo started forward.

"I know," Rell said. She still hadn't moved. She was staring at the gate with an expression Sefa couldn't read — defiance, maybe, or relief, or the particular blankness of someone who has just done the largest thing they will ever do and hasn't caught up to it yet. "I know what I did."

"You told it the truth," Sefa breathed. "That was the— you gave it the one thing we couldn't—"

"It asked what lie I'd tell." Rell's jaw tightened. "I don't lie well. Never have. Ask my brother." A ghost of the twins' old rhythm, there and gone. "So I told the truth about the lie. It's still a lie. It counts."

And the terrible, absurd thing — the thing Sefa would turn over for years — was that it *worked.*

The gate groaned. The rings slowed. The wild flare of light settled into something steadier, and the great black doors shuddered once, twice, and swung inward — not all the way, just a crack, a seam of pale light spilling out along the cliff face like dawn through a shut lid.

From within came a sound none of them had prepared for.

Music.

Distant, layered, many-voiced — the bottled souls of the court, singing their endless song in their bottles, and the sound drifted out through the crack in the gate and fell over the crew like warm water. It was beautiful. That was the horror of it. It was so beautiful that Sefa's grip on the others' gifts slipped entirely, and she felt every buried thing rise to the surface at once — Felix's oath-voice humming awake in their throat, Nia's bond reaching joyfully toward the music like a plant toward sun, Brannick's salt-sense flooding him with the taste of the deep water — and none of it mattered anymore, because the gate had opened, and the mountain already knew.

"Guest-right," Odile said sharply, into the beauty, into the music, her smuggler's voice cutting through like a knife through silk. "We claim guest-right. All of us. Say it."

They said it. All of them, even Rell, even Nia, whose voice came out wondering and young and afraid. The words of the old rite, passed mouth to mouth among smugglers for generations, half prayer and half contract: *guest-right, guest-right, bread and salt and safe conduct, harm neither nor suffer harm.*

The doors finished opening.

Beyond them: a corridor of black stone lit by those same oilless green flames, descending in a wide spiral, and at the bottom, impossibly far down, a glow like sunset underwater — the light of the court, the light of bottled souls, rising up through the dark like drowned stars. The music swelled. The air that breathed out of the mountain was warm and smelled of brine and something sweet, something like flowers that had never grown above the water.

Nia made a small sound and stepped forward before anyone could stop her, and this time Felix did stop her, gently, a hand on her shoulder, and the two of them stood there a moment — the last swordspeaker and the bonded child — silhouetted against the light from below.

"It's beautiful," Nia whispered. "Why is it beautiful?"

Nobody had an answer that wasn't a lie, and after the gate, nobody had the stomach for lies.

"Because it wants us to think so," Odile said at last. "Move. While it's still waking up."

They went down. All eight of them, into the spiral, into the warm dark, into the music. Sefa went last, as befitted the speaker, and at the threshold she stopped and looked back once at the motionless black sea and the tide-moon hanging over it, eight days from full.

The sea was still counting. So was she.

Behind her, deep in the mountain, something vast and ancient turned its attention toward the newcomers with the unhurried curiosity of a host who has been expecting them for a very long time — and Sefa felt, rising up through her gift like cold through wet boots, the unmistakable tidal pull of a mind older than the edict, older than the hunt, older perhaps than the sea itself.

The drowned king knew they were here.

And somewhere below, in a nursery full of singing bottles, a little girl's empty bed was about to be discovered.
