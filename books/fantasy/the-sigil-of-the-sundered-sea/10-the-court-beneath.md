## 10. The Court Beneath

The corridor opened like a throat.

That was Maren's word for it — *throat* — and she said it the way a woman says a thing she's swallowed before and never fully digested. We stood at the edge of it, the six of us, our boots on stone worn smooth by three hundred years of feet that had mostly been dragged.

Below us, light.

Not torchlight. Not the cold blue of Sefa's tide-lamps. Something warmer and worse: a gold that moved, that breathed, that seemed to be *looking back*. It came up the throat of the corridor in slow pulses, like the mountain was swallowing and swallowing and never finished.

"Bottled souls," Maren said. "I told you."

She had told us. She'd told us on the road, in her flat hunter's voice, the voice she used to keep her hands from shaking: *the court is lit by them.* I'd heard the words. Hearing words and standing inside their meaning are different countries.

"Count," Odile said quietly. "Don't stare. Count."

So we counted. That was Odile's way of keeping us human — give the horror a task and it can't crawl all the way into you. Sefa counted lights. Rell counted arches. Ivo counted guards, because Ivo counted everything and it made him feel rich. I counted my own heartbeats until they slowed from a sprint to a walk.

Nia counted nothing. Nia looked.

I put my hand on her shoulder and felt her lean into it, not pulling away, not hiding. Just looking at the gold with an expression I couldn't read yet and would spend the rest of this night failing to read.

"Stay behind me," I said.

"You always say that," she said. "It doesn't mean anything anymore. You know that, right? Everything down here already knows where everyone is."

She was right, which was the worst part. The mountain knew us. I could feel it the way you feel a hand hovering over the back of your neck — not touching, but *deciding whether to touch*. My sword spoke against my hip, low and constant, a sound only I could hear: the old name turning over in its sleep, restless, wanting out.

*Not yet,* I told it. *Maybe not ever.*

We went down.

* * *

The court beneath the mountain should not have been beautiful. Everything I'd been told — by Maren, by the broker's ledger, by three years of whispered rumor in Vhalen's gutters — promised a place of chains and screaming, of souls racked open like oysters.

It was beautiful instead, and that was so much worse.

The great hall rose in tiers of black stone veined with white, like waves caught mid-curl and turned to rock. And the light — the bottled souls hung from the vaulted ceiling in thousands of glass vessels, each one a soft gold ember, each one drifting slightly, the way a lantern drifts when someone carries it through a room. They moved in slow constellations. They rearranged themselves as we walked beneath them, and I understood, with a lurch, that they were watching. That they had been arranged to watch. That whoever hung them here wanted the light itself to be a kind of attendance — every soul in the court present, forever, for whatever happened next.

"They're singing," Sefa murmured beside me. Her eyes were half-closed, her gift reading the air the way a blind woman reads a page. "Low. Under hearing. It's not pain, Felix. It's—" She stopped. Swallowed. "It's patience. They're being patient."

"What does that even mean?"

"It means they think someone's coming for them." She opened her eyes. "They've thought that for a long time."

I didn't answer. There wasn't an answer that fit in my mouth.

Courtiers lined the hall — I use the word loosely, because what stood along the walls were not people. They were tall, gray-skinned shapes wrapped in robes of woven kelp and sailcloth, faces like carvings left too long in rainwater, features smoothed almost away. Their eyes were the only sharp thing about them: dark, wet, attentive. As we passed, they inclined their heads. Not in menace. In *courtesy*.

Maren walked like a woman revisiting a crime scene where she was both detective and accomplice. She kept her gaze fixed forward and her hands visible, palms out, the old protocol. *Unarmed. Delivered. Obedient.* I saw her fingers tremble anyway.

And then the tiers ended, and there he was.

* * *

He sat on no throne. That surprised me. He sat on a bench — a plain, salt-bleached bench, the kind dockworkers rest on between shifts — at the center of a circle of still water perhaps thirty paces wide. The water was perfectly flat, perfectly clear, and beneath its surface, far down, something glowed: a thread of pale light running away into darkness, thin as wire, taut as a harp string.

The seal. I knew it without being told. You know your own kind of wound when you see one.

The drowned king rose to meet us, and I want to describe him honestly, because everything after depends on describing him honestly.

He was drowning. Not metaphorically. Drowning was simply what he was, permanently, the way some men are tall or lame or old. Water moved through him constantly — beaded on his skin, ran from his hair, pooled and vanished beneath his feet. His face had once been handsome in the way of old coins; now the sea had worked at it for centuries, softening the jaw, dimming the eyes, leaving him a likeness of a likeness of a man. His robes were fine and ruined. His voice, when he spoke, was the kindest sound I'd heard since Brannick laughed, and I hated it immediately for that.

"Travelers," he said. "Guests. Forgive the dampness of my hospitality; the sea has poor manners and I have stopped correcting it."

Nobody answered. Odile stepped forward — of course Odile stepped forward, Odile would step in front of a cannon if it meant the rest of us got to stand behind her — and gave him a smuggler's bow, shallow and insolent and exactly correct.

"We're here for the girl," she said. "You have her. We've come to take her home."

"Odile Vasq," the king said, and Odile flinched at her whole name spoken in that gentle ruin of a voice. "Yes. I know you all. I have known you were coming since the broker's ink dried." His dark eyes moved down our line, unhurried, naming us without speaking: the tide-reader, the lock-wrights, the hunter who had delivered here before. Then me. He stopped on me the way a man stops on a familiar face in a foreign city.

"And the swordspeaker," he said softly. "How long has it been, I wonder, since one of you walked these halls awake?"

"I'm not awake," I said. "I'm angry. It looks similar from the outside."

Something moved across his drowned face. I chose to believe it was a smile.

"The child," Odile pressed. "Nia. Where is she?"

"Safe. Fed. Taught. Loved, in the manner available to me, which I grant is a strange manner." He spread his wet hands, an odd little gesture of helplessness from a king. "You may see her. I am not in the business of lying to guests; lies curdle quickly underwater. But first —" and here his voice did something terrible, something warm, something that reached into the chest and turned the key — "first, Felix Marchetti, I would speak with you. Alone, if your people permit it. I promise nothing except honesty, which is more than most kings offer and less than it sounds."

"He knows my name," I said to Odile. Out loud. Stupidly.

"He knows everyone's name," Odile said. "It's the whole problem with this place."

But she squeezed my arm, and Sefa caught my eye and shook her head slowly — *don't* — and Maren said, very quietly, "He won't harm you. Harm isn't how it works down here. It's never how it works down here. That's what nobody believes until it's too late."

I looked at Nia. Nia looked at me. She was not afraid. That frightened me more than anything else in the hall — the child standing in a court lit by stolen souls, unafraid, curious, *at home in her shoulders* in a way she had never been in Vhalen.

"Go on," she said. "I'll count arches with Rell."

* * *

He led me to the edge of the still water, to the bench itself, and asked me to sit, and I sat, because refusing courtesy in a place like this felt like striking a match in a powder store. The seal-light glowed beneath the surface between us, that single taut thread running down into the dark.

"Do you know what you're sitting above?" he asked.

"A seal," I said. "Keeping the Sundered Sea off the coast."

"Mm. And do you know what a seal is *made of*?"

"An oath." I kept my voice flat. "A swordspeaker's oath. Yours."

His head tilted, water sliding from his jaw. "They taught you true things in Vhalen, then, before they taught you fear. Yes. Mine. Spoken — let me count — three hundred and eleven years ago, by a younger man who stood roughly where you sit and hated roughly what you hate." He folded himself onto the bench beside me, close enough that I could smell him: brine and old paper and, underneath, faintly, impossibly, woodsmoke. A hearth-smell. A home-smell. I don't know what he intended by it. I don't think he intended anything anymore. I think the sea took his intentions centuries ago and left only the habits.

"Three hundred years," I said.

"Give or take a decade. Time runs strangely when you are the anchor of a thing. You don't move through it; it moves through you." He gestured at the hanging lights above, the drifting gold constellations. "Do you know why I take them? The gifted ones. The bonded ones."

"To hold the seal," I said. "To feed it. To keep yourself from—"

"From fraying." He said it plainly, without drama, and that plainness was the cruelest thing about him. "An oath is a rope, swordspeaker. You know this better than anyone alive. And a rope under constant strain wears. Mine wore. I was alone under it — there was no one to share the load, no one to renew it, no one who could even *visit*, because I had made myself the lock and locks do not receive guests. So I found other ropes. Other voices. Little oaths, bought and bonded and bound into the weave, each one taking a fraction of the strain." He looked up at the souls. "Each one taking a fraction of the *me* out of the work. Do you understand? I did not harvest them out of hunger. I harvested them out of arithmetic."

"That's what he said too," I said. "The broker. Arithmetic."

"Then the broker learned it from the same school I did." No shame crossed his face. That was the horror — not that he was unrepentant, but that repentance had simply eroded out of him, the way features erode, the way names erode. "Every soul in this court holds back the sea. Every soul in this court is why your coast still exists. Call me monster if it helps you sleep; monsters are useful furniture. But know that the alternative to my theft is Vhalen under forty fathoms, and let that sit in you a while."

I let it sit. It sat like a stone.

"Nia," I said.

"Ah." He closed his eyes briefly. "The child. Yes. Let us speak of the child, since she is why you came and why you will not leave without her, and we both know it, and pretense would insult us both."

"She's eight years old."

"She is nine in the spring. She told me. She is also, by every measure my stewards possess, the strongest new-gifted soul registered on this coast in sixty years." He opened his eyes and looked at me, and for one moment the drowning fell away and I saw the man underneath, the original, the young oath-taker, and he was tired in a way that made my bones ache in sympathy. "Her bond could hold this seal for another century. Alone. Freely given, even — though freely given is rare, and I confess I stopped waiting for it around year two hundred."

"So you buy children instead."

"So I *keep the sea off ten thousand homes*," he said, and his voice cracked at last, just barely, a hairline fracture in ancient porcelain, "by means I am not proud of, using strength I did not ask for, alone, in the dark, forever. Yes. Judge me. I have had three hundred years of practice being judged by people who live above the waterline."

* * *

Here is the thing I have never told anyone, and that I told him, sitting on that bench, because he had disarmed me with honesty the way a pickpocket disarms you with a smile.

I understood him.

Not the theft. Never the theft. But the arithmetic — the cold, clean, *merciful* arithmetic of one burden taken so many others needn't carry it. I had lived it for three years. Every charm I sold in the night market was a small lie that kept a larger truth safe. Every day I stayed silent about what I was was a day the hunters didn't come for the household, didn't come for Nia, didn't come for the people I loved. I had done my own arithmetic in the dark. I had simply done it smaller.

"You're going to offer me something now," I said. "I can feel it coming. It's been coming since the door."

"Yes," he said.

"And you'll dress it up as mercy."

"No," he said, and turned to face me fully, and the water ran off him and the seal-light lit him from below like a man standing on his own grave. "I will dress it as what it is: an exchange. You are a swordspeaker whose oath is intact — I can hear it sleeping in the blade at your hip, and it is *loud*, Felix Marchetti, it is louder than mine ever was. An oath like yours, woven fresh into this seal, would strengthen it beyond anything my frayed remnant manages. With your rope in the weave, I could release the bonded souls. All of them. The children. The brokers' stock. Three hundred years of my theft, undone in an afternoon." He paused. "All but one."

"Nia."

"All but none, if you prefer — her bond is young, barely written, easily unwoven. But her strength is wasted up there selling luck-knots to dockworkers. Down here it would hold back an ocean." His voice gentled further, and I finally understood what Maren had tried to warn me about. "You misunderstand me if you think I want her. I want *relief*. I want to stop. I have wanted to stop since before your grandmother was born. Give me your oath and I will give you every soul in this court, every one, carried up these stairs and set free in the morning sun — and the girl stays, fed and taught and loved in the manner available to me, holding back the sea that would otherwise drown everything either of you has ever cared about."

He spread his hands. The courteous, ancient, drowning king. The reasonable monster.

"One rope for hundreds," he said. "One willing voice for hundreds of stolen ones. Tell me the arithmetic is wrong. Tell me, and mean it, and I will admire you for it."

* * *

I sat there and I did the math. Of course I did the math. I am Felix Marchetti, last of the oath-bound swordspeakers, and math is the only language fear speaks fluently.

My oath. My name — the name I haven't spoken aloud in three years, the name that lives coiled in the blade because I paid it there, willingly, at nineteen, to buy a power I swore I'd never use. If I gave the oath, I'd give what remained of me. I'd end up — what? Like him? Like this? A softened likeness on a bench in the dark, harvesting strangers to keep my rope from fraying, telling myself it was arithmetic?

Or I could refuse. Refuse, and take Nia home, and leave the seal to its slow unraveling, and wait for the coast to learn what the Sundered Sea does when no one holds it.

Or —

There was a third shape forming, and I didn't look at it directly, because I already knew what it was. I'd known since the road under the cliffs, since the night I told Nia what an oath costs. Some prices you pay so no one else has to. That was the whole creed of my life. That was the whole *curse* of my life.

"One condition," I said.

The king waited.

"Nia sees her people tonight. Before anything. Before any bargain is struck, before any oath is weighed. She walks out of this hall and sits with Odile and eats real food and laughs at Ivo's counting, and I watch her do it. Then we talk terms."

Something flickered across the drowned face — surprise, maybe, or its fossil. "You negotiate for a visit."

"I negotiate for proof she's still a child," I said. "Everything else is arithmetic, and I've seen what your arithmetic grows into."

For a long moment the king said nothing. Above us the bottled souls drifted in their slow constellations, patient, singing under hearing, certain someone was coming for them. The seal pulsed beneath the water between our feet, steady as a heart that had forgotten how to stop.

"Granted," he said at last, and rose, water sheeting from him, and offered me his hand with the grave courtesy of a host ending an audience. I did not take it. He withdrew it without offense, and I understood that he hadn't expected me to take it, and that somewhere under three hundred years of drowning he respected me a little for it.

"Then come," he said. "Your people are waiting. And, swordspeaker—" he paused at the edge of the light, and his voice dropped to nearly nothing, nearly human, "—when you speak with the child tonight, listen to what she says about the sea. Not what you expect her to say. What she says."

Cold walked up my spine.

Because I had seen Nia's face in this hall, and I had chosen not to read it, and now the king was telling me it said something I didn't want to hear.

We walked back up the throat of the corridor together, the king and the thief and the last swordspeaker, toward the gold light and the drifting souls and the girl counting arches with Rell — and somewhere below us, taut as a harp string, the seal held, and waited to find out whose voice would be next.
