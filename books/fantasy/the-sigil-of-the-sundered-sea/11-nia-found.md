## 11. Nia, Found

The nursery smelled like warm bread.

That was the first thing wrong with it, though it took me three days to understand that it was wrong at all. Everything down here was soft in ways the harbor district never was: no fish rot, no tar, no Brannick's boots thudding through the floor at dawn. The floors of the soul-nursery were pale stone worn smooth as sea glass, and they were always a little warm under my bare feet, like something enormous and slow was breathing underneath them.

Which, I suppose, it was.

They had given me a room with a window.

Not a real window — I'd figured that out by the second day, pressing my palm against the glass and feeling no cold come through, seeing no weather change behind it, ever. It showed the sea. Always the sea, always the same hour of late gold light, waves rolling in long and patient toward a shore that never got closer. When I touched the glass, the water seemed to lean toward my hand.

"Is it alive?" I asked the woman who brought my meals. Her name was Ilsette, and her voice was the voice of someone who had been kind for so long that kindness had worn grooves in her, the way water wears stone.

"The window?" She set down the tray. There was honey cake on it. My favorite, which she could not have known, because I had never told anyone down here my favorite anything. But the seal had told them. That was the part I kept forgetting. The seal knew things about me I hadn't decided yet.

"The sea," I said. "In the window."

Ilsette smiled the way you smile at a child asking if the moon is lonely. "Everything down here is the seal's, little gull. The window included. Eat before it cools."

I ate. It was good. That was the second thing wrong with the place, or maybe the first, depending how you counted: everything was good. The bed was the right amount of soft. The light never flickered. When I cried the first night — because I did, quietly, into the pillow that smelled faintly of some flower I couldn't name — the walls made a sound like a mother humming, low and tuneless, until I stopped.

Nobody had ever hummed to me when I cried in Vhalen. In Vhalen, Odile put an extra blanket on your pallet and pretended not to notice your face was wet, and that was love, and you knew it because it cost her something. Down here everything was given freely because nothing down here cost anybody anything except me.

* * *

On the fourth day they let me walk to the gallery.

It was a long hall above the seal-chamber — Maren would have called it a maintenance gallery, Odile would have called it a catwalk, but the court called it the Listening Room, and that was the honest name, because that was all anyone did there: listen.

The seal was below us, visible through a floor of cloudy crystal, and it was not what I expected. I don't know what I expected. Fire, maybe. Chains. Something out of the stories Brannick told after too much rum, all teeth and drowning. What was actually down there was a pattern of light laid over black water like lace laid over a table — vast, geometric, patient, glowing a color I didn't have a word for until later, when I decided it was the color of holding on.

And beneath the light, the sea. Not the Sundered Sea as the priests screamed about it, the devourer, the godless flood. Just: the sea. Moving. Breathing. Pressing up against the underside of the world with a weight like a sleeping animal leaning against you in winter, and saying — not in words, not exactly, more in the way a house says things when you live in it long enough —

*here. here. here.*

I stood at the rail with both hands wrapped around it and listened until my knees went weak.

"You hear it." The drowned king stood at the far end of the gallery. I hadn't heard him arrive. He moved the way deep water moves, without any decision visible from the surface. Up close he was worse than in the throne room, because up close you could see he was drowning — not dying, drowning, continuously, his beard drifting in currents that had no source, his eyes full of a pressure that had been sitting on them for centuries. "Most souls feel only pressure. Fear. You hear it speaking."

"It says *here*," I said.

Something moved across his face that might have been grief, if grief could rust. "Yes," he said. "It has said that for three hundred years. To whoever listens."

"Why does it say that?"

He looked down through the crystal floor for a long time. "Because," he said finally, "someone promised to stay."

Then he left, and I stayed at the rail, and I want to be honest about what happened next, because everyone else in this story keeps lying to themselves and someone has to keep the true ledger.

I loved it.

I loved the sea's voice. I loved the warmth of the floors. I loved that nobody in the whole mountain wanted to sell me, use me, or look through me the way the witch-hunters looked through people on the docks, tallying what they could take. I loved that when I asked Ilsette questions she answered them, all of them, even the frightening ones, patiently, as if my curiosity were a guest she was honored to host.

The cage had a window, and the window faced the one thing in the world that had ever said *here* to me like it meant it.

That's how you build a trap that works. Not with locks. With listening.

* * *

On the sixth day, I dreamed the sea's dream.

I knew it was the seal's dream and not mine because in it I was very old, older than the mountain, and my hands were made of light-lace, and I was holding back something enormous and grieving, and the holding-on hurt in a way that had stopped being pain and become simply *true*, the way hunger becomes true if it lasts long enough. And in the dream I heard the promise — the original one, the words worn smooth as the nursery floors:

*I will stand between you and the drowning. This I speak, and this I am, until I am unspoken.*

I woke up crying again, but not the homesick cry from the first night. This was the other kind, the kind where your body knows something your mind hasn't caught up to.

Because I recognized the voice in the dream. Under three hundred years of erosion, under the fear and the rust, it was a young voice. A swordspeaker's voice. Someone who had once stood in a market square in some city that wasn't Vhalen and sworn themselves away the way Felix had, name into blade, self into oath — except this one had never had a household waiting with extra blankets and feigned blindness. This one had held the line alone until the line ate them.

And now the line was hungry again, and I was the meal they'd bought to feed it.

I sat up in the warm dark, hugging my knees, and made myself say it plainly, the way Odile taught me to count coin: plainly, twice, out loud.

"They bought me to hold the sea."

The walls hummed their lullaby-hum, gentle as ever.

"And part of me wants to let them," I said, quieter. Because that was the truest entry in the ledger, and I wasn't going to hide it even from the walls. Some small, starved part of me — the part that had slept in smuggler lofts and eaten whatever the tide brought in — had heard the sea say *here*, had felt the warm floors, and had thought: *finally, somewhere that wants me. Finally, a job worth doing. Finally, I matter enough to be the thing that holds.*

That's the horror nobody warns you about. Not that the cage is cruel. That the cage is *kind*, and you have to sit inside its kindness and decide, alone, whether being wanted is the same as being free — knowing that if you get it wrong, either the coast floods, or you spend your life as lace-light holding back a grief the size of an ocean, alone, forever, humming to yourself.

Felix would know what to do. Felix always knew what to do, even when doing it broke them.

But Felix was up there somewhere in the gold-lit halls, wearing the face they'd worn when they chose not to read mine, and I was down here, six days fed and warmed and sung to, and the tide-moon — I'd seen it through the false window, swollen and white — was coming, and with it a rite, and with the rite a choice that everyone else seemed to think had already been made for me.

I pressed my hand flat against the wall.

"I haven't decided," I told the seal, through sixty feet of stone. "Do you hear me? Whatever they told you. Whatever the broker wrote in his ledger. I haven't decided."

And the sea, vast and patient and drowning-slow, leaned its whole impossible weight against the underside of the world and answered — not *here*, this time, but something new, something that sounded almost like a question, and it frightened me more than any hunter's salt-iron mask ever had.

Somewhere above me, boots were moving through the gold-lit corridors. I didn't know whose. I only knew that the mountain had begun to wake, and that whatever came next, I would have to meet it standing — and mean whichever word I chose.

*Here,* said the sea.

Or, said the sea, and for the first time in three hundred years, the seal itself seemed to lean in and wait for my answer:

*— somewhere else?*
