## 12. Nineteen Becomes Nine

Odile had bribed men before. She'd bribed harbormasters, tide-clerks, a customs officer with a wooden leg and a taste for Vhaleni brandy, once an entire watch-house on the eve of a raid. Bribery was not corruption to her; it was plumbing. You found the joint where the pressure leaked and you paid it to keep holding. She had never once thought of herself as good at much, but she had always believed she was good at that — at knowing what a person's leak looked like.

She had been wrong about the steward of the court beneath the mountain.

His name was Corvane, and he received them in a side chamber off the great stair, a room designed, Odile suspected, to make visitors feel like they were being stored rather than hosted. Bottled light hung from the ceiling in iron cages, each globe pulsing faintly, and Odile made herself not look at them too long because she had begun, in the last two days, to understand what the globes were, and looking at them felt like reading someone's letters over their shoulder while they wept.

Corvane sat behind a desk of black coral. He was thin in the way of eels, all smooth movement under stillness, and he wore the court's livery — grey and pearl — as if it were a skin he'd grown into rather than clothes he'd chosen. When Sefa laid the purse on his desk, he did not touch it. He looked at it the way a man looks at weather.

"Three hundred crowns," Sefa said, "and a standing arrangement besides. The house in Vhalen moves goods for the court. Salt-iron, documents, whatever crosses your desk that shouldn't cross anyone else's."

Odile watched the steward's face and saw nothing move in it at all. That was her first mistake, believing there would be something to see. Men who could be bought showed you the price in their eyes before their mouths caught up. Corvane's eyes were like the globes overhead: lit from within by something that wasn't him.

"You misunderstand the economy of this place," Corvane said. His voice was soft, well-mannered, terrible. "You believe the court trades in coin because you have only ever traded in coin. The court trades in *time*. Every soul in this mountain is time. Time against the sea. Coin is what we give people like you so that you may continue to believe you live in a world where everything can be purchased." He folded his hands. "I cannot sell you what does not belong to me. And I do not own the girl. The seal owns the girl. I merely keep the ledger."

"Then tell us who keeps the seal," Odile said.

"The king keeps the seal." A small smile, the kind that has teeth behind it but hasn't decided whether to show them. "And the king does not negotiate. He receives guests. You are guests. Guests are welcome until they are not."

Sefa, beside her, had gone very still in the way that meant the tide-reader was reading something, though there was no water in the room except what hung in cages. Odile put her hand flat on the desk, between the purse and the steward's folded hands, and made her voice the one she used on dock crews when the cargo was shifting.

"Here is what I know," she said. "You have nineteen days until the tide-moon. You have a bonded child whose family has walked into your mountain unarmed, which no one has ever done, which means either we are fools or we are the kind of desperate that makes trouble. Fools you can ignore. Desperate makes noise. Noise reaches the hunters outside your gates, and the hunters reach the crown, and the crown sends soldiers, and soldiers do not care about your economy of time. They care about edicts. So consider that my coin, steward. Not the purse. The quiet."

For one moment — one — she saw it land. Saw the leak. Saw the place where the pressure might be held. Corvane's gaze flicked toward the door, toward the sound of the mountain breathing around them, and Odile thought: *there*. That's the joint. That's where he leaks. He is afraid of exactly what I am afraid of.

Then the moment closed, the way water closes over a dropped thing, and Corvane said, "The court thanks you for your counsel," and gestured, and two guards came through the door, and Odile understood that she had not been negotiating at all. She had been auditioning.

They were escorted out. Not thrown out — *escorted*, courteously, past the great stair where souls in bottles burned pale and steady, down corridors of wet stone that sang faintly when the tide turned somewhere below them. At the last turning, Corvane paused and said, almost kindly:

"For what it is worth, the girl is comfortable. Children bond easily here. They forget faster than adults do. In nine days she will not remember wanting to leave."

And then the corridor swallowed him, and Odile stood in the dark with Sefa and understood that she had just paid three hundred crowns to learn the exact shape of the thing she was fighting, and that the thing was worse than greed. It was patience.

* * *

It got worse in the counting-house.

They had gone back to the guest-cells the court kept for petitioners — damp rooms, honest beds, a courtesy that felt like being kept fresh, like fish on ice — and it was there, six hours after the failed audience, that the summons came. Not for Odile. For Ivo.

A runner in grey livery, a boy of perhaps fourteen with the flat eyes of someone raised inside a mountain, brought a card of black coral with white script on it. Odile read it twice. It requested the presence of *the lock-wright known as Ivo* at the counting-house, to assist in *an administrative matter regarding bonds and sureties*. It was signed by Corvane. It was, Odile realized with a cold sliding feeling in her stomach, worded exactly like a work order. Like a job. Like something you'd send a craftsman.

"They want his hands," Rell said. She was sitting on the edge of the bed, and her voice had gone strange, very level, the levelness of someone holding a door shut from the inside. "Lock-wrights open things. They've seen us. They know what we are. They want him where they can hold him."

"We don't answer it," Maren said from the doorway. She had her arms crossed and her hunter's mask pushed up on her head like a pair of spectacles, and she looked, Odile thought, like a woman arguing with her own reflection. "We don't answer a summons in a place like this. Answering is how they take you."

"And not answering?" Felix asked. Felix was by the window, looking out at the inner dark of the mountain, at the slow green glow of the flooded deeps far below, and their voice was quiet in the way it went quiet before violence or truth, and Odile had learned in three years to hear the difference. This was truth. "Not answering tells them we know what the summons is. Right now they think we're fools. Fools get ignored. Desperate gets managed. But *aware*—" Felix turned. "Aware gets harvested early."

The argument went around the cell three times, four, five. Maren wanted flight — there were maintenance ways, she said, gate-rites she knew, they could be out of the mountain by morning and come back armed and many. Sefa wanted patience — the tide-moon was the deadline, and deadlines cut both ways; the court could not renew the seal without its anchor, and its anchor was a child, and children were unpredictable even caged. Rell said nothing at all, which frightened Odile more than any argument, because Rell without words was Rell doing arithmetic.

And then Ivo stood up, and said, "I'll go."

"No," Rell said. One word. The first one she'd spent in an hour.

"I'm the one they asked for." Ivo's voice was the twin-voice, calm and practical, the voice that had talked Rell down from a collapsing scaffold in the Tanneries when they were sixteen. "If we run, they hunt us through every corridor Maren knows and half the ones she doesn't. If we stay, we're guests, and guests get waited on. If I go and sit in their counting-house and look at their ledgers—" he smiled, the twin-smile, quick and crooked, "—I'll learn where the seal-chamber actually is. Better than Maren's memory of protocols. From the inside."

"That's not a plan," Maren said. "That's volunteering."

"It's both," Ivo said. "Most good plans are."

Odile should have stopped him. She knew that later, in the way you know a wave is going to break before it breaks — the pull under your feet, the sea drawing back off the sand. She was the oldest one in the room. She was the one who'd recruited him. She was the one who'd told them all, back in Vhalen, in the smoke of burning charm-stock, *no one goes alone into anything anymore.* And now the boy she'd gathered out of wreckage was volunteering to walk alone into the one place in the world built to keep people, gently, forever, and she let him go because his logic was better than her fear, and that — she would carry this longer than anything else — that was the mistake. Not courage. Arithmetic. She let arithmetic decide, because arithmetic was easier to argue with than love.

He kissed Rell on the forehead at the door. Neither of them said goodbye. Twins don't, Odile understood. Goodbyes are for endings, and twins refuse to admit anything ends.

* * *

He didn't come back.

Hours passed. The mountain turned its tides. Odile counted doors in the corridor by walking it, forty paces down, forty back, the way she'd paced the deck of a ship becalmed once for eleven days. Sefa read the water in the walls and reported, each hour, that the tide was wrong — running inward, toward the deeps, when it should have slackened. Something below was drinking.

At the ninth hour, the runner came back. Not with Ivo. With a document.

Black coral again, white script, and this one Odile read standing up, and then read again, and then held very carefully, because her hands wanted to do something with it that would not help anyone.

*In consideration of services rendered and to be rendered, the lock-wright known as Ivo is hereby entered into surety against the debts outstanding of the petitioner household. Surety shall be discharged upon renewal of the seal at the coming tide-moon.*

Surety. Bonded. Collateral. The words were bureaucratic, clean, almost gentle, and underneath them was the same machinery that had taken Nia — a broker's ledger, a signature, a soul entered into a column. They hadn't punished Ivo for anything. They hadn't even accused him. They had simply *filed* him, the way you file a deed, the way you file a death. Somewhere in the counting-house there was now a line with his name on it, and lines in that ledger did not get crossed out. Lines in that ledger got renewed.

Rell took the document from Odile's hands and read it and did not cry and did not speak and did not move for a full minute, and then she folded it along its creases, precise as a lock-wright folding a template, and put it inside her coat, over her heart, and said, "Nine days."

Because that was the other thing. The document said *tide-moon*, and Sefa, reading the water in the walls, went white and said the words none of them wanted: the tide-moon had moved. Or their count had been wrong — it didn't matter which; the result was the same. What they had counted as nineteen days was nine. The deadline had halved while they sat in a damp cell being courteous to their jailers. Three days lost to the failed audience, three days lost to waiting, three days lost to a mountain that measured time in tides instead of mornings. Nineteen had become nine the way a wound becomes a scar: slowly, and then all at once.

Maren said, "We should have run when I said."

Felix said, "No. Now we know where the counting-house is."

And Odile stood in the middle of the cell with the whole crew looking at her — Felix, Sefa, Maren, and Rell, Rell most of all, Rell with her brother filed away in a ledger over her heart — and she understood that the looking itself was the message. Nobody had appointed her. There was no vote pending. But they were looking, and in a crew, looking is a ballot, and she felt it land on her shoulders like cargo.

She had never wanted this. She wanted to say that first, even if only to herself. She had never wanted a war. She had wanted a house above a harbor, a ledger that balanced, a family that came home. She had spent thirty years learning exactly one skill: keeping people fed and uncaught in a city that ate both. Smuggling was not bravery. Smuggling was bookkeeping with the serial numbers filed off. She had built the household in Vhalen the way you build a seawall — not to win anything, just to stand between the water and the people, one more season, one more season, one more season.

But the water had come inland. It had come inland wearing masks of salt-iron, and then it had come inland wearing courtesy and black coral, and it had taken Brannick, and Nia, and now Ivo, and the seawall was standing in the middle of the sea asking it politely to stop.

So she stopped asking.

"Here's how it is," Odile said, and her voice came out lower than she meant it to, steadier, the voice she'd heard once from her mother in a flood year, hauling salvage out of a drowned house with her sleeves rolled. "We stop being guests. Guests wait. We're done waiting. Nine days. Here's the count.

"Three days to find the seal-chamber — Ivo will find a way to get word, or Rell will find a way to get to him, and either way we map it. Two days to move everyone into position without the mountain noticing, and yes, Maren, that means using your protocols, all of them, the ones you hate yourself for knowing. Two days for Felix to get to Nia, because the seal renews at the tide-moon and if that happens with her bonded and alone, we lose her the way the king lost himself — slowly, comfortably, forever. And the last two days—"

She stopped. Because the last two days had no plan yet, and she refused to lie to them, and lying to this crew would cost her more than any hunter ever could.

"The last two days," she said, "we make our own luck. All of us. Together. I don't know how yet. But I know this: the court thinks we're a household of petitioners. It thinks families beg. It has forgotten — the king has forgotten, three hundred years underwater has made him forget — that a family is not people who share a roof. A family is people who share a debt and refuse to let one member pay it alone. That's what we are. That's what we've been since the night market. And there is no ledger in this mountain with a column for that."

Sefa nodded slowly. Maren uncrossed her arms. Felix looked at her with something Odile couldn't name and didn't try to.

Rell spoke last. She unfolded the document, smoothed it flat on the table, and set her palm on her brother's name.

"Nine days," she said. "Then we break the ledger."

That night the mountain sang in its bones, the tide running inward, drinking, and Odile lay awake in the damp cell listening to it and doing the only command work left to her: she memorized the crew. Felix's silence before truth. Sefa's stillness before danger. Maren's guilt worn like armor plate. Rell's arithmetic. And somewhere below, in a counting-house full of bottled light, a boy with crooked smile and careful hands, filing himself into their enemy's books on purpose, buying them a map with the only currency he had.

She had never wanted this war. But wars don't ask.

And in nine days, the tide-moon would rise over the Sundered Sea, and the seal would call for its renewal, and the drowned king would expect a child's soul to pour into the old machinery like oil into a lamp.

He expected a lamp.

He was about to learn he'd lit a fuse.
