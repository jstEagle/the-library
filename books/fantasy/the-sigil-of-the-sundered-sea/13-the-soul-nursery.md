## 13. The Soul-Nursery

**FELIX**

The nursery smelled of salt and honey.

That was the wrongness of it, Felix thought as they came down the last of the spiral stairs behind Maren — the wrongness that made the skin crawl worse than any dungeon stench would have. The soul-nursery beneath the mountain did not smell like a prison. It smelled like a crèche. Like warm milk and clean linen and the sea at low tide, the way it smells to children who don't yet know what drowning is.

The corridor opened into a vaulted chamber the size of a chapel, and every inch of it glowed.

Bottles lined the walls from floor to ceiling — not glass, something older and softer, like blown moonlight — and in each one hung a small light that moved. Not flickered. *Moved*, the way a sleeper turns over. Some of the lights drifted toward the bottles' surfaces when footsteps passed, pressing close, like moths or like hands against windows. Felix had seen bottled souls before, once, in a broker's cellar in Vhalen: they had been dim, frantic, guttering things. These were not. These were tended. These were *kept*.

"Gods," Maren breathed beside them. Her voice was flat, hunter-flat, but her hand on the wall was shaking. "I delivered four crates through this door. I never—" She stopped. Started again. "I never saw past the antechamber. They told us the vessels were insensate. That there was nothing in them to suffer."

"And you believed that?"

"I *needed* to believe that." She said it without flinching, which was, Felix was learning, how Maren did everything now. Guilt worn openly, like a badge turned inside out. "Come on. The bonded children are kept in the inner ring. The protocol puts them nearest the seal-wall."

"Because they're strongest."

"Because they're strongest," Maren agreed, and hated it, and said it anyway.

There were wardens — two of them, robed in grey-green, faces hidden behind masks of pale coral that should have been beautiful and were instead obscene, like skulls grown rather than assembled. Maren handled them the way she'd promised she could: with a stolen sign-countersign murmured in the court's old liturgical cadence, one palm raised, three fingers folded. *Maintenance of the vessels. No audience required.* The wardens inclined their coral heads and let them pass, because the mountain had never imagined that its own protocols could be turned inside out by someone who had once served them. That was the thing about locks built by people who believed themselves eternal, Felix thought. They forgot that keys outlive hands.

They walked between the bottle-walls for what felt like a hundred years.

Felix kept their eyes forward. It was cowardice and they knew it, and they did it anyway, because if they looked — if they looked at even one of those small turning lights and thought about what each one had been, a dockworker's daughter, a fisherman's son, a child who'd once sold luck-knots in a night market and been very careful about it — then their voice would rise in their throat, the true voice, the oath-voice, and they would shatter every bottle in this room and damn whatever consequences came after. Three years of hiding. Three years of being small on purpose. And here was a whole cathedral of smallness, harvested and shelved.

Nia was in the inner ring.

She sat on a window-seat — an actual window-seat, cushioned, overlooking a wall of water held back by nothing visible — braiding and unbraiding a length of blue cord. She looked healthy. She looked rested. She looked, and this was the part that stopped Felix's heart mid-beat, *content*, and when she glanced up and saw them her face went through joy and terror and guilt so fast it was like watching weather cross a valley.

"You came," she whispered. Then, immediately, anguished: "You *shouldn't have come.*"

"Nia—"

"The tide-moon is in nine days." She stood up, clutching the blue cord. Behind her, through the impossible window, the Sundered Sea pressed against the seal-wall like a living thing, vast and patient and lit from below by its own drowned light. "You shouldn't be here. If they find you here they'll bond you too, and then there'll be *two* of us and it won't help anyone, it doesn't work like that—"

"Nia." Felix crossed the distance and took her shoulders. She was so light. She had always been too light; Odile used to say the sea got into some children early and hollowed them out a little, making room. "We're taking you home."

And Nia — clever, watchful Nia, who had watched Felix too closely across a night market and known exactly what they were — shook her head.

"No," she said, small-voiced and absolutely certain. "I can't go. Don't you understand? If I leave, the seal weakens. He told me. He showed me." Her eyes went to the window, to the mountain of dark water straining at its invisible leash. "Every bonded soul here is a stitch holding it shut. I'm one of the stitches. You pull me out and the whole seam starts to go, and then—" Her voice cracked, and steadied, which was somehow worse. "Then Vhalen floods. The harbor district first. Odile's house. The market where you sell your knots. Everyone we know, under the water."

* * *

**MAREN**

Maren had heard a great many children cry in her years as a hunter. She had been the cause of more of that crying than she would ever be able to burn off, no matter how many doors she held open or protocols she stole. But she had never heard anything quite like this: a child arguing *for* her own captivity, calmly, with evidence, while her rescuers stood helpless in a room full of glowing bottles.

It was the calm that frightened her. Hunters were taught to break resistance, and Maren knew every technique, and none of them applied here, because Nia wasn't resisting out of fear or confusion or loyalty to her captors. She was resisting because she had done the arithmetic herself, alone, in this soft-smelling room, and the arithmetic was correct.

"The king showed you?" Maren asked. She kept her voice level, the debriefing voice, the one that got statements instead of tears. "Showed you what happens if a vessel is removed?"

"He didn't have to show me much." Nia's chin lifted. "I can feel it. That's what the bonding does — that's what nobody outside understands. It's not a chain. It's a *thread*. I'm tied into the seal and I can feel the whole weight of the sea leaning on it, all the time, like—" she searched, found it, "—like carrying a bucket of water and you can't ever set it down. When I sleep, my half of the carrying keeps going. And I know, I just *know*, that if I set it down, everyone else holding has to carry more, and some of them are so tired already. Look at them." She gestured at the bottle-walls, and her face was fierce with a tenderness that had no business existing in this place. "Some of them are almost out of light. If I add mine back onto theirs—"

"Nia." Felix crouched to her height. Maren watched the swordspeaker's face do something complicated — watched the oath-voice sit right there behind the teeth, wanting to promise everything, unable to promise anything. "We're going to get you out. All of you. That's why we're here. There are people above us and behind us working on it right now."

"How?"

"I can't tell you yet."

"Because you don't know yet." Nia wasn't cruel about it. She was nine years old and she said it the way a tide-reader reads a chart. "You came to get me and you haven't figured out the rest. That's okay. That's how Brannick does things too — he says start moving and trust your feet. But Felix, listen." She took Felix's hand in both of hers, and Maren saw the swordspeaker go absolutely still, the stillness of a person touching something live. "If you take me out of here tonight, and the seam starts going, and Vhalen drowns while you're still figuring out the rest — I won't survive that. Do you hear me? *I* won't survive knowing that. You wouldn't be saving me. You'd be drowning me in a different way, one that lasts forever."

The chamber was quiet except for the faint sound of the sea against the seal-wall, patient as breathing.

Maren thought: *this is what they do to them. This is the real harvest.* Not the light in the bottles. The guilt. They take a child and they make the child the jailer of everyone she loves, and then no lock is needed at all. She had helped build this machine for eleven years and she was only now, standing in it, seeing the shape of the whole engine.

"There's another way," Felix said quietly. "We're going to find it. Sefa's reading the seal architecture right now. We have nine days."

"You had nineteen," Nia said. "What happened?"

Nobody answered. Ivo's name sat unspoken in the air between them, and Nia read its absence the way she read everything.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh no. Who was it?"

* * *

**FELIX**

"Ivo," Felix said, because lying to Nia was a door that closed the moment you looked at it. "He tried to buy time with the steward. It cost three days and his freedom. Rell went in after him yesterday — traded herself for him. They're both bonded now."

Nia's eyes filled, and she let them, and she didn't wipe them away, and Felix understood suddenly that crying was a luxury she'd rationed carefully in this place and was spending deliberately, now, in front of people who loved her. "Rell and Ivo," she whispered. "They'll feel it too. The thread. They'll feel the sea leaning on them tonight, both of them, and they won't know what it is and they'll be scared."

"They've got each other."

"That helps." She nodded slowly. "That actually helps. Being bonded alone is—" she stopped, chose a different word than the one she'd been about to say. "Being bonded alone is the worst part."

Felix knelt there on the warm stone floor of a room full of stolen souls and understood that they had come to rescue a prisoner and found a warden instead — a warden of her own conscience, appointed by a king who hadn't needed to lift a finger. Every argument Felix had prepared on the road dissolved. *You'll be free. You'll come home. Odile's waiting.* All of it true and all of it insufficient, because the child had asked the only question that mattered and answered it honestly: *and then who holds the sea?*

"I can't promise you the answer yet," Felix said. "I want to. I want to stand here and swear to you that we'll walk out of this mountain with every bottle on these walls and every thread untied and the sea held by something better, and I can't, because I don't know it's true. Sefa thinks — Sefa has a theory. About what the seal actually needs. But theories aren't promises, and you deserve better than a promise I can't keep."

Nia studied them for a long moment. Somewhere in the bottle-walls, a small light drifted close to its surface, listening.

"What's the theory?" she asked.

Felix glanced at Maren. Maren gave the smallest nod: *your call.*

"Sefa thinks the seal doesn't need souls at all," Felix said. "Sefa thinks it needs oaths. Freely given ones, renewed — and that the king forgot that three hundred years ago, and started substituting other people's lives for his own word, and called it necessity ever since. If Sefa's right, then every bonded soul in this room is a substitute. A counterfeit coin spent so long the counterfeiter believes it's real."

"And if Sefa's wrong?"

"Then we're back to the arithmetic," Felix said. "And I pay it. Whatever it costs. That's not a promise, Nia, that's just — that's the shape of me. You've known that since the night market."

Something moved through the child's face then — not hope exactly, hope was too big a word for what nine days and a theory could hold. More like a handhold found on a long climb. She looked down at the blue cord in her fingers, and began, absently, to braid it again.

"Odile sent you?" she asked.

"Odile is holding the door."

"Brannick?"

"Brannick is coming. I don't know how yet. But he broke a rule for you once, and he doesn't break rules twice for nothing."

That earned the ghost of a smile. Then it faded, and Nia looked past them both, at the window, at the sea, and her voice dropped to nearly nothing — the smallest voice Felix had ever heard carry the largest weight.

"Nine days," she said. "But Felix — the renewal rite isn't at the end of the nine days. It starts at the next high tide. Tomorrow night. The king comes to the nursery himself, and he takes the oldest threads and reweaves them into the seal, and the vessels he uses for the reweaving—" her fingers tightened on the cord until the knuckles went white, "—they don't come back afterward. They go into the wall. Permanently. He chooses them tomorrow, and he always chooses the brightest, and I'm the brightest."

* * *

**MAREN**

Maren had performed gate-rites. She had signed delivery manifests for human beings. She had stood in an antechamber while a crate sang and told herself stories about resonance and cargo-shift. None of it had prepared her for the sound of a nine-year-old saying *he always chooses the brightest* in the tone of a soldier reporting enemy movements, because that was what Nia was doing, Maren realized. Reporting. The child had made herself a scout inside the enemy's house, gathering intelligence with no one to deliver it to, for months, and now her officers had finally arrived and she was briefing them.

"We move her tonight," Maren said. It came out harder than she intended. "Protocol or no protocol. There are maintenance corridors behind the east bottle-wall — I mapped them from the manifests. Two hours to the sea-gate. We take her now and we argue about seals later."

"And the thread?" Nia asked, not moving. "You cut it tonight, rough, in the dark, with no rite and no replacement — do you know what a rough-cut feels like? The king told me that too. He tells me everything. That's how he keeps me. He's very honest, the king. It's the most horrible thing about him." She looked up at Maren, and her eyes were wet and steady. "A rough-cut feels like drowning from the inside out, and the seal takes the shock, and the shock goes somewhere. Into the other stitches. Into the tired ones. How many bottles on that wall are almost empty, hunter? You counted them coming in. I watched you count."

Maren had counted. Forty-one vessels in the outer ring running below a quarter-light. She said nothing, and Nia nodded as if she'd spoken.

"So we do it properly or not at all," the child said. "That's my condition. You want to take me out of here? Fine. But you do it the right way — you find the real answer, the oath-answer, Sefa's answer, whatever it is — and until then I stay and I hold my thread and I keep the tired ones from carrying more. That's not me choosing the cage." Her voice finally broke, all the way through, and she let it. "That's me choosing *you*. All of you. The whole coast of you. I get to choose something in here, and that's what I choose."

Felix made a sound Maren had never heard from a swordspeaker — small, involuntary, like a rib cracking.

Maren stepped forward and did the only thing left in her inventory. She knelt, took Nia's hands, and spoke in the flat honest cadence that had gotten her this far into the mountain and might, someday, get her somewhere further into forgiveness.

"Tomorrow night, then. Before the rite. We come back with the whole crew and we do it properly, or we die trying, and either way you will not stand in front of that king alone." She squeezed the small cold hands. "That's a hunter's oath, Nia. Broken ones like me only get to make a few. So believe it."

Nia searched her face — reading her, the way she read everything, the way she'd once read Felix across a market stall — and whatever she found there, she accepted it. She leaned forward and pressed her forehead to Maren's, briefly, the way the twins parted.

"Go," she whispered. "Before the wardens change shift. And Felix—" she caught the swordspeaker's sleeve as they turned. "When you see Odile. Tell her the blue cord means I'm counting days. She'll know."

They went, down the maintenance corridors, past the bottle-walls, and Maren did not look at the dim lights either, and neither did Felix, and behind them the sea leaned on the seal and waited, patient as arithmetic, nine days short of everything.

Above them, muffled through a hundred feet of mountain, a bell began to toll.

Not the alarm. Something older. Maren froze on the stairs, blood going to ice, because she knew that bell — she had rung its counterpart at three other courts in her hunting years, and it meant only one thing.

The king had chosen his vessels for the reweaving.

Early.
