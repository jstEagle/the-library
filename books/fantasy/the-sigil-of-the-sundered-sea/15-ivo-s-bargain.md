## 15. Ivo's Bargain

Rell had always known the arithmetic of twins. Before she could read, she knew it: one of us, two of us, half and half and whole. When the bread ran short in their ninth winter, Ivo ate less without being asked. When the lock-wright guildmaster in Kettering said he'd take an apprentice but only one, they'd flipped a coin behind the tannery and Rell had lost on purpose, because Ivo wanted it more and she could live inside his wanting like a warm coat. Everything between them had always been a ledger with two entries, balanced to the copper.

So when she stood outside the steward's door in the drowned court — listening to Ivo breathe through the wall where they kept him, slow and drugged and bonded, his heartbeat audible to her across stone the way it had been audible across cribs — she did not do the math. She simply read the sum that was already there.

*One of us. Two of us. One goes in.*

The steward's chamber sat above the soul-nursery, which meant the walls themselves were faintly warm, which meant Rell had spent the last hour pressing her palm against them and hating herself for finding comfort in it. The corridor was lit by bottled souls in niches — the court called them *lamps*, Maren had told them, with a face like a woman confessing to a massacre — and each one hummed a note slightly off from true. Rell counted them the way she counted everything when her hands needed steadying: forty-one lamps, nine turns of the corridor, three guards at the steward's door who changed on the tide-bell and could be bought for exactly the purse Odile had failed to spend three days ago.

Bribery hadn't worked. Odile had tried gold, then threats, then the particular Vhaleni blend of both, and the steward — a gray, courteous creature with eyes like wet slate — had declined all currency except one. *The mountain does not trade in coin,* he'd said. *It trades in bond.* And then he'd taken Ivo as collateral for the crew's trespass, and the arithmetic had written itself in the dark ever since.

Rell knocked.

The door opened without a hand touching it. Of course it did. The steward stood inside amid shelves of small brass instruments she didn't recognize and ledgers whose pages turned themselves, and beyond him, through an archway hung with beads of green glass, Rell could see the nursery's glow — soft gold, the color of lamplight in a window you're walking home toward. They made the cages beautiful here. That was the worst thing about this place. Everything terrible was beautifully lit.

"Lock-wright," the steward said. "You've come to negotiate."

"I've come to conclude." She kept her voice level. Below it, she was aware of her own pulse doing something traitorous and fast. "You want a bond. You have my brother. I'm offering you a better deal."

"There is no better deal than a soul already in hand."

"There is if the one in hand is defective." She let that land. Let the steward's slate eyes narrow. "You've had him four days. Check your instruments. Ivo's gift is in his hands, not his blood — he can open anything, yes, but there's nothing in him that sings. Ask any lamp in this hallway. Ask the seal itself. A lock-wright's talent is a tool, not a voice. Yours harvest voices."

The steward was quiet. The ledger nearest him rustled its own pages, impatient, like a dog scenting rain. "And yours?"

Rell smiled. It cost her everything she had, and she had learned from Felix that the best lies are paid for in advance. "Mine sings. Ask me why I can pick a lock blindfolded and I'll tell you the truth: I hear tumblers. Not with my ears. With something older than ears. I've been hiding it since I was six because a witch-hunter in Kettering nearly took me for it." She stepped closer, into the green glass light, and let him see her hands shake — genuine, unperformed, perfect. "I am worth three of my brother to whatever you feed down there. And you know I'm telling the truth, because your instruments are humming right now. Aren't they?"

They were. She could see it — a row of thin silver needles on the steward's desk trembling in their vials, turning toward her like compass needles finding north. She'd bet the whole crew on that. Sefa had coached her for two days on how a tide-reader masks a gift, so Rell had done the reverse: worn it loud, worn it naked, let the mountain taste her from the moment she walked back into this corridor.

Fear arrived as bureaucracy; desire arrived as hunger. The steward was hungry. She could work with hungry.

"The exchange is not simple," the steward said slowly. "A bond, once registered, must be—"

"—reclaimed before the tide-moon or forfeited, yes, I've read your protocols. Maren performed them." She watched him flinch at the name. Good. "I know what I'm signing. That's more than Ivo got."

"And your crew?"

"My crew will grieve efficiently." The lie came out smooth as oiled tin. *They won't grieve at all,* she almost added. *They'll come for you.* But the steward's needles were still trembling toward her, and some part of the mountain might be listening through them, so she swallowed the promise and let it sit in her chest like a swallowed fishhook.

The steward considered her for a long moment. Somewhere below them, muffled by stone and green glass and the whole weight of the court's courtesy, Rell heard the sea. Not the sea above — the Sundered Sea pressed against the mountain's roots like a patient animal leaning on a fence. She heard it the way she heard tumblers: with something older than ears. And beneath the sea's long pressure she heard something else, thin and steady, a note held for centuries by something that was very tired.

She would think about that later. There wasn't time now.

"Terms," the steward said.

"No chains until the swap is done. I walk to my brother under my own feet. He walks out the same way. Then you can register me however you like."

"Agreed."

"And the crew's trespass debt dissolves with the exchange. All of them. Even the hunter."

The steward's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "The mountain admires thoroughness in its sacrifices."

"I'm not a sacrifice," Rell said. "I'm a payment. There's a difference, and your ledgers know it even if you don't."

* * *

They brought Ivo up through the nursery's side stair, and Rell understood immediately why the steward had agreed so fast, why the needles had trembled, why the whole transaction had gone smooth as a well-cut key: because the mountain had already tasted her, and the mountain was starving.

Ivo looked smaller than he should. Four days of bonding had gone into him like water into chalk — not draining him exactly, but *diluting* him, his edges softened, his quick clever hands lying open and idle in his lap as two attendants walked him forward. His eyes found her and focused slowly, and for one horrible heartbeat Rell saw no recognition there at all, and then it came, arriving like light through a closing shutter.

"Rell," he said. His voice was his voice but slower, a song played at the wrong speed. "You shouldn't be here."

"Neither should you." She crossed the distance. The attendants stepped back with the bored precision of people who had done this many times, and that bored precision frightened her more than any mask or blade had.

"You did the math," Ivo said. Not a question. His eyes were clearing as he spoke, sharpening, the dilution retreating from whatever part of him was most essentially himself — the part that had always been able to see straight through any lock, any lie, any sister. "Don't lie to me. I can always tell. You did the math."

"One of us," Rell said. Her throat closed around it. She made herself finish. "Two of us."

"Half and half," Ivo whispered, "and whole." Their oldest rhyme. He laughed, and it cracked in the middle, and the crack was the most himself he'd sounded in four days. "Terrible arithmetic, Rell. Terrible, terrible arithmetic."

"It balances."

"It doesn't." His hands came up — slow, still diluted, but his — and framed her face the way he had when they were children and one of them had a fever and the other kept watch. "Listen to me. Listen with the old thing, the thing older than ears." She did. Underneath his heartbeat she could feel it now — the bond, a thread of gray silk running from his chest down through the floor, down through the mountain, taut as a plumb line. "Whatever you're planning — and I know you're planning, you have the face you get when you're counting — don't balance it alone. That's the lesson of this whole rotten place. Everyone down here balanced alone. Look what it made."

"Ivo—"

"A kiss on the forehead," he said. "Like when we were small and one of us was scared. No goodbye. We never say goodbye. Goodbye is for people who aren't coming back, and I—" his voice caught, held, steadied, "—I'm coming back. That's not hope, Rell. That's arithmetic. You taught me the numbers myself."

She kissed his forehead. She tasted salt — the mountain's salt, the bond's salt, the sea's salt, all of it — and she felt the gray silk thread shift, sliding out of him, hovering, seeking, patient as a hook.

No goodbye.

The attendants took his arms. He went without resistance, walking backward a few steps so he could keep looking at her, and he was grinning — actually grinning, that gap-toothed grin from the bread-short winter, from the coin flip behind the tannery — and then the archway of green glass beads took him, and the beads chimed once, softly, like a door in a house where someone is expected home.

The thread snapped taut against her chest.

Rell gasped. It didn't hurt, exactly. It *registered*. Somewhere far below, an instrument wrote her name into a ledger whose pages turned themselves, and the mountain settled around her new weight the way a house settles around new furniture — accepting, adjusting, indifferent.

The steward appeared at her elbow, courteous as fog. "If you'll come with me, lock-wright. The registration is painless. Most find the nursery quite comfortable."

"I know," Rell said. "That's what frightens everyone."

She walked. What else was there? Behind her, somewhere above, the crew was waiting in the guest-quarters Odile had bullied out of the court's etiquette, and Rell had left a letter under Sefa's pillow because she was a coward in exactly one respect and had chosen it carefully: *Don't tell Ivo where I am until he's strong. Don't let Felix do anything noble. The seal needs an oath freely given — Sefa, check the architecture again, check it twice — and check whether a lock-wright counts as a voice.*

Because that was the thing. The thing she hadn't told the steward, hadn't put in the letter, hadn't told anyone, because telling it might make the mountain listen harder:

When the gray thread snapped into her chest, Rell had heard the seal.

Not the sea. The *seal*. The note beneath the note, the one the tired old thing had been holding for three hundred years. And a note, held that long, develops harmonics. Overtones. Places where a second voice could slip in and share the load — if the second voice knew the first note's true pitch.

Rell had perfect pitch. Rell had always had perfect pitch. It was why she heard tumblers, why locks sang to her, why she'd hidden her gift since she was six years old in Kettering while her brother took the blame for every strange silence in the house.

The seal was singing flat.

Three hundred years of holding one note alone, and it had drifted, the way any lone voice drifts, and the king drowning at the bottom of it had forgotten the original pitch entirely — or maybe he remembered and couldn't reach it anymore, arms too tired, too old, too afraid. But Rell could hear where the note *should* be. She could carry it in her head like a key carried in a pocket.

And a lock-wright, hearing where a mechanism wants to go, cannot leave it broken.

*One of us,* she thought, following the steward down into the warm gold light of the nursery, past the bottled souls humming their almost-true notes, toward the cage made comfortable. *Two of us. Half and half—*

The thread pulled. The mountain listened.

*—and whole.*

Far above, muffled by stone, she heard the tide-bell ring the change of watch, and under it, fainter, the sound of the crew's door opening — someone coming to look for her, footsteps she knew, running now.

Sefa had found the letter early after all.

Rell smiled, and let the mountain take her weight, and began to count.
