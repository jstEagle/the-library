## 17. The Tide-Moon

Maren had stood at this gate nine times before.

She had stood here in salt-iron and gray wool, with the protocols memorized on her tongue like a catechism, and she had watched other people's children go through it in crates that breathed. She had never once stood on the outside of the gate with no authority, no warrant, and a stolen liturgy in her head — only the memory of what she'd done with it.

The tide-moon was nearly full above the mountain's crown, swollen and green-white, close enough that you could see the scars on its face. The sea below the cliffs was answering it. Maren could feel the water pulling even through rock, a slow tidal groan underfoot, as if the whole coast were inhaling.

"The rite begins when the moon touches the peak," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "We have maybe an hour. Less."

Odile stood beside her at the lip of the sea-gate terrace, looking down at the approach road — one road, cut into the cliff face, switchbacking up from the drowned causeway. It was a beautiful piece of engineering and a butcher's dream: narrow enough for two abreast, walled on the seaward side, open to the sky. Anyone coming up would come in file. Anyone holding the top would hold everything.

"Say it again," Odile said. "The part about the wards."

"Gate-rites run on truth," Maren said. "The mountain listens for lies the way a spider listens to web-vibration. But there are counter-signs — hunter protocols, the ones we used to bring our own people through without tripping the alarm." She hated saying *we*. She said it anyway. "They're keyed to the gate-steward's cadence. I know the cadence. Sefa's masking should hold the crew beneath it, but if any hunter comes up that road wearing their full regalia, the protocols will read them as friendly and read us as—"

"Meat," Odile said.

"Guests who arrived without invitation," Maren corrected, because precision mattered, and then: "Meat. Yes."

Below them, far down the switchbacks, something caught the moonlight. Not water. Metal.

* * *

The hunters had tracked them. Of course they had. Maren had spent six years learning how witch-hunters moved, and the answer was: patiently, and by assuming everyone eventually went home. The crew hadn't gone home, but Rell's lie at the Gate of Nine Questions had tolled an alarm three days ago, and alarms traveled. There would have been a rider on the causeway road within hours. There would have been orders from Vhalen by dawn. The edict didn't stop at the shoreline; nothing stopped, not ever, not until someone made it stop.

"Twelve," Sefa said, flat on her belly at the terrace edge, reading the column below with her gift half-open. "Maybe fourteen. Salt-iron masks. Two carrying bond-cages on mules."

Bond-cages. Small ones. The size you'd use for a child, or for whatever the court's steward wanted brought back alive as leverage. Maren's stomach did something old and familiar, a clench she associated with paperwork done correctly.

"They'll reach the gate in twenty minutes," Sefa said.

"Then we don't fight them at the gate," Odile said. "We fight them on the stairs."

Maren turned to look at her. In the three weeks since the Tanneries, Odile had been the quartermaster of this disaster — food, routes, bribes, tempers — and she had been good at it in the unglamorous way of people who keep other people alive. Maren had never once seen her touch a weapon. She knew why. Everyone in the smuggler house knew why. Odile had buried two brothers behind a fish-market stall in the Harbor Riots and had sworn on their graves that she would never again be the one holding the blade when the arithmetic came due.

But Odile was looking down at the hunters now with an expression Maren recognized from the mirror, years ago, the day before her first delivery. It wasn't rage. It was bookkeeping.

"There are eight of us," Odile said. "Four can't fight. Felix is underground already, and every minute we spend is a minute closer to Nia becoming an anchor. So." She ticked it off on her fingers, and her hands didn't shake, and Maren thought: *she's been rehearsing this in her head for days.* "Ivo and Rell hold the terrace chokepoint — twins work best in a corridor, yes?"

Ivo, still raw from his three days in a bond-collar, cracked his knuckles. Rell just nodded, jaw set.

"Sefa reads the ward-lines and tells me where the ground is lying. Maren—" Odile looked at her, and there was no forgiveness in it, and no cruelty either, just the clean weight of use. "You're going to give me those protocols out loud, all of them, and then you're going to stand next to me and tell me exactly how these people kill gifted souls, so I know what I'm refusing to do to them."

"And you?" Maren asked.

Odile reached into her coat and drew out a short boarding-cutlass, smuggler's steel, ugly and honest. She held it the way you'd hold a tool you resented needing.

"I'm going to draw blood for my people," she said. "Finally. And I am going to hate it, and I'm going to do it anyway, and afterwards nobody is going to make me feel noble about it. Agreed?"

Nobody argued. The tide-moon touched the peak of the mountain, and below them the sea roared once against the cliffs like a struck bell, and the gates of the drowned court began to glow gold along their seams.

* * *

They came up the switchbacks in file, exactly as the architecture demanded, and the first four died before they understood they were in a battle.

Not killed — Maren kept count, and she would keep counting for the rest of her life — but *dropped*: Ivo's weighted lines took the lead pair's legs, Rell dropped a cut portcullis-chain across the third and fourth, and the column compressed onto itself like a fist closing. Hunters trained for witches, for running targets, for burning circles in market squares. They did not train for a corridor fight against people who knew the ground better than they did.

The fifth hunter cleared the bodies and came on fast, salt-iron mask reflecting the gate-light, and raised one gloved hand and spoke a word of registration — the binding-word, the one that froze a gifted soul mid-flight — and it broke over Sefa like water on stone, because Sefa had spent nineteen days learning to be nobody.

The hunter hesitated. That hesitation was the whole war.

Odile hit him from the blind side and put him down with the pommel, not the edge. Maren saw it. *Pommel first,* she thought. *She's still trying.*

Then the rest of them crested the stair together, seven masks in a wedge, and the hesitation ended.

What happened next lasted maybe ninety seconds and Maren would remember it in fragments forever: the gold light of the waking gate washing everything the color of drowning; Sefa screaming ward-coordinates; Ivo roaring as a hunter's salt-iron gauntlet closed on his collar and the old bruise of the bond-collar underneath; the bond-cages on their mules toppling and rolling, and one of them splitting open, and nothing inside it but packing straw — bait, a decoy, which meant somewhere in that column was the real cage, which meant—

Which meant the sixth hunter wasn't attacking at all. She'd peeled off in the chaos, low and quick, dragging the true bond-cage toward the gate, and the gate's seams were glowing wider, and inside the cage something small was curled up breathing too fast.

Maren moved without deciding to. Six years of protocols poured out of her body ahead of her mind. She hit the gate-threshold — past the ward-line, into the mountain's jurisdiction, the place she had sworn never to stand unarmed again — and spoke the steward's cadence, the stolen liturgy, loud and perfect, and the gate *believed* her. The wards read her as command. The gate-seams stuttered, dimmed, confused.

The hunter spun, saw the gray wool and the stance, and recognized her.

"Pemberton's ghost," the mask said — her old rank-name, the one they'd given her after her third delivery. "They told us you were dead."

"I was," Maren said, and took the cage, and the small thing inside it — a boy, seven, maybe, salt-eyed and terrified — grabbed her wrist through the bars.

Behind her, steel rang. She turned in time to see the last of it: two hunters down, one fleeing back down the switchbacks with Ivo's line singing after him, and one — the wedge-leader, big, methodical — locked blade-to-blade with Odile at the very lip of the terrace wall.

Odile was losing. Slowly, technically, with terrible competence — she'd learned enough in three weeks to not die immediately, and not one second more. The hunter pressed, and pressed, and Odile gave ground step by step until her heel found the low parapet and the drop beyond it, forty feet of basalt and tide-surge.

"Yield the cage," the hunter said. "And the souls you've stolen. And your own registration, and I swear you'll see Vhalen again."

"You're offering mercy," Odile said, breath ragged, "to people whose crime is existing."

"I'm offering the law."

"The law is a procurement contract." Odile's voice cracked on it — not with fear, Maren realized, but with fury finally finding its floor. "I've seen the ledgers. My daughter is down in that mountain because your law *sold* her." And Maren saw it happen, saw the moment the quartermaster's arithmetic finished, saw Odile stop retreating.

She didn't fence. Fencing was for people with time. She stepped *inside* the hunter's blade — taking a gash along her forearm to buy the distance — and drove the cutlass through the gap under the mask's jaw, up into the brain, and held him as he fell so he wouldn't take her over the wall with him, and lowered him onto the stone with a strange, awful gentleness.

Then she stood there bleeding and shaking, looking at her own hands.

"Maren," she said, without turning around. Her voice was wrecked and level at once. "Tell me you got the cage."

"I got the cage."

"Then somebody bind this arm before I pass out on my sister's mountain." A breath. "And nobody say anything kind to me right now. I mean it. Not one word."

Sefa bound the arm in silence. The boy in the cage watched with huge eyes. Below, the fleeing hunter's torch dwindled down the switchbacks toward the causeway — toward reinforcements, toward Vhalen, toward everything — and no one had the strength left to chase him.

* * *

They dragged the fallen behind the parapet, because Odile insisted, because some ledger of hers required it. Then they sat in the gate-light with their backs to the warm stone, five battered people and one rescued child, and listened to the mountain.

It was fully awake now. Bells in the deep places, layered and slow, like a heart learning to race. Through the gate-seams came a sound none of them had heard before except in dreams: the sea pressing against the seal from the outside, patient as gravity, singing the first bar of a storm-song.

"He's awake," the boy whispered. He had not let go of Maren's wrist. "The king. He knows the moon's full. He's scared."

"How do you know that?" Rell asked.

The boy looked at her like she'd asked how he knew fire was hot. "Can't you hear him?"

Maren could, a little. Everyone gifted could, tonight, with the tide-moon overhead and the seal thin as old rope. Under the storm-song there was another sound, older, worn almost to silence: a man's voice, very deep, reciting an oath he no longer believed, the way you'd recite anything you were afraid to forget.

"Felix is down there," Sefa said quietly. "With Nia. Right now. Under all of it."

"Then we go down after them," Ivo said.

"No," Odile said. She was looking at her bandaged arm, at the blood soaking through, at the dead men behind the parapet, and Maren watched her arrive at the decision the way she arrived at everything — reluctantly, completely. "Felix went down alone because this was always going to end between oath-speakers, and we'd only be in the way of the thing that matters. Our job was the door. We held the door." She pushed herself upright, swaying, and put her good hand on the boy's shoulder. "Our job now is to be here when they come back up. All of them. Whatever shape that takes."

"And if the mountain doesn't let them come back?" Rell asked.

The tide-moon stood at its zenith. Far below the terraces, the Sundered Sea heaved against the cliffs, and the whole coast groaned like a ship changing its mind.

Odile turned to face the gate, and the gold light washed over her face, and she did not flinch from it.

"Then we break the door off its hinges," she said, "and we go get them."

Inside the mountain, the bells changed rhythm — faster now, urgent, a summons or an alarm — and the gate-seams began, slowly, to widen.
