## 18. The Oath Spoken

The seal-chamber was not what Felix had expected, which was the first cruelty of it. They had imagined something vast and cold, a cathedral of pressure at the mountain's drowned root. Instead it was small. Low-ceilinged. Almost domestic — a room the size of Odile's kitchen, carved from black stone that wept saltwater in slow beads down the walls, each bead catching light from somewhere Felix could not find.

At the center stood the seal.

It did not look like a seal. It looked like an anchor, because it was one — a great iron hook the height of two men, half-sunk into a basin of churning water that had no business being inside a mountain. The water turned without wind. It made no sound. Chains ran from the anchor's crown up through the ceiling and out of sight, and where they passed through stone, the stone had grown around them like scar tissue over wire.

And on the anchor's flukes, wrapped around the iron like ivy around a fence post, was the king.

He had come ahead of them. Of course he had. He had known they were coming since the moment Rell's lie rang the alarm, maybe since before that, maybe since the broker's ledger first wrote Nia's name in ink that never dried. He sat on the anchor with his hands folded in his lap like a man waiting for a ferry, and his court robes drifted around him in a current that touched nothing else in the room.

"You came by the stairs," he said. His voice was the tide going out. "Maren would have used the gate-rite. Faster."

"Faster isn't safer." Felix kept their sword drawn. It had been drawn since the nursery, since Nia had pulled her hand free of theirs and said *the sea is singing to me, Felix, can't you hear it?* They had not sheathed it since. Their arm had gone numb hours ago. They let it stay numb. "Step away from it."

"From my own anchor?" The drowned king tilted his head, and water sheeted off his jaw. "Child, I have been stepping away from nothing for three hundred years. There is nowhere to step."

Felix crossed the chamber slowly, boots finding the grooves worn into the floor — and there were grooves, Felix realized. Paths. Worn smooth by centuries of pacing, of circling, of a soul walking the same twelve feet of stone until the stone remembered him. The whole room was a footprint of one man's captivity.

"I know what you are," Felix said.

"Do you?" Something flickered across his face — hope, or its corpse. "Sefa read it, I imagine. Sefa reads everything. Yes. I was a swordspeaker once. Before Vhalen had a name. Before your edicts and your brokers and your little luck-knots sold in night markets." His eyes found Felix's sword and stayed there. "I spoke my oath into a blade too. Mine is still in there somewhere. Under all of me. Do you feel yours?"

Felix felt theirs constantly. It had been three years of feeling it — the old name rattling in the steel like a coin in a cup, every time they gripped the hilt. *Marchetti.* Spoken into iron when they were nineteen and certain that oaths were free. They had learned otherwise.

"What happened," Felix said, "when your oath ran dry."

The king laughed. It was a terrible sound, because there was nothing cruel in it. Only tiredness, deep as the trench offshore.

"What happens to any well. You draw and draw and draw, and one day the bucket comes up empty, and you tell yourself the water will return. It doesn't. And the sea keeps pushing. And the seal needs feeding. So I began taking souls to feed it. Bonded ones, at first — criminals, debtors, people whose oaths had already been spoken and broken, who owed the world a price. Then anyone gifted. Then children." He said it plainly, without excuse. That was somehow worse than pleading. "I told myself each one would be temporary. That I would find another way before the next one broke. Three hundred years, swordspeaker. I never found another way, because I stopped looking. Fear does that. Fear is very good at stopping clocks."

"I'm not here to judge you."

"No," he agreed softly. "You're here to replace me."

The words landed in the chamber and the water in the basin seemed to lean toward them, listening.

Felix did not deny it. There was no point. Maren was holding the gates above with protocols stolen from her own disgrace; Odile was holding the entrance hall with a smuggler's knife and a merchant's fury; Sefa was holding the wards; Rell was somewhere below, traded, unbonded now but unfound. Every one of them was spending themselves so that Felix could stand in this room and do the only thing left.

They had rehearsed the oath on the road under the cliffs. Not aloud. Never aloud. But in the dark, watching Nia sleep, Felix had shaped it silently, word by word, the way you test a rope before you trust it with your weight.

*By the name in this blade, by the breath that named it, I bind my voice to the Sundered Sea. Let it take what it needs and hold what remains. I am the anchor. I consent.*

Consent. That was the word Sefa had brought back from the seal's architecture, glowing in their mind like a fish lantern: *it needs an oath freely renewed. The king forgot.* The seal didn't want a prisoner. It wanted a volunteer. It had been fed prisoners for three hundred years, and prisoners leak, and leaking souls had to be replaced, and the replacing had become the harvest, and the harvest had become the edict, and the edict had put Nia's name in a ledger.

One willing oath could end all of it. Felix had understood that in Sefa's telling the way you understand a cliff edge: completely, and without any sense that understanding changes anything.

Because someone had to stand here. Someone had to speak. And Felix Marchetti — who had already given their name to a blade, who had spent three years selling harmless charms and speaking to no one of what they were — had been quietly, patiently ready to give the rest for weeks.

They stepped into the worn groove in the floor, into the path the king had paced for three centuries, and raised their sword.

"Don't," the king said.

That stopped them. Not the command — they'd have ignored the command. The tone. It cracked in the middle, the way ice cracks under a boot.

"You don't want this," Felix said.

"I have wanted nothing else for three hundred years except to hear someone say those words." The king unfolded himself from the anchor, and up close, in the strange borrowed light, he looked less like a monarch and more like what he was: a man who had been standing at a post so long he had forgotten there was anywhere else to stand. "But listen to me. Listen, because no one listened to me, and look what it cost. The seal will not simply accept you. It will *keep* you. It will keep you the way the sea keeps a hull — patiently, thoroughly, forever. You are young. Your oath is fresh. You will last longer than I did, and that is not mercy, that is arithmetic. A longer drowning."

"I know the terms."

"You know the terms the way I knew them. At nineteen. Certain that oaths are free." His eyes were kind, and that was the unbearable part. "Say it anyway if you must. But say it knowing that the child you came to save is upstairs hearing the sea sing through your voice the moment you give it."

Felix's grip tightened on the hilt. "Nia is safe—"

"Nia is *gifted*," the king said gently. "Gifted children hear everything. Especially the things we wish they wouldn't."

Felix spoke anyway.

They planted their feet in the groove worn by a dead man's pacing, and they lifted the blade, and they began. *By the name in this blade —*

"—by the name in this blade," said another voice, high and clear and absolutely awake, "*by the breath that named it, I bind my voice to the Sundered Sea.*"

The world stopped.

Nia stood in the doorway. She was barefoot — she had run, Felix saw that immediately, run from wherever the hunters' chaos had left her unguarded, down stairs and through halls, following the sound of Felix's voice the way she had followed the sea's singing all along. Her hair was loose. Her eyes were wet. And her mouth was moving, and the words coming out of it were the words Felix had rehearsed alone in the dark for nineteen days.

"*Let it take what it needs,*" she said, "*and hold what remains. I am the anchor. I consent.*"

"No." Felix moved — lunged, actually, three steps across the chamber, sword still raised, the most undignified thing they had ever done — and caught her shoulders. "No. Nia. No. You don't understand what—"

"I understand better than you." She looked up at them, and she was eleven years old, and her eyes held the patient depth of the harbor at slack tide. "You've been teaching me wards for a week. You taught me that a ward fails when it's built from fear, because fear pulls tight and then it snaps. You said the best ward you ever saw was woven by a woman who wove it laughing." Her small hands came up and closed over Felix's wrists, over the numb arms, over the sword-arm that had carried them both since the alley. "This is a ward, Felix. Around everyone. Around the whole coast. And you'd build it out of guilt, and the king built his out of fear, and both of those snap. Mine won't. Because mine isn't a sacrifice."

"Nia—"

"I'm not sacrificing anything." She said it with the fierce, offended clarity of a child correcting an adult's arithmetic. "I *love* the sea. You heard me say it in the nursery and you looked at me like I was broken. I'm not broken. It sings, and I hear it, and nobody else in the whole world hears it the way I do, and you want me to spend my life pretending I can't hear it so that you can spend yours drowning instead?" Her voice shook and held. "That's not saving me. That's just choosing who breaks."

The king had gone very still on his anchor. Watching her. Felix watched him watch her, and saw on his ruined face the beginning of something he might not have felt in three centuries: the understanding that he had been doing it wrong. All of it. The harvest, the ledgers, the bonded crates singing for three days. Not evil. Just *alone*. One soul holding the sea back because he never believed anyone would hold it with him.

"The seal needs an oath freely renewed," Nia said, turning her head toward him, and her voice dropped into something older than eleven years old. "Freely. Yours wasn't, anymore. Hers—" she nodded at Felix "—wouldn't be, not really, not underneath. But mine is. Mine has been since the dreams started. Maybe since always."

She stepped out of Felix's grip. She walked past them, small bare feet on stone worn smooth by a dead man's pacing, and she laid one hand flat against the anchor's iron.

And she spoke the oath again. All of it. Slowly, clearly, in the voice of a child who had decided, and meant it, and would not be argued with — and Felix stood behind her with a raised sword and nothing in the world to do with it, because there is no way to fight a choice. That was the whole horror of it. You can fight a cage. You cannot fight someone opening a door and walking through it smiling.

The seal took her.

Light went up through the chains like dawn going up through water — white, then green, then a color Felix had no name for and suspected had no name yet, born in that instant between the sea and a girl's consent. The basin churned. The beads of salt on the walls blazed. The whole mountain hummed down through its roots, a note so low it was less a sound than a change in the floor, in the teeth, in the blood.

And Felix stood in the light of it, in the triumph of it — the seal renewed, the coast saved, the harvest ended, everything they had climbed the drowned road and lied at the gate and bled in the halls to achieve — and felt their heart crack straight down the middle like river ice in spring.

Because it had worked. That was the thing no one warned you about. The terrible plans work. The child volunteers, and it works, and the light is beautiful, and everyone lives, and you stand there holding a sword with your old name in it and understand that you would trade every minute of the victory to unhear the sound of an eleven-year-old saying *I consent* in a voice that meant it.

Nia turned around. The light was fading off her, settling into her skin, becoming ordinary, becoming hers. She looked tired, suddenly. Deeply, humanly tired, the tiredness of a long walk finally finished.

"It doesn't hurt," she said, surprised. "Felix. It doesn't hurt at all. It's like — holding something heavy with both hands instead of one. That's all it is." And then, softer, with a small crooked smile that was pure Odile, learned at that woman's knee: "You can put the sword down now. You look silly."

Felix's knees gave. They sat down hard in the groove worn into the floor, in the path of a king's three-hundred-year pacing, and they laughed, or wept, or did whatever that ragged sound was, while above them the chains sang and the sea, far away beyond the mountain, beyond the cliffs, settled against the coast like a dog lying down at last.

The drowned king slid from the anchor. He did not dissolve — not yet — but he knelt, and his voice, when it came, was almost too quiet to hear over the singing chains.

"Beneath my fear," he whispered, wonderingly, touching the iron where her hand had been. "It's still intact. My first oath. It's still *there*."

Felix looked up. "What?"

The king raised his head, and for the first time in three hundred years, the drowning in his eyes receded — just slightly, just enough to see shore.

"She's calling it," he said. "Can't you hear her? She's calling all of us."
