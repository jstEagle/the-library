## 21. Edict Undone

The Crown's assessor had hands like a clerk and eyes like an appraiser, and Odile Vasseur had spent eleven years learning to tell the difference between the two, because in Vhalen they were never the same person.

"Madam Vasseur," he said, "you are claiming that a royal edict—signed by the Lord Chancellor's own seal, enforced by the Office of Purification for three years—is, in substance, a procurement contract."

"I'm not claiming it," Odile said. "I'm showing it to you. That's what makes this different from every other conversation you've ever had with someone like me."

She slid the ledger across the table. It was water-stained at one corner, salt-crusted along its spine, and heavy enough that the assessor braced his elbow against the desk before pulling it toward him. Behind her, in the antechamber of the Chancery, Sefa stood with their arms folded and their tide-sense pulled in tight around themselves like a cloak drawn against cold. Maren leaned on the wall with her arms crossed, wearing no mask of salt-iron for the first time since Odile had known her, her face bare and unremarkable and watching everything.

The assessor opened the ledger.

Odile watched him read. She'd learned long ago that you could tell more from how a man read than from anything he said afterward. A liar skimmed. A frightened man skipped. This man went slowly, line by line, his finger tracking down columns of names, dates, tonnage—and prices. Prices per head. Prices by gift-class. Prices adjusted seasonally, like grain or herring, because souls apparently kept better in some months than others.

His finger stopped moving.

"That's the Chancellor's countersignature," he said quietly. "On page forty."

"Yes."

"And this column—" He turned the page. Turned another. "These aren't arrests. These are *deliveries*."

"The Office of Purification was never hunting anyone," Odile said. "It was harvesting them. The edict gave the hunters legal cover; the brokers gave them supply lines; the court beneath the mountain gave them a buyer. The crown has been selling gifted souls to a drowned king for three years at a fixed rate, and the paperwork is immaculate." She let that sit. Clerks respected silence. "You can burn us all as liars if you want. But the ledgers came out of that court's ruin, and there are forty-one crates of matching correspondence in the harbor warehouse under my name, catalogued, sealed, and witnessed by a disgraced witch-hunter who performed the deliveries herself and will say so under oath, under pain, under whatever the Chancery likes to threaten people with these days."

Maren didn't move from the wall. But something in her shoulders settled, the way a ship settles when it finally takes the wave it's been bracing for.

The assessor closed the ledger with the care of a man handling something that might go off.

"You understand," he said, "that if this is true, it doesn't stay in this room. It goes up. And if it goes up, heads come off. Possibly mine, for being the one who received you."

"That's not my problem," Odile said.

"Madam Vasseur—"

"It isn't," she said, and she found she wasn't angry about it, which surprised her. She'd expected to be angry. She'd carried anger up the coast road like ballast, and somewhere between the mountain and the city it had gone light, and now here it was gone, and what remained underneath was just tiredness and a strange, clean patience. "I've been a smuggler my whole life, Assessor. I know exactly how much trouble I'm handing you. I'm handing it to you anyway, because the alternative is that I hand it to every crier, every dockworker, and every grandmother in the Tanneries by nightfall, and then your heads come off *messily*. I'm offering you the tidy version. That's more mercy than the crown ever showed anybody in those columns."

* * *

It took nine days.

Nine days of the Chancery sealing doors, of quiet men arriving at the harbor warehouse with carts and counting the crates twice, of Maren sitting through four depositions with her hands flat on the table while clerks asked her to describe, again, the sound a soul makes in a crate over three days. Nine days of Odile waiting in the smuggler house—which was not yet anything new, only empty, swept, the old charm-shelves bare—waking before dawn and standing at the window watching the harbor, half expecting witch-hunters in salt-iron masks to come up the street anyway, because three years of fear does not unlearn itself in nine days.

On the tenth day, the criers went out.

Odile heard it from the rooftop, where she'd gone to escape the sound of her own thoughts. The crier at the Saltgate stood in the gray morning light with the proclamation scroll in his hands, and even from three streets away Odile could hear the difference in his voice—a crier reads what he's given, but this one read it like a man reading the end of a war:

*"By order of the Regent's Council: the Edict of Purification is dissolved. All registrations of gifted persons are annulled. All bonds, seizures, and holdings of gifted souls are void and unlawful ab initio. The Office of Purification is disbanded—"*

And then, quieter, the part they'd fought over in council for nine days, the part the Chancery had wanted to bury in clause fourteen and the scandal would not let them bury anywhere:

*"—and the Crown acknowledges, with shame, the unlawful procurement of souls under the prior administration, restitution for which shall be made—"*

Restitution. Odile sat down on the roof tiles and laughed until it hurt, because of course that was the word they'd chosen. Not *atonement*. Not *return*. Restitution, as if you could price what had been taken from Nia, from Felix, from the ones who hadn't come back—the ones whose names were in those columns marked *delivered*, whose crates had sung for three days, whose king had drowned holding them and crumbled anyway when the seal was remade without them.

Below, the street was coming alive. A dockworker stood in the middle of the lane with his luck-knot still knotted at his wrist—Odile recognized the knotting style, Felix's work, three years old at least—and he was just standing there, listening, his mouth open. An old woman came out of the chandlery and asked the crier to read it again. He did. He read it three times, and each time the crowd got bigger, and nobody cheered, which Odile understood completely. Cheering was for victories. This was something else. This was a city finding out it had been an accessory and not knowing where to put its hands.

* * *

The pardon came down quietly, the way the crown does everything it's ashamed of. No ceremony. Names cleared in batches, posted in the Chancery window where debt notices used to go. Sefa walked past the posting on the fourth day, read their own name, and said nothing at all, which from Sefa was a eulogy and a christening both.

The medal was less quiet, because the crown cannot resist making its amends ornamental when the amends cost nothing.

They summoned Odile to the Regent's Council chamber itself—white stone, high windows, a table long enough to feed a village—and a minister with a voice like warmed butter explained that the Council wished to recognize her service. There was a medal. There was talk of a commission. There was a phrase Odile suspected had been workshopped: *a citizen of singular courage and civic devotion.*

"Before you answer," the minister said, "consider what it would mean. For you. For your household. A woman of your—background—standing with the crown's gratitude. It would open doors."

Odile looked at the medal. It was silver, small, tasteful, stamped with the crown's sigil, and it was worth, she estimated, about four days' honest wages, which meant it was worth exactly nothing, because the crown had paid for it out of the same treasury that had paid for the crates.

"Do you know what we found at the bottom of that mountain?" she said. "Not treasure. Not the king's hoard. We found a nursery. Little rooms, warm-lit, soft beds, kind wardens. Everything a child could want except the door unlocked. That court understood comfort perfectly. It used comfort as mortar." She set the medal back on the table, gently, the way you return something borrowed. "If I take that, I'm saying the crown and I are on the same side of the ledger. We're not. You bought souls for three years and called it law, and you're pardoning the survivors because the alternative is trials, and trials have witnesses. Keep your medal. Open the doors yourself—that's the only thanks I want, and it costs you nothing but habit."

The minister's smile stayed fixed the way paint stays fixed on rotting wood.

"There will be those who call that ingratitude."

"There already are. They're mostly in this building."

* * *

The smuggler house reopened on a market morning, because Brannick would have insisted on a working day, not a feast day. Feasts were for things that were finished. Nothing was finished. Things were just beginning differently.

Odile had argued with herself for a month about the sign. The old sign had read VASSEUR & CO., IMPORTS, and everyone in the district had known precisely what was imported. She'd burned it in the yard the week they got back, and it had taken surprisingly little persuading to burn—it was just wood and paint, but it felt like burning a skin she'd grown out of.

The new sign hung crooked for two days while Rell, who had come back thin and quiet from the mountain's collapse with a scar on her palm and her brother's hand never far from hers, planed it true. Then it went up straight, and Odile stood across the street and read it until she'd memorized it, the way you check a knot by looking away and looking back:

**VASSEUR HOUSE — CHARMWORK, TEACHING, HARBOR AID. NO QUESTIONS ASKED EXCEPT ONE: WHAT DO YOU NEED?**

Felix had suggested the last line. Odile had cut it down twice before letting it stand.

The ground floor held the charm counter—real charms now, taught openly, sold honestly, the luck-knots going fast once the market decided that magic was neither crime nor miracle but trade. The back room held desks, because Nia—who had come back from inside a seal with sea-light still faintly in her eyes and a calm that unsettled adults who didn't know its history—had declared that the first lesson would happen within the month and that she would be teaching wards to children younger than she was. The cellar, scrubbed to the stone, held bunks and a kettle and a rule chalked on the wall in Ivo's careful hand: *anyone may sleep here two nights, no questions except one.*

Maren took the room above the kitchen. She still flinched at loud knocks. She was teaching gate-rites and hunter protocols to anyone the house sent her way, and she had started, haltingly, to sleep whole nights.

Sefa came and went with the tides, keeping a bunk they rarely used, reading weather for fishing fleets now instead of smuggling runs, and pretending not to be proud when the fleet captains started paying in advance.

On opening morning the line stretched past the chandlery. Odile worked the counter beside Felix, who had their sleeves rolled up and their voice—whole, unhidden, doing what it was made for—talking a nervous fisherman's daughter through her first warding-knot. Every so often Odile caught fragments of it over the noise: *no, tighter there—that's it—you feel how it holds? That's not the string. That's you.*

Near dusk, when the line had thinned to stragglers and the kettle needed a second fire, Nia appeared at Odile's elbow, sea-eyed, solemn, holding a scrap of paper.

"This came with the evening tide-post," Nia said. "The runner wouldn't wait. He looked scared."

Odile wiped her hands and took it. The paper was Chancery stock, good linen rag, the kind that meant money or trouble, and it bore six words in a clerk's precise hand, unsigned, unsealed:

***The ledgers were incomplete. Some pages were removed before delivery.***

Below that, an address in the Tanneries. And below that, in smaller writing, almost an apology:

***Someone else knows what was missing.***

Odile read it twice. Across the counter, Felix was laughing at something the fisherman's daughter had said, and the lamplight was coming up gold along the harbor, and the city outside was busy learning how to live without its oldest fear.

Odile folded the paper once and put it in her pocket, next to the coin she still carried from her first run, and went to tell the others.
