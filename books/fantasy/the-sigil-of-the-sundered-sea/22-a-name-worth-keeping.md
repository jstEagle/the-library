## 22. A Name Worth Keeping

The harbor wall at dawn was the only classroom in Vhalen that didn't charge admission.

Felix arrived before the students did, the way they always did now, and stood where the stone met the sea and let the water talk. It had been talking for four months — softer than it used to be, a murmur instead of a threat, the Sundered Sea settling into its new arrangement like an old dog circling down onto a familiar bed. Somewhere beneath all that gray-green calm lay the honest ruin of the mountain court, collapsed into rubble and tide-pools, and somewhere in the market behind them, luck-knots were selling themselves.

That last part still unsettled Felix more than the sea did.

They heard the footsteps before they saw them — small ones first, then heavier, then the twins arguing in the particular register of two people who agreed completely and would die before admitting it.

"You're early," Nia said.

"You're earlier."

"I live closer."

"I *live* here," Felix said, gesturing at the whole gray morning, "in a philosophical sense."

Rell snorted. Ivo elbowed her. The two of them had come back from the steward's ledger with matching scars neither would discuss and a habit of standing shoulder to shoulder like bookends holding up a shelf no one else could see. Rell's collar still showed the faint silver line of the bond-mark, fading week by week as the shared seal drank it away. Felix had watched it go with something close to awe. Every willing oath woven into the new seal was slowly unbinding every old one, and the arithmetic of it — love canceling out debt — was the kind of math Felix had never trusted until they'd seen it work.

"Class is full," Odile called from the top of the harbor stairs, coming down with a basket of bread rolls and Sefa at her elbow. "Eleven students. Eleven. I counted twice."

"Twelve," said Maren, arriving last, because Maren was always last now and always unapologetic about it. She'd traded her salt-iron mask for a plain leather one that covered nothing but old burns, and she wore it like an apology she'd finally finished making. She carried a crate on one hip. Inside it were practice-blades — wooden, blunt, and carved along the tang with each student's chosen name.

That was the lesson. It had been the lesson since the first morning, when Felix stood on this wall with seven terrified volunteers and told them the truth their own teachers had never told them.

"Sit," Felix said, and eleven children of the outlawed sat on the harbor stones, and the sea breathed against the wall below them like a patient audience.

* * *

The first class had been seven: three dockworkers' kids, a tide-reader's apprentice, two sisters who'd survived the Tanneries purges by hiding in a brine vat for two days, and Nia. All of them gifted. All of them marked by the edict's collapse — pardoned on paper, hunted still by rumor, by landlords, by the particular cruelty of neighbors who remembered what it had been legal to fear. Odile had put out word through the smuggler channels, the way you'd put out word about salvage, and the wrecked and desperate had come up out of the city like things surfacing.

Felix had drawn their sword — the sword, the one that held a name — and laid it flat across their knees, and said:

"This blade ate my name. That's what I was taught an oath costs. Everything. You speak your truest self into the steel and you walk away hollow, and everyone told me that was the price, that was the *honor*, that was how it worked." They'd looked at Nia then. They always looked at Nia then. "Everyone lied. Or rather — everyone believed a lie a frightened man told himself three hundred years ago, alone, at the bottom of a mountain, and built a kingdom on top of it so he'd never have to say it out loud."

The drowned king had been a swordspeaker once. Sefa had found it written in the seal's own architecture, legible to anyone willing to read without flinching: an oath freely renewed, renewed, renewed, century after century, until the renewer forgot that renewal was supposed to be *shared* and started taking souls instead of voices. Fear was all he had left, and fear is a currency that only spends downward.

"So here's the truth," Felix told the seven, and now told the twelve. "An oath freely given costs nothing and gives everything. Not the other way around. Never the other way around. If it costs you, someone's skimming."

Maren set the crate down and began handing out practice-blades, name by name. This part took time, because choosing a name was not a formality. Felix made every student sit with the wooden blank for a full class before carving anything — sit with it, hold it, ask it nothing, tell it nothing, just learn its weight. *You don't give the blade your name,* Felix taught. *You choose the name you'll answer to when everything else has been taken from you. And if you've chosen well, nothing can take it, because you gave it away on purpose.*

Today's carvings were done. Today was speaking day.

Nia went first, because Nia always went first, because Nia had stood inside a living seal and organized it from within like a foreman re-tiling a roof, and the rest of them were, frankly, amateurs. She stepped to the wall's edge, wooden blade in both hands, thirteen years old and lithe as a gull in the wind off the water, and spoke her oath in a clear voice that carried all the way to the fish-market stalls.

It wasn't grand. That was the thing Felix kept trying to teach and kept failing to teach, because students wanted grand. Nia's oath was: *I am Nia, who was sold and not lost, and I keep what is given to me.* Nine words. The sea rose slightly against the wall — a courtesy, a nod, the way it had learned to acknowledge the new anchor-choir — and settled.

One by one the others spoke. A dockworker's daughter whose oath was four words long. The brine-vat sisters, who spoke theirs together, which should have been impossible and worked anyway, because shared oaths were the entire point and the sea had opinions about efficiency. Sefa's apprentice, trembling, whispering hers, and the water hearing it anyway, because the sea was not listening for volume. It was listening for willingness.

When the last voice faded, Felix drew the sword.

The old blade came out of its scabbard with a sound like a long exhale, and the morning light moved along it, and there — as there had been since the night on the drowned coast road when Felix had first admitted aloud what the forging cost — was the absence where a name used to be. A smooth stretch of steel that reflected whoever looked at it and gave back nothing of itself.

Except.

Four months now, the reflection had been doing something strange. Four months, and Felix hadn't told the class, hadn't told Odile, hadn't quite told themself. But today, with twelve fresh oaths still hanging in the salt air, Felix looked into the blank steel and saw — not a name. Not yet. But a shape where a name might grow. A warmth in the metal like a hearthstone at midnight. The sword was learning something too. Or remembering.

*You don't have to refill it,* Nia had told them once, weeks ago, sitting on this same wall with her legs swinging over the drop. *Nobody says you have to. But you could. Whenever you want. Freely.*

Not yet. Soon. Felix slid the blade home and turned to the class.

"That's the whole curriculum," they said. "Everything after this is footnotes."

* * *

The market took some getting used to.

Felix walked it most evenings now, the long way home, past the charm-stalls where luck-knots hung in braided rows like onions and nobody whispered and nobody looked over their shoulder. Three years Felix had sold harmless charms with guilty hands, hiding the power coiled in their voice, selling knots that were only rope. Now half the knot-sellers in the night market were gifted, and their knots actually *worked*, and customers haggled over them cheerfully and complained about prices and asked the sellers to bless their fishing nets and their daughters' weddings, openly, in the open air, under lanterns.

A broker had tried, in the second month, to set up shop again near the Tanneries. He lasted six days. Not because anyone hurt him — though Odile had paid him a visit, and Odile's visits had a way of rearranging a man's priorities — but because no one would sell to him and no one would sell *for* him and his ledger, when it surfaced during the edict hearings, had become the single most damning exhibit in the crown's scandal. *Procurement contract*, the newspapers called the Purification Edict now. The hunt had been harvest; the harvest had been invoiced; and Vhalen's magistrates, faced with ledgers showing their own signatures in the margins, had pardoned the gifted very quietly and burned the paperwork very loudly.

Odile had declined the medal. Felix had been there, in the chamber, arms crossed, when the minister offered it, and had enjoyed every second of what followed.

"I ran contraband for nineteen years," Odile told him, in a voice that carried to the gallery. "You're offering me a medal for surviving your law. Keep the medal. Open a school." Then she'd walked out, and the smuggler house on Rope Lane had reopened within the fortnight under a painted sign that read **HOUSE OF THE SUNDERED CHOIR** in Odile's own handwriting, which meant it could never be mistaken for anything official, which was exactly the point.

Now the house held eleven beds, a kitchen that fed anyone who knocked, a map room where Sefa charted the sea's new moods, and a waiting list. Gifted families came up the coast road from the drowned villages. Hunters came too — a few, quietly, without their masks, asking Maren if there was room for a person trying to be otherwise. Maren asked each of them the same question Felix had once asked her: *What will you do with what you know?* Some answers were good enough. Some weren't. The door opened either way, but only some people walked through it twice.

Evening was settling gold over the harbor when Felix reached the far end of the market and found Nia at the knot-stall, arguing with a fishwife about whether a storm-knot should really cost double.

"It's *woven against the weather*," Nia said.

"It's *string*," the fishwife said fondly.

"It's string that remembers my oath!"

"Half price and I buy two."

"Done," said Nia instantly, and caught Felix watching, and grinned the grin that had gotten them all through a mountain. "Don't start. You taught me negotiation."

"I taught you wards. The negotiation is Odile's fault entirely."

They walked the seawall together, the long way, past the cranes and the tar-smell and the bell buoy that rang differently now — brighter, Sefa insisted, though Felix suspected the buoy was the same and the listeners had changed. Below them the water lay quiet, and beneath the quiet, if Felix let their gift reach, the choir. Twelve harbor-wall oaths and a hundred more from up and down the coast, and underneath them all, worn smooth as a river stone, the king's original vow — intact, Nia said, it had been intact the whole time, buried under three centuries of fear like a coin under floorboards — still humming its first note. *I hold the sea so that others need not.* He'd just forgotten he was allowed company.

"Your sword's doing it again," Nia said.

Felix glanced at her. "Doing what?"

"Warming up. Getting ready. You look at it like a person deciding whether to knock on a door." She swung her legs above the drop. "You should knock. Whenever you want. Freely. That's the whole rule."

"The whole rule," Felix agreed.

"You keep saying soon."

"I keep meaning it."

"Well." Nia leaned her head briefly against Felix's arm, thirteen years old and older than the mountain. "Soon's a good name. If you're picking."

Below them, the sea murmured against the wall — and then, unmistakably, in a voice like a wave finding a cave-mouth, it answered. Not words. Not yet. But a shape where words might grow, warm as a hearthstone at midnight, patient as a name waiting to be worth keeping.
