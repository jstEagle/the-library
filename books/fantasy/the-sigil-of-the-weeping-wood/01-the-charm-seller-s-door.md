## 1. The Charm Seller's Door

The rain came down on Vaelmoor the way it always did in the month of Hollows — not falling so much as settling in, like a guest who had decided the house was his and you were welcome to stay if you kept quiet.

Isandro Coldharbour watched it through the fogged window of his shop and thought, not for the first time, that he should have chosen a drier city for his life of crime.

"Coldharbour's Bonecraft & Curiosities," said the sign above his door, and it was even mostly true. He did sell bonecraft. The curiosities were real curiosities too: a jar of river pearls gone cloudy with age, a taxidermied pike with an expression of permanent moral disappointment, three shelves of buttons that no one in ten years had ever bought. And beneath the floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth and packed in salt like the contraband they were, lay forty-one small carved bones that whispered with the voices of the dead.

The Edict of Silence made them illegal, of course. Everything worth having was illegal these days. Thirteen years since the Sundering of the Weeping Wood, thirteen years since the mages burned their own covenant to save their own skins and then passed laws making sure nobody else could practice what they'd abandoned. Isandro had been twenty-three when the Edict came down, newly widowed, newly gifted, and newly determined to be stupid about all three.

He heard the widow before she opened the door. That was the thing about his gift — or curse, depending on the hour — he always heard people coming. Not their footsteps. Their *dead*. Widow Tamsin Brase carried her grief the way other women carried shopping baskets, and inside the basket, cooing softly, was her husband's ghost.

*She'll forget the bread,* the ghost of Odo Brase was saying as she fumbled with the latch. *Third time this week. She forgets the bread because she's thinking about me, and honestly I'd rather she forgot me and remembered the bread, we can't have her wasting away—*

Isandro unlocked the door before she knocked.

"Mistress Brase." He stepped back to admit her into the warm dimness of the shop. "You're soaked through."

"You're open late," she said, wringing rainwater from her shawl onto his clean floor without apology. She was a broad woman, sixty-odd, with the forearms of someone who had spent thirty years hauling fish nets and the eyes of someone who had spent one year crying into them. "I walked past twice. Couldn't make myself come in. Then I couldn't make myself walk away. So here I am, being ridiculous at your doorstep."

"The best customers usually are." He steered her toward the stool by the stove, poured her a cup of the barley tea he kept simmering more for the smell than the taste. It smelled like honesty. It tasted like punishment. He'd never worked out how to fix that, and at this point he considered it part of its charm. "You're here about Odo."

Her face did something complicated. "You know his name?"

"I know everyone's name, mistress. It's the trade." This was true in a way he didn't intend to explain. He knelt by the stove and fed it another knot of driftwood, buying himself a moment. Ten years of this, and the opening still never got easy. Every widow, every father, every child who climbed his steps with that same terrible hopeful look — each one was a small door he had to open knowing what stood behind it.

"I want—" Mistress Brase set down her cup with a clack. Her hands weren't steady. "They say — and I'm not saying I believe it, mind, I'm saying they say — that there's a man on Candlemaker's Row who can carve a bit of bone so it holds a person's last words. So a widow could hear them. Whenever she wanted."

"They say correctly," Isandro said.

"It's illegal."

"It is."

"And you're admitting it just like that?"

"I find denial wastes time we could spend on tea."

That startled a laugh out of her — wet, ragged, but a laugh — and something in her shoulders came down half an inch. Good. Grief sat easier on a body that could laugh.

Odo's ghost, meanwhile, had noticed where he was and gone very quiet, the way the dead did when they realized a living person could actually hear them. Isandro felt the silence of him like a change in the weather. Most days he wished he could turn the hearing off. Days like this one, when the dead were frightened or hopeful or both, he almost wished he were deaf instead.

"He drowned," Mistress Brase said, to the teacup. "Eight months ago. Off the Miren quay, netting season, a swell came out of nowhere and took him and two others. They found him three days after." She swallowed. "He was alone when it happened. At the end. Three days in the water and I keep thinking — what did he think? Did he think about us? Was he afraid? Was he angry at me for telling him to take the deep-water run because the pay was better? Because I told him, you see. I told him the money was good."

"Mistress—"

"I need to know his last words. That's all. Whatever they are. Even if they're horrible. Even if they're *her name*, some girl from his younger days, I don't care, I just need to know what a man says when he's alone in the water dying." She finally looked up at him, and her eyes were fierce and terrified in equal measure, which was, Isandro had learned, the standard expression of anyone who loved someone they'd lost. "Can you do that?"

Here it was. The moment. Ten years of it and it never got lighter.

"I can't conjure his last moments," Isandro said gently. "I'm not a miracle-worker. I'm a carver." He rose and went to the counter, to the drawer beneath it, and drew out a length of bone — sheep femur, cured and cleaned, smooth as a river stone. "What I can do is this. I carve the charm while holding something of theirs. A possession. Something they wore against their skin long enough to soak up a little of who they were. Then I listen. What comes through isn't a speech. It's fragments. Echoes. The strongest things they carried. Sometimes those are last thoughts, if the death was strong enough to leave a mark. Sometimes it's just... them. A habit. A joke. A worry."

"What came through for your wife?"

The question landed like a dropped glass. He kept his hands steady on the counter and gave her the truth, or the shape of it. "A song. Half of one. She used to hum it while kneading bread, off-key, deliberately, because she knew it annoyed me." He smiled, and meant maybe a third of it. "It's the most precious thing I own."

Mistress Brase looked at him for a long moment, and he watched her decide — the way they all decided, standing on the edge of the small crime, weighing the law of Vaelmoor against the law of the heart. The Pale Office had informants on every corner. Possession of a whisper-charm was five years in the Sunken Cells. He watched her weigh it anyway.

"Do it," she said. "Whatever it costs."

"It doesn't cost coin," he said. "Well. It costs some coin. But mostly it costs courage. You'll hear him, mistress, but hearing is a door that doesn't close again. Some folk find that a mercy. Some find it keeps the wound open." He'd buried two customers under that warning over the years, in the sense of attending funerals, though mercifully not because of the charms. "Think first. Think tonight. Come back tomorrow if—"

"No." She said it flat, final, the voice of a woman who had hauled nets through storms. "I've thought for eight months. Tonight I stop thinking."

*Thank the saints,* said the ghost of Odo Brase, from somewhere near the ceiling. *Because I've been trying to tell her about the bread for weeks and it's like shouting into a fog.*

Isandro pressed his lips together to keep from smiling. The dead were like that — impatient, practical, desperate to be useful. Living people imagined ghosts full of portent and mystery. In Isandro's experience they were mostly worried about unfinished chores.

* * *

He worked by lamplight while the widow drank her tea and pretended not to watch, which fooled neither of them.

The carving itself was honest work, the only honest work in the whole dishonest business. Sheep femur, scored with fine lines, shaped into a lozenge small enough to close in a fist. His knife — Maren's knife, actually, though he'd never told anyone that; a carver giving his dead wife's boning knife a second career seemed either beautiful or obscene depending on the day — moved in the old patterns, grooves that weren't decoration but structure, channels for something to flow along. He'd taught himself the patterns from a book he'd burned a page at a time, memorizing and destroying, until the knowledge lived only in his hands.

And all the while, the listening.

People thought the magic happened in the carving. It didn't. The carving was carpentry. The magic happened in the *opening* — the deliberate unstoppering of himself, letting his hearing reach past the room, past the rain, down into whatever thin country the dead inhabited. It cost him something every time, like pressing on a bruise to check it still hurt. He opened now, holding the ring Odo Brase had worn for forty years — a plain brass band worn thin as wire — and let the man's presence pool into the bone like water into a channel.

Fragments came. Salt water. Cold, enormous cold. The green flash of a wave seen from below. And threaded through it all, stubborn as a weed:

*The bread's on the second shelf, Tam. Second shelf, not the third, you always put it in the third and it goes stale—*

And underneath, quieter, the thing men think when they are drowning and know it, the thing Odo had carried for three days in dark water while his heart decided whether to give up:

*Don't let her blame herself. Don't let her carry that. Tell her it wasn't the deep run. It was my luck running out, that's all, luck runs out, it wasn't her fault—*

Isandro closed the channel and sat for a moment with his eyes shut, breathing. Eight months dead and the man's whole remaining self was a loaf of bread and a plea for his widow's peace. That was the dead for you. Strip away everything and what stayed was love and logistics, in roughly equal measure.

"All right?" Mistress Brase asked quietly.

"Yes." He cleared his throat. "Yes. It's done."

He sealed the charm with wax from a black candle — theater, mostly, but the dead appreciated ritual; it gave them a sense of occasion — and pressed it into her palm, folding her fingers over it. "Hold it when you want to hear him. Hold it and think of him and listen. It won't speak in crowds or in wind. It wants quiet. And mistress —" he held her gaze — "it holds what he carried. If it's hard to hear, that means it was true."

She nodded, jaw tight. Then, abruptly, she leaned across the counter and gripped his wrist with a hand like a boat winch.

"My sister says you're a criminal," she said. "Says I should report you to the Office and save us all trouble." Her grip tightened. "My sister's an idiot. Bless you, Coldharbour. Whatever happens to you for this — bless you."

Then she was gone, out into the rain, clutching a piece of sheep bone like it was the crown jewels, and Isandro stood alone in his shop feeling the familiar cocktail of the aftermath: warmth, guilt, fear, and the low persistent hum of Odo Brase fading out of range, still fussing about bread shelves.

He counted the night's earnings — four coppers and a button, offered apologetically — and began closing up. Shutters. Bar. Stove banked. The false-bottomed drawer checked. Forty charms left below the floorboards, plus the forty-first, the one that lived upstairs in a box under his bed and that no sum of gold would ever buy.

Maren's charm. Half a song, off-key, deliberate.

Ten years, and he played it maybe once a season, like a man rationing the last bottle of a vintage that would never be made again. Her voice was in there, or an echo of it, and every hearing used it up a little — he was nearly sure of that, nearly sure the charm grew fainter each year — and so he hoarded it, and hated himself for hoarding it, and hoarded it anyway. Somewhere in the city there were other charms he'd sold carrying other voices, and he'd told every customer the same thing: use it. Love isn't a cellar to store things in. Meanwhile he kept his own wife locked in a box under his bed, visiting her four times a year like a dutiful but cowardly son.

Hypocrite, said a voice in his head that sounded annoyingly like Maren.

"I know," he told the empty shop.

The rain thickened against the windows. Vaelmoor settled into its wet gray sleep. Isandro climbed the stairs to his rooms above the shop, ate bread and hard cheese standing up because eating sitting down alone felt too much like a memorial service, and went to bed.

He did not sleep. He rarely slept well anymore. Instead he listened — he couldn't not — to the great soft murmur of the city's dead rising through the floorboards like damp. Vaelmoor was an old city, drowned twice, burned once, plague-stricken thrice, and its soil was crowded. On quiet nights he could lie in bed and feel them all, thousands upon thousands, murmuring their unfinished business into the dark: merchants still arguing prices, children still counting games, soldiers still marching home along roads that no longer existed. A whole invisible city layered over the living one, and only Isandro Coldharbour could hear it, which was either a gift or the loneliest job in the world.

Some nights he thought it might be both.

* * *

The knock came at midnight.

Three knocks, spaced wrong — too slow, too soft, the knocking of someone who had never knocked on a door in their life and was working it out from description. Isandro was downstairs with a candle before his mind fully woke, heart going like a fist on a door of its own.

Nobody knocked at midnight. Nobody knocked at midnight unless they were the Pale Office, and the Pale Office didn't knock, they arrived, boots and iron whistles and that awful polite courtesy that preceded interrogations. Nobody else came to a charm-seller's door at midnight except someone desperate, and desperate people by daylight were trouble enough.

He put his eye to the peephole.

Nothing. Just the dark street, the rain silvering down through the light of his own doorway lamp, the wet cobbles shining like scales.

Then he looked down.

There was a child on his step.

Small — six, seven years old, hard to tell, the way it's hard to tell with children who've been outdoors longer than they've been anywhere else. Mud-caked from hair to shoes, mud of a strange color, almost gray-green, as if she'd waded through something worse than puddles. She stood perfectly still in the rain, which was falling straight through her and soaking her to the skin, and she did not shiver, and she did not knock again, and she was looking directly up at the peephole as though she knew exactly where his eye was.

Isandro's mouth had gone dry. There were rules about this. There were rules he'd built over ten years like a wall around himself: no children, no runaways, no one the Office might already be watching, nothing that brought attention to the door. A child alone at midnight in the rain was every kind of danger there was, wrapped in a small soaked package.

He opened the door anyway. He'd known he was going to before he'd reached the bottom stair. Ten years of hiding hadn't made him careful; it had only made him tired, and tired men open doors.

"Are you lost?" he asked.

The child didn't answer. Of course she didn't — a mute child, he thought, or too frightened, or—

And then he heard it.

Not from her mouth. From everywhere. From the floorboards and the walls and the forty charms sleeping under his shop floor, every carved bone he owned waking at once like birds at dawn, resonating with a sound that was almost a hum, almost a chord, almost — though he had no right to the word and no idea where it came from — almost a *word*, spoken in a language older than any kingdom in the world, older than Vaelmoor, older perhaps than the rain.

Every bone in his house was singing. And the child on his step tilted her head, gray-eyed, mud-streaked, calm as midnight, and waited for him to understand.
