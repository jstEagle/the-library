## 2. A Language Older Than Kingdoms

The child did not sleep so much as she *settled*, the way snow settles on a rooftop — gradually, silently, and with the unmistakable suggestion that she might slide off at any moment.

Isandro watched her from his workbench by candlelight, a half-carved knucklebone of a sheep forgotten under his knife. She had claimed the corner behind the cold stove without being asked, curled into a shape that seemed designed to take up as little of the world as possible, and there she had remained for three hours while he pretended, badly, to work.

He had put her to bed twice. Both times she had climbed back out and returned to the corner like a cat reclaiming its territory, and both times he had decided that arguing with a mud-caked mute child at midnight was beneath him. There was a blanket now. He had draped it over her with all the tenderness of a man throwing a tarp over firewood, and if she had opened one eye to watch him do it, well. That was between her and the dark.

"Right," he said quietly, to himself, because talking to oneself was one of the few vices left to a man in his position and he intended to enjoy it. "You'll explain yourself in the morning, or you won't, which frankly is more likely."

The child said nothing. The child, he was beginning to understand, was very good at nothing.

He turned back to his work — a commission for old Tam Wicker, whose brother had died with an apology stuck in his throat, and who had paid Isandro three silver marks for the privilege of hearing it at last. Ordinary work. Honest work, if you didn't think too hard about the word *honest*, which Isandro had gotten quite skilled at not doing over ten years.

The knife touched bone.

And the bone *sang*.

Not the usual whisper — not the thin, papery murmur of a dead voice caught in marrow, which he could coax out only with hours of careful carving and a patience most men reserved for fishing. No. This was different. This was resonance, deep and round, as though someone had struck the bone like a bell. It hummed against his fingers, then against the bench, then — impossibly — against the other finished charms hanging from their pegs by the window, eleven small bones trembling in their leather cords like leaves in a wind that was not blowing.

Isandro sat very still.

In ten years of illegal craftsmanship, his charms had never once made a sound uninvited. That was rather the point of them. A charm that sang on its own was a charm that got its maker introduced, at length and unpleasantly, to the Pale Office.

The humming rose and fell. And underneath it — woven through it, the way a thread runs through cloth — came another sound.

A voice. Or something like a voice. It was coming from the corner behind the stove.

The child was humming.

It should have been nothing. Children hummed. He had heard street children hum drinking songs with lyrics that would make a dockworker blush. But this was not a tune. It had no beginning and no end, no shape a human throat ought to find comfortable. It moved in long slow phrases, rising and falling like — like wind through branches, like water over roots, like something enormous breathing in its sleep. And every phrase she hummed, the bones answered. They could not help themselves. They answered the way iron answers a lodestone, helplessly, joyfully, ringing out the voices they held: Tam's brother stammering his apology, a drowned fisherman singing a shanty, an old woman reciting names, names, names —

"Stop," Isandro whispered. Not to her. To the bones. "Stop, stop, *stop* —"

The child stopped humming.

The silence that followed was worse. Every charm on the wall went still at once, mid-voice, like a room full of people turning to look at something behind them.

She was sitting up in her corner, watching him. In the candlelight her eyes were the color of rainwater over moss, and they were fixed not on his face but on the charms by the window, with an expression he could only describe as *recognition*.

"You can hear them," he said. It wasn't a question, but it wasn't really a statement either; it was a man standing on a floor he'd just discovered was thinner than he'd thought. "You can hear what's in them."

She tilted her head. Then, slowly, she raised one small hand and pointed — past the charms, past the window, past the wall itself — at the floor. At the ground beneath the city. At whatever lay beneath everything.

Then she hummed again, one single low note, and Isandro felt it in his teeth.

*Oh,* thought some small, sensible part of him, from very far away. *We are in a great deal of trouble.*

* * *

He should have thrown her out. He knew this the way a man knows the stove is hot after touching it once. Everything he had built — ten years of careful, cowardly, quiet survival — depended on being unremarkable. On selling whispers through a side door to grieving people who would never speak of it, and going home to soup, and hearing nothing louder than rain.

Instead, he made tea.

This was, he would reflect later, the defining flaw of his character: when frightened, Isandro Coldharbour did not run. He put the kettle on.

The child accepted tea with both hands wrapped around the cup, blowing on it with great seriousness before taking a sip, and something about the gravity of it — a mud-caked waif handling a chipped cup like a queen with a goblet — loosened something in his chest that had been clenched since midnight.

"Isandro," he said, tapping his own chest. It was absurd; she clearly understood more than she let on. But naming things was a comfort. "Is-an-dro."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she pressed one muddy finger to her own chest and made a sound — not a word, not quite, more a soft exhalation shaped around two syllables. *Seh-fah.* Or near enough. Her mouth didn't seem entirely built for common speech, the way a flute isn't built for whistling.

"Sefa," he tried.

Her whole face changed. It was like watching dawn come up over a field — sudden, total warmth, as though he'd done something magnificent rather than merely pronounced two syllables. She nodded so hard the blanket slid off her shoulders.

"Well then, Sefa." He settled onto the bench across from her, cup in hand, and studied her properly for the first time. Under the grime she was perhaps eight years old, sharp-boned, with hair the color of wet bark cut raggedly at the jaw. Her hands were wrong, he noticed. Not injured-wrong. *Old*-wrong. The knuckles were broad, the fingers long, faint lines running through the skin like grain in wood. He had spent a decade handling bones and he knew age when he saw it, and this child's hands had more of it than his own.

"How did you get here?" he asked, knowing he wouldn't get an answer. "Vaelmoor's three weeks' walk from anywhere worth being, and the gates have been shut at dusk since before you were born. Somebody carried you. Somebody fed you. Somebody—" He stopped, because her expression had gone distant and strange, and because the answer arrived in his head unbidden: *nobody. Nobody carried her. She walked out of the ground like a root finding daylight.*

He drank his tea instead of saying that out loud. Saying things like that out loud was how you ended up on the Pale Office's lists.

Sefa set down her cup, leaned forward, and reached across the table toward his hand.

Every instinct he owned told him to pull back. Instead — because he was tired, and lonely, and because her eyes held the particular gravity of something much older than a child — he let her take it.

Her palm was cool and rough as bark.

The world *opened*.

There was no other word for it. One moment he was sitting in his kitchen; the next he was standing — or felt he was standing — inside a memory so vast it had weather. He smelled rain that had fallen centuries ago on leaves taller than church spires. He heard a sound like a thousand harps tuned to one chord, which he slowly understood was wind moving through a canopy that covered horizons. He felt *roots* — miles of them, braided through dark soil, drinking, holding, remembering — and threaded through all of it, voices. Not human voices. Not the papery dead he knew. These were green voices, slow voices, patient as stone and old as the hills, speaking a language that was less words than *seasons*.

And underneath it, grief. A grief so large it had its own climate. Something had been torn. Something broken at the root and still bleeding sap, still weeping, year upon year upon year —

Isandro wrenched his hand back and knocked over his chair getting away from the table.

For a moment he simply stood there in his small kitchen, heart slamming, the ordinary walls reassuringly close, the smell of tea and lamp-oil anchoring him. Sefa hadn't moved. She watched him with an expression of terrible, patient sadness, the way one watches a bird that has flown into a window.

"What," he managed, "was that."

She pointed at herself. Then at the floor. Then, with both hands, she spread her fingers wide and upward — a gesture of branching, of spreading, of a crown of a tree against the sky.

"No," Isandro said. "No, that's — no." He laughed, a short ugly sound with no humor in it. "That's a story for drunkards and fools. The Weeping Wood is gone. Cut and burned and salted thirteen years ago. There's nothing left of it but the petrified branches they keep in the Office like hunting trophies. I've *heard* about—"

But even as he said it, he was seeing it again: the grief underneath everything, the wound in the green memory, the weeping. *The Weeping Wood.* Named not for rain, the old stories said, but for sorrow.

Sefa tilted her head at him, and in her rainwater eyes he saw that she knew exactly what he'd felt. She had *shown* him. On purpose. Because his bones sang honest — that was what she'd been looking for last night at his door, he realized. Not shelter. Honesty. A man whose craft kept the dead's voices true.

"I carve bones," he said stupidly, to fill the silence. "Sheep, mostly. Cow, if they pay extra. I'm a tradesman. I'm nobody. Whatever you think I am, whoever you think can help you — it isn't me. I hide, little one. That's my whole talent. Ten years of hiding, done well."

Sefa considered this. Then she picked up her tea and finished it, and looked at him with the calm certainty of someone who has all the time in the world, and knows precisely where he will sleep tonight.

* * *

Dawn found him at the window with the shutter cracked, watching Grayharbor Street wake below. Fish carts. A chimney sweep arguing with a cat. The lamplighter making his rounds, snuffing flames gone pale against the growing light. Vaelmoor being ordinary, gloriously ordinary, and Isandro holding onto that ordinariness like a rope over a drop.

Behind him, Sefa stood among his hanging charms, perfectly still, listening to them the way other children listened to music boxes.

He should have burned them all last night, he thought. Every charm. Should have fed the lot into the stove and been a plain carver of buttons and dice by morning. But Tam Wicker had paid three silver marks, and the drowned fisherman's widow came Thursday, and — and there it was, the truth under all his fine excuses: he couldn't bear to silence them. Their voices were the only company he'd kept since Maren. Ten years of hiding, and he had hidden *with the dead*, because the living had become too dangerous to love.

"You're going to be the death of me," he told Sefa quietly. "You understand that? Whoever you are. Whatever's under your skin. You walked into the one house in Vaelmoor that can't afford you."

She smiled at him over her shoulder. It was a disarming smile, gap-toothed and bright, and for one warm moment he almost believed they might manage. Might teach her words and manners and pass her off as a niece from the country. Might—

The whistles began.

Three notes, repeated. He knew them the way he knew his own heartbeat: the Pale Office patrol-call, the one that meant *close the doors and ask nothing*. From his cracked shutter he saw them come around the corner of Tanner's Row — four of them in gray-and-white, lanterns still lit despite the dawn, moving with the unhurried purpose of men following a scent rather than a report.

They stopped three doors down. At the Widow Marsh's house. The widow who had bought a charm two nights ago, whose husband's voice had perhaps sung out in the night, unprompted, answering a call no one else could hear.

One of the whistlers turned. Looked up the street, slow and deliberate, past every door.

Past his.

Isandro let the shutter fall and stood in the dimness with his heart going like a hammer, and felt a small cold hand slip into his. He looked down. Sefa gazed up at him, calm as deep water, and gave his fingers the smallest squeeze.

"They're not here for you," he whispered, not believing a word of it. "Not yet. Not yet."

But the bones on the wall had begun, very faintly, to hum.
