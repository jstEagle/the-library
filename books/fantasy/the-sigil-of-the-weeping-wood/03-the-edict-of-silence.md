## 3. The Edict of Silence

The morning after the child arrived, Isandro did the sensible thing and pretended the whole night had been a dream.

This was easier than it should have been, because Sefa — he had decided to call her that, since *the child* made him feel like a character in a cautionary ballad and she had offered no name of her own, only a hum like wind through a keyhole — slept until nearly noon in his spare room, curled around one of his bone blanks as if it were a doll.

He stood over her for a long moment with a cup of barley tea going cold in his hand. She was maybe eight years old. Mud had dried in the creases of her knuckles like old mortar. Her breathing was slow and even, and every few breaths a sound escaped her — not a snore, not quite. A resonance. It moved through the little room and settled into the shelf of unfinished charms above the bed, where three cow-bone pendants trembled on their leather cords without any draft to move them.

"Right," Isandro said quietly, to no one. "Absolutely right. Nothing strange about this at all."

He went downstairs and opened his shop.

*Isandro Coldharbour, Carver of Keepsakes,* said the sign, and it was even mostly true. He carved buttons, combs, chess pieces, the occasional walking-stick handle for gentlemen who wanted to look more interesting than they were. That was the honest trade. The other trade lived in a locked box under the floorboards: forty-odd charms of bone and binding-salt, each one holding an echo of a dead voice. A grandmother's recipe recited from memory. A soldier's joke, told badly, told twice. Ten years of small crimes, sold quietly to people whose grief was bigger than their fear of the Edict.

The Edict of Silence was thirteen years old now, older than the girl sleeping upstairs. Isandro had been twenty-four when the mages fell and the Pale Office rose, and in those thirteen years he had learned exactly two things worth knowing: how to hide what you are, and how much it costs you to do it well.

His first customer of the day was a fishmonger named Doss who wanted a pipe carved from riverbone, and who talked for eleven straight minutes about his gout while Isandro nodded and shaped the bowl. This was another thing ten years of hiding taught you: patience. You could carve a man's voice into bone while he complained about his feet, and he'd never know, because nobody looks at a quiet man's hands.

It was while Doss was still there, mid-rant about whether pickled herring aggravated or soothed (he argued both sides with great conviction), that Isandro heard the knock.

Not the shop bell. The back door. Three knocks, evenly spaced, patient as a headstone.

Doss kept talking. Isandro set down his knife. Through the thin wall he felt it again — that pressure, that vast green attention pressing against the back of his skull like a thumb against a bruise. The child was awake.

"I'll just see to that," he said.

"—because my mother swore by vinegar, but vinegar's for *pickles*, isn't it—"

"Doss." Isandro smiled the smile he used on widows and bailiffs alike. "Come back tomorrow."

* * *

The knock came again as he reached the back room, and then a voice he knew better than his own reflection.

"Isandro. Open the door. I can hear you breathing."

For one absurd heartbeat he considered climbing out the window. Then he unbolted the door, and Aldric Vane filled it the way he always had — tall, broad, taking up more space than politeness required, rain beading on the gray collar of a Pale Office coat.

Thirteen years ago they had been apprentices together under old Master Corvess, two boys carving practice runes into stolen fence posts, Aldric all ambition and loud laughter, Isandro all careful hands and quiet jokes. Thirteen years ago Aldric had worn carpenter's aprons. Now he wore the pale gray of the Office, and the Office's badge at his throat: a circle with a line struck through it. Silence, made official.

"Aldric," Isandro said. "You're dripping on my floor."

"Your floor's seen worse. I remember when you dropped an entire pot of binding lacquer down your own trousers."

"That was *you*, actually."

"It was, wasn't it." Aldric smiled, and for a moment the smile was the old one, wide and crooked, and Isandro's chest hurt with something that was almost nostalgia and mostly dread. "May I come in? Officially I'm inspecting charm-vendors in the Lantern Quarter. Unofficially, I'm soaked, hungry, and I'd sell my left boot for some of your terrible tea."

"You'd sell your left boot anyway. You have terrible taste in boots." Isandro stepped aside. "Come in."

Lying to a friend, he would think later, turns out to be exactly like carving. The first cut is the hard one. After that, your hands simply keep moving.

* * *

Aldric sat in the back room with his boots steaming by the stove, eating bread and cheese with the enthusiasm of a man raised poor, and Isandro watched him the way a man watches weather.

"So," Aldric said, mouth full. "Charm-vendors. You'd be amazed what people try to sell out here. Love charms made of pigeon bones. Warding sigils that are honestly just scribbles. One fellow was selling 'dragon teeth' that I'm fairly sure were turnips."

"What did you do to him?"

"Confiscated his stock. Ate half of it. It *was* turnips." He brushed crumbs from his coat and looked up, and the humor drained out of his face with the smoothness of long practice. "Isandro. There's been a report."

"Has there."

"Last night. A woman on Tanner's Row says she saw a girl run past her door near midnight. Barefoot. Covered in mud. And this woman — she's a busybody, we've had complaints about her before — swears the girl was humming. Says the sound went through her like a cold wind. Says her husband's old watch, which hasn't ticked in six years, started ticking."

Isandro poured tea with steady hands. Steady hands were the whole trick of his life. "People say anything," he said. "Half this city hears ghosts in their kettles. Grief does that."

"Grief does," Aldric agreed. "But the Office doesn't send me out for grief. They sent me because of *where* the girl came from." He turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "The north road. The Weeping Wood."

Outside, rain ticked against the shutters. Upstairs — faintly, so faintly only a man whose whole life was listening could catch it — something hummed.

"The Wood's been dying since the Sundering," Isandro said. "There's nothing in it but rot and Office patrols. Nobody comes *from* there."

"Somebody did last night." Aldric looked at him. His eyes were the same warm brown they'd always been, and that was the worst part, that cruelty could live behind such ordinary kindness. "If a child came out of that wood, Isandro — a wood-touched child — you know what happens to her. You know what the law is. So I need you to tell me, friend to friend, and I'll ask it plainly: have you seen her?"

And there it was. The first cut.

Isandro thought about Maren, and the memory came with its usual weight, and underneath the weight something new and sharp: *this is the man who sat across from her judges and said nothing.* No. Not nothing. He had said what saved himself, and what he said had helped hang her. Isandro had never learned the exact words. He had never asked, because part of him had needed to keep believing there was an innocent explanation, and part of him had known there wasn't.

"No," Isandro said. "I haven't seen her."

The lie went out of him clean as a shaving off fresh bone. Aldric held his eyes a moment longer — searching, Isandro thought, not for guilt but for permission to stop looking — and then nodded and looked away first.

"All right," Aldric said. "All right. Good."

They sat in silence for a while, two men pretending it was comfortable. Then Aldric laughed suddenly, without much humor in it. "Do you remember when Corvess caught us carving luck-sigils into the fence posts? He beat us both with his belt and then made us dinner. Said magic wasn't the sin — hiding was. And then the Edict came, and he burned everything he ever made, and a year later he was dead, and I—" He stopped. Started again. "I took the Office's coin, Isandro. Someone had to feed my mother. That's what I told myself. I still tell myself."

"I know why you did it."

"Do you?" Aldric's voice cracked, just slightly, on the second word. He recovered fast, too fast, the way men do when they've practiced recovery. He stood, buttoned his coat, became an interrogator again from the boots up. "I'll be in Vaelmoor a week. Checking vendors. If you hear anything — humming, odd weather, clocks behaving strangely — send word to the Lantern post. Not for your sake. For hers. Whatever that child is, the people above me aren't gentle, and I'd rather find her than let them."

He paused at the door.

"She'd have liked this tea," he said quietly. "Maren. She always said yours tasted like hot dishwater, but she drank three cups whenever she visited."

Then he was gone into the rain, and Isandro stood alone in his back room with his heart hammering and the taste of his first lie sitting sour on his tongue.

* * *

That night he didn't sleep. Instead he sat by the banked stove and remembered, because the day had torn the scab off and the remembering came whether he wanted it or not.

---

Thirteen years ago, the Weeping Wood had been the pride of the northern kingdoms. A forest with a mind — the old treaties called it the Covenant, sworn between the trees and mortal folk before anyone alive was born: the wood would lend its magic to those who asked honestly, and mortals would take nothing without asking. Generations grew up trading songs for blessings at the Covenant Stone. Farmers left offerings of bread and blood at the treeline. Children were promised to the wood as godparents, in the old way.

And then the mage-lords of Vaelmoor decided the covenant was an inconvenience.

Nobody ever learned the full truth of the Sundering — the Office saw to that — but everyone knew the shape of it. The mages wanted the wood's power without its price, and they tried to take it, all of it at once, with engines of iron and salt. The forest fought back. What followed was three days and nights of screaming timber and black rain, and when it ended, half the Weeping Wood stood gray and dead, weeping sap like blood, and the other half had gone silent and strange. The mages who survived the backlash needed someone to blame. Magic itself was convenient. The Edict of Silence followed within the year, and the Pale Office with it, and the purges began.

They called them hearings. Isandro had gone to one. Maren's.

She'd been a hedge-charmer, nothing grand — wart-cures, birthing blessings, little knots of protection stitched into baby blankets. She'd also been married to a man who carved voices into bone, though neither of us knew how much that mattered until the end. She'd stood in the white courtroom in her plain brown dress with her chin up, and Aldric had sat at the clerks' table in his new gray coat, and afterward they hadn't come home. They'd taken her to the gallows-tree at the wood's edge, because the Office liked its symbolism tidy, and Isandro had been made to watch, along with forty other neighbors, as the most dangerous thing in Vaelmoor — a kind woman who could coax a fever out of a child — was hanged from a branch that had once sung lullabies to kings.

He had kept one of her charms. Of course he had. The one with her laugh on it, pressed into bone the winter before, back when keeping such things was merely illegal rather than fatal. Some nights he held it and listened until he could sleep. Most nights he couldn't bear to touch it at all.

Ten years. He had hidden for ten years after losing her, selling scraps of other people's dead and hoarding his own, calling it survival, calling it prudence, calling it anything but cowardice.

---

Upstairs, the floor creaked.

Isandro climbed the stairs and found Sefa standing at the window, small hands on the sill, watching the rain. When he entered she turned, and her eyes caught the streetlight and held it — amber, deep, the exact color of sap.

"They came," she said. Or didn't say. Her mouth never moved. The words arrived inside his skull the way sounds arrive through a wall — muffled, unmistakable. "Gray men. The one with the sad face. You lied for me."

"Yes," Isandro said.

"Why?"

Because you remind me of everything I failed to protect. Because lying to him was the easiest thing I've done in ten years and the hardest thing I've done in ten years, both at once. Because —

"Because it was the right thing to do," he said, which was simpler than the truth and true besides.

Sefa studied him for a long time with that vegetal patience, that slow green attention that made him feel less like a man than like soil being considered for planting. Then she crossed the room, took his hand — her palm cool and faintly rough, like bark worn smooth — and pressed it flat against her own chest.

Through the contact it came again, that vastness: roots deeper than kingdoms, seasons turning like pages, a million leaves remembering light. But beneath it, threaded through it, he felt something else now. Something that made his stomach drop.

Fear. The wood was afraid. Something in the deep places of it was waking, and waking wrong, and reaching for the girl the way a drowning man reaches for a rope.

And somewhere far off, in the direction of the Lantern post, a whistle sounded. Then another. Then the bells — the Office's night bells, the ones that meant a search, a sweep, a door-to-door hunt.

Sefa looked up at him. In her silence, in her sap-colored eyes, was a question older than language:

*What will you do now?*

Isandro Coldharbour, liar, coward, carver of keepsakes, looked at the child the dead forest had sent to his door, and heard himself answer before he understood he'd decided anything at all.

"Pack nothing," he said. "We leave tonight."
