## 4. What the Bones Remember

Before, she was many.

She was the roots that held the hill like fingers hold a plum. She was the moss on the north side of everything. She was four hundred years of rain moving through leaf and loam, slow as honey, patient as stone. She was a grove of silver-barked trees at the edge of the Weeping Wood, and she had never once been lonely, because to be a grove is to be a thousand selves agreeing to stand in the same place.

Then there was fire, and iron, and men in gray who sang a song of cutting.

And then—

*Then* was small. *Then* was two hands and one mouth that would not work and a heart going fast as a rabbit's. *Then* was mud and running and a city that smelled wrong in every direction at once.

Sefa did not know which of these she was. That was the trouble. That was the whole trouble, folded up small enough to fit inside a girl's ribs, and it hurt worse than the cutting had.

* * *

The man's house smelled of bone and tallow and old tea.

She had come to it through the wound — because that is what the city was, she understood this immediately, the way you understand your own hand is your own: Vaelmoor was a wound in the world, a place where something living had been cut away and the edges had never healed, only scabbed over with cobbles and roofs and the smoke of ten thousand fires. The streets were scar tissue. The people walking them were cells trying to remember what they had been before the injury, and failing, mostly, but Sefa could feel them trying. A baker kneading dough reached for something green without knowing it. A woman hanging washing out under a gray sky tilted her face to the light exactly the way a leaf does.

They didn't know. That was the saddest part. The wound had been so old that even the body of the city had forgotten it was ever not a wound.

The man with the bones was different.

She had found him by listening — not with ears. Ears were new and confusing and mostly reported things like carts and arguments and the terrible wet noise of her own breathing. She listened with the inside of herself, the part that was still grove, still root, still the slow agreement of standing things. And in all the loud gray wound of the city, one small voice sang *honest*.

It came from his hands.

He kept little white pieces in boxes, and when he touched them they hummed. Not loudly. Not the way the Office-men's iron sang, which was a scream flattened into silence. These hummed like — she searched her four hundred years for the right memory — like sap rising. Like spring deciding. The man carried death around in little carved pieces, and somehow the death in them was gentle, was *kept*, was loved. She had never felt anything like it. In the wood, death was a door the trees walked through and came back from wearing different shapes. Here, death was shut in boxes, and someone had whispered to it until it stopped being afraid.

So she climbed his stairs and knocked on his door, because honest things belong near each other. This seemed obvious. It still seemed obvious. She could not understand why he looked so frightened when he opened it, as though a child arriving muddy in the rain were an omen and not simply correct.

* * *

Humans ate too fast. This was another thing she had learned. They put food into themselves as if they were putting out a fire, and then they looked surprised afterward, as if the food had done something to them instead of the other way around.

The man — Isandro, Isandro, the name tasted like cold river stones — gave her bread and soup and watched her eat with a face full of weather. His face did a great many things. Grove-faces did not do anything; a tree's opinion of a storm takes a season to express. But this man's face was like a bird, flickering from worry to kindness to worry again, and Sefa found she liked watching it. It was like watching leaves decide about wind.

His hands interested her most. Scarred, ink-stained at the creases, always moving — turning, measuring, touching the table as though checking it was still there. Hands that made things. Hands that had spent years whispering to dead things so they wouldn't be lonesome.

*Who are you?* he asked her, with his mouth, in the language of the wound-city.

But her mouth was broken. Or not broken — that was the horror of it — her mouth was *working correctly*, and what came out of it was not words. It was the sound of the grove. When she tried to say *my name is Sefa*, what came out was a low note with wind in it, a chord, a sound like a hillside exhaling, and the man went white as birchwood and stepped backward and knocked over a chair.

The bones in their boxes answered her.

She heard them — felt them — singing up through the shelf, rattling faintly in their drawers, every carved charm in the house turning toward her like flowers toward sun. Her sound went into them and they *rang*. One of them, a small rib-white piece shaped like a leaf, hummed so hard it walked itself to the edge of its box and fell off, and Isandro caught it with a reflex so quick it must have been older than his fear.

"What," he said. Then, quieter, in a voice she felt more than heard: "What *are* you?"

And that was the question, wasn't it. That was the exact shape of the thing lodged in her chest like a splinter the size of a forest.

She was a grove that remembered being a grove. She was a girl who had woken in the burned ash of her own roots with hands and hunger and a heartbeat that panicked. She was neither and both and the space between, and the space between had teeth. At night — both nights she had been a girl, huddled under a fish-cart, then in this warm bone-smelling room — she dreamed in rings. Not pictures: rings. Years laid down inside her like the circles in cut timber, and somewhere in the middle of the rings there was a dark place, a hole, a year that had been cut out, and she could not look at it. Every time she turned her memory toward it, it turned away, the way you cannot see the back of your own head.

She had been *cut*. Something had been taken from the middle of her, and the taking had left her small and singular and terrified, and she suspected — with the slow dread of sap in winter — that whatever had been removed was the part that knew how to be many. How to be *enough*. Because being one felt like starving. Being one felt like a single tree trying to be a forest, pouring everything it had into its own one trunk, and she could feel herself thinning, feel the rings going narrow, and she did not know how to stop.

She looked at Isandro across the ruined chair and the cooling soup, and she decided something with the part of her that was still a grove making decisions slowly and forever.

*Him,* she decided. *This one.*

* * *

That night she did not sleep so much as sink.

Sleeping was strange. Trees did not sleep; they dimmed, they wintered, they drew inward and waited. But a girl-body demanded sleep the way it demanded food, rudely and constantly, and when she gave in, she dropped through layers like a stone through pond-water, and at the bottom there were memories waiting.

She remembered being a grove.

Not seeing — groves do not see — but *knowing*, the way you know your own limbs in the dark. She knew the fox denned beneath her easternmost root, and she let it, because the fox's dreams seeped down through the earth and were pleasant to hold. She knew the mosses by name, all nine hundred of them, the way a mother knows children. She knew the taste of lightning coming three breaths before it struck, a copper feeling in the air, and she would push her trees' roots deep and hold on. She remembered the pilgrims, once, long ago, before the Edict, before the gray men — humans who came to the wood's edge and pressed their palms to bark and said thank you, thank you, for no reason except gratitude, and the wood had drunk their thanks like rain.

She remembered the covenant. Not its words — those lived deeper than memory, in the cut-away dark place — but its *weight*. A promise between the standing people and the walking people, old as either, humming along every root in the world like a plucked string that never quite stops. She remembered that the string had been cut. She remembered the sound it made when it broke.

It sounded like the gray men's song of cutting, but bigger. It sounded like the sky clearing its throat.

And underneath all of it, threaded through everything like mycelium through soil, she remembered Maren.

A woman. Warm-handed. Smelling of bread smoke and lamp oil and fear. Maren had come to the grove in the last bad days, when the fire was close enough to taste, carrying something wrapped in oilcloth against her chest the way you carry a baby or a coal you must not let die. And Maren had knelt in the ash and pressed her forehead to a root and wept, and the weeping had gone down into Sefa like water, and Sefa had — she could almost see it, almost —

Almost. The dark ring. The cut-out year. The memory turned its back on her and she woke with her heart hammering and leaves — actual leaves, small green ones, three of them — growing from the skin of her forearm.

She sat very still in the dark room and looked at them.

They were pretty. That was the worst part. They were perfect little leaves, veined and tender, and they grew out of her the way hair grows out of anyone, naturally, inevitably, as if her body had simply decided that this is what bodies are for. She touched one and it felt like touching herself, which it was, which raised questions she had no way to ask.

Across the room, Isandro slept badly in a chair, his head tipped back, his scarred hands loose in his lap. Even asleep he held one of the bone charms, curled around it like a man holding a rope over a long drop. And the charm — she could hear it even now, faintly, faithfully — hummed to him. Held him. Kept some voice in it safe against his chest while he slept, the way the grove used to keep the fox's dreams.

*Sings honest,* she thought, in the wordless green way she thought. *Even asleep. Even grieving. He carries his dead gently.*

She did not know yet whose voice it was. She would learn. But she already knew the shape of the grief: it was the same grief the wood had felt when the first axe bit, the grief of loving something that has been taken. It rang in him at the same pitch. Two instruments, same broken string.

She lay back down. The three leaves brushed the blanket and she pretended — with the enormous dignity of the very young and the very old — that they were ordinary, that she was ordinary, that tomorrow would be an ordinary day in the wound-city with the honest-handed man.

* * *

Morning brought the whistles.

She heard them first in her teeth — iron-song, flat and screaming, the sound the gray men used to cut the world's hearing off. Down in the street below Isandro's window, figures in pale coats moved through the market crowd, and everywhere they passed, the city flinched. Shopkeepers became very busy. Children were pulled indoors. A whole streetful of wounded cells pretending harder than ever that they had never been anything but a wound.

Isandro pulled her back from the window with a gentleness that shook.

"It's all right," he said, which was a lie, and both of them knew it was a lie, and something in the shared knowledge of the lie — the honesty *inside* the dishonesty — made her trust him more, strangely, not less. A tree knows when a storm is lying about passing. What matters is whether the person beside you knows it too.

He fed her. He talked to her in his low voice, telling her things she half understood — names, dangers, a word he repeated that made his jaw go tight: *Office, Office, Office.* He asked her questions with his eyes that his mouth didn't dare.

And she tried to answer him. She tried with everything she had, gathering up the memory of the burning, the gray men, the covenant snapping like a root in frost, the woman Maren kneeling in ash — gathering it all and pushing it through her broken mouth toward him, *here, take it, understand me —*

What came out was a sound like a whole hillside remembering fire.

Every bone charm in the house began to sing at once. The windows buzzed. Somewhere below, a dog howled, and then another, and the whistles in the street stopped.

Stopped, and turned.

Isandro stood in the middle of his singing shop, grey-faced, looking at her with an expression she had seen once before — on the face of a deer, in the last summer, the day the fire crossed the ridge. Not fear exactly. Knowledge. The animal understanding, arrived all at once, that the world had just become larger and more dangerous than it was built for.

"Sefa," he said — using the name she had pushed at him, the only piece of herself her mouth could still carry — "what did you just show me?"

Below, in the street, a whistle blew three short notes. Then three more. Then three again. A pattern. A summons.

And Isandro Coldharbour closed his eyes, as if he had known all along that this morning would come, and said, very quietly, to himself or to the bones or to whoever keeps accounts of such things:

"They're calling for me."
