## 5. The Pale Office Calls

The summons came on a Tuesday, which Isandro would later decide was the Pale Office's idea of a joke. Nobody's life ended on a Sunday. Sundays were for quiet dread, for sitting by the window and rehearsing your lies. Tuesdays were when they actually came for you.

The letter was delivered by a boy of perhaps fourteen, dressed in the gray-and-white livery that made him look like a piece of overcast weather given legs. He stood in Isandro's doorway holding out an envelope sealed with bone-white wax, and he had the particular expression of someone who has been told not to make eye contact with the recipients of such letters, on the grounds that it unsettles them, and unsettled recipients are harder to escort.

"Isandro Coldharbour," the boy read off the envelope, though he was looking at Isandro's chin.

"That's me."

"You're to appear before the Office of Edict Enforcement at the fourth bell tomorrow afternoon." The boy recited it like a prayer he'd been made to memorize and didn't believe in. "Inquiry is preliminary in nature. You are not under formal accusation."

"Am I under informal accusation?"

"I'm just supposed to give you the letter, sir."

* * *

That evening, Sefa sat cross-legged on his workbench — she had claimed the workbench as her territory within two days of arriving, and Isandro had surrendered it without a fight, partly because she looked so small up there among the shavings and partly because the alternative was explaining to a mute child why she couldn't sit where she liked.

He told her about the summons. He didn't know why; she couldn't answer, and half the time he wasn't sure how much of his speech she understood. But her eyes did something when he talked — a slow, attentive stillness, like a deer deciding whether you were a threat or merely furniture — and after ten years of talking mostly to bones, he found he'd developed a taste for an audience.

"The Pale Office," he said, working a whetstone along a carving knife he had no intention of using but which felt good in his hand, "is the reason your soup has no flavor. They tax salt. Did you know that? Everything in this city is gray or taxed, and usually both."

Sefa tilted her head.

"They were priests once," he went on. "Before the Sundering. Keepers of the old rites, back when the Weeping Wood still stood whole and magic was a thing you did at harvest festivals and funerals and nobody thought twice. And then thirteen years ago the wood broke, and something terrible came out of the breaking — nobody agrees on what — and the priests decided the safest way to keep it from ever happening again was to outlaw everything. Every charm, every cantrip, every hedge-remedy and bone-whisper. All of it." He turned the knife. "They call it the Edict of Silence. Which is a very elegant name for telling people they're not allowed to hear their dead mothers anymore."

Sefa reached out and touched one of his blank carving blanks — plain bone, unenchanted, the stock he kept visible for exactly this kind of conversation. Her fingers rested on it, and Isandro watched, as he had watched before, with a feeling like standing too close to a cliff edge, as the bone gave the faintest hum. Not sound, exactly. More like the memory of sound. Like pressing your hand against a door and feeling singing on the other side.

She pulled her fingers back quickly, as if caught.

"It's all right," he said quietly. "It does that around you. I don't know why."

But of course he had begun, in the nights since her arrival, to form theories. Theories shaped like vast green things.

* * *

The Office of Edict Enforcement occupied what had once been the Cathedral of the Verdant Choir, which was the sort of irony Isandro suspected the Pale Office enjoyed. They had stripped the stained glass, whitewashed the walls, and filled the great nave with filing cabinets. Where the altar had stood there was now a desk, and behind the desk sat Aldric Vane, looking like a man who had spent thirteen years trying to wear a uniform that fit and had finally succeeded through sheer stubbornness.

"Well," Aldric said. "Look what the tide dragged in."

"You sent the tide."

"I send a lot of tides. Most of them come back emptier than they left." Aldric gestured to the chair across from his desk. It was a good chair. That was the first thing Isandro noticed — the interrogation chairs in the stories were always cruel, spindly things designed to communicate suffering. This chair was padded. Comfortable, even. Aldric wanted him relaxed. That was worse.

"Aldric Vane," Isandro said, settling into it. "Last time I saw you, you were stealing apples from Brother Callow's orchard and blaming a ghost."

"The ghost took the blame very gracefully, as I recall." Aldric folded his hands. Up close, the uniform sat well on him but his face hadn't quite agreed to it. There were lines around his mouth that hadn't been earned by smiling. "Ten years since you came to my promotion dinner. You drank my wine, insulted my colleagues, and left before the toast."

"I had inventory."

"At midnight."

"Bone doesn't keep office hours."

Something flickered across Aldric's face — the ghost of the boy who'd blamed ghosts, surfacing briefly through the interrogator like a fish rising in gray water. Then it submerged.

"This won't take long," Aldric said. "A routine inquiry. There have been... irregularities reported in the Lower Tanneries district. Unlicensed charm-work. Someone selling voice-charms to the bereaved."

"How dreadful."

"Mm. The informant described the seller as 'a lean man, middle-aged, hands like a bird's nest.'"

"I'll keep an eye out for him."

"Isandro." Aldric's voice dropped, and for a moment he was not an officer of the Pale Office but a man in a room with an old friend, tired down to the marrow. "I requested this inquiry personally. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means you wanted to see me."

"It means that if anyone else had taken this file, there would be three officers at your door tonight instead of one friend at his desk, and your shop would be turned inside out until they found whatever it is they'd find." He let that sit. "So I'm going to ask you questions, and you're going to answer them, and then you're going to walk out of here and we will both pretend this was pleasant. Can you do that?"

Isandro could hear his own heartbeat. Somewhere in the building, a clerk laughed at something. "Ask your questions."

* * *

The interview lasted two hours, and it was conducted with the courtesy of a chess match between people who love each other and are both trying very hard not to say so.

Aldric asked about his suppliers. Isandro answered. Aldric asked about his customers. Isandro answered, omitting only the ones who were real. Aldric asked where he'd been on the night of the ninth, and Isandro said home, alone, carving, and watched Aldric write down *home, alone, carving* in a hand so neat it looked printed, and understood that Aldric knew it was a lie and was writing it down anyway, carefully, the way you handle something fragile you intend later to break.

And through all of it, Isandro felt the presence of the wall.

It was behind Aldric, running the length of the former chancel: a display of mounted relics, each one labeled in precise clerical script. Petrified branches of the Weeping Wood. Isandro had seen pictures of them in sanctioned histories — dead wood, gray stone-stuff, trophies of the victory over wild magic.

Except these weren't quite petrified. As the afternoon light shifted through the high windows, Isandro saw it: a bead of moisture gathering at the tip of the largest branch. Slowly. Impossibly. A drop of sap, dark as ink, swelling and falling onto the polished floor, where a stain had clearly accumulated over years. Someone dusted around it. Someone had been ordered to dust around it and hated it.

The branch was weeping. Thirteen years after its death, mounted on a wall in a whitewashed cathedral, it was weeping.

"Aldric," Isandro said, as lightly as he could manage, "your wall is dripping."

Aldric didn't turn around. "Yes."

"Petrified wood doesn't do that."

"No," Aldric agreed. "It doesn't."

Silence stretched between them, taut as wire. Isandro looked at the branch again — at all the branches, a dozen of them, and now that he really looked, he saw the faint sheen of moisture on each one, the dark stains beneath, the careful labels. *Recovered, year seven. Recovered, year nine.* They were cataloguing them. Collecting them. Keeping them alive in captivity and watching them weep.

"Why?" Isandro asked.

"Because three years ago," Aldric said, and his voice had gone flat, official, the voice of a man reading from a document he wished didn't exist, "the Office recovered a specimen that was not petrified. Living tissue. Green wood, growing, found in a drainage culvert forty miles north of the city. Since then, further specimens. Increasingly frequent. Increasingly far south." He met Isandro's eyes. "The Weeping Wood is regrowing, Isandro. Somewhere out there, something is putting down roots. And the Edict exists precisely because we do not know what grew out of that wood the last time it woke."

"And you think—"

"I think," Aldric said carefully, "that whoever is helping it would need to be someone who works with the old materials. Bone. Voice. Memory." He paused. "I also think that a child was reported missing from the Tanneries district nine days ago. No family came forward. No records. And I think it's strange, Isandro, that in all our inquiries, no one in your neighborhood seems to remember seeing any child at your address. At all. As if the neighbors can't quite recall. As if something about the house makes them look away."

Isandro kept his face still. Inside, cold spread outward from his sternum. Sefa. Whatever she was, whatever she did to perception, she was doing it to his entire street, and it wouldn't hold forever.

"There's no child," he said.

"I know there's no child," Aldric said. "There's officially no child. I wrote it down myself." He closed the file in front of him, squared its edges, and set down his pen with a click that sounded final. "Here's what happens now. This inquiry concludes with insufficient evidence. You'll receive a letter in a week. But Isandro—" and now his voice cracked, just slightly, just enough to show the man underneath the uniform, "—whatever you're carrying, whatever it costs you, put it down. Walk away from it. Because I have read the internal directives, and I'm telling you as a friend: if this goes above my head, there will be no chair this comfortable, and no one asking nicely. There are people in this building who remember the Sundering firsthand, and they are *afraid*, and frightened institutions with fire-pots do not distinguish between a forest and a little girl."

For a moment, Isandro almost told him. Almost laid it all out — the child on his doorstep, the humming bones, the vast green memory under her skin. Thirteen years of friendship pressed against the back of his teeth.

Then he saw it, in his mind's eye: Aldric at Maren's hearing. Aldric in his fine new uniform, testifying, his voice steady, his eyes anywhere but the accused. He had never learned the full truth of what Aldric had said that day. He had never asked. Some doors you leave shut because you suspect what's behind them, and you're not ready to lose the person the door belongs to.

"Thank you for your concern, Interrogator Vane," Isandro said, and stood.

Aldric watched him for a long moment. Then he slid something across the desk — a small card, plain white, an address written on it in that same printed-neat hand. "My personal residence. If you ever need to talk to someone who isn't wearing a uniform." He paused. "The offer doesn't expire."

Isandro took the card because refusing it would have been louder than taking it.

At the door of the nave, he stopped. He couldn't help himself. He turned and looked back at the wall of weeping branches, at Aldric sitting motionless before them like a penitent before an altar of the god his own office had killed.

"Aldric," he said. "The branch on the end. The small one. It's still alive, you know. Whatever they did to petrify the others — it didn't take on that one. It's growing. Look at the third knot from the base. New wood."

Aldric went very still.

"I thought you should know," Isandro said, "since apparently nobody else in this building can tell the difference between a trophy and a prisoner."

He walked out into the gray afternoon without waiting for an answer, and the door boomed shut behind him like a verdict.

* * *

He made it four streets before he was sure. Not followed — followed would have been simple, expected, manageable. What he had was worse: a whistle.

It came from somewhere ahead, high and thin, a signal-call in a pattern Isandro didn't recognize but his body did, some animal part of him responding to it the way livestock respond to a slaughterhouse smell. Two notes up, one note held. Then, fainter, from a side street, the answering pattern.

The Pale Office patrolled with whistles. Everyone in Vaelmoor knew their calls the way sailors know weather. But this pattern — this was a search pattern. A closing pattern. And it was converging on his street.

*They know,* he thought, walking faster, keeping his pace civil, unhurried, the pace of an honest craftsman going home to his supper. *Not everything. But they know something.*

By the time he reached his own door, the whistles had faded — called off, or redirected, or simply waiting — but his hands were shaking so badly he dropped the key twice. Inside, the shop smelled of bone dust and lamp oil, and Sefa was asleep on his workbench, curled among the shavings like a cat in a woodpile, her small chest rising and falling.

As he watched her breathe, one of the finished charms hanging from the drying rack began, very softly, to sing.

It was a widow's charm. He'd made it six days ago, filled it with a dead fisherman's last words — nothing important, *mind the nets, love, mind the nets* — and it had hung silent ever since, waiting for delivery. Now it resonated, gently, persistently, in time with the child's breathing, and Isandro stood in his own doorway listening to a dead man murmur about nets while a living girl slept in a language older than kingdoms, and understood, with total clarity, that he had perhaps a week. Perhaps less.

Above the rooftops, through the grimy window, the moon was rising. Thin. New.

He counted without meaning to. One.

Six weeks to the thirteenth moon. Six weeks, a hunted child, a road north through country where the trees hanged people, and an old friend who had just promised, with tears in his voice, to burn him alive if the orders came down.

Isandro closed the door gently, sat down at his bench beside the sleeping girl, and picked up his knife.

If the work was going to end, it would end with clean edges.
