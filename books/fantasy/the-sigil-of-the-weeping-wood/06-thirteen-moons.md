## 6. Thirteen Moons

Mother Quill lived at the top of Candlewick Stair, in a house that leaned east the way old women lean into wind, as if all her ninety-odd years had been spent listening to something just out of earshot and she'd grown crooked from the habit. Isandro had bought charms from her once — not carved ones, nothing illegal, just dried feverfew and tallow candles that burned without smoke — back when Maren was alive and they'd both pretended the shop below their rooms was only a bone-carver's.

He hadn't climbed those stairs in thirteen years.

The stairwell smelled of mutton fat and dried yarrow and, underneath it, something older and greener, like moss growing in the dark of a well. Sefa rode on his hip, which she had decided was her right somewhere in the last two days, her thin arms looped around his neck with the unselfconscious grip of a child who has already lost everything and therefore cannot be embarrassed by anything. She hummed against his shoulder. His coat pocket, where three finished charms sat wrapped in wool, hummed back.

"Stop that," he murmured to his pocket. "You'll wake the neighbors."

The neighbors were one deaf cobbler and a cat with opinions. But it felt better to say it aloud than to admit what the resonance actually did to him — the way each note slid through his ribs like a hand feeling for a door he'd nailed shut.

He knocked. The knock was answered by silence, then shuffling, then a voice like gravel poured through honey: "If you're from the Office, I'm eighty-six, half-blind, and I keep a goose in the cellar who has never once been fed. Come in and see how fast an old woman can lie."

"It's Isandro Coldharbour, Mother Quill."

A long pause. Then, warmer: "Coldharbour. Well. The goose is a lie too, but come in anyway. It's been so long I assumed you were dead or respectable."

* * *

Her room was a museum of careful illegality. Bundles of herbs hung from every beam — most of them ordinary, some of them not. A mortar and pestle wore a groove in the table where decades of grinding had worn the wood to silk. And on the windowsill, arranged with the tenderness people usually reserved for icons, sat seven small stones, each one faintly green at its heart.

Isandro looked at them and felt the floor tilt.

"You're a hedge-witch," he said stupidly.

"I'm a widow who sells cough syrup," Mother Quill said, "and I am also, since you've apparently decided to say dangerous things aloud in my home, exactly what you just called me." She squinted at him. Her eyes were milk-pale, clouded with age, but they found him with unsettling precision. "Thirteen years, Coldharbour. Thirteen years I've watched you walk past my window with your collar up and your hands in your pockets, and I never said a word, because a hedge-witch knows better than anyone that survival looks like cowardice from the outside. So don't stand there looking scandalized. Sit down before you drop that child."

Sefa, unbidden, slid off Isandro's hip and crossed the room to the windowsill. She stood before the seven green-hearted stones the way pilgrims stand before relics, and began to hum.

The stones answered.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the terrible part. No light blazed, no wind rose. The stones simply brightened — a slow pulse, green deepening to living emerald, like grass remembering rain — and the sound that came out of them was not a sound at all but a *feeling*, a low vast chord that Isandro felt in his back teeth and behind his eyes, and in the three charms in his pocket, which sang along in their small dead voices like choirboys trying to keep up with an organ.

Mother Quill went very still.

"Well," she said, after a while. "Well, well, well." She lowered herself into her chair by feel, her hands shaking only slightly. "I haven't heard the wood speak since I was a girl with mud on my knees. I'd forgotten the pitch of it. Like a bell under water." She turned her blind eyes toward Sefa. "Come here, little one. Let an old woman hear you properly."

Sefa looked at Isandro. He nodded. She crossed to the chair and took Mother Quill's hands, and hummed again — longer this time, winding, rising and falling in phrases that were almost melodies and almost sentences and neither.

Isandro listened the way he always listened, the way ten years of carving had taught him: sorting, parsing, catching the threads of meaning in the weave of sound. He caught fragments. *Stone. Green. Counting.* Something that recurred, over and over, that might have been a number or might have been a moon.

"She keeps saying something about counting," he said. "Or turning. And there's a word she comes back to — I thought it was nonsense, but—"

"It's Old Rootish," Mother Quill said quietly. "The tongue of the Weeping Wood, before the kingdoms put their names on it. My grandmother spoke six words of it and was proud of all six." Her grip tightened on Sefa's fingers. "Child. Say it again. The counting part."

Sefa hummed it. Twelve rising notes, then a fall, then twelve again — and each time the fall landed a little flatter, a little more exhausted, like a runner whose legs are failing.

Mother Quill counted under her breath. Isandro watched her lips move. He saw the moment the arithmetic arrived at its answer, because all the blood left her face at once, leaving her looking like a candle stub.

"Thirteen," she said. "She is singing thirteen moons. Twelve have turned since—" She stopped. Recalculated. Started over. "Since the Sundering. Since they burned the heart of the Weeping Wood and broke the covenant. The wood has been dying for twelve moons' turnings, Coldharbour. Slowly. The way a man drowns slowly in cold water, still moving, still hoping." She swallowed. "The thirteenth moon turns full in six weeks. When it does, if the covenant isn't reforged at the Covenant Stone—" she touched Sefa's cheek with two fingers, gently, the way you touch something already broken — "then the wood dies. All of it. Every root, every seed, everything grown from it, everything bound to it."

"And everything bound to it," Isandro repeated. His mouth had gone dry. "Which is her."

"The child is not *near* the wood, man. The child *is* the wood. What's left of it. The last vessel, the last seed of it wearing skin." Mother Quill's voice cracked and steadied itself with visible effort, the way old voices do, out of long practice. "When the thirteenth moon turns, whatever is in her goes out like a lamp. And I do not know — I truly do not know — whether what's left behind would be a girl, or nothing at all."

* * *

Afterward they sat in the gray afternoon light drinking nettle tea that tasted like punishment, and Isandro laid it all out, because Mother Quill had earned it and because saying things aloud made them real, and real things could be planned around, and planning was the only prayer he knew.

The Covenant Stone stood in the deep heart of the Weeping Wood, four days north of Vaelmoor by road, longer by any route that avoided roads, and every road north was watched now. The Pale Office had garrisons at Millbridge and Thornford. There were posters on the gatehouses — he'd walked past one that morning, pretending to read a bill of fares beside it — offering silver for information regarding "unlicensed practitioners and wood-touched persons," with a woodcut of a child so generic it could have been anybody, which somehow made it worse.

"Six weeks," Isandro said. "Four days' travel. That leaves margin. That's good. That's—" He stopped, because Mother Quill was making a noise that on a younger person would have been a laugh.

"You're going," she said. Not asking.

"I'm considering the logistics of—"

"You're going," she repeated, "the way your wife went, the way all of you quiet heroes go, announcing it to nobody and calling it logistics." She set down her cup. "I'm coming with you as far as Thornford. I have a sister there — dead these nine years, but her daughter isn't, and her daughter owes me a debt and owns a cart. You'll need a cart. You can't carry a child and a bundle and whatever fool bones you're bringing through four days of watched country on foot."

"Mother Quill—"

"Don't *Mother Quill* me. I buried a husband and a son under this Edict, boy. I have spent thirteen years selling cough syrup to people who needed prayers, and diluting the prayers into the syrup so no one could prove anything, and I am *tired*, Isandro. Tired the way firewood is tired when it's finally been given permission to burn." She smiled then, and for a moment the milky eyes were fierce and young. "Besides. Someone has to translate. Your girl here sings in Old Rootish and you carve in dead men's whispers, and neither of you speaks plain sense."

Sefa, who had been sitting on the floor winding a length of yarn around her wrists, looked up at the word *girl*. Then she looked at Isandro. And she hummed — one short phrase, questioning.

He didn't need translation for that one.

"Yes," he said. "We're going."

She considered this. Then she held up the yarn, offering him an end, and they sat in the leaning house on Candlewick Stair playing cat's cradle while an old witch muttered travel lists in the corner, and Isandro thought: *this is the calmest I have felt in ten years, and we are all three of us going to die.* The thought didn't frighten him the way it should have. It felt, instead, like setting down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying.

* * *

He left at dusk, alone, because a bone-carver walking home after dark attracted less notice than three travelers and a cart.

Vaelmoor in the evening rain was at its best and worst simultaneously: lamplight gold on wet cobbles, gutters running black, the Pale Office lanterns at the street corners burning their flat colorless white that made everyone's face look guilty. Isandro kept his head down and his pace unhurried. In his pocket, the charms were silent now. They had been silent since the stones sang — hushed, attentive, like servants listening at a door.

At the corner of Tanner's Row a poster had gone up since morning, fresh paste still gleaming. Same woodcut child. New line added beneath:

*REWARD DOUBLED. THE WOOD IS NOT DEAD. REPORT ALL REGROWTH.*

He stared at it for a long moment. *Regrowth.* So the Office knew. Knew and feared it, enough to double silver, enough to print the thing they'd spent thirteen years insisting was impossible. Somewhere in the Pale Office's tall gray building, men were reading reports of green shoots in dead ground and losing sleep over them.

Good, he thought, and was surprised by the heat of it. Let them lose sleep.

He walked on. Past the shuttered chapel where he and Maren had married. Past the alley mouth where he'd sold his first charm to a dockworker missing a brother. Ten years of small crimes, each one a stone in a wall he'd built between himself and the truth — and now the truth had walked up to his door in the rain, humming, and knocked.

By the time he reached his stairs he had stopped pretending to himself, which was its own strange relief, like lancing a boil. He was going north. He was going to break every law Vaelmoor had, openly, irreversibly, and either reforge a covenant older than kingdoms or die on the road trying.

Upstairs, the room was dark, and someone was sitting in his chair by the window.

Isandro's hand found the knife under his coat before his mind caught up — and then the figure shifted into the streetlight's reach, and he saw the pale uniform gray, the careful face, the scar Aldric Vane had carried since they were boys falling out of the same apple tree.

"Aldric," Isandro said. His voice came out level, which cost him something. "Most friends use the door."

"The door would have woken the child." Aldric didn't rise. His hands were folded, his posture loose, everything about him arranged to look casual, and none of it working. He glanced toward the curtained alcove where Sefa slept. "She's still here, then."

"What child?" said Isandro, and lied to his oldest friend's face for the second time, and hated how easily it came.

Aldric looked at him for a long moment. Outside, the Office lanterns hissed in the rain.

"I'm going to ask you something, Sandro," he said quietly, "and I want you to remember, later, that I asked it here, in your home, off the record, instead of downstairs where the whistles are." He leaned forward. "The Office has reports. Shoots of green in the ash-fields near Harrow Fen. Sap weeping from petrified branches in our own evidence vaults. Things waking up, thirteen years after we were told the wood was dead forever." His voice dropped lower still. "And tomorrow morning, a requisition crosses my desk for a search of Candlewick Stair. An old woman. A hedge-witch. They're going to burn her out at dawn unless someone tells me why I should lose the paperwork."

The rain filled the silence. In his pocket, Isandro felt his charms go utterly, perfectly still — the dead holding their breath.

"So," Aldric said. "Tell me why."
