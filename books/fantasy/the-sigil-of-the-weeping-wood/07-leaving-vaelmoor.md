## 7. Leaving Vaelmoor

The rain had stopped by the time Isandro made his decision, which felt like a small cruelty. He would have preferred weather to match him — a proper storm, thunder rolling over the slate roofs of Vaelmoor like a drum for the condemned. Instead the sky was rinsed clean and pale as milk glass, and the gutters chuckled pleasantly to themselves, and the whole city seemed to have no idea that Isandro Coldharbour was about to commit the largest crime of his quiet, criminal life.

He stood in the back room of his shop with a shovel in one hand and a lamp in the other, looking at the floor.

Under the floorboards lay ten years of voices.

* * *

It had taken him most of the afternoon to dig. The boards came up easily enough; they always had, and he'd oiled them twice a year so they would never squeak and betray him. Beneath them was a cavity lined with waxcloth, and in the cavity, packed in sawdust like a coffin full of sleeping birds, were the charms.

Two hundred and eleven of them. He counted twice, because he could not help himself, because counting was the kind of thing a man did when he wanted to postpone what came after counting.

Each charm was a curl of bone, carved with sigils no living teacher had taught him — Maren's mother's people had made them, before, and Maren had taught him on their kitchen table with flour still on her hands, laughing at how badly he held the knife. Each one held a voice. A last word, or a long goodbye, or an ordinary Tuesday's worth of talking, pressed into bone the way you might press a flower into a book.

There was Widow Anselm's husband, telling her where he'd hidden the deed to the house in Docksward. There was the ferryman Osk, who'd drowned in the Vael with his boots on, singing half a song about a girl from Tarrowmere. There was old Master Corvel, who had paid Isandro double to make sure his voice outlived the Edict, because Corvel had believed — rightly, it turned out — that history would want his testimony someday.

And there was Maren.

Forty-one charms, all told, holding Maren's voice. Not her last words — he had never gotten those, thank every merciful absence; she had been taken from a crowd and he had not even been there, and the Office did not return the dead to their families, not even as ash, not even as an apology. No: these were older recordings. Her humming while she stirred soup. Her reading aloud from a bad novel and doing all the voices badly on purpose. Her saying *Isandro Coldharbour, if you eat the last of the honey bread I will carve your name into the doorframe and everyone will know.* Her laughing at her own threat, which ruined it entirely, which was very like her.

Ten years, he had rationed her like a starving man rations bread. One listening a month, on the dark of the moon. Some months he had cheated. Most months he had cheated.

Now he was going to burn forty of them and keep exactly one, and the arithmetic of that choice sat on his chest like a stone.

"You don't have to," said Sefa.

She was sitting on the workbench with her legs dangling, watching him with those eyes that were too old for her face and somehow too young at the same time. She spoke rarely, and only in that language that made his bones itch in their drawers upstairs — but lately, word by word, she had begun to gather common tongue the way a branch gathers frost. *You don't have to.* Three words, slightly wrong around the edges, absolutely correct in substance.

"I do," Isandro said. "That's rather the point."

She frowned at the hole in the floor. The charms down there were murmuring. They always murmured faintly, two hundred voices seeping through waxcloth and sawdust, a sound like wind through a keyhole, like a house full of sleepers talking in their dreams. Sefa tilted her head, listening, and her frown deepened.

"They're sad," she said. "All of them. Even the happy ones." She looked up at him. "Why?"

Isandro crouched at the edge of the cavity and picked up the topmost charm without looking at its sigil. Widow Anselm's husband again. *Third floorboard from the hearth,* the dead man said patiently, forever patiently, *she'll never think to look there, she thinks I lost it gambling—*

"Because they're finished," Isandro said. "Every voice in this box belongs to someone who stopped mid-story. And someone who loved them paid me to keep the story going." He turned the charm over in his fingers. "I told myself I was a kindness, Sefa. A smuggler of last words. Ten years of telling myself that." He smiled, and the smile tasted like the lie it was. "But a man who hides in a cellar for ten years isn't brave. He's just buried."

Sefa slid off the bench and stood beside him. She smelled, faintly and always, of green things — of sap and crushed leaves, of a spring morning somewhere far north of this city. She put her small hand on his wrist.

"The wood is dying," she said. Careful words, each one placed like a foot on wet stones. "Mother Quill said. Before the thirteenth moon. If it dies—" She touched her own chest, then spread her fingers outward, taking in the room, the street, the sky, everything. "Everything goes quiet forever."

"And you go with it."

"Yes." She said it without drama, the way children say terrible things, and it landed harder than any adult's eloquence ever could. Then she pointed at the box of bones. "So burn them. Burn the sad ones." A pause, and something almost sly moved across her face, some ancient humor surfacing briefly in a child's expression like a fish under ice. "Keep one."

"One," Isandro agreed. His throat was tight. "You're learning to bargain. I've created a monster."

* * *

He built the fire in the courtyard behind the shop, where the neighbors couldn't see over the wall, though at midnight few neighbors were inclined to look. He stacked the charms in an iron basin he'd used for scalding chickens, and he poured lamp oil over them, and his hands shook so badly that Sefa took the bottle out of his grip and finished the job herself with a surgeon's steadiness that no eight-year-old should possess.

"Wait," he said, before she could strike the flint.

He knelt and lifted out the forty-one Maren charms, laying them in rows on a cloth like a merchant displaying wares. Forty-one little curls of bone. He picked each one up, read its sigil, remembered what it held, and put it either in the fire-pile or in the keep-pile, and the sorting nearly broke him in ways he hadn't planned for.

The humming went first. That was easy; he had her humming in his own skull anyway, memory outlasting magic. The soup-stirring song. Gone.

The bad-novel readings. Harder. He had loved those winter evenings more than he had ever told her, and now he told the bones so, uselessly, and dropped them in the pile.

The honey-bread threat. He held it a long moment. In the end he put it in the fire, because — and this was the thought that finally made the decision for him, arriving whole and clear as a bell-note — because Maren would have laughed at him for keeping it. Would have called him a magpie. Would have said, *Isandro, love, you don't need my voice in a bone. You need to get up off your knees.*

One charm stayed in his hand. The oldest one. Made three weeks before the purges began, before either of them knew the world was about to end. It held nothing important. She was telling him about her day. A customer at the market had haggled shamelessly over apples, and Maren had admired her for it, and she was laughing as she told it, that low laugh that started in her chest and worked upward like something climbing stairs.

Nothing important. Everything. He tucked it inside his shirt, against his skin, where it lay warm as a second heartbeat.

"Ready?" Sefa asked.

"No," Isandro said honestly. "But I believe that's also the point."

She struck the flint. The oil caught with a soft whump, and the flames rose blue-green first, then gold, and then the voices began to rise.

He had expected silence. He had expected bone to burn mute, ash to be ash. But the fire loosened something, and for one impossible minute the courtyard filled with the dead — not screaming, nothing so dramatic — just *talking*, all two hundred of them at once, overlapping, tumbling, the way a great family talks at a feast. Osk's half-song. Corvel's testimony. The honey-bread threat, delivered with perfect comic timing even now, even burning. Widow Anselm's husband, still explaining about the third floorboard, still certain she'd never find it.

Sefa grabbed Isandro's arm. Her eyes were huge. "They're *singing,*" she breathed, and for just a moment she wasn't afraid, and neither was he. For one moment it sounded less like a funeral than a departure — a ship leaving harbor with every soul aboard waving from the rails.

Then the flames climbed, and the voices thinned, and one by one the dead fell silent, and Isandro Coldharbour stood in his own backyard and wept like rain, freely and without shame, until there was nothing left in the basin but a drift of fine white ash that the night wind took away over the wall, northward, toward the wood.

When it was done, Sefa tugged his sleeve.

"Better?" she asked.

He considered it. The silence inside him was enormous — a cathedral of it, vault after vault where voices used to live. It hurt. And underneath the hurt, unmistakably, was something he had not felt in ten years: room. Space. Air.

"No," he said. "And yes. Both at once. You'll learn that trick, living with me. I do everything both at once. Eat dessert before dinner, apologize while insulting, flee the city while grieving." He wiped his face on his sleeve. "Come on. We have a sewer to crawl through and a witch's reputation to ruin."

* * *

They left by way of the Grimmer's Gate, because Mother Quill said the Pale Office watched the river gates and the road gates and the sky, insofar as anyone can watch the sky, but that nobody — not even mages-turned-bureaucrats — willingly watched the sewer-gates. "Pride," the old woman had said, pressing a wrapped bundle of dried herbs and dubious biscuits into Sefa's arms. "An empire's true foundation. Now go, and if you meet anything down there with more teeth than a rat, tell it Quill sent you and watch it reconsider."

The grate was rusted but old rust, flaking, and Isandro's pry bar — kept, like the floorboards, for purposes he had never admitted to himself until tonight — made short work of it. The tunnel beyond breathed out air that smelled of the river's worst opinions. Somewhere ahead, water dripped in a rhythm like a slow clock.

"Charming," Isandro muttered, lighting the lamp. "I once sold a charm to a man whose last words were spoken in this exact sewer. He was bitten by something. I never asked what."

Sefa peered into the dark with an expression of professional assessment. "Small teeth," she said. "Big fear." A beat. "Mostly rats."

"You can *tell* that?"

She tapped her chest, then pointed north, the way they would travel. *The wood knows,* the gesture said. *So I know.* Then, with the gravity of a duchess granting a favor: "I will protect you."

"From rats."

"From *everything.*"

He laughed — actually laughed, the sound strange and rusty in his own ears, a coin found in an old coat. Ten years since he'd laughed in a doorway at midnight. He hoisted the pack, checked the single charm warm against his chest, and ducked into the dark with the child walking close beside him, her hand finding his without either of them arranging it.

The tunnel bent, ran straight, bent again. Rats indeed watched them pass from ledges with small judgmental eyes, and, honoring whatever ancient treaty Mother Quill apparently held with rodentkind, declined to do more than watch. Twice the passage forked and Sefa chose without hesitation, left and then right, following something in the walls that Isandro could not hear anymore and realized, with a pang that was grief and relief in equal measure, that he never would again — not the dead, anyway. Whatever else waited for him at the end of this road, the cellar-full-of-voices was closed to him now, and the silence walked along behind him like a tall companion.

They heard the Office before they saw it: boots on iron, above. The sewer-gate opened onto the embankment stairs, and beyond the embankment ran the canal, and over the canal arched the Tanner's Bridge, lamplit, and on the bridge stood a figure in the gray coat of the Pale Office.

Aldric Vane.

Isandro froze in the shadow of the gate arch, one arm out to stop Sefa, his heart slamming like a fist on a door. Aldric stood at the bridge rail with his hands folded, looking — deliberately, Isandro understood in one cold flash — at the middle distance. Not down. Not at the gate. At a point on the far wall exactly level with his own eyes, with the studied blankness of a man memorizing a spot so he can later swear he saw nothing there.

Three seconds. Five. The boots on the bridge had stopped; another officer must be approaching, footsteps ringing closer, closer—

"Aldric!" the newcomer called. "Report. Any movement on the south approaches?"

A pause. Long enough to hang a man.

"All quiet," Aldric Vane said, in the flat, official voice Isandro had last heard through a courtroom door thirteen years ago. "Nothing moves tonight."

*Nothing moves tonight.* Isandro pressed himself and Sefa back into the dark of the stairwell, hardly breathing, and listened to the boots recede across the bridge. Just before they faded, Aldric's voice came again — lower, unarmored, pitched not for the officer but downward, carelessly, the way a man tosses a stone into a well:

"The third floorboard from the hearth, Coldharbour."

Then the boots crossed fully to the far side, and the bridge stood empty, and the lamplight swayed, and Isandro discovered he had forgotten how his lungs worked.

"He knows," Sefa whispered. "Your friend. He knows, and he let us go."

"That," Isandro managed, "is what frightens me." He stared up through the arch at the empty bridge, at the place where his oldest friend had stood and lied to his own Office — for him? for Maren's ghost? for the boy they'd both once been? "Men like Aldric don't open doors without wanting something on the other side. Either he's decided to betray everything he serves—" 

"Or?"

"Or he wants to follow us alone." Isandro shouldered the pack and turned north, into the dripping dark, toward the road of gallows trees and the dying wood beyond. "Come on, little grove. Let's hope it's the first thing."

Behind them, faint and far, a whistle blew — one note only, uncertain, like a question asked into the dark.
