## 8. The Road of Gallows Trees

The north road out of the lowlands was called the Pilgrim's Way on maps, and the Way of Sorrows by everyone who had ever walked it, and Isandro had walked it twice in his life — once as a young man with Maren laughing beside him about how every pilgrim shrine sold the same terrible honey-cakes, and once now, alone except for a child who had learned her first word that morning.

The word was *no*.

She deployed it constantly, and with great authority. *No* to the dried plums he offered her. *No* to holding his hand crossing the ford, then *yes* a heartbeat later when the water came up past her ankles, which he chose to interpret as growth rather than caprice. *No*, most emphatically, to his attempt to wash the mud from her hair in a horse trough, delivered while she climbed fully into the trough instead, clothes and all, so that technically she had not refused the bath at all, only his terms for it.

"You're going to be trouble," he told her, wringing out his sleeves. "You understand me? Ten years I've kept my head down, and along you come."

Sefa looked at him from the trough, dripping, leaves still tangled in her hair no matter how many times he picked them out — new ones, always new ones, budding from her scalp like some impossible flowering weed — and she said, with grave satisfaction:

"No."

"That's what I thought."

* * *

By midday they reached the first of the gallows trees.

He had been dreading it since Vaelmoor, since Mother Quill pressed her cracked map into his hands and said *the north road, child, there's no other way, but mind the trees.* Every traveler knew the story. Thirteen years ago, after the Sundering, after the Edict, the Pale Office had hanged mages along the roads leading away from the Weeping Wood — not in the city, where hanging was ugly business best done behind walls, but out here, where the bodies could be left as instruction. They had hanged them from the oldest oaks and elms they could find, and the Office's masons had driven iron nails through the bark above each one, iron being what the old faith used to pin a soul in place.

The theory was that the dead could not move on. That they would hang there in the wood forever, whispering their crimes to anyone who passed, until the world forgot magic ever existed.

The theory was wrong in only one particular. They did not whisper their crimes.

They whispered their innocence.

Isandro heard them long before he saw them. It was like walking into a room full of people murmuring behind a closed door — a rustle, a susurration, dozens of voices braided thin by distance. Sefa felt it too; her hand crept into his without asking this time, and her humming stopped altogether, which frightened him more than the voices did.

The first tree stood where the road bent around a hillside, an oak older than the kingdom, its crown black and bare though it was high summer. Iron nails studded its trunk in rows, each one green with age, each one weeping a stain of rust down the bark like old tears. And in the branches —

Nothing. Of course nothing. Thirteen years of rain and birds and rot had taken everything a body could give up. But the tree itself had grown wrong around its history, the wood swelling and knotting in places where rope had bitten, and when the wind moved through the bare crown it made a sound that was not wind.

*It wasn't mine,* said the oak, in a voice like dry leaves. *I never touched the deep roots. I only read them. I only read them. Ask the stone under Hillcross, ask it, ask—*

"Isandro." Sefa's voice, small and rough from disuse. She was staring up into the branches. "Sad."

"Yes," he said. His throat was tight. "Yes, they are."

*They lie,* another voice said, farther down the road, sharper, younger. *Some of us lied. Some of us took payment to curse our neighbors' cattle and we said it was weather. Hang me for that, then, but hang me for true things—*

"They're not all innocent," Isandro murmured. He hadn't meant to say it aloud. But it was true, and he had spent ten years learning that truth mattered even when it cost something. "Magic isn't clean just because it's old. Neither are people."

Sefa considered this with the seriousness she brought to everything, her gray eyes moving from him to the oak and back.

"No," she agreed — but softly, and he understood she was saying *yes.*

* * *

There were eleven of the trees between the ford and the ridge road, and Isandro counted them because counting was better than listening. At the fourth, a magpie had nested in the iron-studded crook of a branch, which struck him as either the bravest or the most irreverent bird in the northern provinces, and he found himself telling Sefa a story about Maren and a magpie — how she'd spent a whole winter feeding one off the windowsill of their rooms above the charm shop, naming it Chancellor, insisting it brought her secrets.

"It stole a spoon," he said. "That was the extent of its statecraft. But she loved it."

Sefa's mouth did something complicated, an expression she was still learning to build, and she produced: "Chan-ell-or."

His chest hurt in the good way, which was somehow worse than the bad way. He had forgotten there were good ways.

At the seventh tree, the whispering changed.

Mostly the dead spoke to themselves, or to the road, or to nobody — thirteen years of talking into empty air will do that. But as they passed beneath the great elm at Miller's Bend, one voice came clear and close and directed, and it used his name.

*Coldharbour.*

He stopped. Sefa bumped against his side.

*I know that tread,* the voice said. It was an old woman's voice, frayed at the edges like cloth washed too many times. *Light on his feet, heavy in the shoulders. The carver. Coldharbour of Copperlane, who bought my sister's finger-bone off a resurrection-man and never asked where it came from.*

Isandro's stomach turned over. He remembered the purchase. Eleven years ago. A knuckle-bone, sold to him cheap, and he had carved it into a hearing-charm for a man who wanted his drowned son's last words, and the charm had worked, and he had never once wondered about the donor.

"I'm sorry," he said quietly. To the tree. To the air. A grown man apologizing to an elm, and meaning it.

*Sorry,* the voice said, tasting the word. *My sister was a hedge-healer. Fever-work, birth-work, nothing dark. The Office hung her anyway, because she wouldn't say where the others ran, and she wouldn't say because there were no others, and she died rather than invent a name to save her neck. And you wore her finger on a string.*

"I didn't know."

*Nobody knows. That is the whole trick of your city, carver. Nobody knows anything, and everybody profits.*

He wanted to argue. There was nothing to argue with. Sefa tugged his hand, and he let himself be tugged, and the voice followed them a little way down the road, growing fainter:

*Tell them true things, Coldharbour. When it matters. Tell them true.*

* * *

They camped that night in the shadow of the tenth tree, because it was the only shelter for miles — a hollow beneath the roots where a shepherd had stacked stones into a rough windbreak years ago, back when shepherds still dared the north road. Isandro built a small fire, small enough to cup in his palms if a rider came, and shared out the last of the honey-cakes he'd bought in the river village, which were, in faithful accordance with tradition, terrible.

Sefa ate hers with the concentration of a scholar.

"The shrine at Hollowford," he said, "sells these same cakes. Same recipe, probably. Two hundred years of pilgrims and nobody has once said, perhaps we should try less ash and more honey."

"Honey," Sefa repeated. Then, experimentally, with the air of someone testing a blade: "No."

"You liked it!"

She smiled — her first real smile, wide and crooked, and it transformed her whole strange face — and held out her hand for a second cake.

Around them the gallows elm whispered its slow litany of names and grievances, and above the fire the stars came out hard and cold, and Isandro thought: *this is the last quiet night. After the ridge, the country opens up, and open country means riders.* He checked the knife at his belt. He had carried it for ten years and used it for carving, whittling, spreading butter, opening letters. He fell asleep telling himself it would stay that way.

* * *

They came at dawn.

He woke to Sefa's hand clamped over his mouth — her palm smelled of sap and river water — and her eyes enormous in the gray light, and her head tilted toward the road. Hoofbeats. Three horses, walking, spread out the way men spread out when they're sweeping ground they intend to search.

"Bounty men?" he breathed.

Sefa nodded. Then she pointed south, back the way they'd come, and shook her head. Then east, toward the rocks above the road, and nodded.

*Three ahead on the road, more behind.* He should have known she'd hear them first. Half the time he suspected she heard everything first.

They broke camp without a fire to kill and got twenty yards upslope before the first rider topped the rise below them and shouted.

The shout was cheerful, which was somehow the worst part. "Morning! Morning, friend — don't run! Running makes it look bad!"

Isandro froze, Sefa's hand locked in his. Three riders below, and now a fourth crashing up through the gorse on their flank — four, then, not three, and Sefa's hearing was good but she was six years old. The lead rider reined in at the base of the slope. He was a broad, sunburned man in a mail shirt gone brown with wear, and he smiled like a man selling knives at a fair.

"There's a description out of Vaelmoor," the man called up. "Man and a girl-child, girl with green in her hair, girl that hums. Wood-touched. There's good coin for wood-touched children — living ones especially. The Pale Office pays by the pound, near enough." He said it pleasantly, a merchant discussing wool futures. "Now, I'll be honest with you, friend. You look like a bone-carver, not a fighter. Give us the girl and walk away. Nobody bleeds, everyone eats."

Behind Isandro, Sefa's hand tightened in his. She wasn't humming. She wasn't crying. She was watching the fourth rider working through the gorse, tracking him with that flat gray attention, and Isandro realized she was waiting to see what *he* did — that she had already decided, with the terrifying simplicity of a child, that whatever he decided would be right.

Ten years. Ten years of keeping his head down, his knife clean, his door shut. Ten years of *nobody knows anything and everybody profits.*

"Come down slow," the bounty man said, and drew a short sword with the unhurried ease of habit. "Or we come up fast."

What happened next happened quickly, and afterward Isandro could never assemble it into order. The rider in the gorse burst through the brush first — closer than he'd judged, much closer — and it was reflex, ten years buried but not dead, that put Isandro's knife into the meat of the man's thigh and twisted as he shoved him back downslope. The man screamed and rolled. The lead rider spurred uphill. And Isandro, who had never in his life done violence with intent, heard himself shouting — Maren's voice coming out of his own mouth, the words she'd used when drunk merchants got loud outside the shop — *you want to try? Come up and try!* — and the fury of it surprised the rider's horse more than the rider, and the animal balked, and in that half-second of chaos Isandro grabbed Sefa and ran.

Not up. Down — back toward the road, toward the gallows elm, the one direction they weren't watching. Behind him a crossbow snapped and the bolt buried itself in a stone with a sound like a tooth breaking.

The elm's shadow swallowed them. And the dead woke.

*THIEVES,* roared every voice in the tree at once, all of them together, a storm of accusation — and whether they meant the bounty men or the world entire, Isandro never learned, but the horses certainly had opinions. They screamed and wheeled and would not approach the iron-nailed trunk, and the riders cursed and fought their mounts, and Isandro ran with Sefa bouncing against his shoulder down the road and into the birch scrub beyond, and did not stop until the hoofbeats faded and his lungs burned and his legs gave out beneath a fallen log far off the road.

For a long time there was only breathing. His. Hers. A wren somewhere, going about its morning with no regard for history.

Then, from downslope, faint across the hollow, a voice — one of the bounty men, calling to his fellows, angry and afraid:

"—leg's opened to the bone, we've got to bind it — leave the girl, there's easier quarry, the Office pays for *any* wood-touched brat—"

And another voice answering, lazy and cold, a voice that would follow them for a long time afterward:

"Easier, yes. But none worth what *she'll* fetch. Look at the hair on her, Doss described it true. That's not some swamp-witch's get. That's *seed-stock*. We find her again."

Isandro sat against the log with Sefa in his lap and discovered that his hands were shaking, and that the knife was still in his fist, and that there was blood on it — not much, a smear, a dark crescent along the edge — and that he could not remember drawing it, only the shock of it biting home, only how *easy* it had been, how his body had simply remembered something his heart had sworn forgotten.

Ten years. Gone in four seconds. He was sick, then, abruptly and thoroughly, into the ferns, while Sefa patted his back with small solemn hands, and he hated himself a little for frightening her, and hated the bounty men more, and hated most of all the calm, competent thing inside him that had known exactly what to do.

"It gets easier," he rasped, when he could speak. "That's what frightens me. Everyone says it gets easier, and they mean it kindly, and it does, and that's the whole obscenity of it."

Sefa studied his face. Then she reached out and closed his fingers over the knife, gently, and pushed his hand — knife and all — down flat against the earth, and said her newest word, her third word, learned from the gallows elm and given back to him now like a key:

"True," she said. "Be true."

He laughed. It came out wet and cracked, halfway to sobbing, but it was a laugh.

"All right," he said. "All right, little grove. True things."

They cleaned the knife in a stream and went on north, off the road now, following the ridgeline by Mother Quill's map and Sefa's unerring sense of which way the old wood lay. Twice that day Isandro caught movement on the hills behind them and could not swear it was birds. Once, at dusk, he heard a single whistle carry down the wind — three notes, rising, the Pale Office's hunting call, the sound that had emptied whole streets in Vaelmoor thirteen years ago.

But it was the next morning, at the edge of the moor, that they found the thing that told them the hunt had truly begun: a notice board, fresh-built, nailed to a waystone where no waystone had borne notice in a decade.

A reward notice. A woodcut likeness of a man with heavy shoulders and light feet, done well enough to chill him. Beside it, a second likeness: a girl with leaves in her hair.

And beneath both, in the Office's careful clerk-hand, a line Isandro read three times before he could make himself believe it:

*Also sought for questioning: Aldric Vane, interrogator of the Pale Office, missing since the night of the twelfth of Harvestmoon.*

The paper flapped in the moor wind. Somewhere behind them, three notes rose and fell.

"He didn't let us go," Isandro said slowly. "In the sewers. I thought—" He stopped. Started again. "Oh, Aldric. What have you *done?*"
