## 9. Aldric's Bargain

The holding cell beneath the Pale Office smelled of wet stone and the particular despair of men who had been caught doing something stupid for money. Aldric Vane had spent thirteen years learning to read that smell like a scholar reads scripture.

He sat across from the two bounty hunters — a pair of brothers named Fitch and Odo Cray, which was somehow exactly what they looked like — and watched them sweat through their second hour of not answering questions.

"Let's go over it again," Aldric said pleasantly. "You had a wood-touched child in a sack."

"A basket," said Fitch, the older one, with the wounded dignity of a man correcting a minor detail that made everything worse.

"A basket," Aldric agreed. "In a basket, then. And the child spoke."

"Made noises." Odo shifted his manacled wrists. "Weird noises. Like wind in a chimney. We weren't going to hurt her. Just deliver her. There's buyers down south pay good coin for—"

"For children who hum in dead languages. Yes." Aldric turned a page of his notebook that had nothing written on it. It was an old trick, learned from a man he'd rather not think about just now: make them wait while you pretend to consult something damning. "And then a carver took her from you at the crossroads by Gallows Row. Describe him."

"Big hands," said Fitch promptly. "Carver's hands. Gray at the temples. Walked like a man carrying something breakable, even before he picked up the girl."

"Talked soft," Odo added. "Offered to buy her off us first. Polite about it. When we said no, he—" Odo touched his jaw, where a spectacular bruise was blooming yellow-green. "He knew where to hit a man. That's all I'll say. He apologized after. That's the part I don't like. You knock a man's teeth loose, don't apologize. Apologizing means he's not used to it."

Aldric wrote *big hands* in his notebook, because it seemed polite to write something, and because his own hand had begun to shake and needed occupation.

Isandro Coldharbour. Of course it was Isandro. Ten years of careful nothing from the man — ten years of Aldric checking the charm-seller's door on Vellum Street twice a year and finding only bone dust and candle wax and that infuriating little brass bell — and now, in the space of one week, a mute child speaking the Old Tongue of the Wood surfaces in his city, and within days she is in the company of the single person in Vaelmoor whose guilt Aldric had privately catalogued as *manageable*.

"Where were you headed when we took you?" Aldric asked. "North road or river?"

"River," said Fitch, too fast.

"North road," said Odo, at the same moment, and then glared at his brother. "We were arguing about it, you great lump, that's why he's asking."

"We'd have gone north eventually," Fitch muttered. "Everyone goes north. The wood pulls 'em. Old wives say the trees call their own home."

Aldric closed the notebook. "Gentlemen, here is my professional advice, offered free of charge. The Office pays informants by weight of usefulness, and the man hunting your carver is going to be me. Anything you remember between now and dawn, anything at all — the color of his coat, whether he limped, whether he cried — you tell the sergeant outside, and it comes to me. Do this, and when this is over you'll be transported instead of hanged. Fail to do it, and I will come back down here, and I will be having a worse day than I'm having now."

"Are you having a bad day?" Odo asked, with the frank curiosity of the very stupid.

Aldric looked at him for a long moment. "My oldest friend knocked your teeth out and stole the most dangerous child in the empire," he said. "So yes. Slightly."

* * *

The Pale Office kept its trophies on the third floor, behind glass that never quite managed to look clean. Aldric walked past them every morning and had trained himself, over thirteen years, not to see them.

Today he stopped.

Petrified branches, mounted on black velvet like hunting prizes. They had been cut from the Weeping Wood in the years after the Sundering, when the Office burned the forest back mile by mile and carried home souvenirs. The wood had been dying even then, but dying slowly, the way old men die — stubbornly, and full of complaints. Even now, petrified and dead these thirteen years, the branches wept. One bead of sap gathered at each cut end, swelled, and fell into a little brass gutter beneath the display, and the gutters were emptied daily by a junior clerk who complained constantly about it, and Aldric had never once had the courage to ask himself why the Office found this acceptable rather than horrifying.

One branch, near the corner, still had bark texture. Still had, if you looked at it wrong, the suggestion of a face.

"Don't," said a voice behind him. "You'll start thinking again, and then you'll be no use to anyone."

Deputy Marshal Corvane stood in the corridor with a folder under his arm and the expression of a man who had risen before dawn to be disappointed professionally. He was not cruel. That was the trap of Corvane — he was merely thorough, the way a millstone is thorough.

"The hunters are talking?" Corvane asked.

"They're talking."

"And?"

"It's Coldharbour. The charm-seller. He has the child, and he's moving north along the gallows road toward the old forest boundary."

Corvane grunted. "Coldharbour. The widow's friend. Half the city thinks he's a saint — sells them their husbands' last words for a few coppers, blesses the sick children with his little trinkets." He said it without heat, which was worse than heat. "Thirteen years we've known what he is, and nobody could ever catch him holding anything that would stand in a tribunal. You interviewed him yourself last month, didn't you? Vane? Your history with him?"

"We were apprentices together. Before."

"Before," Corvane repeated, in the tone men used for the Sundering, the purges, the Edict — the whole catalogue of things that had happened *before*, when the world was a different shape. He held out the folder. "Then you're the natural choice to lead the pursuit. The Marshal wants someone who understands how his mind works. Someone who can take him alive, ideally. A public trial would do the Edict more good than a corpse."

Aldric took the folder. It was heavier than paper should be.

"There's a complication," he said.

"There usually is."

"If it comes to a choice between the child and the carver, I want it understood in advance that the child is the priority. She's the vessel. Whatever the wood intended when it put itself inside a little girl, killing her out of hand would be—" He searched for a word the Office would accept. "Wasteful."

"Wasteful," Corvane agreed. "I'll note it." He turned to go, then paused. "Vane. You testified at the Maren Coldharbour tribunal. I've read the transcript. Clean work."

Clean work. Aldric stood in the trophy corridor, next to the weeping branch with the almost-face, and said, "Thank you, Deputy Marshal," in a voice he did not recognize as his own.

* * *

That night he dreamed of the workshop on Candlemaker's Row, and in the dream it was raining, because in his memory it was always raining there, softly, the way it rained in the good years.

They had been nineteen. Isandro had been apprenticed to Master Ellery the bone-carver, and Aldric to the scrivener upstairs, and in the evenings they'd shared a jug of plum wine on the workshop steps and argued about everything in the world that could be argued about, which at nineteen was everything.

"You carve the dead so people can grieve," young Aldric had said once, watching Isandro's knife move through a knuckle of ox-bone. "That's honest work. Better than mine. I copy contracts for merchants who cheat each other."

"Your work keeps the peace," Isandro said. "Mine just keeps the sad company."

"That's the same thing, mostly."

"No," Isandro had said, laughing, tapping his own chest with the knife handle — carefully, always carefully; he handled a blade the way other men handled a lover. "Peace is when nobody's hurting. Company is when somebody stays with you while you do. Different animals entirely."

And then there was Maren, who had come into the story the way weather comes in off the sea — suddenly, and changing everything. She had been a hedgewife's daughter with green stains permanently under her fingernails and a laugh that made the neighbors complain, and she had loved both of them in the uncomplicated way that Maren loved everything: fiercely, specifically, without ranking. She'd called Aldric "the quiet one" for six years, right up until the week she stopped calling him anything at all.

The dream shifted, the way dreams do, and the rain on Candlemaker's Row turned to ash falling from a sky the color of a bruise, and Aldric stood in the tribunal chamber of the Pale Office, thirteen years younger, wearing his first gray coat that still smelled of the tailor's shop, and the inquisitor asked him the question:

*Did you observe the accused, Maren Coldharbour, practicing proscribed arts?*

And he had said — God help him, he had been twenty-three years old and terrified, and they had shown him what happened to witnesses who recanted, and Isandro had already run, already vanished into the city's cracks, and someone was going to hang for the seedling found in the hedgewife's garden, and it was either going to be both of them or one —

"I saw her," young Aldric heard himself say, in the clear voice they had coached out of him. "I saw her carry living wood. I saw the sap move against the season."

All true. Every word true. That had always been the elegant cruelty of it: they hadn't needed him to lie. They had only needed him to say out loud the thing that would kill her, and he had said it, and they had thanked him for his service, and Maren had looked at him across the tribunal floor with no hatred in her face at all — that was the worst of it, there had been no hatred, only a kind of tired recognition, as though she had known all along what he was made of and had loved him anyway, generously, the way you love a dog that will one day bite you.

She had died in the third year of the purges. Fever, officially. Aldric had read the actual report, later, alone, three times: taken ill during transport, unresponsive, dead by morning. The guard captain who signed it had retired comfortably to the coast.

Isandro didn't know the report existed. As far as Isandro knew, his wife had simply died in custody, of the world's indifference, and the man who had put her there was still his friend, still welcome at his door, still — until last month — greeted with plum wine and that easy, unguarded warmth that Isandro spent on so few people.

*He doesn't know,* Aldric thought, standing in the archive with the cold report in his hands. *Ten years he's mourned her clean, and I let him. I let him grieve like an innocent man.*

He put the report back in its drawer. He had put it back in its drawer perhaps four hundred times now. He was very good at putting it back in the drawer.

* * *

Dawn found him in the interrogation wing again, reading the night's harvest from the Cray brothers, and it was better material than he'd hoped. The sergeant's transcription ran three pages, and buried in the middle of Odo's rambling grievances was a detail that made Aldric sit up straight.

*"The carver asked us where the ferryman's stake was burned. Asked it specific-like. Ferryman's Stake, mile marker eleven on the gallows road. Why would a man ask after a burned tree?"*

Because mile marker eleven was where the gallows road met the drovers' path, and the drovers' path was the only route north that bypassed the Office checkpoint at Harrow Ford. Because Isandro had grown up walking those roads with his master, gathering riverbone, and knew the country the way a priest knows his psalms.

Because Isandro wasn't running. He was traveling. There was a difference, and the difference had a destination, and there was only one destination in the north that mattered.

Aldric set down the pages and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes until he saw orange stars.

The Covenant Stone. The old stories said it had been shattered in the Sundering; the older stories, the ones the Office burned faster than any grimoire, said shattered things in that wood had a habit of growing back. If the child was what the Crays' buyer-network whispered she was — a vessel, a seedling given legs — then Isandro Coldharbour, gentle, guilty, hopeless Isandro, was walking the last vessel of the Weeping Wood directly toward the heart of the only place on earth where the Office's entire authority might come apart like wet paper.

He should wire Harrow Ford tonight. He should have wired them yesterday. Every hour he delayed was an hour Isandro gained, and every hour Isandro gained brought them both closer to a confrontation that could only end one way, and some treacherous cellar of Aldric's heart had apparently decided, without consulting him, which way it wanted that to be.

"Guilt weaponized," he said aloud to the empty room, trying the phrase on. Mother Quill had said it to him once, years ago, over cards, apropos of nothing — *careful, Vane, the Office will find your guilt sooner or later, and guilt makes the best weapon there is, because the bearer loads it himself.* He'd laughed then. He was not laughing now.

He took out a fresh sheet of Office paper. He dipped his pen. He wrote:

*To the Marshal's office. Re: pursuit of the fugitive Coldharbour and the wood-touched child. I request the assignment personally. My familiarity with the fugitive's habits and affections renders me uniquely suited to take him alive. I will require no additional escort beyond a standard squad. I will bring them both back. On my name, I will bring them both back.*

He read it twice. *On my name.* His name, which Maren had once told him meant *vane*, as in weathervane, as in the thing that turns whichever way the wind pushes it. She'd said it teasing. Everything had been teasing, right up until the end.

He signed it, sealed it, and rang for the courier.

Then he sat alone in the gray morning light of the interrogation room, listening to the Cray brothers snore in their cell, and admitted the thing he had traveled thirteen years to avoid admitting, which was this: he was not requesting the hunt to save Isandro from the Office.

He was requesting the hunt to save Isandro from everyone else. And to stand, at the end of whatever road this was, in front of the one man alive whose forgiveness he wanted, and finally say the sentence he had rehearsed in four hundred drawers:

*I saw her carry living wood. And I am the reason they took her.*

The courier's boots echoed up the stairwell. Somewhere north, past mile marker eleven, past the hanged trees and their whispering corpses, his oldest friend was walking toward the wood with a child on his shoulders, and the thirteenth moon was turning, and Aldric Vane — the quiet one, the faithful one, the vane — rode out an hour behind him, carrying orders he already intended to disobey.
