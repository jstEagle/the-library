## 10. The Drowned Chapel

The chapel had drowned long before they needed it.

Isandro found it by accident, or perhaps the road found it for them — a squat gray thing sunk to its waist in a fen that had once, he supposed, been someone's parish. The bell tower leaned like a drunk uncle at a wedding, and half its windows were gone, but the roof held over the transept, and the water inside was still enough to hold the stars. Sefa stood at its threshold and made a sound in her throat — three rising notes — and Isandro had learned enough of her humming by now to translate: *here. This place is old. This place remembers being loved.*

"Chapels usually do," he said. "That's rather the point of them."

She looked up at him with that expression she had, the one that made him feel like a man explaining rain to a fish. He was doing it again, he realized — talking to her as if words were a kindness. They weren't, not for her. But he talked anyway, because ten years of talking to charms had left him unable to stop, and because some small stubborn part of him believed that if he kept speaking common tongue around her, it would take root in her like a seed in wet earth.

It had been nine days since Vaelmoor. Nine days of ditches and hedgerows and one memorable afternoon hiding in a turnip cellar while a Pale Office patrol clanked past close enough that Isandro could smell the iron polish on their cuirasses. Nine days of Sefa's hand in his, her silence growing less frightening and more companionable, the way a fire grows less frightening once you've learned where to sit beside it.

He waded into the nave. The water came to his shins, black and cold and smelling of stone rot, but not moving — fenwater, dead water, safe water. Sefa followed without hesitation. She never hesitated at thresholds. Doors, rivers, the borders between sleeping and waking — she crossed them all as if boundaries were a custom of mortals she found quaint.

There was a dry corner behind the collapsed rood screen, high on a mound of fallen plaster where pigeons had once held parliament. Isandro got a fire going with the last of his dry tinder, cursing softly when it smoked, coaxing it when it caught. Sefa sat cross-legged across from him, dripping, watching the flames with an expression he could only call *homesick*, which made no sense and perfect sense at once.

They ate hard cheese and the heel of a loaf going stale. Sefa ate the way she did everything — completely, seriously, with total attention, as though bread were a sacrament and she its only honest priest. Isandro found himself smiling at her, and then found the smile curdling into something else, because his hand had drifted without permission to the inner pocket of his coat.

To the charm.

* * *

He had burned forty-one charms in the sewer-gate's shadow. He could still feel the heat of them through his glove if he let himself — the little screams of the dead as their voices went up, Widow Cassel's husband saying *tell her I fixed the gate* one last time before the bone cracked, the ferryman, the mason, the child with the fever whose mother had paid him in eggs. Ten years of voices, ash in a brazier, and Isandro Coldharbour standing over it feeling nothing so much as relief, which was its own kind of shame.

All except one.

The last Maren-charm. Carved from a chip of her wrist-bone — no, he corrected himself, from a splinter of her coffin-lid, oak, worn smooth by his thumb these ten years until it had gone the color of honey. He had told himself for a decade that it was a memorial. That he kept it closed because opening it would hurt too much.

That was a lie, and he had known it was a lie, and tonight, in a drowned chapel with a child who hummed in a language older than kingdoms, the lie finally got too heavy to carry.

Because here was the truth under the truth: he hadn't opened it because he was afraid of what she'd say. Not *goodbye*. Something worse than goodbye. Something that would ask something of him.

"You can sleep," he told Sefa, who was already asleep, curled with her back to the warm stones, breathing slow and vegetal, like a meadow at dusk. "Well. Excellent talk."

He took out the charm.

It was small work, his earliest and best — a single bead of bone drilled through and threaded on a leather cord, carved with a spiral that wasn't quite a spiral, the way he'd carved all his listening-charms before he'd learned to make them pretty enough to sell. Ugly. Honest. It sat in his palm and said nothing, because it was closed, because *he* had closed it, the night after her funeral, with a word of sealing he'd invented himself out of grief and spite.

Ten years. He turned it over. His thumb found the groove it had worn.

"Maren," he said aloud, and the chapel swallowed his voice and gave back nothing, and somehow that helped. Dead water, dead air, a dead god's dead house. If ever there was a place to be a coward, this was it. "I'm going to open this now. And whatever you say — whatever you *ask* — I want you to know I've been carrying your voice for ten years like a man carrying a lit lantern in a powder store, and I am tired, love. I'm tired of being careful."

The fire popped. Sefa breathed.

He spoke the unsealing.

* * *

Her voice filled the chapel the way water fills a cup — instantly, totally, from every direction at once.

*"Isandro."*

Not the recording he remembered. That was the first horror and the first mercy both. A charm held a moment, fixed, a bee in amber — her last words, said once, replayable forever. But this was *her*, present-tense, the particular warmth in it, the way she'd always said his name like she was handing him something.

"That's not—" His voice cracked. "That's not how you work. You're a charm. You're a moment. You don't *talk*."

*"You carved me from her coffin,"* said the voice of Maren Halloran, gentle and amused, the way she'd been gentle and amused about everything, including, apparently, the metaphysics of her own remains. *"And you've spent ten years pouring yourself into bone and calling it craft. What did you think would happen? Nothing you make has ever been ordinary, my love. That was always the trouble with you."*

"I burned them," he whispered. "Forty-one of them. I burned everyone."

*"I know. I felt them go past. They said you wept."*

"I did not weep. There was smoke."

*"There was smoke AND you wept."*

He laughed, and it came out wrong, half sob, and he pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. The water lapped at the pillars. Somewhere in the dark above, a pigeon resettled itself with a judgmental flutter.

"I have to tell you something," he said. "I don't know how to— There's a girl. A child. She came to my door in the rain and she can't speak and she hums things that make my bones ring, and she's—" He faltered. He had not said it aloud yet, not even to Mother Quill, who had guessed it anyway with her terrible bright hedge-witch eyes. "She's the wood, Maren. Or the wood's in her. The Weeping Wood. What's left of it, wearing a girl like a coat. And the Office is hunting her, and I'm taking her north to a stone I half believe in, on the word of a woman who reads fortunes in cabbage leaves, and I am so far past the edge of anything sensible that I keep waiting to wake up in my shop with sawdust in my teeth."

Silence. Long enough that his heart began to sink — maybe the moment had passed, maybe the amber had hardened, maybe—

*"Look at her, Isandro."*

"What?"

*"The girl. Look at her while I talk."*

He lifted his head. Across the fire, Sefa slept on, white-haired, thin, one hand curled near her cheek. In the firelight, faintly — he had told himself for days he was imagining it — the veins of her wrists showed green, like light through new leaves.

*"Do you remember the morning they came?"*

The question went through him like cold water through cloth. "Maren—"

*"The purges. The Sundering. Do you remember what I said when I left the house?"*

He did. Of course he did; he had replayed it ten thousand times in ten years, hunting it for a hidden meaning the way he hunted flaws in bone. *I'll be home by dark. Keep the kettle warm.* He'd kept the kettle warm for six hours. He'd kept it warm until the Office returned her effects in a sealed box and he understood that dark, for her, had come early and permanently.

*"Keep the kettle warm,"* the charm said now, and then, softly: *"I lied to you. The only lie I ever told you. I need you to hear why, because I only have a moment — charms are doors, my love, and doors swing both ways, and something on the other side of mine is very patient and I've been holding it shut with your name."*

His mouth had gone dry. "Tell me."

*"I knew about the seedling."*

The words landed one at a time, each with its own weight.

*"After the Sundering — you know what they told everyone, that the mages broke the covenant out of pride, that the wood died of our arrogance. That's true the way a knife's handle is true. It's what you hold. But Isandro — the wood didn't die. Wood doesn't die the way people die. It *leaves*. It pours itself into vessels, into seeds, into anything living enough and empty enough to carry it, and it runs. The mages who broke the covenant thought they'd killed it. They'd only made it small."*

The fire crackled. Sefa's breath moved the flame's tip, just slightly, like wind through a branch.

*"One seedling survived. One. Hidden in the root-cellar of a tanner's shop on Coil Street — do you remember Coil Street? You bought our wedding candles two doors down. I found it three weeks before the purges began. It sang to me, Isandro. All night, every night, through the floor. I couldn't sleep for it. I couldn't eat. And I couldn't tell you, because you would have done exactly what you're doing right now, exactly what I knew you'd do — you would have picked it up and carried it somewhere safer, and there was nowhere safer, and they were watching the houses of anyone with gift enough to hear it sing."*

"So you went out," Isandro said. His voice sounded like someone else's. "That morning. You went out because—"

*"Because the Office had traced the singing to our street. Because they were going door to door with iron rods, and iron finds a seedling the way a hook finds a fish. Because someone had to lead them away from Coil Street, and I was the only one on our street who could hear well enough to know which doors they'd already searched."* A pause, and when she continued, her voice had the steadiness of a woman setting down something heavy. *"I walked them four miles, my love. Four miles in the wrong direction, singing to myself the way the seedling sang, loud enough that any listener would think the vessel was me. And when they caught me, I made sure they believed it. I made sure they never went back to Coil Street. And they didn't. The seedling lived."*

The chapel tilted. Or he did. He put a hand against the wet stone floor to steady himself and found his fingers had gone numb.

"The tanner's shop," he managed. "Coil Street was razed in the second purge year. Everything south of the tannery—"

*"Burned to the ground, yes. And the seedling was carried out of the ashes by persons unknown, and moved seven times in thirteen years, and hunted every step, and it grew anyway, the way living things do, stubbornly and badly and against all advice."* The warmth in her voice deepened, and he heard it coming the way you hear thunder before the lightning shows you where it lives. *"Open your eyes, Isandro. Look at the girl."*

He looked.

Sefa, asleep. White hair fanned on stone. Green in her veins. Breathing like a meadow.

*"Persons unknown,"* Maren said gently, *"is a phrase historians use when the truth is a woman with a candle-shop receipt in her pocket and a talent for hearing. I didn't find the seedling, Isandro. I raised it. For eleven months, in the cellars and the hedges and the homes of people braver than me, I carried the last of the Weeping Wood under my heart like a second child, and I taught it human sounds, and I loved it, and when the net closed too tight to run any further, I gave it the only gift I had left."* 

A pause. When she spoke again her voice was thick.

*"A vessel has to be willing. Did Mother Quill tell you that? No — she wouldn't know yet. But it does. The wood can't wear the unwilling; it slides off them like rain off wax. Every vessel before this one chose. And my girl — my *girl*, ours — she was dying of fever in a hedgerow outside Vaelmoor, an orphan of the third sweep, eight years old, and I held her and asked her. Out loud. I asked her if she would carry something ancient and hurting and beautiful, and whether she'd be angry at me forever, and she nodded."* A breath that was almost a laugh, almost a sob. *"She nodded twice. Once for yes, and once, I think, for *hurry up*."*

Isandro discovered he was crying. Properly, ugly, in front of a dead wife and a sleeping forest and a god whose house had flooded centuries ago. He let it happen. Ten years of careful grief, measured out in thimblefuls so he could keep working, keep selling, keep surviving — and it all came up at once like floodwater through a cellar grate.

"You died for her," he said. "You died for a tree."

*"I died for a choice,*" Maren said. *"The wood didn't take her. I asked, and she answered, and then I stayed between them and the iron for as long as staying worked. Nobody sacrificed anybody, my love. That's the whole point. That's what the covenant *was*, before they broke it — not masters and servants, not worship. Just asking, and answering. Two different kinds of life agreeing to carry each other."* Her voice softened further, until he had to lean toward the charm to hold it. *"Which brings us to you."*

"No," Isandro said immediately.

*"You haven't heard the—"*

"No. I know that tone. Eleven years married, Maren, I know every tone you own, and that's the one you used when you'd already sold the cow and were breaking the news to me gently." He scrubbed his face with his sleeve. "Say it anyway."

*"The vessel burns out."* No gentleness now. Honesty, the kind she'd saved for matters of life and death and which curtains to buy. *"Feeding a forest costs a life, Isandro, and she's been feeding one for thirteen years on scraps and fear. Mother Quill read the moon-count right. Before the thirteenth moon turns, the covenant must be reforged at the Stone — the wood given back its independence, its own roots, its own voice — or the vessel spends herself entirely and dies with it, and everything she's carried dies with her, and this time there is no second seedling."*

"Then we hurry. We're making good time, the Rootbound are ahead of us, Quill says the Stone is—"

*"And reforging requires a full vessel at the Stone. Willing. Whole. Alive at dusk and changed by dawn."* The charm was quiet a moment. Water dripped somewhere in the dark of the tower. *"I know what you're doing right now, Isandro Coldharbour. You are building a third option in your head. You're very good at that — it's how you survived the purges, how you survived me, how you built a life in the cracks of an empire that hates what you are. But there is no third option here. There's the wood's way, or the girl's death, and the wood's way needs her at that Stone with everything she has, and she will give it, because she's the most willing creature I have ever met, and she will spend herself to the last green thread unless someone stands where I stood and carries the cost that isn't hers to carry."*

"And what cost is that?" he asked bitterly. "What's left for me to carry? I'm not gifted enough to feed a forest. I carve trinkets, Maren. I sell whispers to widows. I'm the least magical man in—"

*"You hear the dead."*

The words stopped him like a hand on the chest.

*"Ten years of charms, Isandro. Ten years of holding the voices of the dead in bone, of walking through Vaelmoor with a hundred souls whispering against your ribs, of listening — really listening — to more of the departed than any necromancer of the old courts ever dared summon. You think that's a trick? You think that's trinkets?"* Now her voice was fierce, and it was the fiercest thing he'd ever heard her say, fiercer than any argument, fiercer even than her goodbyes had been in dreams. *"The covenant was broken by men who refused to listen. It will be reforged by someone who listens better than anyone alive. The Stone will ask for a price — it always asks — and when it asks you, you will know the answer, because you've been paying installments your whole life. You hear the dead, my love. You have always heard the dead. The only question left is what you're willing to give up so that the living get their turn."*

He sat with that. The fire burned low. The fen breathed against the walls, and the drowned chapel held them all — the living, the dead, and the vast slow green thing dreaming in a child's body — like a cupped hand.

"If I do this," he said finally, hoarsely. "If I go north and stand at that Stone and offer whatever it asks. Will I hear you again? After?"

The silence stretched so long he thought the charm had guttered out. Then:

*"Oh, Isandro."* Such tenderness in it that his chest physically hurt. *"You still don't understand what I am. I'm not a voice in bone. I never was. I'm the reason you carved the bone. I'm every hour you sat up grieving and got up anyway and made something beautiful with your hands because I taught you that grief is just love with nowhere to put itself. You can burn every charm in the world. I will still be the shape your kindness makes."*

He bowed his head over the charm, over the little spiral that wasn't quite a spiral, and pressed it to his lips like a man kissing a forehead.

*"Now sleep,"* said Maren Halloran, dead thirteen years and still managing him effortlessly. *"Both of you. Tomorrow the road gets worse, and my daughter — ours — will need you rested. And Isandro? Keep the kettle warm. One of these days you'll understand why I always said that, and you'll laugh, and I wish I could be there for it."*

The voice faded like a tide going out. Not cut off — *released*, the seal dissolving, the moment finally allowed to end. The charm lay in his palm, plain bone, silent forever, just a splinter of oak coffin-lid worn smooth by a decade of loving.

Isandro sat by the fire until it burned to embers, and then he did the bravest thing he had done since leaving Vaelmoor.

He set the charm down on the flat of the altar stone behind him, upright, facing the east window where dawn would come. Not thrown away. Not carried. Given a place of honor, like a tool whose work was finished.

Then he lay down on the plaster mound beside Sefa, and sleep took him almost at once, dreamless and deep.

* * *

He woke to grey light and birdsong and a small hand pressed flat against his chest, directly over his heart.

Sefa knelt above him, hair hanging like a curtain, studying his face with an intensity she usually reserved for fires and running water. And she was humming — low, urgent, a pattern he didn't know, repeating, insistent.

But that wasn't what froze him.

At the altar stone, where he'd left the charm, the air shimmered faintly green. And through the east window, where dawn was just beginning to gild the fen, Isandro could see the road north — and along it, at the far limit of sight, a line of figures moving with the unhurried coordination of soldiers.

Pale cloaks. Iron rods catching the light.

Sefa hummed again, louder, and took her hand off his chest and pointed north, at the figures, and then — slowly, deliberately — pointed at herself, and then at the horizon beyond them, where far away and impossibly faint, a smudge of grey-green rose above the fens.

The wood. The true wood, the Weeping Wood itself, closer than he'd dared hope.

And between it and them: the Pale Office, marching.
