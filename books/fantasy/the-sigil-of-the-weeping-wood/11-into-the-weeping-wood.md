## 11. Into the Weeping Wood

Sefa had been a tree once. She was almost sure of it.

The memory did not come to her the way Isandro's memories came to him — in pictures, in little boxes of time with beginnings and ends. It came to her the way weather comes. She remembered being many. She remembered drinking light through a thousand green hands at once, and the slow sweet argument of roots underground, and the long conversations that took an entire season to say one sentence. She remembered patience the way humans remember their own names.

Then she remembered being small. Being one. Being *carried*, in arms that shook, through fire.

Both memories were true. That was the terrible part. She was not a girl who had been a tree, and she was not a tree dreaming it was a girl. She was both, folded together like dough, and every day the folding hurt a little more.

But today — today the trees ahead of her were calling her name.

She could not have said what her name was in that language. It was not a word. It was more like the feeling of rain arriving after drought, or the particular weight of a bird landing on a branch that has been waiting for it. But she knew it was her name, and she knew the ones saying it were dying as they said it.

She stopped at the wood's edge and looked up at Isandro.

He was looking at the forest the way people look at graves.

"Is that it?" he asked, though he knew. He had known for three days, since the road turned gray and the crows stopped flying over it and went around instead, the way sensible creatures avoid a wound.

Sefa nodded. Then, because he had been teaching her and she wanted him to know she was learning, she said: "Home." Her voice came out rough, like a door not opened in years. Words still cost her something each time, like coins counted out of a nearly empty purse.

Isandro smiled, but it was one of his wrong smiles, the kind that didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Home," he repeated. "Right. Well. Let's hope it doesn't mind guests."

He always talked like that when he was frightened — making little jokes that weren't jokes, filling the air with words so the silence couldn't get in. Sefa had learned this about him the way she learned everything now, by listening with more than her ears. Under his voice his heart was going fast, and under his heart there was grief laid out in careful rows, like charms on a table, and under the grief something warm and stubborn that she had decided, privately, to call *him*.

She took his hand.

They went into the Weeping Wood together.

* * *

The first thing was the color. Or rather, the absence of it.

Sefa had seen the world through human eyes for most of a year now, and she had gotten used to how loud colors were — the red of berries shouting, the blue of sky insisting. The wood had none of that. Everything here was gray. Not the gray of stone or ash, which are honest grays, but the gray of a thing that used to be another color and has forgotten which. The trunks rose around them tall as towers and pale as bone, and from every branch hung slow drops of sap so dark they looked like holes cut in the air.

*Drip.*

*Drip.*

*Drip.*

It was the only sound. No birds. No insects. Even the wind moved through the branches without touching them, as if afraid.

Sefa felt the trees before she saw them move. It began as a tingling in her feet, then her legs, then spreading up through her middle like warmth after cold water — and she understood, with a jolt of joy so sharp it was almost pain, that they *knew* her.

Not the way humans knew her, as the mute child, the wood-touched girl, the thing bounty hunters wanted to sell. They knew her as the grove knows spring. They knew her the way she had once known them, root to root, season to season, all those centuries folded into one long green thought.

*Little vessel,* the trees said, in the language older than kingdoms. *You came back. You came back so small.*

*I came back,* she answered, without speaking, without trying. It just poured out of her, the way water finds low ground. *I'm sorry I'm small. I'm sorry it took so long.*

And the whole gray forest shivered.

It started at the edge and moved inward, a ripple running through a thousand pale trunks, and where it passed, things happened. A dead branch split along its length and a single leaf unfurled, gray-green, trembling. Moss woke on the north side of a trunk like a slow exhaled breath. Somewhere above them a canopy that had been bare for thirteen years produced one flower — just one, white and crooked and absurdly brave — and let it fall, and Isandro caught it without seeming to decide to, and stood there holding it in his palm like it might bite him.

"Sefa," he said quietly. "What's happening?"

"They're waking," she said. The word cost her. She paid it gladly.

"Is that good?"

She thought about it. Joy was rising in her like sap; something underneath the joy was beginning, very quietly, to hurt.

"I don't know," she said honestly.

* * *

By the time they made their first camp, deep enough into the wood that Vaelmoor's distant smudge of smoke had vanished behind the hills, Sefa understood what the hurting was.

She was sitting against a trunk — a different trunk than the last time, because the trees kept shifting their roots to be closer to her, politely, the way dogs crowd a beloved owner — and she was feeding them. She hadn't decided to. It was simply happening, the way it happens when you love something hungry: you give, and you keep giving, and you do not notice the plate emptying.

Every leaf that unfurled took something out of her. Every drop of black sap that thickened toward gold, every bit of moss, every brave idiot flower — hers. Drawn up through whatever she was, this folded thing, this girl-grove, and given away.

She watched her own hand. It lay pale in the gloom, and beneath the skin of her wrist she could see faint lines darkening, green-brown, like veins in a leaf. Pretty, if you didn't think about it. She thought about it.

*Sefa?*

Isandro was watching her across the little fire. He had built it small, half-hidden by a fallen trunk, though she suspected no fire in the world would make the Pale Office lose their trail now. He had that look again — the one where his eyes did the asking because his mouth was too proud to.

*Tired,* she told him. And then, when his face did the thing it did, she added: *A little. Only a little.* Which was a lie, or mostly one, and she felt bad about it, but she was learning from him that some lies were small kindnesses, and she wasn't sure yet whether that made them better or worse.

He got up, limped over — his knee had never forgiven the ditch outside Gallows Row — and sat down beside her, close enough that she could lean against his shoulder, which she did, because it was one of the best things in either of her lives.

"You're doing it already," he said. Not a question. He'd felt it through her hand maybe, or read it in the way the nearest branches had crept four feet closer since sunset. "The waking. It's coming out of you."

Sefa considered denying it again. But lying to Isandro felt different than lying about tiredness; it sat wrong in her chest, among all the honest bones he carried.

"Yes," she said.

"How much is left?"

She looked up at him. His face in the firelight was all angles and worry, a face carved by someone who loved difficult material. Ten years of hiding had taught that face to expect loss. She hated that she was about to feed it.

"I don't know," she said. "Enough?"

"Sefa."

"It's like—" She searched for the words. Human words were still hard; they only had one meaning each, poor flat things. "Like soup. If everyone needs soup, and you are the soup."

Isandro stared at her. Then, horribly, wonderfully, he laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him, brief and cracked and warm. "That may be the worst explanation I've ever heard."

"It's true, though."

"I know it's true. That's why it's terrible." He rubbed his face with both hands, and when he lowered them he wasn't laughing anymore. "Listen to me. In the city, when I sold a charm, I always told people the same thing: the dead don't come back. Whatever you hear, it's an echo, a memory wearing their voice. Don't build your life on echoes." He paused. "I told myself that too. For ten years I told myself that about Maren."

Sefa waited. He almost never talked about Maren, and when he did it was like watching someone carry something very heavy up a very long stair.

"But you're not an echo," he said. "You're not a memory wearing a girl's shape. You're *here*. You eat my terrible cooking. You steal the blanket. You laugh at me when I talk to my own carvings, which is rude, by the way, and also deserved." His voice caught. "So we're not spending you. Do you understand me? We get to the Covenant Stone, we do whatever the old ritual is, Mother Quill's book says nothing about a vessel pouring herself out like — like *soup* — and you come out the other side a person. One person. Whoever you choose to be."

"One person," Sefa repeated softly.

The nearest tree rustled. It sounded, to her ear, less like agreement than like a large patient creature declining to comment.

* * *

She dreamed, that night, in the old way — the wide way, the many-way — and woke before dawn with tears already drying on her face.

In the dream she had been the whole wood again. All of it, gray and dying, but *hers*, and every root had cried out to her at once: *stay, stay, come back, be us, be whole, you were never meant to be so small.* And part of her — the tree part, the ancient patient part — had *wanted* to. That was the thing she could never tell Isandro, the thing that sat in her chest heavier than hunger: some part of her missed being enormous. Some part of her was homesick for herself.

But another part had woken in the dream with mud on its boots and a charm-carver's calloused hand holding hers, and that part had said, in rough new words it was still paying for: *No. I'm the girl. I'm the girl who gets soup explanations and steals blankets.*

She crawled out from under the blanket — Isandro, asleep sitting up against a root, had stolen it back sometime in the night, which she supposed served her right — and walked a little way into the darkness between the pale trunks.

The wood was quieter at night, but not silent. The trees murmured in their sleep, the way she imagined old men murmur, full of unfinished sentences. She put her palm flat against the nearest trunk.

*Wake,* she said gently. *Slowly. I'm here. There's time.*

There wasn't time. Thirteen moons, Mother Quill had said, and eleven had turned, and the road behind them had hunters on it, and somewhere behind the hunters came Aldric Vane with his quiet voice and his careful questions, and somewhere behind everything came the thirteenth moon like a door closing.

But the trees had waited thirteen years, and waiting was the one thing they had never forgotten how to do.

*Little vessel,* the trunk murmured under her hand. *You are burning.*

*I know.*

*You will be ash before the Stone.*

*I might not be.*

*You were our spring,* it said, with a sorrow so vast and slow it made her human heart ache inside its little cage of ribs. *We would rather sleep forever than watch you burn.*

Sefa leaned her forehead against the bark. Above her, far up in the gray dark, a branch creaked, and a single leaf unfolded, and another, and another — the tree unable to help itself, waking a little more despite its own promise, drawn to her warmth like frost to a window.

"No," she whispered. "Watch me bloom instead."

* * *

Isandro found her there at first light.

She heard him before she saw him — the uneven footfall, the soft curse when a root caught his boot, the little huff of breath he made when he was pretending not to be worried. He came around the trunk and stopped, and she watched his face do several things in quick succession: alarm at the green-brown lines climbing her forearm, grief at the leaf-shadow in her hair, and then, finally, decision. He knelt down in the wet moss in front of her, joints cracking, and took both her hands in his.

"New rule," he said. "Effective immediately. No more solo conferences with the vegetable kingdom."

"They're scared," Sefa said.

"So am I. You don't see me holding meetings about it." He turned her wrist over gently, studying the darkening lines, and she felt his pulse jump against her fingers. When he spoke again his voice had gone carefully level, the voice he used for customers who were about to cry. "How much did last night cost you?"

"A little."

"Sefa."

"*Some,*" she amended.

He was quiet for a moment. Then he reached into his coat and brought out the carving knife — the good one, the one with the worn horn handle — and set it on the moss between them, and looked at her with an expression she had never quite seen on him before. Fierce and helpless at once, like a man guarding a door he already knew would break.

"I spent ten years keeping the voices of the dead alive in little bits of bone, because letting them go felt like killing them twice," he said. "I know exactly what it is to hold onto something past the point of sense. So I'm not going to tell you to stop feeding them. I don't even know if you can." His thumb brushed the leaf-veins on her wrist, feather-light. "But I want you to hear this clearly, because I'm told I mumble: whatever happens at that Stone, you and I walk out of this wood together. Both of us. I didn't cross half the country and kill my entire stock of ten years' work to deliver a forest a dead girl."

"Not dead," Sefa said. "Soup."

"Don't make me laugh, I'm being stern."

"You're crying."

He wiped his face with his sleeve, roughly, as if the tears were a stain. "Wind," he said. "Very windy forest, this."

There was no wind at all. Around them, in fact, the morning had gone utterly still — and Sefa realized, with a slow spreading wonder, that the stillness was attention. Every tree within a hundred paces had gone silent and taut, listening. To him. To the man with no magic in him at all except stubbornness, promising the dying wood he'd save its last vessel or die trying.

*Honest bones,* the trees murmured, in their language older than kingdoms, and Sefa laughed through her own tears, because it was true, and because for one whole moment the burning in her chest felt less like ash and more like dawn.

Isandro stood, wincing, and shouldered the pack, and held out his hand.

"Come on, then," he said. "Covenant Stone's three days east, if the Rootbound's map is worth the bread we traded for it. Three days of walking, three days of you not turning yourself into mulch, and three days of me telling you about the time Aldric Vane fell in the fountain at the Guildhall, which is a story I've been saving for when morale needed it."

Sefa took his hand. It was warm and rough and certain, and she matched her small quick steps to his slower ones as they walked deeper into the gray wood, past trunks that leaned toward them as they passed, dropping slow bright beads of golden sap like applause.

Behind them, far off through the pale aisles of the forest, something else made a sound.

Not a drip. Not a creak. A whistle — thin and doubled, the two-note signal of the Pale Office — carried on air that had held no wind all morning.

Isandro's hand tightened around hers.

"Don't look back," he said quietly. "Walk faster."
