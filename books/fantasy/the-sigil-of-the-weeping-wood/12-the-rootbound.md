## 12. The Rootbound

The trees began to watch them on the third morning.

Isandro didn't know how else to put it. He'd spent his life around wood — carving it, boiling it, whispering to the small slivers of bone that were, in their way, also wood's cousins, all of them the quiet architecture of once-living things — and he knew what dead timber felt like. It felt like furniture. Like patience. Like nothing.

These trees did not feel like nothing.

They walked a game trail that wound north and east through country where the mapmakers had given up and written *here be ash*, and all day Sefa kept stopping, tilting her head at the gray trunks as though listening to a conversation Isandro couldn't quite catch. Which was, he was beginning to understand, exactly what she was doing.

"What are they saying?" he asked, for perhaps the tenth time since they'd crossed into the wood's outer fringe.

Sefa considered her answer with the gravity of a magistrate. She had words now — a dozen of them, maybe fifteen, each one earned like a coin dug from a riverbed — and she spent none of them lightly.

"*Waiting,*" she said finally. Then, after a pause, with an expression he could only describe as disapproving: "*Long.*"

"Ah." Isandro shifted his pack on his shoulders. "Well. They've been waiting thirteen years, I imagine. A few more days won't kill them."

Sefa looked at him the way she always looked at him when he made a joke about dying things, which was to say: with the flat, unblinking patience of someone who had recently been a grove and found his humor wanting.

"They're not the only ones waiting," he added quickly.

That got him a small nod, which from Sefa was roughly equivalent to a standing ovation.

* * *

The Rootbound found them at dusk, and Isandro would spend the rest of his life failing to explain how it happened. One moment they were making cold camp in a hollow beneath a leaning birch; the next, the forest had grown people.

There was no other way to say it. The shadows between the trunks thickened, and out of the thickening came figures who moved like branches in wind — slow, deliberate, creaking faintly. They were human-shaped, mostly. Two arms, two legs, heads in the right places. But their skin had gone the color and texture of old bark, fissured and gray-green, moss growing in the creases of their necks and knuckles. One of them had a fern growing out of the hollow of his collarbone, and seemed entirely at peace about it. Another's hair had become something between lichen and dreadlocks, and she wore it with more dignity than any courtier Isandro had ever seen.

Seven of them. Ringed around the campfire before Isandro had even gotten to his feet.

His hand went to the knife at his belt, then thought better of it. You didn't knife a tree. He'd tried pruning one once, early in his apprenticeship, and it had held a grudge.

"We mean no harm," he said, which sounded absurd even as he said it, because he was fairly sure *he* was the only party in this clearing capable of meaning harm.

The ring tightened anyway.

Then Sefa stood up.

She did it slowly, the way she did everything, and she lifted both hands — palms out, fingers spread — and made a sound low in her throat. Not humming this time. Something older. Something that made the fire lean sideways and made every Rootbound in the ring go utterly still, the stillness of things that had forgotten how stillness worked.

And then the one with the fern in her collarbone dropped to her knees.

It was not graceful. Bark skin does not bend well at the joints. But down she went, and after her the others, in ones and twos, until every guardian in the clearing was kneeling in the leaves around a mud-streaked eight-year-old girl who looked mildly embarrassed by the whole business.

"*Vessel,*" said the fern-woman, and her voice sounded like wind moving through a hollow reed. "*Little mother. Green-that-remembers.*"

"She prefers Sefa," Isandro said.

Every bark-gray head turned toward him.

He cleared his throat. "I'm just saying. When you've walked three hundred miles with someone, you get to know what they prefer."

* * *

Their leader was called Sorrel, though Isandro suspected that, like everything else about the Rootbound, the name was a concession rather than a truth. She was tall and gaunt under her bark, older than the others by some margin, with eyes the deep brown of heartwood and a face so fissured it took him a long moment to find the expression on it. When he did find it, he wished he hadn't. It was the face of someone who had done arithmetic with lives and never liked the totals.

She led them — Sefa walking among the guardians like a small queen among very strange courtiers, Isandro trailing behind like the queen's luggage — to a settlement built into the side of a ravine. It wasn't a village, exactly. It was more that the ravine had been persuaded to contain dwellings: rooms hollowed into living roots, walkways of woven branch that had clearly grown that way rather than been tied, lanterns of glowing sap-drip hanging from eaves of yew. Everything smelled of loam and rain and, underneath it, faintly, of grief — the specific grief of a place that had lost people and remembered exactly how many.

"You eat," Sorrel said. It was not a question. She pushed a bowl into his hands — some root stew, dense and earthy, tasting the way a cellar smells — and watched him with those heartwood eyes while he ate.

"I do," Isandro said. "Thank you."

"The child does not need it. She draws on the wood now. You have noticed?"

"I've noticed she gets tired when the trees wake up. And that her wounds—" He stopped.

"Grew leaves," Sorrel said. "Yes. We saw."

"It's killing her," Isandro said quietly. "Isn't it. Every time she feeds them."

Sorrel said nothing, which was its own answer, and turned away, and Isandro sat in the root-hollow holding his empty bowl and understanding, for the first time in full, what the next weeks were going to cost.

* * *

They came for him at midnight.

Not soldiers. Not hunters. Just Sorrel, alone, standing at the mouth of his hollow with the sap-lanterns dimmed behind her, looking less like a woman and more like the concept of consequence.

"Walk with me," she said.

"Now?" Isandro asked, because he was tired and frightened and when he was both of those things at once his mouth tended to make jokes without consulting him first. "It's midnight. In my experience, midnight walks with stern women end in one of two ways, and neither is breakfast."

Sorrel waited.

"Right," Isandro said. He got up. "Walking."

She took him along the ravine floor to a place where the trees grew close and old — older than anything else in the wood, their trunks wide as wagons, their bark smooth and pale despite the gray death that had taken the rest of the canopy. There was a circle there. An open space, floored in moss, ringed by those ancient trunks. The Rootbound called it the Listening Ring, he learned later. At the time, all Isandro knew was that the moment he stepped inside it, the hair on his arms stood up, and somewhere very far away, something inhaled.

"You sold voices," Sorrel said.

It wasn't an accusation, the way she said it. It was worse. It was a fact, set down on a table between them like a stone.

"For ten years," she said. "Bone charms. The dead's last words, bottled and sold. We heard of you, in the early years. The carver in Vaelmoor. The one who listened."

"I did," Isandro said. "Yes."

"Why?"

And here is the thing about Isandro Coldharbour, the thing that Maren had loved in him and that he had spent ten years burying under trade craft and careful silence: he had never once, in all that decade, lied to himself about what he was. He lied to Aldric. He lied to customers, a little, about the comfort charms could bring. But in the dark, in his own skull, he had always known the shape of it.

"Cowardice," he said. "Mostly. And grief wearing cowardice's coat."

"Say it properly."

He took a breath. The Listening Ring seemed to lean in.

"When the purges came, I hid," he said. His voice sounded very loud in the moss-floored silence. "Maren didn't hide. Maren ran *toward* the trouble, because that was Maren, and I told myself she'd come back, and she didn't, and instead of going after her — instead of finding out what happened, instead of picking up the work she died for — I stayed in Vaelmoor and I carved little bones and I sold pieces of the dead to people who missed them, and I told myself that was resistance. That keeping the voices alive was defiance." He laughed, and it came out cracked. "It wasn't defiance. It was a man hiding in a cellar, charging four coppers a whisper. I heard the dead every day for ten years and I never once listened to what they were actually telling me, which was *finish it*. Which was *the covenant is broken and someone has to mend it and it will not be us, we're dead, it has to be you.*"

The Ring was silent.

Somewhere above, a branch creaked, and Isandro had the sudden, unshakable sense that it had creaked in agreement.

"And the money," Sorrel said. Relentless. Heartwood doesn't flinch. "You took payment."

"I did."

"You chose your customers. You decided whose dead deserved hearing."

"I did."

"A widow buys her husband's last words. What of the pauper? What of the family the Office burned in the street, whose bones went to the lime-pits? Did you sell them their voices back?"

"No," Isandro said. "No, I— no."

"Say the rest."

He closed his eyes. And the Ring opened them again.

* * *

Later, he would struggle to describe what happened in the Listening Ring, because it was not sound, exactly, and it was not memory, exactly, though it used both the way a river uses a channel. Sorrel raised one bark-split hand, and the ancient trees around the circle *turned toward him* — he felt it, a vast slow attention, like weather changing — and then the dead came.

All of them. Every soul whose voice had passed through his hands in ten years of bone and wire and whispered commerce.

The widow's husband, a fisherman, drowned off the Gray Rocks, saying her name, saying *tell her the blue coat, tell her I liked the blue coat.* The cooper who'd died of lung-rot asking if his shop had been sold and whether the new owner was keeping fair prices. A child — gods, a child, fevered, seven years old, asking for water that no charm could give her. The hanged mage from the Gallows Road whose cousin had paid Isandro a month's rent for his brother's final breath, and the breath had been a curse on the Pale Office, and Isandro had softened it before delivery because he'd been afraid.

He heard them all. Not one at a time. *All.* Ten years of them at once, layered and overlapping, joy and terror and tedium and love, the whole unmixed chorus of the ordinary dead, and underneath it all, beneath every voice like bedrock beneath soil, the same thing he had refused to hear for a decade:

*We trusted you with our endings.*

He was on his knees. He didn't remember getting there. The moss was cold and damp through his trousers and his hands were pressed against the ground as if he could push the whole weight of ten years down into it, and he was weeping, ugly and open, the way he hadn't wept at Maren's funeral because Aldric had been watching, because the Office had been watching, because a man hiding in a cellar learns to grieve quietly or not at all.

"I'm sorry," he said, and meant every soul in the chorus. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm sorry."

The Ring let him say it perhaps forty times before the voices began, gently, to recede — not silenced, but *released*, folding away layer by layer like a tide going out, until the last one left was the fisherman, saying *blue coat, tell her the blue coat,* and then even that faded, and Isandro knelt alone in the moss with Sorrel standing over him and the ancient trees standing over her.

"Do you know why we test you this way?" she asked.

"To see if I break?"

"To see if you *kneel*," Sorrel said. "The Office men break. Breaking is easy; anyone can shatter. But kneelers — kneelers can be trusted with something heavier than themselves." She crouched, with the creaking difficulty of her bark joints, until her heartwood eyes were level with his. "You hid for ten years. That is true, and you will carry it, because carrying true things is the whole work of being alive. But you also walked a child three hundred miles through a dying wood with nothing but a knife and a bad joke for armor. Both things are true. The trees weighed both."

"And?" His voice was wrecked. He didn't care. "What did they weigh?"

Sorrel studied him for a long moment.

"They say you sing honest," she said. "Like the child says. They say your voice is crooked but true, like a fencepost grown around wire." She rose, slowly, joints crackling. "You are forgiven."

"Only barely," Isandro guessed.

"Oh, *extremely* barely," Sorrel agreed. "Come. Sleep. Tomorrow we teach you the old words — the ones the child will need at the Stone, the ones your wife died carrying. And carver—"

He stopped at the edge of the Ring.

"If you fail her," Sorrel said, "we will plant you where you fall and let the moss have its way with you. Understand?"

"Perfectly," said Isandro Coldharbour, and discovered that he was smiling, because for the first time in thirteen years, someone had told him exactly where he stood.

* * *

But sleep did not come easily, and it did not come kindly.

Near dawn, drifting in that thin gray place between exhaustion and rest, Isandro heard it: a whistle. Far off, down the southern slopes, at the very edge of hearing — two notes, rising, repeated.

He knew that whistle. Every soul in Vaelmoor knew that whistle. The Pale Office patrol call, the sound that had emptied market squares and ended lives, and it should not be here, it could not be here, no patrol had ever come within fifty miles of the Weeping Wood—

Then a second whistle answered the first. Closer. Northward.

They weren't patrolling. Patrolling didn't move in pairs across dark country before dawn.

They were closing.

Isandro sat up in the root-hollow, heart hammering, and found Sefa already awake beside him, her pale eyes reflecting the dim sap-light, her small hand pressed flat against the ground as though she could feel footsteps through the roots themselves.

"*Iron,*" she said softly. "*Coming.*"
