## 13. What Loyalty Costs

The rain had followed Aldric Vane north for three days, as though it had been assigned to him.

He sat his horse badly — he had never learned to sit a horse well, and the animal knew it, and had opinions about it that it expressed at every root and rut along the Gallows Road. His squad was half a day behind him, slowed by a broken axle on the supply cart and by Sergeant Omma's conviction that no order was truly an order until it had been discussed over breakfast. By the time they reached the shrine at Harrow's Fork, Aldric intended to be long gone from it.

He had left them a map. That was the word he used to himself — *left them a map* — and not *misled them*, and certainly not *betrayed*. The map showed the road east of the river, which was a perfectly good road, wide and well-drained and entirely wrong. A man could spend years learning the difference between those two sentences. Aldric suspected he was going to learn it in about an hour.

The trees thickened as he rode. Not living trees — the Office had seen to that, years ago, in its tidy way — but the stumps and gallows-poles of the purges, blackened things that leaned together like conspirators. Rain dripped from their broken limbs with a patience that felt personal. Somewhere ahead, past these dead sentries, the Weeping Wood began in earnest: gray, vast, and breathing.

And somewhere in it, if the bounty hunters' testimony held, was Isandro Coldharbour, carrying a child toward a stone that would either save the world or get everyone he loved burned out of it.

Aldric dismounted at the treeline because his horse refused to go further, gave her the last of the dried apples as an apology, and walked into the gray.

* * *

He found the camp by smell first — woodsmoke, faintly sweet, the way the Weeping Wood's own timber burned when it burned at all — then by sound: a low humming, tuneless and old, like wind through a house with no one in it. The child. Then a second sound under it, rougher, human, a man talking softly to something he was carving.

Aldric stood at the edge of the little clearing for a long moment, rain running off his Office-gray cloak, and watched his oldest friend teach a mute girl to whistle through a hollow reed.

It was such a small thing. It was such an ordinary, warm, damning thing. Isandro sat cross-legged by the fire with his coat off despite the cold, demonstrating the reed with the seriousness of a scholar defusing a curse, and Sefa copied him, and produced a sound like a dying goose, and Isandro laughed — actually laughed, head back, helpless — and the sound went through Aldric like a knife finding an old scar.

Thirteen years since he'd heard Isandro laugh. He'd assumed the capacity had been carved out of him along with everything else.

"You're loud," Aldric said, stepping into the clearing. "You were always loud. Even your crimes are loud."

Isandro came up off the ground so fast the fire scattered sparks. One hand went behind him — toward the girl, not toward a weapon; Aldric noted that, and hated how much it mattered to him. The bone carver's face did three things in a single breath: fear, recognition, and then something worse than either, something like pity.

"Aldric." Isandro's voice was careful. "Where's your squad?"

"Lost," Aldric said. "Bad map."

"You drew their maps for six years."

"I know." He let the rain do some of the work of hiding his face. "I'm alone, Isandro. I give you my word on that, for whatever my word has been worth to you lately."

"For whatever it's ever been worth," Isandro said, but there was no venom in it, only exhaustion, and that was somehow more painful. Behind him, the child had gone very still. Her eyes — pale as birchwood, pale as nothing human — fixed on Aldric with an attention that made his skin crawl. It wasn't the look of a child assessing a stranger. It was the look of a forest assessing weather.

"I need to talk to you," Aldric said. "Alone."

"She stays where I can see her."

"She doesn't understand us."

"She understands more than you think," Isandro said quietly, "and I'll not have her hear this alone while I'm pretending to argue with you about the price of bone."

So it would be in front of the girl. Fine. Aldric had rehearsed this speech across three days of wet miles, and in none of the rehearsals had it gone well, so there was no reason to expect better now.

"The Office knows about the Rootbound," he said. "Not precisely where — but the shape of it. Surviving hunters talked. By tomorrow there will be forty men at the treeline with fire-pots and iron, and the day after, sixty, and they will burn every gray tree between here and the Stone to keep you from reaching it. You cannot outrun that. You cannot outcarve that. There is one version of this story where anyone walks away, and it is the short one."

Isandro said nothing. The fire popped. The child hummed, very low, and Aldric realized with a lurch that the nearest dead trees were leaning — imperceptibly, unmistakably — toward her.

"Give me the location of the Rootbound," Aldric pressed. "Give me your confession, signed. In exchange I can deliver the child alive. Unharmed. The First Interrogator wants her contained, not dead — containment means food, shelter, a room, a life, such as it is. And you—" He stopped. Started again. "You'd hang. But hanging is quick, Isandro. What happens here otherwise isn't quick. Fire isn't quick. I've read the reports from the Sundering. I've read what the fire did to the people who ran into the trees instead of away from them."

"You've read reports," Isandro said.

"Yes."

"Maren was a report once."

The words landed exactly where they were aimed. Aldric took them, because he deserved them, and let them sit in his chest like swallowed coals. "Yes," he said. "She was."

"And you're standing in the rain offering me the same bargain she was offered." Isandro's voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about Isandro; he never rose, he descended, calm as well-water. "Confess, name names, live. She refused. Look how the world rewarded her."

"She'd be alive—"

"She'd be *alive*," Isandro agreed. "In a cell. Or worse, free, having sold out everyone she loved, sitting by her fire every night hearing the names of the people she buried under her signature. I know what survival costs, Aldric. I paid it for ten years, one charm at a time. I am *expert* in the currency." He shook his head slowly. "No."

"No," Aldric repeated.

"No. I won't give you the Rootbound. I won't sign anything. And I won't hand you the child, who is not a package to be delivered, whatever your First Interrogator's filing system says." Isandro stepped forward, and up close Aldric could see how thin he'd gone, how the road had eaten him, and how none of it had touched his eyes. "Thank you for coming alone. Thank you for the bad map — don't argue, I know you, I taught you cards, you cheat exactly like this. Now go home. Tell them you lost me in the deep wood. Let them hang you for incompetence instead of complicity; it's a gentler rope."

"It's the same rope."

"It's the same rope," Isandro conceded. "But it's a better story."

And he smiled. Small, crooked, the old smile, the one from the apprenticeship, from the nights when they'd been two nobodies carving bones in a garret and laughing at their own ambitions — and Aldric felt the last load-bearing wall inside himself crack straight down the middle.

Because here was the truth he'd been hauling north through three days of rain, heavier than any fire-pot: his loyalty had never actually been to the Pale Office. It had been to the lie that made the Office bearable. *They must be right,* the lie said, *because I helped them, and I cannot have helped a monstrous thing.* Thirteen years ago he had stood in a white room and given testimony against Maren Coldharbour — true facts arranged into a false shape — and they had promoted him for it, and every promotion since had been a brick laid on that foundation. He had built a career on a woman's grave and called it duty. And now the wood was regrowing, and the child was its vessel, and the Office's own sealed records — Aldric had read them, God forgive him, he'd pulled them himself — said plainly that the Sundering had begun with the mages' strike at the Stone, not the wood's. The Edict of Silence stood on a lie. His whole life stood on the lie.

"I can't go home," Aldric said aloud, surprised by his own voice. "That's the thing, Isandro. I tried to make myself go home and I can't find the road."

Isandro's expression shifted — wariness softening into something older and sadder. "Aldric—"

"I testified against her." It came out of him like a splinter finally drawn. "Maren. They asked me what I'd seen, and I told them the truth, all the pieces of it, and they assembled the pieces into a noose. And I signed it. And I told myself for thirteen years that it was the law, that someone else would have done it, that my silence would only have hanged us both. And maybe that's even true. But I signed it, Isandro, and then I ate well and slept well and got promoted, and I never once — not once — went hungry over it until this week."

The fire cracked. The child watched them both with her tree-pale eyes, and the dead trees leaned closer, drinking the words in, and some rotten part of Aldric thought: *of course, even the dead get a front-row seat.*

"So no," he said. "I won't beg twice. I'm asking you one last time — surrender, and I spend whatever I have left buying her a cell with a window. Refuse, and we fight, and I arrest you or you hurt me, and either way the squad finds this camp eventually, because I'm good but I'm not a miracle." He unhooked his cloak, dropped it over a stump, and drew his baton — iron-cored, Office-issue, weighted — because he would not draw steel on Isandro Coldharbour, not for anything, and both of them knew it. "Refuse," he said again, quieter.

"I refuse," Isandro said. And, absurdly, apologetically, he picked up a stick of firewood.

* * *

Fighting Isandro was like fighting weather.

He'd never been trained — that was the maddening thing. No forms, no drills, no doctrine. Just ten years of a man alone in a shop above the harbor, learning in secret how to move, and a lifetime of wiry strength from the carving knife, and absolutely no honor whatsoever. The firewood came at Aldric's knee before he'd finished raising the baton. He turned it, caught a wrist-blow on the iron core, drove forward — and Isandro simply wasn't there, was sideways, was hitting him again in the exact same spot at the knee, patiently, like a carpenter.

"Your stance," Isandro panted, dodging, "is still — appalling — you lean —"

"My stance," Aldric gasped, swinging, missing, catching a root and nearly going down, "was good enough — to take Maren's — "

He hadn't meant to say it. It escaped him like blood from a wound, and it froze them both.

Isandro hit him. Properly, this time — a short, ugly blow across the temple that rang Aldric's skull like a bell and put the whole gray world briefly sideways. When it cleared, he was on his back in the mud and ash, the sky dripping on his face, and Isandro stood over him with the firewood raised.

And didn't bring it down.

Aldric looked up the length of the improvised weapon to his oldest friend's face, and saw there the entire argument in miniature: the fury, the grief, and underneath both, immovable as roots, the simple fact that Isandro Coldharbour had never in his life been able to destroy anything that remembered being loved.

"Do it," Aldric said. "Honestly. It would simplify matters."

"Don't be stupid."

"It's my defining quality. Ask the Office."

Isandro lowered the firewood. He was shaking — Aldric saw it now, saw the cost, saw that the man was holding himself upright mostly out of habit. "Get up," Isandro said roughly. "Get up and get out of this wood, Aldric. Go home. Confess, don't confess, I don't care anymore. But if your squad burns this forest with her in it—" his voice failed, steadied, failed again, "—then whatever we were to each other dies with it, and I want you to know, before it does, that I already forgave you. Years ago. For her. I had to, or the charms would have driven me mad. So this isn't about forgiveness. This is just arithmetic."

"That's worse," Aldric said, from the mud. "For your information. Forgiveness I could have lived without earning. Arithmetic is going to haunt me."

"It should."

Isandro turned away, gathered the girl against his side — she went willingly, but her pale eyes stayed on Aldric a moment longer, and something passed over his skin like the touch of leaves — and then paused, his back turned, the firelight making a ragged halo of his hair.

"They'll reach the Stone by the twelfth moon," he said. "If you're going to choose, Aldric, choose soon. The moon doesn't wait, and neither does the fire."

Then they were gone, into the gray, the child's low humming fading between the dead trees like a tide going out, and Aldric Vane lay in the mud of the Weeping Wood with rain on his face and his baton in the ashes of another man's fire, and laughed — one short, cracked bark of it, entirely without humor — because after thirteen years he finally understood the shape of his life:

Every road he had ever been given was the wrong one, beautifully mapped.

He got up. He retrieved his cloak. And he turned north, deeper into the wood, walking toward the Covenant Stone with no plan, no orders, and no lie left large enough to stand on — which meant, for the first time in his adult life, he was traveling light.

Behind him, far to the south, faint on the wet wind, came the sound of forty men and their fire-pots, marching.
