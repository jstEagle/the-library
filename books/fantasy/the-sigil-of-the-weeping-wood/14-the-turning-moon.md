## 14. The Turning Moon

The twelfth moon rose the color of a healing bruise, and Isandro knew it was time because the Rootbound stopped singing.

For three days they had sung — if you could call it that. It was less melody than weather, a low vibration that came up through the soles of his boots and settled somewhere behind his sternum, and Sefa had hummed along with it while she ground bark for poultices and braided reeds into rope and did all the hundred small things that had become her way of being alive. But tonight, as the moon cleared the ridge of gray hills to the north, the singing stopped all at once, the way a room goes quiet when someone important walks in.

Isandro set down his knife and the antler tine he'd been carving — plain work now, no voices in it, just a spoon for Mother Ashen who'd lost hers in the bog — and walked out of the lean-to.

The whole grove was standing at its edge, facing north. Forty-odd of them: the Rootbound with their bark-scaled skin catching the moonlight like old armor, the handful of runaways and hedge-folk who'd drifted to the wood over the years, Mother Quill's empty stool by the cold fire where she used to sit and complain about her knees. And above them, the trees themselves had gone still. Not sleeping-still. Listening-still.

"They know," said Wren, the Rootbound woman with the birch-white face. She didn't turn around. "The wood felt the iron crossing the river an hour ago. Two hundred men. Fire-pots."

"Two hundred." Isandro said it flatly, because saying it any other way would have meant feeling it. "And how many of us?"

"Forty-one," Wren said. "Forty, if you don't count the girl, which nobody does, because she's not fighting."

"I count," said Sefa, appearing at his elbow with the particular silent suddenness she'd never lost from her mute months, "as one."

She was looking up at him, and Isandro made himself look back, and it cost him. The change had come slowly enough that he could pretend not to see it day to day — a strand of white here, a leaf-bud there — but the moonlight was honest, and under it Sefa's hair had gone fully silver, fine as spider silk, and along her left forearm where the bounty hunter's knife had cut her on the road, a row of small green leaves grew out of the healed scar, five of them, neat as buttons on a coat.

"You're not fighting," Isandro said.

"I'm the reason they're marching," she said. Her common tongue had grown fast once it started, though she still built sentences like someone arranging stones — carefully, each word chosen for weight. "So I am also the reason to stand. Those are the same thing."

"That's terrible logic."

"It is wood logic," she said, with dignity. "Roots do not argue."

He wanted to laugh. He wanted, more, to pick her up — she was eleven, maybe twelve, light as a bundle of kindling — and carry her south until the hills ran out and there was nothing behind them but sea. He did neither. He put his hand on her silver head instead, careful of the leaves, and together they watched the northern dark where two hundred men were walking toward them carrying fire.

* * *

The war council — Isandro refused to think of it as anything so grand, though Bramble, the youngest of the Rootbound, kept calling it that with obvious relish — convened around the old fire pit an hour later.

It went about as well as councils go when everyone present already knows the answer and is only arguing to avoid saying it aloud.

"We hold the ravine," said Thornhollow, a man so thoroughly merged with an oak that his shoulders had gone knotted and his voice came out with a creak in it, like wind through branches. "Iron can't climb wet stone. We let them funnel in, we drop the deadfall—"

"The deadfall kills four of them," said Wren. "There are two hundred. Do your arithmetic with roots, brother."

"We could scatter," offered a hedge-witch named Pell, who had survived thirteen years by being sensible in a way Isandro found both admirable and exhausting. "Melt into the deep wood. They burn the edge, they get bored, they go home. That's what happened last time."

"Last time," Wren said quietly, "there was no Covenant Stone waking up. Last time the wood was dying anyway. Now—" she gestured, and Isandro followed her hand to where, at the very center of the grove, a single tree stood greener than everything around it, its leaves actually green, actually moving in a wind the other trees couldn't feel. The Stone lay beneath its roots. Every day it grew a little louder. Isandro could almost hear it now, a pulse like a second heartbeat under his own. "Now there is something worth burning down. And they know it."

Silence. The fire cracked. Somewhere overhead, an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"So we stand," Isandro said.

Every head turned to him. He hadn't meant to say it first. He'd spent ten years in Vaelmoor perfecting the art of saying nothing at all, and here he was volunteering opinions in front of people who could probably grow him into mulch.

"We stand," he said again, because apparently the mouth had decided this without consulting him, "because scattering means they follow us deeper, and burning means they find the Stone anyway, and running means teaching Sefa that when something wants you dead, the correct answer is to be elsewhere. I've done the running. Ten years of it. It keeps you alive." He looked at his hands, the carver's hands, nicked and stained. "It doesn't keep anything else."

"Well," said Bramble, breaking the solemnity with the timing of a born troublemaker, "that was very inspiring. I cried a little. Inside, where the sap is."

A ripple of something moved through the group — not quite laughter, but its cousin, the thing laughter grows from. Even Thornhollow's creaking frame loosened. Isandro realized, with a small shock, that this was why they'd looked at him. Not for tactics. For that.

"Here's what we have," Wren said, taking the thread before it frayed. "The ravine. Deadfall at the north mouth. Pell's fog-work, if she has the strength."

"Some," Pell admitted. "An hour of it. Maybe two."

"And fire-pots come with their own weather," said a Rootbound called Marsh, whose skin had gone the brown-green of pond water. "We are flammable, most of us. I say this without shame. It is simply true."

"Then we fight where we're not fuel," Wren said. "Stone and water. And we make them pay for every yard of ash." She looked around the fire. "Anyone who wants to leave, leaves tonight, with my blessing and a pack of food. No shame in it. The wood will remember you kindly either way."

Nobody moved.

"Nobody?" Wren said. "Truly? I gave a lovely speech about no shame. I worked on it."

Bramble raised a hand. "I'm staying, but I want it noted that I considered leaving for nearly four seconds."

"Noted," said Wren dryly, "and entered into the wood's memory."

Isandro caught himself smiling, and then caught himself being surprised at the smiling. Grief and fear were sitting in him like swallowed stones, and yet here, among bark-skinned strangers and a witch with bad knees' worth of absence and a child with leaves growing out of her arm, the stones sat lighter than they had in a decade of comfortable rooms in Vaelmoor.

That, he thought, is either courage or stupidity, and tomorrow the iron will tell us which.

* * *

He found Sefa after midnight, sitting alone beneath the green tree, talking to it.

Not humming anymore. Talking — in her own tongue, the old one, the one older than kingdoms, the syllables falling soft and round like fruit dropping into grass. Isandro hung back in the shadow of a gray trunk and listened, understanding none of it and all of it, the way you understand rain.

After a while she said, in common, "You can come sit. The tree says your feet are loud."

"My feet are a carver's feet. They're delicate."

"The tree says they are loud and delicate both."

He sat beside her. Up close, the tree's greenness was almost unbearable — after weeks of gray and black and ash-colored survival, those leaves were so green they seemed lit from inside, and when the wind moved them they made a sound like a crowd murmuring before a play begins.

"What did you tell it?" he asked.

"That I'm frightened," Sefa said simply. "It told me it is frightened too. Then we were frightened together, which is easier." She plucked one of the leaves from her arm — it came away without pain, and another was already budding in its place — and turned it in her fingers. "These are getting bigger."

"I noticed."

"If I pour much more into the trees, I think I stop being a girl and become a grove." She said it without drama, the way she reported the weather or the state of the stew. "Mother Quill thought so too, before she—" She stopped. They had agreed not to finish sentences about Mother Quill yet; the grief was still too fresh to bear its full weight, so they carried it in pieces.

"Then we don't pour," Isandro said. "You've fed them enough to wake. The rest is ours to do. Bark and bone and stubbornness."

"The trees need more than stubbornness to survive fire."

"Yes," Isandro said. "But they needed you to survive first, and I've decided that's the priority, and I'm bigger than you, and I'm prepared to be unreasonable about it."

Sefa looked at him sidelong, silver hair falling across a face that was still, underneath everything, a child's face — smudged, thin, missing a tooth on the left side that she swore she'd lost to a walnut and Isandro privately suspected had been lost to a bet with Bramble. "You sound like Maren," she said. "From the charm. The part you played me once. 'Sit down, Jonah, and be unreasonable at someone else.'"

The words landed in his chest like a dropped knife. He hadn't known she remembered that. He hadn't known he'd played her that fragment — one evening, early, when she'd been learning what a wife's voice sounded like, and he'd wanted her to know the woman whose seedling she carried.

"She said that to me constantly," Isandro managed. "Usually correctly."

"I wish I'd known her."

"So do I." He paused. "She'd have liked you. She liked anyone who argued with me."

"The tree says that's most people."

"Does it," said Isandro. "Remind me to have a word with the tree."

But his throat was tight, and Sefa saw it, and leaned her silver head against his shoulder, and they watched the green leaves move in wind that belonged to no ordinary sky, until the small hours wore thin and sentries' horns — ram's horn, low, urgent but controlled — began calling the grove to its places.

* * *

Dawn came gray and smelling of smoke that wasn't theirs yet.

They took position in the ravine as the light strengthened: Thornhollow and six of the rooted ones braced at the deadfall above the north mouth, ready to bring half a hillside down; Pell crouched at the stream's edge with her hands already pale and damp, gathering fog out of the water like a woman kneading dough; the hedge-witches strung along the high banks with charms Isandro had helped them carve — plain bone, honest work, no voices, just shapes that held intention the way a cup holds water. Bramble had insisted on a sling and a pouch of river stones and had been assigned, after negotiation, to stay with the noncombatants at the far end of the grove, a decision she was appealing even now.

"And if they break through the ravine?" she demanded of Wren.

"Then you throw stones at them with great enthusiasm and then you run."

"That's not a plan, that's an anecdote waiting to happen."

"It's the plan," Wren said, "and anecdotes are how survivors talk afterward. Go."

Isandro climbed to his place on the eastern bank with a bow he hadn't touched in fifteen years and a quiver of twelve arrows, and told himself the shaking in his hands was the cold. Below, the ravine floor ran with morning mist. Beyond it, through the thinning gray, he could see the treeline of the old wood give way to stump-fields and burned ground — the Office's old work, thirteen years stale — and beyond that, a road.

At midmorning, the road filled with men.

They came in column, two abreast, iron-banded boots striking in time, fire-pots slung on poles like lanterns at a festival. Pale-gray cloaks. White sashes. At their head, a banner Isandro had hoped never to see unfurled outside a courtroom: the Office's sigil, a white hand closed around a black branch. Behind the column came the supply carts, and on the carts, stacked and roped like cordwood, were more fire-pots than Isandro could count.

His stomach performed a slow, deliberate revolt. Two hundred had been the wood's guess. Looking at them, he thought the wood had been optimistic.

Beside him on the bank, a young hedge-witch named Corla whispered, "That's a lot of men."

"It is," Isandro said.

"Should we be doing something clever?"

"We're doing something brave. Clever left last week with Mother Quill."

Corla laughed — a startled, high, terrified laugh — and Isandro was glad of it, glad of any sound in that moment that wasn't the drumbeat of boots.

The column reached the stump-field's edge and halted. Officers moved along the line. Fire-pots were unslung, wicks checked, and the little hiss of them lighting carried up the ravine like the sound of the world's largest, cruelest birthday about to begin.

And then the wind changed.

It came down from the north, out of the heart of the wood — a wind with weight to it, a wind that smelled of sap and loam and something older, and it moved through the gray trees above Isandro's head and every one of them shivered. The mist in the ravine thickened, curled, gathered itself. Pell, down at the stream, gasped and clutched her own wrists, whispering, "That's not me, that's not me, I'm not doing that—"

Below, in the ravine's green-shadowed depths, the deadfall shifted on its moorings like a beast turning in sleep.

On the far bank, Sefa stood at Wren's side, silver-haired and leaf-scarred, facing two hundred men and their fire, and as the twelfth moon's last pale remnant faded from the brightening sky, she lifted her chin and spoke one word in the old tongue, loudly, clearly, in a voice that was half girl and half grove:

Isandro didn't know the word. He didn't need to. Every tree in the Weeping Wood understood it, and answered, and the answering came up through the ground like thunder deciding to grow roots —

and down on the road, the first fire-pot arced up into the air, trailing flame, toward the trees.
