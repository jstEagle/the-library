## 15. Fire in the Gray Canopy

Sefa had learned that the world came in two flavors.

There was the flavor of Isandro — smoke and tallow and a small stubborn warmth, like an ember cupped in two hands that refused to admit it was cold. And there was the flavor of the wood, which was rain on old stone and grief so old it had forgotten it was grief.

Tonight both flavors were burning away, and something new had arrived in their place. Fire had a flavor too. It tasted like the end of names.

She felt them coming long before the horns. The trees told her first — a shudder running through root and branch like a word passed down a line of frightened children. *Iron. Iron and oil.* Then Mother Quill's crows went up from the ridge, all of them at once, screaming in voices that were not crow voices at all.

"They're here," said Wren-That-Was, one of the Rootbound, his bark mouth barely moving. He did not say it the way men said such things. He said it the way a tree says *frost* — with resignation, and a kind of terrible patience.

Sefa stood at the edge of the gray canopy and put her hands against the nearest trunk. Beneath the bark she could feel the wood waking, slow as honey poured uphill. She had been feeding it for days now, pouring herself into the roots the way Isandro poured himself into his carving, and every morning she woke a little lighter, a little grayer, a little less sure where Sefa ended and the grove began.

Her hair had gone white as birch. In the cut on her palm — she'd stopped counting the cuts — a small green leaf had unfurled three days ago and refused to die.

*You are spending yourself,* the oldest tree said to her. It spoke the way mountains would speak if mountains worried. *A vessel who empties herself is only a cup.*

*I'm not empty yet,* Sefa answered, in the language older than kingdoms, the one that lived in her ribs. *And cups are for giving.*

She heard, faintly, the tree's version of a sigh. Trees sighed through everything — leaves, roots, sap, centuries.

Then the first fire-pot arced over the ridge, trailing orange, and there was no more time for conversations with mountains.

* * *

The battle did not look like the battles in Isandro's stories, the ones he told her on cold nights to make her laugh, full of knights falling dramatically off horses. It looked mostly like smoke, and shouting, and men running in lines the way ants run when you pour water on their hill.

But the trees fought. Oh, how the trees fought.

Roots burst from the black soil like whales surfacing, and soldiers who had been standing in one place were suddenly standing somewhere else entirely, or upside-down, or not standing at all. Branches that had hung dead and gray for thirteen years flexed with a sound like old doors opening, and swept whole squads of the Pale Office into the dark between the trunks, where the Rootbound waited. Wren-That-Was moved through the fighting like a rumor, half man and half bark, and when he caught a soldier's spear in his wooden hand the spear simply *grew there*, sprouting leaves, until the man dropped it and fled screaming about witchery.

For one bright hour, Sefa thought they might win.

She stood at the heart of the grove with her palms flat against the Covenant-path stones and gave, and gave, and gave. Every soldier the trees threw down, every fire smothered under a sudden flood of rising sap — that was her, that was pieces of her, her summer afternoons and her memory of the taste of honey bread and her name, her actual name, the first syllable of it going out of her like heat from an open door.

She didn't mind. That was the frightening part. It felt like being useful. It felt like being *loved back*, finally, by something enormous.

"Girl!" Mother Quill's voice cut through the smoke, sharp as one of her own pins. The old hedge-witch came limping down the path with her skirts singed and her crow perched on her shoulder like a furious hat. "Girl, stop pouring. You'll drain the well dry."

"There's more," Sefa said. Her own voice sounded far away to her. "There's still more in me."

"There isn't," Mother Quill said, with the particular gentleness she saved for truths she expected to be argued with. "I've watched wells go dry, child. I've *been* one." She pressed something into Sefa's hand — a charm, rough-carved, bone, smelling faintly of Vaelmoor and of Isandro's workshop. "From your carver. He said — and I quote, because the man has no poetry whatsoever — 'Give this to her before she does something stupid.'"

Sefa almost laughed. Almost. Then the eastern gate screamed.

* * *

The gate was a wall of woven hawthorn, grown thick across the old road, and it was the only thing between the Office's second wave and the hollow where the wounded lay — the burned Rootbound, the broken soldiers the trees had decided to spare, Mother Quill's supplies, everything soft and helpless in the wood gathered into one place because there had been nowhere else to put it.

The Office had brought fire-pots. Dozens of them.

Sefa reached the gate as the first volley landed. The hawthorn caught with a sound like a gasp.

She put her hands into the fire.

Not around it. Into it. She pushed herself down through her arms and into the living wood the way Isandro pushed himself into bone, and she *held*, and the flames roared against her and found nothing flammable left, because she had already given the hawthorn everything she had that could burn. Her white hair whipped in the heat. Leaves sprouted along her forearms and crisped instantly, and she grew more, and they crisped, and she grew more.

Behind her, someone shouted her name in the common tongue — Isandro's voice, hoarse and terrified, somewhere back among the wounded — but the gate needed her, and gates do not get to listen.

The second volley came. The third. The hawthorn held. The hawthorn *greened*, impossibly, defiantly, putting out new shoots in the middle of a firestorm, because Sefa was the Weeping Wood's last vessel and she had decided, with the entire stubbornness of a ten-year-old girl who had once been a grove, that this gate was not going to burn.

Mother Quill appeared beside her through the smoke, coughing, ancient, magnificent.

"Well then," the old woman said, cracking her knuckles like she was about to deal cards rather than defy an army. "If we're doing it this way, we're doing it properly."

What Mother Quill did next, Sefa never fully understood, though she would spend years trying to. The hedge-witch drove her walking-staff into the soil at the gate's foot and *unstopped herself* — all the small hoarded magics of a lifetime of careful, illegal, quiet craft, poured out in one act the way Sefa was pouring herself out, except that Mother Quill's jar had been filling for seventy years and emptied in a minute. The ground drank. The gate drank. A ring of white flowers bloomed up through the ash around the hawthorn's roots, and every fire that touched the ring went out as if pinched by invisible fingers.

"You hold the wood," Mother Quill said, and her voice was already going thin and strange, like a radio finding a station very far away. "I'll hold the gate. Between us, we are *extremely* inconvenient."

"How long can you hold?" Sefa asked.

Mother Quill smiled. It was a good smile. It had a lot of practice behind it.

"Long enough," she said, "to be rude about it," and turned her face to the fire, and held.

* * *

Time went strange inside the burning gate.

Sefa held. The hawthorn held. Mother Quill held, her staff sunk to the crossguard in smoking earth, her lips moving through words so old even Sefa's bones didn't recognize them, her crows wheeling overhead and dropping, one by one, as their borrowed voices burned out of them.

The soldiers came anyway, of course. They came through the smoke in iron masks, and the flowers killed their fires, and Wren-That-Was and the other Rootbound broke their lines again and again, but they kept coming, because armies are like weather that way, and the wounded behind the gate were too many and too slow to move.

A soldier got through. Sefa saw him — a big man, visor down, axe in hand, stumbling toward the hollow where a burned Rootbound boy lay moaning. There was no one between them. There was no one but Sefa, and Sefa was holding a gate together with the last scraps of herself.

She looked at the soldier. She looked at the gate.

She did the arithmetic no child should ever have to do, and then she did something worse: she took the piece of herself she'd been keeping safe — the smallest, most secret piece, the one that remembered being a grove, the one that was the actual seedling, Maren's seedling, the reason for everything — and she spent it too.

The soldier stopped mid-stride as the ground opened under him, gently, like a hand, and closed. The gate blazed green. Somewhere behind her eyes a light went out that she suspected — distantly, calmly, the way you notice rain starting — she was never getting back.

"That," Mother Quill said quietly beside her, watching, "was the stupidest brave thing I have seen in seventy years of stupid brave things." Her voice was almost gone. Her hands on the staff were bark-brown and shaking. "Your carver is going to be insufferable about this."

"He'll cry," Sefa said.

"He'll absolutely cry," Mother Quill agreed. "He strikes me as a crier. Don't tell him I said—"

The last fire-pot came out of nowhere. No arc, no warning — just a shadow in the smoke and then a bloom of orange directly above them, falling, falling, and Mother Quill moved without hesitating, tore her staff free of the earth, and swung it like a scythe, and the ring of white flowers leapt up and out and caught the pot in the air.

It exploded above them in a crown of fire.

When the sparks cleared, Mother Quill was still standing. Mostly. Her staff was gone. Her left side was charred black past recognition, and the ring of flowers was fading around her feet, petal by petal, its work done, its keeper spent.

"Huh," she said, looking down at herself with mild professional interest. "Well. That's that, then."

"No," Sefa said. She reached for her — reached with the wood, with the roots, with everything left, and found nothing there to reach with. She had given it all away. Every drop. "No, wait, I can—"

"Child." Mother Quill sat down slowly, the way old women sit, arranging her ruined skirts with fussy dignity. Behind the gate, the fires were dying; the Office's line had broken; someone was blowing the retreat. They had won. That was the obscene part. They had *won*. "Listen. You listen, because everyone else talks too much. Wells refill. That's what wells *do*. You'll fill again — different water, maybe, but water." She patted the ash beside her, and Sefa knelt there, and the old woman's charred hand came up and rested, impossibly gently, on her white hair. "And tell my crows—" She stopped. Started again. "Tell them they were never borrowed voices. They were mine. All mine. Worst birds in Vaelmoor. I loved them terribly."

Above the gate, the last crow folded its wings and dropped out of the sky like a stone, landing beside her, and did not get up.

Mother Quill looked at the gray canopy, at the first pale threads of green showing in it — the green Sefa had bought with nearly everything — and her face did something complicated and settled into peace.

"Oh, *good*," she said softly. "It's working."

Then the great tree behind her lowered a branch, slow and solemn as a bow, and laid it over her like a blanket, and Sefa was alone with the flowers and the crow and the silence where a sharp old voice used to be.

She did not cry. She was too empty to cry. Crying takes water, and she had none.

* * *

They found her there when the smoke cleared, kneeling in the ash with her hand still raised toward a dead woman and a dead bird, and she heard Isandro coming before she saw him — heard his heartbeat, heard the wet ragged sound of a man running through a battlefield calling a name.

"Sefa! *Sefa!*"

She wanted to answer. That was the thing. She had answers. She had *so many answers* — about the gate, about the flowers, about Mother Quill, about the piece of herself she had spent that she could never, ever tell him about, not tonight, maybe not ever. But when she opened her mouth, what came out was not a word. It was a hum, low and green and wrong, the forest's language spilling out of her because there wasn't enough Sefa left in her to push it aside.

Isandro skidded down through the ash and caught her before she fell, and his arms smelled like smoke and tallow and home.

"I've got you," he was saying, into her hair, his voice breaking on every word. "I've got you, little grove, I've got you, you absolute reckless — don't you dare, don't you *dare* close your eyes—"

Everything was going gray at the edges. Gray like the wood had been. Gray like sleep.

Through the narrowing dark she saw him lift her, saw him turn and shout for help in a voice she'd never heard him use, saw the last light of the burning gate reflected in his tears — and he *had* cried, and she thought, fuzzily, triumphantly, *told you* — and then the gray took even that.

The last thing she felt, as she sank down into whatever waits underneath vessels and wells and worlds, was his hand pressed flat against her chest, right over the place where the seedling used to be.

His lips were moving. She couldn't hear the words anymore.

But she felt them.
