## 16. The Silent Carver

The fire had reached the third ridge by the time Isandro understood what the wood was asking of him.

Not asked. Asked implied a question, and questions could be refused. The Weeping Wood did not refuse things. It simply showed him — through Sefa's slack hand in his, through the faint green pulse beneath her skin that was guttering like a lamp out of oil — what the covenant required. A vessel, whole and willing, at the Stone before the thirteenth moon turned. Not half a girl with her life poured out into gray trunks to hold back fire and iron. All of her. Everything she had.

And she had spent everything she had saving people who weren't even hers to save.

"Stupid," he whispered, brushing ash from her forehead. Her hair had gone white as birch bark, and where the bounty hunter's knife had opened her arm two weeks ago, a small perfect leaf now grew, trembling with her breath. "You stupid, brave, impossible child."

She didn't answer. She hadn't answered since they'd dragged her from the burning gate-line at dusk, since Mother Quill's scream had cut off somewhere behind them in the smoke and Isandro had run because running was the only prayer left in his body. Sefa breathed. That was all. In and out, shallow as a sparrow's, each exhale carrying a smell like rain on warm stone — the wood's own smell, leaking out of her because she no longer had walls enough to keep it in.

Around him, the Rootbound's hollow grove was quiet in the way that only places recently full of screaming are quiet. Wounded lay under bark-shelters. A man with sap-crusted hands wept without sound for someone Isandro couldn't see. The trees above — waking now, truly waking, their gray limbs creaking with something like pain and something like gratitude — dripped black sap onto the ashes, and each drop hissed.

He should have been resting too. His arms were burned from hauling fire-pots away from the eastern barricade; his ribs ached where an Office soldier's shield-boss had caught him; he had not slept in — he counted, and stopped counting when the number frightened him. None of it mattered. What mattered was sitting in the dirt beside him wearing his spare coat, dying by inches because she had given herself away like a woman handing out bread in a famine.

And what mattered more was the thing he had known since the moment he'd lifted her, since the moment her weight had settled against his chest light as kindling: that there was one way to fill her back up. One way only. And it would cost exactly the thing he had crossed a kingdom to protect.

* * *

He found Mother Quill's satchel first. She'd left it wedged under a root-shelf near the western path, and for a foolish moment Isandro knelt there holding it like it might hold her too — her dried herbs, her chalk stubs worn to pebbles, her terrible little clay whistle that she'd used to imitate Pale Office whistles so convincingly that Isandro had once dived into a ditch on her say-so. He closed the satchel and pressed his forehead briefly against its cracked leather.

"I'm going to be angry about this," he told her. "For years. I want you to know that."

The dead, as a rule, did not answer him. But lately the dead had been anything but quiet.

Because he still carried charms. Three of them, strung inside his shirt against his sternum where they had knocked gently against his heart for the whole mad road north. He had meant to burn them with the rest in Vaelmoor. He had meant to. But Maren's voice lived in one of them — the last charm, the hidden one, the one he had kept through every purge and search and sleepless night — and a man could burn his livelihood, but burning his wife twice over was a thing even ten years of cowardice had not managed.

The other two were strangers' voices. An old ferryman who'd wanted his brother's laugh one more winter. A stonemason's daughter, seven years dead, whose father had paid Isandro in bread and shame.

They were awake now. They had been awake since the battle began, murmuring, and in the last hour they had begun to *scream*.

Isandro felt it start again as he stood — a vibration against his breastbone, thin and urgent, like fingers drumming on a door. *Warn him. Warn him.* That was the trouble with bone charms carved honest, with voices caught whole instead of snipped into comfortable echoes: they remembered being alive, and alive people hated watching the living walk into fires.

"Not now," he muttered, pressing his palm flat against his chest as if he could hush them like children. "I'm busy."

They screamed louder. The ferryman's voice came through clearest, ragged with urgency: *"North fork, carver — the north fork burns both ways —"* and then the girl's voice underneath, high and strange: *"the paths remember you, the paths remember—"*

Isandro went cold in a way the smoke couldn't account for.

The paths remembered. Of course they did. He was carrying the dead through a forest that had just remembered how to be alive, and the boundary between those two facts had gone soft as wet parchment. His charms weren't just warning him. The wood itself was listening to them. Using them. Speaking through them the way it spoke through Sefa — except Sefa was empty now, and these small dry bones were the loudest mouths left standing.

He looked down at Sefa, white-haired and leaf-wounded, breathing rain-on-stone breaths.

"All right," he said to the forest, to the fire, to the three dead souls riding against his heart. "All right. I hear you. Lead on."

* * *

He wrapped Sefa in the oilcloth from Mother Quill's satchel, tucked her against his back with the old porter's knot his father had taught him — the one useful inheritance of a childhood hauling crates on the Vaelmoor docks — and took up his walking stick. Then, at the edge of the grove, he paused.

Bark-skin woman. The leader of the Rootbound, whose name he still didn't properly know because she'd never offered it and he'd never presumed. She sat propped against a waking oak, her leg bound with moss, watching him with eyes like knots in wood.

"You're taking the child to the Stone." It wasn't a question. Her voice sounded the way a branch sounds bending — flexible right up until it snaps.

"Yes."

"She'll die on the way, or she'll die at the Stone. Either way you lose her." Something moved in her face that might have been pity or might have been memory. "We've watched vessels go to the Stone before, carver. Long ago. Before your Edict, before your Office. It is always a choosing, and a choosing is always a grief."

"I know."

"Do you?" She leaned forward, and the oak behind her leaned with her, creaking. "You carry the dead on strings around your neck. You have spent ten years listening to voices that cannot argue with you, cannot grow, cannot surprise you. Grief that sits still is easy, Isandro Coldharbour. I have seen men mistake it for devotion." She touched the bark of the tree, tenderly. "This wood does not sit still. Neither did your Maren, from what the Rootbound remember of her. So ask yourself — truly ask — whether you love the echo or the living thing."

The words went in like a splinter driven deep. Isandro stood very still, Sefa's weight warm against his back, and let himself actually consider it — the horror he'd been refusing to look at since the chapel, since the flooded nave where Maren's voice had told him the truth at last: *I hid the seedling because someone had to choose, Isy. Someone always has to choose. Don't you dare choose the echo.*

"I love her," he said, and was surprised to find his voice steady. "Both of them. Enough to be deafened."

The bark-skin woman studied him for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, the way a tree nods in wind.

"The north fork," she said. "Two hours fast. The fire will try to turn you. The Office will try to stop you. And the Stone—" her knot-eyes flickered shut "—the Stone will ask more than you think you have. Go. And carver—" he was already turning, already lifting Sefa higher on his back, "—when it asks? Don't bargain. Bargaining is how cowards pray."

* * *

The north fork was hell with a floor.

Fire had climbed both slopes of the ravine and met overhead in places, so that Isandro ran through corridors of flame with embers snowing down like malevolent weather. Smoke lay thick as wool. He kept low, kept moving, kept counting Sefa's breaths against the back of his neck — a rhythm he'd learned in the drowned chapel, when he'd held her through the night after Maren's revelation and pretended to himself that he wasn't memorizing the sound of something he might lose.

*"Left!"* shrieked the stonemason's daughter in his shirt, and he swerved left, boots skidding on ash-slick stone, as a burning limb came down exactly where he'd been standing and exploded into sparks.

*"Faster, carver, faster,"* urged the ferryman, and there was something almost fond in the dead man's voice now, the way there'd been fondness in his brother's laugh. *"You rowed worse than this on the Graywater, and you complained less."*

"I complained plenty," Isandro gasped. "You just didn't hear it, because you were dead."

*"I heard it,"* said the ferryman, with unmistakable satisfaction. *"Dead men hear everything. That's rather the point of us."*

It should have been terrifying, running through a burning wood with his dead chattering encouragement like spectators at a footrace. It was. But it was also — and this was the part Isandro would never be able to explain afterward, not to Aldric, not to Sefa, not to anyone — warm. The voices had spent ten years being comfort, being crutch, being the soft dishonest pillow he pressed his guilt into every night. Now they were companions. Now they were crew. The ferryman called the turns before Isandro saw them. The girl sang — actually sang, a scrap of some skipping rhyme from her short life — and the rhyme, God help him, kept his feet in rhythm, kept his lungs working, kept despair at a distance where it could shout but not touch.

Love as fuel. He finally understood the phrase. Not the grand tragic kind. This kind: a dead ferryman cheering you uphill because you'd once given his brother's laugh back to a grieving family, and kindness, it turned out, compounded like interest across the veil.

*"The bridge,"* said the girl suddenly, and stopped singing. *"The bridge is gone."*

She was right. Where the root-bridge should have crossed the gorge — Mother Quill had described it, bark-grown, ancient, the last direct path to the Covenant Stone — there was now a gap of forty feet filled with smoke and falling fire, the far anchor-tree a black torch against the night.

Isandro slid to a halt. His legs shook. Sefa's breaths came shallower now, and when he twisted to check her face, he saw with a lurch that the little leaf on her arm had gone brown at the edges.

"No," he said. "No, no, no—"

*"There,"* said the ferryman, quieter now. *"Below. Look below, carver. Dead men have good eyes for dark places."*

Below, at the bottom of the gorge, the river still ran — a thin silver thread between smoking banks. And along the bank, barely visible: a path. Old, overgrown, the kind of path that existed only because water made it. The long way. Hours added. Hours they might not have.

But also — and Isandro felt this knowledge arrive in his chest like a stone dropping into a well — the long way past the place where the Office soldiers had fallen back to regroup. The long way directly through Aldric's line.

He thought of the bark-skin woman's warning. *Don't bargain.*

He adjusted Sefa's weight and started down into the gorge.

* * *

Halfway along the river path, the wood spoke to him directly for the first time.

Not through the charms. Through everything — the surviving trees leaning over the water, the sap hissing where it struck hot stones, the ground itself humming faintly under his boots. It was not language. It was older than language, and it pressed against him like a great damp palm, and what it conveyed was not words but *weight*: gratitude and need braided together, the desperate arithmetic of a dying thing that had measured its remaining life in hours and found the sum wanting.

And underneath the vastness, threaded through it, one clear human note — Maren's note, or the memory of Maren's note, carried in the wood's long green memory the way a bell keeps the shape of its strike:

*You understand now. Say it. Say it aloud. The wood needs to hear a mortal say it, because the last ones who broke the covenant never once admitted what they'd done.*

Isandro stopped in the middle of the burning path, Sefa heavy and failing on his back, and he said it. Out loud. To a forest. With his voice cracking like a boy's.

"They broke it because they wanted power without price. Because a covenant means you owe something forever, and forever is the one debt proud men won't pay." His throat worked. "I did the same. Smaller. Ten years of small same. I hid behind the dead because the living were hard, and I called it devotion, and it was cowardice with better posture."

The hum changed. Softened. Somewhere upslope, a tree that should have been dead put out a single new bud in the firelight, absurd and defiant, and Isandro laughed — a wet, wrecked sound — and kept walking.

The dead in his shirt had gone quiet. Not gone. Quiet. Waiting, the way good crew waits, letting the captain do the one thing only the captain can do.

Sefa stirred against his shoulder. Just slightly. Just enough that her lips moved, shaping something with no air behind it, and Isandro tilted his head and caught it anyway, because ten years of listening to whispers had trained him for exactly this:

*"Stone,"* she breathed. *"Hurts. Go anyway."*

"I know, little seedling." He tightened the porter's knot. "Go anyway. That's the whole religion, isn't it? Everything else is commentary."

Ahead, the river bent east, and around the bend — visible now through the thinning smoke, pale as a moon fallen to earth — the Covenant Stone rose out of a ring of blackened oaks. Still unburned. Still waiting. And between Isandro and it, torchlight moved in disciplined lines: the Pale Office, regrouped, refueled, and marching on the Stone itself with fire-pots stacked on a cart like loaves of bread.

And at the head of the nearest line, tall and tired and holding a torch he wasn't using, stood a figure Isandro would have known by silhouette in any century, in any fire, in any world.

Aldric looked up. Their eyes met across the burning dark.

The dead began, very softly, to scream again.
