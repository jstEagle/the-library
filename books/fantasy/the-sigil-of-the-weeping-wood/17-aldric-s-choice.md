## 17. Aldric's Choice

The fire-pots were heavier than Aldric remembered.

That was a lie, of course. They were exactly as heavy as they had been thirteen years ago, the night he had stood in a different square in a different mood and watched men he knew pour burning pitch into the roots of a forest. His arms simply hadn't carried them then; other arms had, and he had only carried the order itself, which weighed nothing at all and more than everything.

"Commander Vane." Sergeant Brakka fell into step beside him, her boots squelching in the ash-mud of the ruined road. She was a solid woman, gray-stubbled, dependable as a wall. "Scouts say the Stone is half a mile ahead, past the burned stand. The carver's trail goes straight to it."

"And the girl?"

"With him. And — this is the part you won't like — the Rootbound are massed between us and it. Maybe forty of them. Bark-skin, branch-arms. The new recruits keep asking if we're supposed to shoot trees or people."

"We're not supposed to shoot anything," Aldric said. "We're supposed to burn one Stone."

Brakka looked at him sideways. "And the people guarding the Stone?"

"The fire will be very thorough," Aldric said, and hated how smoothly the old language came back to his mouth, like a coat that still fit because he had never truly taken it off.

They marched on. Behind him, sixty soldiers of the Pale Office carried iron and oil through the black skeleton of a forest that was, impossibly, beginning to green at its edges. He had seen the reports and disbelieved them. Then he had seen the first pale shoots pushing up through ash like fingers through a grave-shroud, and he had understood that everything the Office had built its house upon was standing on something alive — and that living things, given time, will always stand up.

* * *

He had requested this assignment personally. That was the part he could not stop turning over, like a stone in the pocket of his mind.

*Give me the hunt,* he had told High Interrogator Selle. *I know Coldharbour. I'll bring him in alive.*

He had even believed it, in the comfortable way one believes things at a desk with tea going cold beside one. Bring Isandro in alive. Sit him down across an interview table, put a cup in front of him the way they'd shared cups once as apprentices, sneaking honey from Master Corvel's stores while Corvel pretended not to notice. Talk sense into him. Get him to surrender the child quietly, serve whatever sentence the tribunals invented, grow old and bitter but breathing.

It had seemed so reasonable. It had seemed like mercy.

Now here he stood at the edge of a burning wood with a fire-pot in his hands, and the orders in his breast pocket said, in Selle's neat bureaucratic hand: *Upon securing the Covenant Stone, destroy it by fire. No witnesses to remain. The vessel-child is to be terminated on site as a matter of doctrinal hygiene.*

*Doctrinal hygiene.* Thirteen years ago, when Aldric testified against Maren Halloran — when he had stood in that white room and said yes, yes, she carved charms, yes, I saw her do it, yes, she spoke to the dead — he had told himself it was survival, and survival was honest, wasn't it? Everyone chose life. Everyone chose the institution over the friend. That was what civilization meant: choosing the large thing over the small one.

Maren had gone to the pyre with her chin up, and she had looked at him — only him, in all that crowd — and she had not looked angry. She had looked *sorry for him*, which was infinitely worse, and which he had spent thirteen years drinking down like slow poison, telling himself he had imagined it.

He had not imagined it.

"Commander?" Brakka again. "You've stopped walking."

So he had. Ahead, through the last black trunks, the ground rose to a hollow where a great gray monolith stood — the Covenant Stone, older than any kingdom, older than any Edict, wearing centuries of moss like a shabby coat. And before it, small and absurd, a man with a carver's hands and no weapon at all, holding a dying child.

Aldric set the fire-pot down in the mud. Carefully. The way one sets down something that might go off.

* * *

Isandro Coldharbour looked terrible. That was the first thing Aldric noticed, and the thought arrived with a stab of affection so sharp it embarrassed him. Ten days in the wilds, hunted, starving, carrying a girl whose hair had gone white as birch-bark — and Isandro still managed to look like a man who had been caught doing something mildly embarrassing rather than something treasonous.

"Aldric." Isandro's voice was hoarse. "You came alone."

"I didn't. Sixty men are ten minutes behind me."

"You always were bad at arithmetic on purpose. Sixty men don't take ten minutes. You sent them around the long way." Isandro almost smiled. "You used to do that with Corvel's errands. Send the other apprentices to the far market so we'd have the workshop to ourselves."

"Don't," Aldric said. "Don't do that. Don't make me remember being young. It doesn't help either of us."

"It helps me," Isandro said. "It's about all that does, lately."

The child lay against his chest, wrapped in a Rootbound cloak of woven living bark. Her breathing was shallow and wrong, each exhale a little too long, as though her body kept forgetting to start again. Leaves — actual leaves, small and green and horrifyingly tender — showed at her collarbone where wounds should have been.

"She gave too much," Isandro said, following his gaze. "To wake the trees during the attack. The covenant needs her whole at the Stone. A full vessel, freely given. She's running out of full."

"So carry her away from the Stone. Take her somewhere the Office can't—"

"To what end, Aldric?" Isandro's voice cracked open, and underneath it Aldric heard not anger but exhaustion, the exhaustion of a man who has done all his crying in private and has none left for performance. "If the moon turns without the covenant reforged, she dies anyway. The whole wood dies. Everything bound to it dies. There's nowhere to run that isn't just a longer road to the same place."

"Then give her to me. Give her to the Office. There are people in Vaelmoor who would study her instead of—"

"Look at your orders, Aldric." Quiet. Terrible. "*Doctrinal hygiene.* You read them. I watched your face when you read them, three days ago at the chapel ford, before you ordered the squad to circle wide. You read the word 'terminated' and your hand shook."

Aldric said nothing. Somewhere behind them, deep in the waking wood, a tree groaned — the long creaking groan of something enormous remembering how to move.

"I testified against Maren," Aldric said.

It came out of him like a tooth coming out — awful, relieving, leaving a hole the tongue keeps returning to. Isandro went very still.

"That night, when the purges started. They asked if I'd seen her carve. I had. Once. A little charm for our landlord's sick daughter, nothing whispered, nothing dark, just — kindness, Isandro. It was kindness with a bone in it. And I said yes. I said yes and they spared me and promoted me and for thirteen years I told myself it was survival." He laughed, and it was an ugly sound. "Do you know what the worst part is? Not the saying yes. The saying yes was fear, and fear is forgivable. The worst part is that afterward, I was *good at it*. I rose. I interrogated. I got commendations. I built a whole life on top of her ashes and furnished it nicely."

Isandro's jaw worked. When he finally spoke, his voice was low and strange. "I know."

"...You know?"

"The charms whisper, Aldric. The dead talk. Maren's voice has lived in my pocket for ten years, and do you know what she says? What she has said, every single time, for a decade?" Isandro's eyes were wet and furious. "'Tell Aldric it's all right.' Every time. *Every time.* I couldn't bear to tell you, because then I'd have to admit I'd been keeping her voice from you while hating you for surviving, and neither of those things was fair, and I chose unfairness for ten years because it was easier than truth. So don't stand there thinking your confession costs more than mine. We were both cowards. You just had better tailors."

For a moment neither of them moved. The wood breathed around them. The child stirred and murmured something in her old tongue — one soft syllable, green-sounding, like a word for *shade* or *mercy*.

Then the horns sounded.

* * *

They came through the burned stand in formation, torches forward, Selle's voice carrying over the ranks: *"The Stone! Burn the Stone! Nothing living leaves the hollow!"*

Aldric stepped out from the treeline and raised his hand, and — because institutions obey shapes, and he was the shape they had always obeyed — the column halted.

"Sergeant Brakka," he said. "Report."

"Scouts confirm the target, Commander. Rootbound withdrawing north with wounded. The hollow is clear except for the traitor and the vessel." Her eyes flicked from him to the fire-pots to the Stone and back, and Aldric saw her arrive, slowly, at the same conclusion he had spent the entire march arriving at. She had carried orders under him for nine years. She knew what his silences meant.

"Selle," Aldric called. "A word."

High Interrogator Selle pushed through the ranks, a thin man with a thin smile, holding his own fire-pot as though it were a decanter of wine. "Commander Vane. You've secured the site ahead of schedule. Exemplary."

"I'm countermanding the burn."

The smile stayed exactly where it was, which was how Aldric knew he'd understood perfectly. "I beg your pardon?"

"The Stone stays. The child lives. The man comes in under my personal guarantee." Aldric pulled the orders from his breast pocket and held them up. "Furthermore — these. 'No witnesses to remain.' Do you know what that phrase means, Selle? It means civilians. It means the wounded Rootbound your squad just let retreat north because *I* ordered the wide approach. If we follow this letter, in one hour there will be forty dead people who surrendered, and their deaths will be signed with my name. And then the wood finishes waking, and the wood, unlike us, holds grudges in writing."

Selle's voice dropped to silk. "The Edict is clear."

"The Edict," Aldric said, "was written by frightened men after they did something unforgivable, and I have spent thirteen years helping them hide it. I was at the Sundering, Selle. I know what broke the covenant. It wasn't the wood."

The silence that followed had weight. Sixty soldiers stood in it, listening, the way soldiers always listen — pretending not to, hearing everything.

"You're relieved of command," Selle said softly. "Brakka. Arrest the Commander."

And Brakka — steady, gray, wall-like Brakka — did not move.

"Brakka."

"He's right about the orders," Brakka said, to the ranks, not to Selle. "You've all read them. Some of you have mothers." A rumble ran through the line that was not quite agreement and very much not obedience either. She turned to Aldric. "What do you need, Commander?"

"Time," Aldric said. "Ten minutes. Nobody advances. Anybody who wants to drop their torch in the mud and remember they're a person instead of a policy — now's your moment."

One torch fell. Then five. Then a dozen, hissing out in the wet ash, and the line sagged the way a rope sags when someone stops pulling. Selle stood in the middle of it, thin and furious, suddenly holding the only lit flame on the field.

"This is mutiny," he said. "This is the pyre for every one of you."

"Probably," Brakka agreed comfortably. "Get in line behind the rest of my sins."

* * *

Ten minutes, Aldric had promised. He gave them to Isandro with both hands.

"Go," he said. "Now. Through the north gap before Selle finds his spine — he will, they always do, spinelessness regenerates like a weed. Take the girl. Reforge your covenant. And Isandro—" He faltered, and found that the next words had waited thirteen years and would not wait a minute longer. "I never told you I was sorry, because sorry felt too small and I hadn't earned big. I still haven't. But Maren's message — tell her I received it. Tell her I finally understand it."

Isandro gripped his forearm, hard, the grip of apprentices hauling each other out of the river canal twenty-five years ago. "Come with us. Both of us, we walk out of this wood and—"

"No." Aldric smiled, and was surprised to find it a real one. "Somebody has to stay here and be spectacularly arrested. It's the only thing I've ever been qualified for." He glanced at the child, white-haired and leaf-wounded, and gentled. "Hello, little one. I helped break your forest. I intend to spend what's left of me helping mend it. Try to leave some mending for the rest of us."

Sefa opened her eyes — gray-green, ancient, six years old — and looked at him for a long moment. Then she lifted one small hand and pressed it, briefly, against his chest, directly over the heart.

Something in Aldric's chest *unclenched*. Thirteen years of held breath, released in a single exhale. He made a sound he would deny to any tribunal.

"Go," he said thickly. "Go, before I get sentimental enough to hug you and compromise my dignity entirely."

Isandro ran, the child in his arms, up the rise toward the Stone, and the trees along his path — Aldric saw it plainly, and would testify to it later, ruined and oath-bound and glad — leaned their branches aside to let him pass.

Behind Aldric, Selle had found his voice and a fresh torch, and was screaming about duty and fire. In front, the Stone waited, gray and patient as a grandfather.

Aldric Vane turned to face the institution that owned him, squared his shoulders like a man stepping into cold water, and began, at last, to tell the truth — loudly, at length, and on the record.

It was, he reflected, about time somebody did.
