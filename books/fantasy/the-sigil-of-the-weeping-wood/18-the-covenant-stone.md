## 18. The Covenant Stone

The burning wood made its own weather now. Ash fell like gray snow, and the wind carried a smell Isandro would remember for the rest of his life, however long or short that proved to be — sweet rot and scorched bark, the smell of a world's oldest living thing being murdered.

He ran anyway.

Sefa weighed almost nothing in his arms. That frightened him more than the fire did. Three days ago she had been a solid, stubborn child who complained about the texture of travel bread and refused to walk through mud when there was a dry stone anywhere within ten yards. Now she was a bundle of sticks wrapped in his coat, her breathing shallow and quick, her hair gone white as birchwood. Where the hunters' arrow had grazed her shoulder at the river, the wound had closed over with something that was not quite skin — a pale green film, fine as new leaf.

The dead were screaming.

They had been screaming since the twelfth moon rose. Every charm left on his cord — seven of them, each one a voice he had carved and sold and bought back over ten years of quiet crimes — had woken at moonrise all at once, chattering and keening against his chest like sparrows in a sack. The grandmother who had died mid-prayer. The drowned fisherman who never stopped apologizing. The soldier boy from the northern garrison whose last word had been *mother*, stretched out into a long, low moan that Isandro could no longer bear to hear clearly.

*Turn back,* they said. *Turn back, turn back.* And underneath, in voices he did not recognize but understood anyway, because Sefa's language had been seeping into him for weeks the way water seeps into bone: *Faster. Faster.*

"Almost there," he told her. She couldn't hear him. Her eyes were half-open, fixed on nothing, and every few steps her lips moved around words in the old tongue — the language of groves and roots, the language she had hummed to him the first night in Vaelmoor while his charms sang back at her unbidden. He had learned enough of it by now to know she wasn't speaking to him. She was speaking to the trees. *Hold on,* she was saying. *I'm coming. Hold on.*

The path broke open onto the hollow.

Isandro stopped so suddenly that his boots skidded in wet ash, and for one absurd moment he thought of Maren, of how she used to grab his sleeve at markets when she saw something beautiful, as if beauty might otherwise escape.

The Covenant Stone stood in a ring of dead oaks at the bottom of a bowl-shaped valley, and it was not a stone at all, or not only. It was a boulder the size of a chapel, black and veined with silver, and it wept — great slow beads of sap that rolled down its flanks and pooled at its base, where the pool had gone black and still. Around it, the grass was green. Only there. A circle of impossible, defiant green, perhaps thirty feet across, while beyond the ring everything burned.

The Office's fire-pots had reached the eastern ridge. He could see them from here, tiny orange blooms opening one after another along the treeline, and between the blooms he could hear — faintly, terribly — the sound of horns.

And between him and the Stone, down the slope, stood Aldric.

* * *

His oldest friend was alone. That was the first thing Isandro noticed, and the second thing was why: Aldric's uniform was scorched down one side, his left arm hung wrong, and behind him, up the slope, two Pale Office soldiers lay motionless in the ash. Not dead — Isandro could see one of them moving, groaning, trying to crawl away from the fight he'd apparently lost. But put out of it. Put out of the way of what came next.

"You're supposed to be burning this place," Isandro said. His voice came out cracked. He hadn't spoken aloud in hours; there'd been no one to speak to but a dying child and the dead.

"I'm supposed to be doing a great many things." Aldric tried to smile and didn't manage it. His face was soot and old guilt, thirteen years of it, worn open at last. "The squad wouldn't turn. So I turned them myself. It went about as well as you'd expect."

"Aldric—"

"Don't." He held up his good hand. "Don't thank me. Don't forgive me. I haven't earned either one, and I don't have time to be embarrassed properly." He looked past Isandro's shoulder at the child in his arms, and something in his face crumpled and reassembled itself, harder. "She can't walk."

"No."

"The Stone wants a vessel. Whole. Willing." Aldric's jaw worked. "I read the old accounts during the trial years. They burned most of them, but I kept some. Out of sentiment," he added bitterly, "which is the only sin I've ever committed that I actually enjoyed."

"She'll live," Isandro said. "If the covenant takes. That's what Mother Quill said. The vessel isn't spent — she's *returned*. Made herself again, whatever herself is." He swallowed. "But it needs more than her. It needs—" He stopped.

Aldric waited. He had always been patient, even as a boy; even when Isandro cheated him at knucklebones and Maren laughed until she cried at their excuses. Patient, careful Aldric, who testified to save his own skin and had spent thirteen years being slowly strangled by it.

"It needs a voice that hears the dead," Isandro said. "Freely silenced."

The words hung in the smoky air. Somewhere upslope, a tree went with a sound like a sigh, collapsing into embers.

"There are other hearers," Aldric said quietly. "There must be. Charm-sellers in every city. We arrested dozens during the purges—"

"They're not bound to the Wood. None of them are. Ten years of carving, Aldric. Ten years of listening. My hearing is *woven into* this place whether I wanted it or not." He touched the cord at his chest, and the charms shuddered against his fingers like small trapped animals. "The Stone doesn't want just any voice. It wants mine. It's been wanting mine since the night Sefa knocked on my door."

"How do you know?"

"Because the dead told me." He managed something that was almost a laugh. "First honest work they've ever done."

* * *

He walked toward the Stone, and Aldric did not stop him. Instead his friend fell into step beside him, favoring his ruined arm, and for a hundred yards neither of them spoke, which was somehow more companionable than anything they'd said since before the Sundering.

At the edge of the green circle, Aldric finally halted.

"I testified against her," he said.

Isandro closed his eyes. "I know."

"You didn't know. You suspected. There's a difference, and you deserved the truth, and I let you believe the Office killed her outright because it was easier than telling you I signed the paper that sent them to your street." His voice shook, and he let it. "Maren hid that seedling, Isandro. She knew what she was doing. She chose it. And I have spent thirteen years choosing the opposite of everything she was, because the alternative was admitting what I'd done." He drew a breath. "So. Truth, then. All of it, once, badly, and then never again: I was afraid. I am sorry. I loved her too, and I loved you, and I destroyed both those things to keep my own neck whole. Say whatever you want to me. I'll stand here and take it, and then you'll go do what you have to do."

Isandro looked at him for a long moment.

"Maren forgave people too easily," he said at last. "It drove me mad. Someone shorted her at market and she'd say *they probably needed it more*. I used to tell her the world would eat her alive." His throat closed. "She'd have forgiven you by now. Probably somewhere around the moment you started crying, which was approximately four minutes ago."

"I am not—"

"Your eyes are streaming. It's the smoke, you'll say." Isandro shifted Sefa in his arms. "I'm not there yet, Aldric. Maybe I will be. Ask me again if the world doesn't end tonight." He stepped across the line of green. "Keep anyone off this circle. Whatever happens. That's how you can start earning it."

Aldric nodded once, turned, and limped back up the slope to face whatever remained of his squad and his career and, very possibly, his life.

Isandro walked on alone.

* * *

The Stone was warm. That surprised him every time — he'd expected cold, ancient, indifferent, and instead the black rock radiated a heat like a sleeping animal, and when he pressed his palm flat against it he felt not silence but *attention*. Enormous, slow, vegetal attention, turning toward him the way a sunflower turns toward light.

The green circle was soft underfoot. Moss, real moss, the first he'd seen in weeks. He knelt and laid Sefa down upon it, gently, arranging her limbs with the fussy care he'd once used on his carvings, and her eyes drifted open.

They were green now. Fully green, iris and all, like light through young leaves.

"You came back," she whispered. In common tongue. Her accent was strange, each word placed with the deliberateness of someone handling unfamiliar tools, but it was her voice — a child's voice, thin as paper now, but hers.

"I came back."

"The trees are so loud." Tears slid sideways from her eyes into the moss. "They're all shouting at once. They think they're dying. They're not dying, they're just scared, but they don't know that, and I can't tell them, I'm too *tired*—" Her breath caught. "You have to tell them."

"How?"

"You know how." She lifted one hand, weakly, toward the cord at his chest. "You've always known how. That's why I found you. Of everyone in the whole world, the bones sang honest in your hands."

Isandro took off the cord.

Seven charms. He held them in his palm and felt them tremble — ten years of the dead, his secret livelihood, his nightly company, his punishment and his comfort both. And beneath them, separate, wrapped in oilcloth against his heart, the last charm he owned that carried Maren. Her voice, recorded in sheep's bone the winter before the purges, laughing at some joke he could no longer even remember, only that she had laughed, and that he had made her laugh, and that for eleven seconds carved into bone he could always have her back.

He had told himself for six weeks that he might not have to use it. That the covenant might accept the other seven. That there would be another way, because there was always another way, because he had spent ten years finding small ways around large truths.

The Stone pulsed warmly under his hand. Patient. It had waited thirteen years. It could wait the length of a coward's last hesitation.

But Sefa could not. Her breath was coming slower. The green film was creeping up her throat now, delicate as frost ferns, beautiful and terrible, the wood claiming her cell by cell because she was offering herself faster than the covenant could complete.

"All right," Isandro said. To the Stone. To the dead. To himself. "All right. I'm done looking for the other way."

He unwrapped the oilcloth.

Maren's charm lay in his palm, small and pale, worn smooth from ten years of being held. He pressed his thumb into its groove and spoke the waking-word, and her voice filled the hollow — bright, exasperated, alive:

*"—no, you absolute man, you cannot sell a grief that good, some things aren't for strangers—"*

Laughing. She had been laughing at him. At the memory of it — at the argument, at her, at the sheer improbable luck of having ever been loved by her — Isandro knelt in the ash-ringed green and wept like a child, openly, without shame, for the first time in thirteen years.

Then he kissed the charm, the way he used to kiss her forehead before sleep, and pressed it to Sefa's chest, over her heart, and laid the seven others beside it in a crescent upon the moss.

He spoke the old words. The ones she had taught him syllable by syllable across six weeks of nights, humming them until his bones learned their shape. He did not know their literal meaning — Sefa had only ever smiled when he asked — but he knew their weight. He knew they meant *I give this freely* and *I do not take it back* and *let what dies be finished and let what lives begin*.

The Stone drank the words out of the air.

Heat flooded up through his knees, his spine, his skull — not painful, exactly, but total, like being immersed in a river of pure attention. And the dead began to leave him. He felt each one go: the grandmother, sighing, released. The fisherman, his apologies finally accepted. The soldier boy calling *mother, mother* as he faded — and then, impossibly, softly, from everywhere and nowhere, a woman's voice answering *here, love, here* — and Isandro understood that the covenant did not destroy the dead at all. It *delivered* them. Ten years he had carried them like stolen letters, and all along he had only ever been a postman who'd forgotten to finish the route.

Last of all went Maren. He felt her charm cool against Sefa's chest, felt the voice in it gather itself — and for one instant, one grace-note of an instant, he heard her clearly, closer than she had ever been through bone:

*"Oh, my darling. Look what you grew."*

Then silence.

Not quiet — silence. An enormous, ringing, architectural silence, the silence of a room whose walls have been taken away. Isandro knelt with his hands pressed flat over his own ears and found that it changed nothing. The hearing was gone. The gift that had defined him, fed him, hidden him, damned him — gone, freely given, freely silenced, and the space it left behind was so vast he felt he might fall into it forever.

Under his hands, Sefa gasped.

The green film was receding — drawing back down her throat, out of her wounds, sinking into the moss beneath her like tide going out. Color flooded back into her face. Her hair stayed white — that, he suspected, would stay — but her cheeks flushed pink, her chest heaved, and her eyes flew open, wide and human and astonished, staring up at the burning sky.

Above them, the fires began to die.

Not extinguished — *refused*. Flame after flame guttered and bent backward, as though the whole burning wood had inhaled, and the eastern ridge went dark, and in the ringing silence Isandro saw steam rising off the cooling ash for half a mile in every direction, and heard — with his ordinary, mortal, diminished ears — the sound he would spend the rest of his life trying to describe:

The Weeping Wood, exhaling.

Sefa sat up. She looked at her own hands, turned them over, pressed them into the moss, and began to laugh — a real child's laugh, ragged and delighted, echoing off the black Stone.

Then she grabbed his sleeve, the way Maren used to grab his sleeve, and pointed down the slope with her whole arm, eyes enormous.

Down in the dark, where the Office soldiers had been regrouping, a horn sounded — one note, uncertain, cut short.

And every tree on the ridge, dead and gray and weeping a moment ago, turned its branches toward the Stone at once, like a crowd turning to look.
