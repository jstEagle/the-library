## 19. Green Returning

Sefa had been falling for a long time.

Not the way a stone falls — stones are in too much of a hurry, and they land badly. She fell the way a leaf falls, which is to say slowly, turning, taking her time about it, noticing things on the way down. She noticed that she was very small now. Smaller than a grove. Smaller than a tree. She was down to the size of one girl, and shrinking, and beneath her the Covenant Stone waited like an open hand.

She remembered being a grove. She remembered being a seedling in Maren Coldharbour's cupped palms, wrapped in a shawl that smelled of rain and tallow and stubbornness. She remembered dying — Maren dying, rather, which was a different thing entirely, though at the time it had felt like the same thing, because when the woman who held you dies, some part of you goes into the ground with her whether you mean it to or not.

*Hold on,* Maren had said. Or perhaps the wood had said it through her. Memory is a dishonest thing; even trees get their stories wrong sometimes.

*You hold on too,* Sefa had answered, or meant to answer, or would answer now, ten years too late, in a language made of saplight and root-song that no human ear could parse.

And then there had been Isandro.

She fell past him as she fell. She saw him from above, from inside, from the roots up: a narrow man with carving-scarred fingers and a face like a closed door that had forgotten it used to be open. He was carrying her body through fire, and he was talking to her — not with his mouth but with the charm pressed to her chest, the last of Maren's voice screaming warnings in a dead woman's cadence — and behind all of it, under all of it, he was humming. Badly. Off-key. The old words she had taught him, the covenant-words, and he got half the vowels wrong, and it did not matter at all, because the Stone did not listen with ears.

The Stone listened with *yes.*

*Yes,* said the Stone. *Yes, yes, yes.*

The world turned green.

---

Waking, for Sefa, had always been a layered business. For ten years she had woken as a girl and dreamed as a forest, two lives braided so tightly she couldn't always tell which thoughts were hers and which belonged to ten thousand trees. Waking had felt like surfacing through cold water with someone else's hair in your mouth.

This waking was different. This waking was *singular.*

She opened her eyes — her own eyes, two of them, set in one small face — and the first thing she saw was the sky, and the sky was green.

Not gray. Not the ash-gray canopy of the dying wood, the color of old dishwater, the color everything had been since before she could remember anything else. Green. Young, furious, unapologetic green, light pouring through leaves so new they were practically glowing, and the light came down in long gold columns like something holy deciding to show off.

She lay on the moss beside the Covenant Stone. The Stone itself had changed too — its carvings, worn nearly smooth by centuries, stood out sharp and dark again, wet with clear sap instead of black weeping pitch. The air smelled of rain and growing things and, faintly, of smoke, because the battle had happened here and battles leave messes no amount of miracle fully tidies.

Sefa sat up. Her body did what she asked it to. All of it, instantly, without negotiation, without the lag she'd grown used to — the half-second delay between wanting her arm to move and the wood agreeing to move it. She flexed her fingers. Ordinary fingers. Dirt under the nails, a blister on one palm from the road, the small honest complaints of a human girl.

She looked down at her arm, where three days ago bark had begun to grow over a burn wound.

Skin. Just skin. Pink and new around the edges of the scab, like any healing cut on any child.

"Oh," Sefa said aloud, because her voice worked too, fully, effortlessly, and hearing it startled her into laughter — a rusty, hiccupping sound, the laugh of someone who hadn't laughed in years and wasn't sure of the mechanics anymore. "Oh. Oh, I'm *me.*"

Which raised, immediately, the question of what *me* meant, and she decided, lying there in the green light with her ribs aching and her stomach growling loudly enough to startle a bird off a nearby branch, that she would let future-Sefa worry about metaphysics. Present-Sefa wanted bread.

Then she heard the crying.

It came from somewhere near the Stone's far side — ragged, ugly, unguarded sobbing, the kind of crying that men do only when they have completely given up on doing anything else. Sefa knew every voice in the wood the way you know your own heartbeat, and this one took her a moment to place, because she had never heard it cry before.

Aldric Vane sat slumped against the base of the Stone. He was burned — the left side of his coat was charred black and hung off him like the skin of a roasted chestnut — and there was blood on his temple, dried in a dark crescent, and one hand lay in his lap at an angle hands don't rest at comfortably. His sword was on the ground beside him, placed down carefully, point away, the way you put down a thing you never want to pick up again.

He was watching the canopy.

Sefa followed his gaze upward. The trees were moving — not swaying, not bending to wind, but *moving*, branches lifting, trunks straightening out of their centuries-long stoop, and everywhere the gray was peeling back like old paint flaking off to show the color underneath. A birch nearby shed its ash-pale bark in curling sheets. Underneath, its true skin gleamed silver-white, and along its length, buds were opening in real time, fat green fists unclenching into leaves.

"It's fast," Aldric said hoarsely, without looking at her. "I thought — they told us it would take years. Generations. They said the wood was finished, that it was mercy to put it out of its—"

He stopped. His jaw worked. He tried again.

"I testified," he said. "Thirteen years ago. Against Maren Coldharbour. I stood up in front of the Office and I said the words they gave me to say, and they promised me it would be quick and clean, and then it wasn't." He looked at his ruined hand. "And I spent thirteen years telling myself I had no choice. And then your friend Isandro had me flat on my back with a knife at my throat, and he didn't kill me, and I thought — *he has a choice. He's choosing. So did I.*"

Sefa walked over and sat down next to him. Not too close. Close enough.

"You're bleeding," she said.

"I'm aware."

"You should bind it. Isandro carries rags. He carries *everything*, it's very strange, his pockets are like—" she searched for the word, found the Common Tongue still awkward and wonderful in her mouth, "—like a burrow."

Aldric made a sound that was half laugh, mostly sob, entirely exhausted. He dragged his good hand across his face and looked at her properly for the first time — this small white-haired girl who had been, until yesterday morning, the vessel of the oldest living thing in the world.

"You spoke," he said. "In the battle. The trees — they moved when you—"

"They don't need me anymore." Sefa said it and felt the truth of it settle into her chest, enormous and light at once. She reached out and touched the moss beside her, the way she had touched earth ten thousand times before, expecting to feel herself reflected in it, expected the ground to be an extension of her own body.

It wasn't. It was just ground. Warm, damp, alive, humming faintly with its own green satisfaction — but other. Separate. A neighbor, not a limb.

The wood lived now on its own. Every tree. The whole vast breathing network of it, awake and self-sustaining, needing nothing from her ever again. She was not a grove wearing a girl. She was a girl who had briefly been a grove, the way you might briefly be a guest in someone's house, and now the house was built and furnished and lit, and the door was open, and she could go home.

Home. Wherever that was.

"Does it hurt?" Aldric asked quietly. "Losing it?"

Sefa considered this with the seriousness it deserved. "It hurts like taking off boots after walking all day," she said finally. "It hurts like the feet are free and also like the feet miss the boot. Both things. At once." She frowned. "Isandro says feelings are allowed to be stupid."

"That sounds like Isandro."

"He is usually right, which annoys him."

That got a real laugh out of Aldric — short and cracked, but real — and then his face crumpled again, and he bowed his head over his broken hand, and Sefa, who understood grief in more languages than most, reached over and patted his knee twice, firmly, the way you pat a horse you've decided to keep.

---

They found Isandro forty yards downhill, sitting on a fallen log with Sefa's cloak folded under him and an expression on his face that Sefa had never seen before and needed several minutes to decode.

He was staring at his hands.

Around him, the aftermath of the battle arranged itself: Office soldiers standing in loose, bewildered clusters, their fire-pots cold and abandoned in the mud; Rootbound emerging from the treeline with bark-skin going soft and pliant at their joints, faces humanizing by the hour, weeping openly among the trees that had sheltered them; Mother Quill's body laid out respectfully beneath a young oak that had, overnight, become a very old oak indeed, as if the wood meant to repay her debt personally. And everywhere, everyone, looking up. Looking at the green.

Sefa ran to Isandro. She meant to slow down. She did not slow down.

She hit him at full speed and he caught her on reflex, arms coming around her, and she felt him stiffen — felt him notice what she noticed, that he did not turn his head toward her footsteps, did not hear her breath against his collar, did not hear her say his name into his shoulder.

"Sefa." His voice was rough. Too loud, slightly, the way voices go when their owner can't hear them. He pulled back and held her at arm's length and looked her over with frantic thoroughness — the white hair, the healing burns, the ordinary skin. "You're—"

"I'm me," she said. "Are you?"

He smiled. It was a terrible smile, cracked straight down the middle, grief and relief fighting for the same territory. He tapped his own ear and shook his head.

Sefa's chest went tight. She had known the price before he paid it — she had been the Stone, in those last moments, or the Stone had been her, the border between them gone thin as vellum. *A voice that hears the dead, freely silenced.* She had watched him offer it. She had watched the silence take him the way deep water takes a dropped coin.

She signed to him — the crude hand-signs they'd invented on the road, *you, all right?* — and he nodded, and then shook his head, and then shrugged, which was the most honest answer available.

Then he signed back, slowly, carefully: *worth it.*

And then, because he was Isandro and incapable of letting a moment stand unattended, he added: *also I am starving. Did the world end or can we eat?*

Sefa laughed until she cried, and cried until she laughed, and a Rootbound woman with newly softened bark-skin brought them hardtack and honeycomb from the stores, and they ate sitting on the log while the wood turned green around them at a pace that seemed almost rude, as if it had been holding its breath for thirteen years and intended to make up for lost time immediately.

An hour later, Sefa noticed the soldiers.

There were perhaps thirty of them left. Their captain was dead; their orders were ash; the man who should have led them sat against the Covenant Stone with a ruined hand and a sword he wouldn't touch. And they were watching the forest come back to life around them — watching a sapling grow a foot while a sergeant stared at it, watching a stream run clear for the first time in a decade, watching the black sap dry and crust and fall away from the trunks like a scab coming off a healing wound.

One soldier — young, freckled, maybe nineteen, still clutching an empty fire-pot like a security blanket — knelt down abruptly in the moss. Not to anyone. To the ground itself. He put his palm flat on it the way Sefa had done an hour ago, and whatever he felt there, it undid him completely. He knelt and he stayed kneeling.

His squadmate stared at him. Then at the trees. Then, slowly, deliberately, he set down his iron-tipped spear, stepped back from it as if it might bite him, and sat down on a root to take off his boots, which struck Sefa as an odd form of surrender until she understood: he was feeling the ground too. Everyone was. You couldn't stand on this soil, this morning, and stay armored.

By midday, most of them had fled. Not routed — nothing so dramatic. They simply drifted away down the south paths in ones and twos, leaving weapons in tidy rows, men who would return to Vaelmoor carrying a story no one would believe and none of them would stop telling. A handful stayed. The freckled boy stayed. By evening there were eleven soldiers helping the Rootbound drag wounded onto litters of woven branches, badly, apologetically, with the desperate helpfulness of people trying to outrun what they'd marched here to do.

Aldric did not flee. Sefa watched him make the decision, watched him look at the south path and then turn his back on it, and thought: *there. That was harder than the fighting. That was the hardest thing he's done in thirteen years, and nobody will ever know how heavy it was except him, and me, and maybe Isandro, who notices things.*

Aldric approached them at dusk. He moved like a man made of bruises. He stopped a respectful distance away and stood there holding his helmet under his arm like a schoolboy called before the headmistress.

"Isandro," he said. Then, remembering, louder: "*Isandro.*"

Isandro looked up.

"I can't hear you either," Aldric said, and there was a bleak humor in it that Sefa recognized — the family resemblance of people who have both loved the same dead woman and been broken by the same living institution. "Deaf and disgraced. We match now. You always did hate losing to me at anything."

Isandro signed something rapid and extremely rude.

"I'm going to assume that was forgiveness," Aldric said, "because the alternative is that I walk back to Vaelmoor having learned nothing, and I refuse. I refuse." His voice cracked on the word. He steadied himself. "I'll testify. About the Sundering. About what the Office actually did to start all this. It'll cost me everything — my rank, probably my freedom, possibly more. I find I don't care." He looked up at the canopy, at the green going bronze-gold in the sunset, at leaves unfolding in the darkening air with a sound like soft applause if you listened closely, which Sefa did, and Isandro couldn't, and somehow that was all right too. "I spent thirteen years building a life on top of a lie. The wood gets to regrow. I suppose I do too. From the roots up. Ugly, first."

He saluted — an old gesture, the apprentice-gesture, thumb to the sternum, that Isandro had taught him in another lifetime — and turned, and walked into the gathering dark toward the road and the city and the ruin of his career, upright, limping, unbowed.

Isandro watched him go for a long time. Then he signed, quietly: *idiot.*

Then, after a moment: *my idiot.*

Then, after a longer moment: *we need to follow him eventually. Someone will have to tell them all the truth. And apparently the truth needs a translator.*

Sefa leaned her head against his shoulder. Above them the first stars came out, and through the gaps in the newborn canopy they seemed brighter than she'd ever seen them, as if the sky too had been waiting for permission to shine all the way through.

Tomorrow there would be wounds to tend and dead to bury and a city full of questions waiting at the end of the road, and a silence inside Isandro that he would have to learn to live in the way you learn to live in a house after a fire — room by room, memory by memory. Tomorrow Sefa would have to decide who she was when no forest needed her, which was, she suspected, the same question every person had ever faced, dressed in different clothes.

But tonight the wood breathed around them, whole and independent and impossibly green, and the freckled soldier was showing the Rootbound children how to fold a fire-pot into a cooking pan, and somewhere down the dark road a broken man was walking toward truth at last.

Sefa closed her eyes, and slept — just a girl, just one life, dreaming only her own dreams.

They were enough.
