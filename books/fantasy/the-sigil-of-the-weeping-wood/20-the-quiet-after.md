## 20. The Quiet After

The first thing Isandro noticed was how loud the world was without the dead in it.

That sounded like a contradiction, and he spent a long while sitting against the Covenant Stone trying to work out whether it was one. But it was true. For ten years his skull had been a crowded room — Maren's voice threading through everything, the widow's husband murmuring from the shelf, the drowned ferryman humming, the child who'd died of fever in Copper Lane reciting the names of her dolls over and over because that was all she remembered. He had lived his whole adult life with company he could never set down. And now the room was empty, and the emptiness rang like a struck bell.

Birds. That was the shock of it. Birds were *loud*. Somewhere above him in the greening canopy a wren was conducting what could only be described as an argument with the entire concept of morning, and Isandro realized he had never once heard a bird plainly — not without three layers of whispering dead underneath it, like silt at the bottom of a clear stream.

He laughed. It came out cracked and strange, half sob, and he let it.

The Stone was warm under his palm. Not stone-warm. *Alive*-warm, the way a sleeping animal is warm, and beneath his hand he could feel it breathing in a rhythm too slow to be anything mortal. The last charm — Maren's charm, the one he'd pressed to Sefa's chest — was gone now. Not destroyed. *Spent*. Poured into the covenant along with his hearing-for-the-dead, along with every voice he'd ever carried, along with the small criminal empire of whispers he'd built one widow at a time.

He kept reaching for it. That was the humiliating part. Again and again, in the space of an hour, his mind reached for Maren's voice the way a tongue goes to the gap where a tooth was. *I'll just listen for a moment,* he'd think, and then remember, and the remembering was its own small fall each time.

*You don't need them to hear me.*

She'd said it before, hadn't she? Years ago, alive, exasperated, watching him carve at the kitchen table at some ungodly hour because a client's grief wouldn't wait and neither would his conscience. *Isandro. Put down the bone. You don't need them to hear me.* He'd smiled and kissed her hair and gone on carving, because of course he did. Because listening to the dead had been easier than being present for the living. The dead never asked him to take out the ashes. The dead never noticed when he stopped looking at them mid-sentence.

Maren had known. His wife, sharp-eyed Maren, who'd died screaming in a Pale Office cellar while he hid in Vaelmoor carving charms out of her memory — Maren had known, ten years ahead of time, that her voice was becoming a crutch, and had told him so with love and irritation in equal measure, and he hadn't listened.

Well. Now there was nothing *to* listen to. The irony was so clean it was almost elegant.

He wept for a while. There didn't seem to be any shame in it here, under the turning-green trees, with the sap running gold instead of black and the whole wood exhaling something like relief. He wept the way he should have wept thirteen years ago, when they burned her name into the Edict rolls and told him mourning magic was still magic. He had grieved her in installments since then — a coin of sorrow paid out whenever he needed comfort, collected back whenever he needed resolve. This was different. This was the whole debt at once, and it flattened him, and it was honest, and honesty, he was learning, hurt like a broken bone heals: badly, and then rightly.

When he finally surfaced, Sefa was sitting beside him.

He didn't know how long she'd been there. She had that talent — the stillness of things that grew rather than walked. Her hair had gone white as birch bark during the burning days, and the leaves that had sprouted from her wounds were browning now, curling, dropping off one by one onto her shoulders and the ground, leaving behind skin. Just skin. Ordinary girl-skin, scraped and scarred and smudged with ash. She looked smaller without the green in her. She looked, for the first time since the midnight knock at his door, entirely like a child.

She was watching him with an expression of profound concern, the way children watch adults cry — not frightened exactly, but deeply suspicious that crying might be contagious.

"I'm all right," he said, which was the traditional lie.

Sefa frowned. She opened her mouth. Closed it. Opened it again, and made a sound — a small, frustrated hum, the old language rising in her throat and finding nothing there to hold it. Since the Stone, the forest-tongue was gone from her too; whatever she had been, vessel and grove and last green syllable of a dying covenant, she had spent all of it. What was left was a girl of perhaps eight years who owned one dress, one guardian, and no words in any tongue anyone spoke.

Except.

"You," she said.

Isandro went very still.

"Don't." A pause, brow furrowed, hands gripping her knees with the effort of it, a climber feeling for holds on a sheer face. "Need. Them."

Her eyes came up to his, fierce and brown and utterly human, daring him to make this hard.

"You don't need them to hear me."

The words landed in the enormous quiet of him and echoed there, off walls that used to be full of voices, and found — nothing to bounce against but himself. Just himself. It was enough. It was, he understood at last, the entire point.

"No," he said, and his voice broke on it, and he found he didn't care. "No, I don't."

She considered this for a moment, nodded once with terrible solemnity, and then — because she was also, unmistakably, a child — threw herself sideways into him and demanded, "Hungry."

Isandro laughed until his ribs hurt, and held her, and agreed that yes, hungry was probably the correct word for both of them.

* * *

They ate ash-bread and late blackberries with the Rootbound around a fire that evening, and Isandro learned that celebration among people who were half tree looked very much like celebration among people who weren't, only slower.

The Rootbound woman with bark skin — Ysolde, she was called, though she said names sat strangely on her now — sat across the flames with her roots-arms folded and watched him with an expression he was beginning to recognize as affection wearing armor.

"You look terrible," she observed.

"I feel wonderful."

"Liar."

"Both things can be true." He turned his hands over, studying them. They were just hands again. For ten years those hands had been instruments — vessels, antennae, little doors. Now they were cracked and burn-blistered and they were going to ache tomorrow, ordinary ache, the kind you earned with work instead of with grief. "Everything's quiet," he said. "In here." He tapped his temple. "I keep waiting for it to feel like loss. Mostly it feels like a room after the guests have gone home. You know. The good kind of tired."

Ysolde was quiet a moment. Firelight moved on her bark-face, catching in the deep grain of it.

"The first year is the worst," she said finally. "You'll talk to them. Out loud, in empty rooms. Catch yourself waiting for answers." A dry rustle that might have been a shrug. "Then one day you realize you've been talking to yourself for an hour, and you'll be embarrassed, and then you'll be free. In that order. Always in that order."

"And after the free?"

"After the free," she said, "you plant things."

Across the fire, Mother Quill's absence had a shape. Someone had set her hat on a stone, absurdly upright, and no one had moved it, and no one would. Isandro raised his cup of berry-wine — Rootbound wine, which tasted like a tree's opinion of fruit — toward the hat, and around the fire, silently, every hand rose and fell. Even Sefa's, copying the grown-ups with the gravity of someone performing a sacred rite, then immediately stealing his blackberries while his cup was up.

"Thief," he said.

"Sefa," she said, pointing at herself with tremendous dignity, mouth stained purple.

"Yes, well, Sefa. Those were mine."

"Hungry," she explained, unrepentant, and Ysolde made the creaking sound that was apparently her laugh, and somewhere overhead the new leaves whispered in a wind that carried nothing but air.

Later — much later, when the fire had gone down to embers and the wounded were sleeping in their rows and the smell of smoke had given way to the smell of green — Sefa crept over to where Isandro lay awake and installed herself against his side with the efficiency of long practice.

"Isandro," she whispered.

"Mm."

"Trees are loud."

He blinked at the stars through the canopy. "Loud?"

She waved a hand vaguely at everything — the wood, the dark, the world. Her vocabulary was still small and she deployed it with the precision of a surgeon and the scope of a conqueror. "Inside. Trees. Loud. Happy-loud." She frowned, searching. "Like birds. Like your bird. The angry one."

"The wren."

"The wren." She said it carefully, tasting the word. Then, softer, drowsy: "Not sad anymore. Was sad. Long-long time. Now—" She made a gesture he couldn't follow and gave up, wriggling deeper into his side. "Now happy-loud. You sleep. I listen."

And she did — he felt her breathing slow, felt her go heavy against his ribs, a warm weight with a heartbeat in it, and Isandro lay staring up through the branches at the thirteenth moon, risen and full and *past*, already waning, already letting go, and thought: she listens now. That's hers. The hearing passed to her, or she always had it, or the covenant left it in her like a candle in a window. Either way, the wood had a voice again, and the voice was a child who liked blackberries and stole them, and somehow that was the most hopeful thing the world had produced in thirteen years.

He slept better than he had in a decade.

* * *

They stayed in the wood six more days, because you cannot march the wounded anywhere and because, frankly, nobody wanted to leave.

It was Sefa who ended it. On the sixth morning she stood at the southern edge of the trees — where the gray had retreated and the green ran right down to the old road like water finding its level — pointed back the way they had come, toward Vaelmoor, toward smoke and law and the world, and said:

"Home. Go home."

"Not the wood?" Isandro asked. He'd half assumed she belonged here now, that the trees would fold her back in, that she'd been returned like a borrowed thing.

Sefa looked at the forest for a long moment. Something passed over her face — recognition, farewell, the particular sadness of closing a door you're glad exists but don't need to live behind. She touched the nearest trunk, laid her whole palm flat against it, and stood there while — Isandro was almost sure, though he couldn't have said how he knew, since the dead were silent and the trees spoke only to her now — while something vast and patient said goodbye.

Then she took her hand back, wiped her nose on her sleeve, and repeated, with the finality of a judge: "Home. Go home."

So they went home.

The road south was a procession of the limping. Rootbound who could pass for human walked wrapped in cloaks, hoods pulled low over bark-faces. The Office soldiers who had survived — those Aldric had turned, those who had simply stopped, dropped their fire-pots, and sat down in the burning paths and refused — came too, at a careful distance, unarmed, uncertain whether they were prisoners or pilgrims. Most of them, Isandro noticed, kept looking back at the treeline the way you look back at a house you grew up in. One young soldier marched beside Isandro for a while and finally worked up the nerve to ask, in a whisper, whether it was true that the trees had *screamed* when the fire reached them.

"They sang," Isandro said. "Right up until the end. Then they fought. Then they forgave us, most of them. The ones that could."

The soldier nodded several times, absorbing this, and said, "I joined the Office because my village flooded and the Office fed us." Another nod. "Nobody told me what we were feeding."

"No," Isandro said. "They never do."

Vaelmoor appeared out of the autumn haze on the ninth day, red-roofed and rain-hung and smelling of coal smoke and fish, and Isandro stood on the last hill with Sefa's hand in his and felt something he couldn't immediately name. It took him a quarter mile of walking to identify it.

It was dread. But it was also hope, and they were braided together so tightly he couldn't tell which strand was which, and he suspected that was simply what the rest of his life was going to feel like.

Because the city gates were open. That wasn't strange; the gates were always open by day. What was strange was the crowd gathered inside them, and the notices nailed up on either side of the archway, fresh ink, official seal half-torn away by wind, and — strangest of all, impossible of all — people standing beneath the notices *reading aloud*. Reading the forbidden words of the old covenant-songs off torn parchment in broad daylight, in the open street, while a Pale Office sentry stood beside them and did nothing.

Sefa tugged his hand. Pointed. "Words," she said. Then, frowning at her own insufficiency, she tried again, hauling up new words one at a time like buckets from a deep well: "Many. Words. Everywhere."

"Yes," Isandro said slowly. "There are."

And then a figure detached itself from the crowd by the gate — tall, burned, moving with the stiff carefulness of a man whose body was mostly bandages under his coat, one arm bound and useless, his Office grays scorched gray-black and his insignia torn off at the shoulder. Aldric Vane stopped ten feet away and stood there swaying slightly, as if he'd walked a very long way on very little sleep, which, from the state of him, he clearly had.

His eyes went to Sefa first. Something in his face came apart and reassembled itself. Then they went to Isandro.

"Aldric," Isandro said carefully. "You're supposed to be dead. Or disgraced. Preferably both."

"I'm working on it," Aldric rasped. His voice was ruined — smoke, or shouting orders he shouldn't have shouted, or both. He fumbled one-handed at his coat and produced a folded paper, official, sealed, and held it out. "You'll want to see this. And Isandro—" His jaw worked. Ten years of friendship and thirty years of history moved behind his eyes, none of it sayable in a city square. "The Office has questions about the Sundering. Real ones. And there's a tribunal. In three weeks." He swallowed. "And they're going to want to hear from the man who reforged the covenant."

Behind them, in the open street of Vaelmoor, someone began to sing one of the forbidden songs, badly, and nobody stopped them.

Sefa squeezed Isandro's hand and looked up at him, and asked the question with her whole face: *What now?*

Isandro took the paper. Took a breath of coal-smoke and fish-smell and rain. "Now," he said, "we find out if the truth survives a courtroom."
