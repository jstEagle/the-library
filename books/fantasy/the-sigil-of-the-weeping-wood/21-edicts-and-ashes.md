## 21. Edicts and Ashes

The rain came back to Vaelmoor the way it always did — sideways, insulted, as if it had somewhere better to be. Isandro Coldharbour stood at his workbench with a bone blank in one hand and a carving knife that had never once whispered to him, and he was, by every measure that mattered, content.

The bone was cow femur, bought honest from Brannic's slaughter-yard for two coppers. It would become a button. Or possibly a toggle, if the grain argued with him. It would not sing. It would not carry the last words of a drowned fisherman or a grandmother's recipe for fever tea. It would sit in a coat and hold a coat shut, and it would do so without breaking any edicts, royal proclamations, or municipal ordinances, of which there were currently fewer than there used to be.

"Isandro," Sefa called from the front room, "a man is asking whether you can carve his mother's face onto a pipe."

"Onto a pipe?"

"Onto the bowl part."

"Tell him I can, but it'll smoke strangely and look like she's disapproving."

A pause. Then, faintly: "He says his mother always disapproved, so that's fine."

He smiled at the blank in his hand. Ten years ago — eleven now, though he'd stopped counting the way a man stops touching a bruise — he would have heard the dead murmuring under his floorboards like tenants behind on rent. Now there was only the rain, the scratch of the knife, and the sound of a girl who had once been a forest learning how to complain about porridge.

It was, all things considered, a good trade.

* * *

Vaelmoor had not been saved. That was the first thing Isandro had learned in the months since they came home, and the thing he had to keep relearning, because some part of him kept expecting the world to behave like a story.

Cities did not turn green overnight. They turned green the way a kettle comes to boil — slowly, unevenly, with a lot of rattling nobody could quite explain.

The Edict of Silence hadn't been repealed. It had simply begun to be ignored, which in Vaelmoor amounted to nearly the same thing. The Pale Office still stood on its street of white stone, but its whistles sounded less often, and when they did, half the time the officers found nothing but an old woman tending herbs that grew in spirals no botanist could account for, and the old woman would offer them tea, and somehow everyone would leave feeling slightly embarrassed about the whole business.

The regrowth made denial impossible. You could burn a wood once — the Sundering had proven that, thirteen years and a mountain of ash ago. But you could not burn it twice when it was growing up through your cobblestones. Green shoots had split the flagstones of Tanner's Row. The great drain-grates along the canal sprouted ferns that no amount of iron scraping discouraged. The city's gardeners declared war on them; the city's children declared the gardeners mad; the war went badly for the gardeners.

And everywhere the new wood grew, it wept — clear sap now, sweet-smelling, not the black tar of the dying years — and people collected it in little vials and wore it on cords around their necks, which was, Isandro noted dryly, exactly the kind of charm-trade he'd spent a decade risking the gallows for, except now the Office couldn't decide if it was contraband or commerce.

"They're taxing it," Sefa reported one evening, coming home from the market with bread and news in equal supply. "Sap-tariffs. There's a clerk whose whole job is sap."

"There's a clerk whose whole job is everything, eventually," Isandro said. "That's what cities are for."

But underneath the ordinary strangeness ran something less ordinary, something that had started with Aldric Vane.

* * *

They had come back to Vaelmoor in late autumn, when the first frost silvered the wood's edge and the Rootbound — those half-tree survivors who had tested Isandro and forgiven him barely — stood at the treeline watching them go with expressions that were impossible to read, being partly bark. The journey back had been quiet. Aldric had stayed behind, broken-burned, refusing to travel with them.

"He said he has testimony to give," Sefa told the Rootbound leader, translating from the tree-speech she still dreamed in. "He said someone has to say out loud what the Sundering actually was."

What the Sundering actually was: Isandro knew now, the way you know a wound's shape after carrying it long enough. Not a righteous purge of dangerous magic. A land grab dressed in scripture. The mages who wrote the Edict had not feared the Weeping Wood — they had coveted it, and when the Wood refused the covenant's terms, when it declined to be owned, they broke the covenant deliberately and burned what wouldn't kneel. Maren had died because she saw it happen. Because she carried away the seedling that proved it. Because she chose, at the end, the same choice Isandro had finally understood at the Covenant Stone: that someone has to choose, freely, or nothing is ever truly given.

Aldric had testified for six days.

Isandro hadn't gone to watch. He'd wanted to — had walked as far as the tribunal steps twice and turned back twice, telling himself it was discretion and knowing it was cowardice wearing discretion's coat. But the whole city heard it anyway, because tribunals leak like old roofs. By the third day, the taverns were full of it. By the fifth day, the pamphlets were. By the sixth day, three junior officers of the Pale Office had resigned publicly, two senior mages who had signed the original Edict fled the city ahead of warrants, and Aldric Vane — decorated interrogator, loyal son of the institution, the man who had once testified against his friend's wife to save his own skin — stood in front of the tribunal and said, in a voice witnesses described as very quiet:

"I lied thirteen years ago to save myself. I am lying no longer, even though this time the truth will cost me everything I lied to protect. I have learned the difference between loyalty and surrender, and I am ashamed of how long it took me."

They stripped him of rank, naturally. Stripped his pension, his quarters, his name from the rolls. He was fined into penury for perjury retroactively committed. And then, because Vaelmoor is Vaelmoor, the guild of lamplighters — who remembered that during the purges Aldric had quietly looked the other way more than once, that he had misfiled reports, lost warrants, misplaced suspects — offered him a job holding a ladder.

He took it.

"You could visit him," Sefa said one night, catching Isandro sharpening knives he didn't need sharpened. "You know that, right? He'd want—"

"I know what he'd want."

"He held a ladder today for four hours. Mother Quill's neighbor saw him. She says he's terrible at it."

That surprised a laugh out of him. "Terrible how?"

"He keeps offering to interrogate the ladder."

Isandro put down the knife. Looked at the fire. Felt the enormous silence where the dead used to live — it never fully stopped being enormous; you just learned to build rooms inside it.

"Tomorrow," he said, and was surprised to find he meant it.

* * *

He went on a grey morning, and found Aldric on a street corner near the tanneries, holding a ladder while another man repaired a lamp bracket, both of them losing gracefully to the physics of ladders.

For a moment neither spoke. Eleven years of friendship, three years of enmity, and several months of mutual avoidance sat between them on the cobbles like a fourth person.

"The lamp won," Isandro said at last.

"It cheated." Aldric climbed down, slow, favoring the side the fire had taken. Up close he looked thinner and older and oddly lighter, like a man who had set down luggage he'd forgotten he was carrying. "Isandro."

"Aldric."

"I owe you—" Aldric began, and Isandro raised a hand.

"Don't. Please. I spent a year wanting to hear exactly that sentence, and now that you're saying it, I find I don't want it. What I want is stranger." He looked up at the lamp, the guttering flame, the ordinary morning. "I want to know if you'd do it again. Testify. All of it. Knowing what it costs."

"Yes." No hesitation. That was the thing about Aldric — he had always been a man who answered fast and paid slowly. "Would you?"

"Carve buttons?" Isandro pulled one from his coat pocket and handed it over. Plain bone, honest work, no voice in it at all. "Every day."

Aldric turned the button over in his scarred fingers. Something moved through his face — grief, mostly, and under it, faintly, the boy he'd been before either of them knew what institutions were for.

"Maren would have liked this," he said quietly. "You, doing this."

"She'd have liked it better if I'd done it ten years earlier instead of selling whispers in alleys like a smuggler of ghosts."

"She'd have liked that too, honestly. She liked trouble." Aldric smiled, and it cracked something open in them both, gently. "Come by the lamplighters' hall sometime. We've a stove that works and wine that doesn't deserve to."

"I'll bring the girl. She collects stories about you being terrible at things."

"She'll be well supplied."

They shook hands, and it was awkward, and it was enough. Walking home, Isandro realized his eyes were wet and let them be. Grieving Maren truly, without her voice to lean on, meant grieving her in daylight too, in small unexpected weather. He was getting better at standing in it.

* * *

Winter came, and with it the tribunal's final rulings, which satisfied no one, which Isandro took as the mark of genuine justice.

Magic remained unlawful. Formally. On paper, the Edict of Silence still stood, its ink unblotted. But the enforcement had died of embarrassment. An Office memo — leaked, naturally, within a week — instructed officers that "matters of folk practice and woodland affinity are to be noted, not prosecuted," a sentence that would have been unthinkable a year prior and was now read aloud in taverns to general laughter.

So the hedge-witches came out of their cellars. Mother Quill's students — Quill herself was gone, burned holding a gate open in the Gray Canopy, and Isandro still could not think of her without his chest tightening — began openly teaching herb-lore and hedge-singing above a bakery on Cordwainer Street. Old charms resurfaced in market stalls, sold with a wink and a shrug. Nobody was hanged. Nobody even got fined, except once, spectacularly, a merchant who tried to corner the sap market, and that fine was universally agreed to be deserved on general principle.

Not utopia. Isandro was careful never to call it that, especially to Sefa, who sometimes looked at the greening city with an expectation in her face that worried him.

"It's not going to be fair," he told her one evening, carving by firelight while she practiced her letters. "The men who wrote the Edict — most of them died rich and old. The ones who didn't flee will retire comfortably. Justice here moves like a glacier with a limp."

"But it moves?"

"It moves." He blew a curl of shaving off the bone. "Slowly, badly, and usually because somebody stands in front of it and refuses to shut up. Your friend Aldric is currently the most refused-to-shut-up man in Vaelmoor."

She considered this, chewing her pencil — a habit he'd given up correcting, since the pencils were cheap and her expression while doing it suggested deep jurisprudence.

"And the trees?" she asked. "Do the trees get anything?"

Ah. There it was. The question underneath everything, the one he suspected she carried the way he once carried charms: *what am I owed for what was taken?*

"The trees get the treaty," he said. "The settlers ask permission now. Before felling, before building, before cutting so much as a switch. It's in the charter — your words, translated. The whole northern valley. First place in history where the forest gets a vote." He hesitated, then added, honestly: "Some settlers hate it. Some ignore it. Some will cheat. But it's written down, and written things outlive angry men."

She nodded slowly and went back to her letters. But that night, he heard her on the rooftop — she slept on the roof half the time, claiming the stars needed company — humming to the gutter-ferns, and the ferns leaned toward her like cats, and he decided that whatever else the world failed to fix, it had fixed at least one thing beyond repair.

* * *

Spring found Isandro Coldharbour with a waiting list.

Word had gotten around, the way word does, that the carver on Bellows Lane worked plain bone and worked it beautifully — buttons, combs, pipe-bowls, toys, the little handles for midwives' tools, walking-stick grips carved with the owner's initials. Once, a woman had asked, shyly, if he could make something that would let her hear her dead husband again.

"No," he'd said, kindly. "No one can. I'm sorry."

And then, because kindness is a craft too: "But I can carve his initials into a comb, and every morning when you use it, you'll think of him without my help. That lasts longer than any whisper. Trust me — I'm an expert on the difference."

She'd ordered three.

His hands had changed. Ten years of secret work had taught him precision, but fear had lived in his fingers the whole time, a tension he hadn't noticed until it was gone. Now the knife moved like it had nothing to hide, and the work came off the bench cleaner for it. Honest bone, honest cuts. Some nights he finished a piece and held it up to the light and listened to it — out of pure habit — and heard exactly nothing, and felt the silence settle around him like a coat that fit.

The dead were silent forever. That was the price, and prices like his didn't get refunded. But he had learned — was still learning, would probably always be learning — that hearing the dead was not the same as honoring them. Honoring happened at the bench, and the market stall, and the lamplighters' hall where he now drank indifferent wine with a reformed enemy, and the rooftop where he left blankets for a girl who communed with gutter-plants, and the letters he was slowly teaching himself to write for the tribunal archives, testimony after testimony, standing in front of the glacier, refusing to shut up.

Survival without truth was never living. He'd paid full price to learn it. He intended to get his money's worth.

* * *

On an evening in late spring, with the canal ferns waist-high and the whole city smelling of green and river mud, Sefa came down from the roof unusually quickly, which meant news, which lately meant the wood.

"They sent for us," she said, breathless. "Both of us. From the Stone."

"Sent for us how? The trees don't write letters."

"A runner. One of the Rootbound — the young one, the one who's mostly birch. He ran the whole way." She pressed a hand flat to her chest, and he saw the old gesture, the one she made when the forest's memory stirred in her. "Isandro — the trees are singing. Not humming. *Singing.* All of them, all at once. The elder says it's never happened, not even before the Sundering. They want us at the Covenant Stone by the dark of the moon."

He set down his knife. Outside, far to the north, past the rooftops and the smoking chimneys and the greening streets, something vast was making a sound he could not hear — and yet, faintly, in the hollow where the dead's voices used to live, in the scar tissue of his sacrificed gift, he thought he almost could.

"Well," he said, reaching for his coat. "I suppose the buttons can wait."
