## 22. The First Green Year

### Isandro

The trouble with teaching a language that had no word for *mine*, Isandro discovered, was that everyone wanted to learn it and nobody wanted to do the homework.

"Again," he said to the circle of settlers gathered in the dooryard of the cottage at the wood's edge. Eleven of them this morning, which was a record. Last month there had been four: a stonemason with a bad knee, two sisters who'd fled a debt in Corvane, and old Brammel, who attended mostly for the bread Sefa baked and admitted it freely.

"Again," he repeated, and held up his hands.

Eleven settlers held up their hands. The gesture was called *thel-enn* — literally, *the asking of the ground* — and you made it before you took so much as a fallen branch. You pressed your palms flat toward the earth, waited three heartbeats, and if nothing in your chest flinched, you were permitted. If something did flinch, you were meant to walk away and think about why.

It was, Isandro had come to believe, better law than anything the Pale Office had ever written. Though admittedly the bar there was subterranean.

"You're rushing it," he told a broad-shouldered farmer named Doss, whose palms had hovered a full foot above the soil as though the ground might bite. "You don't ask permission of a thing you're afraid to touch."

"I'm not afraid," Doss said.

"You're afraid. That's all right. The wood likes afraid. Afraid is honest." He crouched and pressed his own hands flat to the grass, and felt — as he always felt now, faintly, like warmth through a wall — the vast slow attention beneath him turn its regard upon the yard. "There. See? Nothing happened. The world didn't end. It just noticed you. That's all asking is."

Doss tried again. This time his palms actually touched the earth, and his flinch was visible from across the yard, and Isandro nodded as though a great treaty had been signed, because in a way it had.

Sefa appeared at the door with a tray of rolls and the expression she wore whenever she'd been listening longer than anyone realized. She was taller than she'd been a year ago — they grew fast when a forest stopped drinking them dry — and her hair had come back dark except for one white streak over her left ear that she refused to let anyone suggest dyeing. *It's where the oldest tree touched me,* she'd said once. *I'm keeping it.*

She set the tray down and looked at Doss with the frank, unblinking appraisal that still unnerved newcomers. Then she hummed — two notes, low and rising — and Doss's face went slack with wonder.

"He asked properly," she translated, because her students didn't know yet that the wood answered in more than feelings. "It says his flinch is a good one. He's sorry about the fence he cut last autumn without asking."

Doss went the color of beets. "How does it know about the fence?"

"The wood knows about everything inside the fence," Sefa said, deadpan, and handed him a roll. "That's what a fence is. A promise."

Isandro laughed before he could stop himself, and eleven settlers laughed with him, and somewhere under their feet something enormous and slow rumbled what he could only describe — though he had never described it to anyone, because who would believe him — as satisfaction.

### Sefa

The man called Doss thought too loudly. Most of them did. Their thoughts came off them like heat off a forge — worry-worry-fence-debt-mother-money — and Sefa had learned in the first green months that if she stood very still near someone thinking hard, she could taste it, the way you could taste rain coming.

Isandro said this would fade. He said most of what the wood had poured into her was draining away now that the trees could feed themselves, leaving only what was truly hers. She wasn't sure she wanted it to fade. Being a grove had been terrible and being a girl was sometimes lonely, but the space between — this narrow country where she could feel a forest breathing through the soles of her boots and also burn bread because she got distracted by a beetle — this space was hers. Whatever *hers* meant in a language without the word.

She carried the empty tray back inside and paused at the window.

Isandro was showing them the hand-shapes now, the grammar of roots. He was terrible at it. His fingers were carver's fingers, precise and clever, and they still insisted on making each sign perfectly, like little bone charms, when the whole point was that the signs should move like water, like sap, like something growing rather than something built. The settlers copied his stiff little gestures faithfully. Sefa had given up correcting him months ago. Watching him be wrong in front of people, cheerfully, on purpose or nearly, was one of her favorite things in the world, and she had learned in the burning year that favorite things were not to be wasted.

A year ago she had lain in a hollow of ash with leaves growing out of her wounds, pouring herself into dying trees, certain — in the way trees are certain, absolutely and without drama — that she was ending.

And then a man with ruined hearing had knelt beside her, pressed a small white charm against her chest, and spoken her own language back to her in an accent like gravel in a bucket, and the world had turned green.

He couldn't hear the wood anymore. She knew what it cost him; she'd felt the silence close over him at the Stone like a door shutting, felt him stand alone inside it. But sometimes, watching him work — he still carved, plain bone now, buttons and combs and little animals for children, honest work for honest coin — sometimes his hands would stop over a piece and go still, and his head would tilt, just slightly, the way it used to tilt when a charm whispered to him.

She didn't tell him. Some things you keep the way the wood keeps seeds: quietly, underground, until the season is right.

### Isandro

Brammel stayed behind after the others left, ostensibly to help stack the chairs, actually to talk.

"They're saying in Vaelmoor," the old man said, working a chair into place with tremendous unconcern, "that Vane testified before the full Conclave last week. Stood up in front of all those gray robes and told them the Sundering wasn't the wood's doing. That the Office broke the covenant first, thirteen years back, and burned Maren Halloran and nine others to cover it."

Isandro kept stacking. His hands were steady. They'd had a year of practice at being steady.

"Aldric always did have a talent for arriving late to the truth," he said.

"They've stripped his rank. Struck him from the rolls." Brammel set down the chair. "Man gave up everything."

"No," Isandro said. "He gave up a lie he'd been carrying so long he'd mistaken it for a spine. That's lighter than it sounds. I know, because I carried the same one."

He thought of Aldric often now, mostly without anger. There was even a letter, unopened, in the drawer where the charms used to live — the one drawer in the house with no bones in it at all. He'd get to it. Or he wouldn't, and that would be an answer too.

What he thought of more was the quiet.

For ten years his life had had a soundtrack: the murmurous dead, the charms whispering on their strings like restless birds, Maren's voice rising out of the walnut box every night when cowardice needed soothing. He had built a whole marriage's afterlife out of stolen minutes of her voice, and told himself it was devotion. It had taken losing it — losing all of it, the widow-charms and the stranger-voices and her — to understand that he hadn't been keeping her. He'd been keeping himself from grieving her. There was a difference, and the difference had teeth.

Now the silence was just silence. Enormous, some nights. He woke sometimes reaching for a sound that wasn't there and lay in the dark doing the arithmetic of absence.

But other mornings —

Other mornings he'd be splitting kindling, or kneading dough badly while Sefa corrected him, and he'd feel it again: that faint, warm, through-the-wall presence. Not a voice. Not the dead. Something alive and patient underneath the world, noticing him.

Only a once-hearer could have known what it was. And somehow, impossibly, faintly, he did.

* * *

### Sefa

On the evening of the anniversary, they walked into the trees together, as they had decided weeks ago they would, though neither had said aloud what they hoped to find there.

The paths were unrecognizable now. Where the fire had taken the gray canopy, new growth stood thick as a crowd — birch and rowan and oak shooting up with the haste of things making up lost time, the black-scarred trunks of the survivors wearing garlands of ivy like medals. Sap ran clear now, sweet-smelling. The wood wept only in spring, and only, Sefa suspected, for remembrance.

They reached the Covenant Stone at dusk.

It had changed. A year ago it had been a bare gray monolith in a clearing of ash. Now moss climbed it in green rivers, wildflowers crowded its base in reckless profusion, and the first stars caught in the shallow basin at its top where rainwater gathered and never quite evaporated.

Sefa put her palm against the stone and made the sign. *Thel-enn.* The asking.

*We are here,* she said in the old language, the one older than kingdoms, the one she was teaching to farmers and stonemasons and one stubborn carver who pronounced it like a man gargling river stones. *One year.*

The wood answered.

Not in words. In breath.

The whole forest exhaled — every tree at once, a sound like the world letting go of something it had been holding for thirteen years, a long silver sigh that moved through the canopy overhead and out across the valleys and up into the darkening sky where the thirteenth moon, full and round and exactly one year turned, hung listening.

Sefa heard it with her whole body, root to crown.

But she watched Isandro.

He had gone very still, head tilted, the way he tilted — and she saw his eyes widen, saw his breath catch, saw the look of a man hearing a voice in a room he knew to be empty. Not the dead. Not the whispers of bone. Something else. Something that had perhaps been waiting a year, politely, at the edge of a silence, for the season to be right.

"It exhales," he said hoarsely. "Every year?"

"Every year," she said. "On the night the covenant was reforged." She hesitated. "You heard it."

"I heard something." He laughed, and it cracked a little in the middle, and he wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand as though annoyed at them. "I'm supposed to be deaf, you know. I had plans. Quiet ones."

"You're not deaf." She took his hand — the scarred one, the carver's one — and pressed it flat against the Stone, the way she'd taught eleven settlers to press their palms to the ground that morning. "You paid. Paid things leave a shape. Like a scar. The wood fits itself into the shape."

They stood there together, the once-hearer and the once-grove, while the sigh traveled away over the hills and the moon rose higher and somewhere behind them, far off toward the city, a thousand lamps were being lit by people who no longer feared the dark between the trees.

"Do you miss them?" she asked quietly. "The voices. Her voice."

Isandro was silent a long time.

"I miss her," he said finally. "That's different. Missing her voice was easy — it was like missing a warm coat. Missing *her* is harder. It's honest." He smiled down at her, tired and whole. "Surviving without truth was never living. Someone told me that once. Well. Showed me."

"I showed you lots of things."

"You showed me one thing. The rest was bread and grammar."

She laughed, and the nearest trees rustled, and high above them the canopy — green now, green all the way to the horizon, green as far as any eye could follow — moved like a single creature settling comfortably into sleep.

Some prices, Isandro thought, buy more than they cost. He could not hear the dead anymore, and he never would again, and the silence where his wife's voice used to live would ache on some nights for the rest of his life.

But the wood lived. The child spoke. And tomorrow there would be bread to ruin, and grammar to mangle, and a drawer with a letter in it that he was almost, almost ready to open.

He felt the ground notice him, warm through his boots, patient as spring.

He asked. It said yes.
