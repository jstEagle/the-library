## 1. The Date That Shouldn't Exist

There were three certainties in Rosalind Winterbourne's life: the mountain passes of Thornfell were always colder than you remembered, the customs officers at the Grey Gate were always hungrier than they looked, and nobody — not one living soul in the whole grey-green sprawl of the kingdom — could tell you what a Wayfarer looked like.

Rosalind had spent thirty-one years making sure of the third one.

It was a craft, invisibility. Not the flashy kind that stage-magicians sold to drunk lords at midsummer fairs, but the slow kind, the honest kind: the stoop of the shoulders just so, the coat dyed the exact mud-brown of a thousand other coats, the face arranged into an expression that made people's eyes slide off like rain off oiled canvas. Rosalind had practiced it so long that sometimes, catching their reflection in a shop window, even they felt a small flicker of *who?* — which was, they supposed, the point.

Tonight the contraband was candles.

Twelve dozen of them, beeswax, unsanctioned, smuggled up from the lowlands in false-bottomed flour sacks because the Chandlers' Guild held a royal monopoly on wax and charged prices that would make a bishop blush. The parish priest of Little Hallowbrook had paid half upfront and wept with gratitude, which Rosalind found excessive but profitable. A candle was a small rebellion. You lit one, and for a few hours your corner of the world refused to be dark. Rosalind appreciated that in an object.

"Oi," said the mule.

The mule did not actually speak. But after eleven years together, Rosalind had gotten fluent in the particular grammar of its ears, and right now both ears were pinned flat and its tail had gone stiff as a hearth-brush, which translated roughly to: *something is wrong and I would like it noted that I told you first.*

Rosalind stopped on the switchback road above Thornfell Vale and listened.

Wind in the pines. Somewhere below, a dog barking itself hoarse at nothing. The creak of the flour sacks shifting on the mule's back — twelve dozen candles chiming faintly against each other like nervous teeth.

And under all of it, a silence. The wrong kind. The kind where birds should have been settling for the night and weren't.

"All right," Rosalind murmured, patting the mule's neck. "Noted."

They moved off the road, up a goat track they'd known since childhood, toward the one shelter within reach before true dark. Which meant — and here Rosalind felt the small, familiar tug of something between homesickness and dread — the old Guildhouse.

* * *

The Wayfarers' Guildhouse sat on its shoulder of hill like an old hound too loyal to admit it had been abandoned. Once, Rosalind had been told, it had bustled. Couriers coming and going at all hours, saddle-leather and song, the great seal of the guild stamped on a hundred doors across the kingdom. Now the roof over the east wing had folded in like a dropped cake, ivy had taken the west wall hostage, and the front door hung open to anyone — which was fine, because no one came.

Rosalind let themselves in through the stable door instead, out of habit. Front doors were for people who existed.

Inside smelled of dust and cold stone and, beneath it, something older and stranger — a smell like a book left shut for a very long time, like paper holding its breath. Rosalind got the mule settled in what had once been the tack room, fed it the good oats reserved for bad nights, and built a small fire in the common room hearth, using wood from the pile that some previous version of themselves had stacked there years ago and no one else would ever use.

Supper was bread, hard cheese, and an apple with opinions. Rosalind ate sitting on the floor with their back against a fallen beam, because the chairs had all gone soft with rot, and watched the fire do its small brave work.

It was while chewing the apple that they noticed the light.

Not the firelight. Another light, faint and greenish-gold, leaking from under the door of the scriptorium — the sealed room at the end of the hall that every Wayfarer learned, before anything else, never to open.

Every Wayfarer. All of whom, by Rosalind's best count, numbered exactly one.

"Well," said Rosalind to the apple core, "that's new."

They stood. Their knees objected; knees always object when the mind has decided something interesting is happening. Rosalind walked down the hall slowly, the way you'd approach a sleeping bear, or a letter from a tax collector — carefully, and with a strong desire to be elsewhere.

The door of the scriptorium was not locked. It had never been locked, precisely. It was *sealed*, which was different: the frame bore the guildmaster's mark pressed into lead, a century old if it was a day, and every courier had been taught that the seal was guildmaster Voss's own doing and Voss's alone could break it. Ede Voss, who had walked into the palace a hundred years ago with a book under his arm and had never walked out again.

The seal was broken. The lead lay on the floor in two neat halves, as if it had been opened gently, from inside someone's memory of how it should be done.

The green-gold light came from the lectern in the center of the room, where a book sat open.

Rosalind knew the book without ever having seen it. Every Wayfarer did. The Ash Codex — the guildmaster's book of prophecy, bound in grey leather gone soft as moleskin, its pages said to be ash-paper fired in dragon-breath and written in ink that only appeared when it intended to. Children in the guild — Rosalind had been one, once, briefly, before — used to dare each other to say its name three times fast. Nobody ever managed it. Something about the name stuck in the throat like a fishbone.

Now it sat there, glowing faintly, patient as a spider.

"You're supposed to be sealed," Rosalind told it.

The book did not answer, which was a mercy, and continued glowing, which was not.

Here is the thing about Rosalind Winterbourne, and they would deny it if asked: they were curious the way other people were hungry. They could go without, for a while, on discipline alone. But eventually curiosity ate them from the inside, and the only cure was knowing.

They crossed the room. They looked down at the open page.

The page showed a date.

Ink — fresh, black, still faintly glossy at the edges, as though the pen had lifted from it moments ago — read, in a fine cramped hand:

*The seventeenth day of Deepwinter, in the year of the Guttering Flame.*

Rosalind's mouth went dry.

That was today's date. Not approximately. Not in the vague way prophecies are dated, all *in the days to come* and *when the rivers run backward.* Today. To the day. To the strange old calendrical honorific the guild had used and nobody else had spoken aloud in a hundred years.

And the ink was fresh.

Rosalind looked around the empty room. Dust on every surface, thick as grey fur, undisturbed except for one line of footprints — their own, just made, crossing from the door. No one else had been here. Nothing else had feet. The window was shuttered and had been, by the look of the cobwebs welded across it, since before Rosalind was born.

They looked back at the book. Below the date, in the same wet-black hand, more words were appearing — letters surfacing through the paper like fish rising in a pond, one stroke at a time, unhurried, deliberate:

*Beneath these hills walks the last of the wayfarers, unseen and unseeing.*

Rosalind stepped back. Their heel caught the fallen half of the lead seal and they stumbled, caught the lectern, steadied themselves, and stood there breathing hard in a room full of century-old dust, feeling suddenly, horribly *visible*.

Because here was the crack in things. Here was the flaw in the invisible life.

For thirty-one years, Rosalind had built their safety out of being no one. No records. No debts. No loves that lasted past a season, no enemies worth naming, no face that stayed in any mind longer than a doorway. The Wayfarers' Guild itself had faded from the kingdom's books until most people couldn't have told you whether it had ever existed — and Rosalind had told themselves that was grief's price and also grief's gift. You can't hunt what you can't remember.

But this book remembered. This book had waited a hundred years in the dark with the guild's name in its throat, and tonight — tonight, of all nights, while twelve dozen criminal candles cooled in their sacks and a suspiciously silent forest pressed against the walls — it had opened itself, broken its own seal, and begun to write about *them*.

Unseen and unseeing.

"That last bit's rude," Rosalind said. Their voice sounded thin in the stone room. "I see plenty. I see exactly as much as I choose to, which is a professional distinction, thank you."

The ink kept rising.

*The conductor comes. Let the Choir be gathered. Let the flame remember what the crown has eaten. On this night the count begins, and the count is eight—*

The letters stopped. Mid-word, mid-stroke. The fresh gloss faded from the ink like breath fading from a mirror, and the page went still, and the green-gold light dimmed down to nothing, leaving only the firelight from the hall and Rosalind's own shadow thrown long and wavering across the ash-grey pages.

Then, in the silence, from somewhere far away — beyond the vale, beyond the passes, from the direction of the capital, impossibly far for any sound to travel and yet arriving anyway, arriving *inside the bones* rather than the ears — came a single deep note. Like a bell struck underwater. Like a voice remembering a song it hadn't sung in a hundred years.

The mule screamed in the tack room.

Rosalind was moving before thought caught up — down the hall, through the door, hands on warm hide, murmuring the nonsense syllables that meant *I'm here, I'm here, nothing's eating you yet.* The mule pressed its head against their chest and trembled like a plucked string, and outside, the wind had changed direction entirely, flowing uphill toward the capital as though the whole night were being inhaled by something vast and patient and hungry.

Slowly, the trembling eased. The wind settled. The ordinary sounds of the world crept back — the dog, the pines, a late owl complaining about the weather.

Rosalind stood in the stable doorway a long moment, looking north, where the palace beacon atop Mount Cerule burned its eternal dragon-fire light — visible even here, a fleck of gold above the snowline, burning every night of Rosalind's life and every night of their parents' lives and their grandparents', the one constant in the sky, the flame that never guttered.

It flickered.

Just once. Just for a heartbeat, like a candle in a draft, like a thing deciding whether to keep going.

Rosalind's hand drifted, without permission, to the locket at their throat — the one thing they carried that wasn't cargo, the one thing with a face inside it — and closed around it hard enough to hurt.

"Right," they said quietly, to the mule, to the dark, to the book waiting down the hall with its unfinished sentence. "The count is eight. Eight what?"

Down the corridor, faint and impossible, the sound of a pen scratching began again.
