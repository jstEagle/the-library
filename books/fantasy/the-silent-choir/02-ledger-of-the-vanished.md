## 2. Ledger of the Vanished

The candle had burned to a stub before Rosalind Winterbourne worked up the courage to touch the book again.

They told themselves it was caution. Caution was a professional virtue; a courier who rushed got caught, and a courier who got caught got hanged, or worse, licensed. But if they were honest—and honesty was not a professional virtue of theirs at all—they would have admitted that what kept their hand hovering an inch above the Ash Codex was something closer to fear. The old-fashioned kind. The kind with cold feet and a dry mouth.

The Guildhouse ruin creaked around them in the way that only very old buildings can, a sound like a grandmother shifting in her chair, settling in for a long story. Rain found the holes in the roof and made small percussion on the flagstones. Their contraband candles—forty tallow sticks of unlicensed light, rolled into blankets and smuggled through three checkpoints in a handcart full of turnips—glowed in a ring around them like a tiny, illegal campfire.

"Right," Rosalind said aloud, because talking to oneself was another vice they'd never managed to quit. "You're a book. Books don't bite. I've been bitten by books exactly once, and that was a ledger with a bad-tempered clerk attached."

The Codex did not respond. It sat on the lectern where they'd found it, behind the collapsed shelf in the guildmaster's private study, looking exactly as a century-sealed book should look: black as a burnt log, its covers stitched with silver thread gone grey, its pages shut by a wax seal stamped with the Wayfarers' sigil—a road forked like a lightning bolt inside a circle.

Except the seal was broken. Broken tonight. And except the first page, which last night had been blank, now bore fresh ink.

Rosalind had checked twice. They were a careful person; carelessness was for people with names the kingdom knew. Last night, after finding today's date written in the Codex's margin in ink still wet enough to smear, they had fled up two flights of stairs, slept badly in the old dormitory with one eye open and a candle knife in their fist, and come back down at dawn to confirm they hadn't dreamed it.

They hadn't. The date was there. *Today.* In a book sealed since before their grandmother's grandmother could walk.

And below the date, new this morning, in the same unhurried hand: *Read.*

"Well," Rosalind said. "No."

They stood up. They paced. They ate half a turnip out of spite and habit both—the turnips had become their rations somewhere past the second checkpoint, and spite was a food group when you lived the way they lived. They inventoried the study for the fourth time: dust, dead moths, a chair with three legs, the collapsed shelf, the lectern, the book.

*Read.*

"You're very rude for a piece of stationery," Rosalind informed it, and sat back down, and broke every rule they'd ever set for themselves by reaching out and turning the page.

* * *

The first page of the Ash Codex was not prophecy at all.

It was a ledger.

Rosalind knew ledgers the way priests know psalms. Every guild kept them; every courier had copied entries until their wrist ached and their letters went crooked. This one began the way all guild ledgers begin—with a founding statement, the sort of pompous declaration masters liked read aloud to new apprentices so everyone understood what solemn business they'd joined.

But the words underneath were wrong. Wrong the way a familiar room is wrong when all the furniture has been moved two inches left.

*"Let it be recorded,"* the entry read, in a hand that slanted forward like someone walking briskly into wind, *"that the Wayfarers' Guild is founded this day not for the carrying of goods."*

Rosalind stopped. Read it again. Held the page close to the candlelight, checking for trickery—watermarks, hidden writing, some joke by a bored scribe a hundred years dead. Nothing. Just ink, brown-black and patient, and beneath it more ink:

*"Goods rot. Goods burn. Goods are taken at borders by men with stamps. We carry instead that which no stamp can seize and no fire wholly burns: memory. What a village was before the plague. What a mother's voice sounded like. What the king swore on his coronation day, and what he swore privately after, when the crown was heavy and the hall was empty. These we take into our keeping and carry where they must go, whole and unwitnessed."*

Rosalind sat very still.

Outside, the rain kept up its small applause. Somewhere in the walls, a mouse was doing whatever mice do at dawn—probably also reading forbidden books, knowing this building.

Because here was the thing. Here was the crack running straight down the middle of everything they'd believed about their own life.

Rosalind had been raised by the Guild the way a stone is raised by a river: gradually, without ceremony, and mostly by being pushed along. They'd arrived at Thornfell chapter-house at six years old with no family name anyone admitted to and a talent for going unnoticed that the guildmasters had called "a gift for the work." They'd learned routes and seals and the seventeen ways to say "nothing worth your attention, officer" with your posture alone. They'd learned that cargo was cargo: candles, letters, medicines, occasionally a fugitive wrapped in canvas and assured repeatedly that this was perfectly normal.

Nobody had ever mentioned memories. Nobody had ever said the word *choir*. The Wayfarers carried things. That was the whole of it. That had been the whole of it since—

Since when? Rosalind reached for the founding date, the little historical anchor every apprentice memorized, and found the anchor rope simply ending in water. They knew the Guild was old. They could not, for the life of them, remember how old, or who had founded it, or why. There was a hole there, smooth-edged and comfortable, like a socket for a tooth long since pulled. They'd never noticed it was a hole. Holes that comfortable you don't notice.

That frightened Rosalind considerably more than the wet ink had.

"Someone took it out," they said quietly. "Out of my head. Or out of everyone's heads. Or—" They stopped, because the third option was sitting right in front of them wearing a black cover: *or the Guild wrote its own history wrong on purpose, and I'm holding the correction.*

They turned the page.

* * *

The second page was worse, in the way that truth is always worse and always, irritatingly, more interesting.

It described the work. Not smuggling—though the ledger was frank that smuggling paid the bills, "as honest labor rarely does"—but the true trade. Couriers trained not just to move unseen but to *hold*: to take a memory given freely by its owner, keep it safe in the vaults of their own mind, and deliver it intact across distance or decades. There were exercises listed. Breathing patterns. A warning against carrying grief while hungry ("the stomach argues with sorrow, and sorrow wins"). A diagram of the hand-position used for giving a memory over, palms up, fingers loose, "as one receives rain."

And then, halfway down the page, underlined twice in ink darker than the rest:

*"For memory kept too long in one vessel goes strange. It must be sung back. This is the office of the Silent Choir: those couriers whose gift runs to return rather than carry. Where a courier bears a memory silently, a chorister gives it voice, and in the giving restores it to its proper owner—or, where the owner is beyond restoring, to the world, so that nothing loved is ever altogether lost. The Choir sings seldom and softly. Its members are recorded here and nowhere else."*

Below that, a list of names.

There had once been dozens. The early pages held columns of them—names in a dozen different hands, added year by year, some with small annotations in margins (*"has a laugh like a cart on gravel,"* someone had written beside one; *"do NOT let her harmonize after wine,"* beside another). Reading them, Rosalind felt something ache in their chest that had no ready name: the warmth of strangers' affection preserved like flowers in a book.

Then the columns thinned. The handwriting changed. Wars happened—you could tell by how suddenly the annotations stopped being funny. Names appeared with dates of death appended in a different, careful hand. By the page before last, only three names remained. By the last, one.

The final entry, in that same forward-slanting hand that had opened the book, read:

*"Ede Voss, guildmaster, sole remaining chorister, writes this record against the day the Choir is needed again. Let whoever finds this book understand: the thing in the palace wears our king's face, and it is hungry, and it has already eaten half of what we were. I go to Thornfell Palace to see what may be done. If I do not return, let the Codex stand sealed until the date is written upon it. The conductor will know the date. The conductor will be the last of us, and will not know that they are the last, and that is how it must be, for reasons I am not permitted to explain even here."*

The ink ended there. Below it, blank parchment, waiting.

Rosalind became aware, distantly, that they had stopped breathing somewhere around *hungry*, and started again somewhere around *conductor*, and that their hands were shaking in a manner entirely unbefitting a professional.

"Ede Voss," they said, testing the name. It fit oddly in the mouth, like a key cut for a lock slightly different from yours.

A century ago, a guildmaster had walked into the palace and never walked out. Everyone knew that story—it was one of the few stories about the Guild that *hadn't* been forgotten, probably because it came with such a satisfying moral about ambition and locked doors. Children in Thornfell still dared each other to knock on the palace gates and shout his name.

But children didn't say he'd been the last chorister. Children didn't say he'd known the king would be eaten. Children certainly didn't write books that opened themselves a hundred years later and said *read* in wet ink to a candle smuggler eating raw turnips in a ruin.

Rosalind looked at the blank space below Ede Voss's name.

It occurred to them—not as a thought, exactly, but as a cold finger drawn along the spine—that the ledger wanted a new entry. That the blank line was less a gap than an invitation. That *the conductor will be the last of us* was a sentence with a shape, and the shape was suspiciously person-sized, and the person it fit was currently sitting in an illegal ring of candlelight trying very hard to be nobody.

"No," Rosalind said again, firmly. It was becoming their signature word of the morning. "Absolutely not. I deliver candles. I am excellent at delivering candles. I have never once been late, I have never once been searched successfully, and I do not sing. I hum. Humming is not singing. Humming is what a kettle does."

The Codex offered no rebuttal. But the blank line seemed, in the candlelight, to deepen very slightly—as if the parchment had drawn a slow breath.

* * *

Rosalind spent the rest of the morning doing sensible things with great intensity.

They repacked the candles. They checked the handcart's false bottom, oiled its axle, re-hid the turnips over the contraband in an arrangement they were rather proud of, having spent an entire evening teaching themselves to stack root vegetables decoratively. They walked the perimeter of the Guildhouse and confirmed that nobody had followed them in last night and nobody was waiting outside now. The street beyond the broken gate was empty in the ordinary way: a fishmonger arguing with his scale, two children playing at soldiers with stick-swords and elaborate casualties, a city mender patching cobblestones with the serene incompetence of a man paid by the day.

Ordinary. Deliberately, aggressively ordinary.

Which was, of course, exactly the problem. Rosalind had built their whole life on the principle that ordinary things don't get noticed, and here was a book that had reached across a century specifically to notice them. You couldn't smuggle your way out of being *named*. There was no false bottom for a calling.

By midday they'd run out of sensible tasks and were forced to confront the fact that they'd read the same paragraph of the ledger eleven times without absorbing a word of it. They gave in, as they'd known they would since dawn, with the particular resentment of a person conceding an argument they'd already lost.

"Curiosity," they muttered, hauling the Codex off the lectern and wrapping it in a spare blanket. "That's all this is. Not destiny. Not prophecy. Curiosity, which is a flaw of character, and I'll thank the book not to flatter itself."

But their hands, wrapping the black cover, were gentle. Gentler than they'd meant them to be.

Because underneath the fear—underneath the cold feet and the dry mouth and the firm intention to put this cursed thing in a hole in the cellar and nail the hole shut—something else had kindled, small and stubborn, the way contraband candles kindle: a picture of the world the ledger described. Villages that remembered their dead properly. Mothers' voices carried whole across mountains. Nothing loved altogether lost.

Rosalind had spent their entire life being forgettable on purpose. It had never once occurred to them that forgetfulness might be the enemy. That someone, somewhere, might have declared war on forgetting—and lost—and left the war to them.

At the ruined gate, they paused. Behind them the Guildhouse creaked its grandmother's creak. Ahead lay Thornfell, the checkpoints, the turnips, the life.

In the blanket, faintly, the Codex was warm. Books aren't warm. Rosalind chose not to think about that, in the manner of a professional, and stepped out onto the road toward the palace district—because if a guildmaster had vanished into it a century ago, and if a king wore a crown that drank lamplight, then somebody owed Rosalind Winterbourne a few answers, and they intended to collect.

They did not yet know that the feast at the palace was tonight, or that a place had somehow been laid for a wine merchant named Master Fenwick who did not exist until an hour ago.

The book, presumably, knew. The book wasn't telling.
