## 3. The Crown at Dinner

The forgery papers had cost Rosalind three weeks' savings and a small moral compromise involving a customs clerk named Pibb who enjoyed being flattered. The wine was real, which helped. Nobody questions a merchant whose product makes them hiccup.

"You're nervous," said the candle crate, from its place among the barrels.

"I'm not nervous," Rosalind said. "I'm strategically quiet."

"Those are different things?"

"One of them has a plan."

The candles—forty tallow-and-honey stubs of excellent quality and entirely illegal provenance, because the Chandlers' Compact held the palace contract and the Compact's enforcers had opinions about competition that involved kneecaps—shifted slightly as the cart went over a rut. Rosalind patted the nearest barrel the way one pats a horse, or a co-conspirator. "Besides. We're not here for trouble. We're here to pour wine, take coin, and leave before anyone remembers we existed. That's the whole art of it. Invisibility is just good manners practiced consistently."

They had been practicing consistently for eleven years.

It was a strange kind of life, the one Rosalind Winterbourne had built. Most people dreamed of being seen—of walking into a room and having it brighten, of their name spoken with warmth by people who meant it. Rosalind had spent those years learning to be the opposite: the face nobody quite placed, the name that slid off memory like rain off oilcloth. *Rosalind* became *Ros*, then *that wine fellow*, then nothing at all within an hour of parting. It was not loneliness, exactly. Loneliness implied you wanted company. It was more like... tidiness. A life kept deliberately unremarkable, the way you keep a candle shielded from wind.

And yet last night, in the ruined Guildhouse where they'd sheltered from the rain, a book sealed for a century had opened itself to their hand, and tonight's date had been sitting there in fresh ink like a trap set specifically at ankle height.

So perhaps "strategically quiet" was doing some heavy lifting.

* * *

The feast was for the Harvest Vigil, which meant half of Thornfell's nobility crammed into the Long Hall wearing their second-best silks (the best having been eaten by moths or scandal), and the other half sulking about not being invited. Rosalind had been hired through a factor who had been hired through a cousin who owed someone money, which is how all legitimate business worked if you followed the thread far enough.

The hall was beautiful in the way that expensive things are beautiful when you don't look too closely at what paid for them. Three hundred candles. Roasted swan glazed until it gleamed like lacquered armor. A string quartet playing something wistful in a minor key, because Thornfell's composers had never met an emotion they couldn't make sadder.

Rosalind worked the room with two decanters and a professional smile. Pour for the duchess first—she tips. Avoid the man with the mustache—he talks. Compliment the steward's boots without looking at his feet, which requires practice but is achievable.

"The vintage," said a courtier near the high table, holding up a glass of the red Rosalind had just poured, "tastes like a summer that never happened."

"It's from the southern valleys," Rosalind offered.

"No, no—a *summer that never happened*. That's the joke." He beamed around the table. Several people laughed with the particular enthusiasm of people who have already laughed at this joke once tonight.

Rosalind smiled, poured, moved on. Merchant's rule: hear everything, remember only inventory.

An hour passed. The swan was carved. The quartet cycled back around to the wistful thing. And then the doors at the far end opened, and the herald called out words that Rosalind didn't fully process because every sound in the hall seemed to fall off a cliff at once:

"—his Majesty, King Aldous of Thornfell!"

*There he is,* thought Rosalind, with the strange double-vision of someone who has spent a week reading prophecy. *The king. Or the thing wearing him.*

Aldous came down the hall slowly, one hand resting on the arm of a chamberlain, and Rosalind studied him with the guiltless thoroughness of a tradesperson assessing a potential customer. He was not old. That was the first wrongness. By every account Aldous had reigned for—well, accounts disagreed, actually, which was its own quiet alarm; the histories gave his reign anywhere from thirty years to sixty, and nobody found this odd. But the man himself was somewhere past fifty, silver at the temples, upright enough, with a face built for coins and portraits: strong jaw, kind lines around the mouth, eyes that were—

Eyes that were nothing.

That was the phrase that arrived in Rosalind's mind uninvited and refused to leave. Not cruel eyes, not tired eyes, not even dead eyes. *Nothing* eyes. Like windows in a house where someone had arranged the furniture to be seen from outside, and you could admire the arrangement, and it looked lived-in, and there was nobody home.

He smiled. It was a magnificent smile. It reached exactly the muscles required and not one further.

"Your Majesty!" cried the mustached courtier, surging up. "We are honored! Tell us—how did Your Majesty find the harvest festival at Millbrook? They say the pumpkin contest caused a riot."

"A riot of joy, I hope," said the king, and the table laughed, and Aldous laughed too, warm and timed perfectly, and said: "Well. I hope it was a riot of joy."

And Rosalind, refilling a glass three places down, felt the temperature of their own blood drop several degrees.

Because that was it. That was *exactly* it. The same cadence. The same little pause before "riot." The same self-deprecating tilt of the head. Word for word, beat for beat, the joke the mustached man had made an hour ago—except now it was coming out of the king's mouth as though he'd invented it, delivered to a table that had already heard it and laughed anyway, because what are courts for, if not laughing at jokes twice?

*A memory,* Rosalind thought, pouring wine with a hand that did not shake, because merchants' hands never shake, that was the whole religion of it. *He's wearing someone else's evening like a borrowed coat.*

The Codex's page had been sparse—the first entry legible after a century of ash-dust, the rest still smudged and waiting—but one line had burned itself into Rosalind's memory the way lines do when they arrive at three in the morning:

*The crown does not rule. The crown consumes.*

Up close, at the distance of a decanter and a bow, Rosalind could see the crown itself. It sat on Aldous's head the way a leech sits on flesh—settled, comfortable, fed. It was old gold, unadorned except for seven pale stones, and here was the thing Rosalind would spend the rest of the night failing to explain away: the candles nearest the throne burned strangely. Their light bent toward the crown. Not reflected—*drawn*, the way water finds a drain. Where the lamplight touched those seven stones, it simply stopped, the way sound stops at the edge of a deep well.

The king raised his glass, and the whole hall rose with him, and for one moment three hundred faces turned toward the high table lit by three hundred flames that were all leaning, very slightly, toward one man's head.

"To the harvest," said Aldous. "To abundance. To—" a fractional pause, a flicker, like a lamp guttering— "to the harvest," he finished smoothly, "and to all of you, my dear, dear friends."

Nobody noticed the stumble. Nobody ever noticed anything in this kingdom; that was practically its founding principle. But Rosalind noticed, and felt something cold settle behind their sternum and begin arranging itself into conviction.

*It's real.* The villain of the thing. The eater. It wasn't metaphor, wasn't mad monks' scribble, wasn't the sort of story guildmasters tell apprentices to keep them off the roads at night. There was a thing wearing the king, and it was hungry, and it was eating him one borrowed evening at a time.

Rosalind poured the duke of Harrowmere's wine and did not spill a drop.

* * *

Feasts have a rhythm, and a good servant learns to move inside it like a fish inside current. Between courses there is a slack tide—nobody wants wine, everybody wants the privy or gossip—and Rosalind used the slack to drift closer to the high table under the impeccable pretext of clearing plates.

Up close, the wrongness got worse.

The king's skin was fine. His hands were clean. His rings fit. Everything about him was *maintained*, the way a ship in dry dock is maintained—painted, caulked, gleaming, and going nowhere. He turned to speak with the lady on his left, and Rosalind watched his face do something terrible: it waited. Just a half-second, a tiny lag between her question and his answer, like a puppeteer checking the script. Then the warmth arrived, right on cue, and he said, "Ah, Lady Vess, your son's recital—I heard it was *triumphant*," and Lady Vess glowed, because her son's recital had indeed been triumphant, and how lovely that the king remembered.

Except.

Except that Rosalind had heard Lady Vess tell three separate people about the recital tonight, and each telling had included the detail that she'd mentioned it *to the king earlier this evening*, and each telling had grown slightly more wounded that he hadn't seemed to recall it properly at the time. Now he recalled it perfectly. Now it was triumphant. As though he had spent the intervening hour quietly taking delivery of her evening, the way a warehouse takes delivery of goods.

The crown drank the light. The king drank the conversation. And underneath both transactions, so faint Rosalind might have imagined it, there was a hum—not sound, exactly, more a pressure behind the ears, like standing near a struck bell that hasn't quite stopped ringing.

Rosalind knew that hum.

Knew it the way you know a smell from childhood—suddenly, bodily, before thought. The Ash Codex had hummed like that. The moment their fingers had touched the seal and it had parted like warm wax, the ruined Guildhouse had filled with that same pressure, that same held note, as though a hundred years of silence had finally been given permission to end.

Which meant either the book and the crown were cousins—

—or the book and Rosalind were.

"More wine, sir?"

Rosalind startled. A footman stood beside them, young, bored, professionally blank. "You've been staring at His Majesty's shoulder for a full minute," the footman said, without judgment. "It's usually the crown people stare at. Less conspicuous."

"Occupational habit," Rosalind said. "Assessing the table's mood. Determines whether I lead with the red or the white."

"And?"

"Red," Rosalind said. "Definitely red. Everyone at that table is pretending to be happier than they are, and red forgives that."

The footman almost smiled. "You're the new factor's man. From up-country."

"That's me. Nobody important."

"Mm." The footman collected a plate. "Funny. Could've sworn I'd forget you by morning anyway. All you lot blur together."

*Ros,* thought Rosalind, watching the footman walk away with the plate. *That's the trick working. That's eleven years of careful nothing, doing its job.* And yet the reassurance landed strangely tonight, next to a sealed book that had written today's date in fresh ink, beside a crown that ate light. Invisibility had always felt like a choice. Tonight, for the first time, it felt like a hiding place someone else had prepared.

*Someone else had prepared.* The thought arrived and would not leave. The Guildhouse ruin, unlocked for them alone. The Codex, sealed against every thumb but theirs. Eleven years of being unremarkable, and it had never once occurred to Rosalind to ask *why it came so easily*.

* * *

The feast wound down the way feasts do: gradually, then all at once, in a rustle of silk and a chorus of performed reluctance. Rosalind stacked crates by the service door and counted the night's coin twice, and might have slipped out into the dark and put the whole thing behind them—might have, if the king hadn't stopped at the door.

Aldous paused on the threshold of the Long Hall, and the chamberlain leaned in to murmur the schedule, and the king nodded along, and then—just for a breath, just while the herald fumbled with the great doors—he looked out over the departing court with an expression no portrait would ever capture.

It was fear.

Not the king's fear. Something older and wearier, something buried so deep in the man's face that it surfaced only in the crack between one performance and the next, like groundwater seeping through stone. For that single breath, Aldous looked out at his glittering, oblivious court the way a drowning man looks at sunlight through water. His lips moved. No sound came out. But Rosalind, close enough now, close enough to count the pale stones in the crown, read the shape of it:

*Help.*

Or possibly *remember.* At this distance, with a century of dust on every word, the two looked nearly identical.

Then the doors opened, the torchlight of the corridor fell across his face, the performance closed over him like water, and King Aldous swept out of his own feast, radiant and hollow, trailing laughter he'd borrowed from better evenings.

Rosalind stood very still among the crates, holding a decanter of a summer that had definitely happened, and let the cold recognition finish its work. The Regent was real. It wore the crown. It ate evenings and recitals and kings, and it was patient, and it had been patient for longer than anyone could agree on.

Which meant the Codex was real too. Which meant the date was real. Which meant that somewhere in this city—or beyond it—there was an old woman named Maren Ostwick whom the guild rolls listed as a lockkeeper gone half-blind, and who was, according to a book that shouldn't exist, the last person alive who could tell Rosalind what they actually were.

Rosalind looked down at the decanter, then at the crates, then at the door through which the king had gone.

"Well," they told the candles, quietly, in the emptying hall. "I suppose we're going to see Maren."

The candles said nothing, but forty tallow-and-honey stubs of excellent quality seemed, somehow, to approve.

Outside, the autumn dark had come down over the capital like a lid, and somewhere above the rooftops—so faint it might have been wind, might have been imagination, might have been the pressure behind the ears again—that held bell-note lingered on, patient as hunger, waiting to be answered.
