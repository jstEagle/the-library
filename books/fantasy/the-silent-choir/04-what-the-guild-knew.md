## 4. What the Guild Knew

The lockhouse at Weir Nine sat where the Thornwater narrowed to a throat, all mossy stone and sagging roof, with a garden gone gloriously to seed and a row of wind chimes made from old keys that clinked like a conversation nobody had bothered to finish. Rosalind had walked the towpath since before dawn, and by the time the house came into view their boots were soaked through and their mood had curdled from determined to merely stubborn.

They had told themselves all night that they were only passing through. That the Codex could wait. That a date inked in a sealed book was probably a clerical accident committed by some long-dead clerk with a taste for mischief.

They had also packed an overnight bag before making this argument, which rather undermined it.

The gate was unlocked. The garden path was swept. Someone half-blind did not sweep a path like that; someone half-blind *remembered* sweeping it, and their feet remembered too.

"Maren Ostwick?" Rosalind called at the door. "It's Rosalind Winterbourne. From the Guild."

Silence. Then, from somewhere deep in the house, a voice like gravel poured slowly into a sack:

"Nobody's been 'from the Guild' in thirty years. Come in anyway. You'll drip on my floor either way."

* * *

Inside smelled of lamp oil, dried mint, and the particular mustiness of a house kept warm for one person. Maren Ostwick sat in a chair by the window with her hands folded in her lap like a woman waiting out a sermon. She was smaller than Rosalind remembered — guild elders were supposed to be enormous, permanent things, like hills — but her eyes, clouded as they were, still had the trick of looking directly at you, which was worse than seeing.

"You're wet," she said.

"It's raining."

"It's always raining. Sit. There's tea if you make it yourself, because I stopped trusting my hands near boiling water after the incident."

"What incident?"

"That's the point," said Maren, with evident satisfaction. "I don't remember. Now. Why does a candle-smuggler who signs her ledgers with a false name walk two days up the Thornwater to visit a blind old woman?"

Rosalind sat. "How do you know I sign with a false name?"

"Because I trained you, child, and because I'm blind, not stupid. Those are different afflictions." She turned her face toward the window, toward the river she couldn't see. "You found something."

It wasn't a question, so Rosalind didn't answer it. Instead they asked the question that had been sitting in their chest since the Guildhouse, heavy as a stone in a courier's pack.

"The Ash Codex. The seal on it was guildmaster's wax. Century old. And last night's date is written inside it. Fresh ink."

Maren was quiet long enough that the key-chimes outside filled the room with their small tin conversation.

"I wondered when it would open," she said finally. "Not *if*. When. Ede Voss was thorough about most things, but she was never thorough about time. Time does what it likes." She held out one hand, palm up, an old teacher's gesture. "Read me the first page. Word for word. My eyes gave up on small print years ago, and the Codex never had any other kind."

Rosalind hesitated. They hadn't brought the Codex — carrying it felt like carrying a lit lantern through a powder store — but they had copied the page out three times in three different hands, because that was what couriers did with dangerous cargo: they split it.

They unfolded the copy and read.

"'The Wayfarers' Guild was not founded to carry goods,'" Maren recited along with them, voice dropping into a rhythm worn smooth by decades of repetition, "'but to carry memory. What a kingdom forgets, a wayfarer keeps. What a kingdom loses, a wayfarer finds. In the last extremity, the scattered singers shall be gathered, and the Silent Choir shall sing back what was taken.'"

The words came out of Rosalind's mouth and landed strangely in the room, as though the house itself had heard them before.

When they finished, Maren said nothing for a while. Outside, a barge went past on the river, its engine chugging patiently, its crew laughing about something. The ordinary world, going about its business, unaware.

"That's real," Rosalind said. It came out smaller than they meant. "The Choir. It's not a story the guild told greenhorns to make hauling candles feel noble."

"Oh, it's real," Maren said. "And it's why there's no guild left to tell stories about it." She turned her clouded eyes on Rosalind. "You think we fell to hard roads and bad ledgers? We fell because we *sang*, once. One verse. Half a verse. A hundred years ago, when the crown began to smell wrong, a handful of our people tried to wake the memories sleeping under the palace. The Regent found every one of them. Not killed them — killed is quick, killed leaves graves and grief and grief remembers. No. It *ate* them. It ate them out of the world. Their names, their faces, the sound of their voices, the fact that anyone had ever loved them. By winter, their own mothers couldn't have picked them out of a crowd. By spring, the guild had forgotten why it existed. The ledgers just... stopped making sense. Entries about deliveries no one recalled making, to places no one recalled being."

Her hands tightened in her lap.

"The survivors did the only sensible thing. They scattered. They buried the songs. They made the Wayfarers small — smugglers, couriers, lockkeepers, people who carry boxes instead of memories. Invisibility as armor. You were raised in that armor, child. We all were. The last generation of us grew up learning how to be forgettable on purpose."

Rosalind thought of their childhood — of masters who never quite used their name, of records that misspelled it in inventive ways, of a life spent moving through crowds like water through a net. They had always assumed it was poverty. It had never once occurred to them it might be policy.

"You knew," they said. "All these years. You knew what the guild was for."

"I knew what the guild was *for* the way you know what a sword is for," Maren said. "Knowing doesn't mean you want to pick it up. I watched better singers than me be unmade. I decided I'd rather grow old tending locks than be a footnote in the Regent's dinner." She smiled, and it was a terrible, gentle smile. "But then, I wasn't named in the Codex, was I?"

The fire popped. Rosalind realized their hands were shaking and made them stop.

"How do you know about the naming?"

"Ede Voss wrote the whole book, girl. Every word. I copied it twice before my eyes went. And the conductor's line — the ones who lead the Choir — it runs in families. Families the guild hid so carefully that most of the hiders forgot why they were hiding anything." She leaned forward. "Winterbourne. Wintervourne, before someone shaved a syllable off to make you harder to find. You are standing at the end of a very long line of people whose entire job was to be forgotten, so that someday they could do the one thing in the world guaranteed to make the Regent look at them."

"That sounds less like an inheritance and more like a debt."

"It's both. Most inheritances are." Maren reached out and caught Rosalind's wrist with surprising strength, her blind gaze fixed somewhere just past their shoulder. "Now listen to me, because your eyes are young and mine are done, and I will say this plainly while I still can. The thing in that crown eats *memory*. Not flesh. Memory. And do you know what memory it feeds on best? Not the king's. Not the kingdom's. *Yours.*"

"What do you mean?"

"The memories other people keep of you. That's how it found the choristers. Not through their songs — through everyone who'd ever heard them. Sing, and the song travels. It lodges in ears. It makes people remember hearing you. And the Regent follows remembering like a shark follows blood." Her grip tightened. "The Regent eats what remembers you. Sing, and it finds you. Do you understand? It isn't hunting your voice. It's hunting everyone who carries you inside their heads, and it will eat them until there is no one left in the world who knows your face, and then, child, then you will be exactly what the guild spent a century training you to be — nothing at all, invisible forever, safe and hollow and *gone*."

The room seemed to tilt slightly. Rosalind thought of Tam Broadhurst's town, of the faces in the taproom, of every friend-of-convenience collected over twenty years of invisible trade. Thought of the mother whose face they could no longer fully picture, and felt a cold finger trace down their spine.

"There has to be another way," they said. "There's always a route around. That's the whole trade."

"There is no route around a thing that eats the map," said Maren. "Believe me. I've looked. I've looked for forty years, and all I've ever found is the same road, going the same direction, and it goes through Thornfell and down under the palace and it ends at the dragon." She released Rosalind's wrist and sat back, suddenly very tired. "Ede Voss walked that road a hundred years ago. Walked into the palace with the Codex under her arm and her choir scattered behind her, and she never came out. Whatever's wearing the crown now — whatever's been eating Aldous alive for years while his court applauds — it started as a person who took that same road believing she'd found a shortcut."

* * *

They sat by the fire while the rain thickened against the windows. Maren made tea after all — Rosalind boiled the water, Maren supervised with the fierce precision of a woman who trusted no one else's pouring — and for a while they talked about nothing. The state of the towpath. The scandalous price of lamp oil. Whether the new lock-warden at Weir Seven was competent or merely punctual, which Maren maintained were entirely unrelated qualities.

It was, Rosalind realized, the first conversation they'd had in years where they were not performing a version of themselves. No false name. No merchant patter. Just two people who belonged to the same vanished thing, keeping each other company against the dark.

"You could come with me," Rosalind said, when the tea was half gone. "When I go. To the capital. An extra pair of eyes— well. An extra pair of opinions."

Maren laughed, a dry rattle. "Oh, I'll come. Don't you fret about that. But not yet, and not because you asked." She set down her cup. "I have preparations. Letters to burn. Songs to dig up out of the garden where I buried them, literally, in a biscuit tin, because paper can be searched and my memory apparently cannot be trusted to hold anything longer than the price of lamp oil. You go on ahead. Find the others. The Codex will tell you where to look, or it won't, in which case follow your nose and distrust everything charming."

"That's most of the world."

"Yes. Isn't it a nuisance." She turned her face to the window again. "One more thing, and then I'll let you sleep, because you look like a scarecrow that lost an argument with weather. The Codex gave you a date. Tonight's date. Which means whatever Ede saw coming, it arrives *now* — not next season, not when you've finished practicing. The dragon under the palace has been singing alone for a hundred years, child, and even dragons run out of breath. When it stops, the anchor holding that thing in the crown stops with it. And then the Regent won't need to hunt anyone anymore. It'll simply take the whole table."

Outside, the key-chimes clinked. Somewhere downriver, thunder muttered, or perhaps it was the weir.

"So I ask nicely," Rosalind said, mostly to themselves. "I gather singers. I walk into the teeth of the kingdom. And if the Regent looks at me—" They stopped. "Maren. If it works the way you say — if singing means everyone who knows me gets eaten — then winning costs the same as losing."

Maren reached over and patted their hand, blind and certain, finding it on the first try.

"Now you understand the family business," she said warmly. "Welcome home."

* * *

Rosalind slept badly in the spare room, under a quilt that smelled of lavender and old decisions, and dreamed of a feast hall full of applauding courtiers who slowly, one by one, forgot they were clapping.

They woke before dawn to the sound of Maren in the garden, humming.

It was the first time Rosalind had ever heard her hum, and the tune was simple and old and terribly familiar — a lullaby-shape, a walking-song shape, something meant to be carried over long distances without wearing out. Rosalind lay in the dark and listened, and felt the strange thing happen that they'd first noticed with the smuggler's coin: the sense of a presence behind the melody, a morning, a person, a life folded into the notes like a letter into an envelope.

Then the humming stopped mid-phrase.

Rosalind was at the window in three strides.

Maren stood in the middle of her ruined garden with her face tilted up toward the grey sky, absolutely still, listening to something Rosalind could not hear. Far away — impossibly far, south and east, beyond the passes, beyond Thornfell — Rosalind felt it too, faint as pressure behind the ears. A vast attention, turning over in its sleep. Tasting the air.

Tasting the song.

"Get dressed," Maren said calmly, without turning around. "And bring me my stick. It seems, my dear, that the Regent has noticed the Codex opening — and it would very much like to know who read it."
