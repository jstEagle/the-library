## 5. First Verse

The coin had been in Rosalind's pocket for three days before it started singing.

They noticed it first as a warmth, the way you notice a stone that has sat too long near a fire — not alarming, just wrong in a small, deniable way. It was a smuggler's coin, the old Thornfell half-marks that predated the crown's mint, stamped with a mountain and no king. Rosalind had taken it from a dead man's coat in the ruins of a waystation on the Grayfell road, along with two candles nobody would miss, because a courier who didn't loot was a courier who starved.

On the third night, sheltering under the leaning eave of a shepherd's hut with rain coming down like the sky was settling an old grudge, Rosalind took the coin out to count their money for the fourth time, hoping the arithmetic would improve.

It didn't. But when they held it up to the last of the candlelight, they heard it.

Not with the ears. That was the strange part. The sound came in behind the ears, the way a name comes back to you, or the smell of bread baking in a house you haven't entered yet. A hum. Low, patient, threaded through with something almost like words.

Rosalind sat very still.

The hut was theirs alone; the shepherd had gone to market and left the door unlatched in the trusting way of people who lived far from anything worth stealing. Rain drummed the turf roof. And the coin sang.

It took Rosalind a while — huddled there with the candle guttering — to understand what they were hearing. It wasn't music exactly, or if it was, it was the kind of music a life makes: rhythms of walking, of lifting, of laughing at something once and never quite the same again. And underneath the rhythm, images that weren't images, arriving whole:

A grey morning. Salt wind. A woman saying *you'll be careful*, not as a question, because she knew better. Boots on wet cobbles. The specific weight of this coin in a palm, warm from being carried, being counted, being loved a little, the way you love a thing that stands between you and hunger. Then a road. Then men shouting. Then —

The hum stopped where the man stopped. It didn't end so much as hold its breath, forever.

Rosalind set the coin down on the floorboards as carefully as if it were a sleeping animal, then picked it up again immediately, because setting it down felt like a betrayal.

"Well," they said aloud, to the empty hut, because saying things aloud was how they kept themselves company on long roads. "That's either a gift or a curse or I've finally eaten something I shouldn't have."

The candle flame leaned in the draft. Outside, the rain kept on. Rosalind wrapped the coin in a scrap of cloth, put it away, and lay awake until well past midnight listening to the rain and thinking about Maren Ostwick's blind eyes turning toward them in the dark of the lockhouse, and her voice, dry as autumn reeds: *The Regent eats what remembers you.*

*Sing,* she'd said, *and it finds you.*

Rosalind hadn't sung. They'd only listened.

Surely that was different.

Surely.

* * *

By morning, curiosity had done what fear couldn't and pried Rosalind loose from caution entirely.

This was, they reflected later, a family trait. Their mother had been the same — the kind of woman who would open a locked box found in a hedge rather than walk past it, who said *what's the worst that could happen* in a tone that suggested she genuinely wanted an answer. Rosalind had inherited her hands, her stubbornness, and apparently whatever this was: the thing that made a smuggler's coin sound like a church bell.

They spent the day testing it, the way you test a loose tooth — gently at first, then with increasing commitment to the pain.

An old key from the hut's door: nothing, or nothing much, a faint dull note like a word spoken too many times. The shepherd's own things were absent, but his grandfather's crook still hung by the door, and that one hummed — summers, mostly. Hill grass. A dog whose name was lost but whose enthusiasm survived intact. Rosalind stood holding a shepherd's crook in an empty hut, crying a little without entirely understanding why, and decided that was enough field research for the moment.

But on the road east toward Thornfell, they passed a shrine — one of the little roadside ones, a stone niche with a chipped figure of some saint whose name had weathered off, and a shelf of offerings left by travelers. Most were recent: fresh bread going stale, a child's carved horse, buttons. But at the back, half-hidden, was a wedding ring worn thin as wire.

Rosalind touched it and the world went white-gold.

Fifty years compressed into a breath: a girl in a borrowed dress, a boy who couldn't stop grinning, rain on a festival day so that everyone crowded under one awning and that was the whole courtship right there. Children, arguments, forgiveness, the slow erosion and the slow rebuilding, hands growing old together until the ring hung loose on a shrunken finger. And at the very end — the end was always at the very end — someone else's hand closing over the ring, lifting it off with love and grief braided so tightly together you couldn't tell which strand was which, and carrying it here, to leave it where travelers passed, so that somebody, anybody, would know those two had happened.

Rosalind came back to themselves sitting in the mud beside the shrine, cheeks wet, heart hammering.

"Right," they said. "That's definitely not candles."

Because that was the thing. Rosalind Winterbourne had spent thirty-odd years cultivating invisibility into an art form. They could walk through a marketplace and be forgotten by the time they reached the far gate. They could sit at a tavern table all evening and leave without the keeper remembering whether they'd paid. It was a discipline, a craft, a survival strategy learned young and practiced daily — and now, apparently, they could pick up a stranger's ring and live fifty years of someone else's marriage in a single heartbeat.

Maren had known. Maren, with her clouded eyes and her lockkeeper's patience, had looked at Rosalind across her cluttered table and told them exactly what they were without ever quite saying the word. *The Wayfarers weren't founded to carry goods, child.* And Rosalind had walked out of there agreeing to think about it, which was courier-speak for *I will avoid thinking about it with tremendous energy.*

Well. Avoidance had been lovely while it lasted.

* * *

It was three days later, in a room above the Drowned Ox inn at Millbrook, that Rosalind discovered the other half of it — the half Maren had actually warned them about.

The Drowned Ox was a good honest inn with bad honest wine, and Rosalind had a room to themselves and a small hoard of old objects acquired in the manner couriers acquire things. They laid them out on the bed like a merchant's samples: the smuggler's coin, a brass button, a broken pipe stem, a ribbon gone grey. Five little relics, five little songs.

They hummed back.

It wasn't planned. That was the truth they clung to afterward — it wasn't planned, it wasn't even really a decision. The coin's song caught in their chest the way a tune catches, and their throat answered before their mind could lodge an objection. A low note, matching the coin's pitch, and then another, finding the shape of the dead smuggler's grey morning, his mother's voice, his boots on the cobbles.

And the song *opened*. That was the only word for it. Where before it had been a sealed jar of memory, humming faintly through the glass, now the lid came off. The grey morning poured out. Rosalind heard the salt wind properly, felt the wet cobblestones, saw the harbor light through the man's twenty-year-old eyes — and the memory, instead of fading as memories did, grew *brighter*. Sharper. As if Rosalind's voice were oiling a hinge that rust had seized, and everything the hinge connected to swung wide.

For one shining minute, Rosalind Winterbourne stood in a room above the Drowned Ox and also stood on a dock forty years gone, alive in a dead man's morning, and it was the most extraordinary thing that had ever happened to them.

Then the temperature of the world changed.

There was no thunder. There was no dramatic sign, no cold wind, no candle snuffing. What there was, was a *pressure* — distant and enormous, the way you feel a storm before it crests the mountains, a change in your ears, a heaviness behind the eyes. Rosalind stopped humming mid-note.

The pressure paused.

It felt — Rosalind would struggle for years to describe this accurately — it felt like attention. Vast, patient, and hungry in the way winter is hungry, which is to say without malice and without mercy both. Something far away had turned its face toward the mountains, toward the small bright noise of a song that should not exist, and was considering it.

Then the pressure slid past, like weather moving on to trouble some other valley, and the ordinary sounds of the Drowned Ox — clinking mugs, a drunk singing badly two floors down, rain starting up against the window — rushed back in.

Rosalind discovered they were standing pressed flat against the wall, as if stone might hide them, holding the coin in a fist gone white.

They stayed that way for a long time.

When they finally moved, it was to blow out the candle, sit on the floor in the dark with their back against the bed, and conduct a calm and reasonable conversation with themselves, which went roughly as follows:

*You hummed at a coin and something the size of a kingdom noticed.*

*I hummed at a coin and something the size of a kingdom noticed.*

*Maren said sing and it finds you. You didn't sing. You hummed. Apparently the difference is negligible.*

*Apparently.*

*So don't do it again.*

*No. Absolutely not. Never again.*

Rosalind looked down at the coin in their hand. Inside the cloth wrapping, faint as breath, it was still singing — the grey morning, the salt wind, the woman saying *you'll be careful*. Forty years dead and still going, patiently, faithfully, waiting for someone to listen.

"Damn it," Rosalind said quietly.

Because here was the problem, the flaw in the plan, the crack straight down the middle of any resolution to never do it again: those songs were all that was left of people. Not their bones — bones were everywhere, bones kept poorly. The songs. The grey mornings and the weddings and the dog whose name was lost but whose joy remained. And the thing wearing King Aldous's crown ate memories the way rot eats timber, silently, structurally, until the whole edifice of a life collapsed into nothing anyone could recall. Every song Rosalind ignored was a song closer to silence forever.

They were, they realized with a sinking feeling that was equal parts dread and recognition, the kind of person who was going to keep doing this. Their mother had opened the box in the hedge. Rosalind hummed at coins. It ran in the family.

"But carefully," they told the ceiling. "From now on, carefully. Quietly. No more — whatever that was."

The ceiling offered no objection.

Rosalind slept badly, dreamed of storms crossing mountains, and woke with the coin's song lodged behind their ears like a splinter they were beginning, horribly, to treasure.

* * *

Over the following week, Rosalind developed rules. Rules made it feel like method rather than compulsion, and Rosalind was nothing if not devoted to the fiction of their own self-control.

Rule one: never sing indoors with the shutters closed. Sound needed somewhere to go, and a small room concentrated it like a lens. (This rule was based on no evidence whatsoever, but it made Rosalind feel better.)

Rule two: never hum louder than a whisper, and never longer than ten breaths. Ten breaths was enough to open a song's lid without — presumably — ringing dinner bells for the thing in the capital.

Rule three: never, under any circumstances, sing a song that belonged to anyone the Regent might still be looking for. This rule was the hardest, because it meant skipping songs that were bright and recent and raw, and lingering instead among the old ones, the faded ones, the smugglers and grandmothers and long-dead shepherds whose mornings had already been half-forgotten by everyone but the objects they'd touched.

Rosalind worked east along the valley road, taking a legitimate delivery of candle-wax to Millbrook's chandler, then a parcel of letters to a farmstead upriver, keeping their cover moving the way a good courier did. And each evening, in hedgerows and field-barns and once memorably in the lee of a cairn while sleet tried to peel their face off, they listened.

A thimble sang of a seamstress who'd sewn her daughter's wedding dress by candlelight and pricked her thumb at the exact moment the girl said *yes, Mama, he's kind.* A horseshoe sang of a blacksmith's apprentice and the day he finally got the curve right and his master — gruff as a gravel bed — had simply nodded, and how that nod had kept the boy fed with pride for a year. A clay marble sang of a summer, one whole endless summer, seen from knee-height, when the world smelled of cut grass and someone's mother called them in for supper and the child had run home through the dusk knowing — knowing with absolute certainty — that tomorrow would come and be the same, and that this was happiness.

Rosalind wept at the marble for a full quarter hour and then laughed at themselves for doing it, and then hummed it back anyway, softly, ten breaths, letting the summer open like a flower pressed in a book for sixty years.

It was, they admitted on the ninth night, lying in hay with their boots off and their ribs aching from laughter at nothing, the best week of their invisible life.

Which was, of course, exactly when it went wrong.

* * *

The trouble began with a fiddle.

Rosalind had stopped at the Ferryman's Rest, a river inn where the ferryman doubled as landlord and the landlord doubled as a gossip, which made him useful and dangerous in equal measure. Rosalind delivered letters, drank one pint slowly, and was preparing to leave when a carter produced a battered fiddle and struck up a jig, and the taproom filled with the ordinary beautiful racket of people enjoying an evening.

And the fiddle — the fiddle *sang*.

Not the jig. Under the jig. The instrument itself, old and much-mended, poured out its history the way a cracked jug pours: every dance, every wedding, every wake, the hands that had held it, the night it had been played in a barn while snow sealed the doors and the dancing got faster to keep the cold out. Sixty years of a valley's Fridays, all of it rising off the strings like heat shimmer.

Rosalind gripped their mug and breathed through it. Fine. Listening was fine. Listening was passive. Listening was —

The carter finished the jig to applause, grinned, wiped his bow, and said, "Anyone care for a turn? My grandfather played this at the founding feast of Millford bridge, they say. Be a shame to let it sit quiet."

And every eye in the taproom, in that casual swiveling way of taprooms, passed across Rosalind — the stranger in the corner, the nondescript traveler, the person nobody would remember — and the carter's gaze snagged there, friendly and expectant, and Rosalind heard themselves say:

"I couldn't play it."

"No," the carter agreed cheerfully, "but could you sing? Fiddle likes a voice under it. Grandfather always said so."

The taproom waited, pleasantly, dangerously. And Rosalind — tired, warm, off guard, and possessed by the specific madness of a person who has spent nine days learning that songs want answering — heard the fiddle's sixty years of Fridays rise in their throat, found the low note underneath the jig, the note the fiddle had been humming to itself all evening, and gave it back.

Softly. Two bars. Hardly anything. A hum, really, barely above breath.

But the room changed.

It was subtle — nobody spilled their drink or cried out — but every conversation dipped for a half-second, the way birdsong stops when a hawk crosses the sun. The carter's grin faltered. An old woman by the fire put her hand flat on the table as if steadying herself. Somebody's grandmother, dead these twenty years, was suddenly almost present in the room, in the smell of the rushes and the taste of small beer, and forty people felt her pass through them like a draft and didn't know what they'd felt.

And Rosalind felt the pressure arrive.

Not gradually, this time. Not like weather on the horizon. Like a hand laid flat against the back of the neck. The mountains between here and the capital — Rosalind felt them the way you feel a wall in the dark, a vast blank weight of stone and distance, and beyond the stone, something turned. Considered. *Listened.*

Ten breaths. Rosalind counted them the way a drowning person counts waves, and on the tenth they closed their mouth, and the note died in the taproom air, and the pressure hung there a moment longer —

— and then, distantly, with what Rosalind could swear was something like disappointment, it withdrew. Southward. Toward the crown.

Rosalind put down coins for the pint, thanked the carter in a voice they were proud of for staying level, and walked out into the night at exactly the pace of a person with nowhere urgent to be, which was the hardest walk of their career.

They made six miles by moonlight before letting themselves shake.

* * *

The barn they sheltered in was empty except for hay and a broken cart, and Rosalind sat in the dark doing the arithmetic they'd been avoiding since the Drowned Ox.

One hum in a lonely hut: a storm on the horizon. One hum in a crowded taproom: a hand on the neck. The pattern was plain, and it pointed somewhere ugly. The Regent wasn't searching the mountains. It was *listening* for them — and every verse Rosalind gave back made them easier to hear. Maren had said it plainly enough: *sing, and it finds you.* The gift and the exposure were the same thing, two ends of one thread, and Rosalind had spent nine wonderful days pulling it hand over hand.

They should stop. Any sensible courier would stop. Deliver the candles, deliver the letters, fold this whole impossible inheritance shut like a book, and go back to being no one, safely, permanently.

Rosalind reached into their pack and took out the coin.

In the dark, without touching it, they could hear it — the grey morning, the salt wind, the woman's voice. Waiting. All of them waiting: the thimble, the marble, the horseshoe, the fiddle, the ring in the roadside shrine. A whole country of small patient songs, humming in drawers and pockets and shrines, holding the kingdom's true memory the way roots hold a hillside — unnoticed, essential, and losing ground year by year to the quiet rot in the palace above.

"You're going to get me killed," Rosalind told the coin.

The coin sang on, faithful as ever, about a morning forty years gone.

Rosalind sighed, settled deeper into the hay, and began — quietly, ten breaths, rule two — to hum.

They were three verses in when they heard, far down the valley road, the unmistakable sound of a horse moving at a steady canter through the night. Not hurrying. Not wandering. Coming on with the unhurried certainty of something that knows exactly where it is going.

Rosalind put the coin away, doused their candle, and watched through the gap in the barn boards as a rider drew level with the gate below — a slim figure in travelling grey, face turned upward toward the barn, toward the dark, toward the last fading ghost of a dead smuggler's morning still hanging in the air like smoke.

The rider's face, even in moonlight, was oddly hard to look at. Rosalind found their eyes sliding off it, unable to say afterward what the man had looked like at all — only that he was smiling.

"Sweet," the rider said, to no one visible, in a voice like a well-lit room. "I do love a singer."
