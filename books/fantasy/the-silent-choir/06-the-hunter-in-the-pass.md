## 6. The Hunter in the Pass

The trouble with being invisible, Rosalind had learned over thirty-odd years of practice, was that you had to keep doing it on purpose. Invisibility was not a cloak you owned; it was a fire you fed, every single day, with small deliberate acts of nothing. You did not linger at the baker's stall. You did not learn the guard's name even when he told it to you twice. You tipped exactly the average amount and smiled exactly the average smile and let your eyes slide off people the way rain slides off oiled canvas.

Rosalind was very good at feeding that fire.

Which is why it was so deeply annoying, three days out of Thornfell's low quarter and halfway up the Kestrel Pass, to realize someone had been watching them anyway.

It started, as most bad things do, with something small. Rosalind had stopped at a wayshrine — one of the old ones, pre-crown, the kind with the worn hollow where pilgrims used to press their palms — to eat a heel of bread and argue with a marmot over a second heel of bread. The marmot was winning. It always won; this was a long-running dispute between them, conducted across several summers, and Rosalind had come to respect its tactical brilliance.

And then the birds went quiet.

Not startled-quiet. Startled-quiet has a shape to it: a flutter, a scold, a settling. This was the other kind. The held-breath kind. The kind that happens when something walks through a wood that the wood does not recognize.

Rosalind put down the bread. The marmot, showing more sense than most kings, vanished under its rock.

Down the switchback trail, perhaps a half mile below, a man was climbing. He walked like a soldier who had been taught to walk like a farmer, which is to say he got it almost right and missed in ways only another trained walker would notice: weight too centered, arms too still, boots placed rather than fallen. His face —

Rosalind looked at his face and felt their attention slide off it like water off the oiled canvas.

That was the thing. That was the thing that turned annoyance into a cold, careful knot under the ribs. Rosalind looked directly at him — sun on him, close enough to count buttons if there had been buttons worth counting — and could not afterward have said whether he was tall, or young, or bearded, or scarred. There had been a face. There had been a man-shaped arrangement of features above a grey traveling coat. And then it was gone from the mind like a word on the tip of the tongue, except the tongue never found it.

*Forgettable,* thought Rosalind. *Deliberately forgettable.*

They knew what that meant, now. Three days ago they had known almost nothing about the Silent Choir, and now they knew slightly more than almost nothing, which in Rosalind's experience was precisely enough knowledge to get you killed.

Maren Ostwick's voice, dry as autumn leaves, came back to them across the miles: *Sing, child, and it finds you.*

Rosalind had hummed along to a smuggler's coin. Once. For perhaps four seconds. On a mountainside forty miles away.

Apparently four seconds was plenty.

* * *

They did not run. Running was for people who had already been seen, and besides, running uphill was undignified and Rosalind had standards, most of them low but fiercely defended. Instead they finished the bread — the marmot re-emerged and took its victory portion, because even an apocalypse should not interrupt established tradition — and considered.

The pass narrowed ahead into the Kestrel Stair, a mile of switchbacks cut into the cliff face, no cover, no side trails, one way up and the same way down. Below it, the trail wandered through pine and boulder for another six miles before the valley opened toward the mining settlements. If the forgettable man reached the Stair first and simply waited, Rosalind would walk into his hands like a parcel.

So the question was not *can I outrun him.* The question was *what does he actually know?*

He knew there was an unlicensed singer — that phrase had a bureaucratic ring to it, the sort of phrase that gets written in reports, which meant there were reports, which meant there was an office, which meant the crown cared. But reports are written by watchers, and watchers see shapes, not souls. What did the report say? *Traveler of indeterminate trade, medium height, unremarkable face, hummed at a coin.*

Unremarkable face. Well. Two could play at that game. Rosalind had spent a lifetime being nobody; it was almost flattering that the crown had finally sent a professional.

Almost.

They broke camp early and did not take the trail. There was a drover's path — steep, ugly, goat-approved — that cut above the pines, and Rosalind took it, moving fast in the grey before dawn, and by full light they had put two ridges between themselves and the Kestrel Stair and allowed themselves the luxury of believing it might be enough.

It was on the third ridge, pausing to check their backtrail, that they saw him again.

Same coat. Same almost-farmer's walk. Same face that refused to stay faced. Except now he was *above* them — a ridge over, moving parallel, unhurried, a man strolling through his own garden. And here was the part that made Rosalind's mouth go dry: he wasn't following tracks. There were no tracks to follow on bare stone. He wasn't following sight, because Rosalind had been careful, so careful, hood up, silhouette broken.

He was following the song.

Rosalind remembered it then, with horrible clarity: the coin in their palm, the smuggler's last morning humming up through the metal — bacon smoke and a woman laughing and the particular gold color of a morning that the smuggler had evidently treasured right up until it killed him. Rosalind had hummed back. Four seconds of harmony. A kindness, really. A little *I heard you* offered to a dead man.

And apparently that kindness had lit them up like a beacon in a world where something enormous and hungry kept a very good map.

* * *

By evening of the second day, Rosalind had learned three things about the forgettable man.

First: he did not sleep. Or if he slept, he did it standing, in some hollow of himself, because twice Rosalind circled wide in the dark and found him exactly where he'd been at dusk, facing exactly the direction Rosalind had gone.

Second: he was patient in a way that felt less like discipline and more like absence. He didn't hurry because hurrying was a thing wanting things. Whatever lived behind that sliding-off-the-mind face wanted exactly one thing, and it was willing to walk forever to get it.

Third — and this was discovered when Rosalind, in desperation, doubled back within a bowshot and threw a stone to distract him — the man turned toward the stone, assessed it, and dismissed it in under a heartbeat. He wasn't tracking movement. He wasn't tracking sound.

He was tracking *memory.* Specifically, presumably, the memory-song clinging to Rosalind like smoke to wool. Which meant distance didn't matter. Which means hiding didn't matter. Which meant —

Which meant Rosalind sat down hard on a cold rock in the gathering dark and admitted, privately, to the marmot-less silence, that they were in genuine trouble.

"Four seconds," they said aloud, to no one. "Four seconds of humming and the whole apparatus of the crown comes clanking after me." A pause. "I've smuggled *actual candles* past *actual wardens* with less fuss."

The silence declined to sympathize.

They built no fire. They ate cold beans and thought about Maren, and about the Codex with tonight's date inked fresh in a century-sealed book, and about the word *conductor*, and about how all of this had seemed, three days ago, like a problem for Future Rosalind, whom Present Rosalind had always regarded as a somewhat gullible acquaintance.

Future Rosalind, it turned out, was now. And Future Rosalind was being hunted through the high passes by a man whose face wouldn't stick to the mind, because Past Rosalind had been sentimental about a dead smuggler's breakfast.

There was a lesson in there. Rosalind chose not to look at it directly.

* * *

On the third night, cornered above the tree line with the pass walls rising sheer on either side, Rosalind took inventory.

Options: fight. Rosalind carried a knife and a cudgel and the confidence of someone who had once fought off two bandits, one of whom was elderly. Against a crown hunter? No.

Options: run. The pass ended at the Stair tomorrow. Beyond it, open country — but open country he could cross as easily as they, and he did not need rest, and Rosalind very much did.

Options: stop singing. Impossible. The songs weren't a choice anymore; since the Codex, they rose in everything — the pass stones themselves hummed faintly with the feet of ten thousand drovers, and Rosalind heard it whether they wished to or not. Muffling it was like muffling a heartbeat.

Which left the fourth option. The one they'd been circling since the second ridge, the way you circle a wound.

The locket.

It hung around their neck under their shirt, warm from their skin: cheap tin, dented, holding two portraits painted miniature, the kind itinerant limners sold at fairs for coppers. One was a landscape — the marshes where their mother had grown up, she'd said, though Rosalind had never verified it. The other was a face.

Their mother's face.

Rosalind did not remember their mother. This was a fact they had made peace with so long ago the peace had gone smooth as a river stone. She had died when they were four — fever year, the lockkeepers said, though no one living had been there — and whatever memories a four-year-old holds, Rosalind's had gone the way of childhood things, worn away by roads and years until only the locket remained, and the certainty that the painted woman had laughed often, because the portraitist had caught her mid-smile, and you cannot fake that particular crinkle at a sitting.

She had raised them for four years. She had named them. She had, presumably, held them, fed them, sung to them badly. And all Rosalind had ever had of her was a smear of paint the size of a thumbnail, smiling out of a dented tin box.

And now the idea arrived, the way the worst ideas do, fully formed and reasonable-sounding:

*A song needs somewhere to live.*

Serle — Rosalind had taken to calling him that in their head, purely for convenience, though where the name came from they couldn't have said; it felt like a name that fit a forgettable man — Serle tracked memory-song. The coin's song had drawn him across forty miles. So give him a louder song. Give him a song so bright and specific and *freshly woken* that he'd chase it while the singer slipped away in the opposite direction.

Objects could hold memory. Rosalind had proven that. And a locket worn against the skin for thirty years, holding a beloved face, loved and touched and carried through every season of a life —

A locket like that wasn't an object. It was a vault.

You could wake it. Rosalind was fairly sure you could wake it — set the memory loose as sound, let it pour out into the night air like light from a cracked lantern, a beacon singing *here, here, here* in a dead woman's voice.

But a memory poured out is a memory spent. That was the law of it, the law Maren had hinted and the Codex implied: songs woke memories, but waking was spending. The coin's smuggler was gone either way; his morning had survived only because the coin kept it. Wake the locket fully, and the morning inside it — her face, her smile, the one thing in the world that was hers — would burn off like fog in sun.

And Rosalind would be carrying an empty tin.

"No," they said aloud, firmly, the way you say *no* to a dog eyeing the table.

The idea sat there, wagging its tail.

"It's all I have of her."

The idea pointed out, gently, that it was also all a hunter needed of *them.*

"I'll find another way."

The idea observed that Rosalind had been saying that since the second ridge, and meanwhile the forgettable man had gained half a mile.

Rosalind sat with it a long time. The stars came out, hard and clear at altitude, and somewhere below, patient as winter, the man who did not sleep made his campless camp.

Here is the arithmetic Rosalind finally arrived at, alone in the dark, and it is not a comfortable piece of arithmetic: a mother who died twenty-six years ago, leaving a painted smile, versus a living person with work undone — a Codex, a Choir, a king being eaten alive in his own palace, and a prophecy with Rosalind's date inked in it. One grief weighed against many lives.

Every courier knows that sum. It's the whole job, boiled down: what do you carry, what do you drop, and can you live with the dropping.

Rosalind had dropped a lot of things over the years. Contraband, once, into a river, with wardens closing in. A partner's letter, once, unreadable with blood, delivered anyway. Pride, frequently.

They had never dropped her.

"I'm sorry," Rosalind said. It came out rough. "I'm sorry. I need to live, and you'd — " Their voice caught and they hated it and pushed through. "You'd tell me to run. You'd be furious if I got caught over a picture. You spent four years keeping me alive; I'm not going to waste it being sentimental."

The locket lay in their palm, dented tin catching starlight, and said nothing, the way the dead are polite about these things.

Rosalind unclasped it. Held it up. And sang.

* * *

It is one thing to know a memory will pour out of an object. It is another thing entirely to stand inside the pouring.

Her voice filled the pass.

Not words — Rosalind hadn't expected words, and got none — but *her*, unmistakably, the way a hearth is unmistakable: warmth and flour and a tune hummed off-key, a lullaby assembled from three other lullabies because she'd never learned one properly, laughter caught mid-note, the smell of woodsmoke and wet wool, hands — *hands*, Rosalind hadn't known about the hands, cool and certain on a hot forehead, a four-year-old's fever breaking under those hands — and underneath it all, steady as a drumbeat, love. Not the word. The fact. The plain, enormous, unadorned fact of it, sung in the only voice Rosalind had ever belonged to.

They knelt in the scree and wept like a child with a fever, and did not stop singing, because stopping would ration it, and rationing it would make it worse, and anyway the song had a job to do.

The locket grew hot. Then it glowed — a soft gold, the exact gold, Rosalind realized with a sob-laugh, of the morning the smuggler's coin had held, the universal color of treasured mornings. The song climbed the pass walls and rolled out over the valleys like a bell tolling, and it carried *her name* — it carried her name, and Rosalind heard it for the first time in twenty-six years, *Elowen, my name is Elowen, remember me, sweetheart, somebody remember me* — and Rosalind memorized it, gripped it, drove it into themselves like a peg into stone, *Elowen*, because if the song must burn then the name at least would survive in the one place the hunter couldn't reach.

Then the tin went dark and cold and silent in their hand, and the pass was just a pass again, and Rosalind knelt alone in the dark holding an empty box.

They did not look at it for a while.

When they finally did, they closed it carefully, respectfully, the way you close a door on a sleeping room, and put it back around their neck. Empty. Warm still, briefly, from the burning. Rosalind decided they would carry it anyway. Some containers deserve to keep their post.

* * *

The song rang across the high country for most of an hour, fading by degrees, and Rosalind used every minute of it.

They moved south and down, away from the Stair, along a ledge route they'd noted days ago and hoped they'd never need — narrow, exposed, stupid with loose rock, the kind of path goats write home about disapprovingly. Twice they nearly went down. Both times something steadied them — a root, a handhold, a stubbornness — and Rosalind chose to believe it was Elowen's last gift rather than luck, because belief was free and luck was insulting.

Behind and above, they permitted themselves one glance.

He was there, on the far ridge, a man-shape against the stars, and he was *running*. Actually running, coat flying, the almost-farmer's walk abandoned — because the song had done what songs do to people who have forgotten what they're for. Even a hollow man, it seemed, would chase a mother's lullaby across a mountain. Rosalind found that unbearably sad and filed it away with everything else they'd deal with later.

By dawn the song was gone, the last of it spent, and the pass behind them held nothing but ordinary silence. Rosalind dropped into the pinewoods, crossed a stream twice to spoil scent, and walked until the sun stood high, and then walked more, because grief is heavy and walking is how couriers put it down.

Somewhere past noon, in a fold of the hills where a drover's shelter leaned against the slope, Rosalind finally stopped, sat, and took stock.

Alive: yes. Hunted: yes, but aimed at a ghost now. Locket: empty. Mother's name: *Elowen*, held, safe, theirs.

Cost: total.

And yet — and this was the strange thing, the thing Rosalind turned over like a foreign coin — they did not feel emptied. They felt *lit*. As if the burning had done what fires do, which is show you the shape of the room. She had been real. She had sung off-key. Her name was Elowen and she had asked, in the last of her, to be remembered — and her child had failed at that for twenty-six years without knowing it, and had now, in the act of losing her utterly, finally answered.

*I remember you,* Rosalind said silently, to the empty tin, to the mountains, to whoever kept such accounts. *Somebody remembered you. It was me. It counts.*

Resolve is a funny thing. People think it arrives like armor. Mostly it arrives like this: scraped raw, wrung out, sitting on a log outside a drover's shelter with red eyes and a plan that consists entirely of the next step. Rosalind's next step was southeast, two days down-valley, to a mining town called Bracken Hollow, where the Codex's second page — deciphered in stolen lamplight, in another life three days ago — had marked a name.

*Tam Broadhurst. He hears with his hands. Find him. Tell him the choir is called.*

Rosalind stood, settled their pack, and gave the shelter's crumbling chimney a courteous nod, as one does to older buildings.

"All right, Mother," they said. "Let's go find the others."

Far behind them, at the head of the pass, the forgettable man stood motionless over a patch of bare stone where a song had died, and did not move for a very long time — and if any shepherd had chanced by and looked at his face, they would have found it oddly difficult, afterward, to describe what was wrong with it.

Something had changed. Something in the hollow of him had *heard*.

And far to the north, in a palace above a dying dragon, something wearing a crown lifted its head from a hundred small feedings and listened to the echo of a lullaby it had not authorized — and for the first time in a century, the Regent felt something that was almost, but not quite, entirely unlike hunger.
