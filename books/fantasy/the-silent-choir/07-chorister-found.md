## 7. Chorister Found

The town was called Greymarrow, though the sign at its edge had been defaced so many times that Rosalind half suspected the residents had forgotten what a marrow was and kept the name out of spite. It sat in a bowl of hills like a stone dropped in a pocket, all slate roofs and coal dust, with a mine shaft at its heart breathing warm air into the winter morning the way an old man breathes into his hands.

Rosalind came down off the cart road with their hood up and their guild pin turned inward, the way they'd worn it for years, and let the town tell them about itself. That was the trick of it—every place sang, if you'd learned to listen. Thornfell's cities hummed with too many voices layered over one another, a chord sustained so long nobody heard it anymore. Villages sang in rounds, each family carrying its own thread of tune. And mining towns—

Mining towns sang in minor keys.

Greymarrow's song rose from the ground itself, from the black seam under everyone's feet, and it was heavy with the names of men who hadn't come back up. Rosalind had felt such songs before, but this one had something extra stitched through it: a second melody, low and steady and patient, running underneath the grief like a beam propping up a ceiling.

Someone here was holding a memory-song on purpose. Had been, from the feel of it, for years.

* * *

Finding him took two days, because hiding was a trade Rosalind respected and recognized. They posed as a stone merchant scouting quarry contracts—which was true, in the way that most of their covers were true; Rosalind had learned long ago that the best lie was a job you'd actually done—and asked after masons in the way merchants do, casually, over bad ale.

"Tam Broadhurst," the innkeeper said, and then, when Rosalind waited, said nothing more.

"Good work?"

"Good work." The innkeeper wiped the counter with the particular thoroughness of a man who has decided a conversation is over.

It took a barber, three drinks bought for a mine foreman, and one outright bribe of decent tobacco before the shape of it emerged: Tam Broadhurst, forty-odd, deaf since a fever in his boyhood, the finest stonemason in three valleys and the strangest. He read lips well enough to work, spoke rarely and flatly, and had one habit the town found unsettling. He would lay his palm flat against old walls. Gravestones. The lintels of houses where someone had died. He'd stand there with his hand pressed to the stone, eyes closed, sometimes for an hour, and when folk asked what he was doing he'd say—"checking the mortar"—in that flat voice, and go back to his chisels.

"He knew the Harrow collapse was coming," the foreman said, tobacco going unsmoked in his fist. "Three years back. Put his hand on the shaft wall one morning and wouldn't take it off. Made such a nuisance of himself they sent him home. Two days later, eleven men dead." He looked at his hands. "Nobody thanks a man for being right about a thing like that."

Rosalind walked to the churchyard that evening as the light went blue and thin, and found the mason there, as expected, kneeling by a grave with his bare hand flat against the headstone. Snow gathered on his shoulders. He was a big man, built like something quarried rather than born, with grey coming into his beard and a face that did its feeling somewhere private, behind the eyes.

Rosalind didn't speak—they knew better than to startle a man mid-listen—but stood downwind where he'd see them, and waited.

After a while Tam lifted his hand from the stone and looked up. His gaze went over Rosalind's face the way a mason reads grain in timber: not unkind, but measuring.

"You're the one humming," he said. His voice was rough from disuse, oddly pitched. "Since yesterday. At the inn. Under the noise." He got to his feet, joints cracking like green wood splitting. "You shouldn't do that here."

"Why not?"

"Because things hear you." He brushed snow from his knee with great care. "I've spent thirty years learning to keep my hands quiet. You walk around ringing like a bell." He squinted at them. "Who taught you? Nobody teaches it. That's how I know you're trouble."

"My mother," Rosalind said, "taught me lullabies. The rest I taught myself, badly."

Something moved behind Tam's eyes—not warmth, exactly. Recognition of a fellow amateur in a trade neither of them had asked to join.

"There's a grave here," he said, nodding at the stone. "My wife's. Edith Broadhurst. Died four winters past, of the lung sickness the mine gives free with every shift." He laid his hand back on the granite, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping shoulder. "She used to sing while she baked. Badly. Off-key and loud, and she'd laugh at herself halfway through every verse." His thumb moved once across the stone. "The stone still has it. All of it. Every song, every Sunday, twenty-two years of her voice, soaked into this rock like rain into chalk. When I put my hand here, I can feel her singing." He looked up, and his face was dry and terrible. "That is what I am doing when people think I'm checking the mortar."

Rosalind knelt in the snow beside him and, very slowly, held out their own hand toward the headstone, asking permission with the gesture. Tam studied them for a long moment. Then he nodded.

Rosalind touched the stone.

The song came up through their palm like water through a wick—Edith Broadhurst's baking songs, off-key and loud, a woman laughing at herself halfway through every verse, flour on her hands, a kitchen full of the smell of raisins and coal smoke—and Rosalind's throat closed around it, because it wasn't remarkable, none of it was remarkable, it was just a life, an ordinary good life, sung into stone by sheer repetition and love until the rock couldn't help remembering.

They pulled their hand back. "Tam," they said carefully. "Do you know what you are?"

"A stonemason."

"What you are besides that."

He stood there in the failing light with snow in his beard, and Rosalind watched a man weigh thirty years of silence against a stranger's five words.

"No," he said at last. "And I'm not going to find out tonight. Go away, bell-ringer. Come back never."

* * *

Rosalind did not come back never. They came back the next morning, and were refused. They came back the day after, and were refused more loudly, which at least involved lip-reading practice for both parties. On the third day Rosalind brought bread from the inn and left it on Tam's step with a note: *You don't have to sing. But someone should know what your hands can do.* The note came back through the letter slot folded into a paper dart that caught Rosalind above the ear with impressive accuracy.

On the fourth day, Rosalind woke to shouting in the street.

Not ordinary Greymarrow shouting, which was a constant weather. This had a different texture—frightened, confused, gathering. Rosalind pulled on their coat and followed the sound to the town square, where a knot of miners had formed around the notice board beside the assay office, and the air above them shimmered faintly, the way it did when memory was being handled roughly.

Two men in grey stood at the board. They wore no livery, which was its own uniform; the Regent's agents dressed like clerks, like tax assessors, like men whose faces you forgot while looking at them. One was peeling papers from the board with a little knife, unhurried, tidy. The other held a ledger and read aloud from it in a pleasant, bored voice:

"—Edith Broadhurst, listed as deceased, wife of Tam Broadhurst, mason. Correction: no record of any marriage between Tam Broadhurst and any Edith. The parish register shows a blank page. The gravestone—" the clerk consulted the ledger, "—will be corrected. There is no Edith Broadhurst. There never was."

The miners murmured. Rosalind saw it happen in real time, saw the fog roll through their faces—the foreman who'd taken their tobacco blinking and saying, "Broadhurst? Married?"—saw a whole town's memory of a woman being peeled away like paint, quietly, efficiently, by men with knives and ledgers and bored voices.

And through the crowd, pushing, shoving, huge hands gentle and desperate, came Tam Broadhurst.

He couldn't hear the clerk reading. He didn't need to. He could feel it—that was the thing Rosalind understood even then, watching him come—he could feel the erasure happening, the way you feel a tooth being pulled from your jaw through the bone. He reached the board and grabbed the first agent's wrist, and the little knife clattered to the cobbles, and for a moment the square went absolutely silent.

"The stone," Tam said, and his flat voice cracked open on the second word. "You'll leave the stone."

"Sir," the clerk said kindly, "there is no Edith Broadhurst. Your grief does you credit, but you're confused. You've never been married. Perhaps the fever that took your hearing took other things—"

"She sang badly!" Tam roared at him, at all of them, at the world. "Off-key! Loud! Twenty-two years! She's in the stone, I can *feel* her in the stone—"

"Yes," the clerk said, with genuine gentleness, and that was the worst part, the kindness of it. "We know. That's why we've come."

The second agent had already produced a chisel. A good one. Guild-make, Rosalind noted distantly, some part of their mind cataloguing the horror of it—a tool of the Wayfarers' craft, sold or stolen, turned to this.

What happened next happened fast. The agents worked in pairs, one to distract, one to erase, and Tam was grieving and alone and strong, strong enough to break a wrist but not strong enough to fight the fog itself, the soft grey attention that slid over a crowd and made them forget why they were standing there. Rosalind felt it wash over the square and acted without deciding to act—stepped forward and began to hum.

Low. Steady. Not a memory-song, not yet, just a drone, a foundation note, and beneath their feet the whole town's buried grief answered it, rose up through the cobbles like heat, and Rosalind shaped it, aimed it, poured it into one wordless chord that hit the agents like a wave hitting a sea wall.

Both men staggered. The clerk's pleasant face went white. The one with the chisel fumbled it, and in that fumbling half-second Tam Broadhurst closed his enormous hand around the man's collar and lifted him off the ground like a misbehaving apprentice.

"You will not," Tam said, each word placed like a stone in a wall, "touch her stone."

"They'll send more," Rosalind said quietly. "Tam. They'll always send more. That's the arithmetic of it."

The mason stood there in the middle of the square, holding a crown agent off the ground, surrounded by neighbors who were already forgetting why they'd gathered, and Rosalind watched the last thing inside him finish breaking—not the grief; the grief had been load-bearing for four years and would hold. What broke was the belief that hiding would save anything.

He set the agent down. The man fled, and wisely.

"They took her name," Tam said. He was looking at his own hands as if they belonged to a stranger. "Out of the books. Out of their heads. It's still in the stone. It's still—" He stopped. Started again, quieter, in that rough unused voice. "How long do stones last, bell-ringer?"

"Centuries," Rosalind said. "But not forever."

"Then teach me to sing it somewhere that lasts forever." He turned, and for the first time since the churchyard there was something in his face that wasn't grief or stone. It was aim. "You came here for a reason. I've known since you started humming. So say it. Say what I am."

Rosalind told him. Most of it—the Codex, the Choir, the road to Thornfell, the dragon under the palace, the crown that ate memories. They left out, for now, the parts about prices and burning and what Maren had called the cost, because a man should be allowed one night to pack his tools before he learns the whole weight of the freight.

Tam listened with his eyes fixed on Rosalind's mouth, and when they finished he was silent a long moment. Then he said:

"My wife would have liked you. She liked stubborn people with bad plans." He picked up his satchel of chisels from where he'd dropped it and slung it over his shoulder. "One condition. We stop at the churchyard before we leave. I want to put my hand on her stone one last time, and I want you to learn the song. All of it. Every verse. If the kingdom's going to eat her name, then the kingdom can choke on it later."

"That," Rosalind said, and found their own voice had gone rough, "may be the first sensible thing anyone's agreed to in this whole affair."

They walked to the churchyard together through the falling snow, the big mason and the invisible courier, and behind them the town of Greymarrow went on with its evening, already forgetting the disturbance in the square, already forgetting the woman who had sung badly and loudly for twenty-two years—all except one headstone, which remembered everything, and one pair of hands that had spent a lifetime listening, and were now, at last, going to answer.

They were halfway to the churchyard gate when Rosalind felt it—a pressure on the edge of their awareness, far off to the south, vast and slow and attentive, turning toward Greymarrow like a great eye opening. The hum of the square had carried. The Regent knew there was a singer in these hills now.

And riding hard along the pass road behind that attention, closing fast, came something else: a single rider, moving with the terrible efficiency of a man who never needs to be remembered to be obeyed.

Tam felt Rosalind stiffen. "Trouble?"

"Worse," Rosalind said. "Company."
