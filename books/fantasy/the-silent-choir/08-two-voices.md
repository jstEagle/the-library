## 8. Two Voices

The chapel of Saint Hessel had been deconsecrated so long ago that even the pigeons had stopped treating it as sacred ground. Its roof was a suggestion rather than a promise, its bell tower housed a family of starlings with strong opinions, and its floor was a mosaic of moss and broken slate that crunched like biscuit underfoot.

"Charming," said Tam Broadhurst, in the flat tone of a man who had grown up in a mining town where a roof over your head was considered showing off.

"It has good bones," Rosalind said.

"It has *bones*. That's different."

They had come to the chapel because it was the only building in Hollowmere with a cellar deep enough to muffle sound, and because Maren's letter — delivered, as all her letters were, by a boy who charged triple for discretion and quadrupled for speed — had said, in her cramped lockkeeper's hand:

*If you've found your second voice, take him to Hessel's. The old guilds built their vaults under chapels. Nobody thinks to look under God anymore.*

Tam stood in the doorway a long moment before entering, and Rosalind pretended not to notice. He was a big man, broad as a door himself, with hands like weathered oak and a habit of touching walls as he passed them — reading them, Rosalind now understood, the way she read ledgers. He'd been deaf since the fever took him at nine years old, and he had spent thirty years hiding the fact that he could feel songs in his palms, in his sternum, in his teeth. Thirty years of pretending not to flinch when the miners' hymns came through the chapel floor on feast days. Thirty years of being alone inside a world of vibration.

He wasn't pretending anymore. It seemed to Rosalind that he walked differently since the night they'd found each other — or perhaps that was just grief. His wife Elsbeth existed now only in records the Regent's agents hadn't bothered to erase: a marriage line in a parish book, a name scratched on a locket, the calluses on Tam's own hands from forty years of holding hers. He carried her the way you carry a stone in your boot. Not comfortably. But carrying it anyway.

"The acoustics," Tam said, and then caught himself, because he didn't say *acoustics*, he signed it — one hand cupped, the other fingers fluttering against it like water finding a basin. He spoke aloud too, always aloud, because he'd learned speech from feeling throats and watching mouths and refusing, with the particular stubbornness of the Broadhurst line, to be silenced by anything as trivial as deafness. "The room will carry it well."

"You can tell already?"

"I can tell everything already." He touched the pillar beside him. "This stone is humming. Something sang here once. Recently." A pause. His jaw tightened. "Not recently. A hundred years ago. The stone keeps it like a bruise."

Rosalind felt nothing when she touched the pillar. That was the difference between them, and they were still learning its shape. She heard memory-songs the way other people heard music — faint, at the edge of things, requiring attention. Tam *felt* them, full-body, whether he wanted to or not. When she had first hummed back at the smuggler's coin in front of him, he had grabbed her wrist hard enough to leave marks, his eyes wide with something between terror and joy, and signed: *You hear them? You HEAR them?*

*I am them,* she'd signed back, badly, having known the hand-language for three days.

*Then we are both in trouble,* he'd said, which was the most honest assessment anyone had made of her situation in months.

* * *

They began, as all rehearsals begin, badly.

Rosalind had spent the morning practicing the fragment from the Ash Codex — the first page of true notation she'd found tucked behind the ledger of vanished names, a single line of melody annotated in a cramped, precise hand: *Begin here. One voice calls. Two voices answer. What is answered cannot be un-answered.*

She sang it alone in the chapel's nave, and it did what it always did: stirred the dust, made the smuggler's coin in her pocket hum along like a drunk uncle joining a chorus, and accomplished precisely nothing else.

"Now together," she said.

Tam positioned himself with his back against the central pillar, barefoot — he'd removed his boots without comment an hour ago, and Rosalind had learned not to ask about the things Tam did with stone and floors and feet. His palms flat against the pillar. His eyes closed.

"How do you want to do this?" she asked. "I'll start, you come in on the third bar—"

"No." He shook his head. "You're thinking of it wrong. You're thinking I follow you." He opened his eyes. They were pale grey, the color of granite, and entirely steady. "I don't follow. I *answer*. There's a difference. A follower waits. An answerer listens." He tapped his own chest twice. "I have been listening my whole life. Everyone else was just too loud to hear what I was saying back."

So Rosalind sang the opening line again, and instead of waiting for the third bar, she listened.

It was the hardest thing she'd ever done. Listening while singing went against every instinct a courier had — couriers moved fast, traveled light, trusted no one else's footsteps on the road. But she let the note go out of her and into the stone, and she waited, and there —

There.

It rose up through the floor and out of Tam's throat, a low tone she felt in her molars before she heard it with her ears, and it was *hers* but changed, hers reflected off something vast, and where her line had been a question his was the shape of the question being held gently, turned over, understood.

The coin in her pocket rang like a struck bell.

The dust in the air began, very slowly, to dance.

"Oh," Rosalind breathed, and lost the line, and the spell broke, and the dust settled, and Tam grinned at her — actually grinned, the first time she'd seen him do it, and it transformed his whole grave face into something young.

"Again," he said. "And this time, don't stop when it works. That's the whole secret. Don't stop when it works."

* * *

The second time, they got four bars. The third time, eight. By late afternoon they had the whole phrase, and the chapel had begun doing things.

Cobwebs lifted and hung in the air like slow smoke. The broken slates rattled themselves into a loose pattern across the floor — Tam swore afterward that he could feel them arranging, could feel the *order* coming back into them, order that had been sung into them once and had been waiting a century for someone to remind them of it. And beneath their voices, beneath the harmonies threading and climbing, Rosalind began to hear something else.

A third voice. Very far down. Very patient.

"Do you feel that?" she whispered, breaking the song again, unable to help herself.

Tam had gone rigid against the pillar. His hands were pressed flat to the stone and his lips were moving silently, translating what came up through his arms. "Under us," he said. "Under us there's a room. Under the room there's—" He stopped. Started again. "There's a *door*, and the door isn't locked, it's *waiting*, and it has been waiting so long that the waiting has become part of the door." He looked up at her, and his face was the face of a man standing at the edge of something enormous. "Rosalind. It knows our names."

"That's either very good or very bad."

"With us," Tam said, "it's usually both."

The door, once they found it, was exactly where doors in stories were: beneath the altar, under a slab that weighed approximately as much as a small cottage and required twenty minutes, two crowbars, and a sustained exchange of creative profanity to shift. (Tam's profanity was better than Rosalind's. Mining towns, he explained with dignity, refined a person.)

Below the slab, steps descended into dark. The air that came up smelled of cold stone and paper and — faintly, impossibly — beeswax. Fresh beeswax. As if someone had polished the furniture below last week.

"A hundred years," Rosalind murmured. "Nothing should smell fresh."

"Stone remembers polish," Tam said. "Everything remembers something. That's the whole problem with the world, if you ask me. And the whole point of it."

They went down with a candle apiece, because some habits survive even the discovery of magic.

The vault beneath Saint Hessel's was not large, and it was not grand, and it was the most beautiful thing Rosalind Winterbourne had ever seen.

Shelves lined the walls — hundreds of them, floor to ceiling, carved directly from the living rock — and on the shelves sat objects. Thousands of objects. Thimbles and tankards, wedding rings and whetstones, a child's wooden horse with one ear chewed off, a soldier's letter sealed but never sent, a hairbrush still holding three long silver hairs. Every object Rosalind's lantern touched gave off a faint hum, a different pitch for each one, so that the whole room sounded like the moment before rain — a gathering, a holding of breath.

"It's a library," she whispered.

Tam ran his palm along a shelf and shuddered. "No. Libraries hold books. This holds..." His hands moved, searching for the sign, settling on one she didn't know and then simplifying it: *lives*. "Each one is a life. Or part of one. Someone chose these. Someone said: this object held this memory best. This ring remembers the proposal. This brush remembers her mornings." His throat worked. "Someone thought memories were worth shelving. Worth keeping dry."

"The Wayfarers' Guild," Rosalind said slowly. "Maren told me we were founded to carry memories. I thought she meant it the way old people mean things. Poetically." She turned in a slow circle, taking it in. "We weren't couriers who sometimes handled strange cargo. We were *this*. We were always this. The candles and the wine and the contraband were just — cover. The real deliveries were people's lives."

At the vault's center stood a desk. On the desk lay a book.

Rosalind knew it before she touched it. Of course she knew it. The same cracked leather, the same ash-grey binding, the same weight of paper that felt warm the way a sleeping animal feels warm. She had left the Ash Codex locked in her room at the inn, wrapped in oilcloth, under a floorboard she'd pried up herself.

She checked anyway. Later she would check. But first she crossed the room and opened the book on the desk, and there it was — the same book, or its twin, open not to the prophecies but to a page near the front she'd never seen, because the pages before the ledger had been fused shut by age and she'd never dared force them.

Now they fell open at a touch, as if they had only been waiting.

*My name is Ede Voss,* said the writing, in the same cramped, precise hand as the musical annotation. *I am guildmaster of the Wayfarers, and I am writing this on the last night I expect to be myself.*

* * *

They read until the candles burned down, taking turns aloud — Tam reading haltingly, tracing the words with one finger while his other hand rested on the desk to catch any tremor of memory in the wood, Rosalind reading the rest, her voice growing quieter as the page grew darker.

Ede Voss had written quickly, in the way of a person who knows the candle is short. The Wayfarers' Guild, he recorded, had kept the kingdom's memory for six hundred years — not hoarded it, *kept* it, the way a lockkeeper keeps water: moving it, channeling it, returning it to those who had lost theirs to plague, to grief, to fire and flood. Every chorister carried songs that belonged to other people. That was the covenant. *What we hold, we hold for others.*

And then the crown had been forged.

*Aldous was crowned in the year the dragon-fire was bound into the gold,* Ede wrote, *and the smiths believed they were making a symbol. They were making a mouth. The fire remembers everything it has ever touched — that is its nature, that is why we sing to it — and a crown set upon a king's brow becomes a listener set upon a throne. It began small. A forgotten treaty restored. A dead friend's voice remembered. The court marveled at how wise the king had grown.*

*But the crown does not only remember. It hungers. And it has learned, from sitting above a man's thoughts, how to eat.*

*I have studied it for ten years. I know its nature as well as any living soul can. And I believe — I must believe — that it can be unmade. Not by armies. Armies are noise, and it eats noise. It must be sung apart, thread by thread, by voices it has never tasted. A choir. Eight voices, the old arithmetic says, though I confess I no longer remember why eight, only that the number came to me in a dream of bells and would not leave.*

*Tomorrow I walk into the palace and present myself as an archivist. If I succeed, I will return and burn these pages. If I fail—*

The ink changed here. Blotted, hurried, the letters leaning as though written by a hand that was shaking.

*If I fail, whoever finds this: do NOT come after me. I mean this. I have seen what the crown leaves of the people it takes, and I would rather be wholly devoured than half-rescued. Grieve me and move on. That is a courier's whole art, in the end — you deliver what you carry, and then you put it down.*

*One more thing, and then I seal this vault, and I am sorry for the trick of it, but the vault answers to two voices and I needed to be sure that whoever opened it was not alone. Alone is how it takes us.*

*Find the choir. Sing the king's own name back to him. And if you are a Voss — and I suspect you are, because you opened this, and only blood opens this — then I am sorry for what I have passed down to you. I tried to end the inheritance. Inheritances are stubborn.*

*— E.V., Guildmaster, on the last night of himself*

The silence after the last word lasted a long time.

Tam sat down heavily on the floor, his back against the desk, and signed without speaking, his hands slow: *He knew. A hundred years ago, he knew it would be someone from his own line. He built the lock and hid the key in his family.* His hands stilled. *And then he went and got eaten by the lock.*

"He thought he was protecting us," Rosalind said. Her voice came out strange. "Hiding the Wintervournes. Making us invisible. Small. Forgettable on purpose." She laughed, and it wasn't a kind sound. "All those years I wondered what was wrong with me. Why my mother taught me to walk soft and give no name and keep moving. I thought it was shame. I thought we were running from debt, or scandal, or — something. We were running from a *prophecy*. From this." She held up the book. "From a job opening."

"Rosalind." Tam rarely used her name. When he did, it landed like a hand on the shoulder. "Read the warning again. *Do not come after me.*"

"I read it."

"And?"

"And he also wrote instructions for an unbinding, and arithmetic for a choir, and he addressed the last page to whoever found it knowing — *knowing* — that only his blood could find it." She closed the book gently, the way you close a sleeping child's door. "That's not a warning, Tam. That's a man telling his family not to worry while leaving the lamp lit and the kettle on. Guildmasters." She shook her head. "Even a hundred years dead, he can't stop managing people."

Tam huffed — his version of a laugh, felt more than heard. Then his expression shifted, and he laid two fingers against the desk's surface, frowning.

"What?"

"There's something in the desk." He knocked softly. "Wood remembers pressure. There's a drawer that's been opened ten thousand times and one drawer that's been opened... never."

It took Rosalind three minutes and the surviving crowbar. Inside the false bottom lay a bundle of letters tied with guild-green cord, a conductor's baton of black ironwood worn smooth at the grip, and a small portrait in a frame of cheap tin.

The portrait showed a woman with dark hair going grey at the temples and Rosalind's exact chin, standing in front of a Guildhouse that still had its roof. On the back, in Ede Voss's hand:

*My daughter, Sable, who refused the road and stayed to keep the vaults. All conductors come from her line. If you are reading this and your chin matches hers, then hello, cousin. I'm sorry about everything.*

Rosalind stared at it for a long time.

"My mother had that chin," she said finally. "She used to say it was a Broadchin, from her mother's side. Some border family nobody talked about." She looked up, and her eyes were bright and furious and shining all at once. "Voss. Tam. My mother's family was *Voss*. We didn't even change the name much. We just — dropped a letter and kept walking."

Outside, far above them, the starlings in the bell tower burst suddenly into flight, all at once, as if something enormous had turned over in its sleep.

And down in the vault, every object on every shelf hummed a half-step louder, and Rosalind would swear for the rest of her life that the sound arranged itself — briefly, unmistakably — into a word.

The word was *welcome*.
