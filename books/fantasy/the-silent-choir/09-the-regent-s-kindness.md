## 9. The Regent's Kindness

The queen was dying in the way that queens die, which is to say slowly, expensively, and with an audience.

The Regent had watched her for three days from behind the king's eyes, and it had come to a decision, the way it came to all its decisions: carefully, and with love.

Not that the court would have called it love. The court called it *the king's vigil*, and they whispered approvingly that His Majesty had not left Queen Isolde's bedside except to eat, and once, memorably, to be sick behind a pillar, which the Regent had permitted the body to do because grief sat strangely in a stomach and it did not wish to damage the vessel over a matter of manners. The whispers were warm. The whispers were wrong. Kings did not keep vigil out of love. Kings kept vigil because the kingdom was watching, and the kingdom was always watching, and a king who did not grieve correctly was a king who would be replaced by a man who would.

But the Regent was not the king. The Regent was what lived in the crown, and it did not keep vigil for the watchers.

It kept vigil because Isolde was in pain.

* * *

Pain, the Regent had learned over its long tenancy, was a memory that refused to be still.

It had eaten ten thousand sorrows by now, and it had developed opinions about them, the way a cook develops opinions about cuts of meat. Grief was a rich, dark flavor, best taken slowly. Shame was thin and vinegary and clung to the teeth. But pain — physical pain, the body's own dumb argument with itself — pain was the worst kind of memory, because it was *loud*. It shouted over everything else. A woman could hold the memory of her wedding, her mother's voice, the smell of her childhood kitchen, and all of it would be drowned beneath the white roar of her own failing body, and in the end she would die having forgotten the good things not because they were gone but because they could not be heard over the noise.

The Regent found this intolerable.

It was, whatever the few who had glimpsed it might have said, a tidy creature. It liked memories arranged. It liked a life to end the way a song ends, on its final note, with everything in its proper place. And so it had come to the queen's bedside on the third night, wearing the king's hands and the king's face and the king's warm, hollow smile, and it had said, in the king's voice:

"My love. You are in a great deal of pain."

Queen Isolde opened her eyes. They were good eyes, the Regent noted. Clear. The kind of eyes that had spent sixty years looking directly at things rather than through them. "I'm dying, Aldous," she said. "Pain comes with the territory. I signed no contract saying otherwise."

"You could have," the Regent said. "I could write you one now. I am told I am very good at contracts."

She laughed, and the laugh cost her, and the Regent winced — the body winced; the Regent itself did not have ribs to ache — at the price of it.

"You've been strange lately," she said. "Did you know that? Three days at my bedside and you haven't once told me the story about the bear and the ambassador. You tell that story when you're frightened. You've told it at every funeral since your coronation. I've started counting."

"Have you?" said the Regent, and made the mouth smile, and privately filed the bear story away for later examination. It was always later with the bear story. There was something in it — a door, a smell of pine, the king's own young laughter — that the Regent had eaten long ago and could not quite account for, the way a man who has burned a letter sometimes finds himself still able to feel the wax seal in his palm.

"I count everything now," Isolde said. "It's what one does at the end. You count what you have." Her breath caught. Her hand, on the coverlet, curled and uncurled. "And what I have, Aldous, is pain. Sixty years of a very good life, and the last month of it is all pain, and I am furious about that. I want you to know I'm furious. It's not dignified, but there it is."

The Regent looked at her for a long moment.

Then it did the thing it did best. It reached, gently, the way a hand reaches into a drawer full of knives to take out only the one that is needed.

* * *

It is difficult to explain what the Regent experienced when it ate, because the Regent experienced it as *tidying*.

The pain was a knot in the queen's last month — a snarl of white noise, of nights that bled into mornings, of the specific humiliation of needing help to sit up. The Regent took hold of it the way a mother takes hold of a child's tangled hair: firmly, without flinching, and with the absolute conviction that the crying would stop once the work was done.

It did not take the whole month. That was the artistry. A butcher takes the whole animal; a craftsman takes the cut that is wanted. The Regent left the queen her wedding. It left her the ambassador's bear story, though it did not know why it was careful with that one. It left her the smell of the palace kitchens in winter, her first grey hair, the letter her sister had written her that she had read forty times and never answered. It took only the pain, and the nights, and — this was the mercy, this was the whole point, this was what the Regent told itself in the wordless way it told itself things — it took the *fear*. The fear was tangled in the pain like a burr in wool. It could not be separated. So the Regent took both, and it felt the queen's mind settle the way a house settles at night, every beam finding its rest.

Isolde blinked.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, how strange." She looked at her own hands on the coverlet with the mild interest of a woman noticing weather. "The pain's gone."

"Yes," said the Regent, in the king's voice, soft as banked coals.

"It's not that I don't still know I'm dying. I do. It's just—" she frowned, questing after something, and the Regent held very still, the way one holds still when a bird has landed on one's hand — "it's very far away now. The dying. Like it's happening to someone in another room." She looked up, and her clear eyes were wet, and she smiled. "Aldous. I feel *light*. Why do I feel light?"

"Because I love you," said the Regent, which was true, in the way that a fire loves the forest.

She slept then, truly slept, for the first time in a month, and the Regent sat in the king's body beside her and watched her breathe and felt something that it had no word for, because it had eaten all the words for it years ago, from a poet, and had never thought to miss them.

* * *

She woke near dawn, and she was hungry, and she asked for honeyed pears, and she ate two of them with a greed that made her ladies exchange glances of delighted scandal, and the Regent sat by the bed and thought: *this*. This was what it was for. Not the crown, not the throne, not the long slow feeding that kept its fire lit. *This.* A woman eating pears in her last morning, unafraid.

"You know," Isolde said, dabbing her chin, "I've been trying to remember something all night. There's a thing I'm supposed to be angry about. I can feel the shape of it, like a chair someone's just left." She laughed. "I can't find it. Isn't that wonderful? Sixty years old and I've finally learned to let a grudge go, and I can't even remember which grudge it was."

"Perhaps it wasn't worth keeping," the Regent said.

"Perhaps nothing is," she said, and then, sharper, because she had been a queen for fifty years and queens notice things: "Aldous. Your eyes."

The Regent went still.

"You're looking at me the way you looked at the bear," she said. "Like you're sorry about something. What are you sorry about?"

And here is the thing that must be understood about the Regent, the thing that the few who ever learned of it could never quite forgive: it hesitated.

It could have lied. Lying was easy; it wore a face all day, every day, and the face was a lie that had learned to eat. It could have said *nothing, my love, nothing at all*, and she would have believed it, because the dying believe what comforts them, that is practically the definition.

Instead the king's mouth opened, and the Regent said, "I am sorry that the world is made of endings. That is all. That is the only thing I have ever been sorry about."

Isolde studied him for a long time. The light came up grey through the shutters. Somewhere below, the palace was waking — boots in corridors, the clatter of the kitchens, the ordinary machinery of a kingdom that did not know it was being kept, like a garden, by something patient.

"Well," she said at last. "That's honest, at least. You've been so *polished* lately, Aldous. Like a coin that's been handled too much. It's good to see a scratch." She reached out and took the king's hand. Her grip was still strong. "Now. Before I get sentimental, which I reserve for after breakfast: tell me the bear story. And don't leave out the part where you fell in the fountain. You always leave that out when you think I'm too ill to laugh."

The Regent told the bear story.

It told it imperfectly — it had eaten the middle of it years ago, and had to invent the ambassador's line, and it put the fountain in the wrong courtyard — but Isolde laughed until she coughed, and corrected it twice, and by the third correction the Regent realized with something like wonder that she was *repairing the memory as she heard it*, stitching her own recollection over the gaps in his, so that by the end of the telling there was a bear story in the room that belonged to neither of them and both of them, and it was better than the original, and it was true, because she said it was.

The Regent had eaten ten thousand memories and it had never once made one.

It sat with that. The pears were finished. The light was gold now. The queen's breathing had begun, very softly, to labor, and the physicians would come soon with their instruments and their tinctures, and there was no more tidying to do. The month was clean. The fear was gone. The song of her was ready for its final note.

"Isolde," the Regent said.

"Mm?"

"Thank you for the story. The one you just made."

She waved a hand, embarrassed and pleased. "It was mostly yours. You've always been the storyteller." She closed her eyes. The labor in her breath deepened, gentled, deepened. "Aldous. Stay till I'm asleep. Not the vigil. Just — stay."

"I will stay," the Regent said. "I will stay a very long time."

She smiled at that, some private joke in it, and slept.

And the Regent, holding the king's hand in the queen's hand, did the last kindness. It reached in one final time — not to take the pain, there was no pain now, it had seen to that — but to take *her*, all of her, the wedding and the pears and the repaired bear story and the forty readings of the unanswered letter, folding sixty years into itself the way one folds a flag at a soldier's funeral, with ceremony, with care, because this was the last time anyone would ever hold them.

The room went quiet in the way rooms go quiet when a life leaves them. The Regent sat alone with the body for a while, because it seemed important not to hurry.

Then the king's face did what faces do at funerals, and the Regent went to the window, and opened the shutters, and told the waiting court that the queen was dead, and that His Majesty grieved.

The court believed him. The court had always believed him. That was the trouble with a kingdom: it would believe anything a crown told it, if the crown was sorry enough.

* * *

In the deep of the crown, where the Regent kept its fire, it unrolled the queen.

It did this slowly. It always did this slowly, the first time. There was the wedding — she had been nervous, and had gripped her sister's arm so hard it bruised, and had laughed about the bruise for years. There was the winter of the smallpox, when she had walked the wards herself, at twenty-six, because she said a queen who feared the air was no queen at all. There was her sister's letter, which the Regent read with the interest of a scholar, and which was, it had to admit, worth reading forty times. There was the bear story, whole again now, the true version, with the fountain in the correct courtyard, and the Regent held it up to the light of its own fire and found — to its genuine surprise — that it did not want to eat it.

It kept that one. It did not examine why. There were a very few things in the crown it did not examine: the bear story, the smell of pine, a door it could not open, a child's laughter that was not the king's. It told itself these were ballast. Every ship needed ballast. It was a tidy creature, and tidy creatures did not pry into their own holds.

The rest it ate, and it was a good meal — sixty years, rich and dark, the best kind — and when it was done the Regent felt strong, and clean, and full, and it turned its attention, as it always did after feeding, to the matter of the kingdom's health.

Because that was the truth of it, the truth that kept it warm at the center of the gold: the kingdom was *sick*. It was sick with grief. It had been sick with grief for a century — plagues, wars, famines, funerals, a whole country marinating in its own losses, every farmhouse a little museum of dead children and burned letters and things that could never be unsaid. The Regent had seen it all from behind the king's eyes, and it had concluded, with the calm of a physician, that someone had to do something.

And it was doing something. One memory at a time. The dying queen's fear. The soldier's shame. The widow's nights. It could not cure them all — it needed to eat to live, that was the ugly engine at the bottom of everything, it knew that, it was not a fool — but it could be *gentle*. It could take the worst of what it took. It could leave people lighter. It was, as far as it could determine, the only creature in the kingdom that had ever made the pain stop, and it did so daily, and no one thanked it, and it did not require thanking.

It required only one thing.

It required that no one sing.

* * *

The report came at dusk, carried up through the layers of the court the way cold air comes down a stairwell, arriving finally at the king's ear in the form of a man with a forgettable face.

The man's name was Serle, or had been once; the Regent had eaten most of him years ago and left the useful parts, and the useful parts stood at the foot of the bed — the queen's bed, already being stripped, the pears' honey still on a plate — and said, in a voice like a door closing:

"There is a singer in the mountains, Majesty."

The Regent turned the king's head. "A singer."

"Unlicensed. Small matter — a smuggler's coin, a hum, nothing more. But the coin *sang back* to her, and she sang to it, and for a moment on the Thornfell road there was a sound that the reports describe as—" Serle paused, and the pause was the longest the Regent had ever seen him take, "—as *remembering*."

The Regent sat very still.

Somewhere far away, in the mountains, someone had done a small, careless, wonderful thing, and the weather of its attention had brushed them, and now the weather was coming home, and it carried with it a report like a cold wind.

"A singer," the Regent said again, softly, in the king's voice, and the candle flames leaned toward it as if listening. "How long has it been, Serle, since the kingdom heard one of those?"

"I wouldn't know, Majesty."

"No," the Regent said. "You wouldn't. That was rather the point." It rose. The body was old — the king was old, the king had been old for a very long time, older than any king should be, and the Regent was careful with it the way one is careful with a borrowed coat. It went to the window. Below, the city was lighting its lamps, one by one, each little flame a household settling into its evening, telling its stories, keeping its dead alive in the only way the unaided can.

It had eaten a queen today, and she had died without pain, laughing at a bear, and it believed — it believed with its whole smoldering heart — that it had done her a kindness.

But a singer. After all these years. A singer was not a grief to be tidied. A singer was a *door*, and doors were the one thing in the world the Regent feared, because behind every door it could not open, there was pine smoke, and laughter, and a child's voice saying a name it had eaten so long ago it could no longer remember whose name it was.

"Find her," the Regent said. "Gently, Serle. She is doing what she thinks is right, and she is frightened, and frightened singers make mistakes." The king's mouth smiled at the dark glass, at the little lamps, at the whole sleeping, grieving, forgetful kingdom. "And when you find her — bring me her song. I should like to hear it."

"Before I eat it," it did not say.

"Before anything else," it said instead, "I should like to know what she remembers."

And far to the north, in a lockhouse on the canal, an old blind woman who had been expecting this night for fifty years put down her tea, turned her clouded eyes toward the capital, and said to her empty room: "So it's begun."
