## 10. Serle's Offer

The town of Marlow's Cross had one inn, two shrines, and a strong opinion about strangers, which was that they should keep moving. Rosalind had been counting on that opinion. Invisibility was easier to maintain in places that wanted nothing to do with you.

They took the room above the stable because it was cheap and because its window opened onto the stable roof, and the stable roof onto the wall behind the farrier's, and so on out of town in a chain of small escapes that Rosalind had walked twice already that afternoon, memorizing it the way other people memorized prayers.

Tam sat on the bed with his boots off, rubbing his feet and watching Rosalind pace.

"You're doing the thing," he said.

"What thing?"

"The walking thing. Like a fox in a small den." He patted the mattress beside him. "Sit. Whatever's coming will come whether you're sitting or pacing. You'll just be less tired if you're sitting."

"I'm not tired."

"You're always tired. You hide it badly." He said it without malice, the way he said most things, his hands moving as he spoke out of habit even though Rosalind heard fine and he knew it. "Also, you've checked the window four times in ten minutes. If someone were coming, they'd have come by now."

As if the world had been waiting politely for exactly that sentence, there came a knock at the door.

Rosalind went very still. Tam felt it — he'd learned to read her stillness through the floorboards, through the air, through whatever sense he'd built to replace the one he didn't have — and his hand drifted to the mason's maul he kept by the bed, an ugly, honest tool that had ended two conversations already on this journey and was willing to end more.

"Through the window?" Tam signed.

"Roof first," Rosalind signed back. "If I'm not down in a minute, go without me."

"No," Tam said, aloud, pleasantly, and did not move.

There was no time to argue, because the knock came again, and then a voice, mild and reasonable as a man asking directions: "Mistress Winterbourne. Or shall I say mistress courier? Mistress singer? I find I don't know what to call you. It's rather refreshing."

Rosalind knew the voice from Maren's warnings and from the shape of the last three weeks — the agent who moved through the passes like weather, the man whose face slid off the memory like rain off oilcloth. Serle.

She could have gone out the window. Some part of her, the well-trained part, the part that had survived twenty years of contraband candles and tax collectors and one memorable incident involving a bear, was screaming *go now*. But another part, the newer part, the part that had hummed back to a smuggler's coin and felt the Regent's attention roll over the mountains like a storm front — that part wanted to look at him. Wanted to know what the Regent sent when it sent gently.

She opened the door.

* * *

He was smaller than she'd imagined. That was the first absurd thought she had: all this dread, and the man was of middling height with sandy hair going grey at the temples and a face that — she tried to hold it, and couldn't. She looked directly at him and it was like looking into water. She could see eyes, a nose, a mouth; she simply could not have described any of them afterward, and she suspected that by tomorrow she would remember only that a man had been here, and that she had felt watched.

"May I come in?" he asked. "I promise not to touch anything. I know how you people feel about your things."

"How we people feel," Rosalind said. "Which people would that be?"

"Wayfarers," said Serle, and stepped past her into the room before she decided whether to let him. He looked around with genuine, almost wistful curiosity — at Tam's maul, at the Ash Codex's satchel where it hung from the bedpost, at the candle stubs Rosalind had never gotten around to selling. "I haven't been inside a Wayfarer's room in thirty years. They always smell the same. Beeswax and wet wool and, underneath, ink. You're all ink under everything. Isn't that strange? The guild that carries everything and writes nothing down."

"We write plenty down," Rosalind said. "We're just careful whose hands it ends up in."

"Yes." He turned. "That's what I've come to talk to you about. Whose hands things end up in." He nodded at Tam. "Master Broadhurst. Your wife's grave in Kellin is tended, incidentally. Weeds pulled, stone washed. Someone does it every week. Do you wonder who?"

Tam had gone white. Rosalind hadn't known a face could hold both fury and grief at once like that, held perfectly still, like a cup filled to the brim.

"You leave her name out of your mouth," Tam said quietly.

"It isn't in my mouth," Serle said. "That's rather the point. It's nowhere. Not in the parish rolls, not in the mine ledgers, not in her sister's letters — her sister writes to her now as though she'd never existed, thanks her for things she can't remember receiving. There is exactly one place left in this world where Ellen Broadhurst exists, Master Broadhurst, and it is in your hands. In here." He tapped his own knuckles against his palm. "You feel her, don't you? When you work stone. A rhythm. I'm told that's how it comes to your kind."

"Get out," Rosalind said.

"In a moment. First, my offer." He sat down uninvited on the one chair, folding his hands like a man settling in for a long sermon, and looked up at them both with an expression of such tired honesty that it took Rosalind a moment to recognize it as the most dangerous thing in the room. "You're going to the palace. Don't bother denying it — the Codex has a destination, the Codex always has a destination, it was written by someone who thought in destinations. You'll take the south road when the passes close, you'll come through the river gate because the land gate has doubled its watch since autumn, and you'll get within forty yards of the throne room before we stop you. Forty yards is generous. I've argued for it personally."

"You've argued—"

"I am very good at my work," Serle said, "and my work includes telling the Regent how much rope to give. Now. My offer." He reached into his coat and set something on the table between them: a folded paper, sealed with plain wax, no crest. "Safe passage. All three roads open, the watches blind, the river gate staffed by men who've been paid to be elsewhere. You walk into the palace, you sing your song, nobody raises a hand against you. In exchange—" his eyes went to the satchel on the bedpost, "—the book stays here. Burned, buried, given to me, I genuinely don't care which. It just doesn't reach the palace."

"And why," Rosalind said slowly, "would you offer me the palace at all? If the Regent wants me stopped, why hand me the door?"

"Because the Regent doesn't want you stopped," Serle said. "It wants you *arrived*. It wants you standing in front of it with your little choir, singing your heart out, right where it can reach you. It has wanted that since the coin. Everything since the coin has been it clearing the road and calling it pursuit." He smiled, and there was something broken in the smile. "It thinks in invitations, mistress courier. It has never once chased anyone in a hundred years. It only ever opens doors and waits to see who's foolish enough to walk through."

Rosalind's skin crawled, because some cold part of her recognized the shape of it. The cave with her childhood carved in its walls. The vials she hadn't seen yet but somehow already dreaded. Every step of this road had been suspiciously well-lit.

"And the Codex?" she said. "Why does it fear a book?"

"Fear." Serle considered the word. "No. The Regent doesn't fear the way we do. But there are pages in that book it cannot read. Pages written in a hand it can't parse, instructions folded inside prophecy the way a pill is folded inside cheese for a difficult dog. It has been trying to read that book for a century. It has eaten four archivists trying. So no, it doesn't fear it. It *hates* it. And it has tasked me with seeing that it never gets sung." He stood, smoothing his coat. "That's the offer. Passage for the book. Think of it, mistress courier — no hunters, no blizzard crossings, no funerals along the way. Just arrive, sing, and be done. You could be home by spring."

"We don't have a home," Tam said.

"Don't you?" Serle said softly. "What a sad thing for a man with a maul and good hands to believe."

* * *

Rosalind made herself sit after he left, though sitting felt like agreement and every nerve said *run*. Tam lit one of the contraband candles — the good ones, rose-scented, meant for a bishop's mistress in the capital — and the room filled with a warmth that felt almost like mockery.

"He knew your name," Tam signed. "Your real one. Winterbourne."

"He knows a lot of names."

"That's not comforting, Ros."

"No," Rosalind admitted. "It isn't."

They sat with it. Outside, Marlow's Cross settled into evening — a dog barking itself hoarse at nothing, a smith closing up with the particular bang of a man who resents his anvil, someone two streets over practicing a fiddle badly enough to constitute a public nuisance.

"The passage," Tam said at last. "Is it real? Could we trust it?"

"I think it's real," Rosalind said. "That's what frightens me. It's not a trap in the usual shape. It's worse — it's a trap shaped exactly like what we want. An easy ending. Walk in, sing, done, home by spring." She laughed, short and joyless. "Do you know how long it's been since anyone offered me an easy ending? I didn't know they still made them."

"So we refuse."

"Do we?" Rosalind got up and paced — the fox in the den again, Tam watching her with fond exasperation — and then stopped, because pacing wasn't working and honesty was all she had left. "Tam, I've been turning it over since he walked out, and here's the ugly truth: he told us nothing we didn't half-know. Yes, the road might be open because the Regent wants it open. Yes, walking into the palace might be walking into its mouth. But the Codex says the dragon beneath the palace is dying, and when it dies, everything the Regent has ever eaten stays eaten forever, and everyone we've met — Maren, Wren—" she caught herself, "—everyone, Tam, becomes a hole where a person used to be. So the question isn't whether the invitation is safe. The question is whether we can afford to be careful anymore."

Tam was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, "My Ellen used to say, when the mine flooded and the owners wouldn't pay for pumps: *they want us desperate, so don't be.*" His hands moved slow and heavy. "Serle wants you frightened. The Regent wants you frightened. Frightened singers make mistakes — I heard him say it through the door before he knocked, or read it off your face when you did. So." He looked up at her. "Don't be."

"When did you hear him say—"

"You talk to yourself when you pace," Tam said. "You always have. Loudly, for a woman who makes her living being invisible."

Despite everything, Rosalind laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her — and something in the room loosened by a single degree.

She took the sealed paper Serle had left and held it over the candle flame. It caught, curled, and was gone, and she felt no better for it, because burning an offer doesn't burn the wanting.

But she kept the wax seal. She didn't fully know why. Maybe because it was the only proof the conversation had happened, and she was a courier, and couriers kept receipts.

* * *

They left Marlow's Cross before dawn, taking the roof-route out of town in the blue-grey dark, and it was on the far side of the second wall, dropping down into a lane smelling of frost and pig, that Rosalind heard it.

Singing. Distant, from the direction of the south road — a voice, young and bright and impossibly polished, working its way through a ballad Rosalind half-recognized and couldn't place, each note landing precisely where a note ought to land.

Too precisely, some old guild instinct whispered. Nobody sings that clean. Life leaves smudges.

A traveler coming up the lane — a slight figure with a lute on their back and a smile like they'd rehearsed it in front of a very flattering mirror — lifted a hand in greeting.

"Well met!" the stranger called cheerfully. "Going south? So am I! Terrible weather for it. Mind if I walk along? Roads are safer with company — and I do love an audience."

Tam glanced at Rosalind. Rosalind glanced at Tam. Somewhere north of them, an old woman's warning and a dead guildmaster's prophecy and a monster's patience all seemed, briefly, to chuckle in unison.

"Company," Rosalind said, and managed a courier's bland, forgettable smile. "Of course. The more, the merrier."
