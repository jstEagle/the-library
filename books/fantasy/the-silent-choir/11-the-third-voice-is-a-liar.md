## 11. The Third Voice Is a Liar

The trouble with Wren began, as most trouble does, with a song.

They had stopped for the night at the Drowned Goose, a coaching inn outside Marlow's Ferry where the ale was thin and the fire was generous, and Rosalind was counting coins over a bowl of barley stew when the balladeer took the little stage by the hearth. She was small and quick-eyed, with a lute that had been mended so many times it looked like a quilt, and she opened with something about a miller's daughter and a river. The room went quiet the way rooms do when they don't know yet why they're listening.

Rosalind knew why.

It was the second song that did it — a lament for the flooded village of Ashcombe, drowned forty years ago when the dam broke above it. Rosalind had heard true memory-songs now for weeks. They were rough things. They caught and stumbled. A smuggler's coin hummed its owner's last morning in fragments, half-swallowed, like a man talking through a door. Maren's songs came out crooked, bent by eighty years of living, and that crookedness was how you knew they were real. Memory, sung honestly, always limped a little. It had been carried too far to walk straight.

Wren's Ashcombe lament didn't limp. It walked like a soldier on parade. Every verse landed exactly where it should, every grief was precisely proportioned, the mother's cry in the third verse arrived on schedule like a coach from the capital. It was beautiful. It was flawless.

And that was wrong. That was as wrong as a face with no pores, as bread with no crumbs, as a road with no stones turned at the edges.

Rosalind set down their spoon and listened harder, the way you'd look twice at a coin that seemed too bright.

* * *

"You're staring," Tam said. He'd learned to read lips across a table with unnerving accuracy, though he claimed it was just practice from years of watching foremen shout instructions he couldn't hear. "Either she's pretty or she's trouble."

"Both, probably."

"That's usually the same thing." He tore his bread in half and gave Rosalind the bigger piece, which was his way of saying he trusted their judgment even while mocking it.

On stage, Wren finished the lament and the room exhaled. An old drover wiped his eyes. A serving girl pressed a hand to her chest. Wren bowed, quick and bright, and her eyes swept the room once — not the way performers' eyes do, hunting for applause, but the way a courier's eyes do, checking exits. Counting doors. Noting who sat alone.

Rosalind had done that sweep ten thousand times. You did it when you carried things worth killing for. You did not do it when you sang about millers' daughters.

Afterward, Wren found their table without being invited, which was itself an answer to a question nobody had asked aloud. She dropped into the fourth chair with the ease of someone joining a family, set her lute against the wall, and stole a hunk of Tam's cheese.

"You two have a look about you," she said. "Road-worn but purposeful. I've been following your trail since Harrowgate, actually. Well — your trail and everyone else's. But yours smelled interesting."

"Smelled," Tam repeated flatly.

"Everyone smells of something. You smell of stone dust and secrets. Your friend here smells of candle wax and—" she paused, sniffed theatrically, "—oh, that's interesting. Old paper. Very old paper. And underneath it, something burnt."

Rosalind kept their face pleasant and empty, the courier's mask, the one that had gotten them past a hundred checkpoints. "You have a remarkable nose."

"I have a remarkable everything," Wren said cheerfully. "That's rather my problem. Do you know what a chorister is?"

Rosalind's spoon paused halfway to the bowl. Only for a heartbeat. But Wren caught it — of course she caught it; she caught everything — and her smile widened like a door opening onto a lit room.

"I thought so," she said softly. "I'm descended from them. Third generation, on my mother's side. My grandmother sang memories before the Guild went quiet. She taught me everything — how to find the song sleeping in a thing, how to wake it gentle, how to carry what isn't yours without letting it wear you." She leaned forward. "I heard you humming in the yard this morning. Behind the stable. You thought no one was listening."

"What did you hear?" Rosalind asked, because asking was safer than denying, and denial would only sharpen her interest.

"A coin," Wren said. "A smuggler's coin. A man's last morning — coffee, gulls, a woman laughing in another room. You hummed it back to him. Kind thing to do." Her expression flickered then, just briefly, into something almost wistful. "Nobody sings them back anymore. Everyone's afraid. I haven't been afraid in years, and lately I've been wondering if that's because I'm brave or because I've forgotten what there is to fear."

It was a strange thing to say. It was such a strange thing to say that Rosalind filed it away carefully, in the deep pocket where couriers keep the details that don't fit yet.

"We're not recruiting," Rosalind said.

"I wasn't offering to be recruited. I'm offering to travel together. There are crown agents on these roads asking after an unlicensed singer, and you're traveling with a deaf man who feels music through his boots, and I'd wager my lute you're headed somewhere the crown doesn't want you going. Three travelers draw less notice than two suspicious ones. Also—" she plucked a string, a soft minor note that hung in the smoky air, "—I know the passes. All of them. Even the ones that aren't on maps."

Tam signed something rapid to Rosalind, low, below the table's edge. *Ask her why she really wants to come.*

"Why do you really want to come?" Rosalind asked aloud.

Wren looked at them both for a long moment, and whatever moved behind her eyes, she chose to show none of it. "Because your songs are real," she said, "and mine are getting heavier every year, and I want to know why."

* * *

They let her come. Rosalind told themselves it was tactics — three travelers, local knowledge, a second pair of hands on watch. They told themselves that the Codex spoke of gathering voices, and a voice that could sing memory, even a polished one, might be needed before the end. They did not tell themselves the truth, which was that Wren was funny and warm and made camp feel less like hiding and more like home, and that Rosalind had been alone for so long that the arrival of charm felt like sunlight through a crack, and sunlight makes you forget to check what made the crack.

But the suspicion didn't die. It just got quieter, the way suspicions do around people you like.

It got quieter until the third night out from Marlow's Ferry, camped under a leaning rock in the foothills, when Rosalind woke to hear Wren singing very softly to herself across the dead fire.

It was the smuggler's coin. Rosalind's smuggler's coin — the one they'd taken from the dead man's cache in the pass, the one whose last morning Rosalind had hummed back to him behind the stable at the Drowned Goose. Wren was singing it note for note. Coffee, gulls, the woman laughing in the other room. Perfectly. Every fragment in its place, nothing limping, nothing stumbling.

Except Rosalind had never shown her the coin. Had never mentioned it. Had never hummed it anywhere within a mile of her — except once, behind a stable, which she claimed to have overheard.

Claimed.

Rosalind lay very still in their bedroll and did the arithmetic of fear. Either Wren had ears that could catch a half-hum through stable walls, which was possible. Or Wren already knew the song — knew it whole, knew it complete, knew it better than the coin itself did — which meant she hadn't overheard anything. She had recognized it. The way you recognize your own handwriting.

You cannot overhear yourself into knowing a stranger's memory perfectly. You can only already possess it.

Rosalind stared at the stars through the gap in the leaning rock and felt the warmth of three days of companionship go cold and careful, like milk left out overnight. Above them, far off toward the capital, the sky held a faint bruised glow that hadn't been there a month ago — the dragon-fire beacon, dimming. Somewhere under all that light, the Regent sat wearing a king, eating a country. And somewhere beside their own dying fire, a girl with a mended lute was singing a dead smuggler's morning like she'd been there.

* * *

In the morning, Rosalind found Tam splitting kindling and asked him, casually, to do the thing.

"The thing," Tam repeated.

"You know the thing."

"I enjoy making you say it." He brushed splinters off his hands. "You want me to listen to her songs with my feet."

"With your feet. Yes."

Tam had explained it once, awkwardly, over too much ale: sound was vibration, vibration was touch, and touch was honest in a way ears weren't. Ears could be fooled. Floors couldn't. When a true memory-song passed through stone or timber, it carried a texture — grain, he called it, like wood grain, irregular, specific, grown rather than built. He'd felt it in the chapel vault beneath Harrowgate, when he and Rosalind had sung open the guild's hidden archive: the old choristers' harmonies had a grain to them, deep and worn, like stairs hollowed by centuries of feet.

He crouched, pressed his palm flat against the ground near where Wren had slept, and waited. She was off filling waterskins at the stream, humming as she went — she hummed constantly, Rosalind realized, the way guilty people whistle.

Tam's brow furrowed. His hand stayed on the earth a long time. When he finally looked up, his face had gone still in the particular way it went still when he was choosing words carefully, because signing anger was easy but signing precision took effort.

"She sang last night," he said. "While we slept. Something sad. I felt it through the ground." He signed slowly, searching. "It was smooth."

"Smooth."

"Like river stone. Like glass." He shook his head. "Real songs have grain. Knots. Places where the singer's life bent the memory out of true. Hers has none. It's—" he hunted again, and found the word with visible reluctance, "—it's *made*. Like furniture. Beautiful furniture. Nothing in it grew. Everything in it was cut to fit."

Rosalind looked down the slope to the stream, where Wren stood in the shallows with her skirts kilted up, filling skins, her hair catching the early light, humming something bright and flawless to the water.

"Furniture," Rosalind said.

"Beautiful furniture," Tam agreed grimly. "And I've been sleeping next to it."

They didn't confront her. That was the courier's discipline: you never showed a mark you'd spotted a tail. You watched. You rerouted. You counted what she saw and what she didn't. So Rosalind smiled at breakfast and shared the cheese and let Wren take first watch, and spent the day cataloguing everything they'd told her — the Codex, yes, reluctantly, on the second night, because she'd earned trust the ordinary way, with jokes and good sense and a perfect memory for the passes. The Codex. She knew about the Codex.

She knew about the Choir. She knew about the coin. She knew the passes "even the ones that aren't on maps" — and who mapped the unmapped passes? Who but the crown's agents, the road-sealers, the hunters in Serle's employ?

And yet. And yet. Rosalind turned it over all day like a suspect coin, biting it, weighing it. Because there were other readings. Because Wren had cried — actually cried, badly, sniffling and embarrassed — at Tam's story of his wife, the records erased, the wedding ring he still wore on a cord around his neck because his fingers had grown too scarred for it. Liars could cry. But manufactured things, beautiful furniture, didn't usually weep at grief that wasn't theirs.

Unless it was theirs. Unless that was the whole point.

That evening, Rosalind made a decision that felt like closing a hand around a knife. They would test her. Not with questions — questions bounced off Wren like rain off oilcloth. With a song.

There was a memory Rosalind had never sung to anyone. A small one, worthless, private: the smell of the Guildhouse kitchen on a winter morning, cinnamon and tallow, and old Cook Hesta swearing at a kettle in three languages. Nobody alive knew it. It existed nowhere but in Rosalind's head and in the cracked flagstones of a ruined building in Thornfell. If Wren was what Rosalind feared, she couldn't possibly know it. If she sang it back — if it ever came out of her mouth with that terrible parade-ground perfection —

But that test would have to wait. Because that evening, as they made camp in the shadow of a ruined watchtower, Wren came back from scouting the road ahead moving fast, face pale, and threw down her pack.

"Crown riders," she said. "Six of them. Sealing the pass at the ford. Asking after three travelers — a singer, a stonemason, and a wine merchant." She looked at Rosalind, and her quick eyes were frightened, and her fright looked as real as anything Rosalind had ever seen. "They described us. By name, Rosalind. Yours and mine both."

Tam's head came up sharply. He read the shape of the words off her lips and his hands went still.

"They named me," Wren said, and laughed — a short, cracked, unlovely laugh, nothing like her stage voice. "I've been on these roads eight years and no rider has ever known my name. Isn't that funny? I finally find people worth traveling with, and the crown knows my name before I've told it to them."

She hugged the mended lute to her chest, and in the failing light her shadow fell long across the stones, and Rosalind thought: *furniture doesn't get scared.*

And then, immediately after: *unless it was built to.*

Above the eastern ridge, faint as breath on a mirror, the beacon-glow of the dying dragon pulsed once — dimmer than yesterday. And Wren, watching Rosalind's face rather than the sky, asked quietly:

"How much time do we have? Honestly. Whatever it is you're carrying — how long before it's too late?"

And Rosalind, who had lied professionally for twenty years, found they didn't want to lie to her. Which was, of course, the most dangerous thing of all.
