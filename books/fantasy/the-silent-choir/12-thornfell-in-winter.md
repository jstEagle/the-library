## 12. Thornfell in Winter

The roads closed behind them like a book snapping shut.

Rosalind heard it happen the way you hear a door in another room—not the sound itself, but the sudden quality of silence where a sound should be. For three days they had climbed through Thornfell's high passes, and for three days there had been traffic on the mountain road below them: carts, pilgrims, shepherds with their stubborn flocks, all of it the ordinary noise of a kingdom that still believed itself whole. Then, on the morning of the fourth day, the noise stopped.

They stood on a shelf of granite above the pass and watched it happen. Crown riders had appeared at both ends of the valley road—Rosalind counted eleven at the southern end, nine at the north—and where they stopped, the world stopped. A wool merchant was turned around mid-journey. A funeral procession was sent back the way it came, which struck Rosalind as either very thorough or very grim, depending on how much the Regent's agents understood about grief. Within an hour the only things moving on the roads were the riders themselves, small dark knots of purpose, sealing the arteries one by one.

"They're not hunting us," Tam said. He signed it more than said it, his big hands moving in the quick dialect he used when the wind made speech useless, and Rosalind read him the way she'd learned to read everything: closely, and with her whole body. "Not yet. They're closing the box first."

Wren, perched on a boulder with her cloak pulled up to her chin, looked delighted. Rosalind had learned that this was Wren's response to nearly everything—the woman treated catastrophe the way other people treated weather, as a change of costume. "A box," she repeated. "We're in a box. I've never been treasure before."

"You're not treasure," Rosalind said. "You're evidence."

"Even better."

Tam rumbled something low in his chest, a sound Rosalind felt through the stone under her boots rather than heard. His version of laughter. Then he pointed north, up-valley, where the pass narrowed into a white throat between two shoulders of mountain.

The snow had been threatening since dawn. Now it came.

* * *

It arrived the way blizzards do in the high country—not gradually but all at once, as if someone had tipped the sky. One moment Rosalind could see the ridge line; the next, the world was a gray-white churn in which three feet of visibility was a luxury and the wind spoke in a voice like tearing linen.

They moved by rope. Rosalind had insisted on it back in the mining town, over Wren's theatrical objections—"I have never once fallen off a mountain, I'll have you know"—and now she tied the three of them together with the flat, patient competence of someone who had spent her life making sure other people's cargo arrived. Tam in front, because his feet read stone the way her eyes did. Wren in the middle, because if anyone was going to wander off after something interesting, it would be Wren. Rosalind last, holding the rope and the map and, increasingly, the entire shape of the plan in her head.

They were two days from the chapel vault, maybe three in this. The Codex's route ran through the high passes precisely because the low roads were watched; Ede Voss had drawn it a century ago, and whatever else the guildmaster had been, he had clearly known his mountains. But even Ede's route assumed you could see your hand in front of your face, and the sky had withdrawn that assumption without consultation.

By late afternoon Rosalind called it. There was no calling it loudly—in this wind, shouting was a way of losing your voice—but she caught Tam's shoulder and made the flat chopping gesture that meant *shelter, now*, and he nodded once, gravely, as though she'd proposed something controversial, and began quartering the slope with his head tilted down.

That was the thing about Tam Broadhurst that people who weren't deaf would never understand: he was never lost. Sight lied in a blizzard. Sound was useless to him always. But vibration—the deep conversation of water under stone, of hollow space beneath rock, of wind changing its mind around an obstruction—vibration did not lie. Twenty minutes into the white roar, he stopped, crouched, laid both palms flat against a drift-crusted shelf of rock, and held them there for what felt like a full minute while the storm tried its best to kill them all.

Then he pointed.

Behind the shelf, half-swallowed by scree and old snow, was a crack in the mountainside. Not a cave mouth—a crack, narrow enough that Rosalind went in sideways, dragging their packs, with Wren complaining cheerfully about the indignity of it all. And then, six body-lengths in, the crack opened.

The chamber beyond was dry, and dark, and warm.

Warm. That was the word that snagged in Rosalind's mind like a fishhook, even as Tam was shaking the snow off himself like an enormous dog and Wren was already feeling along the walls for torch brackets. Caves in Thornfell's heights were cold as tombs. This one held a hearth—old, blackened, built against the far wall with proper masonry—and a flue that drew, because when Wren lit their single candle the flame leaned steadily toward a hairline crack overhead instead of guttering. Someone had built this. Someone had maintained it. Someone had wanted it found.

"Well," Wren said, in the tone of a woman discovering a secret room in a house she'd burgled for years. "Isn't *this* familiar-feeling."

Rosalind didn't answer, because she had stopped walking, and the reason she had stopped walking was the walls.

* * *

The candlelight reached them slowly, in layers.

First the hearth, and the stacked wood beside it—seasoned, covered in a century of dust but sound. Then the floor: flagstones, fitted, swept clean of rubble by hands long gone. Then the walls themselves, and Rosalind's breath went out of her all at once, quietly, the way air leaves a room when a door closes somewhere far away.

The walls were carved.

Not decorated. Not marked with the random graffiti of passing smugglers—initials, dates, crude saints. Carved the way a manuscript is written: dense, deliberate lines of figures covering every surface the candle could reach, spiraling up toward the ceiling crack and down into the floor seams, thousands upon thousands of them, and woven through the figures, unmistakable even in shifting light, ran images.

A girl on a rooftop, flying a kite made of red cloth.

A woman's hands kneading bread, flour to the wrists.

A boy—no, the same girl, older—standing in a doorway with a packed bag, while a figure in a Wayfarer's cloak turned away.

A lock gate. A river. A coin pressed into a child's palm.

Rosalind knew these things. Not the way you know a story. The way you know your own hands.

"That's my mother's kitchen," she said, before she could decide whether to say anything at all. Her voice came out strange, flattened, like a message passed through too many hands. "The window sat crooked. She never fixed it. She said the crook let the morning in at the right angle." She was walking along the wall now, the candle raised, and the words kept coming out of her unbidden, each carving unlocking the next. "That's the ferryman at Millbrook who let me ride free if I bailed the bilge. That's—I broke that plate. The blue one. I was seven. I hid the pieces in the garden and my mother pretended for a year that she hadn't noticed, and then one day there was a new blue plate on the table and neither of us ever said a word about it."

She stopped. Her own face looked back at her from the stone—or a version of it, a girl of perhaps twelve, rendered in shallow relief with an expression of ferocious concentration, holding a ledger. Learning the trade. Being taught to be invisible.

Tam had gone very still. He couldn't hear the tremor in her voice, but he could see her hands, and her hands had stopped pretending. Wren had, for once, stopped performing; she stood near the hearth with the candle, watching Rosalind with an expression that had nothing clever in it at all.

"How many?" Wren asked softly.

Rosalind counted, or tried to. "Hundreds. Thousands. It's—it's my whole childhood. Things I've never told anyone. Things I'd forgotten I remembered until right now." She touched the carving of the blue plate, and the stone was worn smooth there, polished by touch—her own touch? No. She had never been here. She was certain she had never been here. "Someone sat in this cave and cut my life into rock. Cut. Not chiseled sketches—look at the tooling. Years of work. Decades, maybe."

"Someone who loved you," Wren said.

"Or someone who was paid very well," Rosalind said, because the alternative was a lump in her throat, and she was a courier, and couriers delivered things; they did not become them.

But her hand stayed on the stone.

* * *

They lit the hearth. The wood burned clean, and the warmth filled the chamber, and Tam cooked a plain meal of oats and dried mutton with the unhurried gravity he brought to everything, and none of them talked much, because the walls were talking enough.

It was Tam, later, who found the signature.

He had spent the evening doing what he always did in new places: reading the stone. He walked the chamber's perimeter with his palms against the carvings, and Rosalind watched him slow, and stop, and go back, and press his hand flat over a section near the hearth where the figures changed character—became smaller, denser, set in rows.

He beckoned her over and tapped the stone twice, then traced a line of glyphs and looked at her expectantly.

Rosalind leaned close. The rows were text—an old script, the kind the Guildhouse archives had taught her to half-read, the kind that appeared in the Ash Codex's margins. And beneath the rows, cut deeper than anything else in the cave, was a name.

**E.V.**

Her stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

Ede Voss. Guildmaster of the Wayfarers. Author of the Ash Codex. The man who had walked into the palace a hundred years ago and never come out. The man whose handwriting was currently folded against her ribs in oilcloth, whose prophecy had named her conductor of the Silent Choir, whose route she was following through these exact mountains.

"He wrote this," she said. "He stood where I'm standing. Before the palace. Before—" Before whatever had happened to him. Before the crown. Before the thing wearing King Aldous had begun eating the kingdom's memory one life at a time.

"Why?" Wren asked, and it was the right question, asked plainly. Why would a guildmaster a century dead spend decades carving a child's childhood into a cave? Rosalind wasn't born yet. Her mother wasn't born yet. Half the memories on these walls hadn't happened when Ede Voss vanished into the palace.

Unless.

Rosalind turned in a slow circle, candle raised, and looked at the walls again—not at the images this time, but at the order of them. They spiraled inward, oldest at the outer wall, newest near the hearth. And near the hearth, in Ede's dense rows of text, wrapping around the name like a frame, was a passage longer than any other. She knelt in front of it, took out the little reading-glass she carried for exactly such purposes, and began, line by line, to translate.

It took her an hour. Tam banked the fire. Wren slept, or pretended to, curled near the warmth with her back to the wall—though Rosalind noticed, and filed away, that Wren's breathing didn't change rhythm for a long time. Listening.

The passage, when she finally had it, was short. It was addressed to no one, or to everyone, or possibly to her specifically across a hundred years of stone, and it read:

*The Regent eats what remembers you. Therefore the Choir must be grown in the dark. Let the conductors' line be kept from every record, every register, every grateful heart. Let them be raised unseen, unnamed in any book, so that when the crown comes looking, it finds no thread to pull. What must survive, carve in stone where only stone remembers. If you are reading this, then the hiding worked, and I am sorry for it, and it was necessary, and one day you will understand why a person must be erased in order to be saved.*

Rosalind sat back on her heels.

The fire cracked. Wind screamed at the cave mouth, faint and far away, like something happening in another country. Tam watched her face with the particular attention of a man who had spent his life reading what people didn't say.

"Invisible," she said at last. "I thought—I always thought the invisibility was just what happened to Wayfarers. The trade. You carry contraband and secrets, you learn not to be noticed. I thought I was good at it." She laughed, one dry note with no humor in it. "I wasn't good at it. I was *made*. My whole life—the guildmasters who never quite learned my name, the clients who paid me and forgot me, the way innkeepers would look straight through me even when I was paying them—Maren warned me the Regent eats what remembers you, and I thought she meant it as a threat. It was a *shield*. Someone built me to be unmemorable. Deliberately. From birth."

"To protect you," Wren said, from her bedroll, her voice careful.

"To *use* me," Rosalind said, but it came out uncertain, and she heard the uncertainty, and hated it. "Or both. Both. The Codex names me conductor. The Codex also says the conductor has to be a person the crown can't find. So they raised me in a blind spot. My mother—" and here her voice did catch, briefly, because the carving of her mother's hands was ten feet away, flour to the wrists, "—my mother knew. Didn't she. All those years of never fixing the crooked window, of keeping our lives off paper, of moving us twice without explanation. She wasn't scatterbrained. She was *following instructions*. Keeping me carvable and unfindable."

Tam signed something, slowly, and Rosalind translated it for herself: *Does it change what you carry?*

She considered that for a long time. The honest answer was yes, and the truer answer was no. She was angry—she could feel it sitting in her chest like a swallowed coal, anger at Ede, at Maren, at the guild, at a hundred years of dead people who had decided the shape of her life without asking. And underneath the anger, in the place where understanding eventually grows if you water it, was the image of the blue plate. Of a mother who had hidden the broken pieces and said nothing, for a year, so that her daughter would never have to feel the weight of having broken something. That was what this was. That was what all of it was. A kingdom-sized version of the blue plate: *we will hide the cost so you can grow up whole, and you may hate us later, and we will accept that, because later is a thing we are trying to make sure you get to have.*

"I don't know yet," she answered Tam honestly. "Ask me at the palace."

* * *

The blizzard held them for two days.

They might have chafed at it—Rosalind had a schedule folded inside the Codex route, and schedules were her religion—but the cave made chafing difficult. It was warm, and safe, and full of her own life written in stone, and she found that she could not stop reading it. While Tam practiced his vibration-reading against the walls (the carvings, he reported with visible wonder, *hummed*—faintly, structurally, the way a bell hums before it rings; Ede had cut them at depths that resonated) and Wren napped with theatrical abandon and sang snatches of ballads to the ceiling, Rosalind walked the spiral inward, decade by decade, meeting herself.

She met the girl with the kite. She met the apprentice learning locks. She met, near the innermost wall, a carving that stopped her cold: a young woman standing at a crossroads under a signpost, and the signpost bore no names. Just four blank boards, pointing four ways.

Beneath it, in Ede's tight script, a single line:

*Here the record ends. What she chooses is hers. Even stone may only witness.*

She put her palm against it and left it there a long while.

On the second night, the wind dropped. By dawn the passes lay silent and blue-white and treacherous-beautiful, and the road below remained sealed—crown riders still camped at the valley mouths, patient as ticks. But Ede's route had never used the valley road. High above the sealed passes, half-buried in the fresh snow, Rosalind found what she had been told to look for: a cairn, and beside it, cut into living rock where no agent would think to police it, a Wayfarer's mark and an arrow pointing up and onward, toward the ridge line and the chapel vault beyond.

Ede's road. Still open. Still waiting, a century on, for the people it was cut for.

They broke camp. At the cave mouth Rosalind paused, once, and looked back at the walls of her own life glowing in the last of the firelight, and did the hardest thing a courier learns to do, which is leave the delivery unopened and keep moving.

But as they climbed out into the blinding morning, Wren—who had been uncharacteristically quiet since the cave, who had barely sung at all, which those who knew her would recognize as a warning sign akin to silence from a river—turned around on the threshold and said, lightly, too lightly:

"You know what strikes me? Whoever carved all this knew things about you that haven't happened yet. Your crossroads. Your choices." She squinted up at the ridgeline, at the arrow of the Wayfarer's mark gleaming under new snow. "Almost like the man could see down the road. Almost like prophecy." A beat, and then, breezy as ever: "Well. No use standing in a doorway. Roads don't shorten while you look at them."

And she started climbing, humming again, some bright tuneless thing, while Rosalind stood a moment in the snow with a question taking shape in her mind—slow, unwelcome, and cold as the wind coming off the ridge:

*How does Wren know what the carvings said?*

She had translated Ede's passage alone, by candlelight, while Wren had lain with her back turned, ostensibly asleep. Tam couldn't read the script at all. Nobody had discussed it aloud beyond the few fragments Rosalind had spoken. And yet Wren had just paraphrased, almost exactly, the sense of the final line—*what she chooses is hers*—a line Rosalind had never voiced to anyone.

Perhaps it was nothing. Perhaps Wren had simply woken, and watched, and pieced it together the way clever people do.

Or perhaps—Rosalind felt the thought settle over her like the snow settling on the pass—there were things about the charming balladeer with the too-polished songs that the mountains had just begun to whisper, and the whisper said: *listen closer.*

She caught up to Tam at the cairn, and they climbed.
