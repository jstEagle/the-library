## 13. Ede's Cave

The blizzard found them an hour past the cairn, the way bad weather always finds travelers—suddenly, and with a certain personal spite.

"We should have stopped at the waystation," Wren shouted over the wind. Her voice carried beautifully even through a storm, which Rosalind had come to suspect was less a talent than a habit of manufacture.

"The waystation is full of crown men," Rosalind shouted back. "Crown men with lists."

"Ah yes. The list." Wren ducked her chin into her scarf. "The famous list of unlicensed singers. I do wonder what they've written about me."

"Nothing," Tam said. It was the longest sentence he'd offered all day. "You're not on any list. You're not on anything."

Rosalind glanced back at him. He was walking with his head down, gloved hand trailing along the rock face as he always did in bad visibility—not for balance, but for reading. Tam read stone the way she read roads: through his palms, through the soles of his feet, through some private grammar of vibration that had kept him alive in collapsing mineshafts and, more recently, alive around Wren.

"What do you mean?" she asked.

He lifted his hand from the rock. Held it there, palm flat against the wind-scoured granite, like a man greeting an old horse.

"There's hollow space behind this wall," he said. "Big one. And the stone's been cut. Not broken. *Cut.* Somebody wanted this found."

* * *

The entrance was a seam in the cliff, half-hidden by an overhang furred with ice, exactly the sort of door that only a person who already knew it was there would ever find. Which raised the question Rosalind didn't say aloud: how had Tam known to trail his hand along *this* stretch of mountain, out of all the miles of mountain behind them?

She filed the question away with the others—the too-polished songs, the way Wren never stumbled on verses she claimed never to have heard—and stepped inside.

Warmth struck them first. Not fire-warmth, but something older: the deep, patient warmth of stone that had decided long ago to stop being cold. Then light, faint and amber, rising from somewhere below. And then the smell—wax and iron and something sweetly rotten, like apples left too long in a cellar.

"Oh," said Wren softly. "Oh, this is old."

They descended a stair cut in spirals so narrow that Tam had to turn sideways, his shoulders brushing walls that hummed faintly under his fingertips. Rosalind watched his face as he went. She'd learned to read him the way he read stone. Right now his expression was the one he wore when a song was true.

"It's singing," he murmured. "Barely. Like something asleep and dreaming."

At the bottom, the stair opened into a cavern the size of a chapel, lit by veins of amber crystal threading the ceiling like frozen honey. And the walls—

The walls were covered in carvings.

Not decoration. Not the abstract spirals and lucky knots that miners scratched to pass the time. These were scenes. Figures. Words, hundreds of words, chiseled in a flowing script that wound between the images like a river between banks.

Rosalind stood very still in the amber light and felt the hair rise on her arms, because she recognized the script. She'd spent two nights hunched over it by candlelight, cross-referencing letter-forms until her eyes burned.

It was the hand of the Ash Codex.

* * *

Tam walked the cavern slowly, trailing his fingers along the carved lines, and every few paces he made a small sound—not quite words, more like a man tasting something. Wren, meanwhile, had drifted to the far wall and was reading aloud in a low, lovely murmur, translating the archaic script with a fluency that made Rosalind's teeth ache.

"'...and the crown was not made to be worn,'" Wren read, "'but to be *answered.*'"

"Don't," Rosalind said.

Wren looked up, wounded. "Don't what?"

"Don't translate it like that. Like you've read it before."

"I haven't." A beat. "It's just a clear hand. Easier than most old scripts."

"It's easier because the Codex uses the same letterforms," Rosalind said, keeping her voice level, almost gentle. "Which means whoever wrote this wrote the Codex. Which we knew. What I don't understand is why it comes off your tongue like a nursery rhyme."

Wren held her gaze for a moment. Then she smiled—that bright, disarming smile that could fill a tavern—and turned back to the wall, and said nothing at all, which was itself an answer, though Rosalind couldn't yet read it.

Tam saved them. He always seemed to arrive at the exact moment a silence curdled, tapping the wall twice. "This figure," he said. "Here. With the torch. It repeats. Every panel. Same face, same coat, hands always doing something—writing, hiding, carrying." He traced the outline. "Someone important."

Rosalind joined him. The figure was tall and narrow-faced, rendered with the loving precision of someone who had known their subject well. In panel after panel it moved through the story: standing before a crowned shape that might have been a throne or might have been a mouth; pressing a book to its chest; leading a line of smaller figures—seven of them, always seven—through darkness.

Below the first appearance of the figure, two words were cut deeper than the rest, worn smooth by centuries of maybe-one-visitor's fingertips.

Ede Voss.

"My family name," Wren said lightly, from across the room.

"No," Tam said, without turning. "It isn't."

Rosalind crossed to him. Beneath the name ran a longer inscription, and she knelt in the amber light and began the slow work of decipherment, sounding out the archaic forms the way Maren had taught her to sound out lock-mechanisms—by feel, by patience, by trusting the parts she knew to carry her through the parts she didn't.

And the wall began to talk.

* * *

*We were carriers,* the wall said, in Ede Voss's careful, urgent voice. *Before we were couriers of goods, we carried the heavier freight. Memory. The kingdom's memory, when the kingdom could no longer hold its own.*

*A dragon sang beneath the palace, and the song was the remembering, and the crown was tuned to the song, and the king who wore the crown was meant to be the song's servant, not its master.*

*I thought otherwise. Write it plainly, since no one else will: I, Ede Voss, guildmaster of the Wayfarers, thought I could wear the crown and keep my self. I walked into the palace with my book under my arm and my name in my mouth, and I intended to conduct the dragon myself.*

*I was wrong. Understand this also, plainly: whatever wears the crown now was once me. Do not mourn it. Unmake it.*

*But hear the rest, because the rest is why you are reading this.*

*Before I went, I hid the conductor's line. The gift does not fall evenly—it runs in blood, thinning and thickening, and the crown will hunt it forever, because a conductor is the one thing the crown cannot digest. Seven voices may be taken. Bought. Made, even—I know what can be made; I am what was made. But the eighth, the one who holds the key and turns it, must be born and hidden and raised without ever knowing what they carry.*

*So I scattered them. I gave the line a plain name and a plainer trade. I taught the Guild to make them invisible—not disgraced, not hunted, simply unseen, passed over, forgotten by everyone except the one elder in each generation who keeps the ledger. If you have lived your whole life unnoticed, unremarked, slipping through doors with parcels no one asks about—if the world has treated you as furniture—then I am sorry. It was the kindest weapon I had.*

*If you are reading this, you are a Winterbourne. You were never invisible by accident. You were hidden. There is a difference, and I pray you never have to learn it the hard way.*

*Rosalind.*

Rosalind stopped reading aloud.

She hadn't realized she'd been reading aloud. Her voice hung in the cavern air, thin and strange, and both Tam and Wren were looking at her, and the last word she'd spoken was still dissolving between them like breath in cold air.

"That's not in the carving," Wren said quietly. All the polish had gone out of her voice. "I've been following your finger. That word isn't there."

"It is," Rosalind said. "Look again."

They looked. The letters were there—she could feel them under her fingers, could see them in the amber light, cut slightly deeper than the surrounding text, as if the chisel had pressed harder. As if the writer had paused over them a long time.

But when Tam touched the wall, he frowned. "That line's smooth," he said. "No cut marks. It's warm, though." His brow furrowed deeper. "It's *singing*, Ros. Right here. One word, over and over."

The cavern seemed to lean in.

"Say it again," Tam said.

Rosalind looked at her own name cut—or sung, or dreamed—into a wall a hundred years dead, and found she couldn't. Her throat had closed around it. So Tam reached out and took her hand and pressed it flat against the stone beside his own, and through her palm she felt it: a pulse, faint and rhythmic, like a heart remembering how.

Her name. Being kept warm. For a century.

By somebody who had known, a hundred years before her birth, exactly what she would need to find.

* * *

She didn't remember sitting down. She was on the floor of the cave with her back against a carved panel depicting, she later noted bitterly, a woman being lied to by smiling people, and the anger arrived all at once, whole, like weather.

"They knew." Her voice came out flat and dangerous. "Maren knew. The Guild knew. Every lockkeeper, every station-master, every gray-haired courier who patted my head and sent me out with contraband candles—they *knew* what I was, and they let me think I was nothing. They let me spend twenty years being furniture. On purpose. Because some dead man decided my life was a hiding place."

Tam lowered himself to the floor beside her with the unhurried care of a big man who has learned to take up space gently. He didn't say anything. He was good at that.

"They gave me the worst routes," she went on. "The coldest passes. The clients nobody else wanted. And I thought—" She laughed, and it came out wrong. "I thought I'd earned it. I thought I was just the one nobody liked. I built my whole self on top of a lie and I *thanked* them for the work."

"You weren't the one nobody liked," Tam said.

"How would you know?"

"Because I was." He said it simply, the way he said everything. "Deaf boy in a mining town. You get to know what it feels like when people decide you're furniture." He nodded at the wall. "Difference is, mine was an accident. Yours was architecture. That's worse. But Ros—" He tapped the panel behind them, right where the carved woman stood. "Read this bit again."

She read it. *There is a difference, and I pray you never have to learn it the hard way.*

"He knew it was cruel," Tam said. "He wrote it down. He didn't dress it up as kindness or destiny. He said *I am sorry* and *it was the kindest weapon I had* and he left it where you'd find it yourself, in your own time, instead of having some elder tell you when you were twelve and too young to be angry properly." He shrugged. "Men who lie to your face build statues. This one built an apology and hid it in a mountain and waited a hundred years for you to be ready."

"That's not forgiveness."

"No. It's just true. Both things can be."

From across the cavern, Wren spoke without turning from the wall. "My family name is in the Codex," she said. "Voss. I told you that the night we met. I told you my grandmother sang, and her grandmother before her, and that we kept the songs clean." She turned. In the amber light her face looked younger and much less certain. "I believed it when I said it. That's the part that frightens me. I believed my own history like it was a memory." Her jaw tightened. "And now there's a wall saying the Voss line ends in the thing wearing the crown, and I don't know anymore which parts of me were written and which parts grew."

Rosalind studied her for a long moment. The paranoia was still there—it would probably always be there—but threaded through it now was something else, something uncomfortable and warm: recognition. Two women, standing in a dead man's confession, each discovering that the story of herself had an author.

"Then we find out which parts grow," Rosalind said. "Together. That's the whole trick of it, apparently. Nobody's memory survives alone."

Wren blinked. "Did you just forgive me?"

"No. I'm agreeing to keep being suspicious of you in a warmer tone."

A surprised laugh escaped Wren—unguarded, unpolished, and therefore the most real sound Rosalind had ever heard her make—and Tam smiled down at the floor, and for a moment the cave held three people instead of a spy, a deaf mason, and a lie that had been given legs and a name.

* * *

They worked through the panels until the amber light dimmed—which, Tam reported with some unease, happened in rhythm, like breathing—and Rosalind transcribed what mattered onto the flyleaves of her own notebook in her own quick courier's shorthand.

The conductor's line. The seven voices. The eighth who turns the key. The warning that the crown cannot digest what is freely given—a phrase she underlined twice, feeling her way toward something without yet seeing its shape. And the route: a final panel showing the mountain paths descending toward the capital, with a mark at the palace's root that could only be one thing.

The dragon.

"She's dying," Tam said quietly, when Rosalind explained it. "The singer under the city. I've felt her for years, you know. Faintest thread, down deep, like a bell rung once a century. Thought it was my imagination." He flexed his hands. "Lately the thread's gotten thinner."

Outside, the storm moaned against the entrance. Inside, Rosalind sat with a dead ancestor's apology warming under her palm and tried to assemble forgiveness out of anger, the way you try to build a fire in wet wood—one dry finding at a time.

They had hidden her to save her. They had hidden her so thoroughly that she'd grown up believing she was worth hiding *from*, not *for*. Both things were true. Tam was right; both things could be. She could be furious at Maren Ostwick and grateful to her in the same breath, could curse the Guild's lies and owe it her life, and the contradiction wouldn't kill her. Apparently that was what inheritance was: a bundle of debts you didn't sign for, carried anyway.

"When we get down from this mountain," she said at last, "I'm going to find Maren, and I'm going to yell at her for approximately one hour."

"She'll like that," Tam said. "Old ones always like getting yelled at. Proves they're still relevant."

"—and then," Rosalind went on, "I'm going to ask her to teach me the conducting. All of it. Everything she kept back." She looked at her name glowing faintly in the stone. "Because if I was raised for this, then fine. I'll do it. But I'll do it knowing. That's the one thing they never gave me, and it's the one thing I refuse to go without."

Wren had drifted to the final panel again. Now she made a small sound.

"This mark," she said. "Under the palace. It's not just drawn. It's dated." She traced the numerals, and her finger slowed, and when she spoke again her voice had gone carefully, professionally neutral—the voice of a performer who has spotted trouble in the audience. "There's a second date beside it. Smaller. Added later, different chisel-work."

"And?"

"And it's a countdown, Ros. Or a schedule." She looked up. "It says the song fails at midwinter."

Rosalind did the arithmetic she had already begun doing in the back of her mind since the pass, since the cairn, since the whisper of *listen closer*. Midwinter was nineteen days away.

Above them, muffled by a hundred feet of stone and a screaming storm, the mountain seemed suddenly very quiet—as quiet, she thought, as a held breath, as a singer gathering what strength remained.

Nineteen days to reach the capital. Nineteen days to gather seven voices, or eight, out of a kingdom that had forgotten how to listen.

"Nineteen days," she said, "and a choir to raise. Well." She closed her notebook and stood, and her knees cracked like the ice on the eaves, and she found, to her surprise, that she was almost smiling. "At least the Guild trained me to travel fast."

Tam rose beside her, and Wren after him, and the three of them climbed back toward the storm with a dead man's confession folded in paper and a living woman's name still humming in the stone behind them, keeping warm.

Behind them, in the empty amber dark, the last panel waited unread—the small one, tucked in the corner, easy to miss. It showed a single figure walking into a palace, book under arm. Beneath it, in letters so shallow they were nearly a wish:

*If I could not return, let someone sing me home.*
