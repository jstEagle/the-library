## 14. The Guttering Flame

The pass above Thornfell had a name once. Rosalind was fairly sure of it — something with *crow* in it, or maybe *crag* — but the map they'd memorized twenty years ago had called it nothing at all, which was the Wayfarers' way of saying that nobody who drew maps had ever bothered to come up here and freeze.

They came up here anyway, the three of them, because the high road was the only road left. Down in the valleys, the Regent's agents were sealing the passes one by one, chaining gates that hadn't been chained since Rosalind's grandmother's day, posting men with forgettable faces at every bridge. Serle's work, most likely, or Serle's kind. But the high road ran along the spine of the mountains where the snow didn't so much fall as simply *live*, permanently, the way moss lives on a roof, and no agent of any crown wanted to die up there for a salary paid in coin that might not be remembered by morning.

So they climbed. And on the third evening, as the light went long and gold across the western peaks, Rosalind crested the last shoulder of the ridge, looked down at the capital far below —

— and stopped walking so abruptly that Tam walked into their back and Wren walked into Tam's, which was how the three of them ended up standing in a small confused pile at the top of the world, staring south.

"What," said Wren. "What is it. You've gone the color of old porridge."

Rosalind didn't answer. They couldn't. Their eyes were fixed on the palace, or rather on the thing above the palace: the beacon tower, the great bronze brazier that crowned the highest spire like a candle-flame crowning a cake. The dragon-fire beacon. It had burned every night of recorded history. It burned through plagues and sieges and the Long Dark Winter that even Maren only half-remembered. Sailors navigated by it. Farmers set their clocks by it. Children in the capital were told it would never go out, the way children everywhere are told things that turn out to be lies, though usually not within their own lifetimes.

It was dimming.

Not out. Not yet. But where it should have been a spear of white-gold fire visible from thirty miles, it was a smudge, an ember behind smoked glass, a coal someone had forgotten to feed. Even at this distance Rosalind could see the light stutter — flare, gutter, flare again — like a heartbeat losing its rhythm.

"Oh," said Wren softly. She had seen it too. For once she had nothing polished to say about it.

Tam made a low sound in his chest and put his palm flat against the rock of the ridge. He stood that way a long moment, head bowed, big shoulders still, reading the mountain the way other people read letters. When he straightened, his hands were shaking, and he signed something quick and urgent before he seemed to remember that neither of them could follow him at speed, and settled instead for two slow, deliberate signs:

*Deep. Wrong.*

"The dragon," Rosalind said. The word came out of them flat and strange, like a coin dropped on stone. "Under the palace. It's dying."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the ridge, because there was nowhere else to make camp and because none of them, having seen the beacon gutter, felt much like walking in the dark. Rosalind got a small fire going — ordinary wood-fire, humble, honest — and they sat around it passing a bag of dried apricots back and forth, which was the closest thing to a council of war the Choir had yet held.

"Explain it to me properly," Wren said. "You've been carrying this around since the Codex. The dragon, the crown, all of it. I've heard you say pieces. I want the whole shape of it."

Rosalind turned an apricot over in their fingers. "All right. The crown runs on dragon-fire. That's not a metaphor — there's a dragon under the palace, has been for a century at least, maybe longer. Its fire is what lets the Regent do what it does. Memory isn't just a thing in your head, it's a *flame*, a living flame, and the dragon is the hearth the whole kingdom's memory burns on. Ashvane, the Codex calls it. The Codex says Ashvane has been singing the kingdom's memory alone for a hundred years." They paused. "One voice. Holding everything. Every harvest song, every wedding, every name. Alone, for a century."

"That would tire anyone," Tam said quietly. He spoke rarely and each sentence arrived like furniture being set down carefully.

"And when the singer tires," Rosalind said, "the song falters. And the beacon—" they nodded southward, toward the smudge of light "—is just the part we can see. The flame up top is the overflow. The excess. What spills out of a full cup. If the beacon's guttering—"

"Then the cup is nearly empty," Wren finished.

"Then the anchor is failing. The Regent lives in the crown, and the crown lives on the dragon's fire, and if the fire goes out—" Rosalind shrugged, and hated how casual the shrug sounded. "I don't know. Maybe the Regent dies with it. Maybe it tears free and takes whatever it can carry. Either way, a starving thing gets desperate, and a desperate thing with teeth doesn't wait politely."

Tam was looking south too, his face lit orange on one side. He said something then that Rosalind thought about for a long time afterward.

"Then we are not chasing it anymore," he said. "It is running toward us. Or we are both running toward the same door."

"Yes," Rosalind said. "The clock just got loud."

* * *

Sleep did not come easily after that, which was perhaps why Rosalind was awake to see Wren slip away from the campfire an hour past midnight, moving quiet as spilled water toward the southern edge of the ridge.

Rosalind told themselves they were only stretching their legs.

The truth — and Rosalind was, by trade and temperament, a person who dealt in truths however inconveniently packaged — was that they followed her. They kept twenty paces back and downwind and told themselves stories about checking the perimeter, and watched Wren stand alone at the ridge's edge with the guttering beacon reflected tiny in each of her eyes, and saw her raise one hand toward it. Not reaching. Something more like a salute. Or an apology.

And they saw her lips move.

She wasn't singing. It was barely a whisper, less than a whisper, breath shaped into words without voice behind them. But the wind off the southern peaks carried strange things, and Rosalind had spent a lifetime learning to catch what the wind offered, and three words reached them clear as bells across the snow:

"— I'm trying —"

Wren stood there another moment, hand still raised, and then her shoulders did something complicated and sad, and she turned back toward camp.

Rosalind stepped sideways behind a boulder with the smooth professionalism of two decades of not-being-seen, and stood in the cold with their heart going like a drummer boy's, and thought: *trying what? Trying to lead us astray, as some part of her was built to do? Trying to warn us? Trying to warn* it?

Or — and this was the thought that lodged under Rosalind's ribs like a fishhook — *trying to be real?*

Because here was the thing Rosalind had been turning over for days now, the pebble in the boot of their mind: Wren's memory-songs were manufactured. Tam had confirmed it, feeling the seams in them the way a tailor feels machine stitching. But manufactured songs shouldn't have *grieved* the way Wren grieved sometimes, at odd moments, over nothing — over a burnt loaf of bread in a wayside inn, over a dead bird on the path, small griefs with no history attached, like a person mourning a life she'd never actually lived. Manufactured things didn't ache. Rosalind knew that better than anyone. You couldn't fake the ache. You could fake everything else.

Wren passed close to the boulder on her way back. She paused. Of course she paused; she noticed everything, that was the trouble with her.

"You're terrible at following people," she said pleasantly, into the dark. "For a Wayfarer."

"I wasn't following you."

"Mm." A pause. Snow ticked down through the silence between them. Then, lighter than air, in the voice she used like a shield: "Beautiful night for it, anyway. The beacon. Have you seen it? Practically romantic. Two dying lights on a mountaintop."

"Wren."

"Goodnight, Rosalind." And she was gone, footsteps soft on the snow, leaving Rosalind alone with the wind and the fishhook under their ribs and the enormous, flickering question of what exactly was sleeping inside their charming, lying, grieving friend.

* * *

Morning came gray and businesslike, and with it came proof that Tam had been right: the door was rushing toward them.

They heard it before they saw it — a sound rolling up the valley from the south, deep and rhythmic, wrong for weather. Tam felt it first, naturally. He was on his feet before either of them, hand pressed to the frozen ground, and his expression when he lifted his head was one Rosalind had never seen on him: fear without surprise, the fear of a man who has felt a thing coming for days and is almost relieved it's arrived.

"Horses," he said. "Many. Below the north col."

Through the thin morning mist they watched them come: a column of riders winding up the switchbacks of the far slope, maybe forty strong, banners snapping — and the banners were wrong, blank gray cloth with no sigil at all, which was somehow worse than any sigil could have been. An army whose emblem was forgetting.

"They're not coming for us specifically," Rosalind murmured. "Forty riders don't get sent after three travelers."

"No," Wren agreed. Her eyes had gone sharp and unreadable. "They're sealing the pass. Last road out of the mountains. Whatever the Regent's doing down in the city, it wants the mountains closed while it does it." She glanced at Rosalind. "Which means it knows the dragon's failing. Which means it's scared."

"Good," said Tam.

Both of them looked at him.

"Scared things make mistakes," he said, with the ghost of something that on another man would have been a smile. "My wife used to say it. Scared things make mistakes, and patient things see them." His hands stilled. "She said many useful things. I am finding I remember them better lately. Louder." He frowned slightly, as if puzzled by his own heart, and Rosalind felt the fishhook twist — because *louder* meant the songs were rising in him, meant the whole kingdom's memory was getting louder as the dragon's grip loosened, meant the flood was coming for all of them whether they sang or drowned.

"We can't go north," Rosalind said, thinking aloud, watching the riders reach the col and begin, methodically, to build a gate. "We can't go back south the way we came. So we go *through*." They pointed east, where the ridge line dipped into a chaos of broken country the maps refused to name — scree fields, icefalls, the high haunted shelf of stone where, two nights ago, half-delirious with cold, Rosalind could have sworn they'd seen carvings in a cave wall. Carvings that had looked, in the blue hour before sleep took them, almost like handwriting.

"There's nothing east," Wren said. "There's never been anything east. That's why it's called nothing east."

"There's a cave."

"You saw a cave in a blizzard."

"I saw *carvings*, Wren. In a wall. Deliberate ones. Someone cut them there, and nobody cuts anything in the middle of the Thornfell wastes unless they mean it to be found by exactly the sort of person who ends up in the middle of the Thornfell wastes." Rosalind shouldered their pack. "Someone left a message in the one place the Regent's riders will never bother to seal. Call it a hunch. Call it the guild blood in me. Either way, the road through the nothing is the only road left, and my hunches have kept me fed for twenty years."

Wren held their gaze a moment longer than was comfortable. Then she smiled her market-square smile and swept an arm toward the eastern crags like a herald announcing a festival. "After you, conductor."

But as they turned east, all three of them — Rosalind, Tam, Wren — looked back once, together, at the beacon.

It had dimmed again overnight. In the gray morning light it was barely visible at all now, a bruise of orange against the pale sky, pulsing slow. Slow. Slower.

And beneath their feet, faint but unmistakable, coming up through the soles of their boots and the bones of their ankles, Tam stopped walking and pressed his palm to the stone one last time.

"It changed its rhythm," he said quietly. "The old song. The tired one." He looked up, and his eyes were wet, and his voice was steady. "It is not singing to hold the kingdom anymore. It is counting."

"Counting what?" Wren asked.

But Rosalind already knew. Anyone who had ever sat up all night with a dying fire knew. The dragon beneath the palace had stopped tending the flame and started measuring what remained of it — the way you count the logs left in the woodpile, the way you count the hours left in the night, the way you count, in the end, the people who might still arrive in time.

"How long?" Rosalind asked.

Tam listened with his whole body, head bowed, hand spread on the mountain's cold skin. When he answered, he gave them a number, and the number was small enough to fit in a closed fist, and none of them spoke again for a very long time.

They walked east, into the unnamed country, toward the cave with the carvings — and behind them, far to the south, the beacon of Thornfell guttered once, twice, and steadied, the way a candle steadies when the person watching it finally understands that soon there will be nothing left to watch.
