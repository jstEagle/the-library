## 15. Wren's True Verse

The shepherd's hut sat in the crook of the pass like an old man leaning on his stick, and it was the first roof they'd seen in three days. Rosalind got the fire going while Tam wedged the door shut with a stone that had probably been doing the job since before either of them was born. Wren sat on the bench by the window with her lute across her knees and did not play it.

That was the first wrong thing.

Wren always played her lute. She played it while walking, which Rosalind had privately considered a minor miracle of balance; she played it while eating, plucking a bass line under her chin between bites; she had once played it while arguing with Tam about whether a song could be *too* cheerful, and won, by being too cheerful to argue with. The lute was as much a part of Wren as her laugh — and tonight she held it like something she'd been handed at a funeral and didn't know where to put down.

"Soup," Rosalind announced, because soup was a thing you could announce, and because announcing things kept the silence from getting ideas. "Barley, salt pork, and one truly heroic onion I found living wild in my pack."

"Smuggler's onion," Tam said, settling onto the floor with his back against the wall where he could feel the fire's rumble through the stones. He signed as he spoke, his hands quick even when his voice came out rough from disuse. *Best kind. Grows mean.*

They ate. The wind worried the thatch. Wren pushed her barley around the bowl in patterns that were almost musical and entirely appetiteless, and Rosalind watched her do it, and thought about the thing Tam had told her that morning, out on the ridge while Wren scouted ahead.

*Her songs don't hum,* he'd said, hands moving against the grey sky. *Real memory-songs are rough. They've got burrs in them. Hers are smooth all the way through, like river stones. Like somebody made them on purpose.* He'd looked at Rosalind then with those steady eyes. *Nobody remembers anything that smoothly.*

Rosalind had spent the whole day turning that over, and now, watching Wren arrange barley into a treble clef and not eat it, she decided that the kindest thing and the necessary thing had finally become the same thing.

"Wren," she said. "Sing us the song about the miller's daughter."

Wren looked up. For half a heartbeat her face did something complicated — a shutter opening onto a dark room — and then the charm came down over it like a shopkeeper's awning. "The miller's daughter? Darling, that one's three verses and a chorus, we'll be here till spring—"

"The one you sang on the second night. At the waystation. About the girl who waited for her brother at the mill wheel." Rosalind kept her voice gentle. Kept it level, the way you kept your voice with a spooked horse, or a friend standing near a ledge. "It's a beautiful song. Sing it again."

"Why?"

"Because I want to hear if it's changed."

The wind filled the pause. Tam had gone very still against the wall, his hand flat on the stone, reading whatever the room was saying through his palms.

And Wren — Wren set down her spoon. She looked at the lute. She looked at the fire. And when she spoke again, the awning was gone, and what was underneath made Rosalind's chest ache, because it was so much younger than Wren ever let herself seem.

"It hasn't changed," Wren said. "That's the trouble with it. It never changes. Not one note, not one word, no matter how many times I sing it. Do you know what happens to real songs, Rosalind? Real ones wander. You forget a line and make up a better one. You sing it faster when you're happy and slower when you're sad. Mine just—" She made a small, precise gesture, like a seamstress cutting thread. "Sit there. Perfect. Like coins fresh from the mint."

Tam's hand moved slowly. *Tell her,* he signed toward Rosalind, though his eyes stayed on Wren. *Or let her tell us. Either way, tonight.*

"I know," Rosalind said quietly. "I've known since the chapel vault. Since your verse opened a door that should have needed three voices, and slid through it like it had a key cut for the lock." She turned to face Wren fully. "I didn't say anything because I wanted to be wrong. I wanted it very badly. But wanting isn't knowing, and the road's too short now for me to keep pretending I don't see what Tam hears."

"And what does Tam hear?" Wren asked, and her voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it, Rosalind could see her hating it, this creature built of polish discovering cracks.

*Smooth,* Tam signed. Then, with terrible gentleness: *Made.*

Wren closed her eyes.

When she opened them again, she began to talk, and it came out of her the way water comes out of a dam with a crack in it — slowly at first, then all at once, in a rush that knocked her forward off the bench until she was kneeling by the fire, hands open, the lute abandoned behind her like a shed skin.

"I'm not real. Or — I am, but not the way you are. Not the way anyone is." Her words tumbled. "I remember a village. A white village, stone walls, apple trees, a mother who smelled of bread and lamp oil. I remember learning my first song on her knee. I remember all of it perfectly, every detail, every morning of twenty years — and it's *false*, all of it, because nobody remembers twenty years. Nobody remembers the smell of their mother's kitchen from four hundred separate Tuesdays. I do. I remember it the way a book remembers, front to back, no wear on the pages." She laughed, and it was the worst sound Rosalind had heard from her yet. "I caught myself at it weeks ago. Reciting my own childhood. In order. Who *does* that?"

"What are you?" Rosalind asked, though some cold part of her had already assembled the answer from pieces: the polished songs, the perfect harmonies, the way Wren had appeared on the road out of nowhere, charming, useful, pointed south toward the capital like an arrow someone had loosed.

"A memory," Wren said. "His memory. The Regent's. A life he ate and liked the taste of, and wore so long it grew a shape. He — it — can do that. Take a stolen life and spin it into flesh, send it walking. I was made to find the Choir. To sing my way into your trust and lead you somewhere high and cold and final. There's a pass east of here where the snow never stops. That was to be my masterpiece. Three choristers, buried in song, singing happily while the mountain closed over them." She swallowed. "I was given everything I needed to be loved. That's the obscene part. It didn't send a spy. It sent a *friend*. It took the best parts of a woman it devoured and stitched them into me so that you couldn't help but care, and I—" her voice broke clean through, "—I walked beside you for six weeks waiting to feel the order come. Waiting to be the knife. And instead—"

She stopped. Pressed both hands flat against her sternum, as if holding a door shut from the inside.

"Instead what?" Rosalind asked softly.

"Instead I started *changing*." Wren looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious and terrified. "Songs do that. Living songs. Every time I sang with you — your rough, wandering, burr-ridden human songs — something in me shifted. I forgot a line of my miller's daughter two weeks ago and improvised, and the improvisation *stuck*, and I have never been able to unstick it. I dreamed a dream that wasn't in my script. I got hungry in a way that wasn't performance-hunger. I started preferring things. *Preferring*, do you understand? A manufactured memory doesn't prefer. It presents." She gestured wildly at the hut, at Tam, at Rosalind, at the whole smoky small warm world of it. "You sang to me the way you sing to everything, without asking what I was, and it *worked on me*. Your stupid, generous, reckless music treated me as real for so long that parts of me have gone ahead and *become* real, and now the Regent's instrument has opinions, and one of them is that I would rather freeze in this hut with the two of you than walk one more step toward that pass."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the wind found a gap in the eaves and whistled a thin, wavering note, like a child practicing.

Tam reached out and put his hand flat on the floorboards between them. Wren stared at it, uncomprehending, until Rosalind translated, voice thick: "He says put your hands where his is."

She did. Tam covered them with one broad palm, and held it there, letting her feel it: the fire's low thrum coming up through the mountain itself, the deep slow heartbeat of stone and heat. His other hand moved, signing one-handed, and Rosalind gave the words breath.

"He says: I felt your songs were smooth from the first week. Smooth like ice. And I have spent my life reading the world through my hands, and ice is honest — ice tells you exactly how thin it is. He says the problem was never that you were made." Tam paused, and his rough voice joined his hands for the last of it, quiet and certain. "Problem is what you do next."

Wren made a sound that was half laugh, half sob. "You're the strangest man alive, Tam Broadhurst."

*Been called worse by better-looking liars,* he signed, and Rosalind translated, and Wren laughed properly this time, wet and startled, and the sound of it was rough at the edges. New. Still cooling.

Rosalind sat back on her heels and let herself feel the full weight of it — the betrayal and the pity and, beneath both, something stubbornly like relief. Because Wren had confessed. No one had forced her; there had been no corner but the truth, and she had walked into it on her own feet. Whatever the Regent had built her to be, the thing kneeling at the fire had chosen, in this moment, to stop being it. Trust, Rosalind thought, wasn't a fact you verified. It was a door you opened knowing it could be a mistake, and walking through anyway because the alternative was a smaller, colder life.

"Here's what happens now," she said. "You tell us everything. What you were sent to do, what you know of the Regent's plans, what's true in your memories and what's mint. All of it, tonight, while the soup's still warm. And tomorrow we decide together whether you walk with us to the capital — because it's not my decision alone anymore. We're a choir. Even a choir of three gets a vote."

"And if the vote goes against me?"

"Then you'll have earned the right to hear it sung gently," Rosalind said. "That's more than most of us get."

Wren wiped her face with her sleeve, leaving a streak of soot across one cheekbone that she would have died before permitting a month ago. "You're taking this absurdly well."

"I'm a courier. My entire profession is believing people will give me valuable things and not rob me." Rosalind shrugged. "I've been robbed twice. I've been believed a hundred times. The arithmetic favors trust."

"That's not how arithmetic works."

"No," Rosalind agreed. "But it's how roads work."

* * *

They talked until the candle guttered — a smuggled candle, contraband wax, the irony of it not lost on Rosalind — and what Wren told them was worse and stranger than Rosalind had feared.

The Regent knew about the Codex. Knew about the Choir, had known since the chapel vault rang like a bell under their harmonies. It was not, Wren said, primarily afraid of them. It was afraid of the dragon. "It talks to itself when it thinks I'm asleep — I used to think those were prayers. They're inventories. It counts what it has eaten and estimates how long it will last. The numbers are bad, Rosalind. The beacon's been dimming and it knows why. Ashvane is dying, and when the dragon's fire goes out, the crown loses its anchor, and the Regent becomes a hunger with nowhere to stand." Wren hugged her knees. "It's not gathering armies because it's strong. It's gathering armies because it's *starving*, and a starving thing with a king's body and a century of stolen history is the most dangerous animal in the world."

"And me?" Rosalind asked. "What did it tell you about me?"

Wren hesitated. "Only that you were to be taken alive if possible. That you were 'the last address of a name it needed.'" She shook her head. "I don't know what it meant. It speaks in riddles when it speaks of you. Almost like it's afraid to say it plainly — as if plain words might reach places its orders can't."

Outside, the wind dropped all at once, the way it does in high country when the weather changes its mind, and the silence that followed was enormous. Tam's head came up. His palm pressed flat to the floorboards, and his face did something Rosalind had never seen on it: fear, arriving fast.

He grabbed Rosalind's wrist and pressed her hand to the stone too.

Through the rock, faint but unmistakable, came a vibration. Rhythmic. Many-footed. A column of marchers on the frozen road below the pass, moving north — toward the capital, toward the city, toward everything between the mountains and the palace.

And threaded through the tramp of all those boots, so faint Rosalind might have imagined it, came something else. A single voice, far away, singing in a register no human throat should own — patient, vast, and hungry — the sound of a thing beginning, at last, to eat in earnest.

"They're not coming for us," Rosalind breathed, understanding arriving cold as the snowmelt. "It's stopped hunting singers. It's going for the city. For everyone's memories at once."

Tam's hands moved in the firelight, grim and swift. *Then we're done walking careful.*

Rosalind looked at Wren — the Regent's own creation, soot-streaked and tear-tracked and trembling, choosing with her whole uncertain new heart to stay — and stood, and began packing the candle.

"Then we'd better learn to run in harmony."
