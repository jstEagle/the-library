## 16. The City That Forgot

The capital announced itself the way a sick man announces himself: by smell, then silence.

Rosalind had been to Corvale four times in their life as a courier — always at dusk, always through the River Gate, where the tanneries and the fish-criers made a wall of noise you could lean against. You could navigate half of Thornfell's largest city with your eyes shut if you trusted your ears: the bell of Saint Ellery's striking the wrong hour because nobody ever fixed it, the knife-grinder's cart singing its way down Cooper's Row, the two brothers on the bridge who argued about dice from morning until the watch made them stop.

Rosalind heard none of it now.

They came down off the pilgrim road with Wren limping between them — her wound from the pass had closed into an ugly silver seam that she kept touching like she expected it to open and confess something — and the three of them stopped at the crest of the last hill and looked down at a city holding its breath.

Smoke rose from chimneys, so people were alive. Lamplight kindled in windows, so someone still remembered to light them. But no dogs barked. No bells rang. The great market square, visible even from here as a pale scar of cobbles, stood empty at what should have been the evening rush.

"It's eating," Tam signed. His hands moved fast, angry-fast. "Not nibbling. *Eating.*"

"Districts at a time," Rosalind said. They'd seen it on the road down — a farmstead where the family still worked the fields but couldn't say which field was theirs, planting seed they'd bought from a neighbor with money whose origin they'd forgotten. A village called — well, that was the trouble; it wasn't called anything anymore. The signpost at its crossroads had been scrubbed blank, not painted over but *unwritten*, letters gone soft as breath on glass.

And now Corvale.

Wren shaded her eyes, though the sun was nearly down. "Which parts?" she asked quietly. "Do we know which parts it has?"

"The river districts first, I'd guess." Rosalind ticked them off on cold fingers. "Names go before anything else. Then trades. Then faces. It starts with what a place calls itself because that's the easiest thing to swallow."

They walked down into it.

* * *

The River Gate stood open and unguarded, which told Rosalind more than a garrison would have. The two watchmen at either side of it looked up when they passed, and their eyes did the terrible thing she was learning to recognize: they saw three travelers, and then they didn't. Not hostility. Just a smooth sliding-off, attention finding nothing to hold.

Except one of them frowned. Just slightly. He looked at the worn guild insignia on Rosalind's collar — the interlocked roads-and-star of the Wayfarers — and his brow creased like a man trying to remember why a song was familiar.

"You'll want—" he started, and stopped, and shook his head. "Sorry. You'll want something. Can't think what."

"That's all right," Rosalind said gently. "So do we."

Inside, the city was worse than empty. Empty would have been restful. This was a city performing itself without knowing why.

In Tanner's Lane, shopkeepers swept their doorsteps at the proper hour. In the fish market, a woman arranged her stall beautifully and then stood behind it with her hands folded, waiting for customers who could not remember wanting fish. A child ran past them chasing a hoop, laughing — laughter, thank every god, laughter survived — and then stopped mid-street and looked around with sudden lostness, as though he'd forgotten the game but his legs hadn't gotten the message.

"He'll be all right," Wren murmured, watching him. "Won't he? Children hold things... lower down."

"I don't know." Rosalind hated saying it. "Hold my arm. Both of you. If anyone asks, we're wine merchants, and we are very boring."

"Boring," Tam signed, deadpan, "is our whole brand."

It almost drew a laugh out of her. That was the thing about Tam — grief had carved channels in him, but water still ran through them, and sometimes it ran sparkling.

They made for the Guild quarter the long way, hugging walls, and it was there — crossing the empty grandeur of Cordwainer Square — that Rosalind saw the first true ruin of it.

A woman sat on the steps of a fountain, well-dressed, middle-aged, hands quiet in her lap. She wasn't weeping. She was worse than weeping. She was *waiting*, the way you wait outside a room where someone is dying, except she didn't know who or where or for what.

Rosalind knelt beside her before deciding to. "Madam? Are you lost?"

The woman turned. Her eyes were clear and brown and terribly present. "I've forgotten my street," she said. Her voice was perfectly calm, which is how Rosalind knew how far gone she was. Calm like that takes effort. "I've lived in Corvale forty-one years. I know that number the way you know your own heartbeat, and I cannot tell you my street. I can tell you the price of ribbon in six towns. I can mend a sleeve you'd swear was new after. But my street—" She laughed, one dry note. "My husband used to tease me about getting lost coming home from market. He'd find me. He always found me." Her hands tightened, just once, in her lap. "I don't seem to have a husband anymore. Isn't that funny. I don't seem to—"

She stopped. Looked at Rosalind's collar again. And something moved behind her eyes — not memory exactly. Memory's *shadow*. The shape a thing leaves in snow after the thing has gone.

"Wayfarer," she said slowly. "You're a Wayfarer."

"Yes."

"I owe you." She said it with the flat certainty of a woman stating her own name. "I don't know what for. But I owe the Wayfarers. Everyone does. Doesn't everyone? Doesn't everyone—" She pressed her fingers to her temple. "There was a flood. Wasn't there? When I was small. And someone came. Someone carried things. I can't see their face. I can't see any face. But I know — I *know*, the way you know bread needs salt — that somebody came, when nobody else would, and they didn't ask us anything, and they didn't stay to be thanked."

Rosalind's throat closed.

"We carried your grandmother's loom out of the water," Rosalind said softly. It was a guess. It was also, somehow, true — couriers didn't ask questions during floods, they carried whatever was heaviest and most loved. "Probably. We were probably there."

"Then take my roof." The woman stood, decisive now, anchored by the one certainty left to her. "Take my table. I can't give you my name because some *thing* has stolen it, but I can give you soup, and soup I remember how to make with my hands even if my head forgets."

* * *

Her house stood on a lane she could not name, three doors from a corner she pointed at with growing frustration until her feet simply took over and led her there, the body keeping its own stubborn records. Inside, it was warm and cluttered and full of evidence of a life she'd been robbed of mid-sentence: two chairs at the table, two cups in the drying rack, a man's coat on a peg by the door that she looked at the way you look at a word in a foreign language you almost speak.

"Myra," she said suddenly, over the soup pot. "That's mine. Myra Tetch." She said it twice more, tasting it, guarding it. "I'm going to say it all night, I think. Myra Tetch. Myra Tetch. While I still—"

"Say it all night," Rosalind agreed. "We'll listen."

Tam helped her chop leeks, and discovered — to his visible delight and Myra's baffled pleasure — that she talked *at* him freely, unselfconsciously, filling silence the way lonely people do, and never once noticed he didn't answer aloud. He signed to Rosalind across the table: *Best conversationalist I've met in years. Doesn't interrupt herself.*

Wren sat close to the fire, hood back, and Myra studied her with those clear brown eyes and said, apropos of nothing, "You look like a girl who's been invented recently."

Wren went white. Rosalind's spoon froze halfway to their mouth.

But Myra only nodded, satisfied, and stirred the pot. "Happens in this city now. People being made up fresh. I saw a man yesterday who'd clearly been assembled out of spare parts of other men — walk, voice, laugh, none of it fitting together right. The Regent's work, they whisper. Though nobody whispers loud, do they, because whispering requires remembering what you're afraid of."

She ladled soup with the authority of a general. "Eat. You owe me nothing, by the way. I owe *you*. Whatever the debt is, it's older than both of us, and I intend to pay it forward until somebody remembers why."

* * *

Later — Myra asleep at last, still murmuring her own name like a rosary — the three of them lay bedded down by the banked fire, and Rosalind stared at the ceiling beams and did arithmetic that hurt.

"She remembers the debt," they whispered. "Not the deed. Not the face. The *debt*. Fifty years of floods and wars and famines, and every time a Wayfarer carried something that mattered more than cargo, it went into these people somewhere the Regent can't easily chew. It ate her husband and her street and possibly her childhood, and it couldn't eat *that*."

"The guildmaster built it that way," Wren said. She was sitting up, knees hugged to her chest, firelight doing strange kind things to her borrowed face. "Ede Voss. The Wayfarers weren't smugglers who happened to carry memory. We were memory-carriers who learned smuggling to survive." She caught herself. "*They.* They were. I keep — I have the guild's knowledge in me, some of it, from him, and it doesn't feel like mine yet."

"How much?" Rosalind asked carefully.

Wren was quiet a moment. "Enough to know there's a cellar under the Guildhouse here. Enough to know Serle knows the palace ways because the palace ways were *built* on courier routes — the crown stole them, century back, and forgot they'd stolen them, because the Regent eats what it steals eventually." Her mouth twisted. "Enough to know I should be terrified of how useful I am."

Tam reached over and tapped her shoulder. When she looked, he signed slowly, kindly: *Useful isn't a sin. Ask the soup.*

Wren laughed — wetly, but it counted.

* * *

They spent the next day moving through the city like surgeons, and what they found refined the horror into something specific.

The Regent wasn't feeding randomly. Rosalind mapped it in charcoal on the hearthstone while Myra was at market (she came back having bought nothing, unable to recall what money was for, but cheerful, because she remembered liking markets): the eaten districts formed a spiral. Tight rings near the palace, unwinding outward. And at each ring's heart, a landmark gone hollow — a church where the priest greeted his congregation with polite incomprehension, a schoolhouse where children recited times tables but no longer knew the town's founding tale, a bridge renamed in no one's living mouth.

"It's not feeding," Rosalind realized, charcoal snapping under their thumb. "It's *harvesting along the lines of power*. Memory pools around meaning — churches, bridges, schools, names. It's drinking from the wells." They looked up at the others, and said the thing they'd all been circling. "The dragon-fire dims. Ashvane dies. And the Regent is gorging itself before the anchor breaks, because when the crown goes cold, everything it's eaten starts to *spill back out*. It wants to be so large by then that spilling won't matter."

"So we're racing its stomach," Tam signed grimly. Then, after a beat: *That's the worst sentence I've ever made.*

"Welcome to the choir."

It was near dusk, leaving the map session, that the knock came at Myra's door.

Three travelers stood in the lane — a carter, a widow, a boy of maybe fifteen — and they wore the particular expression Rosalind was beginning to catalog like a naturalist: shame and hope tangled together, the look of people about to beg a favor they can't define.

"You're Wayfarers," the carter said. Not a question. His hat was in his hands. "I don't— I can't tell you what for. There's just this. Like a stone in the boot of my soul. My daughter's sick. Fever. And every healer I carry to her forgets the road halfway there. And I thought — I don't know what I thought. But you're Wayfarers, and I'd stake my life the Wayfarers help."

Rosalind looked at this man who owed them a debt he couldn't name, asking for help they could barely give.

"We're passing through to the palace," they said. "But we're not passing through *tonight*. Show me your daughter."

The fever broke near midnight. Rosalind will never claim credit — fevers break, and broth helps, and mostly it was the mother's stubborn hands and Tam's cool cloths changed with clockwork patience. But when the girl opened her eyes and said, faintly, "Papa, I dreamed someone carried me over water," the carter had to step out into the yard and stand in the cold for a long while.

Before he left, he pressed a heavy brass key into Rosalind's palm. "Warehouse on Salt Row. Mine. Nobody remembers it's mine — not even the tax rolls, I checked, God knows how. It's yours. Call it interest."

Rosalind tried to refuse. He simply set the key down and walked away, and did not look back, because looking back required remembering forward, and he was spending everything he had just remembering his daughter's fever had broken.

* * *

Salt Row warehouse, near dawn. Wren slept at last, truly slept, curled like a comma. Tam stood at the high window watching the city gray into morning, and Rosalind joined him with the brass key still in their pocket and Maren's absence sitting in their chest like a swallowed coal.

Below, the spiral of forgetting had reached the district boundary two streets over. They could see it — not with eyes, exactly. With the hum. Old objects sang to Rosalind now whether they willed it or not, and the city's objects were going quiet in waves, like lamps blown out along a row. Two streets away, a church bell hung silent in its tower, and Rosalind could feel that it had forgotten what hour it was for.

Then, below — movement. One figure, alone on the pale cobbles, walking toward the warehouse with the unhurried confidence of a man who has never once needed to hurry.

Face forgettable. Coat too fine for the street. Hands folded behind his back like a tutor strolling a garden.

Tam saw him at the same moment. His hands came up sharp and low: *Serle.*

Rosalind's hand found the locket — the empty locket, the decoy's ashes long since scattered on a mountain pass — and closed around it, and they went down the stairs to meet him.

He stopped ten paces from the door, and inclined his head with horrible courtesy, and said:

"You've been busy, conductor. Fevers. Warehouses. Little kindnesses." His smile was thin as paper. "The Regent finds them fascinating, you know. It cannot digest a kindness given to a stranger. It keeps choking on yours." He glanced up at the graying sky, toward the palace hill, where the beacon tower stood dark for the first time in a hundred years. "But I haven't come about choking. I've come because tonight, the last gate into the palace opens for exactly one hour — and I'm the only man alive who knows the route through it."

He held out one hand, palm up. Waiting.

"And I'd like to discuss the price."
