## 17. Palace of Echoes

The drain grate had not been used in a hundred years, which was precisely why Serle's map recommended it.

"It smells like the inside of a boot," Wren said, peering down into the darkness beneath the palace's eastern wall. "A very old boot. A boot that has seen things."

"That's the point," Rosalind said. "Nobody guards a boot."

Tam signed something quick and dry, and Wren laughed before translating. "He says he's carried worse than a boot. He says he *built* a boot once, out of stone, for a rich man's garden, and it was the worst month of his life." She crouched at the grate's edge, her breath fogging in the grey morning cold. "He also says if I scream like that again when a rat touches me, he's leaving me in here."

"I did not scream," Wren said with dignity. "I issued a warning."

They went down one at a time, Tam last and easiest, because Tam moved through dark and narrow places the way water moved through them—without complaint, without wasted motion, hands reading the walls like pages. Serle's routes were drawn on three scraps of hide sewn into Rosalind's coat lining: a drainage run, a collapsed servants' stair, and then a stretch of what the map called *the Cold Gallery*, which Serle had annotated in his small, forgettable handwriting with the single word **quiet**.

Rosalind had asked him about it, back in the pass, in the hour between his offer and their escape from him. He'd looked away.

"The vaults are under the Cold Gallery," he'd said. "The Regent doesn't post guards there. It doesn't need to. The place itself keeps people out. You'll understand when you walk it."

Rosalind understood now.

The drainage tunnel opened, as promised, into a corridor of pale stone so smooth it seemed polished by grief rather than tools. Their footsteps did not echo. That was the first wrongness. The second was the light—or the absence of it. Torches burned in brackets along the walls, old ones, well-fed ones, and gave off nothing. Not dimness. Nothing. Flame without illumination, like a memory of light rather than light itself.

Wren stopped dead. "That's not possible."

"Neither are you," Rosalind said gently, "by most accounts. Keep walking."

Tam touched the wall and shook his head slowly. He signed: *No song. Stone is empty here.* Then he pressed his palm flat against it again, harder, frowning, and added: *Not empty. Full. Full of nothing.*

The Cold Gallery ran for perhaps two hundred paces, and every step of it felt like walking through someone else's held breath. Rosalind found themselves thinking of things they hadn't thought of in years—the smell of the guildhouse kitchen, the weight of Maren's hand on their head—and found the thoughts sliding away like fish, there and gone, there and gone. By the far end they were gripping the Codex through their coat just to remember their own name.

Then the gallery ended, and warmth returned, and color returned, and all three of them stood breathing hard in a small round chamber as if they'd surfaced from deep water.

"What is that?" Wren whispered. "What lives in there?"

"Nothing," Rosalind said. "That's the whole trick. A room with nothing in it, next to a room with everything."

Because ahead of them, behind a door of black iron banded with silver, was the vault.

* * *

The lock was nothing. Rosalind had picked harder locks before breakfast on ordinary mornings. What stopped them was the sound.

When Rosalind set their picks to the keyhole, they heard—faintly, from inside—a hum. Not machinery. Not magic, exactly. Voices. Hundreds of voices, layered and murmuring, the way a seashell holds the sea.

Tam heard it too, or felt it; his head came up sharply. He crossed the chamber and laid both hands against the iron door, and his face changed in a way Rosalind had never seen it change—not since the mine, not since his wife. His eyes went wet.

He signed slowly, hands trembling: *So many. They're all talking at once. Nobody's listening to any of them.*

Wren watched his hands and translated in a whisper, and then said, very quietly, "It's a prison, isn't it. Not a treasury."

"A larder," Rosalind said, and hated the word the moment it left their mouth.

The lock turned. The door swung inward on hinges that made no sound at all—which, after the Cold Gallery, felt almost friendly.

* * *

Shelves.

That was Rosalind's first stupid, human thought: *shelves*. Row upon row upon row of them, rising into a vaulted dark, oak and iron, orderly as a countinghouse, stretching farther than lamplight could follow. And on the shelves, in ranks and regiments, glass.

Vials. Thousands of them. Each one the size of a fist, each one stoppered with wax and sealed with a lead tag, and each one holding a slow-turning curl of light that was no color Rosalind could name—warmth-colored, home-colored, the color of a kitchen at evening or a mother calling you in from the rain.

The murmur was louder here. It came from everywhere. From the shelves. From the vials. Each stolen life, humming to itself in its glass, waiting.

"Oh," Wren said, and sat down abruptly on the floor.

Tam didn't sit. Tam walked. He walked down the nearest aisle with one hand trailing along the shelf edge, touching vial after vial, and tears ran freely down his weathered face, and he did not wipe them away. Rosalind followed and watched his hands as he paused at random bottles, and read what they told him:

*A wedding. A woman laughing so hard she has to hold onto a fence. Snow on a mountain road and a boy singing badly to keep his brother's spirits up. A deathbed where nobody is afraid. The smell of bread. Just the smell of bread, kept all by itself, the way you'd press a flower.*

"He keeps everything," Rosalind said softly. "Not just the big things. The *small* things."

"That's worse," Wren said. She had recovered enough to stand, but she hugged her arms around herself. "A monster who steals crowns and thrones—that's a story. This—" she gestured at the endless shelves, at the bread-smell in its little bottle—"this is somebody's whole afternoon. Somebody's only good day. Filed alphabetically."

It wasn't alphabetical, actually. Rosalind checked, because checking was what couriers did, and the tags ran instead on some other order—by year, perhaps, or by region, or by some taxonomy only the Regent understood. Many of the oldest shelves were dusty. The newest ones, near the center, gleamed.

And near the center, on a shelf at eye height, in the place of honor where a careful librarian puts the volume most often consulted—

Rosalind saw the tag.

Lead seal, freshly stamped, the wax still bright. And on the tag, in the Regent's neat, patient, ancient hand:

**WINTERBOURNE, R. — PARTIAL.**

The world went narrow and quiet, the way it does just before a bridge collapses.

* * *

"Rosalind." Wren's voice, from a long way off. "Rosalind, your face is doing something alarming."

Rosalind reached out and took the vial down from the shelf. It weighed almost nothing. Inside, the light curled slower than the others—sleepy, or shy, or worn thin from use.

*Partial*, the tag said. As though there were more. As though this were one installment among several, a subscription, a thing consumed in courses.

"You've never been here," Wren said. It wasn't a question, but Rosalind answered anyway, because saying it aloud was the only way to make it not true.

"Never. I've never even been inside the capital walls until this winter."

"Then how—"

"I don't know."

But a cold, clever part of Rosalind's mind—the part that read ledgers and noticed dates that shouldn't exist—was already working. *Partial.* When? Where? How do you eat a person who was never within reach?

Maren's voice, from weeks ago, from the lockkeeper's dim cottage, rose up unbidden: *"The Regent eats what remembers you, child. But mind—" and she had tapped her own temple, blind eyes aimed somewhere past Rosalind's shoulder—"it can eat what remembers you *wrong*. Memory's a meal either way."*

Wrong. Someone remembered Rosalind wrong. Someone remembered them *at all*—strongly, closely, intimately enough to fill a vial—and the Regent had taken that remembering and swallowed it, and here was the bottle.

Who? Who in the world knew Rosalind Winterbourne well enough to be worth a meal?

Their mother was dead. Their father had never been more than a rumor with a name attached. The Wayfarers were scattered or gone. Maren knew them, but Maren still breathed, still remembered, and her memories—if eaten—would be filed under *Ostwick*.

Unless.

Unless the memory in this vial wasn't someone else's memory *of* Rosalind. Unless it was Rosalind's own. A year, an hour, a season—cut out of them so cleanly they'd never noticed the gap, the way you don't notice a tooth until the tongue goes looking.

Rosalind stood in the aisle of stolen lives, holding their own name in a bottle, and felt the floor of their selfhood tilt.

"Put it back."

Tam's voice—his actual voice, rough and rusted from disuse, used perhaps twice since Rosalind had known him. Both of them stared at him.

He signed, fast and fierce, and Wren translated with a shaking voice: "He says put it back on the shelf. He says—" she faltered, watching his hands, watching his face—"he says he knows what it is to lose a piece of yourself and spend years not knowing it was gone. He says opening it might be worse than the hole."

"And he says," Wren added, quieter, "that he'll break it over your head if you drink it standing up in a thief's cellar like a fool."

Despite everything—despite the dread sitting in Rosalind's chest like swallowed river-stone—a laugh escaped them. Tam's mouth twitched. Even Wren smiled, wanly.

"We're not drinking anything," Rosalind said. "We're stealing one vial and getting out. That's the mission. That was always the mission—one proof, one sample, something Maren can read." They wrapped the vial in cloth, tucked it inside their coat against the heart, and felt it hum there, faint and strange, like a sleeping bird. Like it recognized the coat. Like it recognized *them*.

"But we look first," they added. "Two minutes. We find the king."

* * *

Finding Aldous was easier than it should have been, and that was its own horror.

The royal shelves occupied the vault's heart: a ring of cases under a dome painted with constellations, each case bearing a name and a reign. There was Aldous's father, Aldous's grandfather, going back and back, dynasty after dynasty, each one reduced to a dozen bottles and a brass plate. And at the ring's center, alone, a case with no plate at all—only a single large vial, older than the rest, its light nearly gone, flickering like a candle in a draft.

Tam touched it and pulled his hand back as if scalded.

*This one is screaming,* he signed. *It has been screaming for a hundred years.*

Ede Voss. Rosalind knew it without being told, the way you know thunder means lightning. The guildmaster who walked into the palace and never came out. Whose handwriting filled the Ash Codex. Whose name was carved, they now suspected, into the walls of a smuggler's cave in the high passes, guarding a childhood that belonged to Rosalind.

Everything pointed inward. Every thread of this long, cold winter led back to this room, to these shelves, to the small dull vial now beating faintly against Rosalind's ribs.

"Time," Wren said. "We're on borrowed time and the lender is ugly."

She was right. Somewhere above them, the beacon flame guttered; somewhere below their feet, a dragon sang itself toward silence; and somewhere in a crown of gold, a hungry thing was feeling around for the disturbance that three intruders made, the way a spider feels a fly's first touch on the web.

They went out the way they'd come in. Rosalind took one last look back down the long central aisle—at the thousands of small lights turning patiently in the dark, the bread-smells and weddings and snow-roads, the whole kingdom shelved and stoppered and humming to no one—

—and made a promise to the room, silent and furious: *We're coming back. All of you. We're singing every last one of you home.*

The Cold Gallery took them again on the way out, ate the sound of their steps and the edges of their thoughts, and let them go at last into the grey daylight like a mouth releasing something it had decided, for now, not to swallow.

* * *

They made camp that night in a tanner's ruined shed outside the walls, and Rosalind did not sleep.

They sat with the vial in their cupped hands while Tam whittled by feel and Wren pretended, badly, to doze. The light inside turned and turned. Up close, in the dark, Rosalind could almost hear it—not words, not yet, but the shape of words. A voice. Young. Familiar in a way that made their skin prickle.

"Open it," Wren said suddenly, from the shadows.

"No."

"Open it, or I will, and I have much worse judgment than you."

"*No.*" Rosalind closed their fingers over the glass. "Maren said wait. Tam said wait. And—" they laughed, low and unsteady—"and honestly, I'm afraid of what I'll find. Everyone keeps telling me I've lost things I didn't know I'd lost. My mother's face. Now this. At some point I have to ask what's left of me that's *original*."

Silence. Then Tam set down his knife, crossed the space between them, and took Rosalind's hands in his great scarred stonemason's ones—hands that had shaped arches out of raw mountain. He turned their palms up, opened the fingers gently, one by one, and set the vial back into them.

Then he signed, slowly, so there would be no mistake, and Wren translated in a whisper:

*"You're asking the wrong question. It doesn't matter what's been taken out of you. Look around. I'm deaf and I'm here. Wren was never real and she's here. What's left of a person isn't what the thieves couldn't get. It's what the people who love you keep handing back."*

He tapped the vial once, lightly.

*"Whatever's in there—it's not the whole of you. It's just the part somebody else was holding. Go and take it back."*

Rosalind looked at the small, tired light turning in the glass. Thought of a date inked fresh in a sealed book. Of a king repeating a courtier's joke with hollow eyes. Of a voice, eleven seconds long, saying *sing it back to me*.

They broke the wax.

The vial sighed open like a window in spring—and memory poured into them, whole and sudden and blinding:

Snow. A palace garden, off-season, forbidden. A boy of ten or eleven with a crooked crown of frost melting in his hair, laughing, showing them a secret door. A summer—a whole year of summers—spent trading secrets through that door with the loneliest child in the kingdom. And the last day, the boy grown suddenly grave, gripping their sleeve with both hands, saying:

*"If I ever stop being me—promise you'll remember me. Promise you'll sing me back."*

The vial slipped from Rosalind's fingers and cracked on the stones, spilling its spent light.

They sat motionless for a long moment. When they finally spoke, their voice came out small and centuries old.

"I know why the Regent has my name on a shelf. It wasn't eating *me*."

They lifted their head, and their eyes were burning.

"It was eating *him*. Every memory anyone ever kept of King Aldous. And mine—" they pressed a hand flat against their chest, where the ghost of the vial's warmth still lingered—"mine was the one it liked best. Because I promised him. Because I *promised*."

Outside, far off across the sleeping city, the palace beacon flared once—bright, desperate, dying—and went dim.

Tam was already reaching for his boots.
