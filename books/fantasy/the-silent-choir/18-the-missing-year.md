## 18. The Missing Year

The vial was small enough to lose in a pocket, which Rosalind supposed was the point.

It sat among the vault's ten thousand brothers on its glass shelf, in the long gallery beneath the palace where the Regent kept its larder. Every vial held a life—or a piece of one—and each was labeled in a small, patient hand. *The miller of Ashford Bridge. His wedding day.* *A cook named Pell. Her mother's kitchen.* *Rosalind Winterbourne.*

That last label had been sitting in their mind like a splinter for two days now.

They had told no one. Not Tam, who slept with his back to the wall and his hands curled loose, ready to feel trouble coming through the floor. Not Wren, whose manufactured dreams sometimes made her whimper in a voice that wasn't quite hers. And certainly not Maren, because Maren wasn't here—Maren was still three days away at the locks, if the letters Rosalind had sent ahead had found her at all—but Maren's voice lived in Rosalind's head rent-free and unpaid, and it said, with terrible clarity: *Don't. Some things are locked for kindness, child, not cruelty.*

But Maren also said: *The Codex doesn't lie. It just doesn't tell you everything.*

And the Codex, open on Rosalind's knee in the lamplit dark of the vault, said this:

*What is stolen can be carried home. What is given freely cannot be taken again. The conductor must know what was taken from them before they can conduct what was taken from all.*

Rosalind read it four times. Then they laughed, once, quietly, the way you laugh when a trap has been built out of your own curiosity.

"Oh, that's foul," they murmured. "He wrote me an instruction manual and made it sound like destiny."

The vault breathed around them. Glass shelves marched off into darkness, each holding its little captive sunrises. Somewhere above, the palace went about its hollow evening—the Regent's court laughing at jokes it had already heard.

Rosalind picked up the vial.

* * *

They did not open it right away, because Rosalind Winterbourne was reckless but never careless, and there is a difference the guild teaches you young: *a courier who rushes dies on the road; a courier who hesitates dies in the town.* You want the narrow path between.

So first they ate. Cold bread, hard cheese, an apple Wren had charmed off a palace gardener with a song so sweet it probably counted as theft under three separate statutes. Tam watched them chew with the particular patience of a man waiting for someone to admit something.

"You've been reading that page all day," he signed. He didn't speak aloud much anymore unless it was important; his hands were truer. "You've got your stubborn face on. The one from the pass."

"I don't have a stubborn face."

Tam raised both eyebrows and let his hands do the rest: *You have seven faces. Stubborn. Hungry. Guilty. Guilty-while-eating. Stubborn-while-guilty-while-eating—*

"All right." Rosalind set down the apple. The vault's lamplight caught the vial where it lay between them, and inside it something moved—not liquid exactly, more like weather seen through smoked glass. "There's a vial with my name on it."

Tam went very still. He was a large man, a stonemason, built like something meant to hold up roofs, and stillness on him was louder than shouting.

"It means I've already been eaten," Rosalind said. "Once. Somewhere. A year of me, sitting on a shelf, and I never even noticed it was gone. Do you understand what that means? You can't miss a year. There's no seam. It's like—" they groped for it— "like a wall with a stone taken out and the others shifted over to fill the gap. Perfect masonry."

Tam's mouth twitched. Even now, even here, the craftsman in him couldn't help appreciating good work.

"Maren would say leave it," Rosalind went on. "Maren would say some locks are kindnesses. And maybe she'd be right. But the Codex says the conductor has to know. And Ede Voss wrote the Codex, and Ede Voss is—" they stopped. They hadn't said it aloud yet, not fully, not to anyone. "Whatever the thing wearing the crown used to be. Which means either the Codex is the monster's trap, or it's the monster's confession. And I've been carrying the thing for months, and I've felt it, Tam. Books don't feel sorry for you. This one does."

Tam considered this for a long moment. Then he reached over, took the vial from the shelf himself, and pressed it into Rosalind's hand. He folded their fingers around the glass with both of his big scarred ones, the way he might steady a chisel before the first strike.

*If it's a trap,* he signed, one-handed, still holding theirs closed with the other, *we'll deal with it together. That's what a choir is. That's the whole entire point.*

Rosalind looked at him—at this enormous gentle man who had hidden half a lifetime inside his own deafness until grief cracked him open—and found their throat too tight for speech. So they nodded, and unstoppered the vial, and drank the missing year.

* * *

Memory does not come back the way water pours. It comes the way dawn comes: slowly, then all at once, then everywhere.

Rosalind was nine years old, and it was raining in Thornfell, and they were angry.

They were angry because their mother had sent them up the mountain alone for the first time—a real delivery, wax seals and everything, a package for a woman in Highmoor who paid in dried apples—and Rosalind had gotten lost twice and soaked once and arrived three hours late to find the whole errand already handled by another courier, and now they were walking home through the rain with wet boots and a wounded heart, taking the long way past the river just to spite everyone, and that was where they saw the boy.

He was standing on the flat rock above the shallows where couriers crossed in summer, and he was crying. Not loudly. The way children cry when they've learned crying gets them nothing: silently, furiously, as though each tear were being extracted under protest.

Rosalind knew every child within two days' walk of Thornfell. They did not know this boy. He wore fine clothes ruined past fine-ness, muddy to the knee, and he had the particular lost look of someone who has run a very long way without any plan for afterward.

"You're standing in the rain," Rosalind informed him, because nine-year-olds are great comforters.

"So are you," the boy said.

"Yes, but I'm allowed to be miserable. You look like you're supposed to be somewhere expensive."

That startled a laugh out of him, wet and surprised, and after that it was easy, the way it is always easy at nine, before the world teaches you that strangers are dangerous. His name was Aldous—he gave only that, and Rosalind, raised by a guild that taught names were currency, didn't push. He had run away from something. He wouldn't say what. He had a cut on his palm he claimed came from climbing and clearly hadn't. Rosalind shared their dinner bread, which was soggy, and he declared it the best thing he'd ever eaten, which was obviously a lie, and they became friends in the way of children: instantly, completely, with the whole soul.

For one summer, the boy came to the river whenever he could slip away. He was hungry for ordinary things. He wanted to know how much bread cost. He wanted to carry a parcel just once, just to see what it felt like to bring something to someone who needed it. Rosalind taught him courier signs, the hand-talk of the roads, and he learned fast and laughed when he got them wrong, and once he said, lying on his back on the flat rock watching clouds:

"When I'm king, I'm going to make sure nobody has to be invisible unless they want to be."

Rosalind had snorted. "Kings don't notice people like us. That's rather the whole arrangement."

"This one will," the boy said, with a ferocity far too large for his body. "I'll remember. I promise I'll remember."

Then the summer ended, as summers do, the way songs end—mid-note, without warning. One week the boy was there, mud to his knees, learning to skip stones. The next week Rosalind waited on the flat rock through three grey evenings and he didn't come, and their mother found them there on the fourth night and knelt down in the cold and took their face in her hands, and her expression was one Rosalind had never seen before and could not, even now, entirely name.

"Listen to me," she said. "There was no boy. There was never any boy. You played by the river alone this summer. Can you say that back to me?"

And Rosalind—nine years old, bewildered, trusting—had said it back.

* * *

Rosalind came back to themselves on the floor of the memory-vault with Tam's arms around them and their own voice hoarse, and it took them a moment to realize they had been singing—the river song, the skipping-stone counting rhyme, the little tune the boy had invented badly and loved fiercely—and that Tam had felt it through the flagstones and answered it, vibration for vibration, keeping them anchored the way you anchor anything in a flood.

When they could speak, what they said was: "I met him. I met him *first.*"

Tam pulled back and looked at them, waiting.

"The king. Aldous. When he was—he was just a boy, he ran away, he spent a summer on the river crossing with me, we—" Rosalind's hands were shaking, and they hated that, and kept talking anyway, because the words had a pressure behind them like spring meltwater. "I taught him courier signs. He promised he'd remember. And then my own mother took the year out of my head to hide me from the crown, and I walked into Thornfell a hundred times since and never once wondered why the palace guard never questioned me, why doors seemed to open, why—" they laughed, a cracked and awful sound— "why I always felt like the city owed me something. It wasn't the guild's luck, Tam. It was a boy keeping a promise the only way he could, through whatever was left of him."

Tam signed slowly, carefully: *The Regent didn't take this year?*

"No." Rosalind turned the empty vial in their fingers. "Look at the seal. Look at the label. This wasn't eaten—it was *filed*. Someone put it here deliberately. Someone hid it in the monster's own pantry because it was the safest shelf in the kingdom." They stared at the small patient handwriting. "Ede Voss took my year. Before he went under. He hid it here and left me a riddle telling me to come find it."

*Why hide it at all?*

"Because of what he asked me." Rosalind's voice dropped. The vault seemed to lean in, ten thousand stolen lives listening. "At the end of that summer. The last day. He was different—scared, older somehow, like something had happened at home. He grabbed my hands and he said, *if I ever stop being myself—if you ever look at me and I'm not in there—you sing it back. Promise me. Promise you'll remember me even when I can't.*"

The silence after that had weight, like snow.

*A hundred years,* Tam signed at last. *He's been asking for a hundred years.*

"And the whole time, the answer to the prophecy was sitting in a glass bottle with my name on it." Rosalind stood, legs unsteady, and looked down at the little vial that had held a summer, a friendship, a promise—the keystone of everything. "I thought I chose this quest. I thought I read a date in a book and decided to be brave. But he asked me when we were nine years old, and I said yes, and then I forgot I'd said yes, and I have been walking toward this throne room my entire life without knowing it."

Tam rose too. He put one huge hand on Rosalind's shoulder, and through the contact Rosalind felt him sign against their collarbone, almost gently:

*Then you're not the conductor because the book says so. You're the conductor because you promised.*

Rosalind closed their eyes. Somewhere above them, faint as a heartbeat through stone, the dragon-fire beacon guttered—dimmer tonight than yesterday, dimmer than the day before. The clock in the mountain, ticking.

"We have to move," they said. "Tonight. Now. Because he's still in there, Tam—I saw him, I *know* his face under that thing's face—and a man who's been drowning for a century deserves better than me arriving late a second time."

They were three steps toward the vault door when the lamps flickered.

Not guttered—flickered, in sequence, down the long gallery, like a breath drawn through ten thousand glass throats. And beneath their feet, through Tam's planted palms, through the soles of Rosalind's boots, came a tremor that was not footsteps and not machinery but something worse: the palace itself shifting, rearranging, as though some vast attention had rolled over in its sleep and begun, slowly, to wake.

Tam's face went pale. He signed fast, urgent: *Something heard the song.*

Rosalind looked at the empty vial in their hand, then at the door, and felt the old courier's certainty settle over them like a cloak: the road behind was closed, and the only way out was through.

"Good," they said, and did not entirely mean it, and pushed the door open anyway. "Let it hear. I've got a delivery to make."

Above them, far off through the bones of the palace, a bell began to ring—an alarm, or perhaps a welcome—and in a corridor they had not yet reached, a king who did not know he was a king lifted his crowned head, and for eleven seconds, remembered the rain.
