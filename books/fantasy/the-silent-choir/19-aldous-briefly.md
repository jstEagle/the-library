## 19. Aldous, Briefly

The trouble with stealing through a palace, Rosalind decided somewhere around the fourth hour of not being caught, was that the palace kept insisting on being beautiful.

They had come up from the memory-vaults through a service stair that Serle's old routes had promised would be empty, and it was empty, mostly—except for the beauty. Corridors opened off the stair like held breath: vaulted ceilings painted with constellations so old that half the stars had been renamed, and the painters' stars hung up there still, stubborn and wrong. Tapestries the length of ships. Windows of glass dyed honey-gold that turned even this grey winter afternoon into something like mercy.

"Stop gawping," Tam signed, catching her at it. His hands moved quick in the lamplight they'd allowed themselves. "Gawping is how people get caught."

"I'm not gawping. I'm assessing."

"You're gawping."

Wren said nothing, which from Wren was its own kind of commentary. Since the cave, since the confession none of them spoke of directly, she had gone quiet in a new way—not the polished quiet of a performer between verses but the raw quiet of someone learning which of her own thoughts were hers. She walked with one hand trailing the wall, as if checking the stone for honesty.

Rosalind checked the corridor ahead again. Still empty. Somewhere far above, muffled by floors and history, the palace went about its evening: doors, footsteps, a burst of laughter with nothing behind it.

They were looking for two things, and they were looking for them in the worst possible place. The first was the way down to Ashvane—the dragon's cavern lay beneath the throne room itself, according to the Codex, and every route down ran past rooms full of people whose eyes the Regent could borrow. The second was Maren. The old lockkeeper had left the locks three days behind them, walking toward the capital on memory and spite alone, and Rosalind had not heard from her since, and was trying very hard not to do arithmetic about roads and winters and ninety-year-old legs.

"She'll be here," Tam signed, without being asked. He had a maddening habit of reading her face the way he read vibration, palm-flat against the world.

"You don't know that."

"No," he agreed. "But I know you, and you'll be no use to anyone until she is here, so we may as well assume it."

It was while Rosalind was composing a suitably ungrateful reply that the corridor changed.

She felt it before she understood it—a pressure in the ears, like the moment before thunder, like the hush when a whole room turns to look at a door. The lamplight guttered though there was no draft. And down the long gold length of the gallery, where it bent toward the royal apartments, came footsteps.

Not guards. Guards had a rhythm, boots on stone, four-four time, boring as bread. These footsteps were slower. Ceremonial. A single set, unhurried, and around them the corridor did something Rosalind had no better word for than *leaned*—the shadows tilting subtly toward the walker, the light bending, everything in the hall orienting itself the way iron orients to a lodestone.

Tam's hand closed hard on her wrist. *Hide,* he signed, sharp enough to draw blood if signs could.

There was nowhere good. An alcove with an urn; a curtain gone moth-eaten at one edge; a door that might be locked and might open onto anything. Rosalind pulled Wren into the alcove, pressed herself and Tam behind the urn's cold bulk, and put her eye to the gap between stone and bronze.

The king came around the bend alone.

He wore no crown. That was the first thing, and it wrong-footed her so badly she nearly stepped out in front of him. She had built a picture these many weeks—had needed the picture—of Aldous always crowned, the Regent perched on his head like a second skull, drinking light at feasts. But he was bareheaded now, an old man in a plain dark robe, walking his own corridor at night like any sleepless grandfather, and for one absurd heartbeat Rosalind thought: *then it's over, then it's only a man.*

Then she saw his shadow.

It moved a half-step behind him. Not lagging—*leading*, just barely, the way a dog on a short lead stays taut against its owner's heel. Where the lamplight should have thrown it long across the floor, it pooled instead, dense and neat, and the light near it seemed thinner, weaker, the honey-gold windows going pale as watered wine as he passed beneath them.

He stopped.

Ten paces from the alcove. Close enough that Rosalind could hear his breathing, which was wrong—not labored, but *arranged*, each breath placed like furniture. He stood before a window looking out at the winter-dark city, and the city looked back with a thousand scattered lights, and for a long moment nobody in the corridor breathed except the man who wasn't really breathing.

Then Aldous Vane, King of Thornfell, vessel of the Regent, began to talk to himself.

"The third stair from the top of the west tower sticks," he said. His voice was pleasant. Court-trained. It made Rosalind's skin crawl precisely because it was pleasant. "One must remember to tell the steward. One must remember to—" A pause. A small, terrible pause, the sound of a man reaching into a pocket and finding it sewn shut. "One must remember."

His hand rose halfway toward his temple and stopped there, hovering, as if the crown were present after all—as if he could feel its weight without wearing it.

*It eats what remembers,* Maren had said. Rosalind watched the sentence happen in real time: the king trying to hold a small true thing, a sticking stair, a steward, a errand—and the thing in him sipping it away like steam off a cup. When he lowered his hand, his face had smoothed. The little furrow of effort was gone. He smiled at the window, at nothing, a host's smile with no guest inside it.

Rosalind's chest ached in a way that had nothing to do with fear. She thought of the vial in her pack—*the missing year*, the child's voice begging her to remember—and had to press her knuckles against her mouth to keep whatever was rising in her from making a sound.

And that was when Aldous turned and looked directly at the urn she was hiding behind.

* * *

Later—much later, in the safe dark of a disused chapel with Tam's hand steady on her shoulder—she would try to reconstruct those eleven seconds and find that they refused to behave like other memories. They came back all at once or not at all, a struck bell rather than a story.

What she knew for certain was this: the king's eyes found hers through the gap between urn and wall, and they were not the Regent's eyes.

The Regent's eyes, she realized, she had seen already—at the feast, weeks ago, through her wine-merchant's mask. They had been hollow-eyed then, yes, but worse than hollow: *tidy*. Eyes like a ledger, like a room where everything has been filed. What looked out at her now from ten paces was not tidy. It was desperate. It was a man waking underwater and seeing light above him and knowing exactly how much air he had.

Aldous's mouth worked. The courtly smoothness cracked across it like ice on a pond.

"Winterbourne," he whispered.

Her whole body went still. Not her name—her *family* name, spoken aloud in a palace where no record of the Wayfarers remained, where her guild insignia bought kindness from strangers who couldn't say why. No one had called her that since—

"You're taller." The whisper cracked into something almost like laughter, wet and disbelieving. "You were knee-high. You stole my— you took the honey cakes off the— I let you, I wanted you to think I didn't—" His breath hitched. His hands, old and spotted, rose slightly from his sides like a man feeling for a railing. "I have been trying to leave myself notes. All over. In the stair, in the— it takes them, it takes them faster than I can write, but I wrote *you*, I wrote you somewhere it couldn't—"

The shadow behind him stirred. Pooled darker. Leaned.

"Listen," Aldous said, fast now, spending his seconds like a dying man spends coins. "Eleven. I get—I've counted—it gives me eleven sometimes, at night, when it's feeding elsewhere, when the city is loud enough to keep it busy—" His eyes flicked to the shadow and back, and the terror in them was so ordinary, so human, that Rosalind's fear simply burned away. "You have to sing it back to me."

"Your Majesty—" she breathed, because what else does one say to a king, even a king dissolving.

"Not that. Not 'Majesty.'" He was smiling now, awful and bright. "You used to call me Dous. Because you couldn't say Aldous. You said—" and here he made a sound, a child's mangled syllable, and laughed at it, and the laugh broke in the middle. "Sing it back to me. Sing me back. Whatever you found, whatever you're carrying—if it's me in there, if any of me is left, sing it *back*—"

Behind him, the shadow stood up.

That was the only way to describe it. The flat darkness on the floor gathered itself vertical without passing through any shape a body uses, and the temperature of the corridor dropped so fast that Rosalind's next breath ached, and the honey-gold windows went grey as ash, and Tam's grip on her arm went from warning to anchor.

Aldous felt it too. She watched him feel it—the light draining out of his face from the edges inward, like frost claiming a pane of glass. But he fought it. Gods, he fought it, jaw clenched, eyes nailed to hers, spending his last seconds on her face the way a drowning man spends them on the surface.

"Don't stop coming," he said. The voice was fraying now, two voices braided badly together, the courtier's smoothness winding through the desperate whisper like a vine through a fence. "Whatever it offers you—don't—"

"Never," Rosalind said.

"*Good boy*," said the other voice, gently, fondly, horribly, and Aldous's face slammed shut.

There was no better word for it. A door closing—but a door has hinges, has a frame, has a moment of passage. This was instantaneous. One blink: the man. Next blink: the Regent, wearing him, the eyes gone ledger-tidy, the smile restored to its hostly warmth. The shadow settled back onto the flagstones like a cat resuming its nap.

"Cold tonight," the Regent remarked pleasantly, to the empty corridor, to the urn, to the moth-eaten curtain. "The servants forget the fires. They forget everything lately. Such a pity."

It paused. Rosalind did not breathe. Beside her, Wren had gone white as bone, and Tam's hand trembled against her wrist—not with fear, she realized, but with vibration: he could feel something through the stone, some deep subsonic attention sweeping the corridor like a lighthouse beam, and he held perfectly, perfectly still, the way he'd learned to hold still his whole life.

"Hm," said the Regent.

And then it walked on. Slower than before. Its shadow no longer led it; it led the shadow, firmly, like a man dragging something heavy that pretended to be asleep. At the end of the gallery it paused beside a tapestry—a hunting scene, hounds and hart, faded to ghosts of color—and touched the fabric once, almost tenderly.

"He liked this one," it said softly. "Or I liked it for him. It hardly matters anymore, does it?"

Then it was gone around the bend, and the light came back to the windows by degrees, honey seeping slowly into grey, and Rosalind discovered that her knees had opinions about remaining locked.

She sat down on the cold floor of the alcove, back against the urn, and put her face in her hands.

*Dous.* She could almost reach it—the memory floated just beyond her grasp, a child's tongue failing at four syllables, someone laughing, the smell of honey. It was *right there*, the way a word is right there on the tip of the tongue. The vial had given her the meeting; the rest was still eaten, still shelved somewhere in the vaults below in rows of glass. But he remembered it. He had carried it a hundred years—no, he had carried it however long he'd lived inside his own siege, hoarding crumbs, writing notes in stairs and tapestries, waiting eleven stolen seconds at a time for a courier who might never come.

"Eleven seconds," Wren whispered. Her manufactured-perfect voice had roughened since the cave; it suited her. "He counts them. He counts the seconds of himself."

"He's counting on us, too," Tam signed, one-handed, his other arm still around Rosalind. Then, seeing her look: "Sorry. Poor timing. But true."

Rosalind laughed—a cracked, wet, entirely undignified laugh, muffled in her palms—and let Tam haul her upright.

"We have the route down now," she said, when she could trust her voice. "He came from the royal apartments. The gallery bends toward the under-stair; I marked the turn. Two days. We wait out the changing of the guard tomorrow night, and we go down to Ashvane, and then—" She stopped. Swallowed.

"And then we sing him back," Wren finished quietly.

"And then we sing him back."

Tam tapped the floor twice with his boot—their signal for *agreed, moving*—and they slipped from the alcove, back along the service stair, down into the palace's forgotten veins. Rosalind went last. At the turn of the gallery she paused, one heartbeat, and looked back at the hunting tapestry, at the faded hounds frozen mid-run after a hart that had, presumably, escaped a century ago.

"Dous," she whispered, testing it, and the word fit her mouth like a key she hadn't known she'd been carrying.

* * *

The chapel they sheltered in sat below the palace's east wing, sunk in the foundations like a tooth in a jaw. Someone had loved it once—there were carved birds on the pillars, worn nearly smooth—and no one loved it now, which made it perfect. They lit no lamps. Tam spread their blankets and took first watch by touch, palm flat to the floor, reading the palace's vast slow pulse through the stone: footsteps, carts, the distant drum of the guard change.

Rosalind lay staring at the dark and did arithmetic again. Not about Maren this time. About seconds.

Eleven. He'd said it like a ration, like a man reporting the size of his last loaf. Eleven seconds, at night, when the city was loud. Which meant the Regent was busiest when the city was loudest—which meant it was *feeding*, constantly, on a capital of two hundred thousand souls, sipping and sipping, keeping the king starved of himself. And the beacon on the ridge had dimmed. The dragon below was dying. Every day the fire guttered lower, the Regent grew more frantic, the meals got bigger, the king's rations of self got smaller.

Two days might be one day too many.

"Ros." Wren's voice, barely a thread, from the dark beside her. "When he said your name. Before the name—when he recognized you—he was happy."

"Yes."

"That's what it's for, isn't it? Being known." A pause; Rosalind could hear her choosing words, carefully, the way one walks on river stones. "I didn't understand that before. I was made of knowing things. Songs, faces, histories—all of it perfect, none of it mine. But when you sang my true verse in the cave—" her breath caught, restarted, "—that was different. That was being *held*. He's been down there a hundred years, holding himself, with no one to hold him back."

Rosalind reached out in the dark and found Wren's hand and squeezed it. "Then we'd best hurry," she said, "before he wears out."

Tam's boot tapped the floor twice. *Agreed. Moving.* Then his hand stilled on the stone, and stayed stilled, and Rosalind watched him frown at the dark in that particular way he had—head tilted, listening with his whole body.

"What?" she breathed.

He held up one finger. Waited. The palace pulsed beneath his palm: footsteps, water in pipes, the great slow heart of the boilers. And then, fainter, farther, from somewhere up in the living levels—a rhythm Tam had spent a lifetime learning to feel through floors, through walls, through the bones of buildings.

Footsteps. Slow. Ceremonial. Unhurried. Passing overhead, going away.

But underneath them, woven through the stone like a second pulse, another set. Lighter. Limping slightly, favoring the left leg. Steadying themselves on walls. Moving through the palace's lower ways with the sure, stubborn, infuriating pace of someone who had walked forty years of lock-gates and did not intend to be slowed down by a mere royal fortress.

Rosalind was on her feet before Tam finished signing the word.

*Maren.*
