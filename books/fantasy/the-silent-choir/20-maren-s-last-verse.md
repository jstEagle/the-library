## 20. Maren's Last Verse

She came up the mountain road at dusk, a small grey figure leaning on a lockkeeper's staff, and Rosalind knew her before they could see her face — by the walk, that stubborn rolling gait of a woman who had spent sixty years arguing with water and winning.

"She can't have," Tam said beside them on the chapel steps. "The locks are four days' hard walking for someone young."

Wren shaded her eyes against the low sun. "That's not someone young."

"No," Rosalind agreed quietly. "It very much isn't."

Maren Ostwick had been the guild's last elder when the guild had been more than a name and a ruin, and she was the last elder still, though the years had taken her eyes first and then most of everything else except the voice. She had kept the canal locks below Thornfell since before Rosalind could walk, opening and closing the great wooden gates on schedule through floods and freezes, because memory needed keeping somewhere, and locks were as good a place as any. She had taught Rosalind their letters with a wet finger on a barge deck. She had taught them which candles burned longest in wind. She had never once told them what they were.

Rosalind had been angry about that last part for two chapters of their life now, and the anger did not survive the sight of her coming up the hill.

"Maren." They went down to meet her, took her arm without asking, felt how thin it was under the sleeve. "You walked."

"I did no such thing." Her eyes, clouded as river ice, found their face by sound alone. "I walked a little. Then I walked a little more. It adds up, if you're stubborn about it. You'd know."

"You're blind."

"I'm *half*-blind, which is a different profession entirely." She patted their hand where it gripped her arm. "I can see you fine, child. You've always been easy to see. That was rather the point of you."

Behind them, Wren made a small sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Rosalind shot her a look over Maren's head — *she doesn't know* — and Wren raised both hands in surrender, which from Wren meant nothing and they both knew it.

"Come inside," Rosalind said. "There's a fire. There's tea. There's a stonemason who'll want to shake your hand until your shoulder dislocates."

"Then we'd best get the shaking done early," Maren said, "so I can enjoy the rest of the evening with one working arm."

* * *

Inside, warmed and fed, she sat by the fire like she'd been sitting there all her life, and told them about the journey in the way of old people who have decided suffering is a form of entertainment they owe the young.

"There's a boy at the third lock who thinks I'm his grandmother," she said. "He does now, anyway. He was very helpful. Carried my pack up the switchbacks and cried when I left, poor lamb, and couldn't say why." She sipped her tea. "I didn't take anything from him. Before anyone says anything. I just let him remember helping an old woman, and let him be fond of the helping. That's not theft. That's interest on a debt the guild owes this whole valley."

"That's exactly theft," Rosalind said, "with extra steps."

"It's *kindness*, with extra steps, and don't take that tone with me, I changed your swaddling." But she smiled when she said it, and the smile did something complicated to Rosalind's chest.

Tam sat across from her, big hands wrapped around a mug too small for them, watching her the way he watched everything — not with his ears, which had never worked, but with his whole body, reading the room through the soles of his feet and the bones of his jaw. When she spoke, he leaned toward the vibration of her voice in the floorboards like a plant toward light.

"You feel that?" she asked him suddenly. "Me talking?"

He nodded. Touched his chest, then the floor, then made the shape with his hands: *you are loud*. In the good way. The way a bell is loud.

"Hah." She looked delighted. "Sixty years being told to pipe down at the locks, and the first honest musician I meet tells me I'm loud. This is the best evening I've had since Aldous's father was king, and I mean that literally, I was there, it wasn't much of an evening either."

Rosalind laughed despite themselves, and then caught Wren watching them — watching Maren — with an expression Rosalind had learned to dread over the past weeks: the look of someone counting something. Wren counted exits. Wren counted supplies. Lately Wren had begun counting people, and looking at each count a moment too long, as if checking whether the number would hold.

Later, when Maren slept by the fire like a cat who had earned every hour of it, Wren came to stand beside Rosalind at the window.

"She walked four days," Wren said softly. "Half-blind. Through passes the Regent's agents are sealing."

"She's stubborn."

"She's *arriving*." Wren turned to look at them, lamplight catching the strange polished quality her face had sometimes, like glaze on pottery. "People arrive when there isn't going to be time later, Rosalind. I've seen it. I've—" She stopped. Started again. "I was made out of other people's arrivals. Trust me on the shape of one."

Rosalind looked at the sleeping old woman and felt the cold slide into their stomach that had been waiting there since the ridge, since the beacon dimmed, since the clock became audible.

"Then we don't waste the morning," they said.

* * *

Maren woke before dawn, which she claimed was a habit from the locks, "because the water never sleeps and neither do the men who lie about tending it," and found Rosalind already awake, already holding the Ash Codex, already unable to open it.

"You've been staring at that book like it owes you money," she said, lowering herself onto the bench. "Well. Open it."

"I don't know how to ask you what I need to ask you."

"Then I'll answer the question you're not asking, which will save us both the embarrassment." She held out her hand, palm up. "Give me the Codex. My eyes are bad but my fingers are better than yours ever were."

Rosalind gave it over. Maren's hands moved across the cover the way they must have moved across lock gates for sixty years — knowing it by touch, by grain, by weight. She opened it to the first page, the page Rosalind had deciphered weeks ago in the ruined Guildhouse, the page that had undone their whole invisible life: *the Wayfarers carry memories; the Silent Choir sings them back.*

"You knew," Rosalind said. Not a question. "All those years at the locks. Every visit. You knew what I was."

"I knew what you *might* be." Maren's clouded eyes stayed fixed on the page she couldn't read. "Your grandmother might have been. Your mother might have been. The line runs quiet, Rosalind, generation after generation, and mostly it stays quiet, and thank every drowned god for it, because a quiet conductor lives long. I checked every year. I listened to you hum while you worked the candles. And every year you sounded like nothing but a girl humming." Her mouth twisted. "And then this winter you started sounding like a door opening."

"The Regent heard?"

"The Regent heard." She closed the book gently, the way you close a coffin. "Serle heard. Everything downstream of the crown heard. So yes, child, I knew, and I lied to you, and you may hate me for it after tonight, but you will not hate me for it *before* tonight, because before tonight I still have work to do."

"What work?"

Maren set the Codex down. She reached beneath her shawl — slow, deliberate, ceremonial, and Rosalind realized with a lurch that they were watching a ritual they'd seen the shape of all their life without ever seeing the thing itself — and drew out a cord, plain waxed leather, from which hung a small iron key worn smooth as a river stone.

"A lock key," Rosalind said stupidly.

"A *conductor's* key." She took Rosalind's hand and pressed it into their palm and folded their fingers over it, and her hands were cold and strong and trembling only slightly, and Rosalind could not speak at all. "Every lock I ever tended, I tended in the guild's name. Every gate, every sluice, every flood season. This is the master to all of them, passed elder to elder since Ede Voss cut it herself. It opens more than canals. It opens the ways under things. It will open the palace, when you get there, if the old routes still answer to guild iron." Her grip tightened. "Do you understand what I'm giving you?"

"I understand you're giving me a job I didn't apply for."

"You applied the day you were born. Nobody consulted either of us." She exhaled, and it shook, just once, and then steadied. "I, Maren Ostwick, last elder of the Wayfarers' Guild, name Rosalind Winterbourne conductor of the Silent Choir. Witnessed by fire and stone and"—she turned her head—"whatever that girl by the door is pretending to be. Say aye or say nothing, it counts either way."

By the door, Wren said softly, "Aye," for herself.

Rosalind's throat wouldn't work. Finally: "Aye."

"Good." Maren patted their cheek with the brisk affection of a woman closing a transaction. "Now put the kettle on. Conductors make the tea. That's in the Codex somewhere, probably."

* * *

They came at midmorning, and Rosalind would wonder forever afterward whether Maren had known the hour in advance or simply arranged the day so that whatever came would find her ready.

There were six of them on the road below the chapel — crown agents in undyed wool, moving wrong, moving *smoothly*, the way Serle's hunters moved, faces that slid off the eye like water off wax. And at their head, unhurried, forgettable, Serle himself, looking up at the chapel with the expression of a man returning to a house he'd once owned.

"They're here for the singer," Wren breathed from the window. "For you. That's a taking party, that's—"

"They're here for a song," Maren corrected, from the middle of the room, and Rosalind turned and saw her standing very straight, staff in hand, and understood.

"No," Rosalind said.

"Someone sings them away from this road, or they follow you to the next town and the one after, and they learn the Choir's numbers, and they take Tam apart looking for the gift, and they take that girl apart looking for what she is, and they take *you* last, because you're the prize." Maren said it all evenly, like reading cargo manifests. "Or one old woman sings them a different road, and buys you the passes while the beacon still burns."

"Maren—"

"Conductor." She used the word like a key turning. "You don't argue with your elder. Guild law. I wrote half of it, the other half is worse."

Rosalind stood there with the iron key biting its shape into their palm and every argument they owned dying in their throat, because the worst truth was the simplest one: it would work. Maren's voice was the strongest Rosalind had ever heard, stronger than theirs, stronger than Tam's silent thunder, a voice that had argued with rivers for sixty years and won. A decoy chorus sung by Maren Ostwick would pull six hunters and a hollow man halfway across Thornfell.

And the Regent ate what remembered you. Sing, and it finds you. Maren had said it herself, months ago, in a lockhouse that smelled of oil and rope, warning Rosalind away from exactly this.

"I'm not warning you away anymore," Maren said, as if she'd heard the thought. She crossed the room — slowly, letting no one help her, daring anyone to try — and took Rosalind's face in her two cold hands. Up close her clouded eyes were almost clear, or perhaps Rosalind was finally learning to see by something other than eyes. "Listen to me. I am seventy-nine years old. I have buried everyone I ever loved, I have forgotten more names than the Regent has stolen, and the finest thing I have done in this long life is keep one hidden child fed and ignorant and alive. Do not you dare take this from me. This is the verse I've been saving."

"Maren." Rosalind's voice cracked clean in half. "I only just got you back. I only just learned what you are to me."

"And you'll have me as long as there's a single note of me left anywhere in the world, which is more than most people get." She kissed their forehead, once, hard, like stamping a seal. "Sing well, conductor. Sing *loud*. Loud enough that wherever I end up, I'll hear whether you obeyed me."

Tam was crying without any noise at all, great silent heaves, and Maren went to him and pressed her forehead to his huge knuckles and said something too low for anyone else, and Tam bowed over her like a mountain bowing over a stream. To Wren she said only, "Take care of them, whatever you are. You're realer than you think. I could always tell real ones. It's my trade."

Then she walked out the chapel door and down toward the road, staff striking stone, spine straight, and began to sing.

* * *

They watched from the high window, all three, though Rosalind would give a great deal never to have watched at all.

Maren's decoy was a guild-song, one of the old road-chants, but she unwound it into something none of them had ever heard — a full chorus sung by one throat, harmonies stacked like masonry, and woven through it, unmistakable even to Rosalind's untrained ear, memories. The locks in spring. A wedding on a barge deck. A boy at the third lock who thought she was his grandmother. She was spending herself note by note, laying her own remembered life down the road like coins behind a fleeing thief, and the hunters took the bait the way hunger takes bread.

They turned from the chapel. They followed her voice west, away from the passes, away from the capital, Serle drifting at their head like a man being pulled on a string he couldn't see.

She sang for two hours. Rosalind timed it against the sun and their own heartbeat and wished they hadn't. The voice carried impossibly far in the cold air, growing thinner and thinner, and near the end it stopped being songs at all and became something else — names, dozens of names, hundreds, every name Maren Ostwick had ever kept alive in her head recited like a rosary, the lockkeepers and the bargemen and the children and the drowned, given back to the wind in case anyone was listening, in case *anything* was listening, so that the Regent would eat a life crammed so full of other people that there'd be almost nothing of her left to swallow—

The singing stopped.

The wind came up the valley and where the song had been there was nothing at all, and Rosalind understood, with a finality that had no grief in it yet, only a terrible clean clarity, that Maren Ostwick had gone somewhere no song would ever bring her back from, and had done it on purpose, and had timed it so her last audible word was a child's name.

They stood at the window a long time. Tam put his hand flat against the wall, feeling for vibration, feeling for anything, and after a while he lowered it.

Wren spoke first, very quietly. "The road west is clear. We should move."

"I know." Rosalind's voice came out steady, which seemed like a betrayal, and also like the only gift they had left to give her. "We move."

They took up their packs. At the door Rosalind stopped and looked back at the empty bench by the dead fire, the cup with the tea ring still in the bottom, the small impression in the rushes where a stubborn old woman had sat and argued with the world one last time.

Then they closed their hand around the iron key until the teeth bit deep, and led the Choir out onto the frozen road, toward the palace, toward the dragon, carrying the torch she had lit in them — burning, furious, and impossible to put out.
