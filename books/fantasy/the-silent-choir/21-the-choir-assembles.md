## 21. The Choir Assembles

The nun's name was Sister Clemence, and she was watering a grave when they found her.

Not tending it—watering it. She stood in the frozen churchyard of the Convent of Saint Ovel with a bucket in one hand and an expression of profound patience on her face, pouring water onto soil so hard with frost it might as well have been iron.

"You know that's going to freeze," Rosalind said, before she could stop herself.

"It already has." The nun didn't look up. She was perhaps sixty, broad as a door, with snow settling on her wimple like the convent had simply decided to keep her. "But the woman under this ground spent forty years complaining about drought, so I water her grave anyway. It's not for her. It's for me. Grief needs somewhere to put its hands."

Tam, standing at Rosalind's shoulder with his mittened hands tucked in his armpits against the cold, made a small sound. Wren, beside him, glanced over. Tam had gone still in the particular way he went still when he felt something in the ground—a vibration, a hum, the ghost of a song pressed into stone or soil or timber. He crouched, laid his palm flat against the frozen earth of the grave, and his face changed.

He looked up at Rosalind and signed, one-handed, quick: *She's one.*

"You're a singer," Rosalind said to the nun.

Sister Clemence finally turned. Her eyes were pale blue and entirely unfooled. "I'm a gardener," she said. "And before that I was a chorister, though there hasn't been a choir worth the name in this kingdom since my voice broke at fourteen and the mother superior told me God preferred humility." She set down the bucket. "You're the Wayfarer. The little one from the locks said you'd come. Maren Ostwick. She walked past here three weeks ago, ate all my honey bread, and told me someone would come asking me to sing again." She wiped her hands on her apron. "I told her I'd buried that voice with Edith. But Maren said—" and here the nun's composure flickered, just once, like a candle in wind—"she said the kingdom was forgetting how to grieve properly. That people were losing their dead twice. Once to death, and once to whatever's eating the remembering."

Rosalind's throat tightened. "That's true."

"Then I suppose I'd better finish my watering first." Sister Clemence picked up the bucket, poured the last of it out over the frozen ground with great ceremony, and patted the earth twice, as if tucking in a child. "Edith, love. I'm going to go sing at some monsters. Try not to be jealous."

* * *

They left the convent at dawn with a fourth voice, and if Rosalind had expected the journey south to the river-lock town of Marrowgate to be grim and silent, they were swiftly disabused of the notion by Sister Clemence, who talked the entire way.

"—and that's why the bishop banned me from the harvest festival, which I maintain was an overreaction, because the goat *was* in the vestry and somebody had to do something—"

"She hasn't stopped," Wren whispered, awed, "since we crossed the wall."

"Neither have you," Tam pointed out, tapping Wren's arm and then signing, *You two are the same person.* Wren flushed with what Rosalind had privately begun to think of as her real-blush—the flush of someone newly made, discovering that embarrassment was a thing that belonged to her now, and finding she rather liked owning it.

It was strange, Rosalind thought, watching them bicker along the frozen road, how quickly a family could assemble out of strangers and shared danger. Three weeks ago Wren had been the Regent's creature, polished and hollow, sent to lead them into ruin. Now she sang her own verse—short, rough, unpolished, four lines about being born in a glass vial and choosing anyway—and when she sang it, Tam said he could feel it in his teeth like a struck bell. A manufactured memory, learning to make true ones. Rosalind did not fully trust it yet. But she trusted it more each day, and trust, she was learning, was less a fact than a practice, like watering a frozen grave.

Marrowgate sat where the Thorn met the lowlands, a town of locks and warehouses and gulls that screamed like unpaid debtors. Maren's list, recited to Rosalind in a lockkeeper's hut that smelled of lamp oil and old courage, had been specific: *the nun at Saint Ovel, the jailer at Marrowgate, and the child at Cinderharrow who plays the fiddle for coins but hears the songs underneath.* Seven voices, counting Rosalind, Tam, and Wren. Eight needed. The arithmetic of that had been sitting in Rosalind's chest like a swallowed stone ever since, but there was no use counting stones not yet gathered.

The jailer's name was Doss Halloway, and he was, by the testimony of half of Marrowgate, the gentlest man in the town and the most feared, because he had held the keys to the cells below the customs house for thirty years and never once lost a prisoner—or forgot one.

"He remembers everyone," the tavern-keep told them, jerking her thumb toward the customs house. "Every thief, every smuggler, every poor fool who slept off his drink in the wrong doorway thirty years back. Names, faces, what they stole, why they cried. It's uncanny, is what it is. Nobody forgets anything around Doss, and nobody can make him."

Rosalind felt the stone in her chest shift. *A keeper of memories,* she thought. *Hiding in plain sight behind bars.*

Doss Halloway turned out to be an enormous man with a beard like winter hedgerow and a ring of maybe forty keys at his belt, none of which, it developed, were strictly necessary anymore, because the cells below the customs house had been empty for years and he kept them anyway out of principle.

"A Wayfarer." He looked Rosalind up and down with slow, shrewd eyes. "Thought your lot were all gone."

"Mostly."

"Maren Ostwick came through in autumn. Told me a story." He folded his arms. "Told me about a time before the Guild carried candles and stolen lace. Said we used to carry the other thing. The heavier thing." His voice dropped, and beneath the gruffness Rosalind heard something raw. "Said the reason I remember every soul that's passed through my cells is that I've been doing the work all along without knowing its name. Holding memory so it doesn't get eaten." He leaned closer. "Now she also told me the thing that eats memory lives in the king's crown, and you're going to go sing it to death, and you want me along." He straightened. "I'll be honest with you, Wayfarer. I've been waiting thirty years for someone to tell me what my remembering is *for*. I thought it was a curse. My wife left me over it. Said living with a man who never forgets anything was like living with a ledger that breathed."

"And now?" Rosalind asked.

"Now I think it might be a post." He unhooked the largest key from his belt and held it out to her, solemn as a knighting. "Cells are empty. Keys are yours. Somebody ought to hold the door for the ones coming home."

Tam, beside him, reached up and gripped the big man's forearm, and Rosalind saw Doss startle—then feel, through the grip, whatever Tam felt: the deep, patient hum of thirty years of kept names, ringing in him like a tower full of bells.

"Well," said Doss Halloway, blinking hard. "That's a bit much, that is."

* * *

Cinderharrow was two days further east, in the foothills where the coal seams came near the surface and the air tasted of ash and money that other people made. They found the child in the market square, as promised, playing a fiddle for coins—but the child was not quite what any of them had expected.

She was perhaps eleven years old, small even for eleven, with a burn scar along one jaw and boots held together with wire and defiance. And she was not playing tunes. Or rather, she was playing tunes—jigs, laments, a marching song badly mangled—but underneath them, threaded through them, woven into the spaces between the notes, was something else. Something that made Tam stop dead ten yards away and grab Rosalind's sleeve hard enough to bruise.

*Underneath,* he signed frantically. *Listen underneath.*

Rosalind listened. And heard.

The girl finished a piece, collected a scatter of coppers from a thin crowd, and caught sight of them staring. Her chin came up at once, all sharp edges. "If you're bailiffs, my da says you can't take the fiddle, it's not ours, we're holding it for a man in Marrowgate."

"We're not bailiffs," Rosalind said. "We're Wayfarers."

The girl's eyes went wide, then narrowed with the speed of a child who had learned early that adults' surprises usually cost money. "Prove it."

Rosalind considered, then dug in her pack and produced the smuggler's coin—the one that hummed with a dead man's last morning, coffee and rain and a door opening. She held it out. "What do you hear?"

The girl took it, held it to her ear the way another child might hold a seashell, and went very quiet.

"Porridge," she whispered. "No—he's making porridge for somebody, and it's raining, and he's happy, and he doesn't know it's the last morning, and—" her voice cracked, and she thrust the coin back as if it had burned her, which in a way Rosalind supposed it had. "Why does it do that? Coins aren't supposed to do that. Nothing's supposed to do that. I've been hearing things in things my whole life and my mam says I'm cursed and my da says I'm blessed and they both say *stop talking about it*, and—"

"Your name," Rosalind said gently.

"Bett. Bett Sorrel."

"Bett. You're not cursed." Rosalind crouched to her level, the way she had learned from Maren, who crouched to everyone's level, kings included, on principle. "You're a chorister. One of eight the world needs, and one of seven we've found, and I know that sounds like nonsense, so I'll say the true part instead: there is a thing eating this kingdom's memories, and you can hear the memories it leaves behind, and we need you."

Bett looked at the coin. At Tam, who smiled and tapped his own ear and shook his head—*I can't hear at all, and I'm here.* At Wren, whose four-line song Wren sang right there in the market square, rough and short and hers. At Sister Clemence, who said, "I'm a nun, dear, and I promise you the curses are greatly exaggerated."

Bett Sorrel, aged eleven, survivor of Cinderharrow, holder of a fiddle held in trust for a man in Marrowgate, looked at each of them in turn with the ferocious arithmetic of a child deciding whether to believe.

"Does it pay?" she said.

"Terribly," said Rosalind.

"Food?"

"When we find it."

"Will I die?"

Rosalind held her gaze. She had promised herself, after the locket, after Serle, after Maren walking away into the dark singing a decoy chorus that had not come back—she had promised herself no more lies to children, no more lies to anyone, if she could help it. Lies were the Regent's currency. Truth was all the Choir had.

"Maybe," she said. "I won't pretend otherwise. But I'll tell you exactly what the risk is, and you'll get a vote, and if you want to leave at any gate between here and the palace, I'll carry your fiddle myself and wave goodbye."

Bett considered this for a long moment. Then she slung the fiddle across her back and stuck out her hand, small and chapped and certain. "Da's going to kill me," she said, with relish. "Where are we going?"

"The palace," said Rosalind, and shook the hand.

* * *

They made camp that night in the ruins of a shepherd's bothy above Cinderharrow, and for the first time Rosalind allowed herself the dangerous luxury of counting.

Seven. Sister Clemence, whose grief-song could raise the hairs on a corpse's neck. Doss Halloway, a tower of kept names. Bett, small and fierce, threading truth through the gaps between notes. Tam, the metronome, the spine, who felt every song in stone and bone. Wren, singing her own four true lines. Herself. Seven voices, huddled around a small fire while snow ticked against the broken roof.

She took out the Ash Codex. By firelight, the prophecy's final instruction seemed almost simple, which Rosalind had long ago learned was the way of terrible things: *Eight voices, or the song will not hold. Seven may wound it. Seven may wake the dragon halfway. Only eight may finish it.*

"Say it aloud, then," said Doss, who had been watching her face. "Whatever the book's saying. We're all in the same boat; no sense you drowning quiet."

"The Choir needs eight," Rosalind said. "We have seven."

Silence, except the fire and the snow.

"There's no one else on Maren's list?" asked Sister Clemence.

"No. Nun, jailer, child—that was the end of it. She named six of us besides myself, and here you all are." Rosalind turned the page, hoping—knowing better. The Codex offered nothing. It had offered nothing for days, as if it had said everything it meant to say and was now simply watching, the way a guildmaster watches, from a century away, to see what his words would do.

"Then we find an eighth somewhere else," said Wren briskly. "There must be others. Other people who hear things in things. We found Bett in a market square; the world's full of market squares."

"The Regent's been hunting singers for a hundred years," Rosalind said quietly. "Anyone with the gift who wasn't hidden—like us, like Maren hid our families—has been found. Or emptied." She did not look at Wren when she said *emptied*, and was proud of managing it. "There isn't a list of candidates, Wren. There's us. And there's whoever's left."

"Serle," said Tam.

Everyone turned. Tam rarely spoke aloud in company—it cost him effort, and his voice came out flat and oddly placed, a sound he couldn't hear himself make. When he used it, it landed like a hammer.

"He's the eighth," Tam said. "You know it. You've known it since the pass."

"That's not—" Rosalind began.

"The hunter," Tam signed, and Bett translated aloud without being asked, her voice quickening as she realized the shape of what she was saying. "The crown's man. He was a chorister once, he told you. The Regent took his memories and left him obedience. He's got songs in him—broken ones, stolen ones, but songs. He's the only one left."

"He tried to kill us."

"He *wept*," said Wren softly. "When you offered him the choice. I was there. I know what manufactured looks like, Rosalind, and that wasn't manufactured. That was a man realizing the door was still open."

Rosalind looked into the fire. She thought of Serle's face, that odd forgettable face, crumpling in the pass at the word *sing*. She thought of Maren's voice: *Mercy is strategy too, little one. It's the only strategy that keeps working after the battle ends.* She thought of the arithmetic, cold and simple: seven voices, eight required, and the kingdom forgetting itself district by district while the dragon-fire guttered.

"The eighth voice has to be willing," she said at last. "That's the whole point of everything we're doing. The Regent takes. We ask. If Serle can't truly choose—if the obedience runs too deep—then forcing him makes us no different from the thing in the crown, and the song fails anyway. It would know. Ashvane would know."

"So we ask," said Bett, with the terrifying simplicity of the young. "Asking's easy. I ask for things constantly."

That startled a laugh out of all of them—even Rosalind, even Tam, who laughed the way he did everything, wholly, with his whole body. The fire popped. Snow settled. For a moment the bothy felt almost warm, almost safe, seven voices around a flame like a rehearsal for something none of them dared name.

Then Tam stiffened.

His head came up. His hand went flat against the bothy's stone floor, and Rosalind watched his face do the thing it did when the world spoke to him through the ground—watched it move from listening to understanding to alarm in the space of three heartbeats.

*Far away,* he signed, urgent. *South. Big. Like—* He searched for the sign, didn't find one, and stamped his boot twice against the floor, hard, so they all felt it through their own bones: a tremor, distant and rhythmic. *Like a door slamming. Again and again. Something's walking.*

Bett had her fiddle half-raised like a weapon. Doss had a hand on his keys. Sister Clemence had, absurdly and somehow comfortingly, picked up the water bucket.

"How far south?" Rosalind asked.

Tam held up his palm toward the dark beyond the broken roof—toward the lowlands, the river, the road they had walked—and then tilted his hand, slowly, until it pointed not south but east. Toward Thornfell. Toward the high passes. Toward the palace.

Toward home, if you could call a place full of shelves of stolen souls a home.

"The dragon-fire," Wren whispered. "It's dimming faster. Whatever's coming—it's not sending hunters anymore."

Rosalind closed the Codex and stood, and the seven of them rose with her, one by one, a chord assembling itself in the snow.

"Then we stop counting," she said, "and start walking. Eight, when we find it. Singing, when we arrive. And heaven help whatever stands between us and the throne room—because it has never once heard us coming, and it is about to."

Above the bothy, far to the east, the sky over the palace glowed faintly, fitfully, like a banked fire deciding whether to live or die.

The Choir shouldered its packs and went to meet it.
