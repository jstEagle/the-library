## 22. The Eighth Voice

The arithmetic was merciless.

Rosalind had done it three times that morning, once in ink, once in charcoal, and once in their head while pretending to eat breakfast, because the answer refused to change no matter how many ways you asked it.

Seven voices. The Codex required eight.

The nun sat by the fire mending a sleeve with the serenity of a woman who had already died to everything except her song. Sister Caelle — though she'd laughed when Rosalind first used the title and said, *Sister is for the convent; down here I'm just Caelle who remembers* — had been found in a hospice in Lower Marle, singing plague-names over the dying so that no one left without their whole life being said aloud around them. She hummed even now as she sewed, an old harvest tune threaded through with something older underneath, like a river under ice.

The jailer, Brannic Odo, sat across from her, enormous hands wrapped around a tin cup he hadn't drunk from. He had spent thirty years keeping a list of every prisoner's name in his head, because the records office burned twice and paper was a liar. He remembered four hundred and eleven people. He could recite them alphabetically or by the sound of their coughs.

And the child — Pip Verrin, nine years old, prodigy of the fiddle before her hands were broken by a father who feared what she heard in music — sat cross-legged on the floor drawing dragons in spilled tea. She drew them fat and friendly. Rosalind decided not to correct her. There would be time enough underground for correction.

Seven. With Tam's stone-deep metronome pulse, with Wren's new-made voice still raw as a healing wound, with Caelle and Brannic and Pip and old Hesper the ferryman, whose verse was every crossing he'd ever made and every hand he'd held in the dark water — seven.

The Codex's page lay open on the table, Ede Voss's cramped century-old script patient as a spider:

*Eight throats or none. Seven is a chorus. Eight is a key.*

"Could we count Wren twice?" Hesper offered. "She does talk enough for two."

"I heard that," said Wren, not looking up from rewrapping her bandaged ribs.

"It was a compliment."

"It was *not*, old man."

Rosalind let them argue. It was easier than saying the name sitting behind their teeth like a pulled tooth that wouldn't come loose.

Because there was only one candidate left. Only one person alive who had ever stood inside the Regent's machinery and walked out carrying its leavings. Only one voice that knew the shape of the enemy from the inside.

Maren had named him herself, in her last clear hour at the locks, gripping Rosalind's wrist with fingers like winter branches: *When you're short — and you will be short — you go find the hunter. That's the joke of it, dear. The crown always leaves one door open. It can't help itself.*

Serle.

The man with the forgettable face who had hunted Rosalind through Thornfell's passes. The man who had been a chorister once, before the Regent ate everything he was and left only obedience standing upright in a coat.

Tam touched Rosalind's elbow. He didn't need to sign anything; after weeks together, his hands spoke less than his eyebrows. One raised brow: *You've thought of someone you don't want to say.*

"The eighth voice," Rosalind said aloud, "is in the garrison at Coldmere."

The room went quiet in the particular way rooms do when everyone knows exactly which disaster has just been proposed.

"He tried to kill you," Wren said flatly.

"He tried to arrest me. Repeatedly. With escalating sincerity."

"He burned your mother's face out of your locket." Wren's voice gentled on the last words, which somehow made it worse.

"No," Rosalind said. "I did. He made me choose between my grief and my freedom, and I chose, and that was *my* choice, and I have hated how reasonable it was ever since." They closed the Codex. "He's also the only person alive who knows what the Regent sounds like when it's wearing someone. We need that. We need him more than we need safety, and I hate the arithmetic, but there it is."

Pip looked up from her dragon. "Is he scary?"

"Yes," said Rosalind honestly.

"Good. Scary ones cry the hardest. My gran said that." She went back to her dragon.

Nobody asked how a nine-year-old knew that. Some knowledge arrives too early and simply stays, like weather.

* * *

Coldmere garrison sat at the mouth of the last pass before the capital, all grey stone and greyer duty. The roads were sealed now — the Regent's writ ran on every gate: NO UNLICENSED SINGING. TRAVELERS WILL BE REMEMBERED. Someone in the chancellery had meant it as reassurance and it read like a threat, which told Rosalind everything about who was actually writing the orders these days.

They went in as what they were, more or less: Rosalind in Wayfarers' grey with the guild insignia turned outward, Tam beside them signing that he was a deaf stonemason surveying wall damage, which had the advantage of being true. Pip stayed with the others in the treeline, over her loud objections, bribed into silence with the promise of naming the next dragon they met.

Sergeant Serle received them in a guardroom that smelled of lamp oil and boiled turnips.

Here was the thing about his face: Rosalind had seen it across a mountain pass, in snow, at knifepoint-distance, twice — and standing three feet away now they had to work to hold onto it. Brown hair. A scar? Perhaps. Eyes some color between things. The features kept sliding off memory like rain off slate. The Regent hadn't just erased Serle's past; it had eaten the part of him that stuck in other people's minds. He was a man-shaped absence with excellent posture.

"You shouldn't be here," Serle said. His voice was level, tired, and — this was the strange part — not unkind. It had never been unkind, even when he'd been hunting them. Cruelty requires a self, and Serle had been robbed of his.

"No," Rosalind agreed. "I'm told that's rather the theme of my life. May I sit?"

"You may stand. You may talk. If you sing, I am required to detain you."

"Required," Rosalind said, tasting the word. "That's a strong little word, isn't it. Required. Who requires?"

Something moved behind his forgettable eyes — the flicker of a fish under ice, there and gone. "The Crown requires."

"And what does the Crown give you, in exchange for being required?"

Silence. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, a sentry coughed the exact cough Brannic would have catalogued.

"I'm going to tell you a story," Rosalind said, "and then I'm going to make you an offer, and neither of them is forgiveness. Let's be clear about that up front. I don't forgive you. I don't know if there's enough of you to forgive, and anyway forgiveness isn't mine to give for what was done to *you*. But I watched you, in the pass. When I burned that memory as a decoy — my mother's face — you stopped. For three full seconds you stood in the snow and looked almost sick, and I've thought about those three seconds every day since. A hollow man doesn't hesitate. Something in you recognized what I was doing, because something in you remembers what losing is."

"Stop," Serle said quietly.

"Or stay hollow forever," Rosalind went on. "That's the offer, actually, both halves of it. Here's the story: you were a chorister. Before the crown took you. Maren Ostwick remembered your voice — a boy soprano from the Fenmark choirs, who sang the remembering-songs until the Regent found you and ate your song and your name and your mother's lullaby and whatever your favorite food was and the name of the street you grew up on, and left you nothing but obedience, because obedience is cheap to keep and useful to wear. And now the crown needs eight voices to be unmade, and seven of them are waiting in the trees outside your garrison, and the eighth is a man the Regent built out of its own leftovers."

"Stop," Serle said again, but his hand had gone to the edge of the table, knuckles white, holding on.

"The memories it left you," Rosalind said. "The scraps. The parts of you it couldn't digest — the hesitation in the snow, the sickness when I chose grief over love. It left them in you because they were useless to it. But they're not useless, Sergeant. They're seeds. Sing them back, and they grow. Refuse, and you'll stand in this room until the kingdom ends, required, forgetting even that you were ever a boy in Fenmark who liked — I don't know, what do boys from Fenmark like?"

"River eels," Serle whispered.

It came out of him like a hiccup, involuntary, shocking him more than anyone. He stared at Rosalind with an expression that had no business on a forgettable face: naked astonishment at his own mouth.

"There," Rosalind said gently. "See? You're still in there. Buried under orders, but there. River eels. That's a start. That's practically a biography."

And then, because mercy is strategy and strategy must be honest about its weapons, Rosalind did the thing they had rehearsed for forty miles and dreaded the entire way.

They began to hum.

Low. Almost nothing. Not a memory-song, not quite — just the shape of one, the way Maren used to warm up, three notes rising like smoke. Enough to feel in the floorboards. Enough for Tam, silent by the door, to set his palm against the stone wall and become the drum beneath it, a pulse so deep it wasn't sound at all, only certainty traveling through rock.

Serle made a sound Rosalind had never heard a soldier make. Small. Cracked. The sound of a door that had been nailed shut from the outside beginning, splinter by splinter, to open.

The scraps came up.

Not in words — in weight. Rosalind felt them arrive the way you feel weather change. A woman's hands braiding hair. A bell with a cracked note that everyone loved anyway. Snow, and someone laughing, and the specific cold-bright joy of being seven years old and *fast*. A hymn sung badly on purpose because the singer liked the bad version better. Grief — a fresh, enormous grief, a funeral, a coffin, a name worn smooth from saying —

Serle was crying. Standing at attention, tears running down his forgettable face, making no sound at all, because somewhere deep the Regent had trained even his weeping to be quiet.

"They're mine," he said. "Some of them are mine. I can tell. I don't know how I can tell, but I can — the bell, the bell was ours, St. Aldhelm's, the crack was from lightning the year I was born—"

"Sing it," Rosalind said. "Don't sing it well. Sing it the way you liked it badly."

And he did.

It was a terrible voice. Rusty, wavering, a boy soprano long drowned in a man's chest, cracking on every third note. It was the worst singing Rosalind had ever heard from someone trying.

It was also, unmistakably, a chorister's voice — because underneath the rust, the *shape* was perfect. The instinct, the phrasing, the place where breath became meaning. The Regent had eaten the song and left the singer, because you cannot fully unmake a voice any more than you can unmake a riverbed. Water finds it eventually.

When he finished, the guardroom felt different. Larger. As though a window had been opened in a wall where no one had noticed a window could be.

Serle wiped his face with the heel of his hand, a gesture suddenly so human it hurt to watch. "If I sing with you," he said, "the Regent will know. The moment I raise my voice anywhere near the palace, it will know its weapon has turned around."

"Oh, I'm counting on it," Rosalind said. "It's spent a hundred years eating memories. It's never once had to hear one coming back at full volume, wearing its own uniform. Let it know. Let it lie awake — well. Whatever it does instead of lying awake."

"And if it takes me back? If it reaches into me and switches me off like a lamp?"

"Then we sing louder," Rosalind said. "That's the whole plan. It has no backup plan either, in case you're wondering. Nobody ever does. That's why endings are always so surprising."

A long silence. Outside, snow had begun again, softening the pass, erasing the road the Regent had ordered sealed — as if the world itself were quietly taking sides.

"I want the eels back," Serle said finally. "All of it. Even the funeral. Especially the funeral. You don't get to keep only the good ones; that's how the crown got me in the first place — it offered to take the grief and I was young and I said yes." His jaw tightened. "I remember saying yes. Isn't that obscene? It let me keep the yes."

"That's why it works," Rosalind said softly. "Every trap it ever built, it built out of kindness. That's what makes it worse than any monster that snarls. But that's also—" they hesitated, then committed, "—that's also why it can be beaten. Kindness has a seam in it. Cruelty doesn't."

Serle looked at them for a long moment, and for the first time his face did something remarkable: it stuck. Settled into memory like a stone finding its place in a wall. Grey eyes. The scar was real — left eyebrow, childhood, probably the river. Probably eels.

"Give me an hour," he said. "There are six men in this garrison who've started leaving their posts at night to stand at the wall and hum. They think I don't know. The Regent's feeding faster since the beacon dimmed, and hunger makes people strange — makes them reach for what's missing without knowing what it is. I'll leave the postern unlocked and a lantern burning. After that, I'm a deserter, and you're harboring one, and we're all traitors together."

"We prefer 'unlicensed singers,'" Rosalind said.

"I prefer breakfast," said Tam, from the doorway, in his low rough voice, and it was such an ordinary sentence in such an extraordinary room that Serle laughed — a startled, rusty bark, the laugh of a man discovering the equipment still worked.

* * *

An hour later, eight figures moved through falling snow toward the sealed road and the dark beyond it.

Rosalind counted them twice — habit now, the arithmetic carved into them. Caelle humming her harvest-under-ice. Brannic reciting names under his breath like a rosary, four hundred and eleven, four hundred and twelve counting himself. Hesper grumbling. Pip riding on Tam's shoulders, having won the argument about dragons by simply refusing to lose it. Wren walking close to Rosalind, real as a bruise, precious as one. Tam steady as the ground he walked on.

And Serle, in civilian wool that fit him wrong, carrying nothing but a lantern and a face that finally stayed put.

Eight. The key complete.

Rosalind was still savoring it — a rare, dangerous pleasure, savoring — when the beacon on the palace ridge caught their eye.

It flared.

Not the guttering ember-dim glow of the dying dragon they'd watched from the high passes. A flare — sudden, violent, white-gold, like a scream made of light — and even at this distance, even through snow, Rosalind felt it in their teeth, in the scar-tissue where their memories had burned away. Ashvane's flame, answering something. Or warning of something.

Then, beneath the flare, faint as a thread, they heard it: singing. A single voice, thin with distance and age and effort, rising from under the earth itself.

Maren's decoy chorus had gone silent days ago. This was not Maren.

This was Ashvane — and Ashvane was no longer singing alone.

Beneath the dragon's ancient thread, woven tight as rope, another voice had joined the song. Deep. Wrong. Patient. A voice Rosalind knew from a feast hall lamplight and a corridor encounter lasting eleven seconds.

The Regent had gone down to the cavern. And it wasn't fighting the dragon's song.

It was learning it.

"Hurry," Rosalind said, and the Choir broke into a run through the snow, eight sets of feet hammering the frozen road, carrying the key toward a lock that might already be turning.
