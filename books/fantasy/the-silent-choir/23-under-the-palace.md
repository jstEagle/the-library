## 23. Under the Palace

The way down began behind a wall of wine racks that Tam found by leaning against it and frowning.

"Here," he said, and knocked twice with the flat of his knuckles. The sound came back wrong—hollow where it should have been stone-on-stone, the way a door comes back hollow when there is a room on the other side of it. He knocked again, lower this time, and Rosalind felt it in her own chest, an answering hum like a struck bell.

"How long have you been able to do that?" Wren asked.

"Since I was four." Tam worked his fingers into the seam between two bottles of a vintage no one had drunk since before the Regent wore the crown, and pulled. "My mother said I was listening to the house gossip."

The wall swung inward on hinges gone green with disuse, exhaling air that tasted of cold iron and old candle smoke. Beyond it, stairs descended into dark—not the ordinary dark of a cellar, but the particular dark of a place that had decided, a long time ago, to be forgotten.

Rosalind lit the first of her contraband candles—the same kind she'd smuggled through Thornfell what felt like a lifetime ago, wicks of woven rush, tallow cut with beeswax—and held it out over the stairwell. The flame leaned toward the darkness as if being pulled.

"Well," she said. "It wants us to come down. That's either very good or very bad."

"When has it ever been anything else?" said Wren cheerfully, and stepped past her onto the stairs.

Behind them, at the mouth of the passage, Serle stood with his lantern held high, taking one last look at the wine cellar they'd entered by—the palace's memory-vaults were above them now, shelves and shelves of stolen lives sleeping in glass—and then he ducked under the lintel and let the door close behind them with a sound like a held breath finally released.

Eight voices. For the first time since Maren's song had gone silent somewhere above them, all eight stood in one place: Rosalind with the Codex strapped to her back; Tam with his mason's hands loose and ready; Wren, newly real and wearing it like a coat that almost fit; Sister Anselm the nun, who had sung her prayers for forty years without knowing why they felt incomplete; Jailer Brack, whose cell keys hummed when he walked past certain doors; the child prodigy Liss, nine years old, who kept asking questions in a whisper because the dark seemed like the sort of place that would mind; old Hollis the gardener, who had once sung the orchards awake each spring and thought everyone could do it; and Serle, who carried his obedience like a stone he'd finally been allowed to set down.

And above them all, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, the dragon's song.

* * *

They descended for hours.

That was the thing none of the Codex's maps had conveyed—the sheer depth of it. Ede Voss had drawn the under-palace as three neat pages of passages and chambers, but pages are flat, and the way down was not. Stairs became ramps became ladders became a chimney of rough-hewn stone that Liss climbed like a squirrel while Hollis grumbled about his knees and Sister Anselm told him his knees had no right to complain after forty years of kneeling.

The passages themselves were strange. Not ruined—maintained, somehow, or perhaps simply preserved by their own forgetting—but wrong in small ways. Corridors that curved when they should have run straight. A staircase of exactly fourteen steps that took twenty to climb. Doorways sized for people slightly taller than any of them, as if the builders had expected someone else entirely.

"The palace grew," Rosalind said at last, running her hand along a wall that was warm, faintly, like the flank of a sleeping animal. "Downward. Around the dragon."

"Palaces don't grow," said Brack.

"This one had a hundred years and a reason." She lifted her candle. In its light the wall's stone showed fine veining, like marble, like muscle. "Everything in this kingdom bends around what it can't afford to remember. Why should the stones be different?"

Tam walked with his palm flat against the rock the whole way down. Every so often he stopped, frowned, tapped twice, listened, and pointed them left or right at junctions where the Codex showed nothing at all. When Rosalind asked him how he knew, he shrugged in his slow way and signed—Wren translating, her hands still clumsy at it—"Stone remembers weight. It's telling me which way the heavy things went."

"Heavy things," Serle repeated.

"The big ones," said Tam, and looked down, and did not elaborate, and nobody asked him to.

They ate cold food at a landing where a stream of meltwater crossed the passage, singing over stones in a voice so clear and empty that Liss covered her ears until it passed. They slept in shifts in a chamber whose ceiling was painted with stars—real constellations, but arranged wrong, arranged as they must have been a century ago, and Hollis lay staring up at them for a long time before saying quietly, "I remember when the Shepherd's Crook pointed east," and no one asked him how.

And always, below them, fainter than breath, the song.

By the second day Rosalind could no longer pretend she wasn't hearing words in it. There were no words—that was the horror of it, or the mercy—but there were shapes, the ghosts of meanings worn smooth by repetition, the way a coin worn smooth by ten thousand hands still says *coin* to your fingers. It sang names without sounds. It sang a bridge being built, and a harvest failing, and someone's grandmother laughing at something, and a plague year survived, and a wedding in the rain, all of it braided together into one long thread that never ended and never changed key.

"It's tired," Liss whispered, during a rest stop, unprompted. Everyone looked at her. She hunched her shoulders. "I can hear it better when I'm quiet. It's like—it's like when my tutor made me practice scales for six hours and I got to the point where I wasn't playing anymore, I was just moving my fingers." She looked up, and her eyes were too old for her face. "It forgot the top notes a long time ago. But it keeps going anyway, because if it stops—"

She didn't finish. Nobody needed her to. If it stops, the anchor fails. If the anchor fails, the Regent falls—or worse, doesn't.

Rosalind checked the straps of the Codex and said, "Then we shouldn't keep it waiting," and they went down again.

* * *

The last door was not a door.

It was a gap where the passage simply gave up pretending, widening and widening until the walls fell away into darkness on every side and the candle flames stretched tall and thin, pointing down like compass needles. The air grew warm. Not unpleasantly—warm the way bread is warm, the way a hearth is warm an hour after the fire has died, warmth that was memory rather than presence.

"Everyone hold hands," Rosalind said. "No. I mean it. All of you. If we get separated down here we may never find each other again, and I include myself in that worry."

Wren took her left hand. Tam took her right. The chain formed itself—Liss gripping Wren, Hollis gripping Liss, Anselm and Brack and, at the end, after a pause that lasted three full seconds, Serle taking Hollis's weathered hand as though it might bite him.

They walked out onto nothing, and nothing held them: a ledge of black glassy stone curving away into vastness, and beyond it—

Rosalind had prepared herself for bigness. The Codex had warned her; Ede's maps had a scale bar labeled *not to scale, forgive me*. She had imagined a whale, a hill, a cathedral.

Ashvane was a country.

The dragon lay coiled in a cavern so large that the far walls were rumors. Its body was mountain-dark, scaled in obsidian plates grown dull with age, and here and there along the miles of its length faint seams glowed—a dull orange, guttering, like coals raked over and left to die. Its wings were folded tight against its sides, and even folded they were the size of sails, of fields. Its head rested on the ledge ahead of them, or rather its head rested near the ledge, for the head alone was larger than the Guildhouse Rosalind had sheltered in, larger than the chapel vault where the Codex had first spoken to her. One eye was open.

It was ember-colored, and it was dim, and it was looking at them with an expression Rosalind had seen only once before—on Maren Ostwick's face, in her lockkeeper's hut, the night the old woman had talked until dawn about everyone she had ever loved.

Recognition. And exhaustion. And hope, worn down to a nub, still refusing to go out.

"Oh," said Liss, very softly. "Oh, it's *sad*."

"Don't be silly," began Hollis, and then he looked properly, and stopped being silly forever.

Tam released Rosalind's hand and walked forward alone to the very edge of the ledge. He knelt, pressed both palms flat against the black stone, and bowed his head, and Rosalind watched his broad shoulders begin to shake.

"What is it?" she called softly. "Tam?"

He turned. His face was wet. "He's been holding the whole kingdom," he said, his voice cracked and wondering. "All this time. Alone. Every name. Every face. Every song anyone ever sang in a tavern, every prayer, every argument in every kitchen—he holds all of it, and there's been no one—" His hands curled against the stone. "A hundred years with no one to sing harmony. Do you know what that does? To a voice? To a *person*?"

The great eye blinked, slowly, like a season changing.

And then Ashvane spoke, and the cavern spoke with him, and the ledge beneath their feet thrummed like the body of an enormous instrument:

**"VISITORS."**

The voice was ruinous. It was also, underneath the ruin, beautiful—there was music in it the way there is iron in blood, essential, structural. It was a voice that had once filled skies.

"Yes," Rosalind said. She stepped forward to stand beside Tam, and made herself lift her chin, though her knees had opinions. "We've come a long way. Through Thornfell, through the passes, through your own under-places. We're the Choir. What's left of it, and then some more than that."

**"THE CHOIR."** The word moved through the stone like a slow tide. **"EDE SPOKE OF YOU. HE SPOKE OF NOTHING ELSE, AT THE END. THE CHOIR WILL COME. THE CHOIR WILL SING IT BACK."** A pause, long enough for a heartbeat to be born and die. **"I STOPPED BELIEVING HIM SOME DECADES AGO. FORGIVE ME. HOARDING HOPE IS HARDER THAN HOARDING GOLD, AND I HAVE HAD NO ROOM LEFT FOR EITHER."**

"We brought the Codex," Rosalind said. "His book. His instructions. We know what he tried to do with the crown, and we know what it made of him, and we came anyway."

Something moved through the vast dim shape—too large to call a flinch, more like the tremor that runs through the ground before an earthquake decides whether to happen.

**"YOU KNOW WHAT WEARS THE KING."**

"We do. We've met it. Some of us have lost things to it." Her hand went, without her permission, to the locket at her throat, empty these many weeks, burned out decoy to save her from Serle. "Some of us have lost a great deal."

**"THEN YOU KNOW WHY I AM NEARLY SILENT."** Ashvane's eye dimmed further, a coal settling into ash. **"FOR A CENTURY I HAVE SUNG YOUR KINGDOM TO ITSELF. ALONE. EVERY DAY THE REGENT TAKES MORE, AND EVERY DAY I MUST CARRY WHAT IT DROPS, AND THERE IS ONLY ONE OF ME, AND I WAS NEVER MADE FOR GRIEF IN THIS QUANTITY."** The glow along his flanks pulsed once, feebly. **"I AM DOWN TO THE LAST VERSES, LITTLE CONDUCTOR. WHEN THEY END, I END. AND THEN NOTHING BELOW HOLDS, AND NOTHING ABOVE REMAINS TO BE HELD."**

"I know," Rosalind said gently. "Maren told us. The beacon's been dimming. We saw it from the ridge."

**"MAREN."** The name rolled through the cavern like weather, and somewhere in it, impossibly, was tenderness. **"SHE USED TO SING TO ME THROUGH THE WELL-SHAFTS WHEN SHE WAS A GIRL. OFF KEY. BLESSEDLY OFF KEY. IT WAS THE FIRST HARMONY I'D HAD IN FORTY YEARS."** The eye closed, opened. **"SHE IS NOT WITH YOU."**

"No," Rosalind said. Her voice held. It cost her. "She stayed behind at the Long Stair. She bought us our way down. She's not coming back."

Silence, then—true silence, the first since they'd entered the deep places, silence with weight and intention, the silence of something enormous choosing not to make a sound. When Ashvane spoke again, his voice was smaller, which for him meant merely human-sized, merely heartbroken.

**"SHE WAS THE LAST OF THEM THAT KNEW ME BY NAME."**

"She knew all our names," said Wren suddenly, stepping forward, chin up, hands trembling at her sides. "Even mine. Even though I'm—" She faltered. Even now she couldn't say it easily. "Even though I was made instead of born. She said that didn't make my verse less true. So." She swallowed. "You should know that we intend to finish this. Whatever it costs. That's why we came down. Not for prophecy. For her."

Ashvane regarded her for a long moment. Then, slowly, with a care that made the ledge shudder, the great head lowered until one nostril hung above them like a cave mouth, breathing warm air that smelled of woodsmoke and rain on summer stone and, beneath it, faintly, of every hearth in the kingdom.

**"LITTLE MADE-ONE,"** he rumbled, **"YOU SMELL OF BORROWED THREADS STITCHED WITH TRUE ONES. INTERESTING. INTERESTING AND NEW. I HAD THOUGHT MYSELF PAST SURPRISE."** The vast eye turned to Rosalind. **"AND YOU. YOU SMELL OF EDE'S LINE. OF COURSE YOU DO. HE MARKED YOUR FAMILY SO CAREFULLY, HID YOU SO DEEP—I COULD ALWAYS FIND YOU IF I LISTED, BUT I NEVER TOLD. SOME SECRETS ARE LOAD-BEARING."**

"You knew?" Rosalind's voice came out sharper than she intended. "All this time, you knew what I was?"

**"I KNEW EVERYTHING, CHILD. THAT IS THE PROBLEM. I KNOW EVERYTHING THIS KINGDOM EVER WAS, AND I AM DYING OF IT, AND THERE IS NO ONE TO SHARE THE WEIGHT."** The ember eye banked low. **"UNLESS."**

The word dropped into the silence like a coin into a well.

Rosalind's hand found Tam's shoulder. His muscles were rigid; he was feeling something in the stone that she couldn't yet, some vibration rising through the bedrock, gathering. Behind her, the whole Choir had drifted forward without seeming to decide to, seven faces lit unevenly by guttering candles, watching the last voice of the world's memory gather itself to speak.

**"THERE IS A WAY I MAY RISE AGAIN,"** Ashvane said. **"BUT FIRE THIS OLD DOES NOT REKINDLE FROM ASH ALONE. IT NEEDS FUEL THAT CAN CHOOSE TO BURN. IT NEEDS THROATS. IT NEEDS THE CHOIR TO CARRY WHAT I HAVE CARRIED—TO HOLD, IN MORTAL KEEPING, THE MEMORIES I CAN NO LONGER HOLD ALONE."**

He paused. The pause had pity in it, which was somehow worse than menace would have been.

**"BEFORE YOU ANSWER, LITTLE CONDUCTOR, YOU SHOULD KNOW WHAT IT IS I AM ASKING YOU TO CARRY. ALL OF IT. EVERY LIFE. EVERY GRIEF. AND YOU SHOULD KNOW THAT I WILL UNDERSTAND PERFECTLY IF THE ANSWER IS NO—FOR IT IS A CRUEL REQUEST, AND I HAVE HAD A CENTURY TO LEARN EXACTLY HOW CRUEL."**

The ember eye brightened—one flicker, one spark, the first true light Rosalind had seen in it—and fixed upon her, and waited.
