## 24. Ashvane's Bargain

The cavern did not echo. That was the first thing Rosalind understood, standing at the lip of the great dark with six singers behind them and a torch guttering in their hand: sound went into this place and did not come back the same. Every footstep arrived late and soft, as though the stone itself were listening before it bothered to reply.

Below them, coiled through a hollow that could have swallowed the Guildhouse three times over, lay Ashvane.

They had expected—Rosalind admitted it only to themselves—a monster out of the old woodcuts: wings like storm sails, teeth the length of scythes, fire enough to boil the river. What they saw instead was an old creature gone grey at the edges. Ashvane's scales had lost their color the way a hearth loses its heat overnight, dull charcoal shading to ash-white along the belly. One vast eye opened as they descended the switchback stair carved into the rock—an eye the size of a millpond, amber going pale at the rim like tea left too long—and regarded them without alarm.

Without much of anything, really. The way a very tired person regards a knock at the door.

"Seven," said a voice, and Rosalind felt it more than heard it, a vibration rising through the soles of their boots, through their shins, settling somewhere behind the sternum. "I counted seven on my stair. The Codex promised eight."

"The eighth is coming," Rosalind said. Their voice sounded small in all that dark, and they hated how small.

"Mm." The dragon's head was resting on its foreleg like a dog too weary to lift it properly. When it spoke, smoke curled from its nostrils in thin gray ribbons, smelling of cold hearths and, faintly, of bread. That was the strangest part—the bread. A century of singing memory alone, and apparently what had pooled deepest in the great creature was not kings or battles but ten thousand mornings of someone baking. "Mm," Ashvane said again. "That is what Ede said too."

Tam, beside Rosalind, had gone rigid. He could not hear the words—not exactly—but Rosalind watched his hands rise slowly, palms out, reading the air the way he read everything, and then watched those hands begin, very quietly, to shake.

"What is it?" Rosalind murmured.

Tam turned. His eyes were wet. He pressed one hand flat against Rosalind's forearm and tapped, once, twice, three times—their private signal, worked out over weeks of passes and paranoia, meaning *it's true*. Meaning: *what this thing says is true.*

"He's been holding us," Tam signed, and his hands moved fast now, urgent. "All this time. Every word we've ever spoken in this kingdom—the shape of it is still here. I can feel it. It's like—" he searched, hands faltering, "—like standing under a bell that never stopped ringing."

* * *

They made camp on the wide shelf of stone above the dragon's coils, because Ashvane asked them to sit, and because none of them could have walked back up the stairs if they'd wanted to. Wren built a fire from the driftwood of long-forgotten supply crates, and the light caught the dragon's scales and gave them, for a moment, something almost like warmth.

"You should know," Ashvane said, "before you ask me for anything. And you will ask. They always do, eventually. The ones who find their way down."

"Find their way down?" Rosalind leaned forward. "Others have come?"

"One. A hundred years ago. A man who smelled of ink and stubbornness, who talked the whole way down my stair as though talking were a form of breathing he required." The great eye half-closed. Something that might have been humor moved through the vast face, slow as weather. "Ede Voss. He came to make a bargain, too."

The fire cracked. Somewhere far above, muffled by a hundred yards of stone, the palace went about its evening—boots on marble, a door closing, the ordinary sounds of a kingdom that did not know what slept beneath its throne.

"He asked me for fire," Ashvane said. "Not to burn anything. To *keep* something. He told me what the crown was doing—what the crown had already done to him, by then; I could see it in him, the way his memories had been... trimmed. Like a hedge. Neatly. Lovingly, even. The crown does love what it eats, in its way." A long exhale, and the temperature of the whole cavern dropped a degree. "He asked me to hold the kingdom's remembering while he went up to deal with the thing wearing his king. And I said yes. Because I am a fool with a generous heart, which are the same failing wearing different coats."

"And you've sung alone ever since," Rosalind said quietly.

"I have sung alone ever since." Ashvane shifted, and the ground trembled—not threat, just mass, the honest weight of a body that large rearranging itself. "Do you know what it is, little courier, to carry every memory of a kingdom in one throat? Every grief. Every wedding. The name of every child ever christened in Thornfell, every recipe, every grudge, every lullaby, every debt forgiven and every debt *not* forgiven. I sang them all, night after night, to keep them alive. And the Regent ate what leaked past me. It has been drinking from the overflow of my song for a hundred years, and I have grown too tired to sing loudly enough to waste anything." The eye opened fully again, and Rosalind saw, with a jolt, that there were tears standing in it—great glassy pools catching the firelight. Dragons wept, then. Nobody put that in the woodcuts either. "Three years ago I began forgetting the middle parts of things. Ten years ago, the endings. Soon I will forget beginnings, and then there will be nothing left to hold, and the crown will drink the dregs, and your kingdom will be a place where people wake each morning as strangers to themselves and call it peace."

Nobody spoke. Tam sat with his hands pressed flat to the stone floor, eyes closed, and tears ran freely down his face. Wren had wrapped her arms around her knees, looking smaller than Rosalind had ever seen her. Even Sister Caelia, who had faced down plague carts with nothing but a hymn, had bowed her head.

"So reignite," Rosalind said at last. "You're a dragon. Burn. That's the answer, isn't it? Fire burns the Regent out of the crown, the memories come home, we all go to the pub."

"It would be," Ashvane agreed, "if fire came from nowhere. But fire is *carried*, little courier. Always. My flame is not mine—it is the sum of everything I hold, burning. Memory is fuel. Grief burns hottest; joy burns longest; ordinary Tuesday afternoons keep the embers banked through the worst nights. For a century I have burned myself down to warm the remembering of a kingdom that forgot I existed." The dragon's lips peeled back—not quite a snarl, not quite a smile. "If I simply ignite now, I flash bright and die in an hour, and the last of what I hold goes up as light and heat, beautiful and useless. The Regent would feed on the conflagration like a pig at trough. No. If I am to reignite—if I am to become a beacon instead of a funeral pyre—I need new fuel. Fuel that can *replenish*. Fuel that walks around."

Rosalind's mouth had gone dry. They looked at the six faces around the fire. Tam. Wren. Sister Caelia. Old Joss the jailer with his knuckles like walnuts. Little Merrin, the prodigy, eleven years old and clutching her own elbows. And Serle, sitting apart as always, hollow-eyed, watching everything with the desperate attention of a man trying to remember how to be a person.

"You want us to carry it," Rosalind said. "The memories. In us."

"In mortal throats. In mortal minds. In mortal hearts, which break easier than throats, if I am honest about the engineering." Ashvane's tone gentled, and somehow that was worse. "Each of you would hold lifetimes that are not yours. Not a few—a flood. You would carry a baker's forty years alongside your own twenty. A soldier's dying. A queen's coronation and her arthritis and her secret correspondence with a rival's gardener. You would grieve deaths you never lived through and love people you never met, and you would carry it all *while remaining yourself*, which is the hard part, and the part most likely to fail."

"That sounds like madness," Joss said bluntly. "Begging pardon. But I kept a jail thirty years, and I know what happens to men who take on other folk's sins. They stop knowing which sins are theirs."

"Yes," Ashvane said simply. "That is the risk. Some go mad. Most, if they are held well—held by others who carry their own burdens and can help bear the extra—most simply become *larger*. More. A person who has grieved a hundred griefs learns to be gentle with the grieving. A person who has loved a hundred loves learns to recognize love in strange clothing." The eye turned to Rosalind. "It is not a punishment I offer. It is a sharing of a burden that must be shared or dropped. Those are the only options left. I am nearly empty. The choice is not between carrying and not carrying. The choice is between many shoulders and one tired neck."

* * *

They argued for two hours.

Wren was against it first, loudest, and—Rosalind suspected—out of fear rather than sense. "We don't know what it'll do to *her*," she said, jerking her chin at Merrin, who was pretending very hard to be asleep. "She's a child. She's got barely any self to spare, and you want to pour a kingdom on top of it?"

"She's also the strongest singer among us," Caelia said mildly, "and she knows it, and she'll never forgive any of us if we decide for her."

"I'm awake," Merrin announced, eyes shut. "And I'm in. Somebody has to remember what the good sweets tasted like, and apparently nobody else can be trusted."

Joss wanted terms: shifts, rest, some arrangement whereby they traded burdens among themselves so no one drowned alone. Serle said nothing at all for a long time, and then said, in a voice like a man reading his own eulogy, "I was emptied once already. Whatever it puts in me, it cannot take away something I do not have." Which silenced the tent more thoroughly than shouting would have.

Tam's argument needed no words. He stood, walked to the edge of the shelf, laid both palms against the living rock, and stood there swaying slightly, reading the deep hum of the dragon's fading song. Then he turned and signed, slowly and clearly so everyone could follow:

*"He held everything alone for a hundred years so we wouldn't have to feel the weight. Now he's asking us to hold a little each so he doesn't die of it. That's not a bargain. That's a family."*

Rosalind looked around the fire—at the smuggler's daughter manufactured from borrowed memories, the nun, the jailer, the child, the hollowed hunter, the deaf stonemason who had just defined a family better than any of them could have—and felt something settle in their chest, heavy and right, like a pack finally balanced after miles of walking crooked.

They thought of Maren, who had walked out of the locks on memory alone and stayed behind to sing against hunters, and whose last instruction had been not *win* but *don't let them carry it alone.*

They thought of the vial with their own name on it, and of a boy-king who had begged a child to remember him.

"All right," Rosalind said.

Everyone turned.

"Not 'let me think about it,' not 'we'll vote in the morning.'" They stood, and the torchlight made their shadow enormous on the cavern wall, and they let it. "Here's how it goes. We share it—all of it, evenly, and Tam decides the rhythm of when we take more, because he's the only one of us who can feel when someone's drowning in it before they know it themselves. We hold each other. Every night, we sing what we're carrying to one another, out loud or in sign or however it needs doing, because a memory hoarded alone is just a wound, but a memory shared—" they gestured at Ashvane, at the whole sad magnificent ruin of the creature below, "—a memory shared is a *song*, and songs keep things alive. That's the whole guild lesson, isn't it? That's what the Wayfarers were always for. We just thought we were carrying candles."

"We were," Wren said softly. "We really, really weren't."

"No," Rosalind agreed. "We weren't." They turned to the dragon and lifted their chin, and their voice did not shake, which surprised them, and would have disappointed Maren enormously, since Maren had always maintained that a leader who didn't shake was a leader who wasn't paying attention. "Ashvane. For the Choir of the Silent Song, for the Wayfarers' Guild, last active courier speaking for all present: yes. We accept the burden. We consent to be changed."

The silence afterward lasted four full heartbeats.

Then Ashvane laughed.

There was no preparing for it. The sound rolled up through the stone and shook dust from the ceiling and set the torches dancing, a laugh like a landslide in a cathedral, and Merrin shrieked in delighted terror, and Joss grabbed his mug before it walked off the ledge, and Tam clapped his hands over his ears and grinned so wide it looked painful. When the echoes finally died, the dragon's eyes had changed—the amber brightening at the center, a coal waking under ash.

"A hundred years," Ashvane rumbled, wiping one vast eye with a foreclaw the size of a barn door, "a hundred years I have waited for someone to say *yes* without asking what was in it for them first. Ede haggled. Ede negotiated clauses. You just—" the dragon shook its great head, wonderingly, "—you just *said yes.*"

"Somebody had to," Rosalind said. "You'd have waited another century."

"I might have," Ashvane admitted. "I'm told patience is a draconic virtue. So is gluttony. We contain multitudes." The great head rose, then—for the first time since they'd entered, truly rose—and the movement displaced air enough to flutter every cloak in the camp. "Rest tonight. Eat. Say goodbye to being merely yourselves, because at dawn I begin the pouring, and it will hurt, and you will weep for people you never met, and somewhere in the middle of it you will understand why I never stopped, not once, not in a hundred years."

"Why?" Merrin asked, small and serious in the firelight. "Why didn't you stop?"

Ashvane looked down at her for a long moment, and the old tired tenderness in that ancient face was answer enough—but the dragon gave it words anyway.

"Because somebody has to remember," Ashvane said, "or none of it happened. And all of it happened, little one. All of it. Every bit worth fighting for."

Far above them, muffled through a hundred feet of stone, a bell in the palace began to ring—and stopped mid-peal, cut off clean, as though a hand had closed over it.

Rosalind went still. Tam's hands came up, trembling, and he pointed at the floor, then pressed his palm flat against it, and the look on his face said everything his voice could not.

Something up there had noticed the coal beginning to glow.

Dawn, and the pouring. And whatever wore the king's crown had just begun, very quietly, to scream.
