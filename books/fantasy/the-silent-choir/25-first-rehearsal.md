## 25. First Rehearsal

Ashvane's cavern was not a place so much as a held breath.

Rosalind had thought the dragon would be vast in the way a mountain is vast—impressive, inert, something you stood beneath and felt small before. They had not expected the dragon to be *attentive*. Ashvane lay coiled through the dark like a river of banked coals, scales the size of barn doors, each one faintly warm with a light that came from somewhere deeper than skin. And every scale was listening. Rosalind could feel it the way you feel sun on your face: seven small human figures at the edge of a great dim chamber, and an attention older than the kingdom turning toward them like a flower toward morning.

"You're smaller than the last choir," Ashvane said. The voice did not come from the dragon's mouth so much as from everywhere—the stone, the air, the ember-glow in their own chests. It was a tired voice. A hundred years of singing alone will do that to anyone, even a creature who had once been mistaken for a god.

"We're what's left," Rosalind said.

"Then we begin with what's left." A slow exhalation stirred the dust into constellations. "Stand where you can feel each other. Not hear. *Feel*. Memory-song is not sound, little singers. Sound is only its shadow."

Tam Broadhurst was already moving. He walked to the center of the cavern, knelt, and pressed both broad palms flat against the stone floor. Rosalind watched his face change—the stonemason's concentration, the look he got when reading grain in rock—and then he nodded once, sharply, and signed to them with quick sure hands: *She's already singing. Down deep. Like a heart that forgot it has a chest.*

Wren, sitting cross-legged with her lute across her knees, translated aloud out of habit, then caught herself. "You know," she said to Tam, "a year ago I didn't exist, and now I'm the interpreter for a deaf man and a dying dragon. Identity is strange."

"Sing first," said Sister Ovel, the nun, gently. "Exist after."

* * *

The great song began—not all at once, but the way dawn begins, in pieces.

Ashvane taught it by giving it away. That was the horror and the wonder of it, Rosalind realized within the first hour: the dragon could not simply teach the notes. The song *was* the memories. To learn the melody of Thornfell's founding was to be there, on the mud flats, watching Ede Voss's grandmother drive the first stake while her sister sang the boundaries into the earth so the city would always know where it ended. To learn the verse of the Baker's Bridge was to have flour up to your elbows and a wedding ring warming in your apron pocket because you were saving it for the moment the bread came right.

Rosalind learned the plague years kneeling in ash that wasn't there. Learned the names of the dead in a language of minor thirds, four hundred voices compressed into a single descending line that any child in Thornfell had once known without knowing what it meant—a skipping rhyme, gods help them, children skipping rope over graves their grandmothers had dug.

They broke after that one. Nobody decided to; they just stopped, all seven, like instruments going quiet in the same breath.

"The skipping rhyme," Wren said finally, her voice unsteady. "I used to sing it. In the—wherever I was, before I was real. I sang a memory I never had." She looked at her hands. "I think it was waiting for me. Like a coat on a hook."

"That's the whole point of a kingdom," Maren had told them once, and Maren wasn't here now—Maren was somewhere above them doing something Rosalind refused to finish thinking about—but her voice arrived anyway, the way dead people's voices do. *Memory kept by many is memory kept well.*

Tam tapped the floor twice. *Again,* his hands said. *From the founding.*

He was the one who kept them honest. That surprised no one who knew him, but it surprised everyone who watched him do it. Because Tam could not hear the song—he received it through the bones of the mountain, through his palms and knees and the soles of his feet—and so he heard what none of the hearing singers could: when they drifted. When Rosalind rushed the plague verse out of grief. When Serle flattened a note because the memory underneath it was one the Regent had left him, half-eaten, and touching it hurt. When Wren polished a phrase too beautifully, old habit, the manufactured grace bleeding through even now.

Each time, Tam struck the stone with his knuckles—one sharp knock, felt rather than heard, a heartbeat out of rhythm until they corrected. He became the metronome of the whole. He could not hear the music, so he became its time.

"It's funny," Serle said during the third rest, in his flat careful voice. He was sitting apart, as he always did, a hollow man trying to learn the shape of filling. "I spent my life taking time away from people. Now a man who can't hear a note is the only reason I stay in it."

"That's not funny," said Juniper, the child prodigy, age nine, who had been adopted into the Choir with the gravity of a duchess and the attention span of a sparrow.

"No," Serle agreed. "I suppose it isn't."

But he moved closer to the group after that. Half a step. With Serle that was a parade.

* * *

By what might have been the second day—they had stopped measuring—the memories stopped being lessons and started being weather.

It happened between verses. Rosalind would open their mouth for the coronation hymn and find themselves standing in a harvest field with someone else's calluses on their hands. Ovel mid-prayer would suddenly taste honey-cakes from a festival sixty years gone. The song was not content to be carried; it wanted to be *lived*, and mortal throats were apparently excellent vessels for a century of other people's Tuesdays.

"I've got a baker's hands," Rosalind announced at one point, holding them up and flexing fingers that ached with ghostly flour. "I have never baked anything in my life. These are magnificent hands. There are forty years of mornings in these knuckles."

"They're my favorite part of the song," Wren admitted. "Don't tell the founding verse."

"The founding verse is *jealous*," Rosalind said solemnly. "Look at it sulking in the minor key."

Even Ashvane made a sound that might have been laughter—a subterranean rumble that set the dust dancing and sent Juniper shrieking with delight. "You laugh," the dragon observed, "the way the first Voss laughed. She also made jokes at the edge of terrible things. It is a family trait, conductor. I have missed it."

Rosalind did not answer that. There was too much in it—Ede, the Regent, the cave full of carved childhood, the name Winterbourne worn like a mask for four generations. But they filed it away next to the other things they were carrying, which by now included a miller's wedding, a jailer's mercy, three plagues, two coronations, and the exact weight of a newborn held by a mother whose name meant *riverbend*.

That was the thing nobody had warned them about, Rosalind thought, lying awake on the warm stone while the others slept: identity was not a wall you defended. It was a river you were. And rivers join.

* * *

On the fourth day—or the fifth, or the hundredth; Tam insisted it was Thursday, and no one could argue with him—the Choir sang the founding verse complete for the first time.

Seven voices. One dragon underneath, humming the bass line of the world.

And something happened that none of them had words for, though they all tried afterward, badly, over shared hardtack.

For the length of the verse, Rosalind was not Rosalind. Or rather: Rosalind was, *and* the founder driving the stake, *and* the baker kneading, *and* the nun praying over plague-dead, *and* Tam feeling the song come up through the mountain like spring water, *and* Wren—for the first time, Wren later swore, gripping Rosalind's arm hard enough to bruise—Wren was *inside* a memory instead of beside it, hers, earned, real.

When it ended, the ember-light in the cavern had brightened. Just a little. Just enough that they could see each other's faces clearly for the first time since descending.

Ashvane's breathing had changed. Slower. Deeper. The rattle that had lived in it for a hundred years was quieter.

"Do you understand what you did?" the dragon asked softly.

"Sang," said Juniper. "Obviously."

"We remembered," Ovel said.

"You *shared*." The great head shifted, and one vast eye, amber and ancient, regarded them with something that in a human face would have been wet. "A hundred years I sang this song alone, and it was a tomb. Tonight it was a table. Do you see? Memory does not survive by being kept. It survives by being *given*. You gave it to each other, and it doubled. It *doubled*, little singers. I had forgotten it could do that."

Tam signed something long and fluid. Wren watched his hands, then went still.

"He says," she translated slowly, "that for forty years he's been the only one who remembered his wife properly. Everyone else let her blur. And tonight—" Her voice caught. "Tonight, during the founding verse, she was in it. Some song she used to hum while mending. He doesn't know how she got into the founding verse of Thornfell, but she's *there*, in the harmonics, and he could feel her through the floor."

Silence. Then Ovel reached over and took Tam's hand, and Tam, who flinched from nothing except pity, did not flinch.

"She's in it," Rosalind said quietly, "because you're in it. That's how the song works. It's not a vault. It's a loom. Every thread that joins changes the pattern." They looked around at their impossible choir: a nun, a jailer, a child, a liar-made-real, a hollowed hunter, a deaf stonemason. "Which means everything we carry from here on—we don't carry it alone. Any of it. Even the bad verses."

"Especially the bad ones," Ashvane rumbled. "Now. Sleep. Tomorrow we attempt the Unbinding's opening movement, and it will require all eight voices, and one of yours is currently trying to hide behind the bread."

Every eye went to Serle.

Serle, who had indeed drifted toward the supplies, froze with the guiltless expression of a cat near an unattended fish. "I was counting it," he said.

"You were *hoarding* it," said Juniper.

"I was being *strategic*."

Rosalind laughed—really laughed, for the first time since Maren's song had gone silent somewhere above them—and the sound echoed off the sleeping scales and came back warmer than it went out.

But late in the night, the warmth cracked.

Rosalind woke to Ashvane's silence. Not restful silence—*held* silence, the silence of a creature listening hard. The ember-light in the cavern had begun, very faintly, to flicker. Not with the dragon's rhythm. With another.

"What is it?" Rosalind whispered.

The great eye turned toward the ceiling of the world. Toward the palace. Toward the throne room far above, where a crown sat on a borrowed head.

"It has noticed the light," Ashvane said. "The thing wearing your king. For a hundred years I sang quietly enough that it believed me asleep." A long exhale, and the smell of hot iron filled the cavern. "Tonight you made the song double, conductor. Tonight it was a *table*."

The dragon's scales dimmed deliberately, all at once, like a city pulling down its shutters.

"And things that eat memory," Ashvane finished, very softly, "always notice a feast."
