## 26. The Regent Descends

The first rehearsal had ended an hour ago, and the cavern still hummed with it. Rosalind could feel the song settling into their bones like warmth into stone—Ashvane's great melody, a century of Thornfell carried in seven mortal throats now instead of one tired draconic one. Tam sat against the dragon's foreleg, his palms flat on the scaled hide, feeling the ember-weak pulse beneath. Wren was laughing at something the child prodigy, Ilsabet, had whispered—a real laugh, unpolished, the kind Rosalind had begun to collect the way other people collected coins.

Seven voices. Rested. Ready.

Which was, Rosalind would think later, exactly when it should have known better than to feel ready.

Tam felt it first. His hands went still on Ashvane's leg, and he turned his head the way he did when vibration spoke to him from far away—chin up, eyes half-closed, listening with his whole body. Then he signed, quick and sharp: *Steps. Many. Coming down.*

"How many is many?" Rosalind asked, though they were already moving, waking the others, dousing what little light they'd dared.

Tam held up both hands, then both again, then gave up and simply spread them wide. *All of them.*

* * *

They came down the Long Stair in silence, which was the worst part. An army should make noise—the clatter of arms, the shuffle of boots, the coughs and mutters of frightened people. This army made none. Rosalind, pressed into the shadow of a pillar near the cavern's mouth with Tam beside her and Wren at her shoulder, watched them descend by the dim red glow of Ashvane's embers: rank upon rank of figures in palace livery, moving with the perfect synchronization of clockwork or sleepwalkers.

And then the light reached their faces, and Rosalind understood what Maren had tried to warn her about, all those months ago, in a lockkeeper's hut that smelled of river weed.

They weren't soldiers. They were the forgotten.

Rosalind recognized some of them—not their names, because no names remained anywhere in the world for these people, but their shapes. A woman whose face appeared on no portrait but whose hands had the calluses of a court scribe. An old man in a knight's armor that fit him badly, as if the man who'd worn it properly had been eaten and only the outline left behind. Children. There were children in the ranks, walking down ten thousand steps without stumbling, without blinking, without anything at all behind their eyes except a patient, terrible emptiness.

"They're not dead," Wren breathed. Her voice had gone very small. "Rosalind—they're not dead, are they? I can feel—" She stopped, hand pressed to her own chest, where a manufactured heart beat with borrowed purpose. "I know what empty feels like. From the inside."

"No," Rosalind said quietly. "Not dead. Shelved."

Beside them, vast and dim, Ashvane stirred. The dragon's eye opened—a slit of banked fire—and the old voice that filled the cavern was thin as smoke but steady as bedrock.

*The Regent comes. It has not come down in a hundred years. It did not need to.*

"Why now?" Rosalind whispered.

*Because you are singing its food back into the world,* Ashvane said. *And because I am dying, and it knows that what I anchor, it cannot keep. It has come to make you an offer, conductor. It will be generous. That is how it hunts. It was a guildmaster once, and guildmasters know that the surest road runs through the door a traveler wants open.*

The last figure down the stair wore no armor and carried no weapon. He wore the crown, and the crown drank the torchlight the way it always had, so that King Aldous seemed to walk in a small private night. But up close—close enough that Rosalind could see him across forty feet of cavern floor—he looked wrong in a new way. Thinner. The hollows under his eyes had deepened to bruises. The dragon-fire that powered the crown was guttering, and whatever wore it was guttering with it, and both of them knew it.

The Regent stopped. The army of the forgotten stopped with it, two hundred throats breathing as one.

"Conductor." Its voice was Aldous's voice, warm and reasonable, a king's voice worn smooth by decades of use. "You've been busy."

"I've been rehearsing," Rosalind said. Their own voice came out steadier than they felt. Beside them, Tam's hand found theirs and squeezed once: *I'm here.*

"So I hear." The Regent smiled with Aldous's mouth. It was a good smile. It had been practicing being a good smile since before Rosalind's grandmother was born. "Do you know, I remember this cavern? Not as myself—I remember it through the memories I've eaten. A kitchen maid who smuggled lovers down here. A prince who hid from his tutors among the roots. You all come to dragons when you want to disappear. It's very human of you."

"You didn't come down here to reminisce."

"No." The Regent clasped its hands behind its back, a scholar giving a lecture, a king granting an audience. "I came to offer you a trade. A fair one. I'm told fairness matters to Wayfarers—it's in your founding charter, the one nobody remembers. *We carry what cannot carry itself.* Lovely sentiment. I've always tried to honor it."

"By eating everything that couldn't fight back?"

"By carrying it," the Regent said, and there was something almost tender in its voice, and that was the most frightening thing yet. "Every memory I take is kept, Rosalind Winterbourne. Perfectly. No rot, no grief, no slow fading. The queen Elenor died three years ago in agony, and her daughter remembers only that her mother smiled at the end—because I took the pain, and kept the smile. Your kingdom's plagues are remembered as bad winters. Its wars as misunderstandings. I have spared these people everything that would have broken them." It tilted its head. "You call it theft. I call it the gentlest thing anyone has ever done for them."

"And yourself?" Rosalind said. "Did you spare yourself gently?"

Something flickered behind the king's eyes. Just for a moment—something old and tired and horribly familiar, something with the shape of a person in it. Then it smoothed away.

"I've come," the Regent continued, "to make you rich beyond any courier's dreaming."

It raised one hand. From the shadows of the stair, two of the empty retainers came forward, moving between the others like water finding its channel. One of them carried a small velvet cushion. On the cushion sat a glass vial, stoppered with wax, and even across the distance Rosalind knew it instantly—the same shape, the same pale gleam, as the vial they had already broken open in the memory-vaults above. But this one was different. This one made their chest ache before they'd taken a single step toward it, the way a house makes your feet turn toward it in a strange town.

Inside the vial, faint as breath on cold glass, swirled a year.

"The missing year," the Regent said. "The one my vaults hold under your name. The one where a boy with a crown he didn't yet wear met a child who smelled of candle-smoke and river mud, and they spent a summer being ordinary together, and he made her promise something." Its voice softened cruelly. "He made her promise to remember him. And she forgot, because I needed her to forget, because a conductor who remembers her king is dangerous. I took it for my own protection. You understand. Self-preservation is the first law of anything alive."

Rosalind's throat had gone tight. Behind her, Wren whispered, "Rosalind, don't listen—" and Tam's grip tightened, and Ashvane's ember-eye watched with a sorrow older than kingdoms.

But Rosalind stepped forward. One step. Because the vial was right there, and the year was right there, and somewhere inside it was a summer and a promise and a boy who had begged to be remembered, and every step they'd taken since the Ash Codex had been, if they were honest, a walk back toward this exact moment.

"I'm listening," they said.

"The terms are simple." The Regent gestured, and the whole army of the forgotten shifted aside, opening a corridor from the cavern to the stair—as if to say *the door stands open; nothing here needs to be fought.* "The Choir stops. You leave the way you came, with the Codex, with your deaf stonemason, with your manufactured girl, with all your scattered voices. You go back to being invisible. No one hunts you—my word on it, and my word has held for a century. And in exchange"—it lifted the vial from the cushion and held it out, and the little swirl of light inside turned like a slow golden season—"you drink your year. All of it. The summer. The promise. Him."

The cavern was silent except for the faint tick of cooling stone.

"You'd give up your greatest weapon," Rosalind said carefully. "A conductor with her memory restored is exactly what you shouldn't create."

"Oh, I'd keep a copy, naturally. In the vaults. Insurance." The Regent smiled again. "But you'd have yours. Truly yours. Given freely—which is more than the dragon offers, incidentally. Ask Ashvane what its bargain costs. Ask it what it means to carry lifetimes in a mortal throat. Ask it whether the singers who finish this quest will ever again be entirely themselves." Its eyes—Aldous's eyes, kind and hollow—moved past Rosalind to the others. "Ask your deaf friend what it will feel like when a hundred strangers' weddings flood through a body that can't even hear its own name spoken. Ask your balladeer whether a thing made of borrowed memory can survive holding a thousand more. I am offering mercy. To all of you. Walk away whole."

And there it was—the hook set so gently Rosalind hadn't even felt it enter. Not *stop.* Never *surrender.* Just: *walk away whole.* As if wholeness were a place you could retreat to. As if the people they'd been before the Codex were still sitting somewhere, waiting for them to come home.

Wren came to stand at Rosalind's shoulder. "It's lying," she said. "Partly. Mostly. I don't—Rosalind, I can't always tell anymore, that's the horror of it, it made me, it lies in my native language—"

"It isn't lying about the vial," Rosalind said.

"No," Wren admitted miserably. "It isn't. That's the trap. Everything else might be a lie, but that's true, and truth is the best bait there is."

Tam signed slowly, deliberately, making sure Rosalind watched his hands: *The boy in the vial. Is he still in the crown? Or is only the thief wearing him?*

Rosalind looked at the Regent. Really looked.

"That's the question, isn't it," they said aloud. "My friend asks: is Aldous still in there?"

For a long moment the Regent said nothing. The torchlight-drinking crown sat on a brow suddenly slick with sweat. And when it answered, the voice had lost its warmth, and what remained underneath was neither king nor monster but something desperately, pathetically practical:

"He is what I am made of. We are the same hunger now. If you sing me apart, you sing him apart too. That is the truth your prophecy hides from you, conductor. There is no clean unbinding. There is only me, or there is nothing, and I am offering you the only version of him that can be handed back whole." It extended the vial further. "Take it. Take him. Stop the song. Be kind to yourselves. Be kind to me."

*Rosalind.* Ashvane's voice filled the cavern like a tide. *It lies even now. The king is not the hunger. I have held this kingdom's memory for a century, and I tell you: the boy's true name is still sung in my flame. Faint. Buried. But mine. The Regent cannot reach it, because the Regent cannot eat what is given rather than kept—and that boy gave himself to his kingdom once, wholly, the way you are about to. It has never owned him. Only his shadow.*

The Regent's face convulsed—for one naked instant, the mask of reasonableness tore, and Rosalind saw the thing beneath: vast, starving, terrified, a century old and dying in a guttering crown, clutching its hoard of stolen lives against the dark the way any miser clutches gold.

Then it mastered itself, and held out the vial one final time, and its voice was gentle as snowfall.

"Well, conductor? What will it be? Your year—or your song?"

Rosalind looked at the vial. At the little golden swirl of a summer that had been stolen from them, of a promise they'd made and been made to forget, of a boy who was perhaps still waiting somewhere inside the thing that wore him. They thought of Maren walking out of the locks with nothing but memory, singing a decoy chorus into the dark. Of Wren bleeding real blood from manufactured wounds. Of Tam's hands on stone, keeping time for a world that had never once kept time for him. Of the baker's hands, the plague winters, the founding of Thornfell—all the lives waiting in seven throats to be given back.

They thought about what a memory was worth when the thief handed it over.

And they found, to their own quiet astonishment, that the answer had been forming in them since the vaults, since the cave, since a locket burned in a mountain pass: a memory returned by the thief is still the thief's. Even this one. Especially this one. The year belonged to them—but taking it from those hands would mean the Regent had decided the terms of their whole life, had chosen which pieces and which price, forever. Rosalind Winterbourne had spent thirty years invisible by someone else's design. They would not spend the rest of it assembled from someone else's charity.

"No," Rosalind said.

The word fell into the cavern like a stone into a well.

"We'll take the year," Rosalind said, stepping forward, and the army of the forgotten stirred like grass in wind. "We'll take everything in your vaults. Every life you've shelved. Every name. We'll take the crown off his head and the hunger out of his shadow, and we'll do it the hard way, the expensive way, the way that costs us pieces we'll never get back—because that's the only way anything has ever truly been carried. Not kept. Carried." They drew a breath, and heard behind them, soft and ragged and rising, the first note of the Choir gathering itself. "You offered us mercy. Here's ours: you were a person once. Ede Voss wrote instructions for your unmaking and hid them inside a prophecy, and we found them, and we're going to sing them. And when you come apart—and you will come apart—whatever's left of him goes free too. That's the deal. It doesn't require your consent."

The Regent stood very still.

Then, softly, with Aldous's mouth and a hunger older than the kingdom, it said: "Then we do this the expensive way."

It closed its fist around the vial. The glass did not shatter—it *vanished*, swallowed back into wherever the Regent kept its hoard—and the army of the forgotten raised their heads as one, two hundred empty faces turning toward seven singers and one dying dragon, and began, in perfect unison, to advance.

Behind Rosalind, Tam struck the stone floor with his palm—once, twice, three times—and the Choir fell into step behind his rhythm like a heartbeat finding its body.

Ashvane's ember-eye flared, one last coal refusing the dark.

*Sing,* said the dragon.
