## 27. Rosalind's Refusal

The Regent held out its hand, and in that hand was a small glass vial, and in that vial was a year.

Rosalind knew it at once—the way you know your own handwriting, even scrawled on a stranger's wall. The liquid inside caught the ember-light of Ashvane's cavern and threw it back wrong, warm where it should have been cool, gold where it should have been green. A summer. Their eleventh summer, or what remained of it, sloshing gently against the glass like a firefly in a jar.

"Your missing year," the Regent said, in Aldous's voice, and the terrible thing was how kind it sounded. Not a performance. Kindness had worn this voice so long it had grown comfortable in it, the way a boot grows to a foot. "Whole. Uncracked. I have kept it carefully. I keep everything carefully."

Behind Rosalind, the Choir stood in a half-circle among the dragon's cooling coals: Tam with his broad scarred hands hovering near the stone, Wren pale but upright, Sister Caelow with her rosary wound tight around her knuckles, the jailer Brannic breathing like a man trying not to be noticed by a storm, little Isbet clutching her fiddle case as though the fiddle might be drafted into service at any moment—and perhaps it would be—and old Serle at the far edge, hollow-eyed, holding his one small bundle of surviving memories against his chest like an infant.

Seven voices. The eighth still raw from its making.

And before them all, wearing a king like a coat, stood the thing that had eaten a century.

"You could have it back," the Regent said. "The summer. The boy who begged you to remember him. Every hour of it, returned to its proper place inside your head, as though it had never been taken." It tilted Aldous's head, and lamplight—or what passed for light down here—slid across the crown, and for a moment Rosalind saw the crown's metal drink the glow and give none of it back. "All I ask is that you stop. Put down the song. Walk up the long stair and out into your small lives, and I will never trouble any of you again."

"You're lying," Brannic said flatly. "It lies. They always lie."

"I do not lie," the Regent said, and there was something almost wounded in it. "Lying is wasteful. Lies require you to maintain two versions of the world, and I am—" Aldous's mouth curved, rueful, kingly, charming, "—conserving."

* * *

Rosalind looked at the vial for a long time.

It would be so easy. That was the trap of it, and they knew it was a trap, and they looked anyway, the way a hungry person looks through a bakery window knowing full well the price of bread. One year. One ordinary, sunlit, unremarkable year of being eleven years old and meeting a lonely prince in a garden who made them laugh until their ribs hurt, and who had gripped their sleeve and said *if I ever stop being myself, remember me*—one year, handed back like a lost glove.

Tam's hand found their shoulder. He couldn't hear the negotiation, but he could feel the shape of it through the stone—could feel the Regent's voice as a smooth, oily tremor, and Rosalind's silence as something else. He didn't sign anything. He just stood there, solid as the mountain, and waited.

Wren was watching Rosalind too. She, of all of them, knew what it was to be offered a self by someone else's hand. She said nothing either. But her eyes said, *I know which door this is, and I know what's behind it.*

"Here is my difficulty," Rosalind said at last, and their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect. "It's not that I doubt you'd return it. I believe you would. You keep everything beautifully. That's the horror of you—you're not careless, you're *meticulous*."

The Regent inclined Aldous's head, gracious, accepting the compliment as a compliment.

"But a memory returned by a thief," Rosalind said, "is still the thief's."

Something flickered across the king's face. Something under the king's face.

"You didn't steal it to keep it safe," Rosalind went on, and now the words were coming faster, the way water comes through a crack in a dam, "you stole it because that's what you are. You took my year the way a magpie takes a button—not because you needed the button, but because taking is the shape of your heart. And if I take it back from your hand, then every time I remember that garden—and I *would* remember it, every day, I'd live in it—I'd be remembering through you. Your keeping. Your careful, meticulous, devouring keeping." They shook their head slowly. "I wouldn't be getting my summer back. I'd be renting it from you forever, and calling it mine."

"That is sentiment," the Regent said, "not logic."

"It's both," said Rosalind. "That's rather the point of memories."

Aldous's jaw tightened. And beneath it—Rosalind saw it plainly now, the way you see a second face pressed against a window—a hundred years of Ede Voss shifted and settled, like sediment stirred in still water.

"No one has ever refused me *that way* before," the Regent said quietly.

"What way?"

"They refuse out of duty. Out of fear. Out of some shining idiot love of kingdom and crown." Aldous's eyes—hollow, hollow, hollow—fixed on Rosalind. "You refuse because you would rather grieve honestly than be comforted dishonestly." A pause. "Do you know how many people I have offered peace? Whole kingdoms' worth of grief, and I could lift it, all of it, the way a mother lifts a splinter from a child's foot. And they weep with gratitude." His voice dropped. "You look at the splinter and say *it's mine*."

"Yes," Rosalind said.

"It hurts you."

"Every day."

"And you want to keep it."

"With both hands."

The Regent stood very still. And Rosalind understood, in that frozen moment, that they had not argued with a monster. They had argued with a wound that had learned to walk, and the argument had gone in, and wounds do not forgive the things that probe them.

"You are right," the Regent said softly. "And I hate you for it."

The crown flared.

* * *

There was no wind in the cavern, but the coals of Ashvane's great sleeping body suddenly bent sideways, all of them, as though a gale had come down the stairwell—and what came down the stairwell was worse than wind. It was forgetting. Rosalind felt it hit the edges of their mind first, a soft gray nibbling at the borders, the taste of their own name going faintly papery—

"*Down!*" they screamed, and the Choir dropped, all seven of them, as the Regent raised both of Aldous's hands and the air itself began to peel.

It did not attack the way armies attack. It attacked the way rot attacks—everywhere at once, patient, intimate. Rosalind felt it reach for Wren first, because Wren was newest, thinnest-walled, a self only days old with mortar still wet between her bricks. Wren cried out and clutched her head. Sister Caelow grabbed her and began to pray aloud, fast and furious, and the prayer helped—it was a memory too, a memorized one, worn smooth as a river stone by ten thousand repetitions, and the Regent's gray tide broke against it like water against a well-used door.

But it was coming for all of them. Rosalind could feel it sorting them, tasting them, deciding whose memories to eat first to make the rest easy. It reached for Serle, and Serle—who had been hollowed once already—simply folded to his knees and covered his head, and there was nothing left in him worth stealing and the Regent's attention slid off him like rain off glass, and somehow that was the most obscene thing Rosalind had ever witnessed: a man saved by his own emptiness.

They needed to sing. Rosalind knew it the way you know a staircase in the dark—by the absence of the next step exactly where your foot expects it. Seven voices. No rehearsal. No plan. Half of them had never sung a defensive chorus in their lives; Sister Caelow's repertoire was plainsong and funeral rites, Brannic claimed he could only carry a tune "in a bucket," and Isbet was twelve.

But Maren had told them, on the last night they'd ever seen her, in her dry lockkeeper's rasp, with her blind eyes pointed somewhere just past the horizon:

*"A chorus isn't seven songs agreeing. It's seven songs refusing to be one song. Harmony isn't agreement, girl. It's difference, held together on purpose."*

Rosalind opened their mouth and sang the first true thing they could find.

Not Ashvane's great song—they hadn't earned all of it yet, and there was no time. They sang their mother's face. The one they'd burned out of a locket to save their own life in the pass, the one they'd carried for months as an ache shaped exactly like a face, and they gave it a melody now, rough and wordless and entirely theirs, grief set to pitch.

Tam felt it through the floor.

He couldn't hear it—he had never heard anything—but he felt it rise through the stone into the bones of his feet, into his knees, into his hands, and he struck the ground with his palm, once, twice, finding the rhythm of it, becoming its heartbeat. Then he signed to Wren, quick and sure: *Find yours. Sing YOURS.*

Wren lifted her head. Her manufactured memories were screaming in the gray tide—the Regent was pulling its own strings inside her, trying to reel her back into obedience—and for one horrible moment she swayed toward it, toward the easy warmth of being made and maintained. Then she planted her feet, and she sang her own verse instead: four days of real life. Cold passes. Burnt bread. Tam teaching her to swear in miners' cant. Her first true laughter, which no one had composed.

Sister Caelow abandoned Latin and sang her grandmother's lullaby, badly, off-key, gloriously human.

Brannic sang a jailer's counting song for long night shifts—one, two, the prisoners are dreaming, three, four, the walls are keeping—and it turned out he could carry a tune after all, if the tune was stubborn enough.

Isbet, terrified, played. Her fiddle wavered, found Tam's pulse through the floorboards, and locked in.

Serle knelt in the wreckage of himself, and opened his hands, and let his one small bundle of memories sing too—all he had, a handful of moments the Regent hadn't wanted. A garden. Rain on slate. Someone's laugh. It wasn't much. It was everything.

Seven songs. None of them matching. All of them refusing, together, to be erased.

The gray tide hit the chorus and shattered.

Rosalind felt it happen more than saw it—the Regent's assault striking their mismatched, unrehearsed, impossible harmony and simply failing to get a grip, the way a fist fails to close on water. Because that was the flaw in the theft, the flaw that had been there all along, the flaw the Regent had spent a century refusing to see: you cannot eat what is being given away. Its whole existence was taking, hoarding, keeping—and here were seven people hurling their most precious things outward, freely, at each other, at the dark, at the dying dragon, at nothing, at everything—and there was nothing for the gray tide to seize. You can't rob a house whose doors are all open.

The Regent staggered. Aldous's body staggered, actually, physically, one hand flying to the crown as if to steady it, and the sound that came out of him was not royal and not monstrous but simply *pained*, high and cracked and terribly young.

"You—" the Regent gasped. "You can't DO that. That's not how it WORKS—"

"It's exactly how it works," Rosalind said, still singing, tears running freely down their face, their mother's melody pouring out of them without pause. "You taught us. You've been teaching us for a hundred years. Everything kept can be taken. Only what's given is safe."

For a moment—one long, ringing moment—the cavern held nothing but seven voices and a king's ragged breathing and the slow red pulse of a dragon's dying embers.

Then the Regent screamed, and it was not a scream of rage, or not only that. It was the scream of something that has just been shown, clearly, inarguably, the exact shape of its own mistake, and cannot unknow it. The crown blazed white. The coals flattened. Cracks raced up the cavern walls, and somewhere far above, through a hundred fathoms of stone, the palace groaned on its foundations like a ship in weather.

"THEN I WILL TAKE IT ALL," the Regent roared, and the gray tide came again, tenfold, a flood now instead of a nibble, and the choir braced, singing, singing, their harmony bending but not breaking—

But Rosalind saw what the others couldn't: the flood wasn't aimed at them anymore. It was aimed upward. At the city. At every name and neighbor and street in the capital above. If it could not eat seven stubborn singers, it would eat a kingdom's worth of easier prey, and it would grow fat enough to crush them by sheer weight.

"We can't hold this and fight that!" Wren shouted over the rising song.

"We're not fighting it here!" Rosalind caught Tam's eye and signed fast: *Up. The long stair. Make it follow.* "We're leading it somewhere!"

"Where?" Brannic bellowed.

Rosalind looked at the dimming embers of Ashvane, at the great slow heart barely beating in the dark, at the throne room far above where a crown sat waiting on a head that was mostly empty.

"The one room in this palace," they said, "with terrible acoustics."

Tam laughed—actually laughed, a big silent hunching laugh—and slammed his palm on the stone three times, sharp as a drumbeat, and the Choir turned as one body and ran for the long stair, seven voices still singing, still refusing, while behind them the Regent tore itself free of the cavern wall and came after, wearing a dead century's face, hungry as the end of the world.
