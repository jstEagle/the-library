## 28. The Battle of the Long Stair

The under-stair had been built for servants, centuries ago, when Thornfell Palace still bothered to pretend its kings needed anyone beneath them. It was narrow enough that Rosalind could press both palms to the walls at once — cold stone on one side, older stone on the other — and it climbed in long switchbacks through the dark like a throat deciding whether to swallow.

Behind them, down in the dragon's cavern, Ashvane's ember-light still glowed faintly up through the grates, the only warmth in a palace gone quiet as held breath.

"Heard them before I felt them," Tam signed, his hands quick in the dimness. He kept one palm flat against the wall as he walked, reading the stone the way other men read weather. "Boots. A lot of boots. Not marching. *Shuffling.*"

"Shuffling is worse," said Sister Ophelia, who had spent forty years in a convent and had lost none of her bluntness to the habit. She said it out loud, because she always did, even though half the choir couldn't hear her, and then she signed it too, because Maren had drilled them all until signing was as natural as breathing.

"The retainers," Rosalind said. "The ones the Regent emptied." She thought of what they'd seen below — people with faces like letters written in water, standing in ranks, waiting to be pointed somewhere. "They'll have been sent up the stair ahead of us."

"Then we go through them," said the jailer, whose name was Brannic and who carried a door-bar off his own prison like a walking staff. "There are seven of us. We've got a dragon's whole song in our chests."

"We're not fighting," Rosalind said. "We're *singing*. Fighting is just what singing looks like when the song has edges."

Nettie, the youngest of them, eleven years old and small enough that her chorister's satchel nearly reached her heels, tugged Rosalind's sleeve. Her voice came out thin in the dark. "What if I forget my verse? What if I'm scared and I forget?"

Rosalind crouched to her height. Around them, the others went deliberately loud with their preparations — buckles, breath, Brannic thumping his bar against the floor twice for no reason anyone needed.

"You were scared in rehearsal every day for two weeks," Rosalind said. "And you know what you did anyway?"

"Sang wrong?"

"You sang *anyway*. That's the whole secret, Nettie. Nobody's ever once sung correctly while frightened. We just sing while frightened. It counts the same."

Nettie considered this with the gravity of a judge. "Tam can't hear me if I go wrong."

"Tam will feel your feet go wrong before you do," Wren said, appearing at Nettie's shoulder with one of her sudden smiles — the smile that had once made Rosalind's skin crawl with suspicion and now made something in her chest go soft and protective instead. "That's why he stands behind you. He's not following us, love. He's carrying us."

Tam, overhearing via Nettie's rapid signs, shrugged with the modesty of a man agreeing he was furniture.

Above them, faint through the stone, came the shuffling.

* * *

It began the way all their fights began now: with a note.

Rosalind lifted her hand, and the Choir drew breath — seven chests filling in the dark like bellows — and she gave them the first line of Ede Voss's unbinding, pitched low, the way you'd speak to a spooked horse. The song rolled up the stairwell ahead of them, and where it touched the first rank of retainers, the empty ones faltered.

They did not fall. Falling would have required them to have been standing by choice. They simply *stopped*, mid-shuffle, mouths open, eyes like unlit windows, and the song poured past them and up.

"Keep moving!" Rosalind called over her own melody. "We don't stop to admire our work!"

They moved. Up and up, switchback after switchback, the Choir's harmony braiding itself around the stone — Tam feeling the structure of it through his boot-soles and stamping corrections with his heel, Nettie's high line threading above them like wire, Brannic booming the bass so hard that dust sifted from the ceiling and glittered in Ashvane's distant glow. Sister Ophelia sang with her eyes shut and her hands folded, looking less like a warrior than a woman saying grace, which, Rosalind reflected, was exactly what she was doing.

The retainers came in waves. They could not be killed — there was nothing left in them to kill — but the song could unmake the Regent's grip on them, note by note, and each wave that met them dissolved into confusion, wandering back down into the dark, some of them weeping without knowing why.

"It's cruel, isn't it?" Nettie panted, between verses. "Unmaking them. When they didn't ask for any of this."

"It's kinder than leaving them empty," Rosalind said, and did not add what she was thinking, which was: *and someday, if we win, everything eaten comes home, and these poor souls wake up in their own lives with no idea how they got here.*

She was thinking it so hard she almost missed the change in the music.

* * *

The Regent adapted. Of course it did. It had worn a kingdom for a hundred years; it knew how songs worked better than most composers.

Halfway up the fourth flight, the retainers stopped coming in ranks. They started coming in *pairs*, flanking, timing themselves between phrases — slipping forward in the rests, hands reaching, and where they touched, the song snagged. Brannic took a grip on the arm and felt his bass line gutter. Ophelia caught Nettie's shoulder and the girl's high note cracked like ice.

"Rests!" Rosalind shouted. "No more rests! Breathe through it — circular, like the sea — we do not give them gaps!"

So they learned, running and singing, to breathe the way Ashvane breathed: taking in air while still giving out sound, each singer covering the next, an unbroken ribbon of voice wound up the stairwell like thread around a spindle. It was the hardest thing any of them had ever done. Brannic's face went purple. Ophelia's lips whitened. And it worked — the retainers' timing broke against a song with no seams, and for three flights, three glorious flights, the Choir climbed untouched.

Then the Regent stopped being clever and started being heavy.

It came up through the retainers the way cold comes up through a cellar floor. The stone itself seemed to darken. The song, which had been flying, suddenly had weight strapped to every syllable, and Rosalind felt her voice drag like she was singing underwater.

"*Hold the line,*" she managed. "*Whatever comes. Hold—*"

The next flight's worth of retainers came all at once, shoulder to shoulder, a wall of empty flesh filling the stairwell from wall to wall, and they did not shuffle this time. They braced. They linked arms. They had become, in the Regent's desperation, an actual barricade of stolen people.

"We can't sing through that," Ophelia said. "Not close enough to touch. It'll pull the voices right out of us."

"Then we stop singing," Rosalind said, "and we climb."

"With what?" Brannic hefted his bar. "I'll crack skulls, but they don't feel skulls."

"I'll make the gap." Wren stepped forward, and there was something in her stance Rosalind had never seen before — not the polished confidence of the balladeer, not the manufactured grace. Something rawer. Newer. "I'm the only one here who knows what it's like inside those. I was made by the same hand. Let me try."

"Wren—" Rosalind began.

"They won't fight me," Wren said. "I think — I think part of them will recognize what I am. Or what I was." She looked back at the Choir, at Nettie, at Tam, and her smile wobbled in a way no manufactured smile ever could. "Keep singing. Whatever you hear. Keep singing."

She went down the stairs alone, into the dark, humming.

* * *

What happened next, Rosalind could never afterward separate into clean pieces. Memory does that with horror; it keeps the sound and loses the order.

Wren reached the barricade. The retainers turned toward her — all of them at once, a hundred faces moving together, which was the single worst thing Rosalind had yet seen in this palace of horrors. And Wren stood in front of them and opened her mouth, and what came out wasn't a battle-song at all.

It was a lullaby. One of the Regent's own manufactured melodies, one of the too-polished songs she'd used to deceive them, sung now with cracks in it — sung *badly*, on purpose, ragged and human — and the retainers swayed toward her like grass toward sun.

For a moment it worked. The wall loosened. Arms unlaced. A gap opened, wide enough for two, then three—

And the Regent noticed what she was doing.

Rosalind felt it happen through the song itself: a great attention turning, like a searchlight, like weather. The retainers snapped back into formation. And something else came up the stair — something fast, low, wrong — and struck Wren the way a wave strikes a swimmer.

She screamed.

The Choir broke. There was no holding a line through that sound. Rosalind was moving before she'd decided to, down the steps, Brannic roaring beside her, and they found Wren crumpled against the wall with the retreating retainers scattering around her like startled birds.

She was bleeding.

That was the detail that stopped Rosalind's heart. Wren was made of manufactured memory — woven stuff, borrowed stuff, a puppet of the Regent's devising given a soul by accident. And manufactured memory was pouring out of her through a gash along her side, and it did not look like blood. It looked like light going out. Like a fire seen through closing doors. Each pulse of it carried images — Rosalind could see them, everyone could, glowing faintly in the air: a harvest festival that had never happened. A teacher who had never lived. A childhood assembled from spare parts, leaking away onto the palace stones.

"Nononono—" Nettie was there, kneeling, pressing her small hands against the wound as if a child's pressure could hold a soul in a body. "Wren. Wren, look at me, keep your eyes open—"

"Pretty good trick," Wren whispered. Her color was wrong. Her voice was wrong — thinner, as though the wound had taken some of her volume with it. "Sung badly. On purpose. Nobody ever taught me to sing badly. You all did." She tried to laugh and it cost her visibly. "Best thing I ever learned."

"Can you walk?" Rosalind asked, hauling Wren's arm across her shoulders. "We carry you. We climb. We get you to Ashvane, the flame will—"

"Will it?" Wren asked, very quietly.

Nobody answered. Because nobody knew. Dragon-fire had rekindled memories for a century. But Wren wasn't a lost memory. Wren was a *made* one. And the fire that restored true things might not know what to do with a person stitched together from lies.

"Up," Rosalind said, because the alternative to up was nothing. "Everyone up. Brannic, take her other side. Nettie, you're on her verse now — both verses. Ophelia, we resume in four bars. We sing her up this stair whether she can hear it or not."

"She can't hear it," Tam signed grimly, catching up. Then he pressed his hand flat over Wren's heart, held it there a moment, and signed again, slower: "But I can feel this. She can feel this. Put me beside her."

They climbed. Oh, how they climbed. The Choir sang the unbinding up six more flights with Wren hanging between Brannic and Rosalind and Tam's hand never leaving her sternum, feeding the vibration of the song straight into her chest, the only road left to her. The retainers came and the song broke them. The Regent pressed down and the song bent but did not snap. Twice Rosalind looked down at Wren's face and saw her lips moving, shaping harmonies she could no longer voice, and twice that sight wrung more effort out of the Choir than any command could have.

At the top of the eighth flight, the stair ended at a door banded in iron, and beyond it — muffled, distant, but unmistakable — was the sound of the city. Shouting. Screaming. The wholesale sound of a capital forgetting itself.

And under it, thinner than ever, Ashvane's beacon-song, guttering.

Rosalind set Wren down gently against the wall. The girl's eyes tracked her, still present, still hers — but dimmer, like a lamp turned low to save oil.

"How much of you is left?" Rosalind asked. No point pretending otherwise. Wren had never tolerated pretense since the day she'd become real.

Wren considered. Held up her hands, wobbling them: *some.* Then she signed, clumsily, with fingers that kept losing their certainty: *Enough for one more song. Maybe. Ask me tomorrow.*

"There might not be a tomorrow," Ophelia said softly, and no one told her off for it, because it was true.

"Then we spend tonight getting her to morning," Rosalind said. She put her hand on the iron door. Beyond it lay the last ascent, the throne room, the crown, the thing that had been Ede Voss. Beyond it, everything.

Behind her, Tam's hand shot up, palm flat, sharp as an alarm.

He was staring at Wren. His fingers moved slowly, so everyone could read them:

"The leaking has stopped."

Rosalind whirled. It was true — the glow of Wren's wound had steadied. Not healing. But no longer pouring out. As though something had... plugged it. As though something outside her had decided she was worth keeping intact.

And in the silence of the landing, very faintly, from far below and far away — from the direction of the locks, of the river, of the roads Maren Ostwick had walked alone — came a chorus. An old woman's voice, cracked and stubborn and utterly unmistakable, singing a decoy's song with everything she had, drawing the Regent's attention like a moth to a lamp.

Maren had come. Maren was buying them time with the only coin she had left.

Rosalind pressed her forehead briefly against the cold iron door. Then she stood, and wiped her face, and turned to her battered, bleeding, impossible family.

"One more flight of stairs," she said. "Sing her there. Sing her all the way."
