## 29. Wren's Choice

The landing at the top of the under-stair was barely wide enough for the seven of them, and it smelled of dust and old candle smoke and, now, blood.

Wren lay on her back with her head cradled in Tam's enormous hands, which was rather like watching a sparrow nest in a quarry. Her balladeer's coat—embroidered silver thread, three seasons out of fashion, because Wren had stolen it from a man who had stolen it from a woman who could actually afford it—was dark across the chest where the Regent's empty retainers had caught her with a spear that should have missed. She'd stepped into the breach to shield the child prodigy. She'd known exactly what she was doing. That was the part Rosalind couldn't stop looking at.

"Stop looking at me like I'm a eulogy," Wren said. Her voice was thin but still carried that performer's polish, the edges sanded smooth. "I've died on stage before. Well. Fainted dramatically. Close enough."

"You were speared," Tam said. He couldn't hear his own voice, so he never softened it, which Rosalind had come to love about him. "That's not close enough to anything."

"I'm aware." Wren tried a smile. It worked, mostly. That was the terrible thing about Wren—the smile always worked, even now, even when there was nothing behind it left to sell.

Because there wasn't much behind it at all. That was the truth they had all been circling since the chapel vault, since Tam's palms had read her songs against the stone and found them hollow as painted eggs. Wren's memories were manufactured. Stitched together by the Regent from scraps of other people's lives—a harvest festival she'd never attended, a first kiss borrowed from a shepherd, a mother's lullaby in a voice that belonged to someone dead two hundred years. She was the Regent's creation, sent to lead them astray, and somewhere between the blizzard and this stair she had chosen not to obey.

And now she was bleeding manufactured blood onto real stone, and it was pooling just like the genuine article, and nobody knew what that meant except that it looked fatal.

Rosalind knelt beside her. Around them the stairwell echoed with the fading clash of retreat—the hunters below had pulled back to regroup, and the silence was worse than the fighting. Somewhere far overhead, muffled by ten thousand tons of palace stone, the beacon flame guttered. Ashvane's ember-heart, dimming. The clock in the dark.

"Wren." Rosalind took her hand. Cold. Too cold, too fast. "Listen to me."

"That's rich, coming from you. You sing at people."

"Wren."

"Fine." But her eyes were losing their focus, drifting toward some middle distance where performers go when the audience stops believing.

"You said you have memories," Rosalind said. "Borrowed ones. A whole life stitched out of other people's leavings. Offer it."

Wren blinked. "I—how did you—"

"You've been working up to offering it for an hour. You keep touching your coat pocket where you used to keep your songbook. You're composing your last number, Wren. You're terrible at secrets. You once told me a surprise birthday party *during* the surprise birthday party."

A laugh came out of her, wet and startled. Tam made a low sound in his chest—not a chuckle, more a landslide considering its options—and even Sister Eluned, the nun who had not smiled once in four days of marching, turned her face away to hide something that might have become one.

"So what if I was?" Wren said. "Think about it. My head is full of the Regent's stolen goods. Plagues and weddings and a baker's hands and the founding of Thornfell—I've got lifetimes in here. Enough to fuel the eighth voice. Enough to finish the Choir without—" her breath caught, and she pressed on through it, professional to the last, "—without anyone else having to carry something new. Use me. Sing me out. I'm a song already written. All you have to do is perform it."

The Choir went quiet around them. Joss the jailer shifted his weight. Little Merrin, the prodigy, nine years old and still shaking from the spear that had nearly found her, looked at Wren with the enormous unguarded eyes of children who understand death before they understand taxes.

"It would work," Maren's voice said in Rosalind's memory. *The eighth voice needs a life to sing. Any life. The fire doesn't check whose.*

It would work. That was the horror of it. They could open Wren up like a strongbox and pour out every stolen year, and the great song would have its eighth part, and the Choir would rise complete, and Wren would die having been useful—which was, Rosalind suspected, the only thing the Regent had ever taught her to want to be.

"No," Rosalind said.

Wren's face did something complicated. "You don't get to be sentimental. Not now. Not with everything—"

"I'm not being sentimental. I'm being accurate." Rosalind squeezed her hand. "You're asking us to sing the Regent's version of you. To make your last act on this earth a performance of the lie it built you out of. And worse—you're asking the eighth voice of the Silent Choir to be *stolen goods*. We are about to walk into that throne room and sing a kingdom back to itself, Wren. Every note has to be true. You told me that. In the pass, in the blizzard, when you thought I wasn't listening. You said, 'The song fails if one voice is lying.' Where do you think you learned that?"

"I learned it from a memory that isn't mine!"

"Then it's mine now," Rosalind said. "I heard you say it. Which means it happened. Which means it's true, and it's yours, and so is everything else you've actually done since I met you."

Wren stared at her. Blood loss was making her eyes glassy, but underneath the glass, something was turning over.

"The harvest festival wasn't real," Rosalind went on, softer now. "But the night you told us about it by the fire, and Tam laughed so hard he cracked a slate—he remembers that. Your first kiss was a shepherd's, borrowed. But the night you kissed Tam's forehead when his hands were too cold to feel the stone—that was nobody's but yours. Nobody wrote that. The Regent didn't put it in you. You *did* it." She looked around at the others. "All of you. Tell her."

Sister Eluned knelt on Wren's other side, robes pooling, and took her free hand with the brisk tenderness of a woman who had bandaged a hundred dying strangers and never once called any of them stranger. "You stole my bread in Thornfell," she said. "Week ago. I let you, because you hummed while you did it, badly, and it was the first music I'd heard inside these walls in eleven years. I remember that. I hold it."

Joss crouched down, jailer's keys still clinking at his belt out of pure habit. "You cheated at cards," he said. "Three nights running. I let you win the third night because you were so pleased with yourself it seemed cruel not to. I remember being annoyed. I remember being fond. Both are mine, and both are about you."

Merrin crept forward and put one small hand flat on Wren's sternum, just beside the wound. "You pushed me out of the way," she said simply. "I was slow and you were fast and now I'm here. That's the realest thing anybody ever did for me."

Tam couldn't speak gently—he didn't know how loud he was—but he leaned down until his forehead nearly touched Wren's, and he pressed her palm flat against his own chest, against the great slow drum of him, so she could feel what he felt the way he felt everything. His heart, hammering. Fear for her. Grief ahead of schedule. All of it vibrating through skin into skin.

*You are held*, that touch said, in the language he had. *You are held, therefore you are.*

Wren's breath hitched. "I'm dying," she whispered. "Don't waste—don't waste good remembering on a forgery."

"You're not a forgery," Rosalind said. "You're a page somebody else started writing on. And we're going to finish the sentence ourselves. That's how identity works, Wren. It isn't what you keep in the vault of your own skull. It's what other people hold of you. The kingdom forgot itself because no one was left to remember it. The Regent starves because it eats everyone who could hold *it*. And you—" her voice caught, and she let it, because a conductor who hid her own grief taught her singers to hide theirs, "—you thought you were nothing because you thought remembering only counted if it happened inside your own head. It doesn't. It never did. So we're going to do it properly. All of us. Out loud. Are you listening?"

"I'm always listening," Wren whispered. "It's my whole tragedy."

"Then hold still," said Rosalind Winterbourne, courier of the Wayfarers' Guild, last conductor of the Silent Choir, "and be remembered."

She began to sing.

Not Ashvane's great song. Not yet. Something smaller. She sang the way she'd delivered contraband candles through Thornfell—quietly, carefully, along routes nobody was watching—and what she carried was Wren's actual history, the brief true ledger of her, assembled from six days of shared road:

*Born of nothing, or near enough—of a crown's hunger and a stolen shape,* she sang, and did not flinch from it, because a true history does not edit. *Made as a key to pick a lock, sent walking in another person's boots—*

"That's not flattering," Wren managed.

"Hush. It gets better."

*—and on a mountain road she met a courier with a crooked smile, and a stonemason who spoke in earthquakes, and for the first time the lie was given a choice.* Tam's palm struck the floor, once, a heartbeat beneath her melody, steady as bedrock. Merrin's clear child-voice slid in above, holding the fifth like it was easy. Sister Eluned, who had sworn a vow of quiet and broken it gladly, added a drone low and warm as hearthstones. Joss rumbled. The two choristers gathered from the locks and mines wove themselves in, and then—

Then Wren herself.

It was hardly more than breath at first. A thread of sound rising off her lips like heat off summer stone. But it found the harmony, because harmony was the one thing the Regent had built into her truly—it had made her a singer, and songs, unlike their makers, get to choose their own loyalties.

Rosalind sang the blizzard, and the cave, and the night Wren had stood watch three hours past her turn because Rosalind's hands were frozen and she'd pretended not to notice. She sang the card games. The stolen bread. The terrible humming. She sang the moment on the Long Stair when a manufactured girl had stepped in front of a real child and discovered, at the point of a spear, that she had become someone worth the price.

And the strange thing—the thing Rosalind would write in the New Codex later, in ink she mixed herself because some things deserve ceremony—was that as they sang, the wound changed.

Nobody could have explained it. Sister Eluned tried afterward and gave up halfway through, waving her hands. But the bleeding slowed. The gray went out of Wren's lips by degrees, the way frost leaves a window when someone finally lights the stove. The borrowed memories inside her—harvest festivals, shepherd's kisses, lullabies two centuries cold—did not vanish. They settled. They stopped being costumes and became wardrobe. And threaded through them, growing stronger with every verse, was the small stubborn crop of moments that belonged to her alone, watered by six voices and one drumming palm, putting down roots in soil that had spent its whole existence refusing to grow anything.

The song ended. The stairwell rang with it for a moment, stone remembering sound the way stone does.

Wren breathed.

It was not a dramatic breath. It was a ragged, ordinary, human breath, the kind with a cough attached, and she immediately complained about the cough, and Tam laughed—that big silent laugh of his that shook his shoulders like a cart over a rutted road—and Sister Eluned was already tearing bandages from her sleeve with the expression of a woman who intends to be furious with God later but is busy right now.

"Well," Wren said faintly. "I appear to be alive. Someone check whether I'm real. Pinch me. Joss, you cheat at pinching, someone else—"

Merrin hugged her, gently, avoiding the bandage, and Wren went stiff with astonishment and then wrapped both arms around the child and held on like a drowning woman who has just been informed, reliably, that the water is only waist-deep.

"I felt that," she said into Merrin's hair. Wondering. "That's mine. That's—nobody lent me that."

"No," Rosalind said. "That one you earned yourself."

Tam helped her upright, slowly, one enormous hand braced behind her back, and Wren stood swaying on the landing of the Long Stair, alive, real, precarious as new ice, wearing a coat three seasons out of fashion and a history six days long.

"So," she said, when she had her breath back. She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, quick and businesslike, the way performers fix their makeup mid-show. "Eighth voice. Still short one singer. Unless one of you wants to count my heartbeat, which is currently doing percussion entirely on its own."

"We'll find our eighth," Rosalind said. But her stomach had already done the arithmetic, and she knew—as did every soul on that landing—who was left. Who was down in the dark below them even now, regrouping with the hunters, forgettable-faced and obedient, holding in his emptied head the only remaining life in Thornfell that the Regent had touched and not wholly consumed.

Serle.

Later. Rosalind filed it away with all the other costs, in the drawer that never quite closed.

Above them, through the ceiling of the world, the light changed.

It was subtle—one moment the torch-glow on the stones lay amber as it had for a century, warmed by the distant beacon of Ashvane's fire filtering down through palace and mountain alike. The next moment it was thinner. Grayer. As though someone had turned down the sun a quarter-turn.

Tam felt it first, of course. His whole body stiffened, palm flat against the wall, and his face did the thing it did when the stone spoke to him of something bad.

"What is it?" Rosalind asked.

He signed, fast, and Merrin translated in a small voice: *"The city. Above. The singing in the stones—the little songs, the houses and the bells and the names of streets—they're going out. Not one at a time."* The child swallowed. *"All at once. Like snuffing candles by the handful."*

Rosalind climbed the last few steps to the arrow-slit window at the landing's edge and looked out over the capital.

The districts nearest the palace still glimmered with ordinary evening lamplight. But beyond them—whole quarters of the city, streets she had smuggled candles down, rooftops she had slept under—had gone strangely dark. Not unlit. The lamps were burning. It was the light *around* them that had dimmed, the warmth, the sense of lives stacked upon lives, the hum of ten thousand small remembered things. Whole neighborhoods had gone quiet in the way an empty house is quiet.

The Regent had stopped feeding politely. With its dragon-anchor failing and a choir rising in its cellar, it was eating the city wholesale—swallowing names, birthdays, the knowledge of neighbors, the reason anyone stayed—to buy one final assault.

Wren came to stand beside her at the slit, newly-real fingers gripping ancient stone. "How long do we have?"

Rosalind watched a district's worth of window-light flicker like a guttering wick.

"Not long," she said. "Sing while we climb."

They climbed.
