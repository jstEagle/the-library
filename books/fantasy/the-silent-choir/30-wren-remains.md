## 30. Wren Remains

The song ended the way a fever breaks—not all at once, but in stages, each note cooler than the last, until Rosalind found themselves kneeling on the cold stone of the under-stair with their hands still raised, still conducting, as though letting go of the music might let go of Wren too.

Wren was breathing.

It seemed impossible that such a small thing could matter so much, and yet the whole cavernous dark of the palace's bones seemed to arrange itself around that single fact: *breathing*. In. Out. A little ragged at the edges, like a bellows with a split seam, but breathing.

"She's bleeding," Tam said, not looking up from where he knelt beside her. He said it with his hands first, shaping the words, then with his voice—his voice always came second, rough and reluctant, a tool he'd learned to use for other people's sake. His palms hovered over Wren's side, reading her through the stone-dust and the torn cloth. "But it's—" his brow furrowed, hands moving again, searching for the word the way he searched for vibrations in a wall. "*Ordinary* blood. It pools slow. It clots."

"Ordinary," Rosalind repeated. The word tasted strange. Nothing about this night had been ordinary; they had half believed that a made thing would come apart like smoke when wounded, that there'd be nothing to bind but air.

But Wren bled like anyone. Perhaps that was the whole point. Perhaps that had been the point all along.

They worked by touch and by the memory of Maren's gruff field-lessons—*moss on the wound if you've nothing better, and shame on you for having nothing better*—and by the time they had her bound and propped against Serle's broad, unprotesting shoulder, Wren's eyes had found them.

"You sang me," she said. Her voice was hoarse. Not from the screaming, though there had been screaming on the Long Stair, plenty of it. From something else. As if the throat she was using had only just been broken in.

"I did."

"It wasn't very good."

Rosalind laughed—a startled, cracked sound, half sob—and pressed their forehead briefly to Wren's cold one. "No. It wasn't. I had poor material."

"Liar." But her mouth curved, and the curve was new. Rosalind had watched Wren smile a hundred times on the road—that easy, polished, performing smile, the smile of a song written to please. This one wobbled at the corners. It didn't know where it was going. It was the first honest smile Rosalind had ever seen on her face, and it was crooked, and it was beautiful, and it terrified them more than the Regent's army had.

"The others?" Wren asked.

"Alive. All of them." Rosalind counted them off silently, the way they counted candles in a smuggler's pack, out of habit, out of need: Sister Alenne with her broken arm splinted by her own sash; Jailer Corvus, who had not spoken since the stair but who had carried the child prodigy Pip up forty steps without setting him down once; Pip himself, white-faced and clutching his fiddle case as though the instrument were a talisman, which, Rosalind supposed, it was; Tam; Serle; and Ashvane's ember-glow pulsing far below like a heart learning its rhythm again. Six voices besides their own. Seven.

One short.

* * *

They made camp in what Serle said had been a gardener's rest-house, a hundred years ago, back when the under-palace had gardeners. Now it was a round room with a collapsed roof-open sky above and a dead fountain in the middle, and it was the safest place any of them had slept in weeks, which told you everything about the state of the world.

Wren slept first, which surprised no one and frightened everyone. Real bodies needed sleep. Made things hadn't. Every hour she lay there breathing was another hour of evidence, and Rosalind found themselves checking—once, twice, a dozen times—with a hand hovering near her mouth the way you check a candle you can't afford to lose.

Tam sat beside them on the fountain's rim, shoulder warm against theirs. For a long while he said nothing, which was his way, and which Rosalind had long ago stopped trying to fill.

Then, quietly, with his hands resting on the stone between them so the words came through his fingers as much as his mouth: "She felt different. On the stair. When I touched the wall near her."

"How?"

He turned his palm up, considering. "Before—her songs were smooth. Too smooth. Like river stones, all the edges worn off. Manufactured." His fingers curled slightly, remembering. "Tonight, when she threw herself into the breach—when she *chose*—there were edges. Sharp ones. Fear. Regret. She regretted lying to us. She regretted every day of it." His hands went still. "You can't manufacture regret. Believe me. I've tried, after Ellin died. I sat in my workshop and tried to carve grief into stone and it came out pretty and dead every time. Grief has to be *lived*."

"So she's real."

"So she's becoming real." He looked at Wren's sleeping form, and his expression was the one Rosalind had seen him wear over unfinished masonry: the look of a man assessing whether the wall would stand. "Becoming is worse than being. Being holds still. Becoming can be interrupted."

Above them—far above, through the broken roof, past forty floors of stolen history—the city made a sound.

It was not loud. That was the horror of it. It was a kind of *absence*, rolling across the capital like a tide going out: bells that had been ringing the midnight watch faltering mid-peal, forgetting what hour it was. Dogs falling silent mid-bark. Somewhere in the wealthy quarter, an entire street of people, Rosalind would learn later, waking at their tables without knowing their own children's names, sitting politely in the dark waiting for someone to tell them who they were.

The Regent was feeding. Not stealing anymore—*devouring*, gulping the city down in wholesale lots, grinding a century of small lives into fuel. Rosalind felt it through the floor the way Tam felt songs through stone: a vast, greedy vibration, a hunger with architecture.

Pip woke crying without knowing why. Sister Alenne began reciting a psalm and lost the middle verses, her lips continuing around words that weren't there anymore, until Corvus gently covered her hand with his own and she stopped, shaking.

Only Wren slept on, dreamless, newly born, while the world above her forgot itself.

* * *

She woke at grey dawn—or what passed for dawn under a sky the color of unwashed wool—and asked for food.

This became, briefly, the center of everyone's universe.

"There's hardtack," Serle offered, producing it with the careful neutrality of a man offering contraband.

Wren chewed, swallowed, and pronounced judgment: "That's punishment, not food." Then her eyes widened, and her hand flew to her mouth, and she stared at them all with dawning, delighted horror. "I have an *opinion*. About *hardtack*. I hate it. I actually hate it—I've eaten a thousand things in a hundred performances and never hated anything in my life, and now—" She laughed, and the laugh caught on something, and she wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand. "Oh, this is *dreadful*. Is it always like this? Everything just—*arriving*? Feelings, uninvited, all hours of the day?"

"Yes," said Tam, with feeling.

"Yes," said Rosalind.

"Yes," said Pip solemnly, aged eleven, wise beyond his years on precisely this subject.

"Well, I don't like it," Wren announced, and took another bite of hardtack out of pure spite, chewing with the defiance of a woman establishing sovereignty over her own mouth. "And I want it noted that I hate this, too, and that I'm keeping it."

"Noted," Rosalind said softly. "In the new Codex. First page."

Wren went still. "You're really writing it, then. Names and all. Even mine?"

"Especially yours. You're the newest entry. You get to watch yourself being written."

Wren considered this. Something moved behind her face—an old reflex, perhaps, the manufactured instinct to deflect with charm—but it foundered against the new, unpolished thing underneath, and what surfaced instead was simply wonder. "Nobody's ever written me down before. I wasn't *sure* there was a me to write."

"There is now," Tam signed, and then, because the moment demanded it and he was brave in ways people forgot to credit, aloud: "A bit thin. Needs building up. Good foundation, though."

Wren stared at him. Then she laughed—really laughed, the ugly snorting kind, nothing like her stage laugh—and the sound bounced around the ruined garden-room and did more damage to the darkness than any chorus they'd yet sung.

* * *

But the eighth place was not given. That was the thing Rosalind kept turning over, watching Wren pick through Serle's spare cloak for something less funerary. The eighth place had to be *taken*, freely, knowingly—and taking it meant singing Ashvane's great song with a self barely a day old. Meant holding lifetimes in a throat that had never owned even one. Meant standing in front of the Regent, the thing that had made her as a tool, and singing her refusal in her own brand-new key.

Rosalind waited until the others were occupied—Corvus teaching Pip to splint properly, Alenne dozing, Tam carving a notch into the fountain rim for reasons he declined to explain—took Wren aside into the archway where the morning came through thin and pale.

"I won't order it," they said. "Conductor or not. This one's yours."

"I know."

"And I won't pretend the risk is small. You're—you're *green*, Wren. A day old. The song will pour lifetimes through you and some of them will stick, and you'll carry strangers' weddings and plagues in your chest for the rest of your life, however long that turns out to be. And the Regent knows your every weakness, because it wrote them."

Wren listened to all this with her head tilted, and Rosalind recognized the posture too late—it was the listening posture of a performer gauging a room, except now the performance was gone and what remained was just attention, plain and costly and real.

"You gave me a choice last night too," she said. "On the stair. You could have sung the eighth voice's fuel straight out of my borrowed memories—Ashvane said it would work, didn't they. A song built entirely of stolen life. Strong. Easy."

"It would have worked."

"But you sang *me* instead. My actual history. Three years of it. The inn at Millbrook, the first lie I ever told, the night I almost ran." Her voice wobbled. "You made me real in front of the enemy, and it cost you the tempo, and it nearly cost us the stair, and you did it anyway. So no, conductor. I'm not asking whether I may take the eighth place." She lifted her chin, and beneath the bruised exhaustion there was suddenly steel—the same steel, Rosalind realized, that had thrown her body into the breach on the stair, except now it belonged entirely to her. "I'm telling you I already have. I just wanted breakfast first. A person should face a monster on a full stomach. It's the least the kingdom owes me."

Rosalind's throat tightened past speech. They settled, instead, for the oldest gesture the guild had—one courier to another, palm flat, heart-side out. *I carry what you give me.*

Wren returned it, clumsy at first, then sure.

* * *

They gathered at midday in the fountain-room, seven singers and one stonemason who would be their spine, and Rosalind laid out what came next with a courier's economy: the descent to Ashvane, the great song, the climb to the throne room. Above them, the silence-tide kept pulling. Alenne had lost the names of three saints. Corvus had written his daughter's name on his forearm in ink and was checking it hourly, lips moving, re-hoisting it into memory like a man bailing a boat.

"We sing tonight," Rosalind finished. "Because tomorrow, the dragon's fire may be ash, and the Regent will be fat with the city, and there will be nothing left in us but our own names. We move while we're still ourselves."

Serle cleared his throat. Everyone looked at him. He looked, in turn, profoundly uncomfortable, a man perpetually astonished to be included in anything. "The throne route," he said. "There's a service gallery behind the east chimneys. If the Regent's sealed the roads above, it won't have thought of the flue-ways. It thinks in memories, not in mortar." A pause. "I built half those galleries. Before." He didn't say before *what*. Nobody asked. There would be time, afterward, if there was an afterward, for Serle to remember himself back together at his own pace, in his own garden, at his own stubborn speed.

"Then we go through the chimneys," Rosalind said. "Like proper burglars."

"Wayfarers," Tam corrected, deadpan, and even Serle's forgettable face managed something adjacent to a smile.

They broke camp as the light failed. Wren stood a moment longer by the dead fountain, one hand resting on the rim where Tam had carved his mysterious notch—and Rosalind saw her press her thumb into it, testing the edge, memorizing the shape. Proof, cut in stone. *I was here. I am here.*

Then she shouldered her pack, and the eight of them—seven voices and one pair of listening hands—started down into the dark toward the dragon, and the ember-glow below rose to meet them like a held breath finally being let out.

Far overhead, muffled by miles of stone, a bell tried to ring the evening hour and could not recall how.

Wren, walking at Rosalind's shoulder in the dark, hummed the missing chime back to it, soft and clear and entirely her own.

"That's the spirit," Rosalind murmured.

Below them, vast and patient and terribly tired, Ashvane waited—and somewhere in the dark ahead, in the space between one step and the next, the temperature dropped, and the walls began, very faintly, to *forget* the shape of the corridor they were making.
