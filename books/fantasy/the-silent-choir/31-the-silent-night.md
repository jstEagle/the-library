## 31. The Silent Night

The silence came at midnight, and it did not come gently.

Rosalind was asleep in the linen closet they'd claimed on the third floor of the palace's east wing—halfway between the memory-vaults below and the throne room above, close enough to everything and far enough from nothing—when the world simply *stopped*. Not faded. Stopped. One breath there was the distant groan of settling stone, the whisper of Wren's candle across the hall, Tam's slow, whistling snore that he would deny to his dying day. The next breath, nothing.

Rosalind woke with their heart slamming and their hands already moving, reaching in the dark for a sound that wasn't there.

They clapped. Once. Twice.

Nothing. Not muffled. *Nothing*—as though their hands had never met, as though air itself had forgotten how to carry anything at all. Rosalind pressed a palm flat against the floor and felt, faintly, distantly, the deep slow pulse of Ashvane far beneath them, ember-dim but stubborn, like a heartbeat heard through a wall of wool. But everything else—the creak of the building, the wind against the shutters, their own ragged breathing—was gone.

"Oh," Rosalind said aloud, and watched the word die shapelessly on their lips. "Oh, you clever, horrible thing."

The Regent had learned.

It had spent a century eating memories one life at a time, patient as rust. But now the dragon was waking beneath its feet, and seven voices had been rehearsing Ede Voss's unbinding in the dark of the under-palace for three nights running, and the Regent had finally done the arithmetic it should have done long ago: a choir is made of ears as much as throats. Take the ears, and even eight perfect voices are eight people standing in a room, shouting into felt.

Rosalind fumbled for the flint and lit the stub of candle they kept by their bed. Light still worked, then. Small mercies. They dressed with hands that shook only a little, which they counted as a professional triumph, and went out into the corridor where Wren was already standing in her doorway, candle held high, mouth open, saying something Rosalind could not hear.

Wren's face did something complicated when she saw Rosalind couldn't hear her either. She set down her candle, took Rosalind's hand, and pressed it flat against her own throat.

*"Tam,"* she said, shaping each word so precisely that Rosalind felt more than read them. *"Get Tam."*

* * *

They found him already awake, sitting cross-legged on the floor of his room with both palms spread against the flagstones like a man warming himself at a fire only he could feel. His eyes were bright and furious and, Rosalind realized with a lurch, entirely calm.

When Rosalind knelt beside him and touched his arm, he didn't startle. He'd known they were coming. Of course he had. He'd felt them coming up three flights of stairs through forty feet of granite, because Tam Broadhurst had spent fifty years reading the world through his bones while everyone around him pitied his ears.

He took Rosalind's hand and pressed it to the floor next to his own.

And Rosalind felt it.

Felt the palace—not heard it, *felt* it—a vast dark instrument with a hundred thousand strings, all of them thrumming. Ashvane's slow ember-pulse at the bottom, deeper than any drumbeat, older than the kingdom's name. Above it, thinner threads: water moving in forgotten pipes, the shift and sigh of stone remembering it was once molten, the far-off tread of the Regent's empty retainers marching in patterns on the floors above. And threaded through all of it, unmistakable now that Rosalind knew to listen with their skin instead of their ears, the Choir itself—seven heartbeats scattered through the east wing, each one a small stubborn percussion against the great silent dark.

Tam looked at Rosalind, and his expression said, with devastating clarity: *You see? This is what I've had my whole life. You're all just visiting.*

Then he laughed—a real laugh, big and rolling, and Rosalind felt it in their chest where they couldn't hear it—and began, with the brisk efficiency of a stonemason who has just found the flaw in a wall, to plan.

* * *

By the grey false dawn that wasn't really dawn anymore (the sky over the capital had been the color of dirty pewter since the Regent began feeding in earnest; nobody remembered what blue looked like), the Choir gathered in the vaulted antechamber below the throne room, the one Maren had called the rehearsal room back when she'd been alive enough to call things anything.

Maren. Rosalind's chest did the thing it always did now, the tight folding-in, when they thought of her. Three nights ago she'd stood in this very room singing a decoy chorus to pull Serle's hunters away from the singers' route down. Her song had worked perfectly. Her song, and she, had not come back. There was a hollow space in the circle where she used to stand, and every one of them stepped around it without ever looking directly at it, the way you step around a grave.

Seven voices. Sister Alenna, the nun who sang like weather. Jailer Brannock, whose bass could loosen teeth. Little Isbet, nine years old and terrifyingly precise, who kept a dead beetle named Colonel in her pocket for luck. Wren, newly, precariously real, singing her own true verse now—short verse, thin verse, a girl with perhaps six weeks of genuine existence to her name—but hers. Serle, hollow-eyed and weeping and there, singing the scraps the Regent had left him because Rosalind had offered him the choice and he'd chosen, barely, to be someone. Tam. And Rosalind, conductor, keeper of the king's true name, holder of a vial's worth of missing childhood.

Eight, counting Tam, whose voice didn't matter and mattered more than anyone's.

Except right now, in the silence, they were eight people mouthing at each other like fish.

Sister Alenna tried first. Rosalind watched her draw the breath, watched her lips form the opening phrase of Ede's unbinding, and heard—nothing. The other choristers' faces went carefully blank in the way faces do when someone very dignified does something embarrassing. Brannock patted her shoulder.

Isbet started to cry, quietly, furiously, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand because crying was undignified and she had decided some time ago that she did not do undignified.

Serle stood apart, staring at his own hands with an expression Rosalind couldn't parse until they got closer and realized it was recognition. He touched Rosalind's sleeve and shaped words against their palm, clumsy but legible:

*"This is how I lived. After it took everything. A world with no sound in it, and orders. It wants us to be me again."*

Rosalind nodded slowly. That was it exactly. The silence wasn't just tactical. It was *evangelical*. The Regent had looked at eight people becoming a choir and thought: I know what it is to be a body in a quiet room with no self in it. I will give them my whole religion.

"Well," Rosalind said, out loud, pointlessly, savoring the feel of speaking even if no one—including them—could hear it. "We'll just have to be worse at being deaf than it is."

Which was when Tam stood up, brushed off his knees, and took command.

* * *

Here is what no one in the palace understood about Tam Broadhurst, including, until that morning, Tam Broadhurst: he had spent fifty years conducting.

Not choirs. Walls. Floors. Bridges. He'd been a stonemason in the mining towns for four decades, and every arch he'd raised, every foundation he'd poured, he had tested not by ear but by touch—laying his palms against fresh stone and feeling whether the structure sang true or buzzed with some hidden wrongness. He knew buildings the way Sister Alenna knew plainsong. He knew, in his wrists, the difference between a wall that would stand for a century and a wall that would come down in a storm.

And now he walked the antechamber the way he'd walk a building site, touching everything, and the Choir watched him work with the particular awe of people watching a door open where they'd all assumed there was only brick.

He found it within an hour.

The antechamber's floor was old work—older than the Regent, older than Aldous, laid by hands that had understood something about resonance that modern masons had lost. Under the flagstones ran channels, dry aqueducts, hollow vaults, a lattice of air and stone that hummed faintly with every footfall. Tam lay down flat on the cold floor, ear and cheek and palms pressed to it like a man embracing the world, and stayed there so long that Isbet crept over and sat cross-legged beside him in solemn imitation.

When he rose, he was grinning.

He gathered them in a huddle and spoke against Rosalind's palm, slowly, while Rosalind relayed to the others by whispering against their ears and writing on the dust with a finger:

*"This room is an instrument. The whole palace is. The Regent killed the air, but it can't kill the stone. Sound doesn't need air if you're standing on it."*

Brannock frowned and shaped a question at Rosalind, who relayed it: *Even if we can feel it, we can't keep time. We can't hear each other drift.*

Tam's grin widened into something almost frightening. He beckoned Isbet over, whispered—wrote—instructions. Then he sat himself down in the center of the antechamber, crossed his legs, planted both palms flat on the ancient flagstones, and became, for lack of a better word, the floor.

What happened next, Rosalind would spend the rest of their life failing to describe properly.

Tam began to tap. Not loudly—with his fingertips, a rhythm so simple a child could follow it, so steady it might have been cut from granite, because in a sense it had been. And the old floor *carried it*. Through the lattice of hollows and channels, through the resonant bones of the palace, the beat traveled outward through the stone to where each singer stood, and every one of them felt it rising up through their boots, through their shins, into their ribs, their throats, their teeth.

Isbet felt it first—children's bodies are honest instruments—and her face split into a smile so fierce it made Rosalind's eyes sting. She stamped her small foot twice, experimentally, and Brannock, ten feet away, jumped, then laughed his great unheard laugh and stamped back. Within minutes the antechamber was full of eight people stamping and tapping and slapping their knees like the world's most solemn folk dance, laughing at each other, tears running down several faces including, Rosalind discovered with some embarrassment, their own.

Then Tam raised one hand—felt, obeyed, silence of motion—and pointed at Sister Alenna, and drew from her the opening note.

She sang, and heard nothing, and panicked for half a breath—until Tam tapped her heel with one finger, a tiny correction, *up*, and she adjusted by feel, and the note settled into the floor and came up through everyone else like warmth through a hearthstone.

That was the morning's discovery, the one Rosalind wrote later in the new Codex with a shaking hand: *A choir does not need ears. It needs a spine. Ours is a man who has been listening to stone since before any of us were born, and the Regent has just handed him the largest instrument in the kingdom.*

* * *

They rehearsed all day, and it was the strangest music Rosalind had ever made.

Ede's unbinding was built, Maren had taught them, on interlocking verses—each voice holding a fragment of true memory while the conductor carried the king's name above it all, the way a keystone holds while every other stone leans in. In the old rehearsals, with sound, they'd corrected each other constantly, chattering between phrases like any amateur choir. Now there was only Tam's rhythm rising through the floor and Tam's hands flashing corrections—two fingers at Brannock meant *softer*, a fist at Isbet meant *hold*, a sweeping gesture along the ground meant the whole ensemble was rushing, which they were, always, because terror makes singers fast.

And something else happened. Something none of them expected.

Without ears, they stopped listening with their heads and started listening with everything else. Rosalind felt Alenna's verse through the floor before it arrived, a tremor like distant thunder, and leaned into her line a half-breath early. Wren, whose manufactured memories had given her an uncanny sense of harmony-as-architecture, began steering Brannock with nothing but the pressure of her shoulder against his. They drifted together like a shoal of fish, correcting without deciding to correct, and by late afternoon they sang the unbinding end to end, silent to their own ears, and Rosalind—conducting blind, feeling the song rise through their own boot-soles like a river under ice—knew with absolute certainty that it had been the finest performance of their life, and would have traded it instantly for one ordinary moment of hearing Isbet giggle.

At dusk, Tam caught Rosalind's eye across the room and jerked his chin toward the door. They slipped out into the corridor together, where he pressed words into Rosalind's hand, and his fingers were not steady.

*"I heard my wife sing once. Everyone said she was bad. Off-key, too loud. I never heard her at all—I only ever felt her, here."* He touched his own breastbone. *"For thirty years I thought that was the poor version of music. The deaf man's consolation prize."* His jaw worked. Then: *"Today your nun sang through the floor, and it was the most beautiful thing I've ever felt, and I want you to write this down somewhere: the Regent took the sound out of the world and gave me the only day in my life I've been the best person in the room at the thing that mattered."*

Rosalind wrote it down. Word for word. Some things you don't paraphrase.

* * *

The Regent noticed on the second night.

Of course it did. Rosalind felt the attention come down through the stone like a change in pressure before a storm—that same vast, cold brush they'd first felt a lifetime ago in the mountains, when humming a smuggler's coin had drawn the thing's gaze across half a kingdom. It swept the palace, hunting for sound, found none, and paused. Lingered. Puzzled, insofar as a hunger can puzzle.

Then, far above, through Tam's floor and Tam's bones, they felt it begin to march.

Retainers. Hundreds of them—the eaten, the erased, the emptied, wearing court clothes and armor and expressions like unscribed wax, converging on the east wing with the patient, unhurried tread of a tide. And behind them, heavier than any of them, one tread that shook grit from the ceiling beams: the Regent itself, wearing Aldous's body the way a hand wears a glove two sizes too big, descending toward the throne room directly above their heads.

Tam stood in the middle of the antechamber, palms flat on his ancient floor, and read the approach the way other men read sheet music. He signed to Rosalind, quick and clear: *Twenty minutes. Maybe thirty. It's bringing the army slow. It wants to arrive.* A grim flicker crossed his face. *It likes arriving.*

"Then let's give it an audience worth arriving for," Rosalind said, knowing they couldn't hear themselves, saying it anyway, and turned to the Choir.

Eight faces looked back at them in the candlelight. Alenna calm, hands folded. Brannock cracking his knuckles like a man about to move furniture. Isbet with the Colonel the beetle in her fist, chin up, terrified and refusing it. Wren, alive by six weeks and by main force. Serle, who had been the enemy's instrument and would now be its opposite. Tam at the center of everything, rooted like the masonry itself.

And above them, coming down the long stair, the soundless thunder of everything the kingdom had forgotten, led by a crown that had once been a man.

Rosalind raised their arms. Tam's hands settled on the stone.

"Places," Rosalind whispered, to no hearing at all. "From the top."

The floor began to beat like a heart, and the Choir opened its mouths, and sang into the silence the Regent had made—a song with no sound in it anywhere except everywhere, rising through stone and bone and the oldest foundations of the kingdom, straight down toward the ember-dim dark where a tired dragon lifted its head and, for the first time in a century, heard company.

Above them, the marching stopped.

The Regent had felt it too.
