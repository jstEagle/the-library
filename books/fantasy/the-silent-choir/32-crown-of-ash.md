## 32. Crown of Ash

The last door between the Choir and the throne room was made of oak older than the kingdom, and it was not locked.

Rosalind had expected a locked door. They had expected guards, or wards, or some final cunning of the Regent's design. Instead the door simply stood ajar, breathing a thin line of gold light into the corridor like a host who has left the lamp on for you.

"I don't trust it," Tam signed. He stood close enough that Rosalind could feel his shoulder against theirs, a habit he'd developed somewhere on the Long Stair and never explained. His hands moved in the small, precise grammar they'd built together over the winter—half guild sign, half invention. "Doors that want you to come through them are doors that want something."

"Doors that don't want anything are just doors," Wren said. She was walking again, which still struck Rosalind as a miracle every time she did it. Her side was bound where the manufactured memory had bled out of her on the stairs; the bandages were ordinary linen and the wound beneath was ordinary pain, and both of those things seemed to please her enormously. "I've been a door that wanted nothing. It's very boring."

"You were not a door," Rosalind said.

"I was a *song* pretending to be a person pretending to be a door. Close enough." She rolled her shoulders, wincing, and grinned at Serle behind her. "Well, hunter? Any advice from the professional?"

Serle's face did its usual work of being almost impossible to remember—the eyes slightly wrong, the jaw slightly generic, a face like a word repeated until it loses meaning. But his voice, when he used it, had gotten steadier since the Eighth Voice chapter of his life began. "The throne room is where I was unmade," he said. "Whatever it shows you in there, it will be showing you what it wants. That is all the advice there is."

"Cheerful," said Wren.

"He's a joy at parties," Rosalind said, and pushed the door.

* * *

The throne room of King Aldous had been described to Rosalind a hundred times by a hundred travelers: the vaulted ceiling ribbed like the inside of a lute, the windows of dragon-glass that caught the fire-light from below and scattered it in warm constellations across the floor, the throne itself of pale ash-wood—wood from Ashvane's own fallen branch, given freely a century before the crown was ever forged.

None of those travelers had mentioned the silence.

It wasn't the Regent's stolen silence—not yet, though Rosalind felt it circling somewhere above them like a hawk deciding. This was an older silence, a swept-and-dusted silence. The room had been kept empty on purpose. Every echo that should have lived here—instruments, voices, the shuffle of courtiers—had been eaten or evicted, and what remained was a room shaped exactly like the inside of a held breath.

And on the throne, wearing the body of King Aldous, sat the thing with the crown.

"Ah," it said.

That was all. Just *ah*, in Aldous's pleasant baritone, the way a grandfather says it when you come home later than you promised. It didn't rise. It sat with its hands folded on its knees, and the crown drank the lamplight the way it always had, and for one absurd moment Rosalind thought: *we walked through fire and forgetting and the Long Stair, and it's going to offer us tea.*

Then it stood, and Rosalind saw it clearly for the first time.

Not Aldous. Not only Aldous. The king's body moved like a coat hung on a frame that didn't quite fit it, and behind the eyes—behind the tired, kind, terrible eyes—there was something else looking out. Something that had been looking out for a very long time.

"You're smaller than your songs," the Regent said. Its gaze moved across them, counting. Rosalind watched it count: Tam, solid as his own stone. Wren, chin up, hand pressed to her bandaged side. Serle, who flinched when its attention passed over him, the way a dog flinches from a former owner. The nun, Sister Cadel, who had begun quietly praying the moment the door opened and showed no sign of stopping. Jailer Brannic, who had brought his keys out of pure professional habit and now gripped them like a rosary. Little Isolde Vay, nine years old, prodigy, who looked up at the Regent with the frank appraisal of a child at an unusually ugly horse.

And Rosalind. Last. Conductor. Courier of nothing but candles, once upon a time.

"Eight of you," the Regent said softly. "He wrote eight."

Rosalind's heart stumbled. "*He?*"

The Regent smiled with Aldous's mouth, and it was a smile Rosalind recognized—not from the feast, not from any glimpse of the king. From the frontispiece of the Ash Codex. From the careful, cramped, beautiful handwriting that had filled a hundred sealed pages.

"Oh," Rosalind said. The word came out very small. "Oh, no."

"You feel it too." The Regent stepped down from the dais, unhurried, and the crown's shadow slid across the floor like oil. "You've been feeling it since the chapel vault, haven't you? Since you read his hand. The way the prophecy turns certain phrases. The joke hidden in the third verse of the founding hymn—he always hid jokes in things, he thought no one would notice, everyone noticed." It stopped six paces away, which was exactly the distance a courteous man keeps, exactly the distance a predator keeps, and Rosalind understood with cold clarity that it did not know the difference anymore. Or had stopped caring which one it was.

"Say it," Rosalind said. Their voice was steady. Somewhere inside, the part of them that had carried contraband candles through checkpoints for eleven years was taking notes: *stay calm, stay polite, stay invisible.* "Say his name."

The Regent looked at them for a long moment. Above them, faintly, through forty feet of stone, Rosalind felt a tremor pass—Ashvane, far below, stirring in the ember-dark, singing the kingdom's memory alone.

"Ede Voss," the Regent said.

Tam's hand found Rosalind's arm. He couldn't hear the name, but he could read faces even better than he read vibration, and whatever he saw in theirs made him step forward half a pace, putting stone where stone belonged.

"The guildmaster," Rosalind said. "The founder. The one who wrote the Codex. The one who—" Their mind skidded, catching on the cave carvings, on Ede's careful letters: *hide the Winterbourne line, hide the conductors, keep them invisible, keep them safe.* A century of invisibility, arranged by this thing. "You *wrote* our lives."

"I wrote your *protection*," the Regent said, and there was something raw under the words, something frayed. "There's a difference. There was supposed to be a difference."

* * *

Wren, being Wren, broke the silence first.

"So let me see if I have this straight." She limped forward, because of course she did; Rosalind could have predicted it the way you predict weather. "You founded the Wayfarers' Guild. You wrote a book of prophecies. You hid a family line for four generations so a conductor would exist to stop you. And then you became the monster in your own book." She ticked the points off on her fingers. "That's either the worst plan in history or the best one, and honestly I can't tell which, and I've *been* a manufactured lie, so I'm something of an expert."

"Wren," Brannic rumbled.

"No, I want to know. He wants to know too—I can tell. He's got the look." She jabbed a finger at the Regent. "You've got the look of someone who's been waiting a hundred years for someone to ask the right question, and nobody has, and it's been driving you mad."

The Regent looked at her for a long moment—with something that was not quite hunger and not quite recognition. "You are the one I made," it said. "You shouldn't have a side."

"Neither should you, by your own rules." Wren's voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word. "But here we both are."

Sister Cadel's prayer changed key. Rosalind heard it without hearing it—the old nun had been murmuring the same psalm for an hour and now her voice dropped into a lower, older cadence, a lament, and Isolde, sharp-eared little Isolde, turned to look at her with wide eyes.

The Regent heard it too. Rosalind watched it hear it—the slight tilt of Aldous's head, the flicker behind the eyes, like a man hearing a song from his childhood in a foreign market. For a moment the whole vast machinery of it went still.

"That hymn," it said quietly. "She sang it off-key. Ede's mother. She sang everything off-key and he loved her for it and he never told her, because telling her would have hurt her feelings and he was a coward about small things and a tyrant about large ones." It pressed two fingers to its temple, briefly, as if pressing a memory back into place—or holding one in. "Why do I know that. Why do I still know that."

"Because you're him," Rosalind said.

The Regent's head came around, and for the first time Rosalind saw the hunger naked in it—not malice, not even greed, just need, vast and undirected, the need of a creature that had spent a century eating the past because its own had burned away. "I am what the crown *made* of him," it said. "There's a difference. There was supposed to be—"

"A difference. Yes. You've said." Rosalind took a breath, and the courier in them did the arithmetic it had done in every checkpoint, every smuggler's den, every dangerous room of their invisible life: *what does this thing want, and what will it trade for it, and how do I walk out of here with more than I carried in.* "Tell me what happened. In this room. A century ago."

"Why?"

"Because I'm about to try to unmake you, and I'd rather know what I'm unmaking."

Something crossed the Regent's borrowed face that might, in a living man, have been laughter. It might, in a living man, have been weeping. On the Regent it was simply movement, worn like a coat.

"He came down here with seven choristers," the Regent said. "To bind the crown. To *control* it—that was the word he used in his own head, and the crown heard it, because the crown hears everything a wearer thinks. Control. Not destroy. Not release. *Control.*" It spread Aldous's hands, an old teacher's gesture, and Rosalind hated how much it looked like the diagrams in the Codex, how much the posture was the author's posture. "The crown is not a thing, conductor. It is a *hunger* with a shape. And when a man stands before it and thinks *mine*, it agrees. It makes itself his. All of it. Every appetite, every method, every century of eaten names—it folds them into the shape of his wanting and hands them back."

"And he wanted control."

"He wanted to fix things. He wanted to carry everything himself, because he'd never trusted anyone else to carry it properly." The Regent's voice had gone strange, distant, reciting. "He was a good man. That is the trap, do you understand? The crown cannot take a cruel man. Cruel men want small things. It needs a *good* man. A man who wants to save everyone. Those are the ones it can use." The fingers curled closed. "Seven choristers tried to pull him back. He wouldn't let go. The crown ate him from the edges inward—his patience, his doubt, his mercy—and what was left standing when they fled this room was me. And I have been trying to finish his plan ever since. No more grief. No more loss. Nothing remembered, therefore nothing mourned. A kingdom with nothing to lose."

"A kingdom with nothing *at all*," Tam said. He couldn't hear himself say it, but he felt Rosalind's translation hands moving beside him, and he said it anyway, loud, in his quarry-deep voice, and then he signed it too, twice, so there could be no mistake: *A kingdom with nothing at all.*

The Regent looked at him. Really looked.

"Yes," it said finally. "He would have argued the same. He argued with everyone. It's why I kept the Guildhouse standing." A pause, and then, almost gently: "Did you think the roof repaired itself? Did you think the Codex stayed dry through a century of storms? I kept your home, conductor. I kept your *crack in the wall*. Every year I told myself: if she comes, if the date arrives and someone comes, I'll know the plan worked. And if no one comes—" The hands opened again, empty. "No one came. Until you."

The room was very quiet. Below them, Ashvane's tremor came again, longer this time, and a fine dust sifted from the vaulted ceiling, glittering in the crown-drunk light.

Rosalind's mind was doing something complicated and painful—rearranging a century of furniture. The Codex. The cave. The invisible life, engineered by a monster's guilty hand. Even Maren, even the locks, even the gentle machinery of their whole sheltered existence: all of it authored by the thing they'd come to burn. Anger rose in them, hot and clean—

—and beside it, unwelcome, something else. Pity. Because they had seen what the crown did to Serle, and Serle had only lost a life. This thing had lost a *self*, and had spent a hundred years mistaking its own hunger for its own goodness.

"You wrote instructions," Rosalind said slowly. "In the Codex. For the Choir. For the unbinding. Didn't you."

"I wrote many things."

"But the final pages. The ones the crown couldn't read." Rosalind took a step forward, and felt Tam shift with them, and Wren, and the whole improbable family of them closing rank like a chord gathering its notes. "You hid them. Inside the prophecy. Instructions for your own unmaking—written by the monster, for the monster, so that someday someone could sing you out of yourself. Because some part of you, the part the crown couldn't digest, has been waiting a hundred years to *stop*."

For the first time, the Regent looked afraid.

Not of the Choir. Not of the swordless jailer or the praying nun or the deaf mason or the wounded lie-that-became-a-woman. It looked afraid the way a man looks at a locked door he himself built, knowing what he locked behind it, hoping desperately that no one ever finds the key—and hoping, in the same breath, that someone does.

"You don't understand what you're asking," it said. Aldous's voice had gone hoarse. "If you sing the final pages, you don't free Ede Voss. There is no Ede Voss left to free. What's underneath me isn't a man, it's a *wound* wearing his name, and if you open it—"

"Then it bleeds," Rosalind said. "Things that are real bleed. We've learned that recently." They glanced at Wren, who lifted her chin, gray-faced and grinning.

The Regent stared at them. And high above, beyond the ceiling, beyond the stone, Rosalind felt the air change—the hawk circling overhead finally folding its wings. The silence came down through the palace like snowfall, total and deliberate, swallowing the sound of their own breathing.

The Regent's mouth moved. In the dead air, Rosalind read the shape of the words rather than heard them:

*Then let us see what you are made of.*

Behind the throne, the dragon-glass windows blazed gold as Ashvane, far below, felt the silence fall—and began, alone in the dark, to sing louder.
