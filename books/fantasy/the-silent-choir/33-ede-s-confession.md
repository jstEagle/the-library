## 33. Ede's Confession

The throne room had gone quiet in the way a held breath is quiet.

Rosalind stood at the head of the choir with the Ash Codex open against their chest, its final pages trembling under their thumb. Behind them, the seven voices waited—Tam's hands hovering at the ready, Wren breathing carefully around her cracked ribs, the nun and the jailer and the child prodigy arranged in the half-circle Maren had taught them to hold. Above them all, on the dais where a king should sit, the Regent wore Aldous's body like a coat it had grown into, and the crown drank the light of every torch in the room.

"You have come a long way," the Regent said, and Aldous's voice was kind, which was somehow worse than cruelty. "Through snow, through grief, through my own corridors. You are tired. I can hear how tired you are. Put down the book."

"The last time someone told me to put down a book," Rosalind said, "I was eleven years old, and it was a ledger I wasn't supposed to read. I read it anyway." They lifted the Codex higher. "You should know something about me before we do this. I always read it anyway."

"Then you know what happens to readers here." The Regent gestured, and along the walls the empty retainers shifted—the forgotten ones, the people it had eaten and kept wearing like borrowed gloves. "I am not your enemy, conductor. I am the kingdom's mercy. Every memory I take is a grief that will never hurt anyone again."

"Every memory you take," Rosalind said, "is a person who stops being a person."

The crown flared.

And Rosalind did the thing they had rehearsed in the dark of the dragon's cavern until Tam could keep the beat of it by touch alone: they opened the Codex to its final pages—the pages no eye had ever reported seeing, because they were never meant for eyes—and began to sing.

* * *

The words were Ede Voss's. That was the thing Rosalind had understood only three nights ago, sitting in Ashvane's ember-light while the great dragon breathed slow as a banked fire: prophecy was the perfect hiding place. The crown ate memories, ate histories, ate anything a person *held*. But a prophecy wasn't held. A prophecy was *given*—spoken aloud, passed mouth to mouth, loose in the world where nothing could be unmade from it. The crown couldn't eat what wasn't kept.

So Ede had written his confession as a prediction. He had hidden his instructions inside the future, where the monster wearing his face would never think to look back.

The Codex's last pages were not verses about conductors or choirs or dates inked fresh across a century. They were a recipe. Step by step, note by note, the unbinding of a thing bound too long—and every step was written in the first person.

*I built the lock,* sang the first voice, and Rosalind's voice carried it alone, thin and clear across the throne room's cold stone. *I built it because I thought a kingdom's memory deserved a better keeper than forgetting.*

The empty retainers stopped moving.

*I built it because I was proud,* sang the second line, joined now by Tam, who felt the rhythm rising through the floor and struck his palm softly against his thigh—a heartbeat for the room. *I built it because I believed I was the one person clever enough to hold everything without being held by any of it.*

The Regent took a step forward. Then another. Its face—Aldous's face, the king's good, tired face—was doing something complicated, the way weather does when two seasons argue over a single morning.

"That book is lies," it said.

"It's a diary," Rosalind said, and did not stop singing.

* * *

The third verse went to Wren.

This had been Tam's idea, in the cavern, delivered in the blunt sign-language he used when he wanted to be understood absolutely: *Let her sing it. She knows what it is to be made instead of born. Nobody else in this room reads those pages right.* And so Wren, whose manufactured memories still bled like real blood beneath her bandages, lifted her voice and gave the room the lines Ede had written about becoming:

*I woke one morning and could not remember my mother's kitchen. I knew there had been a kitchen. I knew there had been a mother. The knowledge sat in me like furniture in an empty house, and I walked among it and called myself home.*

Wren's voice caught on the last word—not from weakness. From recognition.

"I remember the kitchen," the Regent said suddenly. It had stopped walking. Its hands had risen halfway, as if to shield itself from sound. "There was a copper kettle. She burned the porridge every morning and blamed the stove, and every morning she apologized to the stove, because she believed stoves had feelings. Why do I—" The crown flared white, and the Regent staggered. "Those are not mine. Those were taken from me a century ago. Who put them back?"

"A dead woman," said the child prodigy, Ilse, who had not spoken since the under-stair, and whose small voice cut the silence like a needle. "She wrote them down where you couldn't reach. She was smarter than you."

"Ilse," Rosalind murmured, "let the lady finish her verse."

But something in the room had changed. The retainers along the walls had begun, very quietly, to sway. Not toward the Regent. Toward the song.

* * *

The middle pages were the hardest, and Rosalind had saved the hardest truth for the moment it would land hardest.

Because the middle pages were not instructions. The middle pages were apology.

*I have eaten four hundred years of other people's mornings,* Ede had written, in the hand that grew shakier page by page. *I tell myself each one was mercy—that I spared them their griefs, their shame, the deaths they witnessed. But mercy given without asking is only theft with good manners, and I was, in the end, a thief with excellent manners.*

The jailer, Brannock, sang this verse. He had chosen it himself, signing to Rosalind the night before: *I locked men up for their own good once. Let me carry this one.* His bass voice moved through the throne room like a tide going out, pulling things with it.

The Regent had sunk onto the steps of the dais. It sat the way old men sit, carefully, one hand braced on stone, the crown tilting forward over Aldous's brow like something exhausted from carrying itself.

"There was a girl," it said. No one had asked it anything. "In the year I lost myself. She brought bread to the gate—I remember the bread, I cannot remember her face, isn't that the whole of it, isn't that the whole cursed shape of it—you keep the shapes and lose the souls. She laughed at something I said. I have spent a hundred years trying to find that laugh in other people's memories, and every time I found one, I took it, thinking *this must be hers, this might be hers*—" Aldous's hands pressed flat against the steps. "None of them were hers. None of them were ever hers."

The choir kept singing. That was the discipline Maren had drilled into them in the dragon's dark: *the song doesn't stop for the story; the song carries the story.* Sister Ovelin's alto came in beneath Brannock's bass, and then Ilse's bright thread above, and the harmonies braided themselves around the Regent's broken sentences the way ivy takes a wall—not to smother it, but to hold it up.

*If you are reading this—if you are singing this—then I failed,* the Codex said, in Ede's own fading script. *And I am sorry. And here is how to undo me. Forgive me the presumption of writing my own ending. I have written everyone else's.*

* * *

It was near the end that the crown fought back.

They felt it before they saw it—a pressure like deep water, like the moment in the high passes when a blizzard decides whether to be weather or catastrophe. The lamplight bent inward toward the dais. The retainers straightened, swaying no longer, standing at attention like soldiers recalled to duty.

"You presume," the Regent said, and its voice had changed—the kindness draining out of it, leaving something older and hungrier underneath, though even the hunger sounded frightened. "You stand in my house with a book of tricks and call it mercy. I know what you're doing, conductor. You want me to *feel*. Feeling is how you get in. I sealed that door a hundred years ago and I will seal it again—"

"It was your door first," Rosalind said quietly.

The song faltered—one beat, half a measure—and Tam's hand came down on the stone floor like a gavel, and the beat held, because Tam always held.

"You were a person," Rosalind said. They closed the Codex, finally, because the remaining pages weren't needed anymore. The rest was in the singers now. "His name was Ede Voss. He founded the Wayfarers' Guild. He liked—"

"Don't," said the Regent.

"—he liked honey in his tea, according to the ledgers—"

"*Don't.*"

"—and he was afraid of the dark, which is why the Guildhouse windows were always lit, why he left lamps burning in every room of a building full of people who could see perfectly well in the dark, because he never once asked anyone to share his fear but he hoped somebody would notice—"

The crown screamed.

Not the Regent. The crown—a sound like metal remembering it had once been ore, like gold dragged backward through fire. Aldous's body lurched upright, both hands clawing toward the band of metal on his brow, and for one terrible instant there were three faces looking out of it: the king's, the monster's, and beneath them both, faint as a watermark, the face of a man with kind eyes and a century of guilt.

"He tried to control us," the Regent gasped, in a voice that was three voices. "He put us on and thought himself the rider. We showed him what riders become. We showed him—"

"You showed him what hunger becomes," Rosalind said. "He figured it out too late to save himself. So he wrote it down early enough to save everyone else." They stepped forward, up onto the first step of the dais, close enough to see that Aldous's eyes were wet. "That's the confession, Regent. Not the crimes. Any monster can confess its crimes. The confession is that he loved this kingdom enough to spend a hundred years arranging his own funeral—and the funeral is a song, and the song needs a conductor, and I am so very sorry, but the date was always tonight."

The pressure in the room broke like ice on a river.

The retainers sank down along the walls, some of them weeping without knowing why, holding their own hands as if they'd just been returned to them. Sister Ovelin crossed herself. Ilse grabbed Wren's sleeve and didn't let go. And the Regent stood swaying on the dais in the king's body, the crown guttering now, dimming, drinking less and less light, and when it spoke again the three voices had collapsed into one—small, and old, and terribly human.

"The last page," it whispered. "What does the last page say? I could never see it. A hundred years wearing this crown and I could never see past my own beginning."

Rosalind looked down at the final page of the Ash Codex, at the words Ede Voss had inked in a shaking hand a century ago, addressed to no reader and every reader, and found that their throat had closed entirely.

Tam saw. Tam always saw. His hand found the small of Rosalind's back—two firm taps, the signal, *you sing, I'll keep you steady*—and Rosalind breathed, and opened their mouth, and gave the monster its inheritance.

The last page said: *Tell him I'm sorry. Tell him I remember. Tell him whatever I became, I became it trying to keep the door open—and if any part of me is still in there listening, then hear this, brother, hear it however far you've fallen:*

*You can put it down now.*

*You can put it down whenever you want.*

The Regent made a sound no one in the room ever managed to describe afterward. Aldous's knees buckled. The crown slid crooked, smoking faintly, and beneath it the king's face surfaced like a drowning man breaking water—

—and Rosalind felt, rather than heard, Ashvane begin to rise beneath the palace, the dying dragon's heart catching like a struck flint, and knew there was perhaps one song left in the world's time for all of them.

"Choir," Rosalind said. "Positions. Now."

The Regent looked up at them with Ede's kind eyes, and said, in a voice already more man than monster: "Conductor. What happens if I can't let go?"

Rosalind took the first step of the dais. "Then," they said, "we help you. Sing with me."
