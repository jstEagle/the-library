## 34. The Unbinding

The throne room of King Aldous had been built to impress, and it still tried, even now. Banners hung from rafters gone black with a century of dragon-smoke. The floor was a mosaic of the founding of Thornfell—shepherds and stonemasons and one optimistic dog—all rendered in stone so fine that Rosalind had once, as a courier, been warned not to scuff it.

Now the dog's face was blank. So were the shepherds'. The Regent had eaten the mosaic on its way through the room, or perhaps the room had simply forgotten itself in its presence. Only the outlines remained, like footprints after a tide.

And at the far end, beneath the crown that drank the lamplight, stood the thing wearing Aldous.

It did not look monstrous. That was the worst of it. It looked tired and kind and slightly amused, the way Aldous had looked when he'd been alive enough to be amused. Behind it, filing into the gallery like guests arriving late to their own funeral, came the empty ones—the retainers the Regent had erased and kept. They moved beautifully, without hesitation, without anything at all. Their eyes were windows with the shutters closed.

Rosalind stood at the center of the floor with six voices behind them and one name held behind their teeth like a coal they were forbidden to spit out.

*Aldebrand Vess-Aldous, third of his line, who liked his bread burnt and was afraid of geese.*

The vial's year sat inside them now, unpacked and lived-in. A summer courtyard. A boy with ink-stained fingers trading secrets for candied plums. *If I ever stop being myself,* he'd said, eleven years old and terribly serious, *promise you'll remember me.*

"Rosalind Winterbourne," said the Regent, and its voice was warm as mulled wine. "Or should I say Voss? You've read the last pages, haven't you. Ede always did hide the important things where only family would think to look."

"We're not doing banter," Tam signed sharply from Rosalind's left. He couldn't hear the Regent anyway—he felt it, a wrongness in the floor like a toothache—and he wanted no part of speeches. His hands cut the air: *Now or never.*

"Now," Rosalind agreed aloud.

They raised their hands, and the Choir began.

* * *

It is difficult to describe an unbinding to someone who has never heard one, mostly because the people who could describe it are usually being unbound at the time. But those few who survived the Choir's singing—and there would be seven of them, later, plus one king—agreed on certain things.

That it began low, in Tam's hands striking the marble floor in a rhythm older than the palace: *the heartbeat of the foundry, the heartbeat of the loom, the heartbeat under everything.* That Wren's voice came in second, and though her verse was manufactured, thread by borrowed thread, she sang it as if she'd grown it herself, which by then she more or less had. That Sister Ovelle's voice was smoke and Jailer Cray's was iron and little Nettie Corvane, the prodigy, aged nine and missing two front teeth, hit notes that made the banners sway.

And that Serle sang last, and softest, and that his verse—the memories the Regent had left him, obedience and hunger and one afternoon, long ago, of learning to sing—came out sounding, against all odds, like forgiveness.

The unbinding was Ede Voss's composition. He had written it a century ago in the Codex's final pages, disguised as prophecy, because he had known the crown would read whatever he wrote and he needed it to read *foretelling* instead of *instructions*. *Sing the knot, not the cord. Sing the wearer, not the worn. And hold the true name apart from all of it, for a thing that eats memory cannot digest what is freely given.*

The song went into the room like water goes into cracks.

The Regent screamed.

Not with Aldous's voice—that was the horror of it. With a hundred voices at once, layered like rotten timber: courtiers and queens and bakers and soldiers, everyone it had ever swallowed, all of them suddenly awake inside it and none of them pleased about the accommodations.

"You cannot—" it said, wearing the king's face while the king's face wept without permission, tears cutting tracks through the dust of a century. "I *kept* them. I kept them safe. Everything fades, everything rots, but in me they are *preserved*—"

"In you they're *shelved*," Rosalind said, and lifted both hands, and the Choir swelled.

Here was the trouble, the one Maren had foreseen in her last living hour, the reason the conductor's burden was a burden: the unbinding required two minds at once. One to conduct—to hold seven singers and a rhythm and a dying dragon's distant ember-pulse in a single shape. And one to hold the king's true name, separate and untouched, because the moment the knot loosened, the name was the only rope that could pull Aldous back out of the dark.

Two tasks. One Rosalind.

So they did what Wayfarers had always done with impossible loads: they carried it anyway, and complained internally, and got on with it.

*Conduct:* Tam's pulse, steady as a lock-gate. Wren bending her manufactured silver around Nettie's raw gold. Cray holding the bass like a man holding a door against a mob. *Hold:* *Aldebrand, who traded secrets for plums, who was afraid of geese, whose mother called him Bran when she thought no one heard.* Two rooms in the mind, and Rosalind walking the corridor between them, back and forth, back and forth, faster and faster as the song demanded.

The Regent fought the way a drowning thing fights: everything at once, everywhere, no strategy but volume.

It threw memories first. It knew how to do that; it had done it for a hundred years. It reached into each singer and yanked.

Tam staggered—his wife's laugh, torn sideways out of his chest, and for three full measures his hands went still. The rhythm wobbled. In the gallery, the empty retainers took a step forward in perfect unison, and their footsteps sounded like a door closing.

"Hold," Rosalind said, and did the thing they'd sworn not to do, the thing conductors weren't supposed to need: they gave Tam their own memory to lean on. Not theirs to give—a morning in Thornfell, frost on a windowsill, someone humming—but they poured it into the beat, into the space where his grief had been, so his hands had something to keep time *to*. Tam's jaw set. His palms struck the stone. The rhythm came home.

"I remember my wife," Tam signed furiously at the Regent, which was not how arguments worked, but which was nonetheless extremely effective, "and you can't have her."

Sister Ovelle lost her vows for four bars and sang anyway, on pure stubbornness, which she noted afterward was very nearly the same thing. Cray lost the names of his prisoners—not the guilt, cruelly, just the names—and turned the loss into the lowest note any of them had ever heard a human throat make. Nettie, age nine, looked directly at the Regent and lost nothing at all, because she was nine and her memories were too new and too sharp to swallow, like trying to eat a hedgehog.

And Serle—

Serle walked forward. Out of the semicircle, out of the formation, straight toward the throne with his hands open and his forgettable face wet.

"Serle!" Wren's voice cracked across the music.

"It left me one thing," Serle said, and his voice was strange, doubled, as if two men were speaking through one mouth. "One. It thought it was nothing. It thought obedience was *empty*." He stopped ten feet from the Regent, close enough to touch the crown's shadow. "But obedience is just love with the name taken out. And I am putting the name *back*."

He sang the hunter's verse—the verse he'd used to track them through Thornfell, the verse that had cost Rosalind their mother's face—and he aimed it inward, at himself, at the hollow place, and it rang like a bell dropped down a well.

The Regent recoiled. Actually recoiled, one step, the crown scraping light off the air.

"That's mine," it hissed. "I made him."

"No," Rosalind said. "You emptied him. Making is different. You wouldn't know—you've never made anything in a hundred years. You've only ever taken and tidied."

It was, Rosalind would think later, not a strategically necessary remark. But some things need saying.

* * *

The fight settled into something worse than chaos: a siege. The Choir sang; the Regent endured. The knot in the crown loosened by hairs. The empty retainers advanced down the gallery steps, unhurried, shuttered, and Wren and Cray fell back singing to hold the stair, their harmony a wall the erased ones walked into and could not cross—not because it pushed them, but because somewhere underneath the emptiness, their own stolen harmonies recognized themselves and hesitated.

Rosalind ran the corridor between the two rooms faster now. Conduct. Hold. *Bran. Geese. Burnt bread.* Conduct. The vial-year flickered at the edges, and Rosalind understood with cold clarity what the Regent was doing: it wasn't attacking the name. It was *nibbling*. Waiting for exhaustion. A century-old predator knows that everything holds on until it doesn't.

"You're tired," the Regent observed kindly, through Aldous's mouth, while Aldous's eyes begged. "Put it down. Give me the name and I'll give you your year back—I keep my bargains, ask anyone who remembers me, oh wait." Its smile was Aldous's smile, and it hurt to see it crooked that way. "You could stop all this. Go back to being invisible. You were *good* at invisible. Wasn't it easier? Nobody to disappoint. Nobody to lose."

Rosalind's hands did not falter, because Rosalind's hands had carried contraband candles through customs inspections, through blizzards, through one memorable incident involving a goat, and they did not falter for a smiling predator in a stolen face.

But inside, quietly, honestly: yes. Yes, it had been easier.

And that was exactly why they couldn't stop. Because the Regent's whole gospel was *easier*, easier to forget, easier to shelve, easier to be no one—and every person in this room had chosen the harder thing at least once tonight, and the harder thing was the only thing that had ever actually worked.

"Ede wrote your ending," Rosalind said. "A hundred years ago. You've been carrying your own instructions on your own shelf all this time." They smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile, and they were, in that moment, unmistakably a Voss. "You really should read your mail."

The Regent's composure cracked—not much, a hairline, but Rosalind had spent a lifetime reading cracks in sealed things.

It attacked Rosalind directly.

* * *

There is no good way to describe having a memory-eater put its hands in your head, chiefly because it does not use hands. It uses *want*. It found the seam Rosalind had opened for Tam—the shared morning, the frost, the humming—and it pulled.

The corridor between the two rooms buckled. The conducting half of Rosalind's mind watched the name-half tilt, saw *Aldebrand* start to slide toward the thing's open shelf-mouth, and understood in one white-hot instant that they could not hold both.

Could not hold both *alone*.

"Nettie!" Rosalind didn't shout it so much as throw it, and the girl—nine years old, missing two teeth, terrified, magnificent—caught the conducting thread out of the air like she'd been born waiting for it. Her small hands rose. Her voice took the count. She was not as strong as Rosalind and she did not need to be; she only needed to hold the shape for sixty seconds, and children, Rosalind remembered abruptly, are the best holders-of-shapes in the world, because they have not yet learned shapes are supposed to be hard.

Freed of one weight, Rosalind seized the name with both hands, all of it, the whole boy: *Bran, who fed the palace geese against all advice and was pecked with regularity and loved them anyway, Bran who once said "if I ever stop being myself," and the courtyard, and the plums, and—*

The Regent screamed again, and this time the scream had fear in it, and beneath the fear, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well:

it had a *name* too.

Ede.

The knot in the crown slipped another turn. Light came out of it—not lamplight, not dragon-fire, something older, the color of the Codex's margins—and the empty retainers stopped mid-step on the gallery stairs. One of them, a woman in a housekeeper's apron, blinked. Her hand rose slowly to her own face, touching her cheekbones as if confirming they belonged to her.

"Marla?" Cray sang, mid-verse, disbelieving. The housekeeper's mouth moved. No sound yet. But her eyes had shutters, and the shutters were opening.

The Regent felt it happening—felt its hoard waking, its pantry rioting—and did the last thing it had left.

It stopped fighting the song.

It started singing along.

Its voice slid into their harmony like oil into water, smooth, pitch-perfect, *helpful*—and every note it added was subtly wrong, a quarter-tone of rot, and Rosalind felt the unbinding begin to bend, the knot tightening even as they loosened it, because the song was the tool and the tool was being held from the other end.

"Don't listen with your ears," Tam thundered, signing so hard his arms shook, feeling the corruption come up through the floor. "Listen with the *stone*!"

But the Regent was inside the stone, inside the walls, inside a century of this palace's bones, and its false harmony wrapped around them like a warm coat that was also a net, and Nettie's arms trembled, and Wren's borrowed silver started tarnishing note by note, and Rosalind stood in the center of the throne room holding a king's name in a burning grip and understood, with the terrible calm of the exhausted, that the song alone would not finish it.

Something had to go *into* the flame. Something given, not kept. Ede's margin-note surfaced like a fish: *a thing that eats memory cannot digest what is freely given.*

And Ashvane, far below, stirred in the dark, and the ember-pulse under the floor quickened—and dimmed, and dimmed, the dying dragon offering the last of its heat like a hand extended across a table.

Rosalind looked at the crown. At the light leaking from the knot. At Aldous's face, which between the Regent's screams kept surfacing like a drowning man, mouthing over and over the same three words, silent, urgent, patient as a promise kept for twenty years:

*Sing it back. Sing it back.*

"Nettie," Rosalind said. "Keep my shape."

Then they stepped toward the throne, and the fire, and the name burning a hole in their chest—and the Regent, seeing where they were going, finally understood what Ede had planned a century ago, and made a sound that no kingdom's history contained.

Because the price was coming due. And Rosalind meant to pay it at full voice.
