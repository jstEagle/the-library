## 35. The Name in the Fire

The throne room had no roof worth the name. A century of neglect had opened it to the sky, and snow fell through the broken crown of rafters, drifting down past shattered banners to settle on the dais where a dead king's chair stood empty and a living king's chair stood occupied by something that was not a king.

Rosalind stood at the foot of the stairs with seven voices behind them and one name held behind their teeth like a coal.

They could feel the name. That was the strange thing about it—stranger than the vial, stranger than the missing year, stranger even than the memory of a boy in an orchard pressing his hand over Rosalind's small one and saying *if I ever stop being myself*. The name wasn't words anymore. It was weight. It sat in their chest the way a stone sits in a riverbed, and everything else in them bent around it, and they understood suddenly why Maren had walked a hundred miles on blind eyes to hand them this moment: because someone had to be willing to be the stone.

"Rosalind." Tam's hand found theirs, big and warm and cracked as old leather. He couldn't hear the Regent's voice filling the hall, but he could feel it through the floor—that wrong vibration, like a hive full of wasps wearing silk. His fingers asked a question against Rosalind's palm.

*Ready?*

"No," Rosalind said honestly. "But we're going anyway."

Tam smiled, which for Tam was a whole speech. *Good,* his hands said. *Honesty is better rehearsal.*

Above them, wearing Aldous's body like a coat that had stopped fitting years ago, the Regent descended the last steps of the dais. It had stopped pretending to charm. The courtier's smile was gone; what remained was older and hungrier, a face doing its best impression of a face while something behind it leaned forward.

"You are tired," it said, and its voice was gentle, which was the worst thing about it. It had always been gentle. That was how it had eaten a kingdom—one kindness at a time, each grief lifted away like a plate cleared from a table. "Look at you all. You're holding lifetimes that were never yours. A nun's vigils. A jailer's regrets. A child's scales and exercises. You are so heavy, little choir. Set it down. I can carry it. I have carried it for a hundred years."

"We know," Rosalind said. "That's what's sad about you."

Something flickered across the stolen face—not anger. Wounded surprise. "Sad?"

"You think carrying is the same as keeping." Rosalind's voice didn't shake, which surprised them, because inside they were shaking like a bell. "You hold everything and you have nothing. That's not mercy. That's hoarding with good manners."

Behind them, Serle made a small sound—the closest thing to a laugh his hollowed-out throat could still produce—and then went silent, ashamed of it, or maybe proud.

The Regent stepped down onto the floor itself, and the temperature changed. Snowflakes stopped falling near it. Hung there. The lamplight that hadn't been drunk yet began leaning toward it like wheat toward wind.

"Then let us speak plainly, conductor," it said. "You cannot unbind me. Ede Voss tried, and look what became of him." One long finger indicated itself, gesturing at its own borrowed chest with something almost like humor. "I am what happens when a Wayfarer tries to hold too much. You will burn your memories to fuel your song—I can smell the plan on you, it reeks of guildmaster sentimentality—and when you are ash, I will take what's left, gently, and put everyone back to sleep. It's kinder. Truly. Ask anyone." A pause. "Ask anyone who remembers anything."

"That's the flaw in your pitch," Rosalind said. "Nobody does."

And they turned to Ashvane.

* * *

The dragon lay along the far wall of the cavern beneath the throne room, or rather—the way down had been blown open during the battle of the long stair, and now dragon and throne room shared air, firelight and snowlight mingling in the ruined vault above. Ashvane was vast the way mountains are vast: not impressive at first glance, only impossible once you truly looked. Scales the color of banked coals. Eyes like two doors closing slowly.

For a century she had sung alone. The kingdom's memory had poured through her, one voice holding plagues and weddings and the founding of Thornfell and every baker's hands and every soldier's last thought of home, until her song had worn down to embers, until the beacon above the palace had guttered for the first time in living memory, until a courier with contraband candles had looked up at a darkened sky and felt the world crack open.

*Rosalind.* Her voice came less as sound than as heat, rising through boot soles and shinbones. *The flame will take what you feed it. Feed it carefully. Even a conductor cannot give away everything and remain to conduct.*

"I know what I'm keeping," Rosalind said.

It was a lie, mostly. They knew what they were keeping *today*: the Choir, the road, Tam's hands on their shoulder, Wren's terrible beautiful manufactured laugh, the name behind their teeth. Everything else was negotiable, and they both knew it, and Ashvane's slow-closing eyes held something like pity, and something like respect, and something very old that might have been love.

*Then climb into my fire, little Wayfarer,* Ashvane said. *And sing him home.*

The rekindling flame rose from the dragon's breast like a second heartbeat—a column of gold-and-ember light that threw shadows backward across the hall. It did not burn the air around it, but Rosalind could feel it waiting, patient as a doorway, hungry the way a hearth is hungry: willing to be fed, willing to warm, indifferent to what it consumed.

Wren caught Rosalind's arm. Her face, newly real and therefore newly capable of fear, was pale. "You'll forget," she whispered. "Everything you burn, you lose. That's how it works, isn't it? That's how it worked with the locket."

"Yes."

"Then—" She swallowed. "Remember me out loud first. Before you go. So it's in the rest of us too. So burning it doesn't kill it."

Rosalind looked at her—at the girl who had been built from stolen memory to betray them, who had chosen instead, badly and bravely, to become someone—and did the thing that conductors are for.

"Wren," they said, loud enough for the whole hall, for the empty retainers shuffling at the edges, for the snow. "Wren was born three years ago out of a thief's craft, and she has been alive for exactly as long as she has been loved, which is to say since a blizzard in a smuggler's cave when she sang off-key on purpose to make Tam smile and he laughed so hard the ice cracked. She is a liar, a coward twice, a hero once more than that, and she takes her tea bitter enough to strip rust, which no manufactured memory would ever want. She is ours. She is real. Let the record show."

Wren was crying, which was new, and laughing, which was newer, and Tam thumped her back hard enough to rearrange her spine, and somewhere in the ranks of choristers the child prodigy—Pell, nine years old and braver than half the army upstairs—giggled wetly.

"There," Rosalind said. "Now it's load-bearing."

Then they walked into the fire.

* * *

Burning, it turned out, felt like being read.

Not painfully—not at first. The flame took memories the way a tide takes footprints: without malice, without hurry, simply erasing what stood between it and the smooth sand. Rosalind stood in the heart of Ashvane's rekindling and fed it deliberately, piece by piece, choosing as they'd promised to choose.

The taste of the first apple of autumn, gone. Their first delivery run at fourteen, the pride of it, gone. The smell of the Guildhouse's east room, dust and candle-wax, gone. Each loss was small and enormous, a door closing softly in a house they'd lived in all their life, and with each one the flame burned brighter and the name behind their teeth grew heavier, denser, more itself, the one stone they refused the tide.

And the Regent screamed.

It came across the hall like weather, like winter walking, and it reached for Rosalind with a hundred years of practiced gentleness, and found—nothing to grip. That was the discovery, the thing Ede Voss must have known when he wrote his own unbinding into prophecy and hid it where only a reader's heart could unlock it: the Regent could eat what was *kept*. Every memory in the world was kept by somebody, guarded, clutched, and therefore edible. But a song being *given*—poured out as fast as it rose, offered to the fire rather than held against the dark—passed through the monster's hands like water through a net.

Rosalind began the king's name.

Not spoke it. Sang it, at the top of their voice, at the bottom of their strength, giving it away note by note into the flame, and the flame carried it up through the broken roof and down through the dragon's dreaming bones and out into the stones of the palace, and the palace remembered.

Aldous. Not the title—the name. The boy's name, the man's name, the true name pressed into a child's palm in an orchard a lifetime ago: *remember me if I ever stop being myself.*

"I REMEMBER YOU!" Rosalind sang, and meant it in every direction at once—to the king, to the boy, to the friend in the vial, to the empty-eyed courtiers, to the whole forgetting city beyond the walls. "I REMEMBER YOU! ALDOUS! ALDOUS!"

The Regent struck.

It hit the flame like a wave hitting a lighthouse, and the lighthouse held, and the wave broke into a hundred shapes of grief. For one terrible instant Rosalind saw it clearly from inside the fire—saw the thing wearing Aldous as it truly was, which was small. Small and starving and so old, a person-shaped absence wrapped around one original wound: a guildmaster who had wanted to save everyone from sorrow and had learned instead that sorrow is the mortar of a self. It lunged again, and again, snatching at the song, and every note slipped through it, given-given-given, and each failure tore a little more of it away.

Because here was the truth it had never survived learning: you cannot eat a gift. You can steal a treasure, hoard a treasure, drown in treasures—but a thing surrendered freely, the instant it's surrendered, belongs already to whoever receives it, and there is nothing left to take.

The Choir sang the unbinding.

Seven voices rose around the fire—Maren's decoy courage alive in them still, Tam's metronome pulse coming up through the floor so steady that even the snow seemed to fall in time, Pell's clear high line threading the melody like a needle through cloth, the nun and the jailer and the prodigy's mother and Wren, Wren singing her own brief true history at full throat, real as blood. And under them, waking, gathering itself after a century of whispering, Ashvane's great voice joined—the dragon's song rolling up through the flame and out of Rosalind's own burning chest, so that for a few impossible measures conductor and dragon were one instrument, one breath, one name given away over and over:

*Aldous. Aldous. Aldous.*

The Regent came apart the way a knot comes apart when someone finally finds the right end to pull.

Not violently. That was the shocking mercy of it. It unraveled—layer after layer of stolen centuries sloughing off, the faces of the forgotten rising out of it like bubbles from deep water, each one flying free to find its owner. And underneath all of it, at the center, small and blinking in the light of the flame it had feared for a hundred years, was a man.

He was tall and stooped and had ink-stained fingers. He wore a guildmaster's travel cloak, rotted to lace at the hem. He looked at his own hands with wonder, as though he'd left gloves on them for a century and only now felt the cold air.

"Ede," Rosalind sang, softer, still giving, still burning—they were nearly empty now, they could feel it, vast rooms in themselves standing open and unfurnished—but they had saved one verse, the last page of the Codex, the instruction hidden inside prophecy, and they gave it to him now the way you give a blanket to someone shaking: gently, without expectation.

Ede Voss looked up at the conductor in the fire. He did not seem afraid. He seemed, of all things, relieved—tired the way a walker is tired at the end of a long road, when the door is finally in sight.

"Thank you," he said. His voice was ordinary. Human. Hoarse from disuse. "I wrote it all down, you know. Every step. I just—" He glanced up at the crown, cooling on the head of the collapsing body below, and his face crumpled with an old, familiar grief. "I couldn't read my own instructions. Once it had me, I couldn't remember being the one who wrote them. So I hid the ending where only love could find it. Prophecy. Songs. Children." He looked directly at Rosalind, and his eyes were kind, and they were also, unmistakably, family—some deep architecture of cheekbone and brow that Rosalind would have known anywhere if they'd ever seen it before tonight. "You sing beautifully. Your mother did too."

Then Ede Voss closed his eyes, breathed in, and let out one final note—

—not eaten, not taken, but *finished*, a note resolved, a chord come home, a hundred years of hunger ending in a single sound like a bell underwater—and he was gone, peacefully, the way a candle goes out when the morning comes.

The silence afterward rang louder than any of it.

* * *

Rosalind woke on cold stone with Tam's coat over them and Wren's hand gripping theirs hard enough to bruise.

The fire was out—or settled, banked, a dragon-sized hearth glowing contentedly along Ashvane's flank. The snow fell again, freely, landing wherever it liked. On the dais, the crown lay in the wreckage of the Regent's final shape: plain gold, dented, ordinary, cool enough that Pell dared touch it and reported, wonderingly, that it felt like a spoon.

And beside it, breathing.

"Aldous," Rosalind croaked, and started to rise, and discovered that rising required parts of themselves they had spent. They stayed down. Tam propped them up instead, and Wren, and the jailer, and the whole improbable choir gathered around like a congregation, and together they watched the King of Thornfell open his eyes.

He was old.

That was the first thing—older than the boy in the orchard, older than the man in the portraits, aged a century in a moment, because the century had happened to him whether anyone was home to feel it or not. White hair. Deep lines. Hands spotted like autumn leaves. He lay among the ashes of his own captivity looking like what he was: a man who had missed one hundred years and woken to find the bill delivered.

His eyes found Rosalind immediately. There was no confusion in them, no wandering. He looked at them the way you look at a face from a dream you've finally remembered upon waking.

"You came back," Aldous said. His voice was rust and honey. "In the orchard. You said—you said if I ever stopped being myself—" He stopped. His face did something complicated and young despite its age. "I stopped being myself."

"I know," Rosalind said. "I sang you back."

"I know." He laughed—it cracked in the middle, and became weeping, and he didn't fight it. An old king crying among strangers in the ashes, holding the hand of someone whose name the world would soon forget, and none of it, somehow, undignified. "I know. I heard you. All those years, underground, in the dark—I heard someone singing, and I followed it. I followed it all the way home."

Above them, through the broken roof, dawn was coming up gray and clean over Thornfell. Somewhere out in the city, a hundred thousand stolen memories were drifting home to their owners like birds at evening, and people would wake today remembering brothers, mothers, streets, songs, debts, joys—and no one, not one soul in the kingdom, would wake remembering a guild called Wayfarers, or a choir of eight, or a courier who had walked into a dragon's fire and given away nearly everything they had.

Rosalind found they didn't mind. Or told themselves they didn't. Or would decide later which it was. There would be time. There was, for the first time in a hundred years, time.

Ashvane's warm voice moved through the stone beneath them, tired and triumphant and amused. *Well done, little Wayfarer. Now rest. And then—come see me. There is something in my fire I believe you'll want to discuss.*

Rosalind looked down at their own hands, empty as swept rooms, and felt—beneath the exhaustion, beneath the grief with no objects left to grieve for—one small stubborn ember that the flame had not taken, because they had not offered it, because some part of them had held it back without asking permission.

They didn't know yet what it was.

But the dragon did.
