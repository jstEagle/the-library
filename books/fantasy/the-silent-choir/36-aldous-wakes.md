## 36. Aldous Wakes

The last note went out of Rosalind like a rib being drawn.

They had been holding the king's name in their throat for what felt like a season—*Aldous Wren-marched-under-two-banners, Aldous who buried his mother in spring and laughed at his own jokes before finishing them, Aldous who had pressed a honey cake into a child's hands on a winter road and said remember me*—and now the name was no longer theirs to hold. The fire had taken it. Ashvane's flame, rekindled and roaring up through the throne room floor in columns of gold and ember-red, had drunk it down and carried it home.

The Regent came apart the way frost comes off a window: not violently, but all at once, everywhere, surrendering.

It stood in the center of the conflagration wearing Aldous's body—had worn it for a century, though the body had barely aged, kept like a coat in good condition—and as the Choir's song closed over it, the wrongness went out of him by degrees. The too-smooth face creased. The hollow eyes filled. And beneath the crown, which had blazed like a captive star through everything, something flickered that was not hunger.

Ede Voss looked out of the king's face one last time.

Rosalind saw him clearly then—not the monster, but the man underneath the long ruin of him. He was young. That was the unbearable part. A hundred years of eating memories and he still wore the face of a guildmaster in his prime, ink-stained fingers, a smile that had once been famous among Wayfarers for how quickly it arrived. His mouth moved. No sound came out—the palace was still silent, every noise eaten—but Rosalind read the shape of it easily enough, because they had spent a lifetime reading mouths in smugglers' dens and dark doorways.

*I'm sorry.*

And then, softer, shaped almost like a blessing:

*Well sung.*

The flame took him. Not cruelly. It took him the way a tide takes a swimmer who has finally stopped fighting it, and where he had stood there was only light for a moment, and then only air, and then the great fire settled back into its channels in the floor like a dog circling twice before lying down.

Tam felt the change before anyone. His palm was flat against the flagstones—he had conducted the whole final verse through the stone, through the soles of his boots, through his teeth—and now both his hands flew to his ears even though his ears had never worked, an old reflex from a childhood of wanting to hear. He turned to Rosalind, eyes wide, tears already running freely down a face that rarely bothered to hide anything.

He signed, quick and shaking: *Sound. There's going to be sound.*

There was.

It came back not gradually but in a rush, like water finding a breached wall—a vast indrawn breath of noise as the palace remembered itself. Wind in the high windows. Fire roaring properly now, with crackle and spit. Somewhere far above, a bell began to ring, and then another, and then a dozen more, because the city outside was waking too and bells were the oldest language it knew. And under all of it, threading through everything like a golden wire, a voice.

A voice, singing.

It was thin and cracked and a hundred years out of practice, and it did not know any of Ashvane's great song, and it didn't matter. It was singing something small—an old walking tune, a road-song, the kind Wayfarers hummed on cold mornings—and Rosalind knew it the way you know your own heartbeat, because they had heard it once before, from a king in disguise on a winter road, a man sharing a fire with a child and pretending not to be anybody important.

The crown fell off his head.

It didn't shatter. It didn't blaze or burn or crumble to ash, though half the Choir flinched and Serle raised an arm as if to shield them from some final treachery. It simply slid, the way a crown does when the head beneath it tilts forward, and hit the flagstones with a sound so ordinary—a *clank*, gold on stone—that Rosalind nearly laughed. It lay there steaming gently. As they watched, the light died out of it entirely, and it became what it perhaps should have been all along: a heavy, old-fashioned, slightly ugly circlet of gold, the kind of thing a prosperous merchant might buy his wife and immediately regret.

Ordinary gold. Cooling. Done.

And the man knelt in the middle of it all, in the scorched ruin of a coronation robe that had been magnificent a century ago and was now mostly memory, and he wept.

* * *

Rosalind crossed the burning floor toward him, and the strange thing—after everything, after the vials and the vaults and the long stair and Maren's song fading somewhere behind them in the dark—was how ordinary it felt. Their legs hurt. Their throat was ruined raw. They wanted, absurdly, a cup of tea.

The king looked up when their shadow fell across him.

He was old. That was the first shock of him, close-up. The Regent had kept the body preserved, but preservation is not the same as living, and now that the thief was gone, every owed year arrived at once, like creditors at a door. Age poured across his face as Rosalind watched—the crow's feet deepening, the hair going from brown to grey to white in the space of ten heartbeats, hands spotted and trembling. He aged a century in the time it took Rosalind to cross twenty feet of scorched stone, and he did not scream, and he did not look away, and there was something in the way he bore it that told them this, too, was a kind of courage.

He blinked at them. Blinked again.

"You," he said. His voice was wrecked, rusted, beautiful. "You're taller."

Rosalind stopped. Of all the things—

"You gave me a honey cake," the old king said, and the words tumbled out of him like water through a broken dam, a hundred years of held breath escaping. "On the Winter Road. You were—I don't know how old. Small. Angry about the snow getting in your boots. I said, *if I ever stop being myself, come find me, will you? Remember me?* And you—" He pressed a hand over his mouth, and his shoulders shook, and when he lowered it he was laughing and crying in equal measure, which Rosalind thought might be the most honest expression a human face could make. "You said, *kings are easy to find, it's remembering them that's hard.*"

"I don't remember saying that," Rosalind said quietly. It was true. The missing year was still missing; the vial had given them back pieces, glimpses, the shape of a promise, but not every word.

"Well, you did." Aldous wiped his face with the heel of his hand like a farmer, like a soldier, like nobody's idea of a king. "I've been carrying it for a hundred years. Or—it's been carrying me. I don't entirely know how any of this worked." He looked around at the throne room—at the Choir scattered across it, exhausted and singed and staring—at the dead-gold crown on the stones—at Ashvane's fire, banked low and glowing contentedly in its channels like a hearth after a feast. "Did I miss much?"

Wren made a sound that started as a sob and ended as a laugh, and then couldn't stop doing either. Tam signed something long and elaborate, caught himself, and settled for thumping his own chest twice with a fist—the Wayfarer sign for *all of it*, for *you have no idea*, for *yes*. The nun, Sister Caelle, who had not spoken since the battle began, said simply, "Rather a lot, Your Majesty." And Pem, the child prodigy who had frozen on the long stair and been carried the rest of the way on Tam's back, piped up from behind her hands: "There was a dragon."

"There was indeed a dragon," Rosalind said.

"Is she nice?"

"She's tired," Rosalind said. "But yes. She's very nice."

* * *

They got him off the floor eventually, which took four of them and considerable negotiation, because Aldous turned out to be stubborn in the specific way of people who have just gotten back control of their own knees and do not intend to surrender it. He insisted on standing. He insisted on looking at each of the Choir in turn, and when he reached Serle he stopped, and the silence between them was a whole conversation neither seemed able to finish.

Serle stood apart from the others, as he had throughout—arms at his sides, face unreadable, the forgettable face that had hunted Rosalind through half of Thornfell. He had sung. He had taken the eighth place, and given them the memories the Regent had left him—obedience, corridors, the smell of wax, a name that wasn't his—and his voice had held. But singing a thing and being healed by it are different trades, and he looked like a man standing in the wreckage of a house he had helped burn and only just learned he'd grown up in.

"You were mine," Aldous said. "My guard. Before."

"Yes, sire." The word cost Serle visibly.

"I don't remember you."

"No, sire." And then, and Rosalind would swear to this in the new Codex until the day they died, Serle's mouth did something that on another face would have been called a smile. "That's rather the point. We're working on it."

Aldous studied him a long moment. Then he extended his spotted, trembling royal hand, and Serle looked at it as if it were a lit fuse, and took it anyway.

* * *

The walk up out of the dragon's caverns took hours, because Aldous refused to be carried and had to rest every fifty steps, and because none of them minded. Ashvane's renewed warmth followed them through the forgotten ways—the tunnels that had been cold and echoing for a century now breathed with heat, and torches lit themselves as they passed, and somewhere below, faintly, they could feel the dragon humming. Not the great song anymore. Something looser. A working tune. Rosalind suspected she was improvising, and suspected further that dragons, given a hundred years' rest and a full choir's worth of company, might turn out to have a sense of humor.

Pem asked to go say goodbye and was allowed, escorted by Sister Caelle, and came back solemn and glowing. "She says the kingdom tastes better than it used to," Pem reported. "She says somebody put the weddings back."

Somebody had. That was the miracle assembling itself around them as they climbed: the stolen memories, freed from the vaults, freed from the Regent's hoard, were finding their ways home. They passed a chamber of glass vials—all empty now, every one—and Rosalind touched the shelf where their own name had been labeled, and felt nothing but cool glass. What had been theirs was theirs again, or most of it, flowing back through the kingdom like blood through a woken limb.

They emerged at dawn.

The palace above was chaos—the good kind, the kind made of people. Servants who had served a hollow king for a century without ever quite noticing stood in corridors weeping at portraits they suddenly recognized. Guards embraced. Someone had opened the kitchens, and someone else was ringing every bell in the capital, and through the windows Rosalind could see the city spread out below in the early light, alive with smoke and shouting and, here and there, spontaneous dancing. Whole districts that had forgotten their own names stood in the streets calling them out loud, over and over, like children proving they hadn't lost them.

Aldous stood at the great window and looked at it all for a long time.

"They half-remember me," he said at last. Not bitter. Wondering. Down in the square below, a crowd had gathered, and someone was pointing up at the palace, and a ripple went through them—recognition trying to assemble itself out of fragments. An old woman crossed herself. A man took off his hat. Nobody quite cheered, because you cannot cheer for a king you only three-quarters believe in, but they didn't disperse either.

"A hundred years is a lot to forgive a kingdom for forgetting," Rosalind said.

"It's a lot to forgive myself for." He turned. Up close, in honest daylight, he was very old and very plain and had the kindest eyes Rosalind had ever seen on a ruler, and they understood suddenly why a child on a winter road had liked him before knowing he was a king. "You understand, don't you. What it costs. To be remembered imperfectly."

"I'm a courier," Rosalind said. "Nobody remembers us at all. It's rather restful."

"Is it?" He smiled—slowly, creakily, a smile badly out of practice but unmistakably genuine. "Then perhaps you can teach me. When I'm not—" he gestured vaguely at himself, at the century, at the entire collapsed state of his kingdom, "—busy."

* * *

It was Tam who noticed the crown.

They were gathering in an antechamber to eat whatever the liberated kitchens produced—which turned out to be bread, butter, and an enormous pot of stew that a cook named Hesta claimed she'd been dreaming of making for years without knowing why—and Aldous sat among them like the world's oldest, most bewildered guest of honor, asking questions. What year was it. Who won the border war. Was his dog's breed still extant. Tam answered what he could in signs that Rosalind translated and Wren embellished shamelessly.

And then Tam went still, the way he went still when the stone spoke to him, and pointed.

The crown sat on a side table where a servant had deposited it without ceremony, the way one deposits a candlestick someone left in a corridor. Morning light came through the window and lay across it, and for the first time in a hundred years the Crown of Aldous was doing absolutely nothing at all. No glow. No drinking of the light. Just dull, dented, ordinary gold, catching the sun exactly the way any other object catches the sun, giving nothing back, taking nothing away.

Aldous got up, walked to it, and picked it up. Everyone tensed. He turned it over in his hands, frowning at it like a carpenter inspecting shoddy joinery.

"This is heavier than I remember," he said.

"Everything is, sire," Serle said. "You've been lighter for a long time."

Aldous considered the crown a while longer. Then he set it back down on the table—just set it down, casually, next to a butter dish—and returned to his stew, and no one moved it, and no one needed to. Later, historians would argue about where the crown ought to be kept, and curators would propose cases of reinforced glass and wards of sevenfold binding, and Aldous would rule that it belonged on a shelf in the kitchen, where it could be watched by people with better things to do than fear it. But that was later. For now it sat cooling beside the butter, and it was only gold, and the morning went on without it.

* * *

The crowd found them by midmorning.

Not the crowd—there would be councils and proclamations and a hundred tedious ceremonies for that—but a small knot of people who had followed the bells up the hill and stood in the antechamber doorway dripping slush, afraid to come further. An old woman at the front clutched her hat in both hands.

"Majesty," she said, in the voice of someone rehearsing a speech and abandoning it halfway, "we thought—you were gone, we thought you were—we thought the stories were stories." She was crying without seeming to notice. Behind her, a boy of maybe six peered around her skirts at the ancient king and said, loudly, the way children say things, "He doesn't look like the coins."

Aldous laughed. It was a terrible sound, rusty and wheezing, and it went on too long, and Rosalind realized they were laughing too, and so was everyone, the helpless laughter of people setting down something enormous.

"No," Aldous agreed, wiping his eyes. "The coins are very flattering. Wait a hundred years; they'll wear out and look my age."

The old woman stepped closer. "We don't remember everything," she said, apologetic, anguished. "There's holes. Whole years. My mother's name—I know I knew my mother's name—"

"It'll come home," Aldous said gently. "All of it. It's coming home right now. Some roads are longer than others, that's all." And then, because he was who he was, he added: "While you wait, would you like some stew? Hesta's made enough for a regiment."

She would. They all would. The antechamber filled, and filled more, and Rosalind slipped back against the wall with a bowl they weren't sure they'd eat, watching the king they had burned themselves to save ladle stew for a woman who couldn't recall his name, and feeling something in their chest go quiet and warm and finished.

It was done. The quest's whole heart, answered. The king woke. The crown cooled. The choir sang, and the song came home.

It was Wren who brought them the message, an hour later—breathless, singed at the edges still, alive, real, gloriously herself. She pressed a scrap of paper into Rosalind's hand, and Rosalind read it twice, and felt the warmth in their chest shift into something sharper.

It was from the palace record-keeper. A single line, written in a shaking hand:

*Your Majesty — the archives are returning records we never had. Books are appearing on shelves that were always empty. And every one of them, this morning, is missing the same thing: the name of the Wayfarers' Guild.*

Rosalind looked up. Across the room, Tam was teaching the six-year-old the sign for *dragon*, and Aldous was telling the story of the Winter Road badly, and nobody had noticed yet what was beginning.

No one ever did. That was the whole point of them.

Rosalind folded the paper into their pocket, beside the ghost of an ash-sealed Codex, and went to see what the world was about to forget.
