## 37. The Kingdom That Doesn't Remember

The memories went home the way birds go home at dusk—not all at once, but in small returning flocks, each one finding the window it had been born to.

Rosalind stood on the palace balcony in the grey hour after dawn and watched it happen, or watched the evidence of it happen, because memory itself was invisible even when it moved in such numbers. Below, the city stirred with a strange restlessness. A baker opened her shutters and stood a long moment with flour on her hands, staring at nothing, then began weeping over the smell of her own bread—remembering, perhaps, the morning she'd first learned the recipe from a mother who had died believing herself forgotten. Two old men met at a corner and gripped arms like brothers who had been estranged for forty years and could not say why they were crying. A woman ran down an alley shouting a name that no one else in Thornfell had spoken aloud in a decade.

"It's like watching rain fall upward," Tam said beside her. He felt it more than saw it, palms flat against the balustrade, reading the stone. "The whole city's humming. Every bell that ever rang is ringing somewhere under the ground."

"Does it hurt?"

He considered this with the seriousness he brought to all questions, turning his hands as if weighing them. "Like a limb waking up," he decided. "All pins and needles and ache. But *waking*."

Wren was asleep. She had slept for two days since the throne room, curled in a real bed in a real room, breathing the careful breath of someone who still checked sometimes to make sure breathing was something she got to keep doing. Serle sat outside her door. He said he was guarding her. Everyone understood that what he was actually doing was sitting near a person who remembered him, which was a thing he had not been able to do in thirty years, and that this was its own kind of vigil.

The crown lay on a velvet cloth in the empty throne room, cooled now to ordinary gold, so ordinary that a chambermaid had tried to dust it twice and asked, both times, why anyone kept an old hat in here.

* * *

By the third day, the books began to forget.

That was how Rosalind thought of it, though of course it was the reverse—the world's records had never held them at all, and now that the stolen memories flowed back to their owners, the gaps where the Choir should have been closed up like water closing over a stone. Rosalind discovered it in the palace archive, where they'd gone looking for any record of Ede Voss's guildmastership, wanting to know him as something other than the monster in the crown.

There was no record. Not expunged—simply absent. The ledger of the old Guildhouse listed masters going back two centuries, and between one name and the next there was a smooth space, unremarked, the way you don't remark on the space between your own teeth.

Rosalind stood there a long while with their finger on the gap.

Then they pulled every volume within reach and searched: the Wayfarers' Guild, gone from the trade rolls; gone from the road-taxes and the bridge-tolls and the harbor manifests; gone from the ballads, when Rosalind found a songbook whose pages had once, they were suddenly certain, contained a verse about courier-roads through Thornfell and now contained a verse about weather. Gone from the wanted-notices in the constabulary drawer, which was almost funny—Serle's own reports about the unlicensed singer had faded to blank paper with a pin-hole where the seal had been. Even the Ash Codex, open on Maren's advice to its final page, showed only soft grey vellum where the last lines had been sung away. Prophecies, it turned out, were made of memory too, and the kingdom's memory had been spent buying itself back.

"You knew it would be like this." Maren's voice came from the doorway, thin but steady. She had aged ten years since the locks, and walked with a stick she refused to call a stick ("it's a staff, girl, I'm not infirm"), but her blind eyes found Rosalind with terrible accuracy. "Ede wrote it plain, before he went mad. The price of the unbinding. Everything the Choir did would be paid for out of the world's remembering. That's why it worked. You can't eat a song that everyone has already agreed to give away."

"I didn't agree," Rosalind said, and heard the petulance in it, and hated it, and let it stand anyway. "Nobody asked me whether I minded being forgotten."

Maren crossed the archive slowly, click and drag, click and drag, and took Rosalind's hand in both of hers. Her grip was still strong. Lockkeeper's hands.

"No," she said gently. "But you chose it, all the same, the moment you stepped into the fire. There's a difference between being taken and being spent, child, and you were spent. Spent on purpose. Spend yourself on something worth it and you don't get to keep the receipt."

Rosalind laughed despite everything—a wet, cracked sound—and Maren patted their hand as if they were the child prodigy among them.

"The world doesn't remember us," Rosalind said. "So what are we, exactly? If there's no record. If there's no proof. If some clerk tomorrow invents a completely different history of the last hundred years and it sticks, because the truth has nothing left to argue with."

"We're each other's proof," Maren said simply. "Always were. That's the whole secret of the Wayfarers, the one we hid under all the candle-smuggling and the toll-dodging. Memory isn't kept in books, girl. Books burn. Memory is kept in people who love you enough to carry it. Now come along. Wren's awake, and she's asking whether she's allowed to have a favorite color, and I refuse to be the one who answers."

* * *

They gathered that evening in the Guildhouse—the real one, the ruined one, swept out now by eight pairs of hands and lit with perfectly legal candles, which Rosalind still found slightly disappointing.

It should have been a celebration. It was, mostly. Ashvane had sent word by means Rosalind declined to examine too closely (a dream, shared simultaneously by all seven singers, featuring an enormous amber eye and a sensation like being warmly approved of) that the dragon was well and singing again, and that the beacon above the palace burned steady gold for the first time in living memory, which meant, technically, that it now burned in dead memory instead, and Rosalind had laughed at their own joke alone for several minutes before giving up on sharing it.

Tam had brought beer. Serle had brought bread, baked himself, badly, with the fierce concentration of a man relearning pleasure one skill at a time. The nun, Sister Cadelia, had brought wine from the convent cellar and a blessing nobody had asked for and everybody needed. The jailer, Bram Holt, told a story about the Regent's empty retainers wandering lost after the unraveling, and how he'd opened his cells and given every one of them a meal and a road-pass and, in three cases, a job, because a man who guards prisoners learns to spot the ones who just need somewhere to be. The child prodigy, Nell, aged nine and insufferable about it, performed an original composition entitled "Ode to the Big Lizard," which was received with the gravity due to a national anthem.

And Wren sat in the corner with a cup of cider she wasn't drinking, quiet in the way of someone taking inventory of a house she'd only just learned she owned.

"Say it again," she said, during a lull. "My birthday. When is it?"

"Winter's end," Rosalind said. "The twenty-ninth of Thawmonth. You'll be one year old, and we're having a cake, and you're getting a scarf because you complain about the cold constantly."

"And my name is Wren."

"Wren," said Tam. "Small bird. Loud voice. Terrible liar."

"I was a magnificent liar."

"You were a manufactured memory wearing a charming smile," Rosalind said. "You've improved."

Wren smiled—that was new, that the smiles arrived before she decided on them—and looked around the table, at each face in turn, and Rosalind realized what she was doing: memorizing them. Deliberately, carefully, the way a drowning sailor memorizes a coastline. Because Wren alone of all of them existed nowhere but here. No mother held a memory of her. No record named her. She had been built of borrowed lives, and the borrowed lives had gone home, and what remained was whatever the seven of them could hold of her between them.

She was real. Precariously, daily, mutually real. It was the most fragile kind of existence in the world, and she wore it like the crown had never been worn at all.

"I want to sing tonight," Wren said. "Something true. I only have about six minutes of true material, so it'll be short."

"Six minutes is plenty," said Nell, who had once sung for four hours without stopping and considered brevity a personal insult, "if it's good."

It was good. It was six minutes about a cave, and a lie she had believed was herself, and a girl throwing herself into a breach to protect a child she'd known eleven days, and waking up in pain and finding that the pain was hers, and being glad. Nobody applauded when she finished. They did something better: they all hummed the last note back to her, holding it, passing it around the table like bread, until it faded into the candle-smoke.

"That's the thing, isn't it," Serle said quietly. He spoke rarely, and when he did, everyone listened, partly from respect and partly from long habit of fearing him. "What we are now. There's no book of us. No roll, no register. If we die—" he glanced at Rosalind, and finished honestly, because honesty was the discipline he'd chosen to replace obedience— "we die entirely. Except in each other. So we'd better stay alive, and we'd better stay close."

"We'd better write it down," Rosalind said.

The table went still.

"Not prophecy," Rosalind went on, warming to it, standing up because they always stood up when the idea got ahead of their mouth. "Not magic, not sealed pages, none of that cursed nonsense. Ink. Paper. Plain hand. Names, dates, what happened, what things cost. A record no spell can eat because there'll be nothing in it but facts and bad jokes. If the world won't remember us—" they spread their hands— "then we'll do it ourselves. By hand. Like everything worth doing."

"A new Codex," Maren murmured, and smiled her terrible blind smile. "Your guildmaster wrote a book that doomed the world, girl. Write one that keeps it."

* * *

The fading took a week, and Rosalind learned to watch it the way you watch a bruise change color—with a wince that softened gradually into acceptance.

The innkeeper at the Drowned Bell, who had sheltered them through the forgotten districts and sworn he owed the Wayfarers a debt he couldn't name, served them breakfast on the morning after the unbinding and squinted at them with polite blankness, then charged them full price. Rosalind paid it gladly. Somewhere in the man's chest a warmth had settled that he'd spend his whole life calling luck.

The roads reopened. The seals and checkpoints dissolved, their orders unsigned by any officer anyone could recall issuing them. In the capital, the districts that had forgotten their own names woke to find them chalked on their doorposts in Tam's neat stonemason's hand—he'd spent three days walking the streets touching walls, recovering names from the vibration of old stones, carving them back where they belonged. A street called Weaver's Row remembered itself. A square called Mercyfield remembered why.

King Aldous issued proclamations. He restored the courts, the schools, the festival calendars. He ordered a history of the century written, and scholars assembled eagerly, and produced a hundred years with a strange smooth patch in the middle, like a road repaired by many hands in different seasons. Aldous read the draft himself, Rosalind heard, and returned it with a note: *Leave the gaps. Gaps are honest.* He was old now—old beyond his years, a century pressed into him in a moment—but he was *himself*, and the kingdom, half-remembering, loved him with the particular devotion people reserve for something recovered from a fire.

He never spoke of the Choir publicly. But on the night of Wren's first birthday, a cart arrived at the ruined Guildhouse bearing a barrel of palace wine, no card, no seal, only a scrap of paper in a shaking royal hand that said: *For the twenty-ninth of Thawmonth. Some debts you pay without asking to whom.*

Rosalind kept the note. It was, they reflected, the closest thing to a record of them the world would ever hold, and it didn't even have their name on it.

Which was, they were discovering, a strange freedom.

They had spent their whole life invisible by necessity—smuggling contraband through checkpoints, walking past guards who looked through them, building a self out of small careful erasures. And they had hated it, or told themselves they hated it. But this was different. This was invisibility *chosen*, worn lightly, like a cloak you could take off in company. The world didn't know their name, and so the name belonged wholly to the people at the table. The world didn't know their deeds, and so the deeds needed no defending, no explaining, no monument for vandals to deface. Everything they were lived in seven other hearts and in the growing stack of handwritten pages, and there was something in that arrangement so clean, so *theirs*, that Rosalind caught themselves humming as they walked the streets of Thornfell, unnoticed, unrecorded, unburdened.

Not entirely unburdened. There were nights when the cost found them. When Rosalind passed a woman in the market who had their mother's way of counting coins, and felt the absence where a face used to be. When they woke at midnight certain someone had been calling their name across a great distance, and lay listening to the silence where the answer should have been. Grief, Maren said, was just love with nowhere to put itself, and Rosalind was learning to set it down in ink instead—in the new Codex, on a page headed *People I Miss Without Remembering Why*, which was the saddest and funniest page in any book ever written.

But that was later. On this evening, the evening the last record faded, they were all still at the table, and the candles were legal, and the beer was Tam's, and Wren was asking for the fourth time whether she was really allowed a favorite color or whether that was something you had to earn.

"Blue," Rosalind told her. "It was always going to be blue. I've decided."

"You can't decide someone's favorite color for them."

"Someone decided mine for me," Rosalind said. "Guild-issue grey. Worst decision anyone ever made on my behalf. Blue, Wren. Trust me."

Wren looked at her cup, at the candlelight, at the seven faces arranged around her like a fortress made of people, and said, "Blue," in the voice of someone trying on a coat that fits better than expected.

Outside, snow was beginning again on Thornfell, soft and unhurried, falling on a city that remembered its own name, on streets where every bell had its voice back, on a ruined Guildhouse glowing yellow-windowed against the dark—unmarked on any map, unnamed in any chronicle, known utterly and completely to the nine people inside it.

Rosalind refilled the cups, opened the new Codex to a fresh page, and dipped the pen.

"Now," they said. "From the beginning. Tam—you first. Date of birth, place of birth, and don't say 'a cave,' I want the *name* of the cave."

And Tam opened his mouth to answer—and stopped, and frowned, and laid his palm flat on the table as if the wood had spoken.

"Rosalind," he said slowly. "Someone's coming up the road. One set of boots, heavy, tired. And underneath the boots—" his brow furrowed deeper, reading the vibration like print— "there's a song. A real one. Faint. Like someone carrying a tune they've been holding onto for years and can't hold much longer."

The table went quiet. Eight faces turned toward the door.

Through the frost-furred window, far down the white road, a lantern bobbed.
