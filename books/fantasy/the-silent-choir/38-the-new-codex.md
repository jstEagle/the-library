## 38. The New Codex

The Guildhouse smelled of sawdust and beeswax, and Rosalind thought that was as good a smell as any for a beginning.

They had spent three days sweeping. Three days of hauling broken shutters out of the old courtyard, of prying the guild seal off the floor where some crown clerk a decade ago had tried to chisel it away and only gotten as far as the wings before losing his nerve—or his orders, or his interest, history being what it was now, which was mostly guesswork and goodwill. The chisel marks were still there, a scar across the Wayfarers' wings, and Rosalind had decided, after standing over it for a long while with their hands on their hips, to leave them. A record was a record. The house had been hurt, and it had gotten up again. That seemed worth writing down, actually.

So they wrote it down.

The book itself had been Wren's idea, though she'd deny it with the particular vigor of someone who had been manufactured out of borrowed memory and was therefore touchy about the subject of invention. She'd found the blank ledger in the cellar, wrapped in oilcloth, its spine stamped with a guild sigil so old the wings had worn to smudges. She'd carried it up the stairs in both arms like a foundling and set it on the table in front of Rosalind without a word.

"A codex," Tam had said, running his thumb along the cover. He said it the way he said most things—plainly, with his whole chest, because Tam Broadhurst had spent forty years in a world of vibration and had no patience for hedging. "The old one was prophecy. Sealed, cryptic, full of dates that shouldn't exist." He looked up at Rosalind, and his eyes crinkled. "This one should just tell the truth. Boringly. In ink."

"Prophecy was Ede's mistake," Rosalind said. "He hid the truth inside riddles because he thought the crown would be reading over his shoulder. He wasn't wrong. But we're not writing for the crown anymore." They opened the ledger to the first page. The paper had gone the color of weak tea, soft at the edges, patient. "We're writing for whoever comes through that door."

Which was, as it turned out, the whole problem and the whole point, because the first thing you had to decide when writing a book of record was whose names went in it—and the Choir had spent a hundred years being the kind of people whose names didn't go in books.

Rosalind dipped the pen.

*The Silent Choir,* they wrote, and then crossed it out. No. The Silent Choir had been a secret, and secrets were the old guild's whole architecture, and look where that had gotten them: a sealed book, a vanished master, a kingdom that had forgotten its own founding. They crossed it out and wrote instead:

*The Choir. Its members, in their own words, as much as possible.*

Then, beneath it, the names. Not the names the world had given them—the world's names had been the first casualty of the Regent's long dinner—but the true ones, the ones they had told each other in the dark under the palace while the fire was still settling in Ashvane's throat.

*Tam Broadhurst, stonemason, of the mining towns. Deaf since the fever at nine years old. Feels memory as vibration in stone and bone. Conducted the Silent Night when the Regent took all sound from the palace, and did not miss a beat. Married once, to Elowen Broadhurst, weaver, whom the records erased and whose hands he remembers better than his own. He keeps her memory in his palms, and it is the strongest archive I have ever known.*

Tam had reviewed this entry personally, which meant he had read it, gone very still, and then said, "Put down that she laughed at my jokes. Bad ones. Especially the bad ones." So Rosalind had added: *She laughed at his jokes, especially the bad ones,* and Tam had nodded once, satisfied, and gone back to carving.

*Wren,* the next entry began, and then Rosalind stopped, because Wren's entry was the hardest one in the book and they both knew it. How did you record a person who had been made rather than born? How did you date a birthday that had happened in a throne room, mid-battle, in the space of a song?

You started where she said to start.

*Wren. Born—by her own firm insistence and against all reasonable argument—on the night of the Unbinding, in the under-stair of the palace, aged zero. Previously a manufactured memory in the shape of a balladeer, sent by the Regent to lead us astray. She was very good at it. She is better at being real. Her songs are still too polished. We are working on it.*

Wren had read that last line and thrown a bread roll at Rosalind's head, which Rosalind had also recorded, because a book of record that left out the bread roll was not a book of record at all.

* * *

The shelf went in on the fourth day.

Tam had insisted on doing it himself, no magic, no shortcuts, though the only magic anyone had left to shortcut with was Ashvane's distant warmth and the odd lingering hum in old objects, and neither of those would hold up a shelf. He measured the wall in the main room—the wall where the guild's old charter had once hung, back when the Wayfarers had a charter and a roster and a reason to be hunted—and he marked it with chalk, and he cut the brackets from a beam of Thornfell pine that had survived two wars and one Regent, and he set to work with chisel and mallet while the autumn light came slantwise through the mended shutters.

Rosalind watched him work, the way they'd learned to watch Tam work, which was to say: they watched the wood.

Tam heard the world through his hands. Everyone in the Choir knew this, had seen it, had leaned on it in the dark places under the palace where sound itself had been stolen and Tam had stood with his bare palms flat against the stone and *listened*. But it was different, watching him carve. Each stroke of the chisel sent a little shiver up through the timber, and Rosalind could see him reading it—feeling for the grain, for the knots, for the places where the pine remembered being a young tree in a wind. He'd told them once, on the road through Thornfell, that every piece of wood had a song if you pressed it hard enough. He'd said it like it was obvious.

"What are you carving?" Wren asked, hanging upside down off a chair in the way she had, which was a habit Rosalind suspected she'd absorbed from some balladeer's memory and had decided, on reflection, to keep.

Tam didn't answer right away. He turned the beam, blew the dust from it, and ran his thumb along a line of letters taking shape in the pine.

*HERE MEMORY IS KEPT BY HAND.*

"By hand," Rosalind said.

"By hand," Tam agreed. "Not by fire. Not by song. Not by anything that can be eaten." He looked up at them, and his voice, when it came, was quiet in the way that only very large men can be quiet. "The dragon keeps the kingdom's memory, and that's right and proper. But Ashvane nearly died holding it alone, and we nearly died helping, and I'll tell you what I learned down there." He tapped the beam. "Memory that lives in only one place is a memory that can be lost. Memory that lives in ink, in wood, in hands, in *people*—" he spread his arms to take in the room, the mended shutters, the two of them, the whole ridiculous resurrected guildhouse— "that memory has to be lost a hundred times before it's lost at all."

Wren, still upside down, said, "That's the wisest thing anyone's said in this room, and I once lived with a prophecy."

"Put it in the book," Tam said.

So Rosalind did.

* * *

Maren's songs took the longest.

Not because there were many—there were only eleven, eleven songs the old lockkeeper had carried in her head through seventy years and half-blindness and the long walk from the locks to the palace on memory alone—but because Rosalind could not write them down fast enough to suit themselves, and could not write them down accurately at all, and had finally had to do the thing they had been avoiding since the night the Regent died.

They had to ask for help.

"Sing it," Serle said.

They were in the kitchen, because the kitchen was where Serle lived now, in the loose and unofficial way that a man lives in a kitchen when he has no memories but obedience, a garden out back, and a growing collection of seed catalogs that he read with the intensity other men reserved for scripture. He was shelling beans. He was very good at shelling beans. Rosalind suspected that the beans appreciated the attention.

"I can't sing it onto a page," Rosalind said. "That's the whole problem. Maren's songs are memory-songs. They don't live in the notes, they live in the— the *weight* of them. I hum one and the candlesticks hum back. Ink doesn't hum."

"Then write down what they do," Serle said, not looking up from the beans. "You're a courier. You've spent your whole life carrying things that couldn't be copied. How do you do it?"

Rosalind opened their mouth. Closed it. Couriers described. That was the trade, the whole secret trade of it: you couldn't carry a memory whole, so you carried a *description* of it so precise that the right person, in the right place, could reconstruct the loss. A seal broken at the third knot. A letter that smelled of cedar. You wrote down the shape of the absence.

So that was what they did. For six evenings, Rosalind sat in the kitchen with the ledger and hummed Maren's songs one at a time while the room remembered things—the candlesticks, the floorboards, the kettle, once, to everyone's alarm—and wrote down what the room did.

*Song the Fourth. When hummed, the east window fogs, though the fire is lit. Maren said it was a song for leaving. She learned it from a lockkeeper before her, who learned it from a woman crossing the marshes with a child she could not keep. The fog on the window, Maren said, was the marsh coming to say goodbye.*

*Song the Seventh. The floorboards warm. Maren sang this at the locks every midwinter. She said it was for the river, to remind it not to freeze all the way through, because "even a river needs to remember it can still move." She sang it to me once when I could not stop shaking. It worked. I did not ask it to. I record that here as a fact of engineering.*

Serle listened to all of it, every evening, shelling his beans, and on the sixth evening he said, without any particular emphasis, "The one about the river. Sing that one again."

Rosalind hummed it. The floorboards warmed. Serle sat very still with his hands open on the table, palms up, the way he'd stood in the under-stair when he'd agreed to be the eighth voice, and after a while he said, "I think I had someone who sang to me. I don't know who. I don't know when. But the floorboards know it, and I know the floorboards."

He went back to his beans. Rosalind wrote, beneath Song the Seventh: *Serle says the floorboards know it.* And then, because a book of record that left out the important things was not a book of record at all: *He is planting a garden. He does not know why he chose these seeds. He chose them in the first week, before he could have known anything. We are all watching what comes up in spring with considerable interest.*

* * *

Wren's birthday fell on the first frost.

She had announced it herself, three weeks in advance, with a printed invitation, which was a thing no one had known she could do and which she had absolutely absorbed from some long-dead balladeer's memory of a society wedding. *You are cordially invited,* it read, in looping script, *to WREN'S FIRST BIRTHDAY. Gifts of a non-magical nature strongly preferred. Attendance is mandatory. — W.*

"It's not mandatory," Rosalind said.

"It's my birthday," Wren said. "I can be however I like about it."

They were however she liked about it. Tam carved her a wooden whistle, which she could not yet play, which was the point—"You'll learn it by hand," he said, "like everything worth keeping"—and Serle produced, from the garden that had no business producing anything in the first frost, one perfect late strawberry, which he presented on a chipped saucer with the solemnity of a coronation. Wren looked at the strawberry for a long moment. Then she ate it in one bite and burst into tears, and Rosalind held her while she cried, and recorded none of it in the ledger, because some records are kept in the body and that is enough.

But that night, after the candles—beeswax, ordinary, contraband once and legal now, and wasn't that a joke Rosalind intended to enjoy for the rest of their life—after the candles were out and Tam had gone up to bed with the particular thumping tread of a satisfied man, Rosalind sat by the fire with the ledger open and wrote:

*Wren is one year old today. She has: one wooden whistle (unplayed), one strawberry (eaten), one birthday (first), and a family that chose her twice—once when she was lying to us, and once when she was dying for us. The second time is the one that counts. She asked me once what she was before. I told her the truth: a weapon, briefly, and then a question, and then, on a cold stair in the dark, an answer. Nobody is only what they were made to be. That is the whole thesis of this book, if it has one.*

*She says the thesis should be funnier.*

*The thesis is as funny as it needs to be.*

* * *

The last page of the first gathering was the hardest, and Rosalind saved it for a Sunday, when the house was quiet and the light came low and gold through the mended shutters, and the chisel scars on the floor seal caught it and threw it back like old brass.

*Ede Voss,* they wrote. *Guildmaster. Author of the Ash Codex. The Regent. The man who tried to control a crown and was controlled by it, and who spent a hundred years as a monster writing instructions for his own unmaking and hiding them in prophecy so that the crown couldn't read them over his shoulder. He was wrong about nearly everything, and he was wrong in the way that clever, careful, frightened people are wrong, which is the most dangerous way there is. But the last thing he did was the first right thing in a century, and the last note he sang was peaceful. I did not know him. I am his kin, distant, through lines the guild hid so well that I was hidden too. I record him here not to forgive him—I don't, yet, maybe never—but because a book of record that leaves out its monsters is a book of lies, and I am done with lies. He is in this book the way he is in the kingdom: as a warning, and as a man, and as a name on a page that anyone may read.*

*And Maren Ostwick, who is in none of our songs anymore and all of them. She stayed behind on the Long Stair and sang a decoy chorus against the hunters, and her song did not come back, and she did not either. But every song in this book passed through her first, the way water passes through stone, and comes out the other side tasting of her. When we hum Song the Fourth, the east window fogs. I have decided to believe that is her. The book does not require me to believe it. The book only requires me to write it down. I am writing it down.*

Rosalind set down the pen. Their hand ached. Outside, the frost was coming down soft and silver, and somewhere in the kitchen Serle was reading seed catalogs by candlelight, and upstairs Wren was attempting the whistle and producing a sound like a startled goose, and Tam was laughing at it in his chest-deep way that Rosalind could feel through the floorboards.

The shelf was finished. Tam had set the ledger on it that morning, first of its line, spine out, *HERE MEMORY IS KEPT BY HAND* carved into the pine above it. It looked small, one book, one shelf, one house. It looked, Rosalind thought, exactly as big as it needed to be.

They were just blowing out the candle when they heard it: the creak of the gate, the crunch of frost-hardened gravel, a knock at the door—tentative, twice-repeated, the knock of someone who had stood outside a strange building for a long time working up the nerve.

Rosalind went to the door and opened it.

A young man stood on the step, hat in his hands, half-frozen, with the look of someone who had traveled a long way on a rumor. He looked at Rosalind—at their face, at the guild wings scarred into the floor behind them, at the light spilling out—and said, in a rush, before he could lose it:

"I'm told— I was told there are people here who can help me remember my brother. Everyone says he never existed. But I—" his voice cracked, steadied— "but I have a song. My grandmother left me a song, and I don't know what it's for."

Rosalind smiled, and stepped back from the doorway, and the firelight reached out past them onto the frost like a hand.

"We can help," they said. "Come in, and sing."

And behind them, on its new shelf of hand-carved pine, the New Codex waited with its first blank page—because the book, like the guild, like the Choir, like the road itself, had only just begun.
