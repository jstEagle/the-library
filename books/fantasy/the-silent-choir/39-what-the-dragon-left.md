## 39. What the Dragon Left

The invitation came three weeks after the unbinding, delivered not by courier but by dawn itself.

Rosalind woke in their old room above the Guildhouse—the room they had slept in since they were small enough to be carried up the stairs—and found the light through the shutters the wrong color for morning. It was gold, but a deep gold, a hearth-gold, and it moved. It breathed against the boards like something alive deciding to be gentle.

They followed it down through the sleeping house, past Tam's door (a low rumble of snoring, steady as a metronome even in sleep), past Wren's room, where the light paused at the threshold as if checking on her—Wren slept badly still, waking sometimes with her hands pressed flat against the wall to feel that the stone was real—down the stairs, out the front doors, and into the frost-white street.

The light pooled at the end of the lane like spilled honey.

And standing in it was the dragon.

Not vast. That was the first miracle of it. Ashvane had folded themselves down to the size of a hay wagon, wings tucked, ember-hide banked to a soft rose glow, so that what stood in the lane looked less like the oldest living creature in Thornfell and more like an enormous, patient stove that had grown opinions.

"You walk slowly," Ashvane said. Their voice was smoke settling. "For someone I burned a year of memories to save."

"I wasn't sure you'd want visitors."

"Dragons do not *want*," said Ashvane. "We *hoard*. And I have come to make you an offer, little conductor, before my manners rekindle and I start demanding tribute." A slow blink, like a forge door closing and opening. "Walk with me. The mountain is closer than you think, when I am the one doing the thinking about distance."

* * *

Rosalind left a note on the kitchen table—*Gone to see a dragon. Back by supper. Don't wait up; if I'm eaten, the Codex explains everything*—and climbed onto the warm ridge of Ashvane's shoulder, which was less like riding and more like being carried inside a very large, very contented cat's purr.

They went up. Not to the palace—to the high country east of the city, where the passes opened into a valley no map Rosalind had ever carried showed. The frost thickened, then gave way to bare black stone, and then to stone that glowed faintly underfoot, veins of old fire running through it like rivers remembered.

"This is where I slept," Ashvane said, "before the palace was a palace. Before your guild was a guild. There was nothing here but me and the heat and the long dark, and I sang to pass the time. Then people came, and I sang *for* them, and eventually I forgot there was any difference."

The valley opened below them: a basin filled with firelight. Not flame exactly—flame moves, spends, dies. This was slower than that. It lay across the ground in drifts and pools, gold and amber and one deep seam of white-hot blue, and it did not flicker. It *waited*. Rosalind felt it before they understood it: the same pressure behind the sternum they had learned to read as memory-song, except here it came from every direction at once, a thousand thousand voices humming under the world.

"The hoard," Rosalind whispered.

"What the crown stole, the crown kept. What the crown kept, the crown lost, when you sang it loose. And what fell from the crown—" Ashvane lowered their great head, and the ember-light ran along their jaw like sweat, "—fell to me. Dragons keep what falls. It is the whole of our nature. You should know that better than anyone."

Rosalind stood very still.

In the fire, shapes moved. Not figures—*moments*. A drift of gold that rippled with laughter. A pool of amber where someone was teaching a child to whistle. Near the blue seam, something small and bright turned over and over, restless, like a coin spinning on a table that would not lie flat.

"A hundred years of stolen lives," Ashvane said. "The Regent ate them, but eating is only carrying in the mouth. When you unmade it, everything it held spilled back into the nearest fire that could hold the shape of it. Me." A sound moved through the dragon's chest that took Rosalind a moment to recognize as a sigh. "I am full, little conductor. Fuller than I have ever been. It is the strangest grief I know, and I have known several."

"They're going home," Rosalind said. "The memories. Maren said—they disperse, gently, back to their owners."

"In time. Fire is patient about returning things. But some—" the dragon's eye swiveled, vast and kind and terrible, "—some cannot go home. Because the home they belonged to was *burned*, deliberately, by the person who carried them. Some were spent as fuel. Spent on roads, spent on decoys, spent buying a deaf stonemason an hour of escape and a forgettable man his life."

The valley hummed. The small bright thing near the blue seam spun faster.

"No," Rosalind said. Their voice came out flat. "No, those are gone. I watched them burn. That's how it works. That's the *price*—"

"That is how *you* think it works," said Ashvane, "because you are young, and because Ede Voss wrote the rules of singing in a book and left out the appendix. Nothing given to true fire is destroyed. True fire does not destroy. It *refines*. What you burned passed through you and out, and I caught it, because catching is what I do." The dragon settled onto the glowing stone with a groan of cooling iron. "So. Here is the offer, and I will make it once, because dragons have dignity even when they are being generous.

"Everything you burned to save this kingdom is lying in my hoard. Your mother's face. Your mother's voice. The smell of her kitchen. The way she said your name when she thought you were asleep. Every scrap of her you fed to the fire on the pass to buy your life from Serle." A pause, gentle as ashfall. "You may take them back. All, or some, or none. But understand the cost before you choose, because there is always a cost, and yours will not be paid in flame.

"A memory returned is not a memory restored. It comes back *changed*—it has been in the fire, and it has been in me, and it knows things now that it didn't when you carried it. And you—" the eye fixed on them, and Rosalind felt seen all the way down, the way they had spent thirty years making sure no one ever saw them, "—you would have to carry it awake. No more forgetting. You have spent your whole life invisible, Rosalind Winterbourne, and burning your mother was the last wall between you and being known. Take her back, and you are known. To yourself, first of all. That is the heaviest hoard of all."

* * *

Rosalind walked down into the fire alone.

It did not burn. Of course it didn't; it was memory, and memory only burns on the way *out*. Walking through it was like walking through a crowd of strangers who all somehow knew you—brushed on every side by other people's weddings, other people's plagues, a baker's hands kneading dough at four in the morning for forty years without once resenting it, the founding of Thornfell, a dog whose name had been Biscuit, the taste of river water in a summer no one alive remembered.

They found the gold drift easily. It knew them coming. It rose to meet them.

Their mother's laugh.

Rosalind knelt in it and let it move over their hands, their arms, their shoulders, and it was warm the way kitchens are warm, the way laps are warm, and underneath the warmth was the whole of her—not just the face they had burned, but everything around the face, everything the face had meant, arriving all at once after thirty years of absence, and it was too much, it was far too much, and Rosalind sat down in the middle of a dragon's hoard and wept like a child, which was, they supposed later, rather the point.

They stayed until the crying became breathing again. Then they stood, and wiped their face on their sleeve like a courier with no dignity whatsoever, and began the arithmetic.

Because Ashvane was right, and that was the terrible kindness of it. Not everything should come back.

They found the memory of the locket—the moment of choosing, the snap of the hinge, the small white flower of flame—and they looked at it a long time, and they let it lie. They had made that choice on a frozen road with a hunter's footsteps behind them, and it had been the right choice, and taking it back would not unmake the choice; it would only unmake the person who had been strong enough to make it. Some fires you walk through. Some fires you let stay behind you.

They found the memory of their own name spoken aloud by strangers—the first time, in the throne room, when the kingdom had begun to learn them—and they touched it and laughed, wetly, and left it too. Let the world earn names honestly. They'd had enough of gifts with hooks in them.

But the face—

The face they lifted out of the fire with both hands.

It came up gently, the way a sleeping child comes up out of a bed you don't want to wake, and it settled against Rosalind's chest and sank in like rain into dry ground, and suddenly—

Suddenly there she was. Not a description. Not a song hummed from someone else's memory of her. *Her*: the crooked set of her left eyebrow, the scar on her chin from a fall off the Locksbridge when she was nine, the particular way her eyes crinkled slightly unevenly when she smiled so that she always looked like she was laughing at a joke one second ahead of everyone else. Her hands. Rosalind had forgotten her hands, and now they were back, chapped and capable and forever mid-gesture, forever about to tuck a stray hair behind Rosalind's ear.

"Oh," Rosalind said aloud, to no one, to the valley, to her. "Oh, hello, Mum."

The memory did not answer. Memories don't. But it sat warm behind their ribs, next to the heart, in the spot where she had always lived anyway, even in the years when it was only an ache shaped like an absence. The ache was gone. In its place: a woman. Whole. Carried now not as a wound but as a possession—which was, Rosalind thought, wiping their nose, probably what dragons had been trying to tell people all along. That love is a hoard. That you get to choose what goes in it.

Ashvane watched them climb back up the slope, and said nothing, which from a dragon is a great deal of tact.

"Thank you," Rosalind said.

"Do not thank me. Thank the Regent, who was fool enough to store its food in the most acquisitive creature in creation." Ashvane's hide dimpled in something like a shrug. "Though—if you insist on gratitude, spend it well. I am told your guild means to reopen."

"Word travels fast."

"Tam carves loudly." The dragon rose, unfolding by degrees, and the valley of fire sank away beneath them as they lifted into the grey morning air. "One more thing, conductor, and then I will deliver you home and return to my hoard, where I intend to spend the next century learning every memory in it properly, the way they deserve. A dragon should never be a stranger to what it keeps."

They flew south over the frost-locked passes, over Thornfell's smoking chimneys, over the palace—where the beacon tower burned steady and ordinary and gold, no longer drinking the light—and Ashvane spoke without turning their head.

"When you burned your mother's face, you told yourself it was gone forever. It was not gone. It was waiting. Most losses are like that, though few of them have a dragon standing by." A long beat of wingbeats. "I say this because of the queen. The dying one. The one the Regent erased as a mercy."

Rosalind went cold in the wind.

"Her memory is in my hoard too. All of her. The Regent swallowed her whole on her last night, and when it unraveled, she fell to me intact—her pain and her peace, her name and her garden and the lullaby she made up for a son she lost young." The dragon's voice dropped toward embers. "She cannot go home, Rosalind. Her home was her body, and her body is ash, and the son is a hundred years dead. She is a memory with nowhere to return to. Unless someone chooses to carry her. Unless someone decides that a life with no living keeper still deserves one."

The wind roared. The palace fell away behind them.

"Think about it," Ashvane said. "No hurry. Fire is patient. But I thought—" and here the oldest creature in Thornfell sounded, briefly, almost shy, "—I thought perhaps the guild that carries what others cannot, might want to meet her."

* * *

They landed outside the Guildhouse as the light went long and amber, and Rosalind came in through the kitchen door smelling faintly of woodsmoke and wearing an expression that stopped Tam mid-chop and Wren mid-sentence.

"You're different," Wren said at once, narrowing her eyes with the fierce diagnostic attention of someone who had recently become real and took reality very seriously. "Something's *back*. Around your eyes. What happened?"

"I went to see a dragon."

"Obviously. About *what*?"

Rosalind crossed to the window where the evening sun came through, and stood in it, and let them look.

"My mother's face," they said. And smiled the crooked smile—one eyebrow up, one down, laughing one second ahead of the joke—and Wren gasped, because she had heard Rosalind describe that smile a dozen times as a thing inherited and lost, and here it was, worn like a hand-me-down coat that finally fit.

Tam put down the knife. He came over and laid one broad palm flat against Rosalind's breastbone, feeling, as he felt everything, with his hands, and whatever vibration he found there made him nod twice, slow, and squeeze their shoulder hard enough to creak.

"Supper," he signed, because some moments are best honored with stew.

They ate late and loudly. Wren told the story of her first real birthday for the fourth time, improving it with each telling, and nobody corrected her, because that was how memories got strong. Afterward Rosalind sat alone at the big table with a single candle, and took out the New Codex, and opened it past the Choir's names, past Wren's birthday, past Maren's songs copied out in careful ink, past Serle's garden and the nun and the jailer and the prodigy.

On the next blank page, they wrote:

*Ashvane keeps what falls. Today I took back what I burned, and left what I needed to leave, and learned the difference.*

Then they paused, pen hovering, and beneath it added:

*There is a queen in the fire with nowhere to go. Tomorrow we will decide whether the Guild carries her.*

Below that, after a while, in smaller letters:

*Mum had a crooked smile. I have it now. It fits.*

They closed the book, and pinched out the candle, and in the dark of the Guildhouse the New Codex sat on its carved pine shelf with its pages filling the way a hoard fills—one chosen thing at a time—and upstairs, in the rooms where the Choir slept, the house breathed around them, warm and remembering, ready for whoever knocked at the door next.

Outside, far to the east, the mountain glowed softly all night, keeping its thousands of guests, and among them a queen waited in the fire—patient as flame—for someone to sing her home.
