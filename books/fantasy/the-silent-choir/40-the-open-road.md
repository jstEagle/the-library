## 40. The Open Road

The Guildhouse smelled of fresh mortar and old ghosts, which Rosalind decided was exactly right.

They had spent the better part of a season rebuilding it—not restoring, because restoration implied they wanted back what had been, and the ruined guildhall with its scorched rafters and moss-eaten ledger desks had never been theirs. This was something new wearing an old coat. Tam had re-cut every stone himself, running his broad palms over each block until the vibration pleased him, and when Rosalind asked how he knew a stone was true, he'd tapped his chest and said, *It hums honest.*

Wren had painted the sign.

It hung above the door now on iron brackets Serle had forged in his garden forge—a modest thing, the garden being mostly beans and one stubborn apple tree that had survived a century of neglect through what Wren called "pure spite." The sign read:

**THE WAYFARERS' GUILD**
*Memory Carried. Goods Also Carried. Inquire Within.*

"Goods also carried," Maren would have said, if Maren had been there to say anything at all. "As though memory were the afterthought."

"It keeps the tax collectors calm," Serle had replied, and he was not wrong.

* * *

Rosalind woke before dawn on the first morning, as they always did, out of a habit so deep it had survived even the burning. That was the strange arithmetic of what they'd given in Ashvane's flame: most of it gone—the years of smuggling candles through Thornfell's checkpoints, the names of every fence and fixer from here to the coast, the taste of the particular bread they used to buy from a baker whose face they could no longer conjure—and yet the habits remained, like footprints in snow after the walker has moved on.

They lay in the narrow bed behind the guildhall kitchen and listened to the house breathe.

Tam was already up; they could hear him in the yard, splitting wood for the stove with the steady rhythm he kept for everything—*one-two-three-four, one-two-three-four*, the metronome heart of the Choir. Somewhere overhead, Wren was singing in the bath, badly and joyfully, working her way through a song she claimed to remember from a life she'd never lived, which Rosalind had stopped trying to untangle months ago. Some songs were truer for being borrowed. Wren sang them like a woman watering flowers in a garden she'd been given.

Rosalind dressed in the dark and went down to the great hall.

The hall had been the hardest part. The old Wayfarers had built it to be forgotten—every wall angled to scatter attention, every door hidden behind false paneling, the whole architecture of invisibility that had kept them safe for four hundred years. Tam had wanted to knock it all flat. Rosalind had stood in the middle of the empty hall for a long time one evening, listening to the silence where eight voices had sung the kingdom back together, and finally said, "No. Keep the bones. Just open the doors."

So the doors were open now. All of them. Great oak things propped wide with wedges carved into the shapes of dragons—Ashvane's idea of a joke, delivered via a dream Rosalind still wasn't entirely sure hadn't just been indigestion—and morning light came down the length of the hall like a slow tide.

The new Codex sat on its stand by the hearth.

Not prophecy this time. Record. Ede Voss had written his book in ash and sealed it with fear, hiding instructions inside warnings, and it had cost a century and a kingdom. Rosalind's book was ink and honesty. Page one held seven names in their own handwriting, and an eighth added later in careful, deliberate letters—Serle, who still flinched sometimes when spoken to kindly, but who was learning. Below the names, the first entries:

*Wren's birthday. We chose midwinter. She cried. She says next year she wants two.*

*Tam has begun teaching vibration-songs to the miller's daughter, who went deaf last spring from fever. He says she learns faster than he did. He says it like a man describing sunrise.*

*Serle grew beans. They were terrible. We ate them all.*

Rosalind ran a thumb along the page and did not write today's entry yet. Today's entry would come, they suspected. Days had a way of arriving with something worth writing since the choir had learned to listen for it.

* * *

The stranger came at midmorning, while Rosalind was arguing with the kettle.

That was another thing about their burned memories: certain practical skills had gone with them. Rosalind could no longer recall how they'd once coaxed a fire to boil water in under a quarter hour, a trick they apparently had known well enough that Tam still referred to it fondly. So they stood at the hearth negotiating with the kettle like a diplomat, and therefore heard the footsteps on the gravel path a moment too late to compose themselves—which was perhaps why the first thing the visitor saw was the last conductor of the Silent Choir, holding a poker, looking mildly defeated by breakfast.

She was perhaps fifty, weathered in the way of people who worked outdoors, with a basket over one arm and the particular expression of someone who had rehearsed a speech the whole way up the hill and was now furious to find the audience unimpressive.

"You're the Wayfarers," she said.

"We are." Rosalind set down the poker with dignity.

"The candle smugglers." Her eyes narrowed. "My grandmother used to buy off you lot. Blue wax, marked with a wren." A glance toward the ceiling, where Wren's singing had paused, possibly from eavesdropping. "No offense meant to anyone named after contraband."

"None taken," said Wren's voice cheerfully through the floorboards.

The woman shifted her basket. And there it was—that thing Rosalind had seen a dozen times since the unbinding, in market squares and taverns and once from a passing hay cart: the look of a person reaching for something that wasn't where they'd left it. Their grandmother's stories. A debt unpaid. A kindness half-remembered. The restored memories had gone home gently, as promised, but they'd scattered like seed, and some of them had landed in places their owners couldn't quite see.

"I'm told you can help me remember my brother," she said.

The words landed in the hall and rang there, faintly, the way true things do. Tam had appeared in the doorway behind her—Rosalind hadn't heard him cross the yard, but then Tam never announced himself to stone floors—and his hand rested lightly on the doorframe, reading whatever the visitor's grief hummed at.

"Sit down," Rosalind said. "I'll make tea. It'll take a while. I'm terrible at it, and we're going to need the time."

* * *

Her name was Hesta Colm, and her brother had been a lockkeeper on the Thornfell road—one of Maren's locks, though she didn't know that, couldn't know that, because Maren Ostwick had died singing a decoy chorus against crown hunters, and the price of that song was that no record of her remained anywhere in the world except in eight throats and one book.

"He drowned," Hesta said. "Fourteen years ago. And I—" She stopped. Started again. Her hands worked around each other like birds. "Everyone says he drowned. There's a stone. I visit it. But I can't *find* him in my head, if that makes sense. I know the facts like I know the king's tax rates. And I've started wondering whether—" Her voice cracked clean through. "Whether I ever loved him at all. Whether I invented him. Whether I'm the kind of person who invents a dead brother to have something to grieve."

The hall was very quiet. Outside, wind moved through the apple tree in Serle's garden, and somewhere down the hill the town went about its business, hauling and hammering and haggling, a kingdom stitched back together out of songs its singers could barely recall humming.

"The Regent took memories wholesale," Rosalind said quietly. "But it preferred taking the *middle* of things. The feeling-part. It left the facts because facts are cheap—it needed people functional, not happy. So you have the stone, and the date, and the word 'brother.' And the part where he taught you to skip stones, or lied to your mother for you, or sat up all night with you when you were sick—that part got eaten." Rosalind hesitated. "You didn't invent him. You've been standing in the shape he left."

Hesta looked at them with an expression Rosalind knew intimately: the look of someone who has carried a question alone for years and has only just learned it has an answer, and that the answer costs money.

"What would it take?"

"That depends. Do you have anything of his? Something he touched often. Objects hold songs—the longer they're loved, the louder."

She dug into the basket and brought out a clay pipe, blackened with age, bowl worn smooth by a thumb. When she set it on the table between them, Rosalind heard it—faint, cracked, like a voice through a shut door. A man's low whistle. Rain on a lock-gate. Laughter that ended too fast, the way laughter does when someone is embarrassed to have been caught enjoying themselves.

And under it, threading through everything, a second melody. Older. A child's voice, skipping stones.

"There you are," Rosalind murmured.

"Can you—" Hesta's hands had gone still. "Can you give it back?"

"No." Rosalind said it gently, because gentleness was the only way that word could be survivable. "What was eaten stays eaten. But we can sing you the shape of it. We can teach you the song so you can carry it yourself. It won't be the memory. It'll be better than nothing and worse than him, and you'll spend the rest of your life deciding which it is on any given day." They met her eyes. "That's the trade. That's always been the trade. Do you want to hear him anyway?"

Hesta Colm put her hand flat on the table, next to her brother's pipe, and nodded once, the way people nod at executioners and midwives.

* * *

They sang for her in the great hall with all the doors open, because Rosalind had sworn the Choir would never again sing behind closed panels and hidden doors. Tam took the floor and felt the pipe's song through the wood, tapping the rhythm out on the boards until the others found it. Wren carried the high line—her manufactured grace repurposed at last into something honest, a voice built to imitate feeling that had learned to mean it. Serle sang the low drone, the foundation note, his hollowed-out places turned resonant, an instrument made of absence. Rosalind conducted, and let the song move through them, and gave it away.

They gave Hesta her brother's whistle in the rain. His laughter at being caught laughing. Fourteen summers of a man leaning on a lock-gate watching boats rise and fall, thinking private thoughts about the water. And beneath it all, the child's voice, skipping stones—so that Hesta could find herself in him, and know that she had loved him, because the loving had left a mark on the world and marks could be sung.

She wept for an hour. Then she laughed at something Wren said, wetly, and stayed for lunch, and ate Serle's terrible beans with genuine enthusiasm, and left at dusk with the pipe wrapped in cloth against her heart and a page from the new Codex copied out in Rosalind's hand—the song written down, notes and words, hers now, to practice and pass on.

At the gate she turned back. "What do I owe you?"

"Tell someone," Rosalind said. "That's the whole bill. Tell someone he existed. Memory's not magic, it's just people agreeing to hold things for each other. You've got hands. Use them."

She walked down the hill into the darkening town, and the Choir watched her go from the doorway, five figures in the lamplight, and none of them spoke for a while, because some silences are full.

* * *

Later, Rosalind wrote the day's entry in the new Codex by firelight.

*A woman came to remember her brother. We sang him back into her keeping. She paid us in tears and beans-compliments and will tell everyone, which means by spring we'll be overrun, which means Tam will complain, which means he'll secretly be pleased.*

Then, after a pause, they added:

*Maren, if songs travel where you are: yours is still doing work. The decoy bought us everything. I hope wherever the wind took your verse, somebody's humming it.*

They closed the book and stood at the great doors a while, looking out at the road—the open road, running down from the hills and out across the dark country, going everywhere, carrying everything, waiting for morning.

Behind them, Wren was arguing with Tam about whether her birthday could legally occur twice. In the garden, Serle was muttering to his beans. The kettle, at last, was boiling.

On the first morning of the reopened Wayfarers' Guild, a stranger had come to the door and asked to remember her brother.

There would be another tomorrow. And another after that.

Rosalind smiled, and went in to pour the tea.

*We can,* they thought, already composing the answer they'd give a hundred more times, to a hundred more strangers on the threshold. *Come in, and sing.*
